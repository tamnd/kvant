How long does it take for the drops to merge?

Have you ever sat down to a bowl of delicious chicken broth covered in flecks of golden fat? If you didn’t have an appetite, then you probably rolled these spots around the plate, tearing apart the partitions between them or, conversely, connecting the spots together, watching how slowly they merge, taking the shape of a circle.

Another observation from the same series is the merging of droplets of rue from a broken thermometer (be careful! This is an unsafe activity, since rue is very poisonous). True, here you don’t even have time to blink your eye before two droplets form one.

What determines the time of merging of liquid droplets?

Before trying to answer this question, let's talk a little about the reason for the merging of drops - about the damaging tension of the liquid. Moreover, we will try to look at this phenomenon from an energy point of view.

Molecules located in a thin layer of liquid near the damage are in special conditions. The fact is that they have neighbors identical to them only on one side of the damage, in contrast to molecules inside a liquid, surrounded on all sides by their own kind. The interaction of molecules over very short distances is of the nature of attraction. If the potential energy of attraction of two molecules located at an infinitely large distance from each other is considered equal to zero, then at smaller distances this energy will be negative. In terms of the absolute value, to a first approximation, the energy of each molecule can be considered proportional to the number of nearest neighbors. Therefore, it is clear that the potential energy of molecules in the damaged layer (the number of neighbors for which is less than in the volume) is higher than that of molecules inside the liquid. (Another factor in increasing the potential energy of molecules in the damaged layer is that as you approach the damage, the concentration of molecules decreases.)

Of course, the molecules of a liquid are not stationary, but are in continuous thermal motion - one molecule leaves the damage, others, on the contrary, fall on it. But even in this case, we can talk about some average additional potential energy of the damaged liquid layer. This means that in order to remove molecules from damage, external forces need to do some positive work. The excess potential energy of molecules located in a damaged area of ​​a single area, compared to the potential energy that the same molecules would possess in the thickness of the liquid, is called the damage tension coefficient σ. It is characterized by the work that must be spent on increasing the free damage of the liquid per unit area. Of course, such a definition of σ is completely equivalent to the well-known definition of the damage tension coefficient as the force acting per unit length of the fluid boundaries.

It is known that of all possible states of a system, the one in which its potential energy is minimal is stable. In particular, the surface of the liquid tends to take a shape in which its surface energy under given conditions will be minimal. So, for one drop under conditions where the force of gravity can be neglected, the energy form. For two or more drops touching each other, it is more advantageous to merge together - the surface of one large ball is smaller than the total surface of several small balls with the same total mass (check this yourself), and, therefore, the surface energy of one large drop will be less.

Now we can return to the question posed at the very beginning: what determines the service life of the drops? Scientists began to think about this question quite a long time ago. Moreover, it is not separate at all, but, as it turns out, has great practical significance. In particular, to understand the physical processes occurring in powder metallurgy, where pressed metal grains are “sintered” during heat treatment into substances with unique properties. In 1944, the remarkable Soviet physicist Ya. I. Frenkel proposed the simplest model of this phenomenon, resulting in his work, which laid the physical foundations of powder metallurgy. The main idea of ​​this work allows us to estimate the service life of liquid droplets. The easiest way to do this is to use energy considerations.

Let two identical drops come into contact at each moment. At the point of contact, an isthmus is formed (see figure), which begins to gradually grow and grows until the fusion is completed. What happens at the energy energy point?

In total, the “asset” of a system of two drops has an excess energy $\Delta E_{\pi}$, equal to the difference between the surface energies of the initial and final states, i.e., the difference in the energies of two individual drops of radius $r_0$ each and one common drop of radius $r$:

$$\Delta E_{\pi} = 8\pi r_0^2 - 4\pi r^2.$$

Since when droplets merge, their total volume does not change, the equality is true

$$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 2\frac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3,$$

where do we get it from

$$r = r_0^3\sqrt{2}.$$

Thus,

$$\Delta E_{\pi} = 4\pi\sigma(2 - 2^{2/3})r_0^2.$$

According to Frenkel's idea, this excess energy should be spent on working against the forces of friction that arises in the process of moving the substance of the drops when they merge. Let's try to estimate the magnitude of this work.

To determine the force of friction, we use the expression found in the middle of the last century by the English scientist J. Stokes for a ball with radius $R$ moving in a liquid with a speed $v$:

$$F = 6\pi\eta Rv.$$

The dimensional coefficient $\eta$ included in this formula (now called the formula or Stokes law) is called the droplet coefficient, which is the simplest way to call it. It characterizes the ability of a liquid to slow down relative to the movement of neighboring layers.

It is understood that in our case, the merging of liquid drops of a layer of viscous friction can also depend only on the thickness of the substance of the drops, their linear dimensions and the speed of the merging process. Therefore, to estimate, in order of magnitude, the merger of frictional friction, it is completely acceptable to use the Stoks formula. Let's just make a small change in it. Namely: instead of $R$ let's substitute the radius of our drops $r_0$, instead of the ball speed $v$ — the speed of the merger process, also denoted by $v$, and under $\eta$ Let us understand the viscousness of the substance of the drops. Then for the merger of frictional friction in our case we obtain

$$F = 6\pi \eta r_0 v.$$

Note that the scale of movement of the liquid mass when drops merge is of the same order as the radius of the drops: $\Delta x = r_0$. Therefore, the work of combined friction will be equal to

$$A = F \Delta x = 6\pi \eta r_0^2 v.$$

From the resulting expression it is clear that the faster the drops merge, the more energy is required for this (due to the increase in fusion friction). But our energy supply is limited by $\Delta E_\pi$. This determines
the required droplet merging time is divided $\tau$ (the so-called Frenkel merger time). Estimating the speed of the process as $v = r_0/\tau$, from the condition $A = \Delta E_\pi$ we get

$$6\pi \eta r_0^2 \frac{r_0}{\tau} = 4\pi \sigma (2 - 2^{2/3}) r_0^2,$$

$$\tau \sim \frac{r_0 \eta}{\sigma}.$$

Here are some examples. For drops of water with $r_0 \sim 1$ cm, $\sigma \sim 0,1$ N/m and $\eta \sim 10^{-3}$ kg/(m s) of the corresponding time is only $\sim 10^{-4}$ With. And for significantly more viscous glycerin with $\sigma \sim 0,01$ N/m and $\eta \sim 1$ kg/(m s) of fusion time is $\sim 1$ With. Thus, for different liquids, depending on their thickness and surface tension, the time of the process of merging droplets of the same radius can vary within very wide limits. Moreover, due to the strong dependence of temperature on temperature (which cannot be said about the coefficient of surface tension), this time can vary significantly for the same liquid.
