EX-CHAMPION AGAINST COMPUTERS

«Kvant» No. 3 for this year told about an exciting match between two world champions - G. Kasparov and "Deep Cell", which ended with a man winning with a score of 2:0. After this defeat, the electronic champion was improved and competed on equal terms with several grandmasters. This gave him the idea to challenge A. Karpov - in the hope of taking revenge from the ex-world champion for his defeat by the champion. Karpov flew to New York and held one meeting with Deep Cell with a shortened control (1 hour for the entire game), by the way, more profitable for the machine, for which time matters less than for a person.

In the opening and middlegame the computer acted confidently and maintained its balance. However, in the endgame the electronic grandmaster got confused, and Karpov, giving up a pawn, outwitted him. As you can see, the machine still has something to respect from a person.

A. Karpov - “Deep Cell”
Defense of Caro-Kann

1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. Kd2. If an exchange follows on e4, then there is no difference between the knight's attacks on c3 and d2. The idea of ​​the move 3. Kd2 is that in case of 3...g6 White will be able to hold the central pawn with the move c2 - c3. And yet, “Deep Cell”, not paying attention to these theoretical nuances, chooses the option of fixing the bishop.

3...g6 4. c3 Cg7 5. e5. With a strengthened center, White could easily complete the development of his pieces. Instead, they fix their target on foot, and the computer quickly attacks it according to all the rules of such formations.

5...f6! 6. f4 Kh6 7. Kgf3 0—0 8. Ce2 fe 9. fe c5! 10. Kb3 cd 11. cd Kc6 12. 0—0

Qb6 13. Kph1 a5! 14. a4. 14. Kc5 with the idea of ​​Ka4 and Kc3 immediately deserved attention.

14...Bf5 15. Cg5. When making this move, White probably already had in mind the sacrifice of the b2 pawn, otherwise he would have immediately played 15. Kc5. 15...Ce4 16. Kc5 Qxb2. Risky decision. In the already position 16...Kf5, sacrificing the exchange, but successfully completing the offensive 17. Kd7 Q:b2 18. K:f8 Kf:d4 with counterweight.

17. K:e4 de 18. Rbf1 Q3! The only move! 18...Qs3 leads to the loss of the Queen - 19. Rbf3, on 18...Qa2 strong 19. Kd2!

19. Cc1 Fs3 20. Cd2 Fs3 21. Cc1 Fs3. Yes, White has nothing to gain from repeating moves. This result is established by the machine, but not by Karpov. The champion tries to confuse his electronic partner, but until the deep endgame he acts very accurately.

22. Rbf3! Q1 23. Cc4+ Kph8 24. C:h6 Q:d1 25. C:g7+ Kp:g7 26. Jl:d1 ef 27. gf. Perhaps it would have been worth taking the b7 pawn, but the machine's next move, defending it, is difficult to pass even to the grandmaster. 27...Jla7! 28. Cd5 Jl:d8 29. Jl:b5 Ra6! It turns out that the rook’s maneuvers along the “a” file are not so ridiculous: growth 30...Ka7 31. C:b7 K:b5 32. C:a6 Jl:d4 with full equalization. 30. Ce4 Jl:d4 31. Jl:d4 K:d4 32. Jl:b7 Re6 gives White nothing, and the board is a broken draw.

30. Cc4 Ra7 31. Cd5 Ra6 32: Jlc5 Jl:d7 33. Kpg2 Lab6. The cunning rook even manages to show activity. 24. C:c6. Isn't it a shame to exchange a bishop for a knight? It was better to immediately raise the king to the center. 34...bc 35. Kpf2. After 35. Jl:a5 Jlb4 White's chances of success were zero. 35...Jld5! 36. Jl:d5 cd 37. Jlc1 Jlb4 38. Kpe3 Jl:a4. The easiest way to a draw is 38...Jlb3+ 39. Kpe2 Jlb4, but the computer does not miss the opportunity to grab a pawn.
