M1227. Let's call a chess tournament, in which the vices played each other in one game, logical, if for any two vices the one who scored no more points did not top in the personal meeting. (1 point is given for winning, 1/2 for a draw, 0 for a loss.)

Let the tourist be illogical. There are two players $A$ and $B$ such that $A$ scored no more points than $B$, but beat $B$ in a head-to-head match. Then there is a player $C$ such that $A$ played worse with $C$ than $B$ played with $C$ (otherwise with all $n-2$ players except $A$ and $B$, $A$ would have scored no less points than $B$). Let's change the tournament table as follows: the game $A$ with $B$ will be replaced by a draw, the result of the game $A$ with $C$ will be increased by 1/2, and $B$ with $C$ will be reduced by 1/2. In this case, the result - the amount scored by each player - will not change, but the total number will increase.

M1229. Prove that for every natural number $m > 1$ the number
a) $4^m + 5$;
b) $8^m + 9$;
c) $a^m + a + 1$, where $a$ is an integer and is not divisible by 8; is not the square of an integer.

Remainders when divided by 4
(with odd $m > 1$):

| $a$ | $a^m$ | $N = a^m + a + 1$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 0 | 3 |
| 3 | 3 | 3 |

Since $p - 1$ and $p + 2$ are of different parities, this is only possible for $p - 1 = 1$, $p = 2$, but then $p + 2 = 4$ and $3m - 2 = 2$, which is impossible for the integer $m$.

c) Here, if $m$ is even, the same idea works as in point a): the number $N = a^m + a + 1$ is found using k $a^m$; and for odd $m$, as in point 6), it is enough to deal with the remainders when dividing by powers of two.

Let $m = 2r$ first. Then

$$a^{2r} < a^{2r} + a + 1 < a^{2r} + 2a^r + 1 = (a^r + 1)^2,$$

i.e. $a^{2r} + a + 1$ is not a square.

Let now $m = 2r + 1$. Then, considering the various possible remainders 1, 2, 3 when dividing $a$ by 4, we are convinced that $N$ gives remainder 3 when divided by 4 (see the table in the margin), and with $n = 8k + 4$ the number $N = (8k + 4)^m + 8k + 4 + 1$ gives remainder 5 when divided by 8.

But a square can only give remainders 0, 1 or 4 when divided by 8. Indeed, with a whole $k$

$$(4k \pm 1)^2 = 16k^2 \pm 8k + 1, (4k \pm 2)^2 = 16k^2 \pm 8k + 4, (4k \pm 3)^2 = 16k^2 \pm 24k + 1, (4k)^2 = 16k^2.$$

Note that for $a$, square 8, the statement of the problem is not always true: for example, for $a = 72$ and $m = 3$ the number is $a^m + a + 1 = 611^2$. (From the general theorems of algebraic geometry it follows that for every $m > 3$ the equation $x^m + x + 1 = y^2$ has only a finite number of solutions in integers.)

F1238. There is a small air bubble in the middle of the long cylindrical tube with a face. If you place the tube vertically, the bubble will move at a constant speed of $v_0=1$ cm/s.
The tube was placed horizontally and accelerated along the long side to a speed of $v=20$ m/s. Where will the bubble stop? Where will it shift if the floating speed is increased to 30 m/s? Where does he end up after the tube is slowed down?

Glycerin is a very important liquid, so we can assume that at any moment the speed of the bubble relative to the tube is proportional to the acceleration of the tube (and bubble!) relative to the ground - the high friction force is proportional to the relative speed. Considering the relationship between acceleration, speed and displacement of a point, we can say that the movement of a bubble relative to the tube is related to its speed relative to the tube in the same way as the speed of the tube relative to the ground is related to the acceleration of the tube relative to the ground.

The movement of the bubble in a vertical position of the tube is the same as if in a horizontal position the tube was moved with a constant acceleration $a=g=10$ m/s² (the velocity of the bubble is directed in the same direction as the acceleration - the bubble is lighter than water). Let's take into account the numbers from the condition: if the tube accelerates 10 m/s² in 1 second, it will acquire a speed of 10 m/s, and the bubble will shift by 1 cm.

So, a tube speed of 10 m/s corresponds to a displacement of 1 cm. This means that at a speed of 20 m/s the bubble will stop at a distance of 2 cm from the initial position. When the speed increases to 30 m/s, it moves another 1 cm, and when the tube stops, the bubble returns to its original position.

F1239. In a vertical telloisulated cylindrical vessel under a massive piston there is 1 mol of an ideal monatomic gas at a temperature of $T_0$. Let's begin to compress the gas, lowering the piston. After work $A$ was done, the piston was released and it stopped in a new equilibrium position. Find the temperature $T_x$ in this state.

The work $A$ performed on the system goes to change the internal energy of the gas $\Delta U$ and the potential energy of the piston $\Delta E_p$:

$$A = \Delta U + \Delta E_p.$$

For a mole of a monatomic ideal gas, the change in internal energy is given by

$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} R(T_x - T_0).$$

The change in the potential energy of the piston can be found as follows: it is equal to the work that had to be done to quasi-statically move the piston from the initial position to the final one. At the same time, the external force doing work at each moment of time

must be equal to the force of gravity $mg$ acting on the piston. Since the piston is in equilibrium in the initial and final states, this force of gravity is equal in magnitude to the force of gas pressure in the vessel $pS$ (we neglect the pressure of the outside air). Denoting by $\Delta h$ the change in piston height, we obtain

$$\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h = pS\Delta h = p\Delta V,$$

where $\Delta V$ is the change in gas volume. Using the Mendleev-Clapeyron equation for 1 mole of gas, we find

$$\Delta E_p = p\Delta V = R(T_x - T_0).$$

Thus,

$$A = \frac{3}{2} R(T_x - T_0) + R(T_x - T_0) = \frac{5}{2} R(T_x - T_0),$$

and therefore,

$$T_x = T_0 + \frac{2}{5} \frac{A}{R}.$$

F1240. In the circuit shown in figure 1, the capacitance of the capacitor is $C$, the resistance of the resistors is $R$ and $2R$. What charge will flow through jumper $AB$ after connecting a battery with voltage $U_0$? What if resistor $R$ is connected between points $A$ and $B$? All elements are considered ideal.

First, let's look at the case with a jumper (its resistance is zero). Each of the capacitors will eventually be charged to voltage $U_0$, i.e. charge will pass through the resistors

$$q = 2CU_0.$$

However, the current flowing through $R$ is at any moment twice that through $2R$. Thus, a charge will pass through the jumper

$$Q = q_R - q_C = \frac{2}{3} q - \frac{1}{2} q = \frac{CU_0}{3}.$$

Now let's discuss the general case - the resistance of the jumper is not zero. Let's consider a certain moment in time when the battery has already been connected, but equilibrium has not yet been established (Fig. 2). The current flowing through resistor $R$ (jumper) is

$$I = \frac{\varphi_A - \varphi_B}{R} = \frac{q_1 - q_2}{RC}.$$

In a short period of time $\Delta t$ the following charges will occur:

$$\frac{U_0 - \varphi_A}{R_1} \Delta t = I \Delta t + \Delta q_1,$$

$$\frac{U_0 - \varphi_B}{R_2} \Delta t + I \Delta t = \Delta q_2.$$

Let's multiply the first equality by $R_1$, the second by $R_2$ and subtract one from the other. As a result we get

F1241. On an inclined plane with an angle $\alpha$ and a coefficient of friction $\mu$ lies a small washer of mass $M$, on which a charge $Q$ is placed. A uniform magnetic field with induction $B$ is perpendicular to the inclined plane, as shown in figure 1. The puck is released without initial speed. Determine the magnitude and direction of its steady velocity.

Let us immediately note that if $\mu \geqslant tg \alpha$, the washer will not be lifted at all. Therefore, we will discuss case $\mu < tg \alpha$.

Let us consider the forces acting on the puck and lying in the plane making an angle $\alpha$ with the horizon (Fig. 2). This is the component $\vec{F}_T$ of the gravity force, the direction along the plane downwards and equal to $F_T = Mg \sin \alpha$, the frictional force $\vec{F}_{tp}$, the direction against the speed $\vec{v}$ of the puck and equal to $F_{tp} = \mu Mg \cos \alpha$, and the magnetic force $\vec{F}_M$, perpendicular to the speed $\vec{v}$ and equal to $F_M = QvB$. In steady motion, the vector sum of all three forces is zero:

Fig. 1. Fig. 2.

$$\vec{F}_T + \vec{F}_{tp} + \vec{F}_M = 0,$$
or, taking into account the perpendicularity of the forces $\vec{F}_{tp}$ and $\vec{F}_M$,
$$F^2_T = F^2_{tp} + F^2_M.$$
From here the steady speed is found:
$$v = \frac{Mg}{QB} \sqrt{\sin^2 \alpha - \mu^2 \cos^2 \alpha}$$
and the angle between the vectors $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{F}_T$:
$$\beta = \arcsin \frac{\mu}{tg \alpha}.$$
