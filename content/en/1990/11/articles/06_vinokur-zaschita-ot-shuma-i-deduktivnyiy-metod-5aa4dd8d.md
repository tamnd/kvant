NOISE PROTECTION
AND DEDUCTIVE METHOD
(from Dr. Watson's stories)

Candidate of Technical Sciences
R. VINOKUR

“Since the tram began running along my street, my home has ceased to be my fortress. The office is now noisy, it interferes with work and makes people nervous. Thick brick walls almost do not allow noise to pass through, but it bleeds through the window, no matter how tightly I close it... And how opportune is this unexpected appearance of a glazier. He examined the window for a long time and suggested inserting another glass into the frame, so that together with single-layer glazing it would be double glazing: two glasses 3 mm thick *) with a gap of 15 mm between them...”

- Dear Watson! - Holmes' clear voice cut through my thoughts. — It is necessary to increase both the thickness of the glass and the width of the gap. This, of course, will cost more, but the costs will certainly be repaid by the achieved comfort.

“Holmes,” I was surprised, “how did you read my thoughts?”

“Nothing simpler,” the famous detective smiled. “In the newspaper on the table, in your hand, the address of the glass workshop is underlined and the inscription “double glazing is made for noise.” Then, I noticed how you, wincing from the roar of another tram, thoughtfully drew “3+15+3” with your finger on the foggy glass. So, be sorry, that's all.

“Indeed, it’s simple,” I agreed, “but then, won’t you help me figure out this matter?”

- A true detective, in my opinion, should have an idea of ​​​​acoustic efficiency. Add to this my passion for music, and it becomes clear why I am aware of the problem.

I'll try to help you. It is often said that a sound wave travels through a wall. But this should not be taken literally. A wave falling on a wall from some sound source exerts a time-varying pressure on it, the amplitude of which Δp is significantly less than the atmospheric pressure p. However, this impact is sufficient to cause the wall to oscillate in a direction perpendicular to its surface. And these vibrations, in turn, caused air vibrations. In other words, the vibrating wall emits sound. Thus, a transmitted wave is a wave emitted by the wall of a room insulated (insulated) from noise. The energy E of the transmitted wave is proportional to the product $Sv^2$, where S is the area of ​​the wall, v is the amplitude of the vibration speed of the wall surface that faces the insulated room. The smaller the value of E compared to the energy $E_0$ of the incident wave (Fig. 1), the better the noise protection properties of the wall (in other words, sound protection). To understand what these properties come from in the main movement, let’s turn to an analogy. You probably know this circus act: a heavy anvil is placed on the chest of a prone athlete, and he hits it with all his might with a hammer. The audience is in ecstasy: it seems to them that the force of the blows, capable of flattening a knight’s helmet, also adds up to the weight of the anvil. However effective

This is completely figurative. The athlete’s chest is struck not by a hammer, but by an anvil, and the acceleration imparted to the anvil when the hammer hits is very small due to its enormous mass (remember Newton’s second law). Consequently, the speed acquired by the anvil is very low. So, the anvil, as it were, “quenches” the blows of the hammer. In our problem, the role of the anvil is played by a wall (I mean the wall as a single slab or a massive plate); the blows of the hammer are a sound wave falling on the wall, and the shocks of the anvil that the athlete experiences are a transmitted sound wave. And, by analogy, the more massive the wall, the better the sound insulation.

Of course, sound insulation depends on the elastic properties of the wall and on its ability to absorb the energy of mechanical vibrations, converting it into heat, but usually to a lesser extent than on the mass of the wall. More precisely, from its so-called surface density

$$\sigma = \frac{m}{S} = \rho h,$$

where $m$ is the mass of the wall, $h$ is its thickness, $\rho$ is the density of the materials from which it is made. Indeed, if we take the values ​​of $\Delta p$ the same for all points of the wall surface from the side of the incident wave, then the acceleration amplitude of the wall is

$$a = \frac{\Delta p S}{m} = \frac{\Delta p}{\sigma},$$

and during oscillations $v \sim a$. So the larger the surface density

We can consider a simple mechanical system that simulates this process: two absolutely rigid bodies with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, connected by an incredible spring with a stiffness $k$ (Fig. 2). If the first body is acted upon by a force that changes time according to the harmonic law with a frequency $f$, then the system performs forced oscillations. Here the first body plays the role of a tram chassis, the second - the body, and the spring - a shock absorber. Select the values ​​$k$ so that the amplitude of the oscillation speed of the second body $v$ is as small as possible, this is the purpose of depreciation. You can write down the equation of motion of both bodies and, having solved them, obtain the dependence of the value $v$ on the parameters of the system and the frequency of sound. However, if it does not return, I will present the finished results, mostly in quality form.

The proper part of the oscillations of this system is

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{k \left( \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2} \right)}.$$

If the frequency $f$ of the driving force is equal to $f_0$, resonance occurs - the amplitude of oscillations of the second (and the first) body increases sharply, and the greater it is, the less mechanical energy is lost in the system due to its conversion into heat (for example, due to friction). In the frequency range $f \lesssim \frac{1}{2} f_0$ the system oscillates as one rigid body with mass $m = m_1 + m_2$ and the amplitude of the oscillation speed $v$ practically does not depend on the elasticity of the spring. In the case of $f > f_0$, the value of $v$ also depends on the mass of the system, but decreases very quickly with increasing frequency. Therefore, shock absorbers are selected so that the resonant frequency of the system is at least half the frequency of the driving force...

“I know something about shock absorbers,” Holmes said first in a smooth speech. “But how does this relate to soundproofing using double restraints?”

Holmes looked at me slightly sympathetically.

— The layer affected by the plates (Fig. 3) plays the role of a shock absorber. And we can appreciate its toughness. If the thickness of the layer is $d$, then the volume of the impact gap is $V = Sd$ (S is the area of ​​each plate). When mixing the plates in the case of their oscillatory motion, the thickness of the gap can change by a small amount $\Delta x$. Then, according to the Boyle-Mariotte law, we have:

$$(p + \Delta p)(V + \Delta V) = pV,$$

where $\Delta V = S \cdot \Delta x$ is the change in the volume of impact, $\Delta p$ is the change in its pressure. Considering that $\Delta V \ll V$, $\Delta p \ll p$, and neglecting the product of small quantities, we obtain:

$$\Delta p = -p \cdot \Delta V / V = -p \cdot \Delta x / d.$$

Thus, the maximum force with which the oscillating influence acts is

$$F = -\Delta p \cdot S = -\frac{p S}{d} \Delta x,$$

and, therefore, the rigidity of the impact between the plates $k = pS / d.$*
