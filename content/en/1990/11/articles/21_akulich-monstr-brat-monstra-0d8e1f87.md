"Monster" - brother
"Monster"

In the article “The priest had a dog” («Kvant» No. 6, 1989), a whole Russian phrase was published that says something about itself. For example, like this:
This phrase contains twenty-eight letters and one period.
This is certainly true. But the phrase also contains punctuation marks (though only one - a period - but nevertheless...). Therefore, it makes sense to put it like this:
This phrase contains thirty-eight letters and one period.
According to myself, the information has now become much more complete. But this is not enough. Words in Russian are not written clint, but are separated either by spaces or punctuation marks. Therefore, it makes sense to add an additional phrase like this:
This phrase contains sixty-two letters, ten spaces, two commas and one period.

True, you can litter here. After all, a space is nothing more than the absence of a letter, so it turns out something like the paradoxical expression “PRESENT OF ABSENCE.” Also, does having a comma between two words imply that there is no space between the words? It’s hard to say: you can print it this way and that, and everything will be correct. Therefore, it’s probably better not to mess with spaces.
In addition, you can compose phrases that are not just incorrect (that would be too simple!), but directly opposite to the true information:

It is not true that this phrase has thirty-eight letters.
It is not true that this phrase contains sixty-five letters, as well as two commas and one period.
But all this is just speculation about the main thing. This phrase, which is rightly called a “monster” in the article, also contains incomplete information about itself: nothing is said about punctuation marks. So I guess
