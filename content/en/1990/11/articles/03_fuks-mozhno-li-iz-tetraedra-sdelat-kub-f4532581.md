Many mathematical works are difficult for schoolchildren for purely technical reasons: to understand them you need to know certain things. The difficulties that a student will encounter when reading this article are of a different kind: the style of work itself will seem unusual to him. This style is characteristic of much 20th-century mathematics and is worth getting to know.

Hilbert's problems

In the last year of the 19th century, the rather young, but already spring, famous German mathematician David Hilbert gave a presentation at the next (second) world mathematical congress, which took place that year in Paris. The report was unusual in form. It did not include new theorems and did not offer solutions to any problems. On the contrary, it contained the formulations of twenty problems, the solution of which, according to the speaker’s plan, was to become the main stimulus for the development of mathematics in the new, 20th century. And it became one. Hilbert's problems became the most prestigious problems in mathematics; As a rule, the solution to Hilbert's problem led to the creation of a new field of mathematics, and modern mathematics largely consists of the fields that arose in this way. This can be seen by looking at the impressive two-volume book “The Development of Mathematics Growing Out of Hilbert’s Problems,” published in 1976 by America.

Rican Mathematical Society.

Not all of Hilbert's problems are original. Thus, the first gap is the “continuum problem” (is there a set of cardinality intermediate between countable and continual?), which belonged to Georg Cantor and dates back to 1878. Hilbert's problems belong to different modern mathematical disciplines, but they have one thing in common: they are specific, as a rule, each contains a question that can be answered “yes” or “no”. For example: “Is the number $2^{\sqrt{2}}$ transcendental?” (7th space) or “Can an equation of the 6th degree define on a plane a curve consisting of 11 ovals lying outside each other?” (half of the 16th problem), etc.

Almost all of Hilbert's problems have now been solved, although some things have not been done. (For example, a specific question about solving a 7th degree equation, which is part of the 13th problem, did not receive an answer; however, in a certain theoretical sense, the 13th Hilbert problem was solved in the student work of V. I. Arnold.)

Different Hilbert problems required different times to be solved. Thus, the “continuum problem” was (in a certain sense) solved by P. Cohen in 1963, the 7th gap - by A. O. Gelfond in 1934, the above-mentioned part of the 16th problem - by D. A. Gudkov in 1969. But which of Hilbert's problems was solved the best? The only one of Hilbert's problems related to classical geometry was solved before anyone else - the 3rd problem, which deals with equalities.

What's the problem?

Two polytopes are called equidistant if one of them can be cut into (multi-boundary) parts from which the other can be composed. The concept of equistasis can be interpreted broadly (and we will do so): polytopes $S'$ and $S''$ are called equistasis if there exist polytopes $P_1, \dots, P_n$ with the following property: polytopes $S'', P_1', \dots, P_n$ can be cut into polyboundary parts, from which polytopes $S''', P_1', \dots, P_n$ can be composed.

It is obvious that equal-shape polyhedra have identical volumes. Hilbert's 3rd problem asks whether the converse is true, i.e., whether any two polytopes of identical volumes are equidistant.

Dehn showed, and we will look at his proof here, that this is not true, i.e., that polytopes of equal volumes can be (and in fact, as a rule, are) not equistatic.

It must be said that Hilbert’s 3rd problema has a specific formulation: it is not about an arbitrary pair of polyhedra of the same volume, but about two triangular pairs with identical (in shape and size) bases and equal (in length) heights. It can be shown, however, that if the pyramids were also always equidistant, then any two polytopes of equal volumes would be equidistant (see Exercise 1 below). In view of this, to test Hilbert’s hypothesis, it is enough to establish the inequality of any two polytopes with the same volumes. And we will consider a cube and an equal tetrad as such a pair.

Hilbert's talk contains a brief indication of the source of the problem: it will naturally arise when thinking through the concept of volume. Let us assume that the volume of some figure, let it be a polyhedron, is equal to $a$ cubic units. By definition, this means that our polyhedron has the same volume as the beam $1 \times 1 \times a$. What does this mean? A definition suggests itself: polygons have the same volumes if they are equidistant (especially since, as we will see, such a definition is fully suitable for the areas of polygons). However, Archimedes already experienced the inconvenience of such a definition when proving the well-known formula $\frac{1}{3}Sh$ for the volume of a pyramid. Since it was not possible to prove the equivalence of the pyramid and the beam (and we will see that it does not exist), Archimedes had to resort to another argument. The solution to our problem using invariants is divided into the following stages. Need to:

(a) agree on what our invariant will be - a number, a figure, or something else (in our case, “what else”);
(b) describe a method for comparing an invariant with a polyhedron (in our case this will be quite simple);
(c) make sure that this is really an invariant, i.e., does not change under our transformations (in our case, this is not particularly difficult);
(d) calculate the invariant for the cube and terazdar (this will take us one second);

(e) prove that the receiving invariants are different (this will seem very complicated to us, but a rigorous proof will be required).

So let's get started.

Dehn invariant

Let $S$ be a polyhedron with $N$ edges. Let $l_1, \dots, l_N$ be the lengths of these edges, $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_N$ the values ​​of the corresponding dihedral angles, measured in radians. Let us compare the set of pairs $(l_1, \varphi_1), \dots, (l_N, \varphi_N)$ with our polyhedron. In each pair, the first place (positive, but this is not particularly important for us) is a real number, the second place is a real number between 0 and $2\pi$ (since the polyhedron is not assumed to be convex, angles, in principle, can be larger than l. It is more convenient for us to assume that the second place is occupied by a real number, determined up to a term of the form $2k\pi$, $k \in Z$. This set will be our invariant, only we first apply agreement, which sometimes allows us to consider sets made up of different pairs to be identical.

So, the word `набор` will denote a finite set of pairs in which the first place is a real number (any), the second is a real number, which determines, up to a term of the form $2k\pi$, $k \in Z$. We emphasize that when we say “set,” we do not necessarily mean the set that receives from the POLYHEDON; for example, the set may be empty ($N=0$).

(1) any pair $(l, \varphi)$ can be replaced by two pairs $(l', \varphi), (l'', \varphi)$, where $l' + l'' = l$;
(2) on the contrary, if the set contains pairs $(l', \varphi), (l'', \varphi)$ with identical second terms (recall that the identity of the second terms is a concept up to the term $2k\pi$), then we can replace them with one pair $(l, \varphi)$, where $l' + l'' = l$;
(3) any pair $(l, \varphi)$ can be replaced by two pairs $(l, \varphi'), (l, \varphi'')$, where $\varphi' + \varphi'' = \varphi$ (and this equality is the concept “modulo $2\pi$”, i.e. its left and right sides can differ by a term of the form $2k\pi$);
(4) conversely, if the set contains pairs $(l, \varphi'), (l, \varphi'')$ with the same first terms, then they can be replaced by a single pair $(l, \varphi)$ with $\varphi = \varphi' + \varphi''$;
(5) the pair $(l, \varphi)$, in which $l = 0$ or $\varphi = 0$, can simply be discarded from the set.
If two sets are such that one can be obtained from the other by sequential application of several valid operations, then we will say that these sets are equivalent, or similar.
Thus, the Dehn invariant of the polyorganic $S$, by definition, is the set $(l_1, \varphi_1), \dots, (l_n, \varphi_N)$ described above, considered up to similarity.
We will prove two things:
Theorem 2. If two polyorganics are equivalent, then their Dehn invariants are the same (that is, the corresponding sets are similar).
Theorem 3. The Dehn invariants of a cube and a regular tetrahedron are different (that is, the corresponding sets are similar).
It follows from these two theorems.
Dehn's theorem. A cube and a regular tetrahedron are not equal.
(Note that Theorem 3 is valid regardless of whether the volumes of the cube and tetrahedron are equal or unequal, although only the case of equal volumes is important for us.)
Before proving the theorems, we formulate two lemmas about sets; We will prove the first of these lemmas right there; the second is a separate matter.

Something about the similarity of sets
Lemma 1. If the number $\varphi$ is commensurable with $\pi$, then for any l the pair $(l, \varphi)$ can be eliminated from any set, and the new set will be similar to the original one.
Lemma 2. If $l \neq 0$ and $\varphi$ are not commensurate with $\pi$, then the set consisting of one pair $(l, \varphi)$ is not similar to the empty set.
Proof of Lemma 1.
Let $\varphi = \frac{p}{q}\pi$. The pair $(l, \varphi) = (l, \frac{p}{q}\pi)$ can be replaced by a set of 2q pairs $(\frac{1}{2q}l, \varphi)$ (because $l = \frac{1}{2q}l + \dots + \frac{1}{2q}l$, 2q terms), and these 2q pairs can be replaced by a single pair $(\frac{1}{2q}l, 2q\varphi)$ (because $\varphi + \dots + \varphi = 2q\varphi$, 2q terms).
No $2q\varphi = 2q \cdot \frac{p}{q}\pi = 2p\pi$, and according to our rules, together 2pπ can be written 0.
The pair $(\frac{1}{2q}l, 0)$ can be excluded from the set. The lemma is proven.
Lemma 2 might seem very noisy, but there is a subtlety to its proof that we will save for later.
We will prove Lemma 2 after we give the proofs of Theorems 2 and 3.
