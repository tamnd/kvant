It is interesting to consider a similar problem for triangles. When rotating an equilateral triangle around the center by $60^\circ$, its sides will divide the sides of the original one in the ratio 1:1:1; when dividing the sides of some of the triangle in this respect, after drawing straight lines through the division points, a triangle equal (symmetrical) to it is obtained. But for polygons with $n>4$ sides, the corresponding procedure does not preserve area.

For hexagons this is especially easy to check. (The side must be divided here in the ratio $1:\sqrt{3}:1$). Consider a degenerate hexagon whose vertices are the vertices of a regular triangle and the midpoints of its sides (Fig. 3). If the sides of the resulting hexagon are divided in the ratio $1:\sqrt{3}:1$, then after checking the corresponding lines, the resulting hexagon will be a hexagon that is part of the original triangle, i.e., it will have a smaller area. M1227. Let's call a chess tournament, in which the vices played each other in one game, logical, if for any two vices the one who scored no more points did not top in the personal meeting. (1 point is given for winning, 1/2 for a draw, 0 for a loss.)

Let the tourist be illogical. There are two players $A$ and $B$ such that $A$ scored no more points than $B$, but beat $B$ in a head-to-head match. Then there is a player $C$ such that $A$ played worse with $C$ than $B$ played with $C$ (otherwise with all $n-2$ players except $A$ and $B$, $A$ would have scored no less points than $B$). Let's change the tournament table as follows: the game $A$ with $B$ will be replaced by a draw, the result of the game $A$ with $C$ will be increased by 1/2, and $B$ with $C$ will be reduced by 1/2. In this case, the result - the amount scored by each player - will not change, but the total number will increase.
