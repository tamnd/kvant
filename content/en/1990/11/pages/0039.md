Static friction force

How do we usually solve problems in dynamics? We make a drawing, depict the forces and write the equations of Newton’s second law, projecting all the forces and accelerations onto the selected axes. To solve the resulting equations, it is necessary to add formulas to them that reflect the patterns that are subject to the forces acting on the bodies. For example, together with gravity forces we substitute $mg$ ($m$ - body mass, $g$ - gravitational acceleration), together with elastic forces - $kx$ ($k$ - rigidity, $x$ - elastic deformation values), sliding friction forces - $\mu N$ ($\mu$ - friction coefficient, $N$ - normal reaction force). Even at the stage of drawing up a drawing, we rely on directions to determine the direction of forces: the force of gravity is always directed downward, the force of sliding friction is against the relative speed of the body and the surface, etc.

However, not all forces have their own laws. Thus, we are able to determine the force of a normal reaction or the tension force of the lower leg only thanks to the restrictions that they impose on the movement of the body. The reaction force, for example, arises of exactly such a magnitude as to ensure the movement of the body exactly along the surface.

The force of static friction, known to you, has similar properties. The result for determining this force looks something like this: the static friction force always has such magnitudes and direction as to ensure that the body remains at rest relative to the surface on which it can move. This power sometimes causes us great trouble. The first difficulties arise when depicting this force in a drawing. Only one thing is known about its direction - it is directed tangentially to the surface. But in which direction? It's not always clear. In addition, when solving problems, it is necessary to check that the resulting value of the friction force lies within acceptable limits ($0 \leqslant F_{\text{tp}} \leqslant \mu N$); otherwise, slippage begins. And lastly: the force of static friction sometimes acts in a column in an unfamiliar area (for example, in the form of the traction force of a train or car), which is sometimes difficult to even recognize.
