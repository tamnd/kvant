The second task was more difficult. It was necessary to determine the temperature dependence of the reverse current of a low-power germanium diode (D9V diode) in the range of 20–70 °C. Equipment: 4.5 V battery, 100 µA microammeter, capacitor (its capacity is from 3.3 to 4.7 µF), a vessel with hot water, a thermometer, a thread with a weight for making a magnet, graph paper. In the conditions of the problem it was said that the reverse current is small and varies in the range of reverse voltages from 1 to 4.5 V. Also in the conditions there was a warning that water, although not a very good one, is still a conductor. The difficulty was that at low temperatures the reverse current of the diode is only 2-3 μA and it was impossible to change it without a microammeter (the accuracy of which is 4% - a standard school device). A possible option is to contaminate the capacitor with reverse current for a given period of time (this is where the magnet is prepared), and then observe the drop of the microammeter needle when a charged capacitor is connected to it. At other temperatures, it was possible to change the charging time to obtain the same needle throw, without the need to check the proportionality of the throw and the flowing charge. However, it is possible to establish the dependence of the needle drop on the capacitor charge when the charging time changes. To simplify the calculations, it is important to indicate that the reverse current is constant at a given temperature. At a temperature of 50-70 °C, the current can easily be changed in an unrepresented way. The parts of the graph obtained in two different ways can be “counted” taking into account the fact that they smoothly transition into one another. It must be said that few students have completed this task completely. Most placed what they could, simply with a microammeter and kept the capacitor, thread and weight intact (the jury did not award points for saving materials and devices!).

In the first task for 11th grade, participants were asked to change the temperature of their palms using a large vessel with water at room temperature (this temperature was known), a small flask with a long thin pot (the volume of the flask up to the mark on the pot was specified) and graph paper. One of the possible ways: lower the flask, heated in your palms, upside down into water, and then let it cool and change the amount of water that goes into the pot. These changes required a great deal of accuracy and literacy. Note that with such simple equipment it is possible to change small temperature differences quite accurately.

The second task was more difficult - it was necessary to change the capacitance of the capacitor using the following equipment: an alternating voltage source, the frequency of which was not known to the sections, a millimeter of alternating current at 5 mA (detector system), a capacitor of known capacitance, a rod made of materials with high magnetic permeability (ferrite antenna for a radio receiver), a thin wire in insulation for winding the coil. the alternating voltage source had a fairly large internal resistance (which the sections were aware of); The millimeter resistance was also quite high (and for a detector system device, this resistance strongly depends on the current flowing through it - many students did not know about this). Because of this, it was impossible to take advantage of direct changes in currents in the circuit with capacitors.
