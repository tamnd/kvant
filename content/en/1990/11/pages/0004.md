Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
D. FUCHS

Many mathematical works are difficult for schoolchildren for purely technical reasons: to understand them you need to know certain things. The difficulties that a student will encounter when reading this article are of a different kind: the style of work itself will seem unusual to him. The content of the mathematical result will be obtained as a result of pedagogical thinking through things in which, at first glance, there is nothing unclear. This style is characteristic of much 20th-century mathematics and is worth getting to know.

Hilbert's problems

In the last year of the 19th century, the rather young, but already spring, famous German mathematician David Hilbert gave a presentation at the next (second) world mathematical congress, which took place that year in Paris. The report was unusual in form. It did not include new theorems and did not offer solutions to any problems. On the contrary, it contained the formulations of twenty problems, the solution of which, according to the speaker’s plan, was to become the main stimulus for the development of mathematics in the new, 20th century. And it became one. Hilbert's problems became the most prestigious problems in mathematics; As a rule, the solution to Hilbert's problem led to the creation of a new field of mathematics, and modern mathematics largely consists of the fields that arose in this way. This can be seen by looking at the impressive two-volume book “The Development of Mathematics Growing Out of Hilbert’s Problems,” published in 1976 by America.

Rican Mathematical Society.

Not all of Hilbert's problems are original. Thus, the first gap is the “continuum problem” (is there a set of cardinality intermediate between countable and continual?), which belonged to Georg Cantor and dates back to 1878. Hilbert's problems belong to different modern mathematical disciplines, but they have one thing in common: they are specific, as a rule, each contains a question that can be answered “yes” or “no”. For example: “Is the number $2^{\sqrt{2}}$ transcendental?” (7th space) or “Can an equation of the 6th degree define on a plane a curve consisting of 11 ovals lying outside each other?” (half of the 16th problem), etc.

Almost all of Hilbert's problems have now been solved, although some things have not been done. (For example, a specific question about solving a 7th degree equation, which is part of the 13th problem, did not receive an answer; however, in a certain theoretical sense, the 13th Hilbert problem was solved in the student work of V. I. Arnold.)

Different Hilbert problems required different times to be solved. Thus, the “continuum problem” was (in a certain sense) solved by P. Cohen in 1963, the 7th gap - by A. O. Gelfond in 1934, the above-mentioned part of the 16th problem - by D. A. Gudkov in 1969. But which of Hilbert's problems was solved the best? The only one of Hilbert's problems related to classical geometry was solved before anyone else - the 3rd problem, which deals with equalities.
