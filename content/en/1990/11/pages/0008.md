(1) any pair $(l, \varphi)$ can be replaced by two pairs $(l', \varphi), (l'', \varphi)$, where $l' + l'' = l$;
(2) on the contrary, if the set contains pairs $(l', \varphi), (l'', \varphi)$ with identical second terms (recall that the identity of the second terms is a concept up to the term $2k\pi$), then we can replace them with one pair $(l, \varphi)$, where $l' + l'' = l$;
(3) any pair $(l, \varphi)$ can be replaced by two pairs $(l, \varphi'), (l, \varphi'')$, where $\varphi' + \varphi'' = \varphi$ (and this equality is the concept “modulo $2\pi$”, i.e. its left and right sides can differ by a term of the form $2k\pi$);
(4) conversely, if the set contains pairs $(l, \varphi'), (l, \varphi'')$ with the same first terms, then they can be replaced by a single pair $(l, \varphi)$ with $\varphi = \varphi' + \varphi''$;
(5) the pair $(l, \varphi)$, in which $l = 0$ or $\varphi = 0$, can simply be discarded from the set.
If two sets are such that one can be obtained from the other by sequential application of several valid operations, then we will say that these sets are equivalent, or similar.
Thus, the Dehn invariant of the polyorganic $S$, by definition, is the set $(l_1, \varphi_1), \dots, (l_n, \varphi_N)$ described above, considered up to similarity.
We will prove two things:
Theorem 2. If two polyorganics are equivalent, then their Dehn invariants are the same (that is, the corresponding sets are similar).
Theorem 3. The Dehn invariants of a cube and a regular tetrahedron are different (that is, the corresponding sets are similar).
It follows from these two theorems.
Dehn's theorem. A cube and a regular tetrahedron are not equal.
(Note that Theorem 3 is valid regardless of whether the volumes of the cube and tetrahedron are equal or unequal, although only the case of equal volumes is important for us.)
Before proving the theorems, we formulate two lemmas about sets; We will prove the first of these lemmas right there; the second is a separate matter.

Something about the similarity of sets
Lemma 1. If the number $\varphi$ is commensurable with $\pi$, then for any l the pair $(l, \varphi)$ can be eliminated from any set, and the new set will be similar to the original one.
Lemma 2. If $l \neq 0$ and $\varphi$ are not commensurate with $\pi$, then the set consisting of one pair $(l, \varphi)$ is not similar to the empty set.
Proof of Lemma 1.
Let $\varphi = \frac{p}{q}\pi$. The pair $(l, \varphi) = (l, \frac{p}{q}\pi)$ can be replaced by a set of 2q pairs $(\frac{1}{2q}l, \varphi)$ (because $l = \frac{1}{2q}l + \dots + \frac{1}{2q}l$, 2q terms), and these 2q pairs can be replaced by a single pair $(\frac{1}{2q}l, 2q\varphi)$ (because $\varphi + \dots + \varphi = 2q\varphi$, 2q terms).
No $2q\varphi = 2q \cdot \frac{p}{q}\pi = 2p\pi$, and according to our rules, together 2pπ can be written 0.
The pair $(\frac{1}{2q}l, 0)$ can be excluded from the set. The lemma is proven.
