$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{pS}{d} \left( \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2} \right)} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{p}{d} \left( \frac{1}{\sigma_1} + \frac{1}{\sigma_2} \right)}.$$

At $f \lesssim \frac{1}{2} f_0$, a double-confinement soundproofing system is approximately equivalent to a single-layer restraint with an area density of $\sigma = \sigma_1 + \sigma_2$. In the frequency range equal to or close to the value $f_0$, sound insulation is even worse than with such a single-layer limitation, and only at $f \gtrsim 2f_0$ can one count on a significant improvement. Let us estimate the value of the resonant frequency for double glazing made of 6 mm glasses with a gap of 200 mm. Substituting $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 \approx 15$ kg/$\mathrm{m}^2$ and atomspheric pressure $p \approx 10^5$ N/$\mathrm{m}^2$, we get $f_0 \approx 41$ Hz...

I began to describe the appearance of my unexpected visitor. Holmes listened, standing at the window and looking at the house opposite, and only once interrupted me, asking if the uninvited wizzer had a large mole on his left cheek. Having received an affirmative answer, Holmes seemed to have lost interest in the conversation, soon got ready and left, running off the day before. I waited for him with impatience, realizing that there was an interesting story ahead. And so it happened.

“Inspector Lestrade warned me the day before,” Holmes began, sitting comfortably in a chair by the window, “that a very clever thief, John Bertron, who had escaped from prison, had appeared in London. At one time, he carefully studied your note on Sherlock Holmes and, being a naturally observant and communicative boy, could quite legitimately be considered my student if he had not used the inductive method for unworthy purposes. In your house lives a small but very skilled jeweler, a truly careful person who, not trusting locks and safes, hides his products in various secret places in his rooms. Recently he received an order from the government itself, and the newspapers wrote about it. Bertron decided to break into the jeweler's house and steal the message to decorate the jewel. But how to identify hiding places? He noticed that the jeweler sometimes forgets to protect the windows, and they are clearly visible from your house, especially in the evening when the lights are on in the rooms. Under the guise of a glazier, he came to you and, watching from the window, saw where the jewelry was hidden at night. When you inquired about his coordinates, he reported the address of the glass workshop that was visible in the newspaper. Without the necessary qualifications, he could not give you practical advice. This is what aroused my suspicions...
