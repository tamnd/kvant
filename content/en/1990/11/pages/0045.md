It is known that of all possible states of a system, the one in which its potential energy is minimal is stable. In particular, the surface of the liquid tends to take a shape in which its surface energy under given conditions will be minimal. So, for one drop under conditions where the force of gravity can be neglected, the energy form. For two or more drops touching each other, it is more advantageous to merge together - the surface of one large ball is smaller than the total surface of several small balls with the same total mass (check this yourself), and, therefore, the surface energy of one large drop will be less.

Now we can return to the question posed at the very beginning: what determines the service life of the drops? Scientists began to think about this question quite a long time ago. Moreover, it is not separate at all, but, as it turns out, has great practical significance. In particular, to understand the physical processes occurring in powder metallurgy, where pressed metal grains are “sintered” during heat treatment into substances with unique properties. In 1944, the remarkable Soviet physicist Ya. I. Frenkel proposed the simplest model of this phenomenon, resulting in his work, which laid the physical foundations of powder metallurgy. The main idea of ​​this work allows us to estimate the service life of liquid droplets. The easiest way to do this is to use energy considerations.

Let two identical drops come into contact at each moment. At the point of contact, an isthmus is formed (see figure), which begins to gradually grow and grows until the fusion is completed. What happens at the energy energy point?

In total, the “asset” of a system of two drops has an excess energy $\Delta E_{\pi}$, equal to the difference between the surface energies of the initial and final states, i.e., the difference in the energies of two individual drops of radius $r_0$ each and one common drop of radius $r$:

$$\Delta E_{\pi} = 8\pi r_0^2 - 4\pi r^2.$$

Since when droplets merge, their total volume does not change, the equality is true

$$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 2\frac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3,$$

where do we get it from

$$r = r_0^3\sqrt{2}.$$

Thus,

$$\Delta E_{\pi} = 4\pi\sigma(2 - 2^{2/3})r_0^2.$$

According to Frenkel's idea, this excess energy should be spent on working against the forces of friction that arises in the process of moving the substance of the drops when they merge. Let's try to estimate the magnitude of this work.

To determine the force of friction, we use the expression found in the middle of the last century by the English scientist J. Stokes for a ball with radius $R$ moving in a liquid with a speed $v$:

$$F = 6\pi\eta Rv.$$

The dimensional coefficient $\eta$ included in this formula (now called the formula or Stokes law) is called the droplet coefficient, which is the simplest way to call it. It characterizes the ability of a liquid to slow down relative to the movement of neighboring layers.
