What's the problem?

Two polytopes are called equidistant if one of them can be cut into (multi-boundary) parts from which the other can be composed. The concept of equistasis can be interpreted broadly (and we will do so): polytopes $S'$ and $S''$ are called equistasis if there exist polytopes $P_1, \dots, P_n$ with the following property: polytopes $S'', P_1', \dots, P_n$ can be cut into polyboundary parts, from which polytopes $S''', P_1', \dots, P_n$ can be composed.

It is obvious that equal-shape polyhedra have identical volumes. Hilbert's 3rd problem asks whether the converse is true, i.e., whether any two polytopes of identical volumes are equidistant.

Dehn showed, and we will look at his proof here, that this is not true, i.e., that polytopes of equal volumes can be (and in fact, as a rule, are) not equistatic.

It must be said that Hilbert’s 3rd problema has a specific formulation: it is not about an arbitrary pair of polyhedra of the same volume, but about two triangular pairs with identical (in shape and size) bases and equal (in length) heights. It can be shown, however, that if the pyramids were also always equidistant, then any two polytopes of equal volumes would be equidistant (see Exercise 1 below). In view of this, to test Hilbert’s hypothesis, it is enough to establish the inequality of any two polytopes with the same volumes. And we will consider a cube and an equal tetrad as such a pair.

Hilbert's talk contains a brief indication of the source of the problem: it will naturally arise when thinking through the concept of volume. Let us assume that the volume of some figure, let it be a polyhedron, is equal to $a$ cubic units. By definition, this means that our polyhedron has the same volume as the beam $1 \times 1 \times a$. What does this mean? A definition suggests itself: polygons have the same volumes if they are equidistant (especially since, as we will see, such a definition is fully suitable for the areas of polygons). However, Archimedes already experienced the inconvenience of such a definition when proving the well-known formula $\frac{1}{3}Sh$ for the volume of a pyramid. Since it was not possible to prove the equivalence of the pyramid and the beam (and we will see that it does not exist), Archimedes had to resort to another argument. In modern language, he showed that for any $\varepsilon > 0$ one can write a polyhedron of volume greater than $\frac{1}{3}Sh - \varepsilon$ into a pyramid and describe a polyhedron around it
