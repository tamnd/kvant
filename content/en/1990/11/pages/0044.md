How long does it take for the drops to merge?

Have you ever sat down to a bowl of delicious chicken broth covered in flecks of golden fat? If you didn’t have an appetite, then you probably rolled these spots around the plate, tearing apart the partitions between them or, conversely, connecting the spots together, watching how slowly they merge, taking the shape of a circle.

Another observation from the same series is the merging of droplets of rue from a broken thermometer (be careful! This is an unsafe activity, since rue is very poisonous). True, here you don’t even have time to blink your eye before two droplets form one.

What determines the time of merging of liquid droplets?

Before trying to answer this question, let's talk a little about the reason for the merging of drops - about the damaging tension of the liquid. Moreover, we will try to look at this phenomenon from an energy point of view.

Molecules located in a thin layer of liquid near the damage are in special conditions. The fact is that they have neighbors identical to them only on one side of the damage, in contrast to molecules inside a liquid, surrounded on all sides by their own kind. The interaction of molecules over very short distances is of the nature of attraction. If the potential energy of attraction of two molecules located at an infinitely large distance from each other is considered equal to zero, then at smaller distances this energy will be negative. In terms of the absolute value, to a first approximation, the energy of each molecule can be considered proportional to the number of nearest neighbors. Therefore, it is clear that the potential energy of molecules in the damaged layer (the number of neighbors for which is less than in the volume) is higher than that of molecules inside the liquid. (Another factor in increasing the potential energy of molecules in the damaged layer is that as you approach the damage, the concentration of molecules decreases.)

Of course, the molecules of a liquid are not stationary, but are in continuous thermal motion - one molecule leaves the damage, others, on the contrary, fall on it. But even in this case, we can talk about some average additional potential energy of the damaged liquid layer. This means that in order to remove molecules from damage, external forces need to do some positive work. The excess potential energy of molecules located in a damaged area of ​​a single area, compared to the potential energy that the same molecules would possess in the thickness of the liquid, is called the damage tension coefficient σ. It is characterized by the work that must be spent on increasing the free damage of the liquid per unit area. Of course, such a definition of σ is completely equivalent to the well-known definition of the damage tension coefficient as the force acting per unit length of the fluid boundaries.
