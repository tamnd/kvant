If the median AD divides angle A of triangle ABC into parts $\angle BAD = \alpha$, $\angle CAD = 2\alpha$ (see figure), then, equating the expressions for the planes of triangles BAD and CAD

$$\frac{1}{2}AB \cdot AD \cdot \sin \alpha = \frac{1}{2}AC \cdot AD \cdot \sin 2\alpha$$

we get that cos $\alpha = AB/2AC$. This formula allows us to construct the angle $\alpha$, and with it the triangle ABC, using the given sides AB and AC. From the obvious condition $0 < \alpha < \pi/3$ it follows that the problem has a solution (and, moreover, a unique one) at $1 < AB/AC < 2$.

We also present the number of the geometric solution. If $E$ is a point symmetric to $C$ with respect to the median AD, then, as is easy to see, $BE \parallel AD$ (see figure), therefore, $\angle EBA = \angle DAB = \angle EAB$, i.e. triangle ABE is equilateral, and $BE = EA = AC$. M1222. Let $m > 1$ be a natural number, $s$ the largest integer for which $2^s \leq m$. Prove that a) for any $s + 1$ integers, you can choose several numbers and arrange the signs of the plane and the minute of the thread so that the resulting sum is divisible by $m$;
b) the estimate in part a) is unimprovable: there are also s integers such that no sum of several of them, with any arrangement of signs, is divisible by $m$.

a) Consider all possible “non-algebraic” (i.e., without minutes) sums that can be made from $s + 1$ numbers. Their number is $2^{s+1} - 1 > m - 1$. (Each sum can be specified by a sequence of length $s + 1$ of zeros and ones, in which the $i$th place is 1, if the sum includes the $i$th number; the number of sequences is $2^{s+1}$, the sequence of only zeros must be eliminated.) If some two sums give the same remainders when divided by $m$, then their difference is the desired “algebraic” amount. If all remainders are different, then they take all possible $m$ values ​​$0, 1, ..., m - 1$, and the sum giving remainder 0 is the required one.

b) Here is an example of s integers such that no algebraic sum of some of them is a multiple of $m$: 1, 2, 4, ..., $2^{s-1}$. Indeed, any such sum according to the model does not exceed $1 + 2 + 4 + ... + 2^{s-1} \leq m - 1$. On the other hand, these sums cannot be equal to 0: if $2^t$ is the smallest term of such a sum, then the entire sum will be divisible by $2^t$, but will not be divisible by $2^{t+1}$.

M1224. A segment is drawn from the vertex of the triangle to a point on the opposite side, dividing the inscribed circle into three equal parts. can this segment be $a$) high; $b$) median; $c$) bisectis of a triangle?

Answer: a), c) cannot, b) can.

Let us introduce the notation as for figure. Note that if the lengths of the two extreme parts of the drawn segment $AD$ are equal to $(AN=DM)$, then the triangle $ABD$ is obviously equilateral to $(AB=BD)$. Consequently, $AD$ cannot be either the height of $(\angle ADB<90^\circ)$ or the bisector of $(\angle CAD<\angle ADB=\angle BAD)$, and it can only be a median when $a=2c$, where $a=BC$, $c=AB$.

Let now condition $a=2c$ be satisfied. Let us show that for some $b=AC$ the incircle

will divide the median $AD=m$ into equal parts. We will need the following three well known and very useful statements:

1) $m^2=(2b^2+2c^2-a^2)/4$;
2) $AE=(b+c-a)/2$,

where $E$ is the point of tangency of the inscribed circle with side $AB$;
3) $AE^2=AN \cdot AM$

(this is true for any ANM secant).

Substituting $a=2c$ into equalities 1) and 2) and into 3) the value $AE$ from 2), $AN=m/3$, $AM=2m/3$ and $m^2$ from 1), we obtain the condition

$$AE^2=\left(\frac{b-c}{2}\right)^2=\frac{2}{9} m^2=\frac{2}{9} \frac{b^2-c^2}{2},$$

or $9(b-c)=4(b+c)$ (reduction by $b-c$ is possible since $a=2c<b+c$, i.e. $b-c>0$). Hence $b=13c/5$. Since $2c-c<13c/5<2c+c$, a triangle with sides $2c$, $13c/5$, $c$ exists. M1225*. Prove that a) if for natural numbers a and b the number $(a^2+b^2)/(ab-1)$ is a natural number, then it is equal to 5;
b) the equation $x^2-5xy+y^2+5=0$ has an infinite number of solutions in natural numbers.

The following basic idea can be used to prove both statements. Equation
$$x^2-qxy+y^2+q=0$$
for fixed $q$ and $y$, the square relative $x$. If for integer $q$ and $y$ it has one whole root $x$, then it must also have one more whole root (according to Vieta’s theorem, equal to $qy-x$). A similar idea has been encountered more than once in problems about equations in integers.

a) We must prove that if equation (1) has a solution $(x,y)$ in positive integers, then $q=5$. Let us immediately note that for $q=1$ and $q=2$ there are very few such solutions:
$$x^2-xy+y^2+1=(x-y)^2+xy+1>1,$$
$$x^2-2xy+y^2+2=(x-y)^2+2>1.$$
Further, for $q>2$ there are no integer solutions such that $x=y$ (if $x^2(2-q)+q=0$, then $x^2=q/(q-2)=1+2/(q-2)$, and this number does not exceed 3 and is greater than 1). Finally, then, we consider equation (1) as a square relative $x$ for integer $q>2$ and $y>0$ cannot have multiple roots. Indeed, if its discriminant is equal to 0, that is, if $y^2q^2-4(y^2+q)=0$, then $y^2=4q/(q^2-4)$, and this number at $q>2$ does not exceed 12/5 and is not equal to 1, since the equation $q^2-4q-4=0$ has no integer roots.

Now let's remember the main idea: from each solution $(x,y)$ we can get another solution with the same $y$. Among all solutions $(x,y)$ of equation (1) with a given $q$ for which $x>y$, we consider the solutions with the most. $y$. Let these be solutions $(v,y)$ and $(w,y)$. Then $y<v<w$, so $v \geqslant y+1$, $w \geqslant y+2$.

By Vieta's theorem, since $x=v$ and $x=w$ are roots of equation (1), $vw=y^2$, $v+w=qy$. That's why
$$y^2+q-qy=vw-v-w=(v-1)(w-1)-1 \geqslant y(y+1)-1=y^2+y-1.$$
whence $1-y+q-qy \geqslant 0$, or $(1-y)(1+q) \geqslant 0$. But this is only possible if $y=1$ and all inequalities turn into equalities, i.e. $v=2$, $w=3$, $q=5$.

The last reasoning can be replaced by a more visual one if we use the geometric interpretation of equation (1). Each of them (at $q=3$, 4, 5, 6, ...) on the plane $Oxy$ defines a hyperbola, symmetrically relative to the line $x=y$, the vertex of which lies on the segment $1 \leqslant x \leqslant 2$ of this line. If a straight line parallel to one of the axes intersects the hyperbola at an “integer” point, then its second point of intersection with the hyperbola is integer. Using this, it is easy to show that if a hyperbola contains at least one whole point, then there is such a point near the vertex - in the area located below (still close to the $Ox$ axis). So the matter comes down to just a solution with $y=1$, which is already easy to figure out.

6) So, it remains to consider the equation with $q=5$:
$$x^2-5xy+y^2+5=0.$$

F1233. Motorcycle races will take place on a narrow circular track. When starting from a stop, the motorcyclist strives to constantly change the speed. How much of the cruise will he have covered by the time he reaches maximum speed?

Let's say right away that for fast acceleration you need to use the friction force as best as possible.
Let $\alpha$ be the angle between the speed of the motorcycle $\vec{v}$ and the force of friction on the ground $\vec{F}$ at some point in time (see figure). Let us write down the equations of motion of a motorcycle in projections onto the directions of the tangent and normal to the trajectory:
$$mv' = F \cos \alpha, mv^2/R = F \sin \alpha.$$
Differentiating the second equation with respect to time, taking into account the first we obtain
$$2v/R = \alpha'$$
or (since $v/R = \omega = \varphi'$)
$$2\varphi' = \alpha'.$$
From here
$$2\varphi = \alpha + \text{const.}$$
At $\varphi = 0$ (starting point) $\alpha = 0$, so const = 0 and
$$2\varphi = \alpha.$$
At the moment of reaching maximum speed
$\alpha = \pi/2$ and $\varphi = \pi/4$,
which corresponds to 1/8 of a circle.

Problems with infinite circuits of resistors are widely known, but in our case everything is more complicated - after all, the further the light bulb is from the source, the less it is accumulated, which means its resistance is less. Nevertheless, with reasonable procedures, this problem can be solved without going beyond the scope of the school curriculum.

Firstly, this circuit can still be considered infinite (make an assessment yourself - just consider the decrease in the resistance of “cold” lamps).
