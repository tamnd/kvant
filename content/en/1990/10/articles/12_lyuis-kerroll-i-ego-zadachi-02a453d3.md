Epigraph to Lewis Carroll's "The Knot Story"

Some readers of "Alice in Wonderland" and "Through the Looking Glass" mistakenly believe that they know everything, absolutely everything there is to know about Wonderland, Through the Looking Glass and their creator Lewis Carroll. Anyone who thinks this way is deeply mistaken. Many interesting and profound things remain hidden from a hasty and superficial glance.

Take the tasks, for example. Lewis Carroll came up with a great variety of them during his life: arithmetic, geometric, logical, physical, linguistic, etc. He even approached the choice of his pseudonym as a problem, which he solved with figure brilliance and invention: he took his signature names (Charles Lutwidge), “translated” into Latin (it turned out Carolus Ludovicus), rearranged it (it turned out Ludovicus Carolus) and, finally, “translated” again into native English (it turned out to be Lewis Carroll).

Carroll loves to play tricks on the reader by asking seemingly simple but tricky questions. There are still ongoing debates about where the load will go in the “monkey problem” (the monkey begins to climb the free end of a rope thrown over a block, to the other end of which is attached a load exactly equal to the weight of the monkey; will the load rise, fall, or remain in place?), or whether Archimedes’ law is wrong for a bucket floating inside another bucket if there is water in that bucket at the bottom.

Many different tasks are skillfully woven into the fabric of the narrative of “Alice” and “Through the Looking Glass”. Only some of them are transcribed in Martin Gardner's comments. Most are waiting for their discoverer. Only with the emergence of structural linguistics, semantics, semiotics and other new sciences were some of Carroll’s “quirks” (as it once seemed) appreciated, for example,

his linguistic experiments. Let us at least remember the famous “Varmaglot”:
“Varmagism. Squishy little blinkers...

Yu. Danilov
