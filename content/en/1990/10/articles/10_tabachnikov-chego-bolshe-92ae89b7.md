Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. TAVACHNIKOV

My daughter Masha is seven and a half years old and is in second grade. Masha loves to talk about mathematics. Usually, she and I solve problems like those published in «Kvant» for younger schoolchildren.” A few days ago, she and I had a rather serious conversation. We went to a sports school (Masha plays tennis), and she told me that they were studying the Russian language.
- Guess what is more: words or endings? - asked Masha.
“Of course, words, because different words have the same endings,” I answered (let me remind you that words also have “empty” endings).
And then it seemed to me that it was time to talk about mathematics.

- Now tell me, what is more: even numbers or odd ones?
“Odd,” Masha answered immediately (she always answers right away, although not always correctly). “Because the first number, one, is odd (for Masha the numbers are 1, 2, 3, 4,... She has long known that there is no largest number - after all, you can always increase the number by 1).
- How many more odd numbers? - I asked.
- Of course, for one thing.
- So if you remove this unit, it will become equal? - I didn’t lag behind. Then Masha thought about it.
- No, if you remove one, the first number will be two, and it is even. Now there are four more. Strange...
“Nothing strange,” I said, “because there are equal numbers of four and odd numbers.” Let's build them in pairs - like you did in kindergarten. The odd ones are boys and the even ones are girls. Like this: 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, 7-8,... Now do you see that there are equal numbers of them?
“Now I’ll survive,” said Masha.
- Then tell me, what is more: all numbers or even ones?
There could be no doubt about it:
- Of course, there are more numbers.
- But no, they are also equally divided. Only the numbers will be different: 1-2, 2-4, 3-6, 4-8, 5-10,... Got it?
“I see,” she said. To check, I asked what number is in the palais with 98. This task was not very simple: Masha is not very good at multiplying and dividing by 2 “in her head.”
“Like this: there are an infinite number of four numbers, and all numbers, but they are equal,” I summed up the conversation. But it turns out our conversation was just beginning.
- Will it always be like this? - asked Masha.
“No,” I answered, “for example, there are more points on a segment than (natural) numbers.”
- Why?
- Well, this is very difficult, and you will suffer harm...

- What if I understand?
And I decided to try.
“The reasoning consists of two parts,” I began, “first you need to give names to all the points of the segment.”
— Will there be a point called Masha?
- No, each name will use only two letters.
“Let it be the letters “I” and “L,” asked Masha. “These are my favorite letters.”
“Okay,” I agree. Each point will have an infinitely long name, made up of the letters “I” and “L”.
For example,

IIIIIIIIII... or
ILILILILI...

And some points will even have not one, but two names.
— Like Christopher Robin in Winnie the Pooh?
- Yes, something like that.
- How do you find out the name of each point?
“Very simple,” I answered. “You have a point.” Divide the segment in half. The point will fall either on the left or on the right half. If the point is in the left segment, then the first letter of its name is “L”, and if it is in the right segment, then “I”. Then we will again divide the one of the two segments in which the point lies in half. And again let's see in which half the point lies: on the left or on the right. If on the left, then the second letter of her name is “L”, and if on the right, then “I”. And so on...
— What do you mean “and so on”?
- So we divide the next segment in half again and find out the third letter of the name of our point, then the fourth, then the fifth... Got it?
“Not really,” answered Masha. “What if the point falls exactly in the middle of some segment?”
— These points will have two names. The middle of a segment lies both in its left half and in its right half. If we assume that the middle lies in the left half, then the next letter of her name will be “L”. But then this point all the time

- Why do dots need names? - asked Masha.
“We’ll talk about this on the way back,” we were already approaching the sports school. “For now, let’s summarize: each point has one or two names made up of the letters I and L, and vice versa - each such name is answered by some point on the segment.”
... So why do dots need names? - it turns out that Masha did not cancel our conversation.
— To explain why there are more points than (natural) numbers. Imagine that the points of a segment can be re-ordered. Some point will be the first, some will be the second, ..., some will be the hundredth, and so on.
“I see,” said Masha. She already knew what “and so on” meant.
- Let's take a large, large sheet of paper and begin to write down the names of all the points - one name per line. First - the name of the first point. Then the second name of the first point, if it has one, or the name of the second point. Then - the third, fourth and so on. And now you are a même! Now I will create the name of the point, which is not in this table. Let's take the first letter of the first name, the second letter of the third, and so on, and make up a new name from them (at figure these letters are red). Now we place all the letters: “I” - on “L”, and “L” - on “I”. You will get a new name (at figure it is blue). Do you think this name is in our table?
— I don’t know, because the table is so big...
— Maybe it’s in the first line?
Masha thought:
- No, because it has a different letter.
- And in the second?
- No, either, because it has a different second letter. Everything is clear! And in the third - he cannot, and in the fourth... He is not at all in our table of names.
“That’s right,” I confirmed, “this name is not in the table.” This means that the point that bears this name should have been left without a number. Therefore, the point on the segment is greater than (natural) numbers!
We talked for a few more minutes. I told Masha about the German mathematician Georg Cantor, who came up with this whole example 100 years ago. And that, obviously, this is not taught to second-grade students, but to students of university mathematics departments. I said that, in my opinion, this is a very beautiful reasoning. Masha did not agree with this:
— What can be beautiful is a hairstyle or a dress, not mathematical reasoning.
But I think that's where she's wrong.
