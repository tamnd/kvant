Ant and...electronic microcoppe

Here is a close-up portrait of a common black ant. This ant was placed in a chamber of one of the most modern installations for materials research - a scanning electron microcopper with field emission electrons (manufactured by Hitachi, Japan).

How does the device that bears the complex name work?

The main difference between an electronic microcoppa and an optical one is that instead of light rays, it uses electron beams controlled by electronic and magnetic fields. This “replacement” allows one to increase the resolution limits of the device from approximately 4000 to several angstroms. For example, that 1 Å is the diameter of a hydrogen atom;

Thus, researchers today, studying the relief and chemical composition of the surface of materials, are already working with a countable number of atoms.

Rasta electron microcoppers operate on the principle of scanning, i.e. sequential, from point to point, movement of a thin electron beam over the object under study. It is clear that the resolution of such a device is determined by the minimum diameter of the electron beam. But the lower the electron energy, and this is necessary to reduce the destruction of the surface of materials, the more difficult it is to focus such a beam, which means the less detailed the picture of the microworld will be.

Difficulties were overcome thanks to the use in the electron beam of a raster electron microcopper not of an incandescent catalog, but of a so-called field emission catalog, made in the form of a tip, from the surface of which electrons are drawn out by an ultra-strong electronic field. This makes it possible to sharply, not by orders of magnitude, increase the brightness of an electronic projector, which means that the disadvantages inherent in working with low-energy electrons are practically eliminated.

Rasta electronic microcoppers began their triumph in sixty laboratories around the world about thirty years ago. Segdia is one of the main working tools of a researcher in the field of materials, microelectronics, biological medicine, etc.
