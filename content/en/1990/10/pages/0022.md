On the eve of the war

What happened in Goettigen was repeated in many scientific centers in Germany. For example, in Frankfurt, where in 1935 the seminar at which (in the original language!) studied mathematical works of different eras - from Euclid and Archimedes to Fermat and Barrow - ceased to exist. The soul of the seminar was Max Dehn (famous for solving Hilbert's third problem: a regular terraed cannot be cut into pieces from which one can form a cube). Den was arrested in 1938, but was soon released due to prison overcrowding. He managed to escape to Norway, where in 1940 he was caught by the German occupation. In 1941, Den found himself in America after a long and dangerous journey through Finland, the USSR and Japan.

In 1938, the Nazis moved to the “final solution to the Jewish question,” that is, to the mass physical education of Jews. Most of the mathematicians who were in danger had already left Germany, but there were also those who did not want or were unable to leave. A participant in the Frankfurt seminar, P. Epstein, committed suicide in 1939 after receiving a summons from the Gestaplo. F. Hausdorff also committed suicide to avoid deportation to a concentration camp. His fate was shared by his wife and her sister. One of the four mathematicians who headed the German Mathematical Union until 1933, O. Bliomental was arrested in occupied Holland and died in the Terezin concentration camp.

Germany has lost many of its mathematicians. Among the refugees there were not only those who feared persecution for reasons of convenience (for example, the famous logician K. Gödel or a participant in the Frankfurt seminar K. Epstein was any test for Aryan origin). Bot several names

mathematicians who left Germany:

E. Artin, G. Weil, K. Gödel, M. Dehn, K. Siegel, R. Courant, G. Levy, R. von Mises, O. Neugebauer, E. Noether, G. Rademeister, I. Schur. To this list we can add the German mathematicians who worked for a long time in Germany: J. von Neumann, J. Polya, G. Seret.

German mathematical traditions were undermined. Of course, mathematics can be developed without taking into account Jews (as well as any relatively small group: for example, fish-bearing animals or, say, those whose name begins with one of the letters of the alphabet). However, such an unfair restriction will inevitably destroy the structure of science that has developed under normal conditions. And create an environment that is unacceptable to many of those who are not directly affected by these restrictions. At the same time, among outstanding mathematicians there are many people with strong national prejudices. This, alas, once again confirms the thesis about the compatibility of “genius and action.”

I will end with the words of K. Siegel: “Let’s hope that what the deluded fanatics did to the worthy and pure people back then will not be repeated in any way.”
