M1225*. Prove that a) if for natural numbers a and b the number $(a^2+b^2)/(ab-1)$ is a natural number, then it is equal to 5;
b) the equation $x^2-5xy+y^2+5=0$ has an infinite number of solutions in natural numbers.

The following basic idea can be used to prove both statements. Equation
$$x^2-qxy+y^2+q=0$$
for fixed $q$ and $y$, the square relative $x$. If for integer $q$ and $y$ it has one whole root $x$, then it must also have one more whole root (according to Vieta’s theorem, equal to $qy-x$). A similar idea has been encountered more than once in problems about equations in integers.

a) We must prove that if equation (1) has a solution $(x,y)$ in positive integers, then $q=5$. Let us immediately note that for $q=1$ and $q=2$ there are very few such solutions:
$$x^2-xy+y^2+1=(x-y)^2+xy+1>1,$$
$$x^2-2xy+y^2+2=(x-y)^2+2>1.$$
Further, for $q>2$ there are no integer solutions such that $x=y$ (if $x^2(2-q)+q=0$, then $x^2=q/(q-2)=1+2/(q-2)$, and this number does not exceed 3 and is greater than 1). Finally, then, we consider equation (1) as a square relative $x$ for integer $q>2$ and $y>0$ cannot have multiple roots. Indeed, if its discriminant is equal to 0, that is, if $y^2q^2-4(y^2+q)=0$, then $y^2=4q/(q^2-4)$, and this number at $q>2$ does not exceed 12/5 and is not equal to 1, since the equation $q^2-4q-4=0$ has no integer roots.

Now let's remember the main idea: from each solution $(x,y)$ we can get another solution with the same $y$. Among all solutions $(x,y)$ of equation (1) with a given $q$ for which $x>y$, we consider the solutions with the most. $y$. Let these be solutions $(v,y)$ and $(w,y)$. Then $y<v<w$, so $v \geqslant y+1$, $w \geqslant y+2$.

By Vieta's theorem, since $x=v$ and $x=w$ are roots of equation (1), $vw=y^2$, $v+w=qy$. That's why
$$y^2+q-qy=vw-v-w=(v-1)(w-1)-1 \geqslant y(y+1)-1=y^2+y-1.$$
whence $1-y+q-qy \geqslant 0$, or $(1-y)(1+q) \geqslant 0$. But this is only possible if $y=1$ and all inequalities turn into equalities, i.e. $v=2$, $w=3$, $q=5$.

The last reasoning can be replaced by a more visual one if we use the geometric interpretation of equation (1). Each of them (at $q=3$, 4, 5, 6, ...) on the plane $Oxy$ defines a hyperbola, symmetrically relative to the line $x=y$, the vertex of which lies on the segment $1 \leqslant x \leqslant 2$ of this line. If a straight line parallel to one of the axes intersects the hyperbola at an “integer” point, then its second point of intersection with the hyperbola is integer. Using this, it is easy to show that if a hyperbola contains at least one whole point, then there is such a point near the vertex - in the area located below (still close to the $Ox$ axis). So the matter comes down to just a solution with $y=1$, which is already easy to figure out.

6) So, it remains to consider the equation with $q=5$:
$$x^2-5xy+y^2+5=0.$$
