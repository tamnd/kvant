Gross data errors (outliers)

Suppose we want to find out the average height of second-graders in Moscow. Let's survey 1000 schoolchildren at random and record their height. Then we add up the resulting numbers and divide by 1000. Of course, the guys do not give their height exactly, but we can hope that these random errors during averaging will compensate for each other. But one of the schoolchildren joked and said that his height was a kilometer. Of course, this value must be discarded, otherwise the average height of a second grader may exceed 2 meters.

The situation is similar if the computer “joked” and an abnormally large number ended up in memory as a result of a machine failure or an error when entering data. For example, an operator enters decimal numbers into the computer memory; say, the temperature of ocean water measured from a research vessel. It's hot, the ship is rocking, and the operator is having an exciting conversation with a friend... And from time to time he forgets to press the decimal key. As a result, the computer “remembers” approximately the following series of numbers:

...25,627; 26,234; 25,824; 25003;
25,226; 24,988; 24923; 25,231;...

It is clear that with further data processing, outliers will have undesirable consequences. They must be replaced with true or close to true values. In addition, the procedure for eliminating outliers should be automatic, since it usually involves huge arrays of numbers.

To combat emissions, the former American statistician J. Tyoki proposed a procedure called medical filtration. The idea of ​​any such procedure is very simple: each of the numbers in the sequence of measurements must be separated along with several numbers adjacent to it and replaced with the average value of these numbers. The trick is how to choose that average.

Mean and median

Obviously in statistics, the average value of $n$ numbers is their arithmetic mean:

$$\bar{a} = \frac{a_1 + a_2 + \ldots + a_n}{n}.$$

For example, for seven numbers 1, 2, 5, 1, 1, 2, 30, the average value is 6. It turned out to be so large because of the only “outlier” equal to 30. In those sports where the result is evaluated in beams by several judges, before calculating the average score, sometimes the two extreme scores are discarded - the worst and the worst. Let us do the same; our series of numbers is reduced to 2, 5, 1, 1, 2, and the average is reduced to 2.2. You can go further and discard two extreme values, or you can go even further, discarding three extreme values, and take the remaining number - it will be 2 - as the “average”. This “average” number is called dial-number medicine. As you can see, medians exist not only in geometry, but also in statistics.

The strict definition of median is as follows. Let $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{2k+1}$ be a set of odd numbers. Let's rearrange these numbers in ascending (non-descending) order. The median of a set is the number that is in the middle, $(k+1)$ place. We will denote this number by $m(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{2k+1})$.

The two concepts of “averages” that we discussed above—the arithmetic mean and the median—naturally arise when solving the following problems. Let a set of numbers be given; we define its “average” as a number, the sum of deviations from which to these numbers is minimal. The question is how to determine the deviation.

Exercises

1. Prove that for any numbers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n$ the minimum of the function $f(x) = (x-a_1)^2 + \ldots + (x-a_n)^2$ is achieved at $x = a = (a_1 + \ldots + a_n)/n$.

2. a) Prove that for any numbers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{2k+1}$ the minimum of the function $g(x) = |x-a_1| + \ldots + |x-a_{2k+1}|$ is achieved at $x = m(a_1, \ldots, a_{2k+1})$.
