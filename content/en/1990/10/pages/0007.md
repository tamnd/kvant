Hexagonal cells on water - an exact analogue of the image of a honeycomb - are very reminiscent of the structure formed under certain conditions in liquids as a result of thermal convection - the so-called Bénard cells. We have a new example of a self-organizing structure*). The oscillatory upward movement of the water surface leads to spontaneous cooperation and organization of the moving parts of the surface of the layer into separate cells. The formation of such cells in a plane perpendicular to the direction of motion is apparently due to the fact that the dynamic system must choose the most favorable energy state.

A theoretical description of the symmetry of the diametric structure of a scale is difficult. However, it was still possible to construct a qualitative theory that satisfactorily describes the results of experiments on modeling seaquakes. Its essence is as follows.

Gravitational waves on water obey the law of dispersion, according to which the square of the wave frequency is figuratively proportional to the wavelength (in other words, as the wave period decreases, its length decreases). The coefficient of proportional service is a constant value $g$ - the acceleration of gravity. In the case of gravity-capillary waves, a term is added to the dispersion law that takes into account surface tension and fluid density.*)

It turned out that when describing oscillatory processes in a liquid, the coefficient proportional $g$ can no longer be considered constant; in addition to $g$, it is necessary to take into account the additional acceleration created by the oscillations of the amide. Since this additional acceleration periodically changes in time, conditions arise for periodic rocking of the surface under the influence of a periodic disturbing force. Such processes are called parametric resonance.**
