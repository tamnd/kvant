To whom did the racial laws apply in Gettygne? To E. Landd, who took the chair of G. Minkowski after his unexpected death in 1909. When choosing his candidacy, an important role was played by the fact that Landdu was an independent and uncompromising person - it was precisely such employees that the university was interested in at that time! Landdu was an outstanding specialist in analytic number theory, reviving at Gettygne "an era of arithmetic comparable to the era discovered by Gauss in 1801." Colleagues and students were amused by Landd's absence and ruthless straightforwardness, but they respected his fantastic work ethic and dedication to mathematics.

Landdu's mathematical style was very abstract, and he was disdainful of any application. According to Courant, “in 20 years he would have graced the Bourbaki group,” a group of French mathematicians who created a multi-volume and extremely abstract course on modern mathematics. Landdu took an extreme position and assumed: his textbook of analysis does not contain a single drawing and is a building built - floor by floor - on a foundation formed by the axioms of natural numbers, realizing the aphorism of L. Kronecker: “The natural numbers are given from Water; everything else is the work of men.”

Teichmoller, who died in 1943 on the Eastern Front (by the way, back in 1926 the Naists made up the majority in the Gettignen student competition). Landdu retired and died in 1938.

Racial laws applied to Emmy Noether. She inherited her mathematical abilities from her father, a famous mathematician. Noether came to Goettigen during the First World War. Following the recommendations of Klein and Hilbert, she was left at the university, despite the prejudice of many members of the faculty against the woman holding the title of privatdozent (Hilbert reacted to this like this: “... the university senate is not a ban!”).

Noether was an outstanding algebraist. In the textbook of modern algebra you will find “Noether’s theorems”, “Noetherian rings”, etc. After Hilbert’s replacement work on the so-called theory of invariants - the solution was obtained not by constructing increasingly complex formulas, but with the help of new general concepts - algebra experienced a heyday. Noether was the center of the algebraic circle at Gettygne. However, in 1933 she was dismissed from the university.
