Fig. 1.

Candidate of Technical Sciences R. VINOKUR

This happened at the end of the century in the American city of Springfield in Massachusetts. The local college students loved to play baseball and football. However, due to bad weather, sports activities were often moved to the technical hall. To keep his pets from getting bored, physical education teacher James Neusmidt in 1891 came up with a game that did not require a large area, and called it “basketball” - from the English words “basket” - basket and “ball” - ball. This name very succinctly conveys the essence of the game, since no matter how often the rules of basketball were adjusted over its subsequent history, the main element of the game always remained throwing the ball into a basket: first into a natural one (from under peaches), and then into a metal hoop with a net suspended from it without a day.

In the homeland of basketball, in the USA, in addition to game battles, the accuracy of various throws is presented and tested. American Ted Martin scored 2036 free throws in a row (1977), and his compatriot Fred Newman scored “only” 88, but...

figure 1 shows the basic physics of a one-handed standing throw. Let's try to find out at what angle $\theta$ to the horizon it is advisable to release the ball from your hands in order to ensure the most accurate throw.

Let us limit ourselves to an approximate study using elementary physical and mathematical concepts. The height of the ring above the floor is $H=3,05$ m (interestingly, this is the very height at which the upper rims of the peach baskets Naismith nailed to the balcony of the college gymnasium in Springfield are located); the internal diameter of the ring is $D=0,45$ m, the diameter of a basketball is approximately half that. Let the ball enter the ring at an angle $\varphi$ to the horizon (Fig. 2), and we will assume that the trajectory of the center of the ball lies in a vertical plane passing through the center of the ring (we will not take into account the possible deviation of the ball from this plane, considering that the main reason for a miss can only be the overshoot or undershoot of the ball).

Then the conditions under which the ball will pass through the ring without setting it can be written as follows:

$$|\Delta L| \leq l = \frac{D}{2} \left(1 - \frac{1}{2 \sin \varphi}\right),$$

where $\Delta L$ is the displacement of the center of the ball ($O$) from the center of the ring ($A$). This condition makes sense at $\varphi > 30^\circ$. If $\varphi < 30^\circ$, then the ball must hit the ring and, as usually happens (especially if the speed of the ball at the moment of hitting the ring is quite high), it is reflected without hitting the basket. By adjusting the angle $\varphi$, we increase the chances of getting into the basket, since the value $l$ increases. So, if $\varphi=40^\circ$, then $l \approx 0,05$ m, and for $\varphi=60^\circ$ $l \approx 0,095$ m, i.e. almost twice as much. The maximum possible value is $l \approx 0,112$ m (at $\varphi=90^\circ$).

Obviously, the angle $\varphi$ is greater than the angle $\theta$ at which the player throws the ball into the hoop (see Fig. 1). However, when shooting at very wide angles ($\theta \gtrsim 70^\circ$), it is quite difficult to hit the basket, at least from further distances. Not only is it difficult to hit, but sometimes just getting the ball to the hoop takes a lot of effort. (If basketball players throw things at large angles, then most often it’s “not out of luck”: you need to throw the ball over the hands of a defender who is closely guarding his “own” player.) To understand the reason for this, we will conduct simple theoretical studies, supplementing them with an experiment, for which you only need a ballpoint pen or pencil, a sheet of paper, a ruler and a protractor.

Let at the moment when the basketball player releases the ball from his hands, the center of the ball is located at point $B$ (see Fig. 1) and after time $t$ reaches the center of the hoop at point $A$. The initial speed of the throw is $V$, the throw distance (projection of the segment $BA$ on the horizontal plane) is $L$. We consider a “clean” throw - without reflecting the ball from the shield. Neglecting air (and defender) resistance, we describe the movement of the ball as the movement of the materials of a point thrown at an angle of $\theta$ to the horizon:

$$L = (V \cos \theta) t,$$

$$h = L \mathrm{tg} \alpha = (V \sin \theta) t - \frac{g t^2}{2}.$$

The angle $\varphi$ at which the ball flies into the hoop is determined from the condition:

$$\mathrm{tg} \varphi = \frac{|V_y A|}{V_x A} = \frac{|V \sin \theta - g t|}{V \cos \theta},$$

where $V_x A$ and $V_y A$ are the horizontal and vertical components of the ball speed at point $A$, respectively. After simple transformations we get:

$$L = \frac{V^2}{g} \sin(2\theta - \alpha) - \sin \alpha \cos \alpha,$$

$$\varphi = \mathrm{arctg} (tg \theta - 2 tg \alpha).$$

Let us examine the resulting expressions. From (2) it is clear that under the condition $2\theta - \alpha = 90^\circ$ a given throw range is achieved with the minimum possible speed and, therefore, with minimal costs for successfully executing the throw. So the optimal angle for throwing the ball is

$$\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}} = 45^\circ + \frac{\alpha}{2}.$$

In the special case $\alpha = 0^\circ$ (points $B$ and $A$ are located on the same level) $\theta_{\text{опт}} = 45^\circ$ and, as can be seen from (3), $\varphi = \theta$. For a player with average (not “basketball”) height, the ball in the final phase of the throw is from the floor at a height of $H' \approx 2$ m. The most “profitable” in modern basketball is an accurate throw from behind the so-called six-meter line, for which three points are given (for hits from a closer distance, only two points are counted, and for free throws - one point). Polyaga $h = H - H' = 1,05$ m, $L = 6$ m, we get $\alpha = \mathrm{arctg} (1,05/6) \approx 10^\circ$, i.e. $\theta_{\text{опт}} \approx 50^\circ$. From expression (2) it is found that the initial speed of the ball at $\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}}$ in this case is 8.37 m/s; if you run at such a speed, then you can cover a hundred-meter distance in 11.9 seconds, which is not bad for an ordinary physical trainer, but is significantly inferior to the world rekord. However, unlike athletics, very high speeds are not needed here: the greater the initial speed of the ball, the higher its speed at the entrance to the hoop, and this, as already mentioned, will increase the likelihood of the ball rebounding when touching the hoop.

As for the error $\Delta \theta$ in the throwing angle, we will evaluate its influence on the values ​​$\Delta L$, assuming for simplicity that $\Delta V = 0$ (i.e., the initial throwing speed is a constant value). To estimate using error (2), neglecting the possible change in angle $\alpha$. Assuming that the error in the throwing angle is relatively small, after simple calculations we find:

$$\Delta L = L \cdot 2 \frac{\Delta \theta \cdot \cos(2\theta - \alpha) - (\Delta \theta)^2 \sin(2\theta - \alpha)}{\sin(2\theta - \alpha) - \sin \alpha}.$$

(If the reader has not bothered to get this, frankly speaking, not very elegant expression on his own, we advise you to look at the “Appendix” at the end of the article.)

From expression (5) it follows that the change in the throwing range, due to an error in the throwing angle, is directly proportional to the throwing range (it’s not for nothing that throws from behind the six-meter line are more expensive than throws from a closer distance). However, others are important in this case. If we neglect the term that includes the estimate of the value $(\Delta \theta)^2$, then at $\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}}$ we have a paradoxical result: $\Delta L \equiv 0$, i.e. the throw distance does not depend on the error in the throwing angle. If nothing is neglected in (5), then $\Delta L$ depends on the error $\Delta \theta$ exists, although it is quite weak:

$$\Delta L = - \frac{2L(\Delta \theta)^2}{1 - \sin \alpha}.$$

Thus, throwing at an angle of $\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}}$ is optimal not only because the minimum initial speed is required, but also because the error in the throwing angle at $\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}}$ has very little effect on the subsequent throw.

So, the effectiveness of the throw largely depends on the player’s ability to “withstand” the required value $\theta$. What is the acceptable value of $\Delta \theta$? Let us estimate it for the special case $\alpha = 0^\circ$, $L = 6$ m. In this case $\theta_{\text{опт}} = \varphi = 45^\circ$ (see formulas (4) and (3)), and as follows from (1), $l \approx 0,066$ m. Using expression (6), for $\Delta \theta$ we obtain (translating the radian measure into a degree): $|\Delta \theta| \lesssim 4,2^\circ$. Is it a lot or a little? Is it possible for an average person according to psychophysical data not to go beyond this limit? Now let's return to our formulas. Let's see at what angle φ a ball thrown at an angle $\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}} = 45^\circ + \alpha/2$ enters the stab. From expression (3) using simple trionometric transformations (those who are already tired of transformations can immediately look at “Appendix”, paragraph 2) we find:

$$\varphi(\theta_{\text{опт}}) = 45^\circ - \frac{\alpha}{2}.$$

This means that at $\alpha > 30^\circ$ a throw at an angle $\theta_{\text{опт}}$ will not be accurate at all, since at $\varphi < 30^\circ$ the ball cannot pass freely through the stab. Generally speaking, even at $\alpha \approx 20^\circ$ (i.e. $\varphi \approx 35^\circ$), the probability of a hit is still small due to the fact that in this case the value $l$ is relatively small (see conditions (1)). The situation when the angle $\alpha$ is relatively large occurs in the case of a throw from a close distance (up to 1-2 m), when the height of the ring above the center of the ball at the initial moment of its free flight is practically the same, and sometimes even greater than the throw range. However, the angle $\alpha$ can be reduced by throwing the ball in a jump, that is, by bringing the center of the ball at the initial moment to the level of the hoop. Obviously it is believed that a jump throw is needed in order to “outplay” the defender, however, when throwing from close distances, as follows from the results obtained, the jump also helps to increase the accuracy of the throw.

Generally speaking, taking into account the force of air resistance for a basketball shot is associated with solving differential equations, which we, of course, will not do here, but will only indicate the result. Although the force of air resistance somewhat reduces the throw's distance, its influence on the optimal throw angle can be neglected. Thus, for throws from a distance of up to 6-7 m, the optimal angle is lower than the value determined by formula (4) by no more than 2-3°.

The movement of the ball in the grip also depends on its rotation around its center. Such rotation involves adjacent layers of air, which, in combination with the translational movement of air relative to the ball, leads to the emergence of a force acting on the ball perpendicular to its trajectory (Magnus effect).
