Frames of reference in problems of mechanics

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences A. CHERNOUTSAN

While spilling on a boat under place A, an absent-minded man dropped his hat into the river, but, without noticing this, continued to row against the current. After 15 minutes, having discovered the loss, he turned the boat around and, rowing in the same darkness, found the hat under place B, 1 kilometer away from place A. What is the speed of the river?

It is not by chance that we began our conversation about the reference system with this very old and already classical problem. It clearly illustrates the fact that a successful choice of a reference system (R.O.) significantly simplifies, and sometimes even makes the solution of many physical problems simply verbal.

Indeed, to find the speed of the current in our example, we need to find out how long it took for the hat to move between places (since the speed of the hat is equal to the speed of the water). Let's move on to the S.O. associated with the hat. In it, the water is motionless, and the speed of the boat in both directions is the same. This means that the time it takes for the boat to move back to the hat is equal to the time it takes to move away from the hat, i.e. 15 minutes. Then the total time between losing and catching the hat is 30 minutes, and the speed of the current is 1 km/0.5 h=2 km/h.

When moving from one system to another, many physical quantities that describe the mechanical movements of bodies - for example, speed v, acceleration a, etc., change. In this case, the known laws of addition of the corresponding quantities are fulfilled:

$$\vec{v}_1 = \vec{v}_{12} + \vec{v}_2, \quad \vec{a}_1 = \vec{a}_{12} + \vec{a}_2.$$

Here $\vec{v}_1$ ($\vec{a}_1$) is the speed (acceleration) of the body relative to the first S.O., $\vec{v}_{12}$ ($\vec{a}_{12}$) is the speed (acceleration) of the body relative to the second S.O., $\vec{v}_2$ ($\vec{a}_2$) is the speed (acceleration) of the second S.O. relative to the first.*)

Let us immediately note that we will only discuss the forward motion of the S.O. relative to each other.

Now let's look at a few specific problems. First - from kinematics. Let us note right away that within the framework of kinematics, all SOs (at rest, moving uniformly or accelerated, rotating, etc.) are equal; the choice of S.O. is determined only by convenience and common sense.

Problem 1. The speed of the river is $\vec{u}$, the speed of the boat in still water is $\vec{v}_{ОТH}$. What course should a person in a boat keep so that the current carries it away as little as possible?

It is clear that here it is reasonable to discuss two S.O. The requirement to provide the smallest method relates to the S.O. associated with the ground - the angle between the speed of the boat $\vec{v}$ and the perpendicular to the shore line should be minimal. In the S.O. associated with water, the speed of the boat $\vec{v}_{ОТH}$ is given and it is required to find the direction of this speed, for example, the angle $\alpha$ between the speed $\vec{v}_{ОТH}$ and the perpendicular to the shore line. After all, “keeping the course” means setting the direction of the boat’s hull, and it coincides with the direction of the boat’s speed in that S.O. where the water is motionless.

*) All these issues were discussed in more detail in the note “Relative Motion” («Kvant», 1989, No. 9).

The conditions of the problem say nothing about the correspondence between the values ​​$u$ and $v_{ОТH}$. Let's consider two options.

1) $v_{ОТH} > u$. In this case, it is possible to ensure that the boat moves perpendicular to the shore (again, not at all). Let's write down the law of addition of velocities

$$\vec{v} = \vec{v}_{ОТH} + \vec{u}$$

and draw it on figure 1. From the right triangle we obtain

$$\sin \alpha = u/v_{ОТH}.$$

2) $v_{ОТH} < u$. The equality expressing the law of addition of velocities for this case is depicted in figure 2. When the course changes, the end of the vector $\vec{v}_{ОТH}$ is described by a semicircle. The minimum angle between the vector $\vec{v}$ and the perpendicular to the shore line corresponds to the condition that this vector touches the semicircle. From here we get

$$\sin \alpha = v_{ОТH}/u.$$

So, for $v_{ОТH} > u$ sin $\alpha = u/v_{ОТH}$; for $v_{ОТH} < u$ sin $\alpha = v_{ОТH}/u$ (examine the case $v_{ОТH} = u$ yourself).

When considering the free fall of several bodies, it is convenient to use the SO associated with one of these bodies. In it, all other bodies will move rectilinearly and uniformly (we do not take into account air resistance). This example is often called the “Baron Monchhausen method” (do you understand why?). We will use it in the next problem.

The total implus of the system of bodies in this SO is equal to zero at any moment.
Let us use the SO associated with the center of mass to solve the following problem.

Problem 4. Two small bodies are moving along the main horizontal plane, connected by a weightless and inextricable thread of length l. At some instant of time, a body of mass $m_1$ is at rest, and a body of mass $m_2$ has a speed v directed perpendicular to the thread (Fig. 4, a). At this point, tighten the thread.

The center of mass of this system of bodies is located on the thread at a distance $R_1 = m_2 l / (m_1 + m_2)$ from the first body and moves relative to the plane with a speed $v_\pi = m_2 v / (m_1 + m_2)$. Let us choose the SO in which the center of mass is at rest. In it, bodies $m_1$ and $m_2$ perform uniform motion in a circle around a fixed center of mass (Fig. 4, b), while the speed of the first body relative to this S.O. is equal in magnitude to $v_\pi$.

According to Newton's second law, the tension force of the thread acting on the first body is equal to

$$F = m_1 v_\pi^2 / R_1.$$

From where, substituting the expressions for $R_1$ and $v_\pi$, we finally find

$$F = \frac{m_1 m_2 v^2}{(m_1 + m_2) l}.$$

In many cases, when moving to a S.O. associated with the center of mass, the solution to the present problem is simplified, which makes sense to first transfer all the given problems into this S.O. and get the result, and then return it to the original S.O. As an example, consider two problems about an absolutely elastic impact of balls.

Problem 5. Two balls with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, moving at speeds $v_1$ and $v_2$, respectively, experienced an absolutely elastic LOB impact. What will be the speed of the balls after the impact?

In the SO associated with the center of mass, the total implus of the balls is zero both before and after the impact. It is easy to prove that both conservation laws - momentum and energy - will be correct if you simply change the directions of the balls' velocities to the opposite ones. Let's write down the corresponding formulas:

the initial velocities of the balls at the center of mass are equal to

$$u_1 = v_1 - v_\pi = v_1 - \frac{m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{m_2 (v_1 - v_\pi)}{m_1 + m_2}, u_2 = \frac{m_1 (v_2 - v_\pi)}{m_1 + m_2};$$

final velocities of the balls at the S.O. center of mass -

$$u'_1 = -u_1, u'_2 = -u_2;$$

the final velocities of the balls relative to the ground are

$$v'_1 = u'_1 + v_\pi = -\frac{m_2 (v_1 - v_\pi)}{m_1 + m_2} + \frac{m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{(m_1 - m_2) v_1 + 2 m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}, v'_2 = u'_2 + v_\pi = \frac{(m_2 - m_1) v_2 + 2 m_1 v_1}{m_1 + m_2}.$$
