By adjusting the angle $\varphi$, we increase the chances of getting into the basket, since the value $l$ increases. So, if $\varphi=40^\circ$, then $l \approx 0,05$ m, and for $\varphi=60^\circ$ $l \approx 0,095$ m, i.e. almost twice as much. The maximum possible value is $l \approx 0,112$ m (at $\varphi=90^\circ$).

Obviously, the angle $\varphi$ is greater than the angle $\theta$ at which the player throws the ball into the hoop (see Fig. 1). However, when shooting at very wide angles ($\theta \gtrsim 70^\circ$), it is quite difficult to hit the basket, at least from further distances. Not only is it difficult to hit, but sometimes just getting the ball to the hoop takes a lot of effort. (If basketball players throw things at large angles, then most often it’s “not out of luck”: you need to throw the ball over the hands of a defender who is closely guarding his “own” player.) To understand the reason for this, we will conduct simple theoretical studies, supplementing them with an experiment, for which you only need a ballpoint pen or pencil, a sheet of paper, a ruler and a protractor.

Let at the moment when the basketball player releases the ball from his hands, the center of the ball is located at point $B$ (see Fig. 1) and after time $t$ reaches the center of the hoop at point $A$. The initial speed of the throw is $V$, the throw distance (projection of the segment $BA$ on the horizontal plane) is $L$. We consider a “clean” throw - without reflecting the ball from the shield. Neglecting air (and defender) resistance, we describe the movement of the ball as the movement of the materials of a point thrown at an angle of $\theta$ to the horizon:

$$L = (V \cos \theta) t,$$

$$h = L \mathrm{tg} \alpha = (V \sin \theta) t - \frac{g t^2}{2}.$$

The angle $\varphi$ at which the ball flies into the hoop is determined from the condition:

$$\mathrm{tg} \varphi = \frac{|V_y A|}{V_x A} = \frac{|V \sin \theta - g t|}{V \cos \theta},$$

where $V_x A$ and $V_y A$ are the horizontal and vertical components of the ball speed at point $A$, respectively. After simple transformations we get:

$$L = \frac{V^2}{g} \sin(2\theta - \alpha) - \sin \alpha \cos \alpha,$$

$$\varphi = \mathrm{arctg} (tg \theta - 2 tg \alpha).$$

Let us examine the resulting expressions. From (2) it is clear that under the condition $2\theta - \alpha = 90^\circ$ a given throw range is achieved with the minimum possible speed and, therefore, with minimal costs for successfully executing the throw. So the optimal angle for throwing the ball is

$$\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}} = 45^\circ + \frac{\alpha}{2}.$$

In the special case $\alpha = 0^\circ$ (points $B$ and $A$ are located on the same level) $\theta_{\text{опт}} = 45^\circ$ and, as can be seen from (3), $\varphi = \theta$. For a player with average (not “basketball”) height, the ball in the final phase of the throw is from the floor at a height of $H' \approx 2$ m. The most “profitable” in modern basketball is an accurate throw from behind the so-called six-meter line, for which three points are given (for hits from a closer distance, only two points are counted, and for free throws - one point). Polyaga $h = H - H' = 1,05$ m, $L = 6$ m, we get $\alpha = \mathrm{arctg} (1,05/6) \approx 10^\circ$, i.e. $\theta_{\text{опт}} \approx 50^\circ$. From expression (2) it is found that the initial speed of the ball at $\theta = \theta_{\text{опт}}$ in this case is 8.37 m/s; if you run at such a speed, then you can cover a hundred-meter distance in 11.9 seconds, which is not bad for an ordinary physical trainer, but is significantly inferior to the world rekord. However, unlike athletics, very high speeds are not needed here: the greater the initial speed of the ball, the higher its speed at the entrance to the hoop, and this, as already mentioned, will increase the likelihood of the ball rebounding when touching the hoop.
