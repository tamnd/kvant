At the same time, it is known that animals can count (for example, produce a reaction only to every third stimulus). It is natural to assume that in the nervous system there are devices that react differently, for example, to a double impact and to a single one. What is known about the principles of operation of neurons allows us to assert: a single nerve cell cannot perform such a “simple” operation from a human point of view, such as counting. Lack of space does not allow us to describe a device of several neurons capable of producing a response, for example, to every second stimulus.

Eyes and logarithms

Visual receptors, just like others - service, temperature, etc., receive signals from the outside world; they must transmit visual information to the brain accurately and in a timely manner. The transmission of signals from the eye to the brain is carried out by neurons of the “threshold” type; the analog method turns out to be inapplicable when transmitting signals over very long distances. And for threshold neurons, as already mentioned, all impulses are exactly the same, and these neurons transmit information about the magnitude of the input signal to me as part of the impulse.

This is where the problem arises. The illumination at dusk, when objects are still visible, differs from the illumination in bright sunlight by about a billion (i.e., 10⁹) times. The maximum frequency with which a neuron can operate is 1000 impulses per second. It is easy to say that it is impossible to transmit information by changing the frequency of the neuron in proportion to the illumination: if in bright light the pulse frequency is maximum (1000 pulses/s), then when the illumination decreases a million times, the signal will arrive only once every 15 minutes. But during this time it will completely lose its activity!

But it may be reasonable to design the visual system in such a way that its different elements, different neurons each work in their own range of illumination: one at dusk, others on a cloudy day, others in the bright sun. A simple calculation shows that if we take 1 pulse/s as the lower limit of the frequency of a neuron’s operation, which is necessary for sufficiently timely transmission of information, then to cover the range of changes in illumination a billion times, a million neurons will be required - and this is without any “reserve” of readability, without doubling their work! But the main thing is this: at each moment only one cell out of a million will work, and the remaining 999,999 will “eat bread for nothing”: after all, unlike technical ones, living “mechanisms” consume energy (their “gasoline”) not only during operation. And saving energy in wildlife is one of the main conditions for survival.

So, a linear relationship between input and output signals in the case of the eye turns out to be impractical. And indeed, in nature, in this case, another function is used, which by school standards is quite complex.
