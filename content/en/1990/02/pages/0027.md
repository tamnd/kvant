It turns out that a certain vector of maximum activity $A_{\max}$ (Fig. 3) is associated with each cell of the cortex. When you need to move the paw in a different direction, i.e., a certain unit direction vector $e$ is given, the cell finds a projection $A_{\max}$ onto this direction, i.e., the jump product $A_{\max} \cdot e$ is calculated. To find out this, Georgopoulos posed the opposite problem: is it possible, by recording the work of nerve cells, to determine the direction of movement of the paw. Mathematically, this problem can be formulated as a question about the existence of functions inverse to a given one. It is clear that the direction of movement cannot be determined from the activity of one cell: firstly, cosine is an even function, and in the project that interests us, it does not have an inverse. Indeed, if, for example, the direction of maximum activity is direct overeating, and the activity of the neuron is positive maximum, then it is known that the paw moves at an angle of $60^\circ$ to the preferential direction, but it is impossible to determine to the right or left of it. Secondly, one cell has too much of a “dead zone” - the zone when it is completely silent. But if multiple cells are recorded, then it is possible to successfully determine the direction in which the paw is moving (and even predict in which direction it will move, since the cells start working a tenth of a second before the paw begins to move). Back in 1971, American psychologists P. Shepard and J. Metzler discovered a phenomenon that they called “mental rotation.” In the experiments, subjects were shown two figures and asked: is this a development of the figure or the same one, but rotated at a certain angle? The response time turned out to be a linear function of the angle of rotation of one figure relative to the other.

In another version of the experiment, the letter $R$ or its mirror image, the letter I, was shown as a variable; you need to quickly determine which letter it is. In this case, the letter is shown in different positions. And here the response time was proportional to the angle of rotation of the letter relative to the “normal” position.
