A little commotion at the pharmacy

(For the beginning, see p. 50)

Each pill with an increased dose of medicine is 10 mg heavier than the normal one, then dividing 270 by 10, we get 27 - the number of heavier pills.

Let's write the number 27 in the double system: 11011. The binary digits in which there are ones tell us what powers of the number 2 add up to the double number 11011 (or the decimal number 27): 1, 2, 8 and 16. The ones are in the first, second, fourth and fifth double digits. Consequently, unusable pills with increased drug content are in the first, second, fourth and fifth bottles. The dual system also takes pride of place in entertaining mathematics.

Here's a simple card trick that allows you to surprise and amuse your friends. Although it looks nothing like the problem of finding bottles of unusable pills, both the problem and the trick are essentially “binary” - they are based on a binary number system.

Have one of the triels carefully recalculate the card deck. Having placed it in your pocket, ask your landowner to call any number from 1 to 15, after which, putting your hand in your pocket, deliver cards whose total values ​​are equal to the named number (Ace is considered equal to 1).

The secret of the trick is simple. You put an ace, a two, a four and an eight in your pocket in advance. It is impossible to determine at the head the lack of four cards in the deck, and your viewers will be confident that you asked for a recalculation of the full deck. You place the shuffled deck under the four cards already in your pocket. After the number is named, you mentally imagine it as the sum of a power of 2 (for example, if the number 10 is named, then you mentally decompose it into the sum 8 + 2 = 10) and, putting your hand in your pocket, bring out a two and an eight.

From the book “There is an Idea” by M. Gardner, M.: Mir, 1982.
