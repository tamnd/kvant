But in living organizations, it turns out that “everything is the other way around”: a nerve cell (neuron) naturally and simply carries out operations with positive and negative real “numbers”, and in order to “count” even to two, a system of several neurons is required - a primitive “brain”.

How does a neuron work? Like any cell, a neuron is separated from the disruptive intercellular environment by a special membrane - the membrane. There is a potential difference between the internal contents of the cell and the external environment. If the cell is at rest, the potential difference across its membrane does not change. It is natural to take this potential difference at rest as a zero level (just as the melting temperature of ice was taken to be zero).

A neuron can be influenced by other nerve cells—excitatory and inhibitory. The signals received from these cells cause changes in the potential difference across the membrane in two opposite directions*). When different signals arrive to a neuron at the same time, they add up, and, naturally, taking into account the sign, i.e. the neuron sums up the positive and negative signals coming to it; this amount can be positive or negative.

An interesting feature of the neuron’s operation is that, unlike technical adders - from the ancient abacus to the computer - it does not “remember” the received amount for long: if the external influences have stopped, then the accumulated amount begins to kill in absolute value so that the neuron returns to a resting state (potential on_membrane

Such “unreliability” of a neuron is due to the fact that it is not intended for storing, but for transmitting and converting information: the neuron transmits the received signal to other cells of the neuron network (to “target” or “recipient” cells). According to the method of signal transmission, there are essentially two different types of neurons with different operating principles: “analog” and “threshold” neurons.

The first type of neuron acts on target cells with a force proportional to the accumulated amount, but only if this amount is positive. When the amount is negative, it is not transmitted further - the neuron is inhibited. The rule for converting signals by analog neurons is described by the formula $y = k(x + |x|)/2$, where $x$ is the accumulated potential, $y$ is the magnitude of the transmitted signal, and $k$ is the proportionality coefficient.

Neurons of the second type work differently. Such a neuron is “silent” until the amount of influence reaches a certain positive value - a “threshold”. Then the neuron will return and send along its output process - the axon - an electronic impulse (always of the same magnitude), which acts on the target cells. After returning, the neuron “rests” for some time—it is silent, regardless of whether other cells act on it or not, and then, if by the end of the rest the accumulated amount is above the threshold, it sends a new impulse.

As a result, depending on the magnitude of the input signal, its duration and depending on the characteristics of the neuron, the output signal is obtained in the form of a series of pulses of constant magnitude, but of different frequencies. Thus, threshold neurons use a completely non-trivial principle of encoding information by signal frequency.
