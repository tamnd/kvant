G. Fechner formed the famous Weber-Fecher law:
Sensations grow in arithmetic progression, when destruction grows in geometric progression.
This law was published in Feher's book Elements of Psychophysics in 1859. The mathematical expression of the law was also given there:
$E = a \log I + b$,
where $E$ is the omission measure, $a$ and $b$ are constants, $I$ is the looting measure.

Why does a cat need vectors?

The word “vector”, one might say, is a “baby” - apparently, it first appeared in the work of the English mathematician W. Hamilton in 1845. But the corresponding concept was used in physics several centuries earlier in connection with the consideration of the law of addition of forces (“parallelogram rules”). We learned about vectors in animal organization only in recent years.

It started with cats. In 1988, Canadian scientist J. McPheron performed interesting work. She put the cat on a special platform, only this platform in some direction, and watched how the cat maintained its balance. Let's say she's just a platform forward. The cat's legs began to move forward along with the platform, but the body remained in place. Then the cat, in order to return the center of gravity to the correct position above the support points, activates the muscles of the paws and, pushing off from the platform, moves the body forward. If the platform is only to the right, the center of gravity will deviate to the left relative to the support and the paws should create a force directed to the right, etc.

How will this work of the paws occur while maintaining balance?

The most natural thing is to assume that each of the two hind legs *) when pushing forward creates a force directed forward; the sum of these two forces restores the correct position of the body (Fig. 2, $a$). If the platform is pushed to the right, each paw creates a force directed to the right, etc. This hypothesis is consistent with the fact that the cat has powerful muscles that move the paw to the right or backward - they are used for walking and jumping, as well as muscles that abduct the paw outward or towards the axis of the body. However, when McPheron began to find out what was actually happening, it turned out that the picture was completely different: when pushing the platform, regardless of the direction of movement, the cat’s hind legs create forces directed along two straight lines (each paw along its own), located approximately at an angle of $45^\circ$ to the axis of the body. Further, in the simplest case, when the platform just comes to the right, the forces created by the paws are not directed to the right, but also at an angle of $45^\circ$ to the axis of the body (again, see Fig. 2, $a$).
