They didn’t understand what Newton said. And that was a mistake. We must understand the meaning of what Newton wrote, and not discover it in trivial illogicality. Having defined mass before introducing the concept of momentum, not knowing about the existence of the law of conservation of energy (the very concept of “energy” appeared more than a hundred years later), Newton found himself in a difficult position. And he found the only way out, coming up with his Definitio.

Newton himself did not use the word mass and included a different meaning in his definition. In the 17th and 18th centuries, it was believed that all bodies consisted of identical very small particles - they were called monads. The monads were all the same, and different bodies differed from each other only in that the monads in them were packed with different densities. What monads look like and what properties they have cannot be learned from experiments; but scientists at that time were not very worried about experiments; they believed that the laws of nature could be discovered by reasoning alone. The theories of monads were studied by Leibnitz, Euler and other scientists.

The German philosopher Christan Wolf, a popularizer of Leibniz's ideas, was considered a major specialist in monads. Almost all of Europe learns from Wolf's textbooks. Lomonosov Pass is one of Wolf's textbooks, and it was the first physics textbook in Russia. Then everyone knew about monads and, they say, even the court ladies at the Prussian court loved to talk about them. Therefore, for the 18th century reader, Newton's definition was used to make sense.

The theory of monads, of course, came from science a long time ago. In the 19th century they already talked about atoms with different chemical and physical properties, but, surprisingly, in our 20th century it is useful to return to Newton’s idea and say that the mass of a body is proportional to the number of nucleons - neutrons and protons it contains - or even proportional to the volume of the body and “nucleon density” - the average number of nucleons in 1 cm³.

What are the possibilities for causing such a determination? The mass of a neutron is greater than the mass of a proton by about 0.2%. One can, of course, take this difference into account by counting neutrons and protons separately; but, on the other hand, an error of tenths of a percent in many cases does not bother us (at least when solving school problems).

A more fundamental error is due to the fact that nucleons in nuclei are “lighter” than nucleons in a free state. The fact is that the energy of a bound system of particles (atomic nucleus) is less than the total energy of these particles in a free state. To split a nucleus into its constituent nucleons, it is necessary to expend energy equal to the difference between these energies (it is called binding energy). And since there is a connection between energy and mass, expressed by Einstein’s formula - $E = mc^2$, - the difference in energy represents the difference in mass. This means that the mass of an atomic nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its constituent nucleons. This decrease in mass is quite large and amounts to almost 1% (more precisely, 0.6-0.8%).

We know that the atomic mass of hydrogen*) is equal to 1.0078 atomic mass units, and the atomic mass of the main uranium isotone is equal to 238 amu, i.e. less than the sum of the masses of 92 protons and 146 neutrons that make up the nucleus of this isotone. You can make an adjustment for “binding energies,” but you shouldn’t complicate the task.

So, Newton's definition is to distinguish what is not "empty." The concept is correct, it determines the mass with an error of less than a percent.

But we haven't finished our story yet.

The mass we are talking about does not change in kilograms or grams - the unit of measurement is the atomic mass unit (let me remind you that an amu is defined as 1/12 of the mass of a carbon atom¹²C). Convert atomic mass units in the book

*) This is the mass of a neutral hydrogen atom (proton + electron). To use the atomic mass, there is no need to correct for the mass of the electron.
