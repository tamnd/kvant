So, let's get to the proof. Let us mentally carry out such an experiment (it was discussed by E. Mach*)). Let's bend a ring of wire and place three balls A, B and C on it. The masses of these balls satisfy the equalities (*) and (**). Let us now push ball A, giving it speed $\vec{u}$. If there is no friction, then the ball will move along the ring without changing the speed (modulo) until it collides with ball B. After the collision, ball A will stop, and B begins to move with the same speed $u$ (again modulo) with which ball A moves before the collision. (If we did not know in advance that the masses of A and B are the same, then we could find out about this by comparing the velocities of A and B before and after the collision - this is quite good method, or by checking that ball A has stopped.**))

While moving, ball B will collide with ball C after some time. We can also imagine the result of the collision: B will stop, and C begins to move with a speed of $u$ (after all, $m(B) = m(C)$).

The next event will be the collision of ball C with ball A. We have not verified experimentally that $m(C) = m(A)$, and then we cannot state in advance that A begins to move with the same speed $u$ with which it moved at first. To reach this conclusion we must bring to our aid the law of conservation of energy.*)

*) Ernst Max (1838-1916) - a major Austrian physicist and physical scientist. He studied mechanics, acoustics, and optics.

**) These statements are not obvious. They can be justified by citing the fact that balls A and B are the same. But let's not give in!

***) It must be admitted that we could use the law of conservation of energy (or momentum) equally when we argued that balls A and B were placed in rolls after a collision: A stopped, and B “took over” the speed of A. Although this is “obvious,” it cannot be proven without conservation laws. (Beware of the word "obviously"!)

If the speed of ball A after the collision turned out to be greater than $u$, then A would impart this greater speed to ball B when contained, and in this way energy could be obtained from nothing: after each collision with ball C, ball A would move faster and faster!

If the speed A turned out to be less than $u$, then the balls would eventually stop and their kinetic energy would disappear without a trace (recall that the balls move without friction). Both conclusions are false, and therefore the statement that after colliding with C, ball A will move with speed $u$ is true.

Now everything has fallen into place. To satisfy the law of conservation of energy, the speed of ball A after the collision $A \rightarrow B$, $B \rightarrow C$, $C \rightarrow A$ returned to its original value.

We see that for a rigorous proof, logic is not enough; it is necessary to bring to the aid of a physical law - the law of conservation of energy. Without this it is impossible to say that $m(C) = m(A)$!

But for practical purposes, a strict definition is not needed everywhere. In an offended life, we have enough of a less strict, but more understandable definition.

Newton, in the first pages of his great book “Mathematical Principles of Natural Physics,” explained what mass is like this: “The quantity of materials is a measure proportional to its density and volume”*. If, without hesitation, we assume that the quantity of materials denotes Newtonian mass, then such a definition represents only the content: in order to find out the density $\varrho$, it is necessary to divide the mass $m$ by the volume $V$, and therefore the formula $m = \varrho V$ is a consequence of the formula $\varrho = m/V$. I must say that many people perceived it this way.
*) Since this definition is almost always given with errors, we present it in the language in which Newton wrote - in good Latin (after all, they now teach Latin in grammar schools): “Definitio I. Quantitas materiae est mensura ejusdem orta ex illius densitate et magnitudine conjunctim.”
