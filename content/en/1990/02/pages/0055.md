how many words? The pressure that the rolled liquid exerts on the walls of the vessel is due to the fact that the molecules continuously bombard these walls from the inside. But the movement of molecules in a liquid is xaotic, and therefore they bombard any wall of the vessel with the same “zeal”. From here it immediately follows that Pascal’s law, since it is associated with the chaotic nature of thermal motion, is valid not only for liquids, but also for gases.

Let us now move on to another, almost equally “hooligan” experience. Let's put an empty bottle on the ground and be up to the pot with a stick, trying to smash the bottle. The author, again, does not recognize you for destroying glass containers. This is just a thought experiment. Let's assume that you failed to break the bottle. Don't despair - Pascal's law will help you. Fill the bottle to the top with water and plug it with a stopper. And now it’s relatively easy to remove the cork, and the bottle will happily fall apart.

It must be said that Blaise Pascal has nothing to do with this experiment. They say that part of the discoverer of such an exotic method of breaking bottles belongs to Galileo Galilei, who did all this 30→40 years before the birth of the outstanding Frenchman. But to explain the results of the experiment, Pascal's law is necessary.

Let's say you removed one cork with a force of $F_1$. The pressure that you (more precisely, the stick) exert on the cork with an area of ​​$S_1$ when removing it is equal to $P_1 = \frac{F_1}{S_1}$.

This pressure is transferred to the liquid, and the liquid transfers it to the side walls and bottom of the bottle. And if the total area of ​​the walls and bottom is $S_2$, then a force $F_2 = P_1 S_2 = F_1 \frac{S_2}{S_1}$ will act on them.

A simple calculation shows that for a common bottle with a capacity of 1/2 liter the ratio $S_2/S_1$ is on the order of several hundred.

That's exactly how many times your strengthened blow has. Naturally, even if you don’t hit it very hard, the bottle still won’t resist.

The last formula, of course, applies not only to the “bottle” experiment. It has a general character, and Pascal understood this perfectly. This is what he wrote in his “Treatise on the Equilibrium of Fluids”*):

“If a vessel filled with water and closed on all sides has two responses, one 100 times larger than the other, which are covered by pistons precisely fitted to them, then one person pressing on the small piston will balance the force of 100 people pressing on the piston 100 times more, and will overcome the force of 99. And whatever the ratio of these responses, always when the forces applied to pistons, relate to each other as responsibilities, these forces will be in balance.

...It follows from this that a vessel filled with water is a new principle of mechanics and a new machine for increasing strength to an iron degree, because with the help of this means a person can lift any weight offered to him.”

It is absolutely clear that these words fully formulate the idea of ​​a hydraulic lift, a hydraulic press, a hydraulic brake system for perfect automation and many other similar devices.
