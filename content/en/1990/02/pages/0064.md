Frames of reference in problems of mechanics

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences A. CHERNOUTSAN

While spilling on a boat under place A, an absent-minded man dropped his hat into the river, but, without noticing this, continued to row against the current. After 15 minutes, having discovered the loss, he turned the boat around and, rowing in the same darkness, found the hat under place B, 1 kilometer away from place A. What is the speed of the river?

It is not by chance that we began our conversation about the reference system with this very old and already classical problem. It clearly illustrates the fact that a successful choice of a reference system (R.O.) significantly simplifies, and sometimes even makes the solution of many physical problems simply verbal.

Indeed, to find the speed of the current in our example, we need to find out how long it took for the hat to move between places (since the speed of the hat is equal to the speed of the water). Let's move on to the S.O. associated with the hat. In it, the water is motionless, and the speed of the boat in both directions is the same. This means that the time it takes for the boat to move back to the hat is equal to the time it takes to move away from the hat, i.e. 15 minutes. Then the total time between losing and catching the hat is 30 minutes, and the speed of the current is 1 km/0.5 h=2 km/h.

When moving from one system to another, many physical quantities that describe the mechanical movements of bodies - for example, speed v, acceleration a, etc., change. In this case, the known laws of addition of the corresponding quantities are fulfilled:

$$\vec{v}_1 = \vec{v}_{12} + \vec{v}_2, \quad \vec{a}_1 = \vec{a}_{12} + \vec{a}_2.$$
