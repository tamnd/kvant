Congo language
We see that writing dates in the Congo language has the form

kilumbu ya ...ya ngonda...
where in place of each gap there is one word (tatu, mosi, etc.) or a phrase starting with kumi na. A comparison of the lines shows that the word after kilumbu ya denotes a number, and the word after the last place is the ordinal number of the place, i.e. “January” literally means “1st month,” “March” means “3rd month,” etc. The numbers 11 and 18 are rendered (respectively) as “10 and 1” and “10 and 8” (apparently denoting “and”). The literal meaning of the words kilumbu ya and ya ngonda remains unclear, but it is clear that the general meaning of the construction is

date... month number...

March ideas
At the end of the date, the name of the place is indicated, while long names are transferred to the abbreviation: APR - April, MAI - May, AVG - August, OCT - October, NOV - November, DEC - December (coincides with the beginning of the corresponding word in English and a number of other languages).
The first place number of the modern calendar had a special designation. All other dates appearing in the problem refer to the second half of the place; their ancient Roman designation included an indication of the first day of the next place - for example, at the end of March dates it is indicated: KAL·APR, at the end of April - KAL·MAI, etc. The Romans kept a figurative count of days, i.e. they indicated what kind of count a given day would be if we counted from the beginning of the next place and included the beginning itself in the count. For example, for July 27 we have:
July 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, August 1,
- only six days; therefore the ancient Roman designation for this date includes the number VI. (To count back the days of the second half of the place, the sums of modern and ancient Roman numbers are indicated: for September and November, which have 30 days each, they are equal to 32, and for July and October - 33.)
Answer: March 30, А·Д·XII·KAL·MAI, October 20.
