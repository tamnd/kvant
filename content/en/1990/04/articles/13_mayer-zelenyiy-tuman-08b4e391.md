Green fog
V. MAYER

Have you ever seen green fog? No? Don't be upset, this crying can be fixed. Now you will see for yourself and show your commodity not only green, but also blue, and red, and in general - multi-colored fog.

Demonstration experience. It is most convenient to conduct the experiment in a school physics room on a demonstration table. For the experiment, you will first need a light source. This could be, for example, an automatic light bulb designed for an operating voltage of 6 V and producing light with a power of 21 kD, or a school illuminator for shadow projection (you just need to attach its light bulb close to the collecting lens of the illuminator or remove the lens completely). Any other light source will do, as long as it is small in size and does not illuminate nearby objects too brightly. The easiest way to achieve this is to place the source behind the response on a big black screen.

So, install light bulb 1 (Fig. 1) and place glass bottle 2 with a capacity of 20 liters at a distance of 20-40 cm from it. (Such bottles with a diameter of approximately 25 cm and a height of about 45 cm with a neck having a hole with a diameter of 3.5-4.5 cm, continue in hardware stores.) The bottle must be thoroughly washed, immediately before the experiment, rinsed with cold water and the outer surface wiped dry. There should be no drops of water left on the walls of the bottle, either from the equipment or from the inside.

Turn on the light bulbs, create a temperature or twilight in the office and pump air into the bottle. Ask the audience to look carefully at the bulbs and after a short pause, quickly remove the cap from the neck of the bottle. Immediately around the light bulb, within the size of the bottle, you can see bright and rather bright multi-colored beads.

What happens in the bottle? You pump air and the pressure in the bottle increases. When you pause, the air in the bottle becomes room temperature. There is a little water remaining after rinsing for two bottles, and there is a thin film of water on the walls. Therefore, in addition to air, the bottle contains saturated water vapor.

You release the cork, and the bottled air quickly expands. At the same time, since the process is close to adnabatic (that is, it occurs without heat exchange with the environment), the air temperature in the bottle drops, the steam becomes supersaturated and easily condenses into droplets of water on dust particles, which are so abundant in the air.

This creates fog in the bottle. You can easily detect it if you look at the bottle from the side: at the moment fog appears, the light beam coming from the light bulb becomes visible due to scattering. It is immediately noticeable that the mist in the bottle is quite rare. This indicates that, despite the significant, according to our subjective estimates, dust content in the air, there are relatively few dust particles as centers of condensation of water vapor into bottles.

Why do rings appear? The spherical droplets of water that make up the fog float in the air filling the bottle. They are transparent, which means light can be refracted into them, and they are quite small, which means light can diffract onto them. Both assumption and light diffraction can, in principle, produce multi-colored rings. What phenomenon is responsible for the results of our experiments?

The refraction and reflection of light in spherical drops of water explains the rainbow. But you can observe the radius only at certain angles of the wave. In addition, a rainbow, to a first approximation, does not depend on the size of the drops. In our continuation of the experiments, the diameters of the rings changed intermittently, and this can only be explained by a change in the size of the fog droplets. finally, a well-bright rainbow is obtained on large drops of water with a diameter of 1-2 mm, and there are obviously no such drops in bottles. So, we conclude that the multi-colored rings observed in the described experiments are most likely due to the diffraction of light on small drops of water.

Study. After assembling the installation, get crowns around the light source. Slowly skim and release the bag. In this case, the crowns first increase and then decrease. The result obtained can only be explained by the fact that when the pressure increases, the fog droplets become smaller, and when the pressure decreases, they become larger. This is quite natural, since with an adynabatic increase in pressure, the temperature of the gas in the flask increases, and water evaporates from the droplets, and as the pressure decreases, it condenses into them.

Repeat the experiment again and again. You see that the brightness and size of the diffraction pattern are gradually decreasing. After about two minutes, the crowns disappear completely and do not appear, no matter how much money you make on the pear. Let's try to explain this.

We have already said that the decrease in the size of the picture is caused by an increase in the diameter of the fog droplets. The brightness of the picture can decrease only because the total number of fog droplets in the bulb decreases. But why is this happening? Maybe the droplets will evaporate? The reactionary picture is to increase, but everything will turn out just the opposite. The most natural explanation remains - water droplets simply settle on the bottom of the flask.

Indeed, droplets of water condense on dust particles and, settling on the bottom of the flask, carry them along with them. The air in the flask becomes cleaner, the number of condensation centers remains smaller, which means that the droplets of fog become larger and rarer, as a result of which the picture gradually decreases and loses brightness until it disappears completely.

It is clear that the ability of the flask to produce crowns can be restored if dust suspended in the air is introduced into it. For example, do this. Open the flask, bring the end of the glass tube protruding from the stopper to the neck of the flask and squeeze and release the bulb several times. Thus, you will expose the flask to room air, which is always somewhat dusty, and will be able to successfully repeat experiments on the formation of crowns.

Try introducing different “grades” of dust into the flask, collecting it into a rubber bulb from the spines of books that have stood on a shelf for a long time, from fleecy clothes, etc. Monitor the corresponding changes in the diffraction pattern and make sure that the dust does not significantly increase the number of vapor condensation centers in the flask. What if you use smoke?

Bring a little smoke into the flask from a key of cotton wool smoldering at the end of the wire. By squeezing and releasing the bulb, you will get a thick white fog through which you can’t see anything clearly. It appears because smoke particles are very numerous, water vapor condenses on each of them, and as a result there are a lot of very small droplets of water.
