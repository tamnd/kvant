With the invention of the steam engine and steam pumps, the theory of hinged mechanisms began to rapidly develop - systems composed of solid links interconnected by hinges and designed to convert the movement of one or more links into the required movements of other links. For about a year, the development of the theory of hinge mechanisms was determined by the desire to solve in the most satisfactory way the problem that was so necessary for the English mechanic James Watt in developing a steam engine.

Simple Watt straightening mechanism

Watt reasoned as follows. Let us consider two beams $AO$ and $BO'$, rotating around fixed centers $O$ and $O'$. If the ends $A$ and $B$ of the yoke $AO$ and $BO'$ are hingedly connected with a segment $AB$—Watt called it a serb—then some point of the serb will make a movement that little deviates from the rectilinear one (Fig. 2). To determine the most suitable for this purpose of the fixed center $O'$ and the length of the yoke $BO'$, we consider three positions of the yoke $OA$ (Fig. 3): the middle $OA$ and two extreme $OA'$ and $OA''$ and we require that at these three positions of the yoke the point $m$ Serbia is on some straight line $MN$. For such a straight line, Watt took the perpendicular to the average position of the yoke $OA$, passing through the middle of the arrow $SA$ (the arrow is the part of the radius perpendicular to the chord $A'A''$, enclosed between the chord and the arc).

$$OD = \frac{OC + CA + AD}{3}, OC = AC = BC$$

Precise straightening mechanisms. Inversion

All the straightening hinge mechanisms described above were approximated: the straight line was replaced by some suitable curve. The theory of precise rectifying mechanisms is based on one geometric transformation that is of great importance in modern mathematics and physics - inversion.

Let a circle be given with center $P$ and radius $r$ (Fig. 9). Let's take some point $M$, for definition - external with respect to this circle. From point $M$ we draw tangents $MT_1$ and $MT_2$ to this circle and construct the intersection point $M'$ of chord $T_1T_2$ with line $PM$. From the right triangle $PMT_1$ there is

$$PM \cdot PM' = r^2. \quad (1)$$

Conversely, if given a point $M'$ lying inside the circle, then we can easily construct an external point $M$.

Two points $M$ and $M'$ lying on the same ray emanating from the center $P$ of a circle of radius $r$ are called mutually inverse with respect to this circle if the distances from them to the center satisfy correspondence (1). It is obvious that the inverse point of a point on a circle coincides with it and that the center of the circle does not have an inverse point.

The correspondence between mutually inverse points or, in other words, the transformation by means of which from each point $M$ the inverse point $M'$ is obtained, is called inversion with respect to a given circle. The circle itself is called the inversion circle, its center is called the inversion stripe, and the square of its radius is the degree of inversion.

From the above it follows that inversion is a one-to-one (except for the point $P$) transformation of points on the plane. There are no reverse point inversion bands.

The correspondence between points in inversion is mutual: if point $M'$ corresponds to point $M$, then point $M$ corresponds to point $M'$. Each point on the inversion circle is a fixed point.

Let us now consider the property of inversion, which is important for our purposes.

Theorem 1. A line that does not pass through the inversion stripes corresponds to a circle passing through the inversion stripes.

Proof. Let $A$ be the projection of the inversion strip onto a given line (Fig. 10), $B$ be an arbitrary point of this line, $A'$ and $B'$ be the inverse points of points $A$ and $B$. From an arbitrary inversion it follows that $PA \cdot PA' = PB \cdot PB'$, or $PA:PB = PB':PA'$. By virtue of this proportionality, triangles $PAB$ and $PB'A'$ are similar. Since the angle $PAB$ is a right angle, then the angle $PB'A'$ is a right angle. Point $B'$ lies on a circle having segment $PA'$ as its diameter, which is what needed to be proven.

Due to reciprocity, the following statements follow from Theorem 1.

Theorem 2. A circle passing through the inversion stripes is transformed into a straight line perpendicular to the line connecting the inversion stripes with the center of the given circle.

Consequently, if we were able to obtain a mechanism that implements inversion, we would be able to transform circular motion into straight motion and vice versa.

Mechanisms that implement inversion are called inverters.

**Garth Inverter**

Soon after the appearance of the Posely inverter, the English mathematician and mechanic G. Hart built an inverter from an antiparallelogram.
