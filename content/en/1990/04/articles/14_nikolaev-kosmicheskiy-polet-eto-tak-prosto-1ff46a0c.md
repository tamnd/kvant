V. NIKOLAEV

On July 22, 1987, another spacecraft launched from the Baikonur cosmodrome. After 10 minutes, its crew was already in low-Earth orbit. There would be nothing unusual in this event - we have already begun to get used to space flights - if not for one circumstance. In the Soyuz TM-3 spacecraft, cosmonauts went into orbit, discovering the third hundred earthlings living in outer space. The commander of the Soyuz TM-3 crew was Aleksandr Viktorenko. One week will pass, and together with him another Alexander, Laveykin, will return to Earth from the Mir station, closed on the list of the second hundred.

Who are these people who have risen above us all, looking at our world from a height of hundreds of kilometers? Superman heroes or lucky ones who smiled on fate? Is space flight really that scary, or, as one Soviet cosmonaut joked, can anyone fly on their own?

Is it possible to at least assume that the space environment, with all its unknowns and even secrets, is less dangerous for humans than the earthly environment, which seems so habitable to us? There are estimates by American specialists, according to which for every thousand flights of manned spacecraft with an average stay in space of 24 hours (and such short flights have practically never happened for a long time), at least 95 disasters and accidents should be expected. Of these, 65% - at the beginning and end of the flight, i.e. at the launch site and when returning to Earth (50 and 15 percent, respectively), and 25% - during the flight.
Let's look into the past. Over 29 years, 129 space flights were completed.*) Most of them (especially in the early years) were accompanied by the discovery of complexity and danger for crews. We will only talk about a few of them.

On July 21, 1961, US Air Force Major W. Grissom made a suborbital flight on the Mercury 4 spacecraft. During landing, the lock exploded and Grissom's ship sank. The astronaut managed to escape.
Four years later, the Voskhod-2 crew made an emergency landing (due to a failure in the control loop).
In the same year, a fire failure (in the on-board computer) also occurred on the Gemini 4 spacecraft. In another two months, the elements of the power supply system fail, and the directors of the Gemini 5 flight urgently change the test program.
The flight of Gemini 8 (March 1966) almost ended in disaster. After the ship docked with the stage of the Agena launch vehicle, its rotation began. With difficulty using manual control, the astronauts returned the ship to Earth.
1967 turned out to be the most critical year for the American space program. At the end of January, a fire inside the ship at the launch site killed the crew preparing for the first flight of the Apollo program. The crew commander was V. Grissom, already known to us, who in the USA was called the most ineffective of the American cosmonauts. Grissom owns the words he said after the flight on Gemini 3: “If we had to die, we would want people to come to terms with it... The conquest of space is worth the risk...”
A month later, at the completion of the first flight under the Soyuz program, V. Komarov died. The disaster occurred due to the failure of the parachute system.
Tragadini suspended space flights in both the USSR and the USA. They resumed after a year and a half. At first everything went relatively well. But in 1970, the next blow came: a liquid oxygen tank exploded on the Apollo 13 spacecraft. Another cylinder was damaged by shrapnel. There is no need to list all the accidents that occurred with Soviet and American cosmonauts before the tragic death of the Challenger reusable spacecraft (in 1986). Let us note only two accidents with the Soyuz launch vehicle. In the first case (1974), as a result of the failure of the third stage engines, the ship (with cosmonauts V. Lazarev and O. In the second case (1983), the accident occurred at the launch pad. However, two seconds saved cosmonauts V. Titov and G. Strekalov from death, but the emergency rescue system (SAS) managed to level the ship, which literally rescued the ship with the cosmonauts from the exploding carrier.

The lack of SAS on the reusable Space Shuttle cost the lives of seven American astronauts during the ill-fated Challenger launch.

If we scrupulously count all the small and large accidents that have occurred over the almost thirty-year history of man's exploration of outer space, then the figures cited by American experts will not seem exaggerated.

(Unfortunately, not only the road to the stars is covered with thorns, but also the preparations for it. Cosmonauts died at various stages of preparation for space flight. Among them was V. Bondarenko, who was part of the first “Gagarin” detachment. The cause of his death was a fire that occurred during tests in the trial pressure chamber in 1961, shortly before Yu. Gagarin’s flight. The last on the tragic list is the name of the American astronaut S. Grigg, who died last year in a training flight.)

Until now, the discussion has been about only one side participating, or, more correctly, implementing space flight—the package-space technical. But there is also a second one - the man himself. “An astronaut is not an insensitive phantom, not a doll installed in a complex spacecraft. He is a living person with his own weaknesses...” Let’s quote first in this place so that we can return to it later. Under these words is the signature of a person who knows the profession of an astronaut well: V. Shatalov commanded crews three times, carried out dockings with the Soyuz spacecraft and the Salyut station.

Spacecraft designers, system developers, doctors and psychologists pay great attention to ensuring such space flight conditions under which not only will no harm be done to the astronauts’ body, but also the human dignity that is absent in a machine will be fully realized.

Now, when the maximum duration of a flight in space orbit is 326 days (Yu. Romanenko), the fears that tormented those who prepared Yuri Gagarin’s first flight may seem frivolous. Not everyone may know that, fearing for his psyche, the designers blocked the control system, and the number 125, which allows the spaceship raft, after dialing it on the remote control, to remove the blocking, was written on the inside of the envelope. The envelope was closed on the wall of the Vostok cabin.

Was there any basis for such fears? Judge for yourself. During numerous experiments simulating the conditions of space flight, some subjects developed mental disorders such as hallucinations, loss of orientation, and a feeling of fear.

In the book by Yu. Gagarin and V. Lebedev “Psychology and Space” they describe the reactions of subjects in conditions of loneliness. “The situation itself seemed harmless. But one very qualified pilot felt dizzy, although the camera did not move from its place. Another began to imagine some unfamiliar faces among the control panel instruments. The third, also a pilot by profession, when the “flight” was coming to an end, before his eyes, the instrument panel suddenly began to “melt and drip onto the floor.” The fourth wanted more because of the blurry image on the TV screen, although the screen was perfectly clear. In vain they tried to convince him that nothing had happened - he demanded an immediate end to the experiment and, when he left the chamber, stated that, in addition to visual illusions, he felt the walls of the room closing above him.》 The authors of the book cite many such cases. But what about everyday life when, in addition to loneliness and isolation, one comes to experience weightlessness, sensory hunger, changes in spatial and temporal structure, limitation of personally familiar information, and, finally, a threat to life?...

But, the reader will say, Gagarin survived, just as other cosmonauts survived. And none of them went crazy, did not let down those who sent them into flight... Here is the time to give the second part of the quote from V. Shatalov’s statement: “... but also with their strength - intelligence, ingenuity, courage, with their highest feeling of responsibility for themselves, for their comrades, for the assigned work. At any moment of his stay in space, an accident may happen in such a way that only he, the space priest, will be able to make and implement the only correct decision. For Space is the sum of what is not fully known, it is always a risk, it is work at the upper limit of human capabilities...”

It is a person who makes a car reliable, capable of getting out of a difficult, and sometimes even an emergency, situation. The same book “Psychology and Space” presents the results of a comparison by American specialists of the reliability of the operation of on-board systems of spacecraft with and without the participation of an operator. In one case, the operator had to make a decision on how to control the ship upon receiving signals from the instruments. In the remaining experiments, only automatic machines operated, and the reliability of the equipment, as usual, was increased by amending the circuit elements. Four systems were examined: with double, triple, four- and five-fold showering.

At first, the reliability of all five systems was equal. But already on the fourth day of the simulated flight there was a difference. After two weeks, the reliability of the system with two-, three-, and four-fold showering of elements became unsatisfactory. With fivefold showering, it’s also not high enough. At the same time, the system with human participation has not lost its reliability.

As an example confirming what has been said, we can recall the flight in lunar orbit of the Apollo 10 spacecraft. When two astronauts were reflecting maneuvers on the lunar cabin, which had by that time separated from the main block, its unexpected rotation began. At some point, the astronaut even thought they were falling on the Moon. Only the courage and reaction of the ship's commander T. Stafford made it possible to avoid the fall. Switching the controls to manual, he stabilized the ship.

Despite all the difficulties, the cosmonauts completed the assembly and moved from the spacecraft to the station. What they saw inside allowed them to return to Earth without delay.
