AT WHAT SPEED
IS A GREEN LEAF GROWING?

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. VEDENOV,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
O. IVANOV

You probably noticed in the spring how quickly the waxed and sown field is covered with new greenery. For some time it is black or brown, but then small green sparks of sprouts appear, and right before our eyes, in one or two weeks the field is covered with almost continuous green grass (all this will happen, of course, provided that all the sown seeds have sprouted, the ground is sufficiently moist and well fertilized and the days are sunny). It is interesting that with such rapid growth of plants over fairly large intervals of time, it is possible to replace the change in the amount of greenery in the field at small equal intervals, practically every day. Isn't this strange? After all, it would seem that doing this should become increasingly difficult - the more greenery there is in the field, the more difficult it is to replace changes in its quantity.

Let's try to figure out under what law of plant growth such observations arose. Let's define the amount of greenery in the field as the total area of ​​the leaves of the plants (we consider the beginning of growth, when the leaves almost do not overlap). We will assume that this value is proportional to the average current leaf area of ​​one plant (we assume that all plants emerged at the same time and are in absolutely identical conditions).

Let's use the well-known physiological Weber-Fechner law. This law says that all human senses (hearing, touch, vision) will perceive a change in external influence $\Delta B$ (sound strength, pressure, light brightness) only when the relative change $\Delta B/B$ is greater than the defining threshold value -

$$\delta = \frac{\Delta B}{B} > \delta_{\pi},$$

and $\delta_{\pi}$ does not depend on the magnitude of the external influence $B$. If $\Delta B/B < \delta_{\pi}$, then changes in human influence are not perceived at all. Let's give a simple example. If you put a load $P_0$ weighing several grams on your palm, and then begin to change the mass of the load, then you can confidently say that the load has become larger or smaller only when the relative change in the weight of the load - $|P - P_0|/P_0$ - reaches one third. In this example, the impact threshold of the force change $\delta_{\pi}^P$ is 1/3.

For visual perception, the threshold can be determined in this way. Let's make a set of identical geometric shapes, for example squares, of different sizes and show one square to the subject. Then we will show him other squares, slightly different in size from the first. Only when the relative change in the length of the sides of the square $|a - a_0|/a_0$ exceeds the threshold value $\delta_{\pi}^a$ can the subject answer whether the square is larger or smaller

*) The law names German scientists of the past century, physiology and anatomist E. Weber (1795-1878) and physicist (as well as psychology, physiology and psychology-satirist) G. Fechner (1801-1887). Weber is engaged in an experimental study of ocular sensations and the eye, and Fechner summarized the experimental facts obtained by Weber and other psychologists and physiologists in a unified mathematical formulation.
