Was there any basis for such fears? Judge for yourself. During numerous experiments simulating the conditions of space flight, some subjects developed mental disorders such as hallucinations, loss of orientation, and a feeling of fear.

In the book by Yu. Gagarin and V. Lebedev “Psychology and Space” they describe the reactions of subjects in conditions of loneliness. “The situation itself seemed harmless. But one very qualified pilot felt dizzy, although the camera did not move from its place. Another began to imagine some unfamiliar faces among the control panel instruments. The third, also a pilot by profession, when the “flight” was coming to an end, before his eyes, the instrument panel suddenly began to “melt and drip onto the floor.” The fourth wanted more because of the blurry image on the TV screen, although the screen was perfectly clear. In vain they tried to convince him that nothing had happened - he demanded an immediate end to the experiment and, when he left the chamber, stated that, in addition to visual illusions, he felt the walls of the room closing above him.》 The authors of the book cite many such cases. But what about everyday life when, in addition to loneliness and isolation, one comes to experience weightlessness, sensory hunger, changes in spatial and temporal structure, limitation of personally familiar information, and, finally, a threat to life?...

But, the reader will say, Gagarin survived, just as other cosmonauts survived. And none of them went crazy, did not let down those who sent them into flight... Here is the time to give the second part of the quote from V. Shatalov’s statement: “... but also with their strength - intelligence, ingenuity, courage, with their highest feeling of responsibility for themselves, for their comrades, for the assigned work. At any moment of his stay in space, an accident may happen in such a way that only he, the space priest, will be able to make and implement the only correct decision. For Space is the sum of what is not fully known, it is always a risk, it is work at the upper limit of human capabilities...”

It is a person who makes a car reliable, capable of getting out of a difficult, and sometimes even an emergency, situation. The same book “Psychology and Space” presents the results of a comparison by American specialists of the reliability of the operation of on-board systems of spacecraft with and without the participation of an operator. In one case, the operator had to make a decision on how to control the ship upon receiving signals from the instruments. In the remaining experiments, only automatic machines operated, and the reliability of the equipment, as usual, was increased by amending the circuit elements. Four systems were examined: with double, triple, four- and five-fold showering.

At first, the reliability of all five systems was equal. But already on the fourth day of the simulated flight there was a difference. After two weeks, the reliability of the system with two-, three-, and four-fold showering of elements became unsatisfactory. With fivefold showering, it’s also not high enough. At the same time, the system with human participation has not lost its reliability.

As an example confirming what has been said, we can recall the flight in lunar orbit of the Apollo 10 spacecraft. When two astronauts were reflecting maneuvers on the lunar cabin, which had by that time separated from the main block, its unexpected rotation began. At some point, the astronaut even thought they were falling on the Moon. Only the courage and reaction of the ship's commander T. Stafford made it possible to avoid the fall. Switching the controls to manual, he stabilized the ship.
