Exercise 2. Form calculation 1 in terms of points and arrows.

The idea of ​​cyclicity can be formatted not only in the language of dots and arrows. Another way is in function language:

Periodicity theorem. Let M be a finite set (set of states). Let f be a function from a set M to a set M. Then for all x from M the sequence of states
x, f(x), f(f(x)), f(f(f(x))), ...
starting from a certain moment, it becomes periodic.

In addition, if for different x and y the states f(x) and f(y) are also different (this is the “reverse motion” condition! - why?), then the period begins from the first term.

Exercise
3. Prove the periodicity theorem.
4. Prove that the sum of the length of the period and pre-period in the sequence from the periodicity theorem does not exceed the number of elements of the set of states.

The question arises: calculations 1 and 2 look quite convincing. Then format them, and in two more ways? Then pursue rigor when everything is already clear?

In our case, it’s not a matter of rigor: different formatting methods make it possible to look at calculation from different angles, which allows you to apply it in different situations. Let's give two examples.

Exercise 5. In some companies, each person has exactly two friends. Prove that if all friends join hands, they form one or more choirs. (The calculation with dots and arrows is suitable here.)

Problem 6. Some combination of repeated edges brought the Rubik's cube out of the correct position. Prove that the cube can be solved again by repeating this combination several more times. (The periodicity theorem applies here.)

To solve problem 6, it is enough to look at the original combination of repetitions of the cube as a function that transforms one of its states into another. Since all repetitions can be carried out in reverse order, each state uniquely determines the previous one. Make sure for yourself that different states transform into different ones, and then apply the periodicity theorem. Both ways of formatting identity are often encountered: arrows - in the theory of finite state machines, functions - in the theory of dynamical systems.

We have become acquainted only with some applications of identity, and with the “discrete” case when the state number is finite. But even in the “non-periodic” case, with an infinite number of states, periodicity or, more accurately, “almost periodicity” will often arise, when states are not repeated absolutely exactly, but only approximately. So, every summer is both similar and different from the previous one.
