Modern physics began (in the 17th century) with Newton's formulation of the fundamental equations of mechanics. In the 19th century, Maxwell formulated the equations of electromagnetism. Tracing the path of the basic equations, it is necessary to name A. Einstein, N. Bohr, E. Schrödinger, W. Heisenberg and P. A. M. Dirac. Einstein formed the equations of mechanics that made it possible to study movements at speeds close to the speed of light, and he built a theory of gravity. Bohr, Schrödinger, Heisenberg and Dirac created quantum (or wave) mechanics.*)

The discovery and formation of new equations does not exhaust the activity of a theoretical physicist. Moreover, the overwhelming majority of theoretical physicists do not pretend to change the existing basic equations (read: basic concepts), but are content with solving the problem based on these equations. The verb “to be content” does not carry any destructive meaning.

Problems in theoretical physics arise in two ways.

First way. An experiment discovers something that is called a new phenomenon or property. The choice of word (phenomenon, property) depends on the assessment of the importance of the discovered phenomenon, which independently gives a complete interpretation of what he discovered. But more often, even knowing in general terms what caused his discovery of the phenomenon, he cannot, using known equations (representations), calculate the necessary explanations

It is possible that even with a detailed and detailed study of the discovered phenomenon, its nature does not become clearer. Finding out the nature of a phenomenon, that is, finding out which of the basic equations physicists should use to explain it, is one of the most important problems solved by theoretical physicists. It happens that many years pass from the emergence of a problem to its solution, and all this time the experiment adds new information about an open phenomenon, and the theory, as it develops, secretly prepares itself for solving the problem. Superconductivity was discovered in 1911 and explained in 1956. It took forty-five years to clarify the nature of this amazing phenomenon. Moreover, the explanation did not affect the basic equations of physics. It was found within the limits of existing ideas.

A special place among these problems is occupied by problems whose solutions improve the mathematical apparatus of the theory.
