Let's draw the diagonals of the rhombus. One of them will pass through the point $O$, since the vertices of equilateral triangles $DOB, DAB$ and $DCB$, which have a common base $BD$, lie on the same straight line. Let's put $OA = r$, $OC = \rho$. From triangle $OBM$ we have
$$BM^2 = m^2 - OM^2.$$ (2)

From triangle $BCM$
$$BM^2 = n^2 - CM^2.$$ (3)

Subtracting (3) from (2), we get
$$m^2 - n^2 = OM^2 - CM^2 = (OM + CM)(OM - CM) = OC \cdot OA,$$
or
$$\rho \cdot r = m^2 - n^2.$$
So, in the Poselye inverter the product
$$\rho \cdot r = OC \cdot OA$$
remains constant for any changes in $OC$ and $OA$, which is what needed to be proven.

Consequently, if we make the point $O$ of such an inverter motionless and move the point $A$ along some curve, then the point $C$ will cover the image of this curve during inversion. In particular, if point $A$ moves in a circle passing through the inversion stripes, then point $C$ will move in a straight line. Note that in 1872, the inverter described above was invented independently of Posylya by Chebyshev’s student Lipkin, a student at St. Petersburg University.

**Garth Inverter**

Soon after the appearance of the Posely inverter, the English mathematician and mechanic G. Hart built an inverter from an antiparallelogram. An antiparallelogram is a quadrilateral $ABCD$ (Fig. 12) in which opposite sides are equal and sides $AB$ and $CD$ intersect each other. The fact that a hinged antiparallelogram implements inversion follows from the following two theorems.

Theorem 4. The product of the diagonals $DB$ and $AC$ of the antiparallelogram (Fig. 13) is a constant.
