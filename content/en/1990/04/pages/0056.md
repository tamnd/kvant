There is no need to list all the accidents that occurred with Soviet and American cosmonauts before the tragic death of the Challenger reusable spacecraft (in 1986). Let us note only two accidents with the Soyuz launch vehicle. In the first case (1974), as a result of the failure of the third stage engines, the ship (with cosmonauts V. Lazarev and O. Makarov) landed along the entire circular trajectory in the area of ​​the Amur River. In this case, the overload reached 20 or more units.

In the second case (1983), the accident occurred at the launch pad. However, two seconds saved cosmonauts V. Titov and G. Strekalov from death, but the emergency rescue system (SAS) managed to level the ship, which literally rescued the ship with the cosmonauts from the exploding carrier.

The lack of SAS on the reusable Space Shuttle cost the lives of seven American astronauts during the ill-fated Challenger launch.

If we scrupulously count all the small and large accidents that have occurred over the almost thirty-year history of man's exploration of outer space, then the figures cited by American experts will not seem exaggerated.

(Unfortunately, not only the road to the stars is covered with thorns, but also the preparations for it. Cosmonauts died at various stages of preparation for space flight. Among them was V. Bondarenko, who was part of the first “Gagarin” detachment. The cause of his death was a fire that occurred during tests in the trial pressure chamber in 1961, shortly before Yu. Gagarin’s flight. The last on the tragic list is the name of the American astronaut S. Grigg, who died last year in a training flight.)

Until now, the discussion has been about only one side participating, or, more correctly, implementing space flight—the package-space technical. But there is also a second one - the man himself. “An astronaut is not an insensitive phantom, not a doll installed in a complex spacecraft. He is a living person with his own weaknesses...” Let’s quote first in this place so that we can return to it later. Under these words is the signature of a person who knows the profession of an astronaut well: V. Shatalov commanded crews three times, carried out dockings with the Soyuz spacecraft and the Salyut station.

Spacecraft designers, system developers, doctors and psychologists pay great attention to ensuring such space flight conditions under which not only will no harm be done to the astronauts’ body, but also the human dignity that is absent in a machine will be fully realized.

Now, when the maximum duration of a flight in space orbit is 326 days (Yu. Romanenko), the fears that tormented those who prepared Yuri Gagarin’s first flight may seem frivolous.
