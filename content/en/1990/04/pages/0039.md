F1204. Slight elasticity
a block of mass m can act without friction inside a rectangular
obki of the same mass. The box is on a table covered with a thin layer of oil (see figure). The friction force of the box on the table depends only on the speed v of the box’s movement on the table and is equal to F=—γv. At the initial moment of time, the box is at rest, and the block is located at its left wall and has a speed v₀ directed to the right. How many impacts will the block make on the block if the length of the block L is much greater than the size of the block?

With each elastic collision of a block and a block, they exchange velocities. So, after the first collision, the block will remain and the block will move to the right, after the second - vice versa: the block will remain and the block will move to the right, but at the same time the speed of the block v₁ will be less than v₀. Thus, when the box moves a distance L, 2 impacts are generated. Let's find the full path s taken by the box.

Note that the speed of movement of the box immediately after the block stops is each time exactly equal to its speed before stopping, since the block moves without friction. Therefore, we can exclude from consideration the time intervals when the box is standing and consider its movement continuous. The equation of motion has the form

$$\frac{m\Delta v}{\Delta t} = -\gamma v,$$

where

$$m\Delta v = -\gamma \Delta s$$ and $$mv_0 = \gamma s.$$

Therefore, the number of impacts of the block on the box

$$n = 2\frac{s}{L} = \frac{2mv_0}{\gamma L}.$$

D. Grigoriev

F1205. All other things being equal, in what noise?
Would there be more heat loss due to radiation - in white or black?

The question of the problem essentially boils down to the following: which body - white or black - emits more strongly at the same temperature?

Let us mentally place both bodies in a heat-insulated cavity with ideally reflective walls. It would seem that a black body, absorbing radiation incident on it much more efficiently than a white one, should heat up, and the white body should cool down. In other words, a spontaneous transition of heat from a white cold body to a hot one should occur. This, however, contradicts the second law of thermodynamics. How is this paradox resolved?

The answer is that a black body not only absorbs more strongly than a white body, but also emits more strongly. And then no temperature difference between the black and white bodies will appear in the cavity, and a contradiction with the laws of thermodynamics will not arise.

So, since a black body radiates stronger than a white one, a white fur coat will be warmer. Let us note, by the way, that good radiators intended to radiate heat are best painted black.

O. Nikshina
