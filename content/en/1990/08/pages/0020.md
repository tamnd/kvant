This can be seen from equation (4), which can be rewritten as

$$\frac{p}{T} = \frac{p_0}{T_0}.$$ (5)

Relation (5) shows that at constant volume the ratio of gas pressure to its absolute temperature is a constant value. On the other hand, gas pressure, as we have seen, is determined by the formula (see (1))

$$p = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} \bar{E}.$$

Substituting this value $p$ into expression (5), we obtain

$$T = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} \frac{T_0}{p_0} \bar{E}.$$ (6)

Equation (6) refers to a gas in a closed vessel of constant volume. Therefore, the number $N$ of gas molecules remains constant; the relation $\frac{T_0}{p_0}$, as we have seen, is also constant. Consequently, the coefficient for $\bar{E}$ in formula (6) is a constant value for any gas, i.e.

$$T = \frac{2}{3} A \bar{E},$$ (7)

where $A = \frac{N}{V} \frac{T_0}{p_0}$ is a constant. This means that the absolute temperature of a gas is the same as the average conceptual energy of the otic motion of one of its molecules, only expressed not in joules, but in kelvins. the coefficient $\frac{2}{3} A$ is a conversion factor showing how many times 1 is greater than 1 J/molecule. Likewise, the same length can be expressed in meters and inches. You just need to know that 1 m = 40 inches. coefficient 40 is a conversion factor showing how many times 1 meter is greater than 1 inch.

Generally, formula (7) is written in the form

$$\bar{E} = \frac{3}{2} kT,$$ (8)

Where

$$k = \frac{1}{A} = \frac{V}{N} \frac{p_0}{T_0}.$$ (9)

The coefficient $k$ is called Boltzmann's constant.

From formula (9) it is clear how to obtain the value of Boltzmann’s constant from experiment. To do this, you need to fill a vessel of known volume $V$ with a known mass $M$ of gas (the mass of the gas can be determined by weighing). Then place the vessel in such ice (its temperature is $T_0 = 273,15$ K), change the pressure $p_0$ of the gas using a manometer. Given the mass $M$ of the gas, it is easy to determine the value $N$. Indeed, if the molar mass of a gas is $\mu$, then the number of moles of gas is $M/\mu$; and since there are $N_A$ molecules in each mole of gas ($N_A$ is Avogadro’s number), then the number of $N$ molecules in a mass $M$ of gas is $N = \frac{M}{\mu} N_A$. So, given the mass of the gas $M$, its molar mass $\mu$, the volume of the vessel $V$ and the gas pressure $p_0$ at a temperature $T_0$, we can determine the value of the Boltzmann constant $k$.

This kind of change (and many other things) continued repeatedly. They all give the value for Boltzmann's constant

$$k = 1,38 \cdot 10^{-23} \text{ Дж/K}.$$

As we can see, the value of $k$ is very small. This means that the average conceptual energy of free movements of one molecule, which determines what we call temperature, is extremely small. At a temperature of 1 K, the average conceptual energy of a molecule $\bar{E}$ is

$$\frac{m v^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2} \cdot 1,38 \cdot 10^{-23} \approx 2 \cdot 10^{-23} \text{ Дж/mолекул}.$$

This is the relationship between kelvin and joule per molecule.
