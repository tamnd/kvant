The obvious conclusion is that something passes from a hot body to a cold one (with the same right one could assume that something passes from cold to hot!). Galileo believed that this “something” was a special thermal substance. And most researchers of the 17th-18th centuries adhered to the same point of view and called this substance caloric.

According to the theory based on the concept of caloric, a hot body differs from a cold one in that it contains more caloric than a cold one. The establishment of thermal equilibrium according to these ideas consists in the fact that caloric moves from a hot body to a cold one. This means that every body consists, as it were, of two substances - the substance of the body itself (water, copper, iron, glass) and caloric. Every body is a mixture of the substance of the body and the substance of heat (teloride). The word “temperature” just means mixture. And for almost a hundred and fifty years it was believed that when we measure temperature, we measure the concentration of caloric in the body. Hence the name of the unit of temperature - degree. It was in these units that, for example, the water concentration of rasters was measured.

This view of temperature lasted for a very long time - until the end of the 18th century. That's what they said - degrees of warmth.

But simultaneously with the “material” theory of heat, there was another theory, one of the creators and advocates of which was the great Russian scientist M.V. Lomonsov. This theory was based on the fact that heating of the body can be caused by movement. Lomonsov wrote: “It is very well known that heat arose for movement: from mutual friction, hands corrode, wood catches fire; when flint hits flint, sparks appear; iron heats up from being forged in parts and by strong blows...” From here the conclusion was drawn that heat is not a substance, but the movement of small parts that make up all bodies (“insensitive parts,” as they were then called).

For over two hundred years there was a struggle between these two theories. For a long time the first theory prevailed, but in the end the second prevailed.

Already in the 18th century, experiments were carried out that forced many physicists to change the idea that temperature is the concentration of heat (teloride) in a body.

In 1760, the English physicist and physician Black showed that the same amount of heat supplied to equal masses of different substances leads to different changes in temperature. But if temperature really represented the concentration of heat in a body, then the receipt of the same amount of heat should cause equal masses of any substances to cause the same temperature change. In these experiments, Black discovered, as we now know, that equal substances have different temperatures. But this is incompatible with the caloric theory.

In 1764, the same Black showed that when ice melts, it absorbs a significant amount of heat, but its temperature remains unchanged. This perfect Black heat is often called the latent heat of fusion. In the same way, when water hardens, heat is released and again - without a change in temperature. It is clear that if temperature is the concentration of heat in a body, then it is impossible to absorb heat without increasing the temperature and it is impossible to lose heat from the body without lowering its temperature.

What exactly is temperature, a quantity whose meaning has remained unclear for so long?
