Two finger combination
On the second page of the cover of the cobley issue (Kvant, 1990, No. 1), dedicated to the twentieth anniversary of the magazine, Max Ernst’s painting “Au premier mot limpide” is reproduced. The short accompanying text placed there, unfortunately, misdirects the reader. Here I want to give a different interpretation of the painting and show that its main motive is the Banach-Tarski paradox. Before moving on to the rationale for this test, a few words about the paradox itself.

In 1924, Polish mathematicians Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski proved the following statement:

any ball can be divided into a finite number of parts, from which, without overlaps and voids, two balls of the same radius can be made.

This is the ultimate, amazing paradox. This statement is a paradox on a mathematical, physical, and purely everyday level.

Nevertheless, I repeat once again - this is a strictly proven theorem. By now, the number of parts into which the ball is broken has been reduced to a heel. One ball is made from two parts, and one more from the remaining three - both are equal to the original one. The evidence itself has also become more accessible. I think a fairly real high school student can figure it out. You, of course, understand that the parts into which the ball breaks in the Banach-Tarski paradox are arranged and mixed up in a monstrously complex way. They refer to those obtained from nothing (In the World of Science, 1989, No. 6).

And now, refuting what has just been said, we will present ourselves to the practical doubling of a real ball. It is better if it is a solid ball with a diameter of one or two centimeters. A ball rolled from plasticine will also work. Let's return to the painting by Max Ernst, namely to its fragment.

Place the ball on a horizontal surface. It’s best to have one that allows the catalog ball to slide over it, but not to slide. Cross your index and middle fingers together, as in the picture, and place the crossed fingers with the balls of your feet on the ball. Close your eyes and roll the ball lightly -

You will be mistaken that the ball has doubled.

Unfortunately, when you open your eyes and remove your fingers -

two balls turn into one.

And yet, the spirit of the Banach-Tarski paradox is undeniably present here. Of course, our analysis does not exhaust the entire content of Max Ernst’s painting, but I think that the main thing is captured. By the way, the painting dates back to 1923, the Banach-Tarski paradox was published in 1924 - a significant coincidence!

A. Panov
