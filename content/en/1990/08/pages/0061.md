There is approximately as much acid gas bound in the sedimentary rocks of the Earth as is contained in the atmosphere of Venus. As a result of volcanic activity, large amounts of water vapor and carbon dioxide are released into the Earth's atmosphere. Assuming that the initial temperature of Venus was not very high, a significant amount of carbon dioxide should have combined in the presence of a small amount of water vapor and formed carbonates. At the same time, part of the carbon dioxide accumulates in the atmosphere containing a small amount of water vapor. This created a strong greenhouse effect, which led to heating of the atmosphere and surface. The essence of the greenhouse effect lies in the fact that solar radiation quite easily penetrates into the deep layers of the atmosphere and is absorbed by them and the surface of the planet. But since the planet is in thermal equilibrium (on average a constant temperature), the same amount of energy must be radiated into space. However, the radiation occurs in the ultraviolet, infrared range, where the carbon dioxide atmosphere with admixtures of water vapor is not very transparent. To reach from the atmosphere under such conditions, the source must be very bright. This is why the surface temperature of Venus is so high. Due to the planet's proximity to the Sun, the greenhouse effect could become self-reinforcing: with increasing temperature, the formation of carbonates slows down, more carbon

acid gas remains in the atmosphere, which leads to further acceleration of the greenhouse effect. Then a limit is reached when carbonates in sedimentary rocks are destroyed and the weight of carbon dioxide is released into the atmosphere.

Along with the hypothesis of the extractive dryness of Venus, there is one (as yet unconfirmed) fact that presents the problem in a completely different light. We are talking about a significantly increased concentration of action in hydrogen-containing compounds on Venus. This could be a consequence of catastrophic losses of planetary water in the past, and at first oceans could even exist on Venus. However, we must remember that for now this is just one of the latest hypotheses about the tantalizing history of the table-like “sister” of our Earth. If the hypothesis is confirmed, then one explanation for Venus's loss of oceans may be that life did not originate on Venus. Then, to assess the probability of the emergence of life, we will have not one, but two planets. As for the current physical conditions on Venus, no life form known to us could withstand them.

Note that the physics of high-temperature organic polymers does not seem to explain the absolute ban on the creation of materials suitable for the construction of certain organisms of living beings. But we need some special sources of energy that can be used by living organisms in the oxygen-free atmosphere of Venus.
