If we could send the ball with a key without spinning, like from a catapult, then its behavior would be pedagogically correct, but uninteresting: in the absence of wind, its trajectory would lie in a vertical plane, deviating neither to the right nor to the left, and it would fall to the ground after a relatively short flight.

Fortunately, it is only in rare cases that a golf ball does not have spin when it comes off the key, and it is the spin that gives interest, variety and liveliness to the flight of the ball. It is the spin that explains the deflection of the ball to the right or left, it is the spin that makes the ball soar over the field, or dive, or make furious zigzags, giving rise to the legend of his artistic temperament; he performs these eccentricities like an acrobat, doing one or two somersaults for fun. In reality, however, the ball is the most industrial object, knowing only one rule of behavior, which it obeys with stupid conscientiousness: it always follows its “nose” (front part). This rule is the key to understanding the behavior of all balls in the air, whether they are golf balls, baseball balls, cricket balls or tennis balls. Now, before moving on to considering the essence of this rule, let us trace some of its consequences...

Fig. 1.

Let's imagine that the ball is spinning around a horizontal axis, and assume that its speed is directed horizontally, as at figure 1, a. Then the “nose” of ball A moves up, and since according to our rule the ball

tries to follow its “nose”, it will rise, the flight path will be curved, as shown by the dotted line. If the ball was spun in the opposite direction (Fig. 1, 6), its "nose" A would move down, and the ball following point A would "dive" down.

Now suppose that the ball is spinning along the horizontal axis, as shown in figure 2, 6. Then, if we look along the direction of its flight, we see that the “nose” A is moving to the right, and therefore, according to our rule, the ball too

Fig. 2.

will move to the right along the path shown by the dotted line; in fact, the ball will behave as if the ball was twisted to the right. In reality, a ball spinning to the right is exactly how it rotates around the horizontal axis. If the ball is rotated around the horizontal axis in the opposite direction (Fig. 2, a), then it will fly to the right. This will be a "left twist".

In the case when the axis of rotation coincides with the direction of flight and passes through the “nose” of the ball, it, following our rule, will move without deviation...

Vivid examples of the influence of rotation on the flight of the ball are given by the game of belisball; An experienced pitcher can, by imparting the necessary spin, make the trajectory of the ball curve to the right or left, up or down. In order for the ball to deflect to the sides, the rotation must be around the horizontal axis, while for deflection up or down, it must be around the horizontal axis.

A tennis player uses this effect when he overhands the ball, i.e., hits the top of the ball
