Problem 2. To what depth should a thin-walled glass be cut into water and turned upside down so that it is tired? The mass of the glass is $m = 100$ g, its volume is $V = 200$ ml, atmospheric pressure is $p_0 = 10^5$ Pa.

We will assume that the glass is immersed very slowly. In this case, both the glass and the water filling it will be in equilibrium at each moment of time. Obviously, for this, a force will have to be applied to the glass, balancing the sum of the Archimedean force and the gravity force. Near the surface of the water, this force should be directed downward, since from the data in the conditions of the problem it follows that the mass of water in the volume of the glass is greater than the mass of the glass itself, and, therefore, the Archimedes force at the initial moment is greater than the gravity force. As you dive, the buoyant force will decrease - it is proportional to the volume of the required impact in the glass, and it decreases, since the impact compresses all impacts from the water. We need to find a depth at which the Archimedean force is equal in magnitude to the gravity force. When the glass is immersed to a slightly greater depth, it will tire without outside help.

Now we carry out the corresponding calculations. The equilibrium condition for a glass in water has the form (Fig. 2)

$$mg = F_A, \text{ где } F_A = \rho gV_1.$$

The volume $V_1$ of the locked impact will be found from the Boyle-Mariotte law (equations of the manufacturing process), since the temperature can be considered constant (this is implied in the conditions of the problem, although it was not explicitly stated):

$$p_0V = (p_0 + \rho gh)V_1,$$

where $h$ is the desired depth at which the glass is immersed, $\rho$ is the density of water.

Putting all the equations together, we get

$$mg = \frac{p_0\rho gV}{p_0 + \rho gh},$$

where

$$h = \frac{p_0}{\rho g} \left( \frac{\rho V}{m} - 1 \right) = 10 \text{ м}.$$

Note, by the way, that the pressure of a water column 10 m high exactly corresponds to normal atmospheric pressure, equal to approximately $10^5$ Pa.
