Mechanical model of the laser
We stretch a piece of rubber film from the impact ball onto a glass jar. Let's push the film so that some air comes out from under it and it is driven inside. Secure the film on the jar with a rubber ring. Now let's take a steel ball and release it from a small height in the center of the film. The ball will bounce for a very long time, up to 50 seconds. If the initial height $H$ from which the ball falls is quite small, then all collisions occur at close to the center of the film. However, if $H$ is greater than a certain critical height $H_{кр}$, then the motion of the ball becomes unstable - after two or three collisions it jumps out of the film. Using the perfectly elastic collision model (see the article “Burping Ball and Impact Theory”), we can calculate that $H_{кр}$ is the height of the optical focus of the film surface.

Let us now imagine that we are in zero-gravity conditions, for example on board a spaceship. Then you can place two such jars opposite each other and make the ball move between them. This mechanical device very accurately models the operation of a laser resonant in which the light flux moves between two, for example, spherical mirrors with radii of curvature $R_1$ and $R_2$, $R_1 < R_2$. Here, as in the first experiment, the question arises about the stability of the motion of the ball between two reflective surfaces. In optics, a similar problem is known as the laser resonant stability problem. The answer is quite unexpected. It turns out that when the films are quite close to each other, the ball moves steadily in the vicinity of the segment connecting their centers. As the distance increases, a zone of instability appears - after several collisions, the balls are thrown out of the space between the films. However, surprisingly, with a further increase in distance, a zone of instability appears again. And only when the equations of reflection from each other are reached, instability arises again.
