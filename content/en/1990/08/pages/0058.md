Problem 5 (also by S. Loyd). A certain lady, handing the postal clerk one dollar, said:
“Give me two-cent marks, ten times as many one-cent marks, and for the rest – five-cent marks.”
How did the employee manage to complete this rather puzzling task?
You can do this: let’s denote by x the number of two-cent marks, by y the number of five-cent marks, and from the condition we get that
$$2x+10x+5y=100,$$ or
$$12x+5y=100.$$
Diophantus, the ancient Greek mathematician mentioned earlier, continued his enormous work on finding integer solutions to equations of this type. Among them, they came to be called the Diophantine equation. So, it remains to solve the Diophantine equation

$$12x+5y=100.$$
But we can do without it. Note that the total value of two-cent stamps, and therefore their number, must be divided by 5 (why?). There are 10 times more one-zent stamps, therefore, their number is divided by 50, which is exactly 50 pieces worth 50 cents. Then there are 5 two-cent stamps worth 10 cents; the remaining stamps worth 40 cents are five-cent stamps (there will be 8 of them). Here our solution turned out to be absolutely correct, although we only tried to guess it, without guarantees it was correct, as in the previous problem. (By the way, the problem has one more - “degenerate” - solution: zero one and two-cent marks and 20 five-cent marks.)
