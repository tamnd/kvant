In the sense that interests us (i.e., suitability for life), all the satellites of Jupiter and Saturn are devoid of atmosphere; more precisely, everything except Titan, a satellite of Saturn (almost the size of the planet Mercury), and Neptune’s satellite Triton. Perhaps the last hopes of meeting life in the solar system were associated with Titan. It was already known from ground-based observations that Titan has a dense atmosphere. It was assumed that a strong periqua effect arose in it, thanks to which conditions at the surface could even be acceptable for the existence of life. After the Martian disappointments, there was a lot of talk about the possibility of life on Titan. But Titan did not live up to these hopes - it is one of the coldest worlds in the solar system. Television footage from Voyager showed that the surface of the satellite was indistinguishable through its thick orange atmosphere, consisting mainly of nitrogen. There is no wig effect in the atmosphere; the surface is colder than the atmosphere, its temperature is 93 K. Titan is a world of deep cold.

The two most distant giants, Uranus and Neptune, are even colder bodies than Titan. It is possible, however, that at the bottom of their only hydrogen-helium atmosphere there is a surface that is under conditions of extremely high pressure and temperature.

After receiving new data about the satellites of the giant planets, scientists were attracted by the possibility of the existence of a huge, possibly global, ocean under the thick icy crust of Jupiter’s satellite Europa. Europa is close in size to our Moon and has some sources of internal energy (related to tidal phenomena). Therefore, it has even been suggested that life may exist in this ocean. Critics, however, point out that such an ocean is tightly “sealed” by an icy shell and that the thermal energy of the interior alone is not enough for the origin of life.

The last planet in the solar system is Pluto. It is an icy body, similar to the large moons of Jupiter and Saturn, with a very thin methane atmosphere. It was recently discovered that Pluto, not one, but two small planets, orbits its center with a period of 6.4 days. The surface temperature of Pluto is 40-50 K.

Thus, a very brief review of the physical conditions on the planets of the Solar System shows that, apart from Earth, only the planet Mars, with some stretch, can presumably be considered natural for life. The climate of Mars is close to winter Antarctica, but microorganisms exist in Antarctic conditions...

Limitations for life

The concept of a “planet natural for life” includes many restrictions that could be seen in the example of the solar system. These are, for example, the composition of the planet’s surface, the presence of an atmosphere, water and temperature conditions favorable for the emergence of the only form of life known to us - protein-nucleic acid.

Another important factor is the suitable rotation period of the planet. So, too long a night, it is believed, would hinder the development of plants using photosynthesis. This is Venus, where the duration of a solar day is 117 Earth days. On the other hand, a planet may have a suitable rotation period, but the angle between the equatorial and orbital planes is too large, like, say, Uranus.
