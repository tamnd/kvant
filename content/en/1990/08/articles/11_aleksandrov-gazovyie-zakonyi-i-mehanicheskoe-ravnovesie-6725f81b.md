Gas laws and mechanical equilibrium

D. ALEXANDROV

It is customary to develop a school physics course on individual topics, for example, “Fundamentals of Dynamics,” “Gas Laws,” “Electrostatics,” etc. Matching and physics problems are usually subject to the same classification. Is this good or not?

With a certain amount of experience, such a “distribution” can help the applicant, even after a quick acquaintance with the conditions of the problem, to estimate which physical laws will be useful for solving it. However, often in entrance exams there are problems that are difficult to attribute to any specific topics. This can cause certain difficulties, as a result of which a generally simple task will not be solved.

In this article we will consider a range of tasks that at first glance could be called “gas”. In fact, when dealing with their solutions, in addition to the gas laws, you also come across other laws, primarily the laws of mechanical equilibrium.

Task 1. A balloon taken from a warm room ($t_1 = +27^\circ C$) into a cold room ($t_2 = -23^\circ C$) floats freely in the air for some time. Determine the mass of the rubber shell of the ball. This is the diameter $d = 40$ cm, molar mass of air $M = 29 \text{ г/mоль}$, atmospheric pressure $p_0 = 10^5 \text{ Pa}$. The elasticity of the shell can be claimed.

In appearance, this is a general problem based on gas laws. This impression is realized under conditions under conditions - except in the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation (the equation of state of an ideal gas), there is nowhere else to substitute them. On the other hand, it is very surprising that the mass of the shell cannot be found from this equation. How is life?

Let's read the conditions of the problem again, more carefully; Sometimes the most valuable information is found not in numbers, but in words. Let's pay attention to two phrases: “The balloon... floats freely in the air” and “The elasticity of the shell can be claimed.” In the first sentence, the condition of mechanical equilibrium of the ball is stated: the force of the gravity of the rubber shell and the warm air inside the ball is balanced by the buoyant force (Archimedes' force). According to Archimedes' law, this force is equal to the weight of the liquid or gas in the volume of the body immersed in it. The second phrase means equal air pressure inside the ball and outside.

So, let’s write down the claim for the entire equilibrium condition of the ball (Fig. 1):

$$m_0 g + m_1 g = m_2 g,$$

where $m_0$ is the desired mass of the rubber shell, $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of warm

Problem 2. To what depth should a thin-walled glass be cut into water and turned upside down so that it is tired? The mass of the glass is $m = 100$ g, its volume is $V = 200$ ml, atmospheric pressure is $p_0 = 10^5$ Pa.

We will assume that the glass is immersed very slowly. In this case, both the glass and the water filling it will be in equilibrium at each moment of time. Obviously, for this, a force will have to be applied to the glass, balancing the sum of the Archimedean force and the gravity force. Near the surface of the water, this force should be directed downward, since from the data in the conditions of the problem it follows that the mass of water in the volume of the glass is greater than the mass of the glass itself, and, therefore, the Archimedes force at the initial moment is greater than the gravity force. As you dive, the buoyant force will decrease - it is proportional to the volume of the required impact in the glass, and it decreases, since the impact compresses all impacts from the water. We need to find a depth at which the Archimedean force is equal in magnitude to the gravity force. When the glass is immersed to a slightly greater depth, it will tire without outside help.

Now we carry out the corresponding calculations. The equilibrium condition for a glass in water has the form (Fig. 2)

$$mg = F_A, \text{ где } F_A = \rho gV_1.$$

The volume $V_1$ of the locked impact will be found from the Boyle-Mariotte law (equations of the manufacturing process), since the temperature can be considered constant (this is implied in the conditions of the problem, although it was not explicitly stated):

$$p_0V = (p_0 + \rho gh)V_1,$$

where $h$ is the desired depth at which the glass is immersed, $\rho$ is the density of water.

Putting all the equations together, we get

$$mg = \frac{p_0\rho gV}{p_0 + \rho gh},$$

where

$$h = \frac{p_0}{\rho g} \left( \frac{\rho V}{m} - 1 \right) = 10 \text{ м}.$$

Note, by the way, that the pressure of a water column 10 m high exactly corresponds to normal atmospheric pressure, equal to approximately $10^5$ Pa.

Problem 4. In a communicating cylindrical joint of identical dimensions, one of which is sealed and the second is open, a rtul is placed. The ground levels in the vessels are the same, the length of the part of the sealed vessel filled with air is $l_0$, the atmospheric pressure $p_0$, changed in millimeters of the ground column, is $H$. What will be the difference in air levels in the vessels if the absolute temperature of the air in the sealed vessel is doubled?

The solution to this problem is similar to the solution to the previous one, with the only difference being that the Boyle-Mariotte law must be replaced by the Obedian gas law, since the temperature of the gas changes:

$$\frac{p_0 l_0}{T} = \frac{p(l_0 + x)}{2T}.$$

Here $x$ is the distance at which the ground level in the sealed vessel drops (Fig. 4). The level of water in an open vessel rises by the same amount (the diameters of the vessels are the same), so the hydrostatic equation takes the form

$$p = p_0 + 2\rho gx.$$

Hence, substituting $p_0 = \rho gH$, we obtain the quadratic equation

$$2x^2 + (H + 2l_0)x - Hl_0 = 0.$$

One root of this equation is negative and cannot be the answer to the problem. Therefore we get

$$x = (\sqrt{(H + 2l_0)^2 + 8Hl_0} - (H + 2l_0))/4.$$

Thus, the required difference in the levels of gru in the vessels is equal to

$$h = 2x.$$
