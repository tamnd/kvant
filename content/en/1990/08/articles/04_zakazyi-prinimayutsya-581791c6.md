To solve the general problem, consider one property of tangents to a hyperbola.

Property of tangents to a hyperbola Consider the branch of the hyperbola, $y = \frac{k}{x}$ (in other words, $xy = k$), located at the first coordinate angle. Let $A$ be a point on this branch. Let us draw a tangent to the hyperbola through the point $A$ and denote by $B$ and $C$ the points of intersection of this tangent with the coordinate axes.

Theorem 1. The point $A$ is the midpoint of the segment $BC$.

Proof.

Case I: point $A$ lies on the diagonal of the angle formed by the coordinate axes. Then the line $BC$ is perpendicular to this diagonal, $\triangle OAB = \triangle OAC$ and $AB = AC$.

Case 11: point $A$ does not lie on the diagonal of the angle formed by the coordinate axes. Let's transform our drawing: save it $\lambda$ times to the $Ox$ axis and expand it $\lambda$ times from the $Oy$ axis. This transformation is called a hyperbolic rotation; it takes point $(x; y)$ to point $(\lambda x; \frac{y}{\lambda})$. The product of coordinates does not change during a hyperbolic rotation, so our hyperbolas go to points lying on the same hyperbola. At the same time, the coordinate axes transform into themselves, tangents to the hyperbola transform into tangents to the hyperbola, tangent points into tangent points, midpoints of segments into midpoints of segments. Obviously, any point on our hyperbola can be obtained from the point considered in Case I using an appropriate hyperbolic rotation. This means that from the validity of our statement for the point considered in case I, it follows that it is valid for any point $A$.

The following statements that are important for us almost immediately follow from the proven Theorem 1.

Theorem 2. Tangents to the hyperbola $xy = k$ will cut off triangles of equal area from the angle $xOy$.

Proof. Since the point of tangency is the midpoint of the tangent segment, i.e. $A$ is the midpoint of the segment $BC$, then $OB$ is the double ordinate of the point $A$, and $OC$ is its double abscissa. Posny $S_{OBC} = \frac{1}{2} OB \cdot OC = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2y \cdot 2x = 2k$, i.e. the area does not depend on the point $A$ ($x$ and $y$ are the coordinates of the point $A$).

The converse of Theorem 2 is also true.

Theorem 3. Straight lines drawn from the side of a right angle of a triangle of equal area are tangent to the branch of the hyperbola, the aim of which is the coordinate axes.

Let the straight line $BC$ cut off from the side of the right angle of the triangle of given area $S$, and let $A(x; y)$ be the midpoint of the segment $BC$. Then $OB = 2y$, $OC = 2x$, $S = S_{OBC} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2y \cdot 2x = 2xy$, and the point $A$ lies on the hyperbola $xy = \frac{S}{2}$. The segment of the tangent to the hyperbola drawn through the point $A$, enclosed between the coordinate axes, is divided, like the segment $BC$, by this point in half and therefore this segment of the tangent coincides with $BC$. The theorem has been proven.

If we could send the ball with a key without spinning, like from a catapult, then its behavior would be pedagogically correct, but uninteresting: in the absence of wind, its trajectory would lie in a vertical plane, deviating neither to the right nor to the left, and it would fall to the ground after a relatively short flight.

Fortunately, it is only in rare cases that a golf ball does not have spin when it comes off the key, and it is the spin that gives interest, variety and liveliness to the flight of the ball. It is the spin that explains the deflection of the ball to the right or left, it is the spin that makes the ball soar over the field, or dive, or make furious zigzags, giving rise to the legend of his artistic temperament; he performs these eccentricities like an acrobat, doing one or two somersaults for fun. In reality, however, the ball is the most industrial object, knowing only one rule of behavior, which it obeys with stupid conscientiousness: it always follows its “nose” (front part). This rule is the key to understanding the behavior of all balls in the air, whether they are golf balls, baseball balls, cricket balls or tennis balls. Now, before moving on to considering the essence of this rule, let us trace some of its consequences...

If the angular velocity decreased as quickly as the displacement speed, the bending of the trajectory would not increase as the speed decreased. But since air resistance has a greater effect on the speed of the ball than on its rotation, it is the linear speed that decreases faster.
