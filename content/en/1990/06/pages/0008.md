Please delve into this and be convinced of this: here is the central point of the proof and the main beauty of the design - the ingenious invention of the great topology.

Two points lying in different circles of the 2nd stage, but in the same circle of the 1st stage, do not come closer or diverge when the fingers are first extended: the distance between them is maintained. At the second and third stages they can get closer - no more than 10 times at each stage; at further stages the distance between them does not change significantly. In general, points lying in different circles of the $(n+1)$th turn, but in the same circle of the $n$th turn, come closer (no more than 10 times) at the $(n+1)$th and $(n+2)$th stages, and at other stages the distance between them does not change significantly. It is clear that the increase in the distance between the points is limited (but occurs when the fingers are extended); we can assume that the distance increases by no more than 100 times.

Thus, during our deformation, no two points merge (they don’t even get closer than 100 times). The continuity of deformation is also very significant (although its rigorous proof requires little technique). This means that the Horned Sphere is a real sphere, as we understood it above (see the paragraph “Spatial generalizations”).

Appearance of the Horned Sphere

The part of space contained within the Horned Sphere is structured like an open (i.e., without a boundary) ball. This is easy to prove, but that is not what interests us. The main thing for us is that the appearance of the Horned Sphere is not the geometrical appearance of the objective round sphere, i.e. the Horned Sphere determines the hypothetical formulation of the generalized Schoenflies theorem. The proof of this fact is simple, but very impressive, or this is a real topology. the exterior of a round sphere has the following property: any closed curve lying in it (in the exterior of the sphere) can be contracted in it to a point (that is, it can be continuously deformed into an angularly small or even precise curve, and all the deformation occurs in the exterior of the sphere) - see figure 9. This property of the exterior of a sphere is very clear (although a rigorous proof is quite boring) and we don't prove it; it is called monoconnectivity.
