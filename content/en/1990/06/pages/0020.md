It is interesting that some of the insects that live on the surface of the water (for example, the stenus beetle), in situations that are dangerous to themselves, emit a stream of liquid with a low surface tension. By running away, they create an “impassable” area for themselves, in which the pursuers begin to drown.

Brave glass cutters

How can flour walk along the vertical surface of a window glass? This question also worried Robert Hooke. He believed that the flour was helped by the claws that were located at the ends of each of its six legs. (In 1665, Hooke described these claws in detail in his work Micrographica.) This may be true, but only when it crawls on a rough surface, and not on glass.

English scientists, armed with modern technology, have shown that the ability of flour to move on a very smooth surface is again explained by a strong tension surface. They discovered that at the end of each leg, between the claws, there are hairs that form bristles. Each of the hairs turns out to be a disc-shaped sucker; The surface area of ​​the disk is about $2 \cdot 10^{-12} \text{ m}^2$.

By studying traces of flour walking on clean surfaces, scientists discovered that they were identical to these disks. The prints did not evaporate, and their chemical analysis showed that they were fats. Despite the fact that fat is slippery, it promotes the sticking of hairs and glass. This is due to the fact that the surface tension of the fat is very high. (If the flour legs are degreased by dipping them in hexane for a short time, then the flour loses the ability to move on the glass surface for a while due to sliding.)

Measurements were taken of the force holding flour on a horizontal glass surface. To do this, flour was attached to a special scale and the force required to tear the flour from this surface was measured. The average weight of flour is 0.72 mN. When she stood on four legs, a force of 1.03 mN was required to lift her off, and if she relied on all six of her legs, a greater force was required - 2.4 mN. This proved that the flour was held in place by surface tension forces. (The fact that the dependence of changes in force on the number of paws touching the surface turned out to be non-linear is apparently explained by the different number of drags on the front and back paws of the flour.)

With a bucket on his head

If you have never traveled through Africa or Asia, then you have probably seen them in movies or on television - wives carrying enormous loads on their heads. Often the weight of such luggage reaches 70% of a person’s weight. Naturally, a woman cannot lift such a load on her head without help, and sometimes two men have difficulty lifting the load, which one woman then easily carries away. Is it easier for them to carry loads on their heads?

It is known that an indicator of a person’s energy expenditure can be the rate of oxygen consumption, with 1 liter of oxygen consumed corresponding to energy equal to 20.1 kJ. Scientists have established in experiments on volunteers that with the usual method of carrying a load (behind the back), oxygen consumption increases in proportion to the increase in power, so that a load equal to 50% of the porter’s body weight increases his energy costs by 50%. The same dependence occurred in cases where volunteers were asked to carry a load on their heads, which they had not previously been trained to do.

Imagine the surprise of scientists when they discovered that African women, when carrying a load of 50% of their own weight on their heads, increase their oxygen consumption
