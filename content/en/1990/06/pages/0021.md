A common picture for those places is that the groom returns home with a local court full of water on his head. Surprisingly, the water does not splash along the road. This means that during movement there is no (or almost no) vertical component of acceleration. Consequently, the center of gravity of the “carrier” does not move in the vertical direction.

It is known that during normal walking the height of the center of gravity fluctuates noticeably. figure 2 schematically depicts the positions of a person and his center of gravity in two successive phases of walking. If we assume that when walking, the hora in contact with the ground is straightened, not aligned at the knee, then it is very likely that the center of gravity will be located lower when both legs touch the ground. The highest position of the center of gravity will correspond to the moment when the hora in contact with the ground is in a vertical position.

We can assume that the center of gravity moves along an arc of a circle, the radius of which is equal to the length of the leg, periodically rising and falling by several centimeters.

Naturally, with each step, generally speaking, there is no need to raise the center of gravity; we are forced to expend a certain amount of energy on the wave. Therefore, this (common for us) way of walking can be compared to the eating of a novice car enthusiast who, trying to maintain a constant average speed, alternately presses the accelerator and then the brake, consuming much more fuel as a result.

Unlike Europeans, many residents of Asian and African countries have developed a gait in which they maintain their center of gravity at a constant height, which significantly reduces the cost of walking.

Something about aerobics

Everyone probably remembers a school physics lesson in which they talked about two magnets suspended on one crossbar. Oscillations of one magnet after just a few cycles led to in-phase oscillations of the other. Something worse can be found in the body of a running animal, when the interaction of two “magnets” occurs - the periodic movements of the animal as a whole, and its lungs.

figure 3 schematically shows the breathing “pendulum” of a kangaroo. When inhaling, when the animal's lungs fill with air, the center of mass of the abdominal cavity moves to the left, and when inhaling, it moves to the right. The elastic properties of the diaphragm and other tissues are represented in the diagram by a spring, and the organs that dampen respiratory vibrations are represented by a damper. Thus, the abdominal organs serve as a kind of piston, the vibrations of which occur synchronously with the respiratory movements.
