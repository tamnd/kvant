If one is simply connected, then so is the other. But, unlike the appearance of a round sphere, the appearance of the Horned Sphere is not simply connected: the closed roof enclosing the horn (Fig. 10) cannot be disentangled from the Horned Sphere. (When removed from the horn, the curve would have to pass between each two opposite circles of each turn. And this means that in the process of deformation, the curve should approach the sphere arbitrarily close: close to any predetermined distance. In turn, this means that at some point during deformation the curve will back the sphere, which is prohibited.)

This means that the exterior of the Horned Sphere is structured differently from the exterior of a round sphere—the expected generalization of the Schönflies theorem turned out to be incorrect.

Possible options

Now it's easy for us to be smart. The horns could be extended not outward, but inside the sphere - then the inner region would be different from the ball (and the exterior would be structured like the exterior of a round sphere). It would be possible to extend the horns both inside and outside - then both the exterior and the interior would be non-standard (non-single-connected). You can draw out as many pairs of horns as you like, you can knot and hook them in any way you want - in a word, you can customize an unlimited number of spheres with different exteriors and interiors. However, this unlimited variety no longer surprises us.

Conclusion

Years and decades passed after Alexander’s discovery, but topologists still hoped that a three-dimensional analogue of Schoenflies’s theorem still existed—it was only necessary to exclude overly intricate surfaces from consideration. What if, say, the surface is polydral, that is, multifaceted? And for such surfaces, Schoenflies’s theorem turned out to be a tough nut to crack, but in 1960 it was proved by the American topologist Morton Brown.

Braunn's theorem is true not only for three-dimensional space, but also for space of any dimension. However, in multidimensional spaces, even in the polydral case, not all is well. For example, as R. Carby and L. Siebenman discovered in the 1970s, a portion of four-dimensional space enclosed between two polydralic spheres, one of which encloses the other, may not be geometric to a standard four-dimensional spherical sphere.

But all this is no longer for popular stats.
