Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
D. FUCHS

Experienced readers «Kvant» may remember the image of the Horned Sphere featured on the cover of magazine No. 7 in 1977.

It was figure of the Moscow mathematician A. T. Fomenko, who is also known as a graphic artist - a fragment of this figure is placed on the first page of the cover of this issue. Figure Fomenko conveys, at least in part, the main property of the Horned Sphere - it is extremely beautiful. It is also clear from figure that the individual parts of the Sphere are, as it were, interlocked with each other. However, in order to fully appreciate the beauty of the Horned Sphere - and it deserves it - it is worth getting acquainted with its construction in all its details, and also understand for what purpose it was built.

Background
At the end of the century, the famous French mathematician Camille Jordan began writing a textbook on analysis. It should be said that more than three centuries of the existence of mathematical analysis as a separate discipline in the textbook of this subject have been revised many times. (This process has not stopped even now.) And each time such reworking not only reflected progress in mathematics, but also significantly contributed to this progress. The author of the original textbook invariably discovered natural questions for which there were no answers not only in previous textbooks, but also in mathematics in general. Finding these answers, he becomes the author of not only a textbook, but also Fundamentals of mathematical theorems.

Jordan was not excluded either. Studying the properties of intergals of functions of two variables over a domain and curves on a plane, he came to the conclusion that it was necessary to give a precise meaning to the intuitively clear statement “a curve divides a plane.” A curve is the trace of a continuously moving point. Movement has a beginning and an end. If the end coincides with the beginning, the curve is called closed, and if at no other different moments of time the positions of the moving point are the same, then the curve is called non-self-intersecting or simple. Jordan's theorem states that a closed non-self-intersecting curve divides a plane into two parts. This means that the set of all points of the plane that do not lie on our curve l is divided into two parts, and if two points of the plane lie in one of these parts, then they can be connected by a line that does not intersect the curve l, but if they lie in different ones, then it is impossible (Fig. 1). Jordan's theorem is still included in analysis textbooks, the authors of which strive for greater rigor.
