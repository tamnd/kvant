Obviously, the lowest energy costs during beta should correspond to the case when the inertial forces arising in the animal’s body during its periodic acceleration and deceleration help (rather than hinder) the respiratory movements. Such reasoning leads to the idea that the animal’s respiratory rate should be very close to the frequency of its beta beta.

In special experiments conducted on kangaroos, horses, hares and dogs, this idea was tested. It turns out that the most likely ratio of the frequency of stitches to the breathing frequency (especially with a bistrome beta gallop) is 1:1. In humans, the connection between the beta frequency and the respiratory frequency looks more complex. This ratio can take on a whole range of values ​​(4:1, 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 5:2, 3:2), although the ratio 2:1 seems more likely.

The reason for every independence of breathing and beta frequencies in a person lies, apparently, in the vertical position of his body. His respiratory movements are accompanied by vertical movements of the abdominal organs, and therefore, the forces of inertia during beta, directed horizontally, affect the respiratory “pendulum” of a person to a much lesser extent than is the case in quadrupeds.

Returning to the experiment with two matniks, we note that the forced oscillations of the second matnik will reach their greatest amplitude when the periods of the matniks’ natural oscillations are equal. Therefore, it should be expected that the frequency of natural oscillations of the respiratory “pendulum” should be close to the beta frequency. And so it turned out. Experiments conducted on dogs have shown that the frequency of natural oscillations of the respiratory “pendulum” is about 4 Hz, while the frequency of beta galloping is 3.2 Hz.

Since the frequency of natural oscillations of the respiratory “pendulum” is a constant value for a given animal, then when the beta speed changes, the frequency of the stitches should also be kept constant. This requires minimizing energy costs.

And indeed, both in four-legged runners and in two-legged ones (kangaroos), the beta gallop frequency remains constant, although the footsteps can change several times.

How Bernoulli's principle is used by living organisms

In accordance with Bernoulli's principle ($\rho v^2/2 + p = \text{const}$, where $\rho$ is the density of the liquid or gas, $v$ is the linear speed of their movement and $p$ is the pressure), a change in the flow velocity leads to a change in the pressure in it. As it is easy to say, Bernoulli’s principle directly follows from the law of conservation of mechanical energy, since the value $\rho v^2/2$ is numerically equal to the kinetic energy of units of volume of a moving medium, and $p$ is the potential energy of its surface.
