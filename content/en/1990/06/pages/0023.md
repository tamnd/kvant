From Bernoulli's principle it follows that if a body is moving (or a fluid flows around a stationary body), then the pressure of the fluid in contact with this body will not be the same at different points in the flow. figure 4 shows how the pressure in a fluid flow changes when it flows around a drop-shaped body. In those places where the fluid collides with the body (point A at figure 4), its speed decreases and the pressure of the fluid increases. Flowing around the body, the fluid accelerates, and in some areas close to the body (point B), it moves at a speed greater than at other points of the flow. Therefore, at point B, the fluid pressure drops. Thus, high pressure acting on the part of the body facing the flow will tend to flatten the body, and low pressure near the side surfaces will flatten it.

Man has learned to use Bernoulli's principle quite effectively. figure 5 shows a cross section of an airplane wing. Let us consider the movement of two partial impacts, which were next to each other before the collision with the wing. After they go around the wing from different sides, they end up together again. But particle A will travel a longer distance during this time interval than particle B; This means that when going around, the average speed of particle A is greater. Therefore, from Bernoulli's principle it follows that the average pressure above the wing should be less than below its plane. This pressure difference leads to the generation of lift, depending on the area of ​​the wing and its profile.

How do living organisms use Bernoulli's principles? Of course, the most obvious example is the soaring flight of birds. The aerodynamics of this flight, although still not fully understood, are not much different from the flight of their steel counterparts. However, in nature there are quite a lot of other phenomena, each because of this pressure difference and water is sucked inside the mantle cavity.

Fig. 6. Distribution of water pressure along the surface of a moving squid. (A cross section of the squid with the plane indicated by the point is also shown.)

Most readers, of course, know that the squid, fleeing from its next predator, uses the reactive principle of movement, throwing out a significant mass of water from its mantle cavity. However, for a long time it remained unclear how this cavity was filled with water.

figure 6 shows a schematic representation of a squid and indicates the direction of its movement when throwing out a stream of water from a tube (siphon) located in the head part of the mollusk. The entrance clans, through which water enters the mantle cavity, are located on the lateral surface of the animal in its central part.

All our arguments about the pressure drop of a fluid flowing around a body are also valid in the case when this body is a squid. So the water pressure at the surface of the squid will be minimal in the middle part of its body, near the mantle cavity (see Fig. 6). In this case, the fluid pressure at the cavity clanks will always be slightly greater than the average fluid pressure in the cavity. It is believed that because of this pressure difference, water becomes trapped inside the mantle cavity.
