But the main thing here is that the metal electrode that was in contact with hydrogen received an excess of electrons, and the one that was in contact with oxygen, on the contrary, gave up some electrons. Then, if you connect these electrodes with a conductor, i.e., close the external circuit, the excess electrodes will move from the “hydrogen” electrode to the “oxygen” electrode (Fig. 1). In other words, there is a current in the external electrical circuit that can do useful work. The process will go on continuously if the electrical circuit is kept closed and “flammable material” - hydrogen and oxygen - is supplied to the electrodes.

Thus, the energy of the chemical reaction described, i.e., combustion, in the fuel cell is removed and converted into electrical energy. In accordance with the name of “combustible” materials, the particular element that we considered is called oxygen-hydrogen.

Interestingly, the idea of ​​​​creating a fuel cell in itself is not new - last year it turned 150 years old. In 1839, the English scientist W. Grove discovered the flow of current between two platinum electrodes, partially immersed in saline acid, under conditions where one electrode was in contact with oxygen and the other with hydrogen. By the day, Grove's design was the prototype of the oxygen-hydrogen element. However, at that distant time, neither he nor his contemporaries attached due importance to the results of Grove's experiments. Only a few decades later, many scientists and artists tried to purposefully create a fuel cell suitable for practical use. In the seventies, the remarkable Russian electrical engineer and artist P. N. Yablochkov was an enthusiast of the technical solution to this problem. But then the decision was not given. And now, in our time, fully armed with the scientific knowledge and technical fundamentals of the late twentieth century, scientists and engineers are once again exploring the same terrain. Why did it replace the walls of the fortress and report that these walls are impregnable?

First, about the importance of the task.

The basis of today's energy industry is thermal steam turbine power plants running on coal, oil, gas, and gas. The system for converting fuel into electrical energy here is multi-stage: the heat released during scraping heats the water, the water turns into high-pressure steam, the steam drives the turbine, which, in turn, turns the rotor of the electric generator.

With such a complex, cumbersome process, one cannot do without large energy losses. But if in relatively recent times it was believed that fuel reserves on Earth were practically inexhaustible, today eyes have been opened to a lot, and the efficiency of energy conversion is becoming one of the main problems.
