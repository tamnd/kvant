BIG AND SMALL ON A WALK

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, Doctor of Biology K. BOGDANOV

On water as on dry land

We are all accustomed to hunting animals on those that live in water and on “land animals”. However, there are several species of insects that spend almost their entire lives at the border of water and air. One of them is water striders.

Water striders quickly glide across the surface of the water, reminiscent of skaters on ice. Widespread long legs support a thin elongated body.

The surface film of water with its high surface tension serves as a reliable support for the water strider. If in the path of its movement there is a “full area” - an area where the surface tension is much less - the insect will plunge into the water and will helplessly flounder in it. Such a “complete” can be achieved by dropping a little hexane into water - its surface tension coefficient (at the border with water-air) σ = 18 mN/m is almost four times less than that of water (σ water = 70 mN/m). (Observations show that water striders well distinguish a “suitable” water surface from an “unsuitable” one. A few falling water drops disturb the smoothness of the surface, and the water striders immediately rush to the shore, where they hide until the water becomes calm.)

Let's try to estimate how much buoyancy reserve a water gauge should have. The length of its body is slightly more than 5 mm. It has two pairs of long legs, which mainly hold it on the surface of the water, and two short ones, which it manipulates with prey. The mass m of a water strider does not exceed 0.05 g. The force of surface tension that keeps the insect afloat can be estimated as the product σwater L, where L is the length of the curve along which it acts. From the equality mg=σwater L (this is the condition of retention on the surface of the water) it follows that L should be equal to 0.5 mN/70 mN×m⁻¹≈7.1 mm. What is it really?

Look at figure 1, where in two projections the place where one of the water strider’s legs touches the water is shown. The secret of the insect's ability to float on the surface of the water is hidden in the ends of its legs. They are sadly covered with water-repellent hairs. Since the water strider has only six legs, the maximum length of the curve, perpendicular to which the action of the surface tension layer (T) is about 12 mm.
