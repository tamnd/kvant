A possible path here involves the use of electricity from solar panels to produce hydrogen and oxygen by electrolysis. This “fuel” can be stored for quite a long time, and its cold combustion in a fuel cell again produces electrical energy.

So, with class-hydrogen sources the situation is relatively good. Elements operating on electrochemical fuels such as hydrazine (N₂H₄), methanol (methyl alcohol CH₃OH), and ethylene glycol (HOCH₂CH₂OH) have also found practical application.

One of the promising directions is the use of biological enzymes to promote electrode processes (bioelectrocatalysis). Activation by enzymes can be used to develop so-called biofuel cells.

Let us return, however, to the super-task of research in the field of fuel cells, as it was originally formed. The goal is to implement cold combustion of coal or other natural fuels, and in the future, to solve the problems of large-scale energy on this basis. Therefore, pioneering researchers turned to the problem of electrochemical combustion of coal (carbon). And here a serious difficulty immediately awaited them. The fact is that the carbon oxidation reaction occurs at room temperatures with an insignificant, practically zero rate. This is its significant difference from the rapid reaction of hydrogen oxidation, which, ultimately, allows classon-hydrogen elements to pull ahead.

It’s hard to resist being surprised once again by the harmony of the world around us. After all, almost all living things consist of carbon molecules. And if carbon were easily described by oxygen in the air, life would be impossible. On the other hand, the energy problem in living organisms is solved by description (combustion), and at ordinary temperatures. It is remarkable that the obligatory link in the chain of descriptive processes here turns out to be the cold combustion of hydrogen, the source of which is food. In other words, the classon-hydrogen fuel cell in the biological version is the foundation of the energy of living things, and the classon-carbon fuel cell, if it could function under normal conditions, would lead to the death (or rather, to the “non-emergence”) of this living thing.

It is now easy to understand that the implementation of cold combustion of coal encounters relatively unintentional difficulties. We have to overcome the barBER created by nature itself, which protects living matter. Thus, relying on natural carbon fuels requires a transition to high temperatures. In this case, melts or so-called solid electrolytes are used together with regular aqueous solutions of electrolytes.

High-temperature fuel cells are designed for large-scale, high-power stationary installations that can operate continuously for a long time and provide high efficiency in the use of natural fuel energy. Creating installations of this type requires solving problems at the intersection of many fields of knowledge - from mathematics and electrical engineering to materials and electronics. In terms of its scale and complexity, this problem, taken as a whole, is comparable to the problem of creating nuclear energy. A fairly complete solution can only be expected in the next generations of electrochemical power plants. At the same time, the foundation for a future solution is being laid now.
