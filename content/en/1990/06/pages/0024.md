The extent to which Bernoulli's principle contributes to the mechanism of filling the mantle cavity was determined after mathematical modeling. The pressure gradient (change in pressure per unit length) given by Bernoulli's formula depends on the speed of the squid and provides from 50 to 90% of the required value when this speed increases from 3 to 9 m/s. This calculation gives reason to believe that in reality Bernoulli's principle means a lot for the squid, since its speed is usually 5-10 m/s.

Perhaps no one uses Bernoulli's principles as effectively as fish. The long course of evolution optimized the shape of their body in such a way that the magnitude of the pressure drop near the central part of the swimming fish became almost the same as its increase in front of it (compare Fig. 4 and Fig. 7). Scientists believe that the significant pressure observed near the place where the fish’s heart is located can contribute to more efficient functioning of the latter - less pressure in the heart cavity should increase the blood flow to it.

In addition, the shape of the fish’s body is such that there is a place where the pressure of the surrounding water does not depend on the speed of movement and is always equal to the hydrostatic pressure, and this place coincides with the position of the fish’s eyes. Thus, the fish’s eyes, the organs most protected from deformation, never experience an increase in fluid pressure as the swimming speed increases.

Many people probably know that some beetles spend most of their lives underwater. At the same time, they breathe air, a bubble of which they carry with them all the time. Such “submariners” include, for example, the beetle Potamodytes tuberosus, which lives in the rivers of West Africa. Typically, this beetle, together with an air bubble, attaches to the surface of the stone while in a stream of water. As soon as a beetle with an air bubble finds itself in calm (stagnant) water, the bubble begins to slowly dissolve and disappears after a couple of hours, which forces the beetle to look for a new one. Therefore, having mounted the found air bubble, the beetle tries to always be in a moving stream of water.

Bernoulli's principles again help to explain the stability of an air bubble in a flow of water. As follows from all of the above, the pressure of the water flowing around the continuator, a bubble extended along the flow, should be less than the hydrostatic pressure almost along its entire surface. Therefore, the air pressure in the bubble is less than in the case when the water around is motionless. If the depth at which the water is located can lead to a squeak of air pressure in the bubble below atmospheric pressure. This means that air dissolved (at atmospheric pressure) in water will enter the bubble, where the pressure is lower. As a result, the bubble will grow.

Further, when the beetle is in a stream of water at a depth of several centimeters, the bubble is often stable, despite the hydrostatic pressure that is attached to it. The data shows that for such stability at depths of 1 and 4 cm, flow velocities greater than 1 and 2 m/s are required.
