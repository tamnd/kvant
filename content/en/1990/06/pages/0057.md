Many interesting phenomena in life and in science are associated with the stroboscopic effect. This includes visually stopping movement, assessing the movement of a stationary object during initial observation, using red neon advertising, creating a device for monitoring and measuring the frequency of fast periodic processes, and much more. In all cases, the stroboscopic effect is due to the inertia of vision, i.e., the preservation of the visual image observed by him in the mind for some time (about 0.2 s) after the picture that caused the image has already disappeared.
Many scientific and popular articles have been written about stroboscopy. Several have been published in Laboratory «Kvant». For example, the article by G. Gavrilov “Telector - stroboscope” (1981, No. 5), S. Gavrilov “What is a stroboscope” (1983, No. 1), S. Beluosov and P. Gerasimov “Observation and forostemka of fast-protective processes” (1988, No. 2), V. Uteshev “How to notice the invisible” (1989, No. 4). Read these articles!
And now we bring to your attention another article on the same topic. The author of the article, Vladislav Kreimer, is an 11th grade student at Physics School No. 35 in Donetsk.

Do this experience. Place it in front of the switched-on TV at a distance of 1.5-2 m and look at the oscillating legs of the vertical tweezers. In the background of the screen, you will see that the legs are curved in a wave-like manner (Fig. 1). By changing the distance between the eye and the tweezers, you can achieve the most clear effect.

It is obvious, of course, that the curvature of the tweezers is a purely optical effect, or because the legs are very rigid, they cannot bend like that. It is enough to place the tweezers horizontally, and you will see that when it oscillates, its legs remain straight.

Take a pencil, place it vertically and begin to quickly move it horizontally along the screen. The pencil will appear tilted in the direction opposite to the direction of movement (Fig. 2). Why? It turns out that the whole point here is the stroboscopic effect. The beam, running across the screen from left to right and from top to bottom, cuts out only a certain section of the screen at each moment of time. Therefore, the entire pencil cannot be illuminated at once; only a small part of it will be illuminated. Let at some instant of time the beam illuminate a section of the pencil close to point a (see Fig. 2). Our eyes remember this illuminated area for about 0.2 seconds.
