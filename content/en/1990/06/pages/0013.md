Another problem is environmental protection. It’s relatively easy to get rid of waste such as soot, dust, and harmful substances; in any case, there are no fundamental difficulties here. The situation with energy blockage is much more complicated. Already now, human energy “turnover” in certain regions of the globe is comparable to the thermal turnover of natural processes, and energy consumption is inexorably calculated. The question arises: where to “hide” the energy needed without creeping from thermal power plants. After all, at the current production rates, not today, but tomorrow our planet will “become hot.” Let's not paint a picture of what this could lead to. It is clear, however, that energy must not only be economical, but also economical.

And even if conventional thermal power plants give way to nuclear power plants, there is no need to delude yourself - the existing problems will remain. The reason is that external nuclear and even future fusion power plants remain essentially warm power plants. It’s just that the furnace of the archang steam boiler is replaced by a nuclear reactor, but, as in the ancient times, here it is necessary to first receive heat by discharging the energy carrier and only then, through intermediate steps, convert the warm energy into electrical energy.

The source of the difficulties that arise is very serious - here we come across a fundamental prohibition imposed by the inexorable laws of thermodynamics, which limits the maximum efficiency (efficiency) of any heat engine. Mathematically, this limitation is described by the meaning of the formulation obtained by the twenty-thought French physicist and engineer Nicolas Sadi Carnot and published by him in 1824 in the only scientific work of his entire life, “Reflections on the driving force of fire and on machines capable of developing this force.” Using Carnot's formulation, the results are not very positive: the maximum efficiency of the most modern thermal installations using steam heated under high pressure is only slightly more than 60%. Moreover, this is an ideal value, and taking into account the inevitable loss of efficiency does not exceed forty (at best, forty and a half) percent. Some benefits can be expected from the use of magnetohydridydynamic installations, where the heat bed is plasma (a mixture of infused atoms and electrons). But even here the actual efficiency will exceed 55%.

The laws of thermodynamics cannot be overstepped or circumvented, therefore the only way to resolve problems is to abandon the ineffective “vulgar” waste of energy carriers. It turns out that the implementation of cold combustion is the most important step along this principle of a different path. In a fuel cell (electrochemical generator), the released chemical energy is converted directly into electrical energy. It follows that a fuel cell is not a heat engine and the strict restrictions arising from Carnot’s thermodynamic formula do not apply to it. In other words, the efficiency based on the functioning of fuel cells can, in principle, be close to the coveted Sta percent. Let us note in passing that what has been said applies to other chemical current sources, for example, to galvanic elements on the basis of which batteries in radios, microcalculators, electronic watches, etc. work.

The reason for the sharp difference in efficiency for hot and cold combustion processes is given in a completely clear explanation.
