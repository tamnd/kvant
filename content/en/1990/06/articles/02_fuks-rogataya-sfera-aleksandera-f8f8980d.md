It was figure of the Moscow mathematician A. T. Fomenko, who is also known as a graphic artist - a fragment of this figure is placed on the first page of the cover of this issue. Figure Fomenko conveys, at least in part, the main property of the Horned Sphere - it is extremely beautiful. It is also clear from figure that the individual parts of the Sphere are, as it were, interlocked with each other. However, in order to fully appreciate the beauty of the Horned Sphere - and it deserves it - it is worth getting acquainted with its construction in all its details, and also understand for what purpose it was built.

Background
At the end of the century, the famous French mathematician Camille Jordan began writing a textbook on analysis. It should be said that more than three centuries of the existence of mathematical analysis as a separate discipline in the textbook of this subject have been revised many times. (This process has not stopped even now.) And each time such reworking not only reflected progress in mathematics, but also significantly contributed to this progress. The author of the original textbook invariably discovered natural questions for which there were no answers not only in previous textbooks, but also in mathematics in general. Finding these answers, he becomes the author of not only a textbook, but also Fundamentals of mathematical theorems.

Jordan was not excluded either. Studying the properties of intergals of functions of two variables over a domain and curves on a plane, he came to the conclusion that it was necessary to give a precise meaning to the intuitively clear statement “a curve divides a plane.” A curve is the trace of a continuously moving point. Movement has a beginning and an end. If the end coincides with the beginning, the curve is called closed, and if at no other different moments of time the positions of the moving point are the same, then the curve is called non-self-intersecting or simple. Jordan's theorem states that a closed non-self-intersecting curve divides a plane into two parts. This means that the set of all points of the plane that do not lie on our curve l is divided into two parts, and if two points of the plane lie in one of these parts, then they can be connected by a line that does not intersect the curve l, but if they lie in different ones, then it is impossible (Fig. 1). Jordan's theorem is still included in analysis textbooks, the authors of which strive for greater rigor.

The area of ​​mathematics to which Jordan's theorem relates is called topology. Jordan, however, was not a topologist; rather, he was an algebraist; The theorem on curves interested him exclusively as the author of an analysis textbook. But topologies also existed, and Jordan’s theorem could not pass by their understanding. What are the parts into which a closed non-self-intersecting curve divides a plane? It turned out that these parts are the same for different non-self-intersecting closed curved devices: this theorem was proved in 1908 by the German topologist A. Schönflies. What do you mean devices are the same? Two regions $V$ and $V'$ on the plane are considered devices equally if a one-to-one correspondence can be established between them, continuous in both directions. This means that if we take two points $x$ and $y$ of the area $V$ and begin to move $x$ so that the distance between $x$ and $y$ decreases indefinitely, then the distance between the corresponding points $x'$ and $y'$ will also decrease indefinitely, and vice versa, if the distance between points $x'$ and $y'$, then the distance between $x$ and $y$ decreases without limit.*)

The circle drawn by the circumference divides the plane into two parts: a circle and a plane with a round hole (Fig. 2). Schoenflies's theorem states that every non-self-intersecting closed curve divides a plane into parts, a structure, in the same way as a circle and a plane with a circular hole.

She is extraordinarily beautiful

And simple. The main element of the design is shown in figure 4: on a flat disk we take two small circles and stretch out the “fingers” from them, so that their ends are close, but not in contact. We also consider these ends themselves to be flat disks.

This stretching of the fingers will be performed not from one disk, but from two parallel disks. The extended fingers will be hooked into the “lock” (Fig. 5).

Now let's go over the entire formation. Take a round sphere. From it we extend two fingers, almost closing, as in figure 4 (Fig. 6). The resulting surface consists of two parallel disks located close to each other (top figure 6). From these two disks we extend the fingers - exactly as shown in figure 5. On the surface shown in figure 5 - stick to this figure - there are two pairs of parallel disks (the ends of the extended fingers). These disks will appear (in a reduced form) on our surface. We transform each of these pairs of disks - by stretching our fingers - into a figure figure 5 (even more reduced). On the new surface there will already be four pairs of parallel disks, and we again pull out (very small) fingers from these disks, turning each pair into a kind of figure 5. And so on.

What happens after “and so on” is Alexander’s Horned Sphere.

This is a valid sphere

At first, this still raises doubts. Alexander's sphere looks like a hypnastic weight with a sawn handle and some kind of complex figure made of wire of various thicknesses placed in a sawn slot.
Do the two pieces of the handle agree with each other? After all, at each stage of construction we refer some parts of the sphere to each other - won’t they merge in the limit?

No! The previous construction can be carried out so that when the sphere is deformed, the distance between any two of its points does not increase or decrease by more than, say, 100 times. (I say “let’s say” because in Figures 4 and 5 the dimensions are not indicated - finger lengths, slit width, etc.; all this can be made a little larger or a little smaller; you can choose the sizes so that the number indicated above is exactly 100.)

Let's look at our construction step by step. The first stretch of the fingers affects two circles on the round sphere; the remaining parts of the sphere remain motionless at this stage of deformation and at all subsequent stages. The second step affects four small circles (Fig. 8). The parts of the sphere lying outside these four circles are not mobile at all stages of deformation, except for the first. The third stage affects eight smaller circles (see Fig. 8), the fourth - 16 eight smaller circles (at Fig. 8 they are no longer visible), etc.
There are $2^n$ circles in the $n$-th queue; in each circle of the $n$th order there are two circles of the $(n+1)$th order; points of the sphere that do not lie in any circle of the $n$th order are not mobile at all stages of deformation, starting from the $n$th.

Appearance of the Horned Sphere

The part of space contained within the Horned Sphere is structured like an open (i.e., without a boundary) ball. This is easy to prove, but that is not what interests us. The main thing for us is that the appearance of the Horned Sphere is not the geometrical appearance of the objective round sphere, i.e. the Horned Sphere determines the hypothetical formulation of the generalized Schoenflies theorem. The proof of this fact is simple, but very impressive, or this is a real topology. the exterior of a round sphere has the following property: any closed curve lying in it (in the exterior of the sphere) can be contracted in it to a point (that is, it can be continuously deformed into an angularly small or even precise curve, and all the deformation occurs in the exterior of the sphere) - see figure 9. This property of the exterior of a sphere is very clear (although a rigorous proof is quite boring) and we don't prove it; it is called monoconnectivity. Possible options

Now it's easy for us to be smart. The horns could be extended not outward, but inside the sphere - then the inner region would be different from the ball (and the exterior would be structured like the exterior of a round sphere). It would be possible to extend the horns both inside and outside - then both the exterior and the interior would be non-standard (non-single-connected). You can draw out as many pairs of horns as you like, you can knot and hook them in any way you want - in a word, you can customize an unlimited number of spheres with different exteriors and interiors. However, this unlimited variety no longer surprises us.

Conclusion

Years and decades passed after Alexander’s discovery, but topologists still hoped that a three-dimensional analogue of Schoenflies’s theorem still existed—it was only necessary to exclude overly intricate surfaces from consideration. What if, say, the surface is polydral, that is, multifaceted? And for such surfaces, Schoenflies’s theorem turned out to be a tough nut to crack, but in 1960 it was proved by the American topologist Morton Brown.
