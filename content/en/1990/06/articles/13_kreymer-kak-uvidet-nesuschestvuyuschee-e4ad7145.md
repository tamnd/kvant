Do this experience. Place it in front of the switched-on TV at a distance of 1.5-2 m and look at the oscillating legs of the vertical tweezers. In the background of the screen, you will see that the legs are curved in a wave-like manner (Fig. 1). By changing the distance between the eye and the tweezers, you can achieve the most clear effect.

It is obvious, of course, that the curvature of the tweezers is a purely optical effect, or because the legs are very rigid, they cannot bend like that. It is enough to place the tweezers horizontally, and you will see that when it oscillates, its legs remain straight.

Take a pencil, place it vertically and begin to quickly move it horizontally along the screen. The pencil will appear tilted in the direction opposite to the direction of movement (Fig. 2). Why? It turns out that the whole point here is the stroboscopic effect. The beam, running across the screen from left to right and from top to bottom, cuts out only a certain section of the screen at each moment of time. Therefore, the entire pencil cannot be illuminated at once; only a small part of it will be illuminated. Let at some instant of time the beam illuminate a section of the pencil close to point a (see Fig. 2). Our eyes remember this illuminated area for about 0.2 seconds. After “gluing” all visible areas of the pencil, it will seem to us as if tilted. The angle of inclination to the horizon will be equal to

$$\alpha = \arctg \frac{l_c(v_\pi \mp v_\kappa)}{(N-1)l_c(\pm v_\kappa)},$$

where $l_c$ is the length of the pencil, $v_\kappa$ is the speed of the pencil (a positive value of the speed corresponds to movement to the right), $v_\pi$ is the speed of the beam, $N$ is the number of lines decreased on the pencil projection.

From this formula it is clear that the angle of inclination of the pencil decreases with increasing speed of the pencil and significantly depends on the direction of its movement.
