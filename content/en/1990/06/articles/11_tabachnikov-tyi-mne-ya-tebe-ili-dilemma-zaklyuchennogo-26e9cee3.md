The prisoner's paradox can be formulated in the language of game theory. Two players play, and both simultaneously make one of two possible moves: “confess” (or “P” for short) or “deny” (or “O” for short). If both make move P, they get little; if both choose move O, they get more; if one player makes a move P and the other moves O, then the first one gets even more, and the second one gets nothing. All this can be described in the form of a table.

Table

1st player 2nd player O P
O (3; 3) (0; 5)
P (5; 0) (1; 1)

The winning values ​​(1, 3, 5) were chosen more or less arbitrarily. The table clearly shows that for any move of the first player, the second player will receive more if he makes move P rather than move O - this is the reasoning given above.

In 1979, Michigan scientist Robert Axelrod decided to investigate the prisoner's paradox using a computer. He turned to leading experts with a proposal to hold a tournament of computer programs in the described game. 14 people responded to his call. The tournament was organized like this: each program played 5 series of 200 games with other programs; including with herself. The principles of compiling a program could be arbitrary (for example, the next move P or O can simply be chosen at random), but most programs looked at all the previous moves of the “enemy” and chose the next move based on this. The authors of the program tried to compose them in such a way as to encourage the enemy’s “cooperation” (i.e., choosing the move O), and also to earn points by “betraying”.

The result of the tournament was stunning: it was won by the simplest of the proposed programs, “You for me, I for you,” invented by Anatoly Rappoport from Toronto. She plays like this:
the first move in the game is O;
Each next move repeats the opponent's previous move.
That's it!

Note that the program “You for me, I for you” is not capable of winning a single game consisting of a series of moves of any length; the best she can hope for is a draw. Indeed, as long as the opponent responds to her “benevolent” O moves with his O, both score points; and as soon as the opponent “deceives” the program “You - to me, I - to you” by making move P, it takes revenge for this by moving P, at best, regaining the lost points. But “You to me, I to you” is not vindictive: as soon as the opponent returns to “cooperation” by making move O, she responds in kind.
