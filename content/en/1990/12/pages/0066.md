McMahon cubes and Conway table

At the beginning of this century, English Major Alexander MacMahon, a mathematics teacher at the Royal Military Academy, came up with several problems with multi-colored cups. Before him, sets of cups as a game were used only for the construction of strong buildings and for putting together pictures from pieces placed on their edges. (By the way, “cubes with cards” do not have such a long history - they were invented in Germany in the middle of the last century.) Since then, the number of games with cups has increased immeasurably, and the unrivaled Rubik’s cube took pride of place among them... However, the first were Vetset McMahon and his friend Colonel Jocelyn, who proved the solvability of his main problem. But first things first.

Imagine that you need to paint a cube in 6 colors, each side a different color. For the bottom edge you can choose any of the colors, for the front - any of the 5 remaining ones, for the right - any of the 4 remaining ones, etc. It turns out that there are 6·5·4·3·2·1=720 coloring methods in total. But if you prepare 720 of these cups, you will find that many of them are indistinguishable as soon as you pick them up - after all, it is not written on the cube which side is the bottom and which is the front. Indifferent colorings are obtained from each other by rotating the cube, transforming it into itself. There are 24 such rotations: 9 rotations at angles of ±90° and 180° around axes passing through the centers of opposite edges, 8 rotations at ±120° around the diagonal of the cube, 6 axial symmetries relative to straight lines connecting the midpoints of opposite edges, and an identical transformation. Therefore, for each of the 720 colorings taken into account above, there are 23 more equivalent ones, and the number of significantly different colorings is 720:24=30. It is the column of cups in the McMahon set. They are all shown in the table on the back cover. It also explains how, with the help of this table, which was invented by the American mathematician John Conway, one can quickly and beautifully solve McMahon’s main problem: having laid out one cube from a set - a sample, find among the remaining 29 such 8, from which you can add a doubled copy of the sample, observing the “domino rule”: small cubes must touch with edges of the same color.

If we look at the sample cube along the diagonal, we will see three faces collapsing at one of its ends. Let's denote them by colors 1, 2, 3, and the color of the coined faces, respectively, 4, 5, 6. When using the figure eight, you must include at least one cube with a trihedral corner, painted with the same colors 1, 2, 3, and in the same order. There are six such cups together with the sample (this is the number of permutations of the numbers 4, 5, 6). However, it is not difficult to understand that the “domino rule” leaves from these six only two color options, respectively opposite 1, 2, 3: 5, 6, 4, 6, 4, 5. From the messages of symmetry it is clear that any of these two cups can be placed in the corner (1, 2, 3) of a large cube, then the second cube will need to be placed in the opposite corner (4, 5, 6). (It is interesting that in the Conway table these two cubes and the sample are always located at places of the form Hu, Yx and Zx). Similarly, there are three pairs of cups for the other three diagonals. Thus, the Eight of Cups solving the problem

McMahon for a given sample, was determined unambiguously, although there are two ways to fold a larger cube from it - to get one method from the other, you need to move each cube to the opposite corner. In the Conway table, cubes corresponding to the Hu pattern are located in row Y and column x; cube Yx must be excluded from them. You can check that four cubes in one row, when assembling a large cube, will occupy places at the ends of the diagonal of its lower base and the diagonal of the upper base that crosses it.

McMahon's problem continues: having made a copy according to a given sample, you need to find among the remaining cups the sample and 8 cups that form its copy in compliance with the “domino rule”. With the help of a table, this problem is solved almost automatically - the comme cubes are uniquely determined and are mirror images of the cups of the first set.

Perhaps Conway's table will help you solve the following problem.

1. Observing the “domino rule”, fold a 2x2x2 cube, each face of which would be colored with one of a) heel, 6) four, b) three colors (in each case all given colors must be used). Are all these tasks resolved?

2. Let's abandon the "domino rule". How many ways can you then fold a 2×2×2 cube in a given way? How many sets of 8 cups are there that can do this? Show that a copy cube from such a set can be folded in 2, 4, 8 or 16 ways. Can a 3x3x3 copy cube be folded?

V. Dubrovsky
