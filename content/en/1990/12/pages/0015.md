Now the most important thing could be proven. Prove that from the law of universal gravitation it follows that the trajectories of planets will be ellipses, and the trajectories of comets will be hyperbolas.

The need for such a proof was clearly formulated in 1684 during a conversation between three English physicists - Galday, Wren and Hooke. Ren even announced a prize—a kiniga, which he gave to the one who solved the problem within two months. The prize was never awarded. In May, Halley proposed this problem to Newton, who sent him the necessary proof six months later.

In the Principia, Newton sets out the solution to the inverse problem: he finds what the law of interaction must be for the trajectory to be an ellipse, and shows that it must be the law of “inverse squares”. Moreover, Newton proves that if the law of forces acting between the planet and the Sun deviated even slightly from the inverse square law, then the points of the planet's furthest and furthest distance from the Sun would not be stationary in space. And from the observer it is known that these points (they are called apses) are almost motionless. This Newtonian proof is one of the most brilliant in astronomy.

Let us once again draw attention to what has been said: it can be argued that the inverse square law for attractive forces follows from the mere fact of the immobility of apses; so calculating the force of gravity using Kepler's third law is unnecessary; in fact, the third law is a consequence of the first. For many years, Newton's theory was the basis of celestial mechanics, and although many tried to discover any amendments to the law of universal gravitation, only Einstein was able to find them. Now we know when the law of universal gravitation becomes impure, and we are able to calculate amendments to it. The law of gravity is true as long as the distance between bodies is much greater than the so-called gravitational radius of the attracting body. The gravitational radius of a body with mass $M$ is the quantity

$$R_{\text{rp}} = \frac{2GM}{c^2},$$

where $GM=C$ is the “Kepler constant” already known to us, and $c$ is the speed of light. For the Sun, the gravitational radius is 3 km, and for the Earth - 7 mm. From here we can conclude that the law of universal gravitation on the surface of the Earth may have a relative error equal to approximately $\frac{7 \cdot 10^{-3}M}{6.4 \cdot 10^6M} \approx 10^{-9}$ (radius of the Earth $\approx 6400$ km), and on the surface of the Sun $-\frac{3 \cdot 10^3M}{7 \cdot 10^8M} \approx 0,4 \cdot 10^{-5}$ (radius of the Sun $\approx 700000$ km).
