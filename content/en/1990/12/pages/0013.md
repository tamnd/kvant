Therefore, Galileo responded to Kepler's idea that the ebb and flow of the tides on Earth are associated with the attraction of the ocean waters by the Moon. By Newton's time, things began to change.

Not everyone blindly followed Galileo. Thus, Robert Hooke, based on the analysis of gravity and the attraction of a magnet, tried in 1666 to experimentally determine how the weight of a body changes with height. He conducted experiments with magnets of different lengths in Westminster Abbey in London. It is clear that he was unable to discover anything significant. Further suggesting that the force of attraction varies inversely with the square of the distance, he was unable to put his thoughts into a strict equation form. Hooke did not yet know how to write an equation or solve them, and therefore could not verify the validity of this assumption. Also, another mechanic at that time, the Englishman Wren, could not do anything. Ren also spoke about the inverse square law, but even with him everything is limited to words.

Only in Newton's hands did everything fall into place. The first, and main, idea came to his mind in 1665, when he was fleeing the plague in his family home. As they say, the whole thing began with an apple that fell from a tree. The 23-year-old scientist was struck by the idea that the reason that makes an apple fall to the Earth must be related to the reason that makes the Moon deviate from its straight path. Now this is everyone’s idea, but in Newton’s time the very idea of ​​​​subordinating cosmic bodies by a simple earthly law was unusually bold. But it’s one thing to believe in a bold hypothesis yourself, and another thing to convince others of its validity. To do this, we must at least find a way to test it, find a way, as we say now, to compare theory with experience.

The best place to start was to study the motion of the Moon using existing observational data. This required effort and time. Newton, in addition, was still busy with optical research; and in general, scientists of that time were in no hurry to publish. This is probably why only 20 years later - in April 1686 - Newton presented the first volume of “Principles” to the Royal Society (English Academy of Sciences), and the whole work was published by mid-1687. Only then was the theory presented to other physicists.

Let us write the law of universal gravitation for the Sun and the planet (denoting their masses $M$ and $m$). Power of attraction

$$F = G \frac{Mm}{r^2}.$$

The acceleration imparted by this force to the planet is

$$a = G \frac{M}{r^2}.$$

How did Newton get these formulas?

In his Japanese law of gravitation, Newton proceeded from Kepler's third law: the squares of the average revolution times of the planets are related to the cubes of their average distances from the Sun. (Newton calls this law a half-relation.) Even when Newton compared the fall of an apple and the moon, he knew that if a body moves in a circle, a force acts on this body, attracting it to the center of the circle; he also knew that centripetal acceleration is proportional to the square of the speed and inversely proportional to the distance.

True, part of the discovery of the laws of circular motion is attributed to Huygens, but Huygens published his discovery only in 1673 (although he knew about it back in 1659). Newton therefore had to figure it out on his own.

Newton argues something like this. Kepler's third law states that for planets of the same system

$$\frac{R_1^3}{T_1^2} = \frac{R_2^3}{T_2^2} = ... = const.$$
