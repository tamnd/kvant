Fame did little to change the lifestyle of the artist, who continued to work. He died on March 27, 1971.

Let us now turn to the topic “Escher and Mathematics”. As I already said, mathematicians love Escher. Commentators usually explain how this or that unexpected effect is achieved in his works. Often this is a good excuse for stories about mathematical theories. For example, Escher's ornaments are an excellent illustration of the theory of crystallographic groups (about which «Kvant» is planning stories in one of the upcoming issues).

Escher himself knew little mathematics. However, the famous geometer G. Coxeter invited Escher to his lecture related to the mathematical content of his graviop and lithographs. To mutual disappointment, Asher did not understand almost a word of what Coxeter was talking about. Here's what the artist himself wrote about it:

“I was never able to get a good grade in math. It's funny that I suddenly found myself involved in this science. Believe me, I was a very bad student at school. And now mathematicians use my figure to illustrate their books. Imagine, these learned people accept me into their company as a lost and newly found brother! They don’t seem to suspect that I’m completely mathematically illiterate.”

There is probably some exaggeration in these words. Still, it seems to me that Escher’s work is interesting to mathematicians not only because echoes of specific mathematical results can be found in his works. Rather, they evoked associations with general mathematical ideas. Plato believed that abstract ideas live separately in the “world of pure beings” (such as the ideas of space and time).
