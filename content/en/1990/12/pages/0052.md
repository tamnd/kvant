Once again about surface curvature and tellan expansion

No. 11 «Kvant» for 1989 contains an article by S. L. Tabachnikov “Differential geometry around us.” I was especially interested in the part that talks about the connection between the curvature of a surface and the expansion of bodies. It considers the surface of a heated body in the direction of the external normal at each point at the same distance ε (Fig. 1, a); such movement appears in certain curvatures; his ground is normal). During normal movement, mutually perpedicular segments on the surface of an arbitrary body generally experience different relative distances. This can explain, for example, the continued rupture of sausages during cooking.

Meanwhile, during the tellan expansion of homogeneous amorphous bodies (and cubic crystals), the movement of points of the body is described by Zoomete, a similarity transformation (the center of which for an isolated body coincides with its center of mass; Fig. 1, b). During cooking, the movement of the surface does not always coincide with normal (in part, if you heat a round washer, its hole increases, i.e., the movement of the hole boundary is opposite to normal). Thermal deformation of homogeneous bodies (with uniform heating and free expansion) does not cause internal stresses, since under the specified conditions the bodies are in a free state at each temperature. This means that the described longitudinal discontinuities cannot be associated with purely tellan expansion.

But sausages still burst when boiled! And, moreover, continuously. What then caused this?

It is difficult to think of a better mechanism for longitudinal rupture than that discussed in the article “Differential Geometry Around Us,” normal surface movement. It is only necessary to add that such movement is not associated with tell expansion, but with an additional effect that creates forces acting peredicularly to the surface of the body. Such an effect is, for example, internal pressure. In the case of sausages, it can occur as a result of the appearance of internal compressed steam when the moisture contained in the sausage boils. Here, heating simultaneously leads to two different effects: thermal expansion (homothy) and “pressure” expansion (normal transformation). The first effect changes the size, but not the shape of the body, the second changes both the size of the body and its shape. The actual expansion is described by the superposition of general effects, but the surface rupture can only be associated with the second effect. This is the clarification that should be made to the mechanism under discussion.

It is possible to propose an experiment where only normal transformation would be realized. Let's take inflatable balls, but not spherical, but in the form of “sausages” (Fig. 2). Let's start blowing up the balloons. If this is done slowly enough, the temperature effects will be negated, and the expansion of the ball will be entirely due to the second effect. We inflate the balloons until it bursts. Since the normal deformation of the surface is realized here in its pure form, the rupture of the ball must pass along one of the O′ meridians.

It may turn out that the ball does not burst along the meridian, but in the vicinity of one of the “polios”. This does not contradict the theory: it predicts surface rupture primarily in the region of the crystal filler - and this is precisely the vicinity of the vertices O and O'. By the way, sausages often expand during cooking precisely at the tops - in accordance with the geometry of normal expansion.
