ON THE THEME OF ESHER

Candidate
physical and mathematical sciences
S. TABACHNIKOV

Regular readers of «Kvant» will probably pay attention to reproductions of works by the wonderful Dutch artist M. Escher, which are published from time to time in the magazine. Escher's life, as is often written in biographies of prominent people, was uneventful. Maurice Cornelis Escher was born in Leewarden in 1898. At school, Escher did not do well (another detail in the biography of a famous person!); He was best at drawing. His father, a hydraulic engineer, wanted his son to get a respectable profession; and in 1919 Escher entered the Haarlem School of Architecture and Decorative Arts. In 1922, after studying at the school for two years, Escher moved to Italy, where he lived for 13 years.

Every summer he travels to Southern Italy or Spain. Summer impressions served as material for the engraving, which he worked on in winter. By the mid-thirties, the political climate in Italy had become intolerant. In 1935, Escher's nine-year-old son was forced to wear the uniform of a young fascist; this only followed the family's decision to move to Switzerland.

Cold, snowy Switzerland had a depressing effect on Escher. He approached a shipping company that transported cargo in the Mediterranean with a request to allow him to travel on its ships with payment in graviors carried out along the way. Surprisingly, the proposal was accepted. This was Escher's last great journey. After this, he no longer needed external impressions for creativity.

Since 1941, Escher has lived permanently in Holland. World fame came to him in 1951 after publication in three popular magazines: “The Studio”, “Time” and “Life”. In 1954, a large Escher exhibition was held in Amsterdam, dedicated to the International Mathematical Competition. Ma-

Let us now turn to the topic “Escher and Mathematics”. As I already said, mathematicians love Escher. Commentators usually explain how this or that unexpected effect is achieved in his works. Often this is a good excuse for stories about mathematical theories. For example, Escher's ornaments are an excellent illustration of the theory of crystallographic groups (about which «Kvant» is planning stories in one of the upcoming issues).

Escher himself knew little mathematics. However, the famous geometer G. Coxeter invited Escher to his lecture related to the mathematical content of his graviop and lithographs. To mutual disappointment, Asher did not understand almost a word of what Coxeter was talking about. Here's what the artist himself wrote about it:

“I was never able to get a good grade in math. It's funny that I suddenly found myself involved in this science. Believe me, I was a very bad student at school. And now mathematicians use my figure to illustrate their books. Imagine, these learned people accept me into their company as a lost and newly found brother! They don’t seem to suspect that I’m completely mathematically illiterate.”

There is probably some exaggeration in these words. Still, it seems to me that Escher’s work is interesting to mathematicians not only because echoes of specific mathematical results can be found in his works. Rather, they evoked associations with general mathematical ideas. Plato believed that abstract ideas live separately in the “world of pure beings” (such as the ideas of space and time). In Plato's understanding, Escher's world and the world of mathematics are close neighbors. I will try to illustrate this thesis.

Look at the literature “Belvedere” (see 2nd page), “Waterfall” and “Rising and Falling”. There is something wrong in each of them... Is the staircase the young man is climbing up inside or outside the gazebo? The water flows down all the time - and moves endlessly in a circle. And the monks walk along a closed staircase: they are always going up, while others are going down (by the way, the Golovtsy call meaningless work “monastic work”). Each small fragment of these works flawlessly depicts reality, but overall these fragments add up to impossible objects.

But something similar happens in mathematics!

For example, a circle and a straight line: “in the small”, or as they say in mathematics, locally, are structured the same (if bending is allowed), but “in the whole” - globally - they are completely different. Throw a point out of a straight line and it will break into two pieces; the circle remains connected. Or it will be replaced by a Mobius strip: in the slightest it is indistinguishable from a paper ring, but after going around you find yourself on its opposite side. One of the main subjects of research in topology is objects that are locally structured the same, but globally differently.

Note the frame of the cube in the lower left corner of the Belvedere. Of course, this is an impossible object! The English mathematician Roger Penrose published in 1958 in a psychological journal an image of another impossible object - the “Penrose triangle”. This figure Escher used when working on “Waterfall”. (You will see a whole “constellation” of impossible objects on the cover of Quit No. 2, 1979, and how to get them is explained in No. 5, 1971.)

In order to discuss other general mathematical ideas with which I associate Escher's work, I present this problem to you. You need to prove that there is a place in the city whose image on both maps matches.

The “Less and Fewer” pattern is also well known. This ornament fills the problem about the map. Here, too, the scale decreases towards the center, which serves as the fixed point of all that is good about lizards. On the “Circular Limit” ornament the situation is figurative - the scale decreases towards the periphery figure (you can read about the connection of this ornament with inversion and the so-called modular figure in the article “Geometry of the Circle”, «Kvant» No. 6 for 1977). Finally, in Escher's last work, "Zmen", the scale decreases both towards the center and towards the boundary circle.

The idea of ​​a fixed point is one of the fundamental ones in mathematics. That the processing that reduces the scale has a single fixed point is clear from the solution to the map problem (by the way, why is there a single fixed point?). In fact, any continuous mapping of a circle (or ball) into itself has a fixed point - this is Brouwer's replacement theorem.

The next problem is not at all similar in appearance to the problem of maps, but its solution is based on the same idea of ​​interaction between an object and a sign. Consider a sequence of zeros and ones; the first digit is zero, and then the sequence is constructed according to the rule: to the already constructed piece, a piece of the same length is added to the right, replacing all zeros with ones, and ones with zeros

M = 0110100110010110...

We need to prove that this sequence is not periodic.

We will write 0* together with the pairs 01 and 1* - together with 10. Using these symbols, our sequence is written in exactly the same way as before:

M* = 0*1*1*0*1*0*0*1*...

Careless combination of object and sign can lead to paradoxes like:
what is written on this line is incorrect.
(This is described in the article “The Priest Had a Dog,” «Kvant» No. 6, 1989.) But it can also lead to wonderful results.
The most famous example of this kind is the meaning of Gödel's theorem on the non-hollowness of an argument.
The question is whether any true argumentative statement can be formally proven, that is, deduced from the axioms of argumentation according to the laws of logic (which are also described by axioms). Imagine that you managed to construct a formula that asserts its own unprovability. If this formula is false, then it is provable, and provable statements are true. Therefore, it is true, and therefore unprovable! So, if such a formula can be constructed in the language of argumentation, then argumentation turns out to be incomplete: it contains true but unprovable statements.
This is exactly what the Austrian people managed to do.

To construct the required form, he assigns numbers to all forms and their sequence, according to which the forms are uniquely reconstructed (this is the manifestation of the ideas of object and sign: forms are objects, Gödel numbers are signs). The property of a form to be provable turns out to be recognizable by its number. After this, Gödel constructs forms that express his own unprovability. Of course, this is a very approximate description of Gödel’s work (I recommend on this occasion the replacement book by Yu. I. Manin “Provable and Unprovable”, M.: Radio, 1979). In English there is a book by D. Hofstadter “Gödel, Escher, Bach”, dedicated to Gödel’s theorem and illustrated with reproductions of Escher. (A translation into Russian will be published soon.) Gödel's theorem had the effect of a bomb exploding in the early 1930s, forcing mathematicians to abandon their belief in the omnipotence of the axiomatic method. Of all Escher's works, the best known are his ornaments, i.e., periodic filling of a plane with identical figures. During his trip to Spain, Escher carefully studied and sketched the ornaments in the Alhambra, made during the period of Moorish rule. Arab masters achieved perfection in the art of ornamentation. Islam prohibits the depiction of humans, animals, fish and birds (in accordance with the commandment: “Thou shalt not make an idol!”), so Muslim ornaments are composed of abstract geometric figures. Escher was very interested in the task of composing ornaments using real images as repeating elements. You can judge for yourself the results that he was able to achieve.
