Intersection point $M$
The median of the triangle $ABC$ is the only point of the triangle for which the sum of the vectors $\overrightarrow{MA}, \overrightarrow{MB}$ and $\overrightarrow{MC}$ is zero. The coordinates of the point $M$ (relative to arbitrary axes) are equal to the arithmetic mean corresponding to the vertices of the triangle. From these statements we can obtain a proof of the median theorem. By the way, in the first issue of «Kvant» for this year you can find as many as six proofs of this theorem.

Medians exist not only in geometry, but also in mathematical statistics. Let's say we need to find the average value of some set of numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$. One can, of course, take the arthmetic mean $\frac{a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n}{n}$ as the average.

But sometimes it's inconvenient. Let's say that we need to determine the average height of second-graders in Moscow. Let's interview 100 schoolchildren at random and record their height. If one of the guys jokingly says that his height is a kilometer, then the arithmetic average of the written numbers turns out to be too large. It is much better to take the median of the numbers $a_1, \dots, a_n$ as an average.

Let us assume that there are an odd number of numbers and arrange them in non-decreasing order. The number that ends up in the middle place is called the median of the set. For example, the median of the set of numbers 1, 2, 5, 30, 1, 1, 2 is 2 (and the arithmetic mean is significant)

large - it is equal to 6). We plan to publish an article on the application of the concept of median in statistics in one of the upcoming issues «Kvant».

It turns out that we can talk about media not only for a triangle, but also for a tetrahedron - the fact is that the segments connecting the gravity centers of the tetrahedron faces with opposite vertices always intersect at one point. At this point, four more segments intersect, connecting the midpoints of opposite edges (these segments are sometimes called bi-medians). This point is the center of gravity of the tetrahedron. At the intersection point, the tetrahedron medians are divided in a ratio of 3:1. This can be proven, for example, from mechanical communications by placing weights of unit mass at each of the four vertices of the tetrahedron.
