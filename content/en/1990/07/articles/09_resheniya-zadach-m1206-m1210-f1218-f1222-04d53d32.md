M1207. Prove that for any $x$, $y$ and any natural number $m$ the following inequality holds:
$(x^2 + y^2)^m \geq 2^m x^m y^m + (x^m - y^m)^2$.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the numbers $x$ and $y$ are positive (since $|x|^m |y|^m \geq x^m y^m$). Let's divide both sides of the inequality by $x^m y^m$ and put $t = x/y$, then it will take the form
$$\left(t + \frac{1}{t}\right)^m \geq 2^m + \left(\sqrt{t^m} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{t^m}}\right)^2,$$
or
$$f_m(t) = \left(t + \frac{1}{t}\right)^m - t^m - \frac{1}{t^m} \geq 2^m - 2.$$
We prove the last inequality by induction on $m$.
At $m = 1$ it is obviously fulfilled. Let it now be known that $f_{m-1}(t) \geq 2^{m-1} - 2$ for all $t > 0$. Then
$$f_m(t) = f_{m-1}(t)\left(t + \frac{1}{t}\right) + t^{m-1} + \frac{1}{t^{m-1}} \geq (2^{m-1} - 2) \cdot 2 + 2 = 2^m - 2$$
(here we use the well-known inequality $a + 1/a \geq 2$ for $a > 0$), which is what was required.
Inequality turns into equality only at $t = 1$, i.e. $x = y$.

The idea of ​​a winning strategy for player B when M is a multiple of m(M=ms) is relatively simple. All numbers from M−1 to 0 are divided into pairs, and the player responds to each opponent’s move by drawing a paired number. The difficulty is to guarantee the possibility of a response to any move, and for this, when k>2, the partition is created processively, depending on the moves of A.

Let now M not be a multiple of m: M=ms+n. If 1≤n≤k, then player A takes n matches as the first move, and then acts according to the winning strategy of the second player for M=ms. If M=ms+n, where k<n<m, then player A immediately starts using this strategy for M=m(s+1), conventionally considering that B went first, taking m−n matches (m−n<2k−k=k). This would ensure A's winnings even if B actually had these m−n matches, and even more so when B did not have them.

Now let's explain in more detail how the second player should play with M=ms, if a) k=2 and 6) k=5.

a) Let us divide the numbers from M−1 to 0 into quadruples of consecutive numbers (M=4s), and each quadruple into two pairs of the form (4n−1, 4n−3) and (4n−2, 4n−4). For each move of A, player B responds with a paired number, so A will always “open” a new four by writing down one of its two highest numbers - 4n−1 or 4n−2. After his response to this move, player B will acquire 2 matches, which he will reserve to respond to A's possible re-move to this four (if it is a move 4n−3 or 4n−4). Thus, B is always guaranteed a response move, and therefore a common game.

6) The strategy of the second player for k=5 is generally the same as for k=2. The only difference is to split it into pairs. From the numbers from 0 to M−1, not fours are formed, but groups of m=8 pieces. Each eight is divided into pairs after a certain number has been drawn from it. As in point a), this will be one of the k=5 highest numbers of this eight and will be written out by player A. Answer B and the breakdown into pairs for all five options are given on figure. Briefly, we can say: in response to a move that “opens” a new group, player B draws the smallest possible number from this group, and divides the remaining m−2 numbers into pairs of adjacent ones (without taking into account the two drawn). This rule applies to all values ​​of k. The supply of matches created after B's first move will be sufficient to ensure the possibility of responding to all further moves of A to this group; for k=5 this is clearly visible from figure (the supply consists of 5, 4 or 3 matches), and in the general case it is easy to check, and it is important here that m=4[k/2].

Thus, the answer generated at the beginning of the solution is valid for all values ​​of k.

calculate the work of external forces (taking into account the change in the energy of the balls and the number of impacts on each side of the body - the advancing and the escaping).

We will assume that in the established mode, the average square speed of the balls remains practically unchanged, significantly exceeds in absolute value the speed of the body, and the speed of the center of mass of the balls is practically equal to zero. We will have to check these assumptions in the end.

It is clear that, after hitting the wall, the ball will increase its energy, and after hitting the runner, it will decrease it. The movement of the body occurs along one axis - only this velocity component changes upon impact. Therefore, together with a sphere, we can take a cylinder of cross section $S$, moving along its axis, and take into account the impacts of the balls on its ends. For a ball losing energy

$$\Delta E_1 = m u_x^2 / 2 - m (u_x - 2 v_0)^2 / 2,$$

to receive

$$\Delta E_2 = m (u_x + 2 v_0)^2 / 2 - m u_x^2 / 2,$$

where $u_x$ is the projection of the ball velocity onto the cylinder axis. We calculate the number of impacts on the end using the standard method:

$$N_1 = n S (u_x - v_0) \Delta t / 2, N_2 = n S (u_x + v_0) \Delta t / 2,$$

where $n$ is the “concentration” of balls in the vessel.

In the established case, the average energy of the system remains constant; This means that the change in the energy of the balls per unit time is the power $P$. Let's calculate the speeds of the balls from this correspondence:

$$P \Delta t = \Delta E_2 N_2 - \Delta E_1 N_1 = 4 n S m v_0^2 u_x,$$

$$u_x = P / (4 n m S v_0^2) \approx 30 \text{ м/с.}$$

This is one component of the speed, and the mean square speed of the ball

$$u = u_x \sqrt{3} \approx 50 \text{ м/с.}$$

It actually significantly exceeds the speed of the hull. And the assumption that the speed of the balls does not have time to become equal on average to the speed of the body must be checked differently. Since this installation requires quite a lot of blows between the balls, you need to find the length of the free gap and the time between blows. Actually, this is what is required in the task.

Free gap length

$$\lambda = V / (4 \pi r^2 N) \approx 5 \text{ см.}$$

During the flight of this distance, each ball experiences, on average, one collision; This means that in total $N^2 / 2$ hits will occur during this time (each hit involves two balls). The final number of blows in time $\tau = 1$ min is

F1220. A small canister containing the remaining neon for replenishment is connected for a short time to a large reservoir, where the pressure p is twice as high as in the canister. The container will be disconnected immediately after it is filled. What will be the final pressure of the gas in the can? The exchange of gas with the walls of the can when filling it is to claim.

First, the container contains gas under pressure p/2 at temperature T:

$$\frac{p}{2} V = vRT,$$

where V is the volume of the can, v is the amount of gas. After connecting, a portion of gas $v_1$ will flow from the balloon reservoir into the balloon, which will bring the pressure inside the balloon to the value p at the initial steady temperature $T_x$:

$$pV = (v + v_1)RT_x.$$

For the calculation, we assume that the “external” gas has done some work on the water portion of the gas at a constant pressure p, and due to this work the internal energy of the gas in the can has increased. This allows us to write down a correspondence that includes the total amount of gas in the can and the established temperature.

After some time, the temperature inside the can will be equal to the temperature in the tank, and the pressure $p_x$ will be finally established - it will drop in relation to p by the same number of times as the gas temperature drops:

$$\frac{p_x}{p} = \frac{T}{T_x}.$$

Now let's do the calculations.

Let us select (see figure) that volume $V_H$ of the “external” gas, which then ends up inside the can, and write down the expression for the work:

$$A = pV_H.$$

The volume $V_H$ can be expressed from the equation of state of the gas:

$$V_H = \frac{v_1RT}{p}.$$

Since neon is a monatomic gas, the change in the internal energy of the gas that ends up in the can can be written as

$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} (v + v_1)RT_x - \frac{3}{2} (v + v_1)RT.$$

From the law of conservation of energy

$$A = \Delta U,$$

Taking into account the equation of state of the gas, we obtain

$$T_x = 5/4 T.$$

Then finally

$$p_x = pT/T_x = 0,8 \ p.$$

F1221. Last century, Russian scientist V.I. Sreznevsky studied the evaporation of liquid droplets in the air. Let this evaporation occur at a constant temperature difference due to the supply of a body to the drop from the environment. Considering the flow of a body per unit surface of a spherical drop to be proportional to the temperature difference and inversely proportional to the difference of the drop, find the dependence of the drop’s expansion on time. How long will it take for the drops to finally evaporate if their volume has halved in 10 minutes?

According to the conditions of the problem, in a short time $\Delta t$ an amount of heat is supplied to the surface of a spherical drop of area $4\pi r^2$

$$\Delta Q = \frac{\alpha(T_B - T_K)}{r} 4\pi r^2 \Delta t.$$

Here $r$ is the radius of the drop, $\alpha$ is a constant coefficient depending on the thermal conductivity of the environment, $T_B$ and $T_K$ are the temperatures of the air and the drop $(T_B > T_K)$. This heat is spent on the evaporation of a certain mass of liquid $\Delta m$. If we denote $L$ the specific heat of evaporation of a liquid, then we can write the law of conservation of energy in the form

$$L \Delta m = -\Delta Q = -\alpha(T_B - T_K) 4\pi r \Delta t$$

(the minus sign on the right side indicates that the mass of the liquid kills over time).

But $m = 4/3\pi r^3 \rho_0$, where $\rho_0$ is the density of the substance of the drop; That's why

$$\Delta m = 4\pi r^2 \rho_0 \Delta r.$$

Substituting the last correspondence into the energy equation, we get

$$r \Delta r = -\frac{\alpha(T_B - T_K)}{L \rho_0} \Delta t,$$

or

$$(r^2)' = -\beta,$$

where $\beta = 2\alpha(T_B - T_K)/(L \rho_0)$ is a positive constant. This shows that the square of the radius of the drop kills linearly over time:

$$r^2 = r_0^2 - \beta t,$$

where $r_0$ is the value of the radius at the initial moment of time $t = 0$, and the radius itself kills according to the law (see figure)

$$r = \sqrt{r_0^2 - \beta t}.$$

According to the conditions, during the time $\tau = 10$ min=600 s the radius of the drop decreased by half, i.e. it became $r_0/2$, so

$$\frac{r_0^2}{4} = r_0^2 - \beta \tau,$$

$$\beta = \frac{3}{4} \frac{r_0^2}{\tau}.$$

Let us denote the time of complete evaporation by $\tau_{\text{исп}}$. It is found from the condition

$$r = 0$$ at $t = \tau_{\text{исп}}$,

where

$$\tau_{\text{исп}} = \frac{r_0^2}{\beta} = \frac{4}{3} \tau = 800 \text{ с.}$$

A. Stasenko
