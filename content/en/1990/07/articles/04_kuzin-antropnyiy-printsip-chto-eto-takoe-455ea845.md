ANTHROPIC PRINCIPLE -
WHAT IS THIS?

A. KUZIN

Small introduction

My article is devoted to science’s attempt to answer one of the eternal questions. This question: does a small person mean anything in this huge world? Who is he - the goal of the development of the Universe, the leader of Creation, or something random, nothing in principle different from other processes of nature? Is the appearance of man the result of the entire development of the Universe or is it some kind of random turn? Has life adapted to external conditions that “didn’t even know anything about it,” or are these conditions themselves adapted for the emergence of life?

For a long time, science did not have the facts to answer these questions in a reasoned manner (although there was no shortage of non-reasoned answers). The information about the world that science had was fragmentary; the overall picture of the development of the Universe was not visible. But almost clear centuries of organized service data are bearing fruit. Now we can talk about everything about the scientific picture of the world, about a holistic view of the Universe. Oh, there's still a lot we don't know! Therefore, one should not think that this answer is final; this is not a judicial verdict by which a person is “condemned” to be the crown of Creation. Skrepik is always right about the limitations of scientific knowledge. Questions are eternal because they cannot be answered once and for all.

Scientific formulation of the question about the world and man

The anthropic principle is a child of a thought experiment. The experiment is like this: let’s mentally change something in the laws of nature and think about whether a person can exist at the same time? When answering this question, we must, of course, focus on something important in a person - after all, it is clear that we cannot count on his complete immutability when the rest of the world changes. Please, we will not be mistaken if we say that the two main qualities of a person, without which he cannot be thought of as a person, are reason and freedom. Freedom is the ability to remain oneself, that is, not to be determined entirely by what is around you at a given moment, but to act from within, from oneself. Reason is the condition of freedom, since without it an active response to the environment is impossible (in this sense, animals also have reason). But the natural sciences are not ready to deal with questions about reason and freedom in their general formulation. We need to find something in this global problem that they could cling to. And here is this bake.

Only a complex existing thing can actively resist the environment, understand it and change the communicated demands of its existence. The degree of complexity can be characterized quantitatively, and this is what the natural sciences do.

The anthropic principle is the answer to the question: does every mental world order presuppose the emergence of progressively more complex structures?

I emphasize that the emergence of such structures is only a necessary, and not a sufficient, condition for the emergence of man. But already the analysis of this necessary condition gives us a lot for understanding the process of evolution.

Let us consider the evolution of the Universe from this angle.

According to modern concepts, the Universe is limited in time and space, i.e. its age is finite (about 15 billion years), and so is its volume. The number of particles in the Universe is estimated by scientists to be colossal, but still a finite number $N \sim 10^{80}$. The Universe “started” from a terribly sloped and hot state, the initial radius of curvature was $\sim 10^{-34}$ cm. Since then, the Universe has been continuously expanding, like a balloon inflated with air, and the galaxies, stars, nebulae (matter) that “fill” it are moving away from each other, like patterns on a balloon. Matter, space and time are interdependent; they started together, so questions like “what happened before the universe came into being?” or “what will you see if you reach its edge?” are formulated incorrectly. “Before that there was no time, and it is impossible to reach the limits of the Universe, just as you cannot reach the limits of the Earth.

In the first moments after the “start,” the temperature is so high that no stable structures can form. Further, elementary particles continuously transform into each other. As it expands, the temperature drops and stable particles are formed - electrons, protons, neutrons. This is the first structure, and the first surprising circumstance is associated with its appearance. The circumstance is this: in the initial boiling cauldron there were equal numbers of particles and antiparticles, but when they remained, the symmetry was broken, and the number of particles $N_i$ turned out to be greater than the number of antiparticles $N_a$. Therefore, the Englishness of matter and anti-procession was not complete, not all matter was transformed into light. The wonderful thing is that $(N_i - N_a)/N_i \sim 10^{-9}$, i.e., only one billion particles did not turn into light (we also encountered this number on another occasion).

With further remaining, atoms of the simplest elements are formed - hydrogen and gel. They need a stable proton to exist. As is known, the masses of the proton $m_p$ and neutron $m_n$ differ little: $m_n - m_p \approx 2,5$ $m_e$ ($m_e$ is the mass of the electron). The heavier neutron lives only 16 minutes, then decays into a proton, electron and electron antineutrino:

$$n \rightarrow p + e^{-} + \tilde{v}_e.$$

Inside the nuclei, where the density of nuclear matter and kinetic energy are partial, a figurative reaction occurs. Therefore, inside nuclei, neutrons exist in a state of dynamic equilibrium. If a neutron were lighter than a proton, the proton would decay -

$$p \rightarrow e^{+} + n + v_e$$

(е$^+$ is a positron, $v_e$ is a neutrino), and hydrogen, which serves as the main fuel of stars and the main water necessary for life, would not exist.

But the path of formation of atoms of elements is guaranteed. Note that for now there are only the simplest ones - hydrogen and gel. They were synthesized everywhere at once, and for the synthesis of nuclei of heavier elements they require special conditions: for the synthesis of such nuclei, the simplest stable nuclei are needed as the starting material and high temperatures at which these nuclei can only fuse, and these temperatures must last for a long time (billions of years) - thermonuclear reactions do not proceed quickly. But only over billions, and even hundreds of thousands of years, the Universe already finds itself in a state that fills the present weight - the average density and temperature in it are very low. Such conditions are favorable for the existence of atoms (after all, at high temperatures, electrons will not stay near the nucleus), but are not suitable for the synthesis of their nuclei.

So, the conditions for the sources of atoms and the synthesis of nuclei are incompatible. In order for the conditioning process to go further, it is necessary that the Universe lose its spatial homogeneity. This is due to the action of gravitational forces. When the core of a star is sufficiently enriched in heavy elements, the star explodes with a sharp increase in luminosity (turns into a supernova) and “dumps” most of its mass. Most stars live shorter lives (the lifespan of a star depends on its mass); the reason for their existence is to provide solar-type stars with material from heavy elements.

Quantities like $e, \hbar, G, c$ (speed of light), etc. are called world constants in physics. By themselves, the numerical values ​​of these constants do not know anything, since they change when moving from one system of units to another. But their dimensionless combinations have universal significance. For example, from $e, \hbar$ and $c$ you can make the only dimensionless combination - the number $\alpha = e^2 / \hbar c \approx 1 / 137$. This number is called the fine structure constant; it characterizes the “strength” of electromagnetic interaction. The characteristic energies of interaction between electrons in atoms and free electrons among themselves are only a small (of the order of $\alpha$ to some extent) correction to the rest energy $m_e c^2$. For example, the energy of electrons in atoms with atomic number $Z$ is

$$E \sim m_e c^2 (Z \alpha)^2.$$

Due to the smallness of $\alpha$, processes in which an electron turns into other particles are unlikely. The smallness of $\alpha$ ensures the stability of electron-opposite structures - atoms, solids. Heavy atoms with $Z \gtrsim \alpha^{-1} = 137$ are unstable: the electromagnetic field near the surface of the nucleus is so compressed that it begins to produce electron-positron pairs, which screen the “excess” charge of the nucleus, bringing its value to the value of lesser $e \alpha^{-1}$.

Somehow it happened that the work of a number of German mathematicians of the 20-30s of our century turned out to be connected with textiles. This article is devoted to another subject - fabric geometries created by Wilhelm Blaschke. Fabric geometry has nothing to do with real fabrics - it is simply a branch of geometry connected by many threads to other areas of mathematics. (The name, however, is misleading; and Blaschke received an invitation to a meeting of textile workers. He even decided to change his name to “cot geometry.”)

Fabric Definition

In some domains on the plane there is given a web of d threads (or, more simply, a d-web) if in this domain there are given d families of pairwise non-touching curves and exactly one curve from each family passes through each point of the domain. The curves of each family are called threads of the fabric. Two fabrics are considered identical if one can be transformed into the other by arbitrary deformation (only tearing and gluing are prohibited). Examples of 1 and 2 fabrics are shown in Figures 1 and 2. (Threads of different families are depicted in different colors.)

Let's start studying the d-web. For d=1 there is nothing to explore: a suitable deformation instantly turns 1-webs into a family of parallel lines (see Fig. 1). The situation is no better with 2-weaving. After straightening the first family of curves, we still have the opportunity to move each point along the straight thread passing through it. This is enough to straighten the second family as well (see Fig. 2), so all 2-webs are also constructed in the same way. The substantive theory begins with the case of a 3-web.

Every 2-web on the plane is trivial. But in space the situation is completely different. A three-dimensional region is said to have a 2-web if this region is represented in two ways as a union of pairs

It is even easier to construct an obstacle to a trivial spatial fabric than on a plane. Let us take an arbitrary point $A$, draw it through the curve of the first family and select a point $B$ on it. Through $B$ we draw the curve of the second family and take the point $C$ on it. Now we draw through $C$ the curve of the first, and through $A$ - the second family (Fig. 22). Will these curves intersect? In a trivial matter - yes, but in an arbitrary matter - not at all necessary.

An example of a nontrivial 2-web is constructed as follows. The first family consists of vertical lines, and the second - of horizontal ones. Straight lines lying in the horizontal plane $z = c$ are parallel to each other and make an angle of $c$ radians (Fig. 23) with a fixed horizontal direction. To construct such a fabric, you need to move a horizontal plane with parallel lines marked on it along the vertical axis, turning it at a constant speed. That this web is nontrivial, i.e., not a single quadrilateral on figure 22 is closed for it, I suggest you see for yourself.

The non-closedness of quadrangles made of fabric threads at figure 23 bleibt is an unexpected consequence. Let us construct at each point in space a small flat area containing two threads of spatial fabric passing through this point.

Theorem. There is no damage (further however small) that touches the corresponding area at each point.

Proof. If such a surface existed, then a sufficiently small quadrangle made of fabric threads would lie on this surface and should be closed.

So, in space there is a plane field for which there is not a single damage touching it. This unexpected fact contradicts the intuition developed when working with a plane: on a plane, for any directed field, there exist curves touching it (Fig. 24). Plane fields for which there are no tangential damages are called contact structures and are a very popular subject of research in modern mathematics.
