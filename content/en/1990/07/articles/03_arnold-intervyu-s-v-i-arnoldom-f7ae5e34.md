Interview with
V. I. Arnold

We present to our reader Vladimir Igorevich Arnold - corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences, Lenin Prize laureate, chief researcher at the Mathematical Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences, professor at Moscow State University, vice-president of the Moscow Mathematical Society. Vladimir Igorevich is one of the most active mathematicians of our time with a very wide range of scientific interests. We bring to your attention his answers to our correspondent’s questions.

— How did you become a mathematician? What is the role of family, school, math clubs, Olympiads? Please tell us about your teachers.

— I always hated cramming. Teachers in the lower grades of the city

So I told my parents that a idiot like me would never master the multiplication tables.

The first mathematical opposition was when a real mathematics teacher, Ivan Vasilyevich Morozkin, appeared. I remember a problem about two elders who set out at the same time towards each other, met at noon and reached a foreign city - one at 4 o'clock in the afternoon, and the other at 9. It was necessary to find out when they left.

Algebra wasn't taught yet. Having come up with an “arithmetic” solution (based on messages of dimension or similarity), I first experienced that joy of discovery, the pursuit of which made me a mathematician.

The first mathematical book was “Numbers and Figures” by Rademacher and Toeplitz, at the age of 12. I sorted through several pages a day. A year later, my uncle, technical driller N.V. Zhitkov, told me in one evening what mathematical analysis is. The story ended with the determination of the formula for the surface of water in a spinning glass. After that, I myself found and devoured Grzeville and Luzin’s analysis textbook, and then indiscriminately read all the mathematical books in the library of my early deceased father (I am a fourth-generation mathematician). I especially liked Euler’s “Introduction to the Analysis of Infinite Boys” (partitions, generating functions) and Hermite’s “Course in Analysis” (complex analysis, elliptic internals).

A. A. Lyapunov organized a VNO (“voluntary—or children’s?—scientific society”) at his home. Mathematics and physics coexisted here with chemistry and biology, including genetics, which had just been destroyed (the son of one of our best geneticists studied with me in the same class and then wrote in the questionnaire: “mother is a housewife, father is a housewife”).

At that time*) the University produced math clubs for schoolchildren in grades 7–10. On Sunday

The professors gave lectures to schoolchildren (many of them were published in the series “Popular Lectures on Mathematics”). By the end of school, we already had a pretty clear idea of ​​the advantages (and disadvantages) of most lecturers. Schoolchildren felt falsehood and deception more acutely than students, since they were not yet accustomed to pretending that something is understandable that cannot be understood (today’s downtrodden schoolchildren may have lost this advantage, and there are no more lectures).

The leaders of the circle were A. P. Savin, N. D. Vvedenskaya, T. D. Ventzel, I. A. Vinogradova, now famous mathematicians, all who were students at that time. The circles of that time provided much less knowledge than their modern equivalents, but then every lesson was a holiday. The cult of truth, beauty and independence that dominated the circles (“a student is not a bag that needs to be filled, but a torch that needs to be filled”) limited the amount of knowledge at the expense of quality. It was here, in disputes when analyzing problems, that we learned to fully understand the mathematical side. Because of this, physics moved aside; the beauty of mathematics overshadowed it.

The Olympiads, as well as clubs and lectures, were then organized by the Moscow Mathematical Society and attracted thousands of participants. My success grew from an “honorable review” in the 7th grade to a second prize in the 9th and 10th grades. The emotional significance of the Olympiad was very great, but now I remember more the clubs and lectures. The well-chosen books received in the form of awards at the Olympiads are still valuable: “Visual Geometry” by Hilbert and Cohn-Vossen, “What is Mathematics” by Kurat and Robbins, “Introduction to the Theory of Linear Spaces” by Shilov, “Theoretical Mechanics” and “Analysis” by Vallée-Poussin with a long rectangular sheet “Winner of the Moscow Mathematical Olympics".

— You have been actively involved in mathematics for more than 30 years. Has public attitude towards mathematics and mathematicians changed during this time?

— The attitude of society (not only in the USSR) to fundamental science in general and to mathematics in part is described by I. A. Krylov in the fable “The Pig Under the Oak.” In the difficult years of the forties, mathematics suffered less than other sciences. As you know, Viet was a cipher and delimiter for the French king Henry IV. Since then, some areas of mathematics have been encouraged by all the correctness in the country of mathematical culture.

Over the past 30 years, the representation of mathematicians in society has fallen in all countries. I think that mathematicians themselves are to blame for this (primarily Hilbert and Bourbaki*)), who proclaimed the goal of their science to be the study of all consequences of arbitrary systems of axioms.

— Is the concept of a model applicable to mathematics?

— The development of mathematics fills the rapid rotation of the wheel, from which splashes fly in all directions. A mode is a jet moving tangentially away from the main trajectory. These streams of epilogue works are the most noticeable, and they contain the bulk of the mass, but they inevitably die after a while, coming off the wheel. To stay on the wheel, you need to constantly exert effort in a direction perpendicular to the general flow.

— Since the time of Euclid, the criteria for mathematical rigor, as far as I know, have not changed.

The computer provides great opportunities for experimentation, and I use it, along with the slide rule and the multiplication table. I think that without experimentation of one kind or another, great mathematical results would not have been obtained. Computer experiments have added something to the replacement work of Julius, Fatou, and others on iterations of polynomials. Fractal sets are just a term.

It is difficult for mathematicians to agree that the introduction of a new term, not supported by new theorems, is significant progress. However, the success of “cybernetics”, “fractals”, “synergetics”, “catastrophe theory” and “country attractors” shows the fruitfulness of word creation as a method of scientific work.

“It’s hard to believe,” said Poincaré, “what a huge economy of thought can be achieved by one well-chosen word. Often all it takes is one new word to be invented, and that word becomes a creation.” By leaving scientific terms (“files”, “interfaces”...) without translation, we lose all the power of this method.

As for the “catastrophe theory,” this term was invented to attract the attention of the general public to a truly important mathematical achievement - to the theory of singularities of smooth mappings and to the theory of bifurcations*) of dynamical systems. The simplest conclusions of the theory of catastrophes (for example, that continuous movement from a bad steady state to a good one leads to a deterioration of the state, that the rate of this deterioration increases as we move towards a better regime, which accompanies

The facts of fast rotating bodies in the theory of gyroscopes. When applied to our problem of the motion of a part on a plane, the result is:

Theorem. The orbit remains forever in a narrow ring between two close level lines of functions H, under the assumption that these level lines are closed and that the radius Cv/H is sufficiently small.

The reader who has a computer can check this experimentally. The experiment took place in a system with a discrete analogue of the same theory. Consider the mapping $T = AB$ of the plane onto itself, where $A$ is the rotation by the angle $2\pi/q$, and $B(x, y) = (x, y + a \sin x)$ (q is an integer, say 5, and a small parameter, say 0.05).

Theorem. The point $(P, T^q P, T^{2q} P, \dots)$ lies on the main closed curve for most initial points $P$ (if the parameter a is small enough).

For “atypical” $P$, the trajectory gradually fills a beautiful unbounded funkru, which G. M. Zaslavsky, R. Z. Sagdeev and their co-authors call stochastic scientific.

The same mathematical theory also proves the stability of oscillations of a matrix turned upside down, the “suspension point” of which rapidly oscillates in the vertical direction (we are talking about nonlinear oscillations in the absence of friction). An experimental setup can be easily made on the basis of a vibrating electric razor, or an electric sewing machine (P. L. Kapitsa), or an accelerator with hard focusing (A. M. Budker).

c) The reader's formula for solving the quadratic equation is well known. Equations of degree 3 and 4 are also solved using radicals. Equations of degree 5 are no longer solved in radicals, but they can all be reduced to one specialized equation $f^5 + xf + 1 = 0$. So the answer is expressed in terms of arithmetic operations, roots and one special function $f(x)$ of one variable $x$. An equation of degree 6 can be reduced in the same way to a specialized function of two variables.

From functions of two variables, substituting one into the other, you can create functions of any number of variables (for example, $f(g(x, y), h(z, y))$ is a function of three variables). D. Hilbert formulated his 13th problem as follows: how can non-interruptive functions of three variables be expressed by superpositions of interruptive functions of two variables? Hilbert assumed that the function $f(x, y, z)$ defined by the equation $f^7 + xf^3 + yf^2 + zf + 1 = 0$ is no longer representable.

Theorem. Any nonprime function of any number of variables is represented by a superposition of interruptive functions of two variables.

The representability of functions of a large number of variables by superpositions of functions of three variables was discovered by A. N. Kolmogorov, so all I had to do was move from three variables to two.

The question of representability by superpositions consisting of algebraic functions remains open and very interesting. The “topological complexity” of branching a multi-entry function is expected to translate into representability.

2) Reader «Kvant» knows, of course, what curves defined by an equation of the second degree look like: ellipses, hyperbolas and parabolas. What curves of higher degrees may look like is unknown to anyone. The largest number of components (ovals) of which a curve of degree $n$ can consist is equal to

$$N = \frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2} + 1$$

(points at infinity are taken into account along with common ones, so a hyperbola, for example, consists of one oval).

The question of what the relative position of these $N$ ovals on the plane might be is part of Hilbert's 16th problem. With $n=4$ all $N=4$ ovals are located in another other (why?). Let's make ovals that are inside an even number of others into even ones, and an odd number into odd ones.

(all fourth ovals of the curve of the fourth degree are even).

Theorem. The difference between the number of even and the number of odd ovals of a curve with the maximum possible number of ovals for a given degree $n=2k$ is comparable to $k^2$ modulo 8.

Example Of the 11 ovals of the curve of degree 6, either one, or 5, or 9 can lie inside the others (why?). In particular, all 11 ovals cannot lie outside each other.

The history of this theorem is as follows. Gorky mathematician D. A. Gudkov used all the arrangements of ovals of a curve of degree 6. He noticed that the comparison indicated was always carried out in the examples he examined, and hypothesized that it was always carried out. I.G. Petrovsky asked me to check Gudkov’s work, which was technically very complex. Gudkov’s hypothesis shocked me, since there was no visible connection between the location of the ovals and arithmetic. But I remembered that divisibility theorems by 8 occur in four-dimensional topology. It turned out that the location of the ovals of the plane curve $f(x, y)=0$ is controlled by the four-dimensional manifold formed by the complex solutions of the equation $f(x, y)=z^2$.

The discovery of this connection between ovlov gaps and four-dimensional topology (and through it, with the arithmetic of integer square forms) made it possible to prove Gudkov’s conjecture modulo 4. After this, V. A. Rokhlin, a leading specialist in four-dimensional topology, proved Gudkov’s conjecture in full. Since then, many remarkable results have been obtained in real algebraic geometry (some were even discussed in «Kvant»). However, what are the possible arrangements of the twenty-two ovels of a curve of degree 8 is still unknown.

Specific topological questions of this kind (for polynomials of fixed degree) reduce in principle to finite algebraic calculations. But these calculations, apparently, exceed the capabilities of modern computers - in any case, so far not a single new result in this area has been obtained with the help of computers.
