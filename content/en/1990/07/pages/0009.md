Theorem. The difference between the number of even and the number of odd ovals of a curve with the maximum possible for a given degree $n=2k$ the number of ovals is comparable to $k^2$ modulo 8.

The history of this theorem is as follows. Gorky mathematician D. A. Gudkov used all the arrangements of ovals of a curve of degree 6. He noticed that the comparison indicated was always carried out in the examples he examined, and hypothesized that it was always carried out. I.G. Petrovsky asked me to check Gudkov’s work, which was technically very complex. Gudkov’s hypothesis shocked me, since there was no visible connection between the location of the ovals and arithmetic. But I remembered that divisibility theorems by 8 occur in four-dimensional topology. It turned out that the arrangement of ovals of a flat curve $f(x, y)=0$ governs the four-dimensional manifold formed by complex solutions of the equation $f(x, y)=z^2$.

The discovery of this connection between ovlov gaps and four-dimensional topology (and through it, with the arithmetic of integer square forms) made it possible to prove Gudkov’s conjecture modulo 4. After this, V. A. Rokhlin, a leading specialist in four-dimensional topology, proved Gudkov’s conjecture in full. Since then, many remarkable results have been obtained in real algebraic geometry (some were even discussed in «Kvant»). However, what are the possible arrangements of the twenty-two ovels of a curve of degree 8 is still unknown.

— One of the first popularizers, Faraday, came to the conclusion that “the popular has never been honorable, and the honorable is popularism.” This Faraday effect is easy to explain: as N. Bohr noted (see the article by R. Peierlas in «Kvant» No. 10, 1988), clarity and truth are quantum complementary.

Trying to pre-fly this complementarity «Kvant» deserves all sorts of praise, but, unfortunately, it is somewhat eclectic - the mathematical and physical parts are poorly coordinated. Next to the brilliant article by M. P. Bronstein about RENTNOV’S BEST MANY MODERN POPULARIZERS, alas, look helpless. The departments dedicated to tutoring and chess are especially depressing. The drawings are often hampered by color and, in my opinion, an unnecessary departure from the encyclopedic traditions of the 18th century. The problems are certainly good, especially those for younger students.
