Six-foot fabrics

All fabrics considered so far have been trivial. And you might even get the impression that there were no other fabrics at all. In fact, a trivial 3-web is more of an “unfortunate case” than a general rule: a random web will be non-trivial. But how to prove that any fabric is non-trivial? For this purpose, it would be good to come up with a quantity that vanishes if the tissue is trivial, and differs from zero otherwise.

Let's draw three threads of fabric through point $O$. On one of them we select point $A$ (Fig. 10) and draw the thread of the second family through it until it intersects at point $B$ with the thread of the third family passing through $O$. Through $B$ we draw the thread of the first family until it intersects at point $C$ with the thread of the second family passing through $O$. And so on. We will plot points $D, E, F$ and $G$; Moreover, for the web on figure 3, and therefore for any trivial web, the points $A$ and $G$ coincide. For an arbitrary 3-web this is not at all necessary.

The non-closedness of the hexagon $ABCDEFG$ is an obstacle to the trivial fabric. I suggest you arm yourself with a compass and a ruler and check that for a fabric composed of tangents to two concentric circles and straight lines passing through their common center, the hexagonal figures do not close (Fig. 11). This means that this fabric is non-trivial.

The degree of openness of the hexagon, i.e., the distance between the points $A$ and $G$, changes the difference between a 3-web and a trivial one.
