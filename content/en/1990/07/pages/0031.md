Now let's discuss one more question. Why are the smallest small-feeding waters still larger than the smallest tell-blooded inhabitants of the soup?

The fact is that shallow-feeding waters, even those with an insulating fatty layer, give up a lot of body water, and their existence becomes possible only with a sufficiently large body volume.

The main mechanism that regulates body temperature is its “central heating” - the circulatory system. The blood delivers the body from the internal organs to the capillaries under the skin, and it then separates the body from the surrounding air. For example, for a person at rest at a body temperature of 37 °C and an ambient temperature of 18 °C, body consumption is distributed in this way: approximately 75% is lost due to radiation in the infrared range with a wavelength of 9-10 microns, the rest is due to sweating and the release of air heated in the lungs. As the ambient temperature increases, the radiation consumption of the body decreases: at 30 °C they are about 30%.

Overheating of the body is eliminated by profuse sweating - it takes about 2,500 joyles to evaporate every gram of moisture from the surface of the skin. It is interesting to note that the specific power of a person (that is, the power per unit of his mass) is about 200 W/kg, which is almost 10,000 times the specific power of the Sun.

The thermoregulation system is very flexible and in tropical conditions provides a person with the opportunity to do without clothing. In general, the human body and many other animals are better adapted to cooling than to overheating. A decrease in body temperature by 10-12° is not yet fatal, whereas with an increase in temperature by 5-6° the proteins begin to coagulate. (This is why a medical thermometer does not have divisions above 42 °C.) For humans, death occurs when the body temperature is above 43 °C or below 25 °C. Over millions of years of evolution, humans have “acquired” a system of thermostats that help them maintain their temperature at a constant level.
