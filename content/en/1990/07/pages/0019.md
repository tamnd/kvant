Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. TAVACHNIKOV

Somehow it happened that the work of a number of German mathematicians of the 20-30s of our century turned out to be connected with textiles. Emil Artin, working under a contract with a textile factory, built one of the most popular theories in modern mathematics - the theory of braids (see the article "Braids and Knots", «Kvant» No. 2 for 1989). This article is devoted to another subject - fabric geometries created by Wilhelm Blaschke. Fabric geometry has nothing to do with real fabrics - it is simply a branch of geometry connected by many threads to other areas of mathematics. (The name, however, is misleading; and Blaschke received an invitation to a meeting of textile workers. He even decided to change his name to “cot geometry.”)

Fabric Definition

In some domains on the plane there is given a web of d threads (or, more simply, a d-web) if in this domain there are given d families of pairwise non-touching curves and exactly one curve from each family passes through each point of the domain. The curves of each family are called threads of the fabric. Two fabrics are considered identical if one can be transformed into the other by arbitrary deformation (only tearing and gluing are prohibited). Examples of 1 and 2 fabrics are shown in Figures 1 and 2. (Threads of different families are depicted in different colors.)

Let's start studying the d-web. For d=1 there is nothing to explore: a suitable deformation instantly turns 1-webs into a family of parallel lines (see Fig. 1). The situation is no better with 2-weaving. After straightening the first family of curves, we still have the opportunity to move each point along the straight thread passing through it. This is enough to straighten the second family as well (see Fig. 2), so all 2-webs are also constructed in the same way. The substantive theory begins with the case of a 3-web.
