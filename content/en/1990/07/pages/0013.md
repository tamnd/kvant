As the protostar compacts, the temperature in it increases until the thermonuclear reactions that have begun, accompanied by a powerful release of energy, leave the scatium.

It is important that the first star consists mainly of hydrogen (the hydrogen:helium ratio in the young Universe is approximately 3:1) - and this is precisely because, since protons are lighter than neutrons, there are more of them and they go entirely to the formation of helium. Helium stars would be very hot and would not live long, not long enough for biological evolution to occur on their planets.

“Alchemical” transformations take place in the depths of stars - the nuclei of light elements collide, fuse and turn into the nuclei of heavier elements. And here the condition process is determined by a very subtle “tuning” of the forces of nuclear and electromagnetic interactions. Without this adjustment, the chain of reactions leading from gelium through carbon and oxygen to iron and further could be broken at the first links.

I will give the most striking example that gets to the heart of the matter. At a temperature of $T \sim 100$ ml. In the depths of stars, helium turns into carbon:

$$3^4\text{He} \rightarrow ^{12}\text{C} + 2\gamma$$

(sign $\gamma$ acquaintance-quantum). The probability of meeting three nuclei $^4\text{He}$ in the rarefied plasma of a star is very small (the time during which the reaction occurs is only $10^{-21}$ s), and reaction (1) must proceed very slowly, in any case, not fast enough to produce the amount of carbon that is necessary for the formation of a planet like Earth.

It does not turn out that reaction (1) occurs in two stages. In the first stage, two kernels $^4\text{He}$ form a kernel $^8\text{Be}$:

$$2^4\text{He} + (99 \pm 6)\text{кэВ} \rightarrow ^8\text{Be}$$

(2)

Indication of energy in brackets *) indicates that reaction (2) does not occur without energy supply; This means that $^8\text{Be}$ is profitable to disperse. (If this nucleus were stable, then the “second” stage, leading to the formation of $^{12}\text{C}$, would become less and less probable over time - $^4\text{He}$ would be “exhausted” for the formation of $^8\text{Be}$.) The nucleus $^8\text{Be}$, which has not yet had time to decay, fuses with the nucleus $^4\text{He}$:

$$^8\text{Be} + ^4\text{He} \rightarrow ^{12}\text{C} + 2\gamma$$

(3)

The probability of a two-stage reaction (2-3) is much greater than the probability of reaction (1) because the $^8\text{Be}$ nucleus “lives” 10,000 times longer than the lifespan of helium nuclei. But that's not the only reason. Another surprising circumstance plays a role here. The rate of nuclear reactions does not monotonically depend on the energy $E$ of colliding particles. A sharp increase in the rate of a reaction is called resonance, and the energies at which this occurs ($E_1, E_2$ to figure) are called resonance energies. These energies are determined only by the structure of the reaction product nucleus. So, reaction (3) turns out to be resonant: the resonance energy of the $^{12}\text{C}$ nucleus ($7,656 \pm 0,008$ MeV) is only slightly higher than the sum of the rest energies of the $^8\text{Be}$ and $^4\text{He}$ nuclei ($7,3667$ MeV) - this gap is bridged due to the high temperature in the interior of the star.
