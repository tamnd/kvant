A web is called hexagonal if for any initial points $O$ and $A$ the hexagon is closed at figure 10. hexagonality is not only necessary, but also sufficient for trivial fabric. Idea of ​​the proof: let’s build the hexagon into triangular “honeycombs” (Fig. 13). These links can be transformed by warping into three sets of parallel lines. Digression: Pappus configuration

I promised to show you how fabric geometry relates to other branches of mathematics. In this and the next paragraph we will talk about its mutual relations with projective and algebraic geometry.

Take a ruler and draw two straight lines. On each of them, mark 3 points: $A, B, C$ and $D, E, F$ (Fig. 18). Now connect the pairs of dots: $AE, AF, BD, BF, CD, CE$. Three new points will appear on the drawing: $K, L$ and $M$. And now - attention: apply a ruler to these points and you will see that they lie on the same straight line. This statement is the famous Pappus theorem, and the configuration depicted in figure 18 is called the Pappus configuration.

I will not prove Pappus' theorem. Not because it's too difficult, but because it would take us far from the main stat pool. You can read the proof of this theorem, as well as other statements of this point, in the article “Pascal's Hexagrams and Cubic Curves” in «Kvant» No. 8 for 1987. I advise you to read this very informative article - its topic is related to the geometry of rectilinear fabrics.
