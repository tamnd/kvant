There are ideas
and not alone

In the September issue of the magazine last year, our reader was asked to solve the following problem:

An ancient Greek statue of great artistic value fell overboard from an ocean liner. With the help of underwater television cameras, it was possible to find the place at great depths where the statue lies, partially buried in or.

It is necessary to raise the statue in a fairly short time (according to forecasts, a strong storm is approaching). It is impossible to equip a diving expedition within such a period of time. On board the liner there are cables and winches of sufficient group capacity. Equipment for connecting cables to the statue can be dropped by parachute from an airplane (it can be quickly called from the shore). However, the problem is that the statue is too delicate a load: it can be damaged by cables and arms. How would it be?

We warned those who undertake solving this problem: under no circumstances should they start searching for a solution through a chaotic search of options or inventing cumbersome devices. First of all, you need to understand what needs to be done, and then judge what physical effect you know can help achieve the desired result.

The problem is considered solved if the basic idea is found, i.e. you have found the same physical effect.

So what ideas did our readers come up with in connection with the task “Statue from the Day of the Sea”? Let's turn to the letters.

Many people suggest using suction cups to remove the statue. Here is what Victor Altuv from Krasnoyarsk writes: “It is best to use suction cups. The force with which they will attach to the surface of the statue is proportional to the depth at which the statue is located.” Everything is correct, but Victor did not take into account three things: 1) the statue is uneven, so it is not easy to attach a suction cup to it so that it holds well; 2) the statue fell into the ile, and then, when they learn to lift it, a vacuum will form under it, and the force of hydrostatic pressure will impede the removal of the statue (remember the “Magdeburg hemispheres”); 3) As the statue rises, the pressure pressing the suction cup against the statue will drop, and the statue may tear off. Of course, all these obstacles are pre-emptive. For example, you can improve the suction cups, blur them, or simultaneously with pulling the statue, pick up the statue with a net somewhere in the middle of the way. But this is still difficult to achieve.

Some letters suggest using literature to attach a cable to the statue, and then there will be no difficulty in removing it. Well, not bad.

But problems arise with the subsequent separation of literary works. If the guys knew that there are electrorheological and magnetorheological liquids that change their viscosity up to the point of “hardening” in electrostatic and magnetic fields, respectively, then they would have come up with a completely patentable solution. Such liquids can be easily removed in the absence of a field.

Dmitry Konovalov from Petrozavodsk suggested using liquid metal to attach a cable to the statue: “If you hang a container of solder from the statue and melt the metal using heat, then after the metal hardens we will get a reliable connection, thanks to which it will not be difficult to pull the statue out of the face.” It should be noted that a similar method of lifting objects from the seabed was recently patented in the UK. But you must admit that solder can damage the statue. And then - what is the statue made of? After all, you can’t solder a cable to any material.

The most beautiful solution, in our opinion, is to freeze the statue to the gripping element lowered on a cable. Compressed gases or dry ice can be used as a source of cold, which can be delivered by a special aircraft and lowered by parachute. This solution was proposed by members of the “Young Inventors” circle from the Sepych school in the Vereshaginsky district of the Perm region, A. Shkolnikov (Moscow), A. Skabelin (Baranovich) and many other guys.

The most consistent answers were sent by Alexander Chernov from Moscow and the Andes.
