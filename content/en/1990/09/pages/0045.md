In addition, you can notice that with internal engagement, the rotation of the gears will occur in one direction, and with external engagement, in opposite directions. With the help of such (and more complex) gears, the hands in the clock are connected, power is transmitted in the jack, the speed is changed and the automatic machine is “reverse.”

Now let's consider a more complex plane-parallel motion of a rigid body, when the body rotates, but the rotation itself moves forward in space. The easiest way to find the speed of such movement is to use the concept of an instantaneous center of rotation - this is the name given to the point whose speed at a given moment in time is zero. The velocities of all other points of the body will be the same as if the body were rotating around a fixed axis, passing through the instantaneous center of rotation.

As an example, let us analyze the movement of a wheel on a straight section of track without slipping. Since the wheel does not slip, the point $C$ (Fig. 2), at which the wheel touches the road, has a speed equal to zero. This means that point $C$ is the instantaneous center of rotation. Let's find what the angular speed of rotation of the wheel will be if the speed of its center $O$ (the speed of translational motion of the wheel) is equal to $v_0$:

$$v_0 = \omega r, \text{ и } \omega = v_0 / r.$$

The speed of an arbitrary point $A$ is also easy to find. It is directed along the line $AB$ and is equal to

$$v_a = \omega \cdot CA = 2\omega r \cos \alpha = 2v_0 \cos \alpha.$$

figure 2 also shows the trajectory that each point on the rim of a rolling wheel describes. You will encounter this amazing curve - a cyclide - in many cases: along such a trajectory of motion of a particle in “crossed” (mutually perpendicular) electric and magnetic fields, a ball rolls along a cyclide in a minimum time, etc.

What if we want to determine the speed of the wheels of a moving train? We will find that there are points on the rim of the wheel that move backwards, in the direction opposite to the movement of the train. These are the points of the protruding part of the wheel, for example, point $A$ on figure 3. The instantaneous center of rotation (point $C$) is located on a stationary rail along which the wheel rolls without slipping. The trajectory of the wheel rim point is even more precise than in the previous case - there are return sections of movement (loops) on the trajectory. It is interesting to note that the planets describe similar loop-like trajectories in their motion. They were observed by ancient Greek astronomers, but they could not explain what they saw. Now we know that these trajectories are associated with the superposition of two rotations - the Earth and the observed planet around the Sun.
