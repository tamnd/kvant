M1218. On the segment AC, point B is taken and arcs are constructed: AC=α and BC=β, the sum of their degree values ​​is equal to α+β=360°, lying in the same half-plane from the straight line AC. An arbitrary arc AB intersects them at points K and L (Fig. 1). Prove that in every possible way the lines KL intersect the line AC at the same point.

Let us extend the arc β to the circle (Fig. 1) and extend KL until it intersects with the new arc at point M. Then the lines AK and CM will be parallel, since $<KAB = 180^\circ - \angle KLB = \angle BLM = \angle BCM$. Let O be the intersection point of KM and BC; it is enough to prove that the ratio OA:OC does not depend on the choice of point K. Due to the similarity of triangles OAK and OCM, this ratio is equal to AK:CM. The angular magnitudes of the arcs AK and CM are equal, since the arcs AKC and CMB are equal in angular magnitude (360°-β-α), and the arcs KC and MB subtend equal written angles KAC and MCB and are also equal in magnitude. Consequently, the ratio of AK and CM is equal with respect to the radii of the arcs α and β, and therefore does not depend on K.

In this solution, we implicitly used the fact that point M lies on the “completed” arc. However, it cannot be wrong. In fact, it is easy to see that the chords AK and CM are parallel for any arrangement of the secants ABC and KLM (Fig. 2). And if K and M were on the same side of BC, then the sum of the arcs KC and BM, equal to 2 ($\angle KAB + \angle BCM) = 2 \cdot 180^\circ$), would be equal to the sum of the arcs AKC and BMC, which is incorrect.

From the solution it is clear that point O is the center of the rotation of the arcs AKB and CMB; in fact, the solution is built on this idea. But it was also possible to apply a more cunning transformation - inversion (it was described in the article by V. Uroev “Inversion” in «Kvant» No. 5, 1984). For readers familiar with inversion and its properties, we indicate two different ways to use it in our problem. The first is based on the fact that during inversion with center O and coefficient k (defined by the equality OA·OB=OC²=k), the circles α and β transform into each other, and the variable (red on figure 1) circle transforms into itself. Therefore, the points K and L change places, and, therefore, O∈KL. The second way is to use it with center C and coefficient, say CA·CB. After inversion, the problem conditions turn into the following cumbersome, but perfect utteridge (Fig.3):

from points A and B on one side of AB, rays α and β are drawn so that $\angle \alpha AB = \angle \beta BA$, as well as an arbitrary arc AB intersecting the rays at points K and L; then the circle CKL, where C is a fixed point of the line AB, intersects AB a second time at the same point O.
