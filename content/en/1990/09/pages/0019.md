R. MORIN, R. HOWBY (USA)

Ultrasound diagnostics is a relatively inexpensive method for studying the structure and functioning of organs.

Like reference sound, ultrasound is waves propagating through a medium. In medicine, ultrasonic waves with frequencies from 1 to 10 MHz are used (for comparison: reference sound has frequencies from 30 Hz to 20 kHz). The speed of propagation of sound waves in a material depends on the modulus of volumetric elasticity $B$ and on the density of materials $Q: v = \sqrt{B/Q}$. For soft tissue its value is expressed from 1400 to 1600 m/s. The corresponding wavelengths lie in the range of 0.14-1.6 mm. (In such solid materials as bones, the speed of sound reaches 4000 m/s.) When a wave passes from one medium to another, it is partially reflected from the boundaries, and the ratio of the amplitudes of the transmitted and reflected waves is determined by the ratio of the so-called active impedances of these media (this is somewhat reminiscent of the partial reflection of a fresh quantum: D. Halliday, R. Resnick “Fundamentals of Physics”, Third edition extended, 1988. (John Wiley & Sons, N.Y.)

The acoustic impedance of a medium with a modulus of volumetric elasticity $B$ and a density $Q$ is equal to $Z = \rho v = \sqrt{B Q}$. The greater the difference in impedance between the two media, the more energy is reflected back at their interface. Analysis of ultrasound signals reflected from the boundaries of organs allows one to construct fairly clear images.

An ultrasonic signal is obtained using a device that converts electrical voltage fluctuations into mechanical vibrations. The same device in some electronic circuits operating in pulsed mode can also be used to convert ultrasonic vibrations back into a weak electrical signal. In installations operating in continuous mode, various devices are used to emit and detect waves.

The measurements are quite complicated, since the wave loses a lot of energy when propagating through tissue.
