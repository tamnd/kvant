R. MORIN, R. HOWBY (USA)

Ultrasound diagnostics is a relatively inexpensive method for studying the structure and functioning of organs.

Like reference sound, ultrasound is waves propagating through a medium. In medicine, ultrasonic waves with frequencies from 1 to 10 MHz are used (for comparison: reference sound has frequencies from 30 Hz to 20 kHz). The speed of propagation of sound waves in a material depends on the modulus of volumetric elasticity $B$ and on the density of materials $Q: v = \sqrt{B/Q}$. For soft tissue its value is expressed from 1400 to 1600 m/s. The corresponding wavelengths lie in the range of 0.14-1.6 mm. (In such solid materials as bones, the speed of sound reaches 4000 m/s.) When a wave passes from one medium to another, it is partially reflected from the boundaries, and the ratio of the amplitudes of the transmitted and reflected waves is determined by the ratio of the so-called active impedances of these media (this is somewhat reminiscent of the partial reflection of a fresh quantum: D. Halliday, R. Resnick “Fundamentals of Physics”, Third edition extended, 1988. (John Wiley & Sons, N.Y.)

The acoustic impedance of a medium with a modulus of volumetric elasticity $B$ and a density $Q$ is equal to $Z = \rho v = \sqrt{B Q}$. The greater the difference in impedance between the two media, the more energy is reflected back at their interface. Analysis of ultrasound signals reflected from the boundaries of organs allows one to construct fairly clear images.

An ultrasonic signal is obtained using a device that converts electrical voltage fluctuations into mechanical vibrations. The same device in some electronic circuits operating in pulsed mode can also be used to convert ultrasonic vibrations back into a weak electrical signal. In installations operating in continuous mode, various devices are used to emit and detect waves.

The measurements are quite complicated, since the wave loses a lot of energy when propagating through tissue. For example, for waves with a frequency of 1 MHz, the attenuation is approximately 100 dB/m. This means that this (reflected signal) comes from a distant surface and is very weak. Differences

Ultrasound can be used to measure the speed of blood flow in blood vessels. The measurement method is based on the so-called secondary Doppler effect - the dependence of the frequency of the reflected signal on the speed of movement of the reflector. If reflecting particles (for example, red blood cells) move at a speed and at an angle θ to the direction of propagation of the wave, then the frequency of the wave reflected in the opposite direction will be shifted relative to the original part v by Δv = 2viсos θ/v. For u=0.1 m/s and v=2 MHz at θ=0 this shift is 260 Hz. If we compose the beats from the reflected and original waves, then the beat frequency will lie in the following range (130 Hz). By the frequency shift, one can judge the state of the blood vessels (for example, when the blood vessels dry out, the speed of blood flow decreases). When changing the speed, the emitter is adjusted so that the beats are loud and frequent. The signal sounds like a series of “bang-bang” in time with the beats of the heart. The machine then determines the value of Δ.

The intensity of ultrasound used for diagnostics usually does not exceed 10³ W/m². As far as is known, there are no harmful side effects at these intensities.

At higher intensities, the tissue is heated quite strongly. Sometimes it is used to relieve acute painful pain in the back, shoulder girdle, and also for muscle damage.

At intensities above 7×10⁶ W/m², the temperature increase can lead to tissue damage. At 10⁷ W/m² or more, pressure drops in the ultrasonic wave can lead to the formation and collapse of microcavities. These effects are used in surgery.
