Kinematika of plane-parallel motion

What do the rotation of the Earth around its axis, the movement of clock hands, automatic wheels on a straight section of a path, etc. have in common? From the point of view of kinematics, all these are examples of the so-called plane-parallel motion of a rigid body, in which all points of the body move in parallel planes.

Of course, in the most general case, different points of the body can move in different ways. However, it is nice to highlight two special cases and talk about the two simplest types of motion - translational motion and rotation around a fixed axis. In the first case, all points of the body make the same movements. This movement is the simplest, and we will not discuss it here. In the second case, all points describe identical trajectories - circles with centers on the rotation axis. The main characteristic of such movement is the angular velocity of rotation $\omega$.

Let's calculate, for example, the angular velocities of the clock hands. Since the arrows rotate uniformly, the angle of rotation $\varphi$ changes over time according to the law $\varphi = \omega t$ (equation of uniform rotation). The second hand makes one revolution every 60 seconds; therefore, its angular velocity is equal to $\omega_c = 2\pi/60$ s=0.1047 $c^{-1}$. Similarly, the speed of the clock hand is $\omega_u = 2\pi/(12\cdot 60\cdot 60)$ s=0.0001455 $c^{-1}$.

How can you ensure that the second and hour hands move at different angular velocities, but synchronously? It turns out that you can use a gear to do this: two gears can rotate around different axes, but at the same time they engage with each other (Fig. 1). Since the linear velocities of point $A$ are the same -

$$v_a = \omega_1 R = \omega_2 r,$$

we get

$$\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{r}{R} = \frac{z_1}{z_2},$$

where $z_1$ and $z_2$ are the number of teeth of the gears.

In addition, you can notice that with internal engagement, the rotation of the gears will occur in one direction, and with external engagement, in opposite directions. With the help of such (and more complex) gears, the hands in the clock are connected, power is transmitted in the jack, the speed is changed and the automatic machine is “reverse.”

Now let's consider a more complex plane-parallel motion of a rigid body, when the body rotates, but the rotation itself moves forward in space. The easiest way to find the speed of such movement is to use the concept of an instantaneous center of rotation - this is the name given to the point whose speed at a given moment in time is zero. The velocities of all other points of the body will be the same as if the body were rotating around a fixed axis, passing through the instantaneous center of rotation.

As an example, let us analyze the movement of a wheel on a straight section of track without slipping. Since the wheel does not slip, the point $C$ (Fig. 2), at which the wheel touches the road, has a speed equal to zero. This means that point $C$ is the instantaneous center of rotation. Let's find what the angular speed of rotation of the wheel will be if the speed of its center $O$ (the speed of translational motion of the wheel) is equal to $v_0$:

$$v_0 = \omega r, \text{ и } \omega = v_0 / r.$$

The speed of an arbitrary point $A$ is also easy to find. It is directed along the line $AB$ and is equal to

$$v_a = \omega \cdot CA = 2\omega r \cos \alpha = 2v_0 \cos \alpha.$$

figure 2 also shows the trajectory that each point on the rim of a rolling wheel describes. You will encounter this amazing curve - a cyclide - in many cases: along such a trajectory of motion of a particle in “crossed” (mutually perpendicular) electric and magnetic fields, a ball rolls along a cyclide in a minimum time, etc.

What if we want to determine the speed of the wheels of a moving train? We will find that there are points on the rim of the wheel that move backwards, in the direction opposite to the movement of the train. These are the points of the protruding part of the wheel, for example, point $A$ on figure 3. The instantaneous center of rotation (point $C$) is located on a stationary rail along which the wheel rolls without slipping. The trajectory of the wheel rim point is even more precise than in the previous case - there are return sections of movement (loops) on the trajectory. It is interesting to note that the planets describe similar loop-like trajectories in their motion. They were observed by ancient Greek astronomers, but they could not explain what they saw. Now we know that these trajectories are associated with the superposition of two rotations - the Earth and the observed planet around the Sun.

In conclusion, we would like to introduce you to a device that is simple in design, but important for practice - an ellipsograph ruler (Fig. 4), which is used for drawing ellipses. Points $A$ and $B$ of such a device follow mutually perpendicular guides $OA$ and $OB$, and point $M$ describes an ellipse (think about why). Where is the instantaneous center of rotation in this case? How to find the speed of point $M$, knowing the speeds of points $A$ and $B$?

Consider the rigid triangle $CBA$ (point $C$ lies at the intersection of the perpendiculars to the guides). The projections of the velocities of two points of a rigid body onto the line of their connection are always equal. For points $C$, $A$ and $C$, $B$ this is possible if $v_C=0$, i.e. if point $C$ is the instantaneous center of rotation. The speed of point $M$ can be determined by knowing the speed of point $A$, for example:

$$v_A=\omega \cdot CA, \text{ и } v_M=v_A \cdot CM/CA.$$

Similarly, using the concept of an instantaneous center of rotation, one can easily find the velocities of any points of mechanisms performing plane-parallel motion.
