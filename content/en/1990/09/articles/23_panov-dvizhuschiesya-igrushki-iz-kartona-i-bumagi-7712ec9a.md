Three reversible rings

One of the most attractive designs in FFF is a ring of ten terrahedra. The ring has amazing flexibility and the ability to turn inside out and inside out ad infinitum («Kvant», 1988, No. 7, quadruple cover page). A similar ring can be glued together from washed-out carbon prisms. This is how this toy is made. Cut out four strips measuring 3x42 cm from a batman sheet or thin cardboard. Divide each into seven rectangles 3x6 cm and make gibbs along the dividing lines (Fig. 1). Glue the outer rectangles of the strip together (Fig. 2). Make small cuts in the places indicated by arrows. Pass a thread through them and tie it (Fig. 3). The result is a flexible element consisting of two prisms having a common edge. And here is an even more compact design - it consists of only three such elements as on figure 3. This time, to connect the two connecting elements, two strips measuring $1 \times 9$ cm come across. Each of them needs to be bent second, dividing into rectangles measuring $1 \times 3$ cm. Take two elements and glue a strip to each of them, as on figure 5. Now arrange the elements against each other, as in figure 6. Spread the upper surfaces of the protruding rectangles with glue, at the same time insert them inside the opposite elements and glue them. Now these elements are connected by a double hinge joint (details about it in the FFF). The action of this connection is depicted in figure 7. Now all that remains is to attach the third free element to the first two in the same way - and the stake is ready.

Finally, the most economical eversible design. She glues together from just two elements. Each of them consists of two prisms connected by a rectangle (Fig. 8). Both the upper and lower ends of the elements are connected using a double hinge joint.

Flexman and donkey

Flextman is another one of the FFF heroes. This funny man can walk on inclined planes. The process of its creation is depicted in figure 9. A square is cut out of thin paper with a side of 15-20 cm. An upward fold is made along the diagonals, and a wizz fold is made along the dashed line (Fig. 9, a). Then the square is folded into a triangle (figure 9, b). After this, four operations are performed. The result of the first of them is at figure 9, c. The final result is at 9, v. This is how the flexman gets legs. And four more operations - at the ends of each leg you should bend a small triangle. The Flextman is ready, and if you place it on an inclined plane, cut off its slope, then the Flextman, showing up and waddling, begins to descend along it in small steps.

And I returned to flexman for this reason. Recently I was lucky: I bought a great toy - a walking donkey (Fig. 10). This donkey also walks on an inclined plane, but it can also walk on horizontal surfaces. There is a weight attached to the donkey on a string. If you throw this weight over the edge of the table, then the donkey will walk along the table. The same weight can be attached to a flexman, and it will also move around the table. Flexman and the donkey on the first look at each other with creatures of completely different breeds. Consequently, the mechanics of their behavior are completely identical.
