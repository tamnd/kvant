$(x_1 + \ldots + x_n)^2 = x_1^2 + \ldots + x_m^2 + 2x_1x_2 + \ldots + 2x_{m-1}x_m$

or, use the sign $\Sigma$ for the amount,

$$\left( \sum_{k=1}^{m} x_k \right)^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{m} x_k^2 + 2 \sum_{1 \leq k < j \leq m} x_k x_j.$$

Note that the fraction $1/k$ occurs in $k$ in the last parentheses on the left side. Therefore, after opening the brackets (according to the formula given in the margin), the fraction $1/k^2$ occurs $k$ times, and the fraction $1/(kl)$ for each $l > k$ also occurs $k$ times, and each $l$ corresponds to $l-1$ values ​​$k$ ($k=1, 2, \ldots, l-1$). Therefore, the sum on the left is

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{k}{k^2} + 2 \sum_{l=1}^{n} \frac{k(l-1)}{kl} = \left(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \ldots + \frac{1}{n}\right) + 2 \left(1 - \frac{1}{1} + 1 - \frac{1}{2} + \ldots + 1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) = 2n - \left(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \ldots + \frac{1}{n}\right).$$

Then the lines AK and CM will be parallel, since $<KAB = 180^\circ - \angle KLB = \angle BLM = \angle BCM$. The angular magnitudes of the arcs AK and CM are equal, since the arcs AKC and CMB are equal in angular magnitude (360°-β-α), and the arcs KC and MB subtend equal written angles KAC and MCB and are also equal in magnitude. M1220*. Let us define the sequence $b_n$ by the conditions: $b_1 = 0, b_2 = 2, b_3 = 3, b_{n+1} = b_{n-1} + b_{n-2}$ for $n \geq 3$. Prove that when p is prime, the number $b_p$ is divisible by $p$.

Let's give two solutions. The first is based on the combinatorial interpretation of the numbers $b_n$.

Let us take a circle divided by points $A_0, A_1, \dots, A_{n-1}$ into $n$ equal parts, and consider its divisions into arcs composed of two or three parts; let us call them “suitable $n$-partitions”. Let $a_n$ be the number of such partitions. Then obviously $a_1 = 0, a_2 = 2, a_3 = 3$. Let us prove that $a_{n+1} = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}$ for $n \geq 3$, and therefore $a_n = b_n$ for all $n$. To do this, we assign to each suitable $(n+1)$-partition either a $(n-1)$-partition or a $(n-2)$-partition, and show that the resulting correspondence is one-to-one.

Strachem to a point that is friend of an arbitrary suitable $(n+1)$-partition that follows the arc containing $A_0$ when traversed clockwise. (If the point $A_0$ happens to be between two arcs, then the arc starting at this point in the specified direction of traversal is subtracted; see figure.) To restore the $(n-1)$-or $(n-2)$-partition to the original $(n+1)$-partition, it is enough to create an arc corresponding from two or three parts following the arc containing $A_0$ (and if $A_0$ is the boundary of two arcs, then between these arcs). Due to the reversibility of our correspondence, it is one-to-one.

Let us now consider all suitable $p$-partitions for prime $p$. Let us combine into one group those of them that are obtained from each other by rotations through angles $2\pi k/p, k = 0, 1, \dots, p - 1$. Note that rotations by $2\pi k_1/p$ and $2\pi k_2/p$ at $k_1 \neq k_2$ transform these partitions into different ones. (Otherwise, we would have a partition that transforms into itself under a rotation by $2\pi k/p$, where $k = k_1 - k_2$, and therefore under all rotations by $(2\pi k/p) \cdot n, n = 0, 1, \dots, p - 1$. But for a simple $p$, one of these rotations coincides with a rotation by $2\pi/p$, and such a rotation cannot transfer into itself any suitable partition.) So, in each grouper $p$ partitions, therefore $b_p = a_p$ is divisible by $p$.

The second solution is purely algebraic, but uses some facts that go beyond the school curriculum.

Let $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ be the three roots of the equation $t^3 - t - 1 = 0$ (two of them are complex numbers). Moreover, according to Vieta’s theorem (produced by opening the parentheses in the equality $(t - \lambda_1)(t - \lambda_2)(t - \lambda_3) = t^3 - t - 1$)

F1230. Two distant conductive spheres, whose outer radii are R and 3R, have a wall thickness of R/20. Charges Q and 2Q are placed at the centers of the sphere. What is the minimum amount of work that needs to be done to place these charges in places (small responses are provided in the walls for this purpose)?

Distant spheres from each other are aware that it is possible to overcome their mutual influence. Obviously, the minimum work when “rearranging” charges does not depend on the method of this rearrangement. It is important that in the center of sphere 1 (its outer radius $R_1=R$) there is a charge 2Q, and in the center of sphere 2 ($R_2=3R$) there is a charge Q. Therefore, it is enough for us to transfer the charge Q from the second sphere to the first. The minimality of the perfect work means that there is no possibility of energy dissipation during the movement of induced charges along the spheres.

We will carry out the transfer of charge Q from sphere 2 to sphere 1 in sequential infinite small portions dq. Suppose that at some arbitrary moment in time we have already transferred charge q to the first sphere. Then in the center of sphere 1 there will be a charge $Q+q$, and in the center of sphere 2 there will be a charge 2Q-q. Let's find the work dA that needs to be done to transfer the next portion of charge dq. The electric field of each of the charges ($Q+q$ or 2Q-q), located in the center of the sphere, will be considered as a superposition of fields: the field of a point charge for all meaningful distances r from the center of the sphere and the field of the same charge with the opposite sign in the region $R_1-\delta \leq r \leq R_1$ ($\delta=R/20$ is the thickness of the sphere) for sphere 1 and in the region $R_2-\delta \leq r \leq R_2$ for sphere 2. Then the work dA will be composed of two parts: work on moving the charge dq in the field of point charges $Q+q$ and 2Q-q and work on moving this charge in the region $R_1-\delta \leq r \leq R_1$ and $R_2-\delta \leq r \leq R_2$, where the field is created by point charges - ($Q+q$) and -($2Q-q$).

Let us denote the first part of the work by $dA_1$, and the second by $dA_2$. It is easy to report (this follows from the symmetry of space) that
$$dA_1 = 0.$$
The work $dA_2$ can be written as
$$dA_2 = \frac{2Q - q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{R_2 - \delta} - \frac{1}{R_2} \right) dq + \frac{Q + q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_1 - \delta} \right) dq.$$
Here we use the expression for the potential $\varphi(r)$ for the exact charge $q$:
$$\varphi(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}.$$
Having carried out the summation over all charges (interrating along $dq$ from 0 to $q$), we will find the required work $A$. When calculating the work we use that
$$\int_0^Q dq = Q \text{ и } \int_0^Q q dq = \frac{Q^2}{2}.$$
Finally we get
$$A = A_2 = \frac{3Q^2\delta}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{R_2(R_2 - \delta)} - \frac{1}{R_1(R_1 - \delta)} \right).$$
It is always useful to find the answer in a general form, since this allows you to check the analysis of the result obtained. For example, in this case it is obvious that at $\delta \rightarrow 0$ the work should also tend to zero, as follows from our expression. At $R_1 = R_2$ the work must also be zero, and this also follows from our solution.
According to the conditions of the problem, $\delta$ is much smaller than $R_1$ and $R_2$, so we can write the resulting solution in the form
$$A = \frac{3Q^2\delta}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{R_1^2 - R_2^2}{R_1^2 R_2^2}.$$
When substituting the given values ​​$R_1, R_2$ and $\delta$ we get
$$A = -\frac{79}{4484} \frac{Q^2}{\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \approx -\frac{1}{60} \frac{Q^2}{\pi\varepsilon_0 R}.$$

$$W_H = W_1 - \frac{\varepsilon_0 E_1^2}{2} 4\pi R_1^2 \delta + W_2 - \frac{\varepsilon_0 E_2^2}{2} 4\pi R_2^2 \delta.$$

After substitution we get
$$W_H \approx W_1 - \frac{Q_2\delta}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1^2} + W_2 - \frac{Q^2\delta}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2^2}.$$

F1231. Two cylindrical coils with ferrite cores are placed close to each other and connected in series. The inductance of one of them is 1 mH, the other is 2 mH, and the modified inductance of the coils connected in series is 3.6 mH. Without changing the relative position of the coils, switch them in parallel (see figure). What inductance will we change now?

If the coils were further apart, their mutual influence could be neglected. Then the magnetic flux would be determined by the magnitude

$$\Phi = L_1 I + L_2 I = (L_1 + L_2) I,$$

that is, the resulting inductance would be equal to the sum of the inductances of the coil. In our case this is not the case:

$$L_{06\pi} = 3,6 \text{ мГн} > L_1 + L_2,$$

This means that a significant part of the total magnetic flux is the “mutual” flux created by the current of one coil through the turns of the other. In other words,

$$\Phi_{06\pi} = L_1 I + L_2 I + MI + MI = (L_1 + L_2 + 2M) I.$$

Here, with the letter $M$ we denote “mutual inductance” (more precisely, the coefficient of mutual inductance) - the proportionality coefficient between the current in one coil and the magnetic flux, arbitrary turns of the other coil (the inductance of the coil, catapti, was also called the “self-induction coefficient”). Actually, there are two coefficients of mutual inductance: between the current of the first coil and the flux in the second and between the current of the second coil and the flux in the first. These odds are always the same. For coils having different numbers of identical turns, this equality is easy to prove (try it), in the general case it is quite difficult to do this - however, the fact itself is well known.

Let us now find the coefficient of mutual inductance:

$$M = (L_{06\pi} - L_1 - L_2) / 2 = 0,3 \text{ мГн.}$$

When switching the ends of the coil, their relative position does not change, which means that the “mutual inductance” remains the same, although the contribution of mutual fluxes can be either positive or negative (depending on the order in which the wires are connected). When receiving the answer, we will consider both possible options, counting $M = \pm 0,3 \text{ мГн.}$.

So, let us denote the points of the coil after parallel connection as $I_1$ and $I_2$. The inductive emf conducts the same; This means that the magnetic fluxes are the same:

$$\Phi_1 = L_1 I_1 + MI_2, \quad \Phi_2 = L_2 I_2 + MI_1, \quad \Phi_1 = \Phi_2, \quad I_1 + I_2 = I_{06\pi}.$$

F1232. At a great distance from the Earth there is the planet DVK-1, around which the BK-0010SH satellite is orbiting in a circular orbit. The satellite has a radio station that emits a constant frequency signal. This signal is received on Earth, however, interruptions occur - for 45 minutes there is a signal, so for 45 minutes it is absent, so for 45 minutes there is, etc. The matter is not limited to this - the frequency of the received signal changes relative to the average value $f_0 = 1,5 \cdot 10^9$ Hz according to the law shown at figure ($\Delta f = 3 \cdot 10^4$ Hz). Assuming that the Earth-planet segment lies in the plane of the satellite’s orbit, one can find the mass of DVK-1.

The orbital period of the planet's satellite is very much
$$T = 2 \cdot 45 \text{ мин} = 90 \text{ мин}.$$
Half of this time the satellite is hidden by the planet; This means that he flies very close to its surface. The change in the frequency of the received signal is due to the Doppler effect:
$$\frac{\Delta f}{f_0} = \frac{v}{c}.$$
Here $v$ is the speed of the satellite, $c$ is the speed of signal propagation (speed of light).
On the other hand, from the correspondences of mechanics
$$T = \frac{2\pi R}{v} \ln \frac{mv^2}{R} = G \frac{mM}{R^2},$$
where $R$ is the radius, $M$ is the mass of the planet, $G$ is the granite constant.
After simple transformations the mass of the planet is found:
$$M = \left( c \frac{\Delta f}{f_0} \right)^3 \frac{T}{2\pi G} \approx 2,8 \cdot 10^{24} \text{ кг}.$$
