Where is $\rho[\kappa r/\mathrm{m}^3]$ here? It doesn’t exist, or we simply have nowhere to detail the kilograms included in the dimension $\rho$, there is nothing to reduce them with, so that in the final result the value $u$ changes only in m/s. This is a physical concept: after all, both the gravitational force accelerating the “hump” down and the mass of the “hump”, which determines its inertia, are proportional to $\rho$ - this is why the density has dropped out of formula (2). (A similar situation occurs in the case of a mathematical matrix, whose period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$, for the same reason, does not depend on the mass suspended on a thread of length $l$.)

But if the waves become very small, “ripples” (we will evaluate below what very small means), and the gravitational force acting on the “hump” is small, then another force strives to return the “hump” to the equilibrium position - the tension surface force, associated with the tension surface coefficient $\sigma [\mathrm{H/m}]$. This is similar to how if we lifted a stretched horizontal rubber film upward with our finger: then, due to the tension of the film on the finger, the force $F_\sigma \sim \sigma$ (Fig. 4 6) would begin to act downwards. In this case, the propagation of waves will depend on the value of $\sigma [\mathrm{H/m}]$,$\lambda [\mathrm{m}]$,$u_\sigma [\mathrm{m/c}]$,$\rho [\kappa r/\mathrm{m}^3]$. (We denoted the speed of waves bound to the surface by $u_\sigma$ to distinguish it from $u$ from formula (2).)

Now we have enough experience to use dimensions and obtain an expression for the wave speed (remember that $1\mathrm{H} = 1\kappa r \cdot \mathrm{m/c}^2$):

$$u_\sigma \left[ \frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{c}} \right] \sim \sqrt{\frac{\sigma [\kappa r/\mathrm{c}^2]}{\rho [\kappa r/\mathrm{m}^3] \cdot \lambda [\mathrm{m}]}.$$

If we want to change the sign proportionally to an equal sign, we need to take into account the missing dimensionless factor. According to the exact theory, it is equal to $\sqrt{2\pi}$. So,

$$u_\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi \sigma}{\lambda \rho}}.$$ (3)

Isn’t it amazing that we got the main thing almost “for free”, without knowing any theories: the nature of the dependence between quantities! Here it is worth remembering that, in the opinion of the famous Italian physicist Enrico Fermi, “in physics... there is no place for confused thoughts... those who truly understand the nature of this or that phenomenon must obtain the basic laws from the messages of dimension”).

For “icy” water

$$\sigma \approx 8 \cdot 10^{-2} \mathrm{H/m},$$

$$\rho = 1 \mathrm{r/cm}^3 = 10^3 \mathrm{kr/m}^3.$$

Taking this into account, we plotted the dependence of the wave surface speed, determined by expressions (2) and (3), on the value $\sqrt{\lambda}$ (Fig. 5; $u$ from formula (2) we denoted $u_g$). It can be seen that both curves intersect at $\lambda = \lambda_* \approx 2$ cm. Thus, at very short wavelengths ($\lambda \ll \lambda_*$), their speed is determined by surface tension, at long wavelengths ($\lambda \gg \lambda_*$) - by the acceleration of earth’s gravity.
