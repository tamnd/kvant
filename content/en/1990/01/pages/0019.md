METRIC
SPACES

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
N. VASILIEV

This article talks about the concept of distance, which is often used in modern mathematics both as a basis for constructing general theories and for solving specific problems.

The railway directory indicates that the distance from Novosibirsk to Dushanbe is 3895 km; if you change the distance between these cities on the map (or look in the directory of airline ticket agents), you will get a different number: 2100 km. This, of course, is not surprising: trains cannot travel almost in a direction, like airplanes fly, so railway workers and pilots estimate distances differently.

Residents of a larger city generally rarely change distances within the city in kilometers. For example, if you ask a Muscovite: “How far is it from the university on the Lenin Hills to Beskudnikov?”*) he will most likely answer: “An hour and a half,” and this will be a more useful answer than “28 kilometers.” For example, from the same university to the Shelkovskaya metro station the distance in kilometers is greater, but the “distance in minutes” is less: you can get there (with a transfer) by metro in no more than an hour.

The “distances” we just talked about and the total distance between two points on a plane or in space have some common properties. There are few such basic properties, but they are already enough to, taking them as axioms, build a meaningful and useful theory. Here we will limit ourselves to discussing some personal concepts and individual examples.

Axims and first examples

Let us be given some set X. We say that a distance is defined on it if each two elements a and b of the set X are associated with some non-negative number ρ(a, b) - “the distance from a to b” - and the following three conditions are satisfied (see Fig. 1, a, b, ε):

1°. ρ(a, b) = 0 if and only if a = b.

2°. ρ(a, b) = ρ(b, a) for any two elements a and b from X (“symmetry axiom”).

3°. ρ(a, c) ≤ ρ(a, b) + ρ(b, c) for any three elements a, b and c from X (“triangle axiom”).

A set with a distance (“metric”) defined on it is called a metric space *. The elements x themselves are generally called points of the metric space. figure 2, a shows a metric space of 5 points; and figure 2, b does not satisfy the “triangle axiom” (check!).
