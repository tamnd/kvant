I immediately agreed - after all, I play chess much better than Kolya, and here are not some pebbles, but a real chessboard, a real pawn, which, however, moves almost like a king.
I lost the first game and decided not to rush into the second, but then our neighbors returned, and we had to twist around and return the dock with the pawn to them. “Don’t be upset,” Kolya told me, “you can play this game with a pebble.”
I immediately drew a square in the sand, drew lines dividing it into 64 squares, and placed a pebble on the corner field.
“It’s possible, of course,” said Kolya, “but I had something completely different in mind.”
He put two piles of stones, 7 stones in each. “We take turns taking stones: either one stone from the first pile, or one stone from the second pile, or two stones - one from each pile; Or you can not take stones, but move one stone from one pile to another. The one who takes the last stone gets well.”
“I don’t want to play with your stupid stones,” I said indignantly, “let’s play Gelfand’s game!” Are you afraid? “And this is Gelfand’s very game,” Kolya answered, “Look!” And he drew numbers from 0 to 7 on the sand near the square I had drawn, first along one side, and then along the other side, as on figure 3.
“See what happens. Let the physics stand on the field, say, (5; 6), that is, at the intersection of the vertical, placed by number 5, and the horizontal, placed by number 6 (see Fig. 3). Let's put two piles of stones aside: the first has 5 stones, and the second has 6. In which cells do I have the right to move the physics? In cells (6; 5), (5; 5), (4; 5), (4; 6) and (4; 7). How can we get piles that correspond to the new principles of physics from our two piles containing 5 and 6 stones? Very simple! To get position (6; 5), you need to move one stone from the second pile to the first; position (5; 5) is obtained by taking one stone from the second pile; (4; 5) - if you take a stone from both piles; (4; 6) - if you take one stone from the first pile; finally, position (4; 7) is obtained if one stone is moved from the first pile to the second. Since the initial field is numbered (7; 7), at first there should be 7 stones in each pile. The final field is numbered with numbers (0; 0) - not a single stone remains in the piles.”
“But I like the game on the chessboard much better,” I said. “Look: the cell (0; 0) is a losing one in the sense that if the move is mine, and the physics is already in this cell, then I lost. And the cells adjacent to it (0; 1), (1; 1) and (1; 0) are winning. Then the cells (0; 2) and (2; 0) are losing ones, since from them you can only go to winning cells (on figure 4 losing cells are placed with the letter P, and winning ones with a + sign). Continuing this reasoning, I will be able to determine for each cell whether it is winning or losing,” I said, finishing with the size of the fields of the square (the result was figure 4).
