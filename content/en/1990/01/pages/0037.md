4. Theorem 2: $\lambda \leqslant \sqrt{3}$

This theorem is past the previous one: to prove it, it is enough to explain how to glue a Möbius strip from a strip whose length is greater than $\sqrt{3}$. Let us first assume that its length is exactly $\sqrt{3}$. Then two regular triangles can be placed on this strip (Fig. 9). Let's fold the strip along the sides of these triangles, through the fold directions (Fig. 10; take scissors and paper and do this). The edges $AB$ and $CD$ of the strip will align, with point $A$ aligned with point $D$ and point $B$ with point $C$. The result is a Möbius strip.

In this construction, the main rule was violated - do not wrinkle the paper. But it is easy to understand that if the strips are at least a little larger than $\sqrt{3}$, then the break along the patterns can be replaced by a bath produced in a narrow area (Fig. 11). In short, we are not afraid of a kink along a straight segment; it can be replaced by a bend close to it. (Irreparable creasing of paper occurs when two fold lines intersect, that is, when the sheet is folded onto the like of a handkerchief - all this is known to us from everyday experience.)

What the resulting Möbius strip looks like is shown in figure 12. Its structure can be imagined as follows: three identical regular triangles $ABC$, $A'B'C'$, $A''B''C''$ lie parallel to each other, corresponding vertices above corresponding vertices; sides $AB$ and $A'B'$, $B'C'$ and $B''C''$, $C''A''$ and $CA$ are connected by jumpers. The gluing line runs along the median of one of the triangles.

5. Why can't I find $\lambda$ more accurately?

Until the problem is solved, it is difficult to say why it is not solved. Nevertheless, sometimes in different solved problems it is possible to trace common difficulties, to mark, so to speak, difficult places on the mathematical map, which sometimes allows us to imagine success or failure in solving a particular problem.
