Why is it formed? Let water run symmetrically onto a wedge (the bow of the ship) with vertical edges and an apex angle of 2α (Fig. 2) with a speed of $v$ (obviously, this is the speed of the ship relative to the water). At the very nose of the wedge, we can decompose the vector $v$ into two components: parallel to one of the faces (one of the sides of the ship) $v_\parallel$ and

I pendicular to it $v_\perp$. Thus, water relative to the side in question participates in two movements: it slides along the side with a speed of $v_\parallel = v \cos \alpha$ and “flies” perpendicularly onto it with a speed of $v_\perp = v \sin \alpha$.

In the second movement, it is divided into a layer: the lower layers “dive” under the bottom of the ship (Fig. 3), while the upper layer rises vertically, and we can easily estimate to what height. Indeed, each particle of water from this upper layer, having energy $mv_\perp^2/2$, sharply changing the direction of movement, can climb up to such a height $h$, where its potential energy $mgh$ will be no more than kinetic energy

$$mgh \leq m \frac{v_\perp^2}{2}.$$

From here

$$h \leq \frac{v_\perp^2}{2g}.$$
