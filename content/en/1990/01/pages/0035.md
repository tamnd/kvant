By emphasizing that if a point on a surface is neither boundary nor flat, then a single generatrix that does not end at it passes through it, and the ends of this generatrix lie on the boundary of the surface.

Examples. A sheet of paper, rolled into a tube or into a pound, has no flat (or semi-flat) points. In a tube, the generators form a family of parallel segments; in a pound, they form a family of segments, each diverging from one point. More complex arrangements of generatrices are possible. For example, the generatrix and flat points of the developing surface shown in figure 3, a, are shown in figure 3, b (on it the surface is expanded into a flat sheet of paper): the thin blue lines are the generatrix, and the shaded areas consist of flat points.

Points lying on the boundary of flat points are either boundary points for the entire surface or semi-flat. If the surface is made of a paper polygon (say, from a rectangle), then the flat points make up one or more flat polygons, and each of these polygons has vertices lying on the boundary of the surface, and the sides either lie on the boundary or consists of semi-flat points (see again figure 3, b).
