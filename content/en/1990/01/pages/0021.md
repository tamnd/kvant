Having taken up several exotic examples, we left aside the most natural one.

Example 5. $X$ is the set of all points of the plane, $\rho$ is the general distance with which we deal in school geometry, i.e. $\rho(A, B)$ is the length of the segment connecting two points $A$ and $B$. The properties of $1^\circ$ and $2^\circ$ here and in all the following examples are quite obvious, and we will not say more about them. And property $3^\circ$ is nothing more than the statement “in a triangle, each side is no greater than the sum of the other two.” In the general construction of a geometry course, this statement is a simple theorem.

You, Veroitono, know that on a plane with a rectangular coordinate system $Oxy$, the distance $\rho(M_1, M_2)$ between two points $M_1(x_1, y_1)$ and $M_2(x_2, y_2)$ is expressed by the following formula (see Fig. 3):

$$\rho((x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2)) = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_2)^2 + (y_1 - y_2)^2}.$$

Thus, the same metric space can be described without any reference to geometry as follows: $X$ is the set of all pairs $(x, y)$ of real numbers*), the distance between the pairs $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is given by formula (4). But at the same time, in order to check the property $3^\circ$, you need to prove the following inequality:

*) This set has a special designation $R^2$ and is called the number plane.

Fig. 3.

$$\sqrt{(x_1 - x_3)^2 + (y_1 - y_3)^2} \leq \sqrt{(x_1 - x_2)^2 + (y_1 + y_2)^2} + \sqrt{(x_2 - x_3)^2 + (y_2 - y_3)^2}$$

Try this!

In principle, all geometric theorems could be proven on the number plane by number of algebraic ones and thus construct a geometry course; but, as you can see, the proofs of theorems do not always become simpler along this path - together with the “triangle inequalities” one would have to prove a rather tricky algebraic inequality.

We have already said that distances can be defined differently on the same set. Here are two examples of metrics on the number plane $R^2$ that are different from (4).

Example 5'.

$$\rho'(M_1, M_2) = |x_1 - y_1| + |x_2 - y_2|,$$

i.e. $\rho'(M_1, M_2)$ is equal to the sum of the lengths of the projections of the segment $M_1M_2$ on the axis $Ox$ and $Oy$.

This metric can be called the “metric of a rectangular city”: if all the streets on the city plan are parallel to the axes $Ox$ and $Oy$, then the shortest path along the streets from one point $(x_1, y_1)$ to another $(x_2, y_2)$ is given by formula (5).

Example 5''.

$$\rho''(M_1, M_2) = \max \{ |x_1 - y_1|, |x_2 - y_2| \}$$

(the notation max {a, b} denotes the most of the numbers a, b), i.e. $\rho''(M_1, M_2)$ is equal to the most of the lengths of the projections of the segment $M_1M_2$ on the axes $Ox$ and $Oy$.

The triangle inequality in the last two examples is easily proven using inequality (2). Let's say for $\rho''$:

$$|x_1 - x_3| \leq |x_1 - x_2| + |x_2 - x_3| \leq \max \{ |x_1 - x_2|, |y_1 - y_2| \} + \max \{ |x_2 - x_3|, |y_2 - y_3| \},$$
