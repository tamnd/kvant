Before reading further, try yourself, using at least this “standard”, to estimate $h$ and $\lambda$. This will be your personal “dimension”. We must not, however, forget that the picture is perceived by each viewer very individually. Show this picture to all the children in your class and ask everyone what they think about the values ​​of $h$, $\lambda$, $\alpha$ (it’s good if the person being asked did not hear the answers of other goods; then the “readings” of all students will be similar to the independent readings of the device). Write down the answers you receive, then build a graph where the difference in value $h$ is on the horizontal axis, and the number of people $n$ who called this value $h$ on the vertical axis.

The author continued this with his comrades (a total of 30 people were interviewed) and received the graphs shown in Figures 7 and 8. Interestingly, most of the respondents named the values ​​$h$ and $\lambda$, multiples of 5 cm, some - multiples of 2-2.5 cm, but not less (no one named, for example, $h=27,1357891439$ cm). This is similar to measuring distances with a ruler with a minimum division value of 2-2.5 cm.

Using these graphs, we can find the average values ​​of $\langle h \rangle$ and $\langle \lambda \rangle$ and consider them fairly objective.

The procedure for determining averages is simple. Each value $h_i$ must be multiplied by the number of people $n_i$ who named it, take the sum over all $i$ and divide by the total number of respondents. As a result we get

$$\langle h \rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{30} h_i n_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{30} n_i} = \frac{1}{30} \sum_{i=1}^{30} h_i n_i =$$

$$= \frac{1}{30} (1 \cdot 10 + 2 \cdot 12 + 5 \cdot 15 + 7 \cdot 20 +$$
