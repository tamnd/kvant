If we find liquid flowing out of the cone all the time and no void is formed in the cone, this means that the liquid must be flowing inside the cone all the time. How does the liquid get into the cone? It can’t flow out without getting there. The cones have double walls, the spaces between them are evacuated, and it is very important that liquid cannot pass through them. By means of the wing, located in a wide variety of positions at the neck, it was not possible to detect the existence of a reverse flow. Therefore, we initially decided that there should be a flow along a very thin layer at the very walls (then it could not be detected by the winglet). But with further experiments this hypothesis turned out to be insufficient. I began to change the experimental conditions: instead of wide-necked cones, I used very narrow slits. The idea of ​​these experiments was to, if possible, occupy the entire cross-section of the slit with a reverse wall flow and thus try to change the nature of the observed phenomena.
