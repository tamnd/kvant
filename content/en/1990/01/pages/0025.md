Exercise
1. Prove the inequality $\rho(A, C) + \rho(B, D) \leqslant \rho(A, B) + \rho(B, C) + \rho(C, D) + \rho(D, A)$ for four points $A, B, C, D$ of the metric space.
2. Prove that for any $n$ points $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ ($n \geqslant 3$) $\rho(A_1, A_2) + \rho(A_2, A_3) + \dots + \rho(A_{n-1}, A_n) \geqslant \rho(A_1, A_n)$.
3. Dr. Charadec, who knew strategy well, was interested in the last war and in 1940 became acquainted with the French theater of war. This is probably where the next problem arose. The distance (in effect, like all distances in this problem) from Chalons to Vitry is 30 km, from Vitry to Chaumont 80 km, from Chaumont to Saint-Quentin 236 km, from Saint-Quentin to Rems 86 km, from Rems to Chalons 40 km. Calculate the distance from Rems to Chaumont in this closed polygon. Only Dr. Charadek can do this without a card*).
4. The set $E$ of points of the metric space $X$ is called a $\varepsilon$-net if the $\varepsilon$-neighborhoods of the points of the set $E$ (all together) completely cover the sets $X$; in other words, if for each point $x$ from $X$ there is at least one point of the set $E$ that is no more than $\varepsilon$ away from $x$. (Here $\varepsilon$ is some positive number.)
For example, the set of black dots on figure 7 is a $\varepsilon$-net for the square $0 \leqslant x \leqslant 1, 0 \leqslant y \leqslant 1$ on the plane $Oxy$ with distance (6) at $\varepsilon > 1/10$. For which $\varepsilon$ is this set a $\varepsilon$-net in the sense of distances (4) and (5) on the same square?
5. Let $N(\varepsilon)$ be the smallest number of points in the $\varepsilon$-network of the space $X, M(\varepsilon)$ be the smallest number of points in $X$, the distance between any two of which is at least $\varepsilon$.
Prove that $M(2\varepsilon) \leqslant N(\varepsilon) \leqslant M(\varepsilon)$.
6. Let $C$ be the set of functions defined on the interval [0; 1] and taking values ​​on the same segment, the graphs of which are broken lines. We will determine the distance in $C$ using formula (7).
Prove that you can choose an infinite number of functions from $C$, all pairwise distances between which are equal to one. Deduce from this that for $\varepsilon < 1/2$ in $C$ it is impossible to choose a finite $\varepsilon$-net.
7. In some metric space, can it be that the 3-neighborhood of the point $A$ is entirely contained in the 2-neighborhood of another point $B$ and does not fill it entirely? What will be the answer if we replace 3 and 2 with other numbers?
8. Prove that among $n$-digit numbers, from two digits 1 and 2, it is impossible to choose more than $\frac{2^n}{n+1}$ numbers so that any two of them differ from each other in at least three digits.
9. Construct an example of a sequence of functions defined on the interval [0; 1], which tends to the limit $f_0$, if we use distance (8), and does not have $f_0$ limit, if we use distance (7).
10. Let $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ be two distances on some set $X$, which have the property that $\rho(A, B) \leqslant k\rho_2(A, B)$ for any two points $A$ and $B$, where $k$ is some positive (the same for all $A$ and $B$).
Prove that if $P$ is the limit of the sequence $M_1, M_2, M_3$ ... in the sense of distance $\rho_2$, then $P$ will be the limit of that sequence in the sense of distance $\rho_1$. Using this, prove that on the plane the statement “the limit of the sequence $M_1, M_2, M_3$ ... is the point $P$” has the same meaning, regardless of which of the distances (4), (5), (6) we use.
11. a) Think of a distance $\rho$ on the set of all lines in the plane for which the following conditions would be satisfied: if the sequences of points $A_1, A_2, A_3$ ... and $B_1, B_2, B_3$ ... have two distinct points $A$ and $B$ as their limits, then the sequence of lines $A_1B_1, A_2B_2, A_3B_3$ ... has (in the sense of the distance $\rho$) the line $AB$ as its limit.
6) Prove that such a distance $\rho$ cannot be specified so that the distance between any two intersecting lines depends only on the angle between them.
