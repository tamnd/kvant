(memories
about summer holidays)

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. SAVIN

On a hot July day, Kolya and I went to the beach. Having swum to our heart's content, we lay down on the sand to sunbathe. Next to us, two men were playing chess.

Naturally, we immediately began to discuss the position on the board and give hints: I to one, and Kolya to the other. But the players were unperturbed and did not react to our hints at all. Desperate to turn this game into a masterpiece of chess art, we began to think about what we should do.

“Shouldn’t we throw some pebbles?” - Kolya asked me. “What is this - throw and catch?” "Not really! - he answered, - much more interesting! First, let's play this game.

There is a pile of stones. We take turns taking one, two or three stones. The one who takes the last stone wins.”

I decided to play so that I always had an even number of stones, and I lost. In the second game, I began to take stones so that I always had an odd number of stones - and again I lost. I really didn’t want to lose for the third time, and I decided to try to understand the meaning of the game.

Both the first time and the second time it turned out that before my last move there were

How can I do so as not to end up in front of the four stones again? It is impossible to leave him 5, 6 or 7 stones: having taken 1, 2 or 3 stones, he will again leave me 4 stones. What if I leave him 8 stones? Then he will be forced to leave me 5, 6 or 7 stones, and I forced him to make a move in a situation when there are 4 stones in the pile. And then it dawned on me! If a player receives a pile of stones on his turn, the number of which is divisible by 4, then after any of his moves the number of remaining stones can no longer be divided by 4, and his partner will again be able to make a move leading to a pile, the number of stones in which is again divisible by 4, and so on until all the stones are exhausted. Everything is clear!

Having counted the stones in the pile (there were 25 of them), I raised my head to tell Kolya that I was taking one stone, but... he had already thrown his hand at me from the middle of the river.

When we, having bathed, returned to the old place, I, as if reluctantly, asked: “Shall we play another game of stones?” Should I start? “Well,” answered Kolya, “come on; Only now we will play by the new rules:

From a pile of stones we again take one, two or three stones. The one who takes the last stone prospers. Well, start!

I thought about it, and a minute later, having once again counted the stones in the pile - there were still 25 of them, I raised my hands up. “I give up, I lost!” "Why?" - Kolya was surprised. In response, I arranged the stones as shown in figure 1 - six piles of 4 stones and one more stone.

“In the first game, I won by taking one stone on the first move,” I said, “and then so many stones so that the total of the stones taken before was four stones. And now, no matter how many stones I take, you, taking each time enough stones so that the total of the stones taken before me is four stones, leave me the last stone.”

“Well done,” Kolya shook hands with me. “You found a winning strategy for the second game.” What are we going to do next?

Then we noticed that our chess-playing neighbors had gone for a swim. “Oh, it’s a pity we didn’t ask them to play chess while they were swimming!” - I said, eyes on the empty chessboard. Our neighbors put the figures in a bag. There was only one captured pawn lying on the sand. And after all my failures, I wanted revenge on Kolya.

“You know what,” said Kolya, moved the chessboard and placed this single pawn in the daughter’s corner, “let’s play a game, the author of which is the famous Soviet mathematician, corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences *) I. M. Gelfand. They play it like this: first the pawn stands in one of the corners of the dochka, and then we will move it one by one to one of the neighboring squares, and you cannot move back - and he, having placed the pawn on one of the central squares, showed which squares it can be moved to.

I immediately agreed - after all, I play chess much better than Kolya, and here are not some pebbles, but a real chessboard, a real pawn, which, however, moves almost like a king.
I lost the first game and decided not to rush into the second, but then our neighbors returned, and we had to twist around and return the dock with the pawn to them. “Don’t be upset,” Kolya told me, “you can play this game with a pebble.”
I immediately drew a square in the sand, drew lines dividing it into 64 squares, and placed a pebble on the corner field.
“It’s possible, of course,” said Kolya, “but I had something completely different in mind.”
He put two piles of stones, 7 stones in each. “We take turns taking stones: either one stone from the first pile, or one stone from the second pile, or two stones - one from each pile; Or you can not take stones, but move one stone from one pile to another. The one who takes the last stone gets well.”
“I don’t want to play with your stupid stones,” I said indignantly, “let’s play Gelfand’s game!” Are you afraid? “And this is Gelfand’s very game,” Kolya answered, “Look!” And he drew numbers from 0 to 7 on the sand near the square I had drawn, first along one side, and then along the other side, as on figure 3.
“See what happens. Let the physics stand on the field, say, (5; 6), that is, at the intersection of the vertical, placed by number 5, and the horizontal, placed by number 6 (see Fig. 3). Let's put two piles of stones aside: the first has 5 stones, and the second has 6. In which cells do I have the right to move the physics? In cells (6; 5), (5; 5), (4; 5), (4; 6) and (4; 7). How can we get piles that correspond to the new principles of physics from our two piles containing 5 and 6 stones? Very simple! To get position (6; 5), you need to move one stone from the second pile to the first; position (5; 5) is obtained by taking one stone from the second pile; (4; 5) - if you take a stone from both piles; (4; 6) - if you take one stone from the first pile; finally, position (4; 7) is obtained if one stone is moved from the first pile to the second. Since the initial field is numbered (7; 7), at first there should be 7 stones in each pile. The final field is numbered with numbers (0; 0) - not a single stone remains in the piles.”
“But I like the game on the chessboard much better,” I said. “Look: the cell (0; 0) is a losing one in the sense that if the move is mine, and the physics is already in this cell, then I lost. And the cells adjacent to it (0; 1), (1; 1) and (1; 0) are winning. Then the cells (0; 2) and (2; 0) are losing ones, since from them you can only go to winning cells (on figure 4 losing cells are placed with the letter P, and winning ones with a + sign). Continuing this reasoning, I will be able to determine for each cell whether it is winning or losing,” I said, finishing with the size of the fields of the square (the result was figure 4). Now I can play with that one in Gelfand’s “stone” version of the game: I will move the physics around the board and pick up or move stones accordingly.” “And I can formulate a very simple rule,” said Kolya. “Look, the losing cells are those with both “coordinates” even; winning cells have at least one “coordinate” that is odd. Thus, in Gelfand’s “stone” game, the beginner’s strategy is very simple: he must take stones so that both piles have an even number

Thus, we completely understood the “Gelfand game”, and if so, it became interesting to play it. Another thing is chess! Probably millions of games have already been played there, but a winning strategy has not yet been found. But in a game with pebbles, you can come up with more and more new rules of the game, and finding a winning strategy is no less, if not more, interesting than finding a solution to an intricate chess stage.

Try to find who and how will win if played correctly - the starter or his partner - in the following games:

1. There are two piles of 10 stones each. Two people take turns taking stones from each pile (not necessarily from the same one every time). The one who takes the last stones wins.

2. The conditions are the same as in the previous game, but the player is prohibited from taking such a number of stones that the piles end up with the same number of stones.

3. There are two piles of 9 candies on the table. Each of the two players must first transfer one candy from one pile to another, and then eat two candies from one pile. The one who cannot make his next move loses.

4. There are two piles of peeled nuts on the table, one with 7 and the other with 6 nuts. Two players take turns. The move is that the player eating nuts in one of the piles, and divides the other into two parts (any). If he cannot divide this pile into two parts because there is only one nut in it, then he eats and wins.
