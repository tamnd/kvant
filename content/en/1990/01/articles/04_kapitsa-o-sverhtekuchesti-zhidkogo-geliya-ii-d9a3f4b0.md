ON THE SUPERFLUIDITY OF LIQUID HELIUM II

Academician P. KAPITSA

The study of liquid helium and its properties belongs to the field of physics of the lowest temperatures. This is one of those areas of physics that seeks to study natural phenomena under extreme conditions. We can most likely expect the discovery of new interesting phenomena when we study nature in the extreme conditions permissible for it, such as, for example, under exceptionally strong magnetic fields, high pressure, high electrical voltages, etc., as well as in the region of deep cold approaching absolute zero. Here we can also hope to discover new phenomena that under normal conditions either accelerate from observation or simply do not even occur. In this regard, the region of temperatures close to absolute zero is especially interesting. The work of the last decade has clearly confirmed this.

What is absolute zero of the temperature scale? Latest definition of absolute zero: - 273.13 °C. It is known that we will never be able to reach absolute zero. The explanatory, school definition of absolute zero says that this is the temperature at which the thermal motion of matter stops. But this definition is imprecise. From a modern point of view, based on quantum theory, the existence of movements at absolute zero is allowed. The energy of this movement is non-zero and is determined by the minimum molecular movement that can exist in a given substance. Let me give you a simple example. If a substance is heated strongly, then the electrons of the atoms, which move around the atomic nucleus in certain orbits, will break off and fly away under the influence of temperature movements, and so-called dissociation will occur. When matter cools, atoms slow down, electrons begin to circulate in their orbits again and maintain their motion until absolute zero. But, in addition to the movement of electrons along the orbits of any individual atom, there is also a whole series of combined movements in a solid body, which from a modern point of view should be maintained down to the lowest temperatures. Thanks to this so-called degeneracy of motion in this temperature range, a completely new phenomenon can appear that we cannot observe at ordinary temperatures.

Each body in nature has a limiting amount of heat per unit time that can be conducted through it. It turned out that such a large thermal conductivity, which was experimentally discovered in our latest experiments in liquid helium II, cannot be explained using these views. We can look for a way out of this contradiction either by abandoning the basic views on the mechanism of thermal conductivity, which are firmly established in science, or we must admit that the phenomenon of thermal conductivity in helium II owes its origin to some other mechanism.

As is known, heat can be transferred not only through the described mechanism, as it propagates in solids and, as assumed, in liquid helium in narrow capillaries. Heat can also be transferred in liquid and gaseous bodies through so-called convection currents. For example, convection currents in the air are well known at home; you have repeatedly lowered them when you held your hand over a warm radiator. The same hand does not feel any heat at all if it is held at the same distance from the radiator, but below, since there are no ascending currents of heated air that carry heat to the top by convection. If the intense heat transfer in liquid helium cannot be explained from the point of view of the usual mechanism of thermal conductivity, then I thought that perhaps convection heat transfer was taking place here. To do this, it must be assumed that in liquid helium II, liquid flows arise extremely easily, which is due to the extremely high ability of helium II to transfer heat. Calculations showed that the intensity with which heat was transferred in liquid helium could only be achieved by convection currents that should flow in this liquid with incredible ease. Therefore, by analogy with superconductivity, I assumed that helium II at ultralow temperatures is an extremely flowing liquid, i.e., a liquid that has no growths. All that remained was to test this experimentally.

Observing a small juvenile, especially at low temperatures, turned out to be a difficult experimental task. It was necessary to find a special method for measuring it. When the necessary method was found and developed, the observation itself did not take much time and showed that the growth of liquid helium is indeed vanishingly small. Liquid helium turned out to be over a billion times more fluid than water. We do not have a more sensitive method. Therefore, I assumed that there is every reason to believe that liquid helium does not have adults; I called it super-current. At first it was met with great opportunity. In my experiments they looked for experimental errors in the methodology, measurements, etc. The discovery of superfluidity in liquid helium has thus been comprehensively discussed, and now, I thought, the existence of a superfluid state in helium II can be considered recognized.

When this phenomenon was first formed, it seemed to us that the superthickness of helium II was quite sufficient to explain the large thermal conductivity observed in liquid helium, in accordance with the picture of the existence of convection currents that I have just sketched for you. But the matter turned out to be much more interesting and complicated than we initially thought.

If we stand from the point of view of our general mechanical concepts, which more comprehensively describe the behavior of common substances under general conditions, then it turns out that supercurrent helium cannot transport a body column intensively, as required by the results of measuring convection flows. We are faced with the difficulty of finding a mechanism that could cause the necessary rapid flow of helium during convection. In the general mechanism of body transfer by convection, we owe the movement of the medium to the fact that a more heated liquid or gas becomes somewhat less dense, which is why it tends upward, as if floating up in a denser medium, while colder, more dense liquids tend downwards, “taunt”. Mixing occurs, and it is very important that the reason causing the movement is the force of gravity. But calculations show that this force in helium II is not enough to cause such a large thermal conductivity, which is observed experimentally. This made the phenomenon again incomprehensible. It was necessary to look for some other, new mechanisms to explain it. Through a series of experiments, it was finally possible to come across a completely new mechanism for the movement of helium II.

It turned out that under the influence of temperature differences in liquid helium II, very strong flows arise, somewhat reminiscent of convection. Under the influence of a temperature difference, the liquid begins to move, but this movement is of a special kind, specific to liquid helium II, not known in any other liquid and under any other conditions.
