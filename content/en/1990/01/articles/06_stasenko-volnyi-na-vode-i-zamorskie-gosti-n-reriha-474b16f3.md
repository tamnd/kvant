Wave surfaces

Wave surfaces are a rather complex object of study, but we can learn a lot about them based on very simple messages about the dimensions of the physical quantities that describe this phenomenon. To do this, you must first of all clearly imagine the principle of the phenomenon. A wave is a propagating vibration. Why can the surface of water, disturbed by something from its equilibrium position, fluctuate? Any oscillation is the result of a “play” of two factors: force, which returns to the equilibrium position, and inertia, which forces this equilibrium position to move.

If a “hump” is formed on the surface of the liquid, then, for example, the gravitational force $F_g$ (see Fig. 4a), proportional to the earth’s core gravity $g$, can return the liquid particles to the equilibrium position. Falling down, by inertia the “hump” is held below the equilibrium position; next to it another “hump” will flow out, etc., as a result of which a wave will run, characterized by a certain speed $u$ and length $\lambda$ (the distance between the “humps”). In the case we are considering, the measure of the inertia of oscillating water is its density.

So, the movement of a wave along the surface of a liquid is described by the following quantities (we will immediately write their dimensions):

$$u \left[ \frac{M}{c} \right], \lambda [M], g \left[ \frac{M}{c^2} \right], \rho \left[ \frac{K \Gamma}{M^3} \right].$$

How to connect them with each other? For example, how to find the dependence of inertia in the speed of wave $u$ on other waves $\lambda$, $g$, $\rho$? This is where the dimensions already written out will come to our aid.

We see that the dimension $u$ contains a second as its denominator. Of the three other volings, the dimension of time is included only in $g$ (there the denominator of stops is $c^2$). From here we immediately write $u \sim \sqrt{g}$, and, obviously, $g$ cannot be touched anymore: we received from it the required second in the denominator of the dimension $u$. But at the same time, $\sqrt{g}$ gave us $\sqrt{M}$ in the number, and we need a meter in the number without any root. This means that we can multiply $\sqrt{g}$ by $\sqrt{\lambda}$, and then everything will be in order: the quantity $\sqrt{g \lambda}$ has the dimension we need $[M/c]$.

So,

$$u \left[ \frac{M}{c} \right] \sim \sqrt{g \left[ \frac{M}{c^2} \right]} \cdot \lambda [M].$$

Note that we obtained only proportionality, and not equality, since $\sqrt{g \lambda}$ can be preceded by any factor $k$ that does not have dimension, i.e. $u = k \sqrt{g \lambda}$. maybe $k = 1/2$, maybe $k = 10$. Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences

This article brings us back to a wonderful representative of one-sided surfaces - the Möbius strip. It turns out from a narrow strip of paper twisted 180° and glued into a prickly shape. Here we discuss the question of which strip is suitable for making a model of a Möbius strip.

I will not suggest that you run your finger along the Möbius strip or cut it along the midline. We are talking about things less known. I'll start with a question:

1. What formula should you take for a strip of paper to glue a Möbius strip?

Expected answer: the strip should be narrow and long, with the greatest possible length-to-width ratio. Let’s say we can’t make a Möbius strip from a square sheet.

This is true, but with one caveat that is easy to underestimate: size restrictions only matter if the paper is prohibited from being wrinkled. If crumpling the paper is not prohibited, then a Möbius strip can be glued not only from a square, but from a rectangle of any size - the glued sides can even be angularly times the length of the non-glued ones.

You can do it like this (Fig. 1). Fold the straight sheet into an accordion and bend it an even number of times. Then from this accordion, like from a thick strip of paper, we glue a Möbius strip, placing the corresponding parts of the accordion into each other. From figure 1 it is clear that the sheet of paper from which the Möbius strip was glued turned out to be crumpled.

Let us now assume that the paper strip can be bent, but not wrinkled. Moreover, the width of the strip is per unit. 2. An uncrumpled sheet of paper is a “developing surface”
Since the requirement not to wrinkle the paper is so important, let's see what its mathematical meaning is.

A self-intersecting tape cannot be made from paper, but you can imagine it: like a self-intersecting line on a plane, it “passes through itself,” and you can divide it into parts, each of which does not have self-intersections.
