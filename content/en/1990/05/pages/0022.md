The distance is small equivalent to the surface surrounding this point. The lines of force pierce it and converge at point $P$, which contradicts the incompressibility of the flow. So, the potential is equal to a constant, and the force field is zero.

As you can see, Newton's theorem that the free distribution of charges on a sphere does not create an internal force field is generalized to arbitrary surfaces. Now we will deal with the explicit form of the free distribution for the surface, next in complexity to the sphere - for ellipses.

About homeods

An ellipse is a surface obtained from a sphere by stretching or sloping along three perpendicular axes with tracks, generally speaking, of different coefficients. What is obtained when describing the extension from a spherical layer is called a homeod (Fig. 2). The surfaces of the ellipses limiting the homeod are prepared, and the center of the preparation coincides with their common center.

Let's try to calculate the attraction of an infinite thin homeod at some internal point $P$. You probably agree that this attraction turns out to be zero; and to prove this, one must verify that the attractive forces of the two infinite small primids shaded at figure 2 cancel each other out. These forces are proportional to the masses (i.e., volumes) of the primids and inversely proportional to the squares of the extensions up to the point $P$. The volume of a shaded infinite small solid angle can be calculated as the product of the area $S$ of the perpendicular section of the infinite small solid angle and the length $h$ of the segment of the axis of this angle contained in the homeode. The area of ​​$S$ is proportional to the square of the distance $r$, so the force of attraction of the right prim on figure 2 is proportional to $r^2 h/r^2 = h$. Likewise, the force of attraction of the left primid is proportional to $H$. It remains to note that $h = H$ - after all, the corresponding segments lie on the same line and the lengths of their inverse images in the spherical layer are the same (Fig. 3). So, we have proven the following Newton's theorem:

the force of attraction of a homogeneous infinite thin homeod at any of its internal points is zero.

(From here, as in the case of a sphere, it follows that the attraction of a real “thick” homeod is also equal to zero.)
