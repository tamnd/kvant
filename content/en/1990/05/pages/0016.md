Re-emission
and resonance adjustment

A single braking during absorption is not enough. However, after living in an excited state for a short time, the atom returns to the ground state by emitting a photon, and is then, hopefully, ready to be absorbed again. Therefore, let us consider the emission of photons by excited atoms when they return to the ground state.

Laser photons fly wherever we direct them. The direction of emission of emitted photons changes from case to case. Both the change in the velocity of the atom during recoil and the energy of the emitted photon $\varepsilon'$ depend on the angle $\theta$ between the speed of the excited atom $v$ and the direction of departure of the emitted photon (Fig. 4). According to the law of conservation of energy

$$\frac{m(v_1')^2}{2} + \varepsilon' = \frac{mv_1^2}{2} + E,$$

where $v_1'$ is the speed of the atom after radiation. The law of conservation of momentum must be written in vector form:

$$\overrightarrow{mv_1'} + \overrightarrow{p'} = \overrightarrow{mv_1}.$$

It is clear that when a photon leaves “sideways,” the direction of motion of the atom will change by a certain angle $\varphi$.

Let us again apply an approximate calculation. To a first approximation $\varepsilon' = E$. Since $E/c$ is much smaller than $mv_1$, then again $v_1$ and $v_1'$ weights are close, and the angle $\varphi$ of the weight is small. With radius $mv_1'$ we will make a notch on the segment $mv_1$ (see Fig. 4). A small arc of a circle can be considered a straight segment perpendicular to the segment $mv$. Therefore, from the considered small right triangle, we have the expression $E \sin \theta/c$ for the length of this arc. Then the angle at which the emitted photon will deviate from the direction of motion of the atom is

$$\varphi = \frac{E \sin \theta}{mv_c},$$

and changing the speed of the atom is

$$v_1 - v_1' = \frac{E \cos \theta}{mc}.$$

In the next approximation (we neglect $v_1 - v_1'$ in comparison with $v_1$ and $v_1'$) we obtain the difference between the energy of the emitted photon and the level energy:

$$\varepsilon' - E = \frac{Ev_1 \cos \theta}{c}.$$

The coincidence of $\varepsilon'$ and $\varepsilon$ occurs only when the photon is emitted backward, at $\theta = \pi$ (see (7)). Only in this single case does the recoil during radiation exactly compensate for the retardation during absorption. (Make sure this is an exact result and not an approximation.)

In each individual act of radiation, the change in the speed of the atom depends on the angle of departure of the photon and, according to (10), is in the range from $-E/mc$ to $E/mc \approx 3$ cm/s. Due to the randomness of the departure angle, the average value of the change in velocity during radiation can be considered zero.

The largest angle of deviation of the atom velocity during radiation, according to (9), is

$$\varphi_{\max} = \frac{E}{mv_1c}.$$

At a speed of about $10^3$ m/s this is approximately $3 \cdot 10^{-5}$ rad, and at a speed $10$ m/s it is approximately $3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ rad.
