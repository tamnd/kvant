ABOUT NEWTON
ATTRACTION OF ELLIPSOIDS

V. SHAPIRO

From the editor.
This mathematical article uses concepts that are familiar to you from your physics course: force, potential, equivalent surface... Of course, all of them can be given an exact mathematical definition. But even without this, their supposed meaning is completely clear. Just in case, here are a few facts to help: the force (and potential) that several masses (or charges) create is equal to the sum of the forces (and potentials) created by each of them; if in some regions of space the potential is equal to a constant, then the force field is zero; an equivalent surface is a surface whose potential at all points is equal to a certain constant; the force vector at each point of such a surface is redistributed to it. Finally, the author freely uses (in the spirit of Newton!) the concepts of infinite small nodes, areas, volumes, etc.

Newton on the assumption of a homogeneous sphere

Isaac Newton published his main mathematical achievements in 1687 in “Mathematical Principles of Natural Physiology.” Throughout this treatise, he repeatedly returns to the theory of the acceptance of stationary and rotating bodies and the question of the shape of the earth's surface. Among other things, Newton gives a leganton proof of the following fact.

Theorem XXX from Section XIII “On the agreeable forces of spherical bodies”:

“If equal centripetal forces are directed to individual points of a spherical surface, decreasing in relation to the squares of the distances, then a particle placed inside this surface does not experience any pull from such forces in any direction.”

In other words, a homogeneous mathematical sphere does not accept internal points.

Newton argues this way. Let $P$ be an arbitrary point inside the sphere (Fig. 1). Let's take a small telephone with center $P$ and show that the forces from which two infinitesimal parts of the surface, carved on it by an angle, act on $P$ cancel each other out. Indeed, the force with which it acts on $P$ of each of the parts is proportional to the mass of the part, i.e., its area, and inversely proportional to the square of the distance to the point $P$. The bases of an infinite narrow end with a vertex $P$ can be considered flat. As can be seen from figure 1, which depicts a section of a sphere by a plane passing through the center $O$ and the axis of the ends, this axis makes equal angles to the base. Consequently, the ends are similar, and the ratio of the areas of their bases is equal to the ratio of the squares of the lengths of their axes. Therefore, the forces of acceptance of the two parts in question are mutually destroyed. Newton concludes:

“From this reasoning it follows that the acceptance of the entire spherical surface, as a state of opposite elements, is destroyed; therefore, $P$ this acceptance of the movement does not work out in any direction.”
