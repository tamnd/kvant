But this statement no longer applies to three-dimensional space, but to the straight line $l$. Restricting $f$ to this line, we obtain a function of one variable, which we will continue to denote by $f(t)$. Let $a$ be one of the intersection points of $l$ and $N$. Then $f(a)=0$. We will now express the length of $h$ in terms of $\varepsilon$. Look at figure4:

$$f(a)=0 \text{ и } f(a+h)=\varepsilon.$$

By definition arbitrary,

$$f'(a)=\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}=\frac{\varepsilon-0}{h}=\frac{\varepsilon}{h}.$$

Therefore $h=\varepsilon/f'(a)$ (we wrote the exact equality because both $\varepsilon$ and $h$ are infinitesimal). So we need to prove that

$$\frac{1}{f'(a_1)}+\frac{1}{f'(a_2)}+\dots+\frac{1}{f'(a_n)}=0, \quad (1)$$

where $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ are the intersection points of $l$ and $N$, i.e., the roots of the polynomial $f(t)$.

Recall that $f(t)$ is a polynomial of $n$ degree, having exactly $n$ roots. Such a polynomial (up to the constructed polynomial) can be written in the form

$$f(t)=(t-a_1)(t-a_2)...(t-a_n),$$

where $a_1, \dots, a_n$ are its roots. Differentiating this equality, we get:

$$f'(t)=(t-a_2)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)+$$

$$+(t-a_1)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)+\dots+$$

$$+(t-a_1)(t-a_2)...(t-a_{n-1}).$$

In part,

$$f'(a_i)=(a_i-a_1)(a_i-a_2)...$$

$$...(a_i-a_{i-1})(a_i-a_{i+1})...(a_i-a_n).$$

All terms, except $i$, contain the set $(a_i-a_i)$ and form zero.

We see that equality (1) is equivalent to the identity:

$$\frac{1}{(a_1-a_2)(a_1-a_3)...(a_1-a_n)}+\frac{1}{(a_2-a_1)(a_2-a_3)...(a_2-a_n)}+\dots+\frac{1}{(a_n-a_1)(a_n-a_2)...(a_n-a_{n-1})}=0. \quad (2)$$

It's hard to believe, but this identity holds for any pairwise distinct numbers $a_1, \dots, a_n$. And it is truly proven by the Olympics!

Consider a polynomial of $(n-1)$ degree:

$$g(t)=\frac{(t-a_2)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)}{(a_1-a_2)(a_1-a_3)...(a_1-a_n)}+\frac{(t-a_1)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)}{(a_2-a_1)(a_2-a_3)...(a_2-a_n)}+\dots+\frac{(t-a_1)(t-a_2)...(t-a_{n-1})}{(a_n-a_1)(a_n-a_2)...(a_n-a_{n-1})}.$$

At $t=a_i$, all terms except the $i$th become zero, and the $i$th become 1. Therefore

$$g(a_1)=g(a_2)=\dots=g(a_n)=1.$$

This means that a polynomial of $(n-1)$ degree $g(t)-1$ has $n$ roots, which is possible only if $g(t)-1=0$ for all $t$. In part, the leading term of the polynomial $g(t)$ is equal to zero. But this is the right part of identity (2)! Thus, this identity, and with it Arnold’s theorem, is proven.

We proved the theorem even with “some margin”. Indeed, not only the leading term is equal to zero
