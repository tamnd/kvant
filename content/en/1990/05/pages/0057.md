made of any homogeneous material with a resistivity $\varrho$. Let's denote its length by $l$. Let us imagine this conductor consisting of several parts connected in series, for example three. Let their lengths be $l_1, l_2$ and $l_3$ (Fig. 2). It's obvious that
$$l = l_1 + l_2 + l_3.$$
Let's multiply both sides of this equality by the ratio $\varrho/S$:
$$\varrho \frac{l}{S} = \varrho \frac{l_1}{S} + \varrho \frac{l_2}{S} + \varrho \frac{l_3}{S}.$$
But $\varrho l/S = R$ is the resistance of the entire conductor, $\varrho l_1/S = R_1$, $\varrho l_2/S = R_2$ and $\varrho l_3/S = R_3$ are the resistances of its first, second and third parts, respectively. Thus,
$$R = R_1 + R_2 + R_3.$$ (2)
This is the required formula for calculating the total resistance when connecting conductors in series.
Now imagine the same conductor consisting of several, for example again three, parallel connected parts with cross sections $S_1, S_2$ and $S_3$ (Fig. 3). Similar to the previous case,
$$S = S_1 + S_2 + S_3,$$
or, after multiplying by the common factor $1/(\varrho l)$,
$$\frac{S}{\varrho l} = \frac{S_1}{\varrho l} + \frac{S_2}{\varrho l} + \frac{S_3}{\varrho l}.$$

In accordance with formula (1),
$$\frac{S}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R}, \frac{S_1}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R_1}, \frac{S_2}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R_2} \text{ и } \frac{S_3}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R_3},$$
we get it in a poseable way
$$\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}.$$ (3)

Using this formula, you can find the total resistance when connecting conductors in parallel.
Formulas (2) and (3) are derived here only for the particular case of a specific type of conductor - the same material, the same cross sections in the first case and the same lengths in the second. However, they are also applied to the most general cases.

Is the computer in the refrigerator?

How do you imagine a supercomputer of the 21st century? The size of a skyscraper? Hardly. This is what people might have thought about thirty years ago, when the emerging electronic monsters operated on tubes or, at best, on transistors and actually occupied entire halls. Today's computers are becoming smaller and smaller in size.

Now is the time to remember that when even weak currents flow through conductors, Joule heat is released and, as they say, energy dissipation occurs. Therefore, overminimization should inevitably lead to overheating and the inability of the computer to work. Where is the way out of this impasse?
