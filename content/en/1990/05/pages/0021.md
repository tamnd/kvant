Attraction field of a homogeneous sphere in the external region

To find out what the gravitational field of the inner sphere would be, Newton became interested in the question of what gravitational force the sphere creates in the outer region of space. Due to the symmetry of the problem, this force will depend only on the distance to the center of the sphere (and, of course, on its mass). But how exactly?

Let's forget for a moment that we are studying the forces of attraction and talk about the flow of liquid. Let us imagine that at the origin of coordinates there is a source from which there is an incompressible fluid along the radii of spherical symmetrical distances. Due to incompressibility, the same amount of liquid flows through each sphere centered at the source per unit time. This amount is proportional to the area of ​​the sphere, i.e., the square of its radius, and proportional to the speed of the fluid. This means that the speed of the current is inversely proportional to the square of the distance to the source.

We made an important observation: the velocity field of a spherical homogeneous incompressible fluid is the same as the force field of an exact mass (or electric charge). This means that the force field of an exact mass has the property of incompressibility: the magnitude of its flow through the boundaries of any region that does not contain mass is equal to zero - as much as it flows in, it goes up. The same is true for any distribution of masses (and charges), or the force that several masses create is equal to the sum of the forces created by each of them. We are now in a position to prove the following theorem:

the force of attraction of a homogeneous sphere of mass M coincides with the force created by the exact mass M placed at the center of the sphere.

Indeed, as we have already established, the force field of a sphere is spherical, symmetrical and incompressible. But we know that the only radial velocity field of an incompressible fluid is inversely proportional to the square of the distance to the center, i.e., it coincides with the field of the exact mass. That this mass coincides with the mass of the sphere follows from a comparison of the fluxes through the surfaces enclosing a given sphere.

This completes the study of the attraction of a homogeneous sphere. If we tried to calculate the forces directly, that is, by interpreting the contributions of incompressibly small pieces of the surface, we would certainly encounter great difficulties. Now our task is to generalize the results obtained to more complex surfaces.

On the free distribution of the sphere

The free distribution of a sphere on a closed conducting surface is the distribution that is obtained if the charge is given the opportunity to distribute independently in the absence of external forces. For example, on a sphere such a distribution will be uniform. Free distribution has two important properties.

Every surface is equivalent to the free distribution of a sphere on it.

This is very useful: the potential difference on the surface would lead to a redistribution of the sphere on it.

The force field of the free distribution of a sphere inside a closed surface is equal to zero (and the potential is equal to constant).
