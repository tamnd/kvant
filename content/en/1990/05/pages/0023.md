Let $a, b$ and $c$ be distance coefficients, i.e., with distance, the coordinate $x$ turns into $ax, y$ - into $by, a$ - into $cz$. In this case, the equation of the unit sphere

$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$$

will turn into the ellipson equation

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$$

(think: why did $a, b$ and $c$ appear in the denominator?). In geometry, the coordinates $x, y$ and $z$ are multiplied by the same number; therefore the equation of close surface geometry will be

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 + \varepsilon$$

(a geometry coefficient is $\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}$). So, the density of the free charge distribution on the surface of the ellipson $M$, given the equation

$$f(x, y, z) = \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$$,

proportional to the distance to the infinitely close surface $M_\varepsilon$ given by the equation

$$f(x, y, z) = 1 + \varepsilon$$.

Now we will take a leap of 300 years and cross from Newton's time to our days. Consider the surface $N$, given the equation

$$f(x, y, z) = 0$$,

where $f$ is a polynomial of $n$ degree in three variables. Let us distribute on $N$ a charge with a density proportional to the distance from $N$ to the infinitely close surface $N_\varepsilon$ given by the equation

$$f(x, y, z) = \varepsilon$$.

We are going to prove that such a charged surface does not accept its internal points. But which points are called internal?

Let's look at figure 3, which depicts the surface defined by a neokot polynomial of the 6th degree. Point $R$, of course, cannot be considered internal; How about from the point $Q_1, Q_2$ or $P$? Let's give a definition:

a point is called internal to the surface $N$ if each line passing through it intersects $N$ at exactly $n$ points ($n$ is the degree of the polynomial defining $N$)*. With this definition, neither the point $R$, nor the points $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are internal, and the point $P$ is internal.

Now we can formulate a theorem of the famous Soviet mathematician V. I. Arnold:

the charged surface $N$, given by the equation $n$ of the $f(x, y, z) = 0$ degree, with a charge density proportional to the distance to the infinitely close surface $N_\varepsilon$, given by the equation $f(x, y, z) = \varepsilon$, does not accept its internal points $P$. The sign of the charge varies: positive on the piece of surface closest to $P$, negative on the next one, etc. (Fig. 3).
