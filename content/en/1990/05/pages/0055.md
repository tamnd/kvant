About the electronic resistance of conductors
As you certainly know, the electronic resistance of a conductor depends on the materials from which it is made, the size and shape of the conductor. So, for example, for a homogeneous conductor of constant cross-section $S$ and length $l$ the resistance

$$R = \rho \frac{l}{S},$$

where $\rho$ is the specific electronic resistance. Sometimes it is more convenient to talk not about the resistance of a conductor, but about its inverse value - electronic conductivity.

The first physicist who tried to figure out the significance of the passage of direct electron current through conductors was a modest schoolteacher from Cologne, Georg Simon Ohm (1789-1854). Ohm published the results of his first experiments in 1826.

Of course, Ohm did not have modern high-precision electronic measuring instruments and reliable current sources at his disposal, so Ohm had to solve a number of complex practical problems along the way.

Thus, as current sources in his first experiments, Ohm used voltaic columns - an interlaced layer of two dissimilar metals (for example, silver and zinc), separated by paper soaked in a raster of salt. At the same time, he noticed that the current strength in the galvanic circuit noticeably kills over time. It is clear that in such conditions it was almost pointless to establish such quantitative patterns. When Ohm became acquainted with the works of Seebeck (Thomas Johann Seebeck (1770-1831) - German physicist), who discovered the thermoelectric effect in 1821, he began to use a thermal element in his first, which gives a very stable current.
