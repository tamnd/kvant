Having learned about the experiments of Oersted (Hans Kristin Oersted (1777-1851) - Danish physicist), who discovered the effect of electric current on a magnetic needle in 1820, Ohm decided to characterize the strength of the current by the angle of deflection of the magnetic needle located near the current-carrying conductor. To do this, the conductor was placed in the plane of the magnetic meridian (see Fig. 1), in the absence of current in the conductor, the magnetic needle 6 was located above it and was parallel to it, and the slightly solid wire 7, to which the arrow was suspended, was not deformed. When current flowed through the conductor, the magnetic needle left the plane of the magnetic meridian and twisted the suspension. Ohm turned head 8, where the upper end of the suspension was fixed, so that the arrow was again parallel to the current-carrying conductor, and measured the angle of rotation. This angle was taken as a characteristic of the magnetic action of electric current.

To study the conductivity of various metals, Ohm took wires of the same cross-section, but made of different materials, and connected them one by one into the circuit. As an eton, he chose copper wiring of a certain length, taking its conductivity as 1000 conventional units, and measured the angle of rotation of the head at which the magnetic needle becomes parallel to the current-carrying conductor. Then he included wiring from other metals in the circuit and shortened them until the angle of rotation of the head became the same as in the case of this wiring. Based on the length obtained, it was possible to judge the conductivity of the corresponding materials. Thus, Ohm found that the conductivity of gold is 574 conventional units, silver - 356, zinc - 333, etc.

Then Ohm examined wires made of the same metal, but of different thicknesses, and treated it in the same way as when determining the conductivity of various metals. He found that the resistance of wires made of the same materials is the same if the ratios of their lengths to the relative areas of their cross sections, that is, if the ratios $l/S$ for these wires are the same. As a result, it was found that the resistance $R$ is directly proportional to this ratio: $R \sim l/S$.
