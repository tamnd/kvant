Despite the ubiquity of friction in the world around us and the apparent simplicity of the laws of dry friction, the physics of friction at the microsolar level is not completely clear even today.
From time to time, articles appear in scientific journals in which, on the basis of one or another model, experimental laws of dry friction are derived from the fundamental principles of physics. As estimates show, humanity spends 5-10% of all work done on overcoming friction and its destructive consequences. If the friction disappears, then in machines, machine tools, engines and other various devices involved in modern production processes, energy loss, wear, and noise are reduced.

But, at the same time, if the training disappears, then... chaos will ensue. Moving trains, cars, bicycles, trams will not be able to stay, and those at rest will not be able to move. But can trene disappear? What does “there is no training” mean? What is, finally, friction force? This will be discussed in this note.

As is known, the friction force (more precisely, the dry friction force) $F$ is determined by the friction coefficient $k$, depends on the type of substances from which the rubbing bodies are made, and the quality of processing of their surfaces, and the force $N$ of the normal pressure of one body on another:

$$F = kN.$$

Let us imagine that in all the bodies around us, any small surface near the atomic surface is absolutely smooth, i.e. $k_i = 0$. Then for any magnitude of force $N_i$ acting on this section, $F_i = 0$. Obviously, this would mean that trene as a property of the substance disappeared.

However, it turns out that even if friction as a property of a substance disappears, the force of friction between bodies can nevertheless manifest itself. To show this, let's look at an example.

Let us make from a substance that does not have friction, two bodies whose contacting surfaces have identical grooves with a cross-section in the form of equilateral triangles with a base angle $\alpha$, base length $2l$ and height $h$ (Fig. 1). Let the mass of the upper body be equal to $m$ and, accordingly, the force of normal pressure of the upper body on the lower body is equal to $N = mg$. Let us apply a force $F$ to the upper body along the horizontal plane, “averaging” the grooves, and we will try to use this force to slowly move the upper body relative to the lower one. It is clear that when the upper body moves to the top $l$, its center of gravity rises to a height $h$ and the body acquires potential energy

$$E_p = mgh.$$

In this case, the force $F$ will do the work

$$A = Fl.$$

According to the law of conservation of mechanical energy,

$$E_p = A,$$ or $mgh = Fl,$

where

$$F = \frac{h}{l} mg.$$

Let us denote the relation $h/l$ by $k$, take into account that $mg = N$, and obtain

$$F = kN.$$

It can be seen that in order for one body to begin to move on the surface of another in the absence of friction, it is necessary to apply a force, the magnitude of which is determined in exactly the same way as the magnitude of the friction force.

Thus, the assumption that trene as a property of matter disappeared did not lead to the disappearance of trene's power. In this case, the friction coefficient turns out to be dependent only on the geometric parameters of irregularities on the surface of the contacting bodies.

Of course, everyone is well aware of the problem in which the meaning of the friction coefficient is illustrated using an inclined plane and a body on it. If you change the angle of inclination of the plane to the horizon, starting from zero, then the friction coefficient will be equal to the tangent of the angle $\alpha_0$ at which the body lying on the plane begins to slide. But even in our consideration of an idealized example of the absence of friction as a property of matter, the movement of a body on an inclined plane begins with angles $\alpha_0$, for

Let us return, however, to our first example (see Fig. 1). In fact, the friction force is significant only at the beginning of the movement, so let’s consider what will happen to our ideal bodies when their mutual horizontal movement becomes greater than $l$. The upper body, having risen to the crest of the furrow, begins to fall down, its potential energy will be converted into kinetic energy, and at the end of the descent it will impact the rising edge of the next furrow. If in this case it again becomes possible to completely convert kinetic energy into potential energy, i.e. it will be possible to lift the upper body to the crest of the next furrow, then further movement of the upper body can be carried out without the application of additional force. And this will mean that the friction force has disappeared. If this does not happen, then, despite the complete absence of friction as a property of the substance, the friction force will remain.

Let us consider in more detail the change in the speed of the upper body when it hits the edge of the next furrow dk (Fig. 3). We will assume that the conditions are ideal and there is no energy loss during the impact (i.e., an absolute elastic impact occurs). In this case, the following law is valid: the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection; in this case, the angles are measured from the perpendicular to the surface from which the collision will occur, i.e., from the perpendicular to the ascending edge of the groove in our case (see angles $\gamma$ on figure 3).

Let us denote the speed at the end of the descent by $\vec{v}_1$ (it is directed along the plane of descent from the furrow), and the speed after the impact by $\vec{v}_2$. Let us decompose these velocities into components along the $X$ and $Y$ axes, directed horizontally and vertically, respectively*):

$$v_{1x} = v_1 \cos \alpha, \quad v_{1y} = v_1 \sin \alpha,$$
$$v_{2x} = v_2 \cos \varphi, \quad v_{2y} = v_2 \sin \varphi.$$

From figure 3 we find the correspondence between the angles $\alpha$ and $\varphi$:

$$\varphi = \pi - \alpha - 2\gamma,$$
$$\gamma = \pi - \pi/2 - 2\alpha = \pi/2 - 2\alpha,$$
where
$$\varphi = 3\alpha.$$

Let us use the trionometry formulas known from reference books
$$\cos 3\alpha = 4 \cos^3 \alpha - 3 \cos \alpha,$$
$$\sin 3\alpha = -4 \sin^3 \alpha + 3 \sin \alpha,$$
$$\cos^2 \alpha + \sin^2 \alpha = 1$$

and write down the relative correspondence after the impact and before it:

$$\frac{v_{2x}}{v_{1x}} = \frac{\cos 3\alpha}{\cos \alpha} = 1 - 4 \sin^2 \alpha,$$
$$\frac{v_{2y}}{v_{1y}} = \frac{\sin 3\alpha}{\sin \alpha} = -(1 - 4 \cos^2 \alpha).$$

Under conditions of conservation of energy, the upper body, after hitting the lower one, will rise exactly to the crest of the next furrow dk and only if the velocity vector $\vec{v}_2$ is directed upward along the plane of the furrow dk, and the velocity values ​​remain the same as before the impact. Thisomy meets the conditions

$$\frac{v_{2x}}{v_{1x}} = 1 \text{ и } \frac{v_{2y}}{v_{1y}} = -1.$$
