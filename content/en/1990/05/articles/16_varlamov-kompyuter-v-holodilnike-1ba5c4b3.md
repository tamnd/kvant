Is the computer in the refrigerator?

How do you imagine a supercomputer of the 21st century? The size of a skyscraper? Hardly. This is what people might have thought about thirty years ago, when the emerging electronic monsters operated on tubes or, at best, on transistors and actually occupied entire halls. Today's computers are becoming smaller and smaller in size.

Now is the time to remember that when even weak currents flow through conductors, Joule heat is released and, as they say, energy dissipation occurs. Therefore, overminimization should inevitably lead to overheating and the inability of the computer to work. Where is the way out of this impasse?

One of the visible ways today is the creation of computers of the following elements, in which energy dissipation is practically completely absent. About the miraculous phenomenon of current flow without resistance,

Superconductivity first became known on April 28, 1911, when the Dutch scientist G. Kamerlingh-Onnes, at a meeting of the Royal Academy of Sciences in Amsterdam, reported on the striking effect he had just discovered - the disappearance of the electrical resistance of a mercury sample cooled with a liquid gel to a record low temperature for that time - up to 4.15 K. This phenomenon sharply contradicted the prevailing classical ideas about the electrically conductive properties of metals.

Superconductivity began to be intensively studied. It was soon discovered that the zero resistance of a superconductor apparently holds true not approximately, but strictly (thus, the current excited in a superconducting ring could circulate in it for years without changing in value). Other surprising properties were also discovered. For example, the complete displacement of the magnetic field from the volume of the conductor during its transition to the superconducting state (this phenomenon occurs at moderately strong fields). The range of known superconducting metals has also expanded significantly. The prospects for the practical application of the discovered phenomenon seemed limitless: lossless energy transmission lines, super-powerful magnets, super-energy-consuming batteries, new types of transport...

However, two seemingly insurmountable obstacles stood in the way of the implementation of all these projects. The first is the extremely low temperatures at which superconductivity is observed. Thus, over 75 years of research in many laboratories around the world, the critical temperature - as the temperature of the transition of a substance into the superconducting state is called - was only raised to 23 K. The Nb₃Ge alloy became the “record holder” in 1973. This remnant made almost all areas of using superconductivity unprofitable due to the high cost of cooling the scarce liquid gel.

But there was a second advantage - the already mentioned “fear” of superconductors producing magnetic fields. The reason for this “dislike” is understandable - according to the law of electromagnetic induction, the penetration of the field would lead to the appearance of an infinitely large current in the superconductor, which it is simply not able to pass. However, if “superconducting” currents arise in the surface layer of the conductor, then with their magnetic field they will be able to completely cancel out the external field. Up to a certain point, superconductors “act” like this, but then they “give up” and abruptly transform into a normal, non-superconducting state even at low temperatures (in other words, only the so-called type I superconductors behave this way, but they were studied for a long time, and the existence of others was simply not known). This advantage turned out to be even more unpleasant than the need for low temperatures. Indeed, a magnetic field always accompanies current protection; therefore, it is impossible to pass a sufficiently large current through a superconductor without destroying the superconducting states themselves.

Despite these surprising circumstances, scientists continued to study the phenomenon of superconductivity and accumulate data on the properties of superconductors. It remained unclear and mysterious for almost half a century. Only in 1957 was a consistent microscopic theory created, which explained the amazing properties of most of the known superconductors. The phenomenon of superconductivity turned out to be associated with the appearance of a difference between electrons in some metals. Yes, yes, don’t be surprised, it’s just attraction, although electrons are equally charged particles. The nature of this attraction is purely quantum.

technical character and is closely related to the interaction of electrons with the surrounding crystal lattice. Thanks to this adoption, at very low temperatures, some of the electrons in the metal combine into so-called Cooper pairs (named after their discoverer L. Cooper). They are in a special quantum state, such that they can carry electric current without interacting with the crystal lattice, and therefore without releasing heat.*) For electrons forming a Cooper pair, interaction with the lattice has already been realized (by combining them into a pair), and now they constitute a “caste of untouchables”. It is surprising that the sizes of such pairs on the atomic scale are very large - they can reach hundreds and even thousands of international distances. So the Cooper couple should be imagined not as a double star rotating relative to its center of mass, but as two partners who came to the disco together, but, separated by ten other tandoors, are dancing at different ends of the hall.**)

The creation of the theory of superconductivity of the latter was a powerful impetus for further research of this phenomenon. Around the same time, one of the two main advantages in the way of its practical application was the discovery of the so-called superconductors of the second type, which, while partially admitting a magnetic field (in the form of separate vortices), still retained the property of dissipative current flow. Superconducting magnets arrived in laboratories and in production, but their operation still required a scarce and expensive liquid gel.

High-temperature superconductivity became a kind of Eldorado for physicists in the 60s and 80s, but for a long time its “popular” efforts remained ineffective.

The Nb₃Ge alloy with a critical temperature T=23 K, discovered in 1973, remained a record holder for 14 years, which was still very far from the desired “nitrogen barer”, i.e., before the emergence of superconductivity when cooled with fission liquid nitrogen with a boiling point (at atomic pressure) of 77 K. And so, in 1986, the scales of the world (and, due to the importance discovery, not only physical) was all over the discovery of high-temperature superconductivity. Ceramic (!) compounds turned out to have this property (at first they were very capricious in terms of the method of their manufacture). It is remarkable that until then all superconducting materials in their normal state were metals that conducted electric current well, while ceramic substances in their normal state conducted current much worse.

In a short time, the critical temperatures in the new class of superconductors were raised from 30 to 95 K, and then to 120-125 K. The phenomenon of superconductivity with nitrogen cooling can be demonstrated without much effort even in a school physics classroom.

It seems that the superconducting computer of the 21st century is moving from being a dream to becoming a near future. An important step towards this has already been taken.
