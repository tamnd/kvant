I. VOROBYEV

Didn’t it seem to you that in the combination of these words - “cooling with light” - there is some kind of unsettledness? In fact, on a hot day we look for coolness in the shade, trying to hide from the sun. How about cooling a substance by irradiating it with light? After all, to understand temperature, you need to take away energy, but light brings it. However, a light refrigeration unit has been created, and with its help the scales reach a low temperature of $2,4 \cdot 10^{-4}$ K.

Let's try to find a way to “produce cold” with light.

Team of atoms and team of photons

First of all, let us remember that temperature is associated with the average kinetic energy of thermal motion of atoms:

$$\frac{3}{2} kT = \frac{m v^2}{2}.$$ (1)

To make it lower, it is enough to notice the atoms. Why not use photons for braking? A photon, as is known, has a momentum $p_\phi = \varepsilon/c$, where $\varepsilon$ is the energy of the photon, $c$ is its speed, i.e. the speed of light. If a flying atom absorbs an oncoming photon, then it, the atom, will slow down. But how can a photon “get” into a flying atom?

It is clear that there is no point in “targeting” a single atom; you need to “target” a bunch of atoms with a beam of photons. Naturally, a laser must be used as a source of decelerating light. It emits a highly directed stream of photons of the same energy. To get a directed flow of atoms, let's do the following. Let's evaporate a grain of substance in a vacuum chamber (with an electric discharge or a laser light pulse). Two partitions with small responders will separate a thin beam of vapor from the flying object. This way we get a team of atoms lying towards the laser beam (Fig. 1).

Great, the contours of the refrigeration unit are beginning to emerge. But will the light be absorbed by a relatively rarefied beam? (After all, gases, as a rule, are not transparent. And this means that photons will sing through the thickness of the gas, practically without colliding with its atoms.) And if so, will the atom be forced to slow down in this process and lose some of its energy?

Moreover, both of these issues are closely related.

In this case, the first excited state is separated by energies $E_1$, the second by $E_2$, etc. The steps themselves have a certain thickness: many atoms in a given excited state have energies from $E - \Gamma/2$ to $E + \Gamma/2$, where the value $\Gamma$ is called the width of the level with energies $E$. The probability of encountering an atom outside the level width is small and drops sharply as the deviation from the middle of the step increases.

There is a clear correspondence between the energies of an atom and the energies of the absorbed background: an atom can only absorb a photon whose energy corresponds to the transition from one step to another. This resonant nature of absorption mainly explains the high transparency of gases - if the background energy differs from the transition energy by more than the width of the level, the absorption is sharply weakened and disappears.

So, with a special choice of photon energy, absorption occurs with the transition of atoms from one state to another, in which the internal energy is greater. Due to the shock when absorbing an oncoming photon, the atom will slow down and its kinetic energy will decrease. But this does not contradict the law of conservation of energy. In this case, part of the kinetic energy of the atom and the photon energy go to increase the internal energy of the atom. A joint consideration of the balances of momentum and energy during the interaction of atoms and photons allows us to understand the possibility of light cooling.

Let's put aside the calculations for now. Let's think about this. It is unlikely that a single absorption will exactly extinguish the speed of atoms. Therefore, their fate after absorption is important. The atom itself is stabilized only in the ground state, at the lowest energy level. The lifetime of an atom in an excited state is limited. For the first level, the typical lifetime is on the order of $10^{-8}$ s. On average, after this time, the atom will go to the ground state, emitting a photon. How does the radiation of the background influence the motion of the atom? Will it not compensate for the inhibition at the absorption stage? Will it cause the atom to fly out of the beam and the laser beam? This requires quantitative assessments.

Re-emitted photons can also be absorbed by atoms - resonance conditions are also met for them. But the number of these “secondary” photons is much less than the number of photons in an intense laser beam. In addition, they are emitted by atoms in a variety of directions and large parts quickly leave the atomic beam. So absorption of re-emitted photons can be avoided.

The conceivable processes are not limited to resonant absorption and emission. A high energy photon can knock an electron out of an atom; scattering is possible when a photon is absorbed and emitted in one act of interaction (with elastic scattering - without transition from level to level, with inelastic scattering - with a change in internal energy); two-photon absorption is possible, etc. However, these possible processes of arrival are much less likely, or they can be “detached” by choosing the photon energy range.

Tuning into Resonance

The actual experiment was done with sodium. Let us dwell on the transition from the ground state to the first excited state with internal energy $E=2,1$ eV*) and level width $\Gamma=4,4 \cdot 10^{-8}$ eV. Such a narrow resonance requires high precision in the choice of photon energy. The mass of a sodium atom is $m=22$ $\Gamma\beta/B/c^{2**}$, where $c=3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s is the speed of light. Let us consider the conditions of resonant absorption during a “head-on” collision of an atom moving with a speed $v$ and a photon with some energy $\varepsilon$ (Fig. 3). According to the law of conservation of momentum

$$mv_1 = mv - \varepsilon/c,$$

where $v_1$ is the speed of the atom after photon absorption. According to the law of conservation of energy,

$$\frac{mv_1^2}{2} + E = \varepsilon + \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

Solving these equations gives the photon energy $\varepsilon$, which provides resonant absorption, and the speed of the atom $v_1$ after absorption.

At a temperature of the order of $10^3$ K, necessary for the evaporation of sodium, the speed of atoms is much less than light speed. It is reasonable to use the smallness of $v$ and $v_1$ compared to $c$ and find an approximate solution with accuracy that reflects us. We must also keep in mind that the velocities of the atoms in the beam are different, so only a portion of the atoms resonate. In fact, according to (7) for the upper and lower boundaries of the level we have

Re-emission
and resonance adjustment

A single braking during absorption is not enough. However, after living in an excited state for a short time, the atom returns to the ground state by emitting a photon, and is then, hopefully, ready to be absorbed again. Therefore, let us consider the emission of photons by excited atoms when they return to the ground state.

Laser photons fly wherever we direct them. The direction of emission of emitted photons changes from case to case. Both the change in the velocity of the atom during recoil and the energy of the emitted photon $\varepsilon'$ depend on the angle $\theta$ between the speed of the excited atom $v$ and the direction of departure of the emitted photon (Fig. 4). According to the law of conservation of energy

$$\frac{m(v_1')^2}{2} + \varepsilon' = \frac{mv_1^2}{2} + E,$$

where $v_1'$ is the speed of the atom after radiation. The law of conservation of momentum must be written in vector form:

$$\overrightarrow{mv_1'} + \overrightarrow{p'} = \overrightarrow{mv_1}.$$

It is clear that when a photon leaves “sideways,” the direction of motion of the atom will change by a certain angle $\varphi$.

Let us again apply an approximate calculation. To a first approximation $\varepsilon' = E$. Since $E/c$ is much smaller than $mv_1$, then again $v_1$ and $v_1'$ weights are close, and the angle $\varphi$ of the weight is small. With radius $mv_1'$ we will make a notch on the segment $mv_1$ (see Fig. 4). A small arc of a circle can be considered a straight segment perpendicular to the segment $mv$. Therefore, from the considered small right triangle, we have the expression $E \sin \theta/c$ for the length of this arc. Then the angle at which the emitted photon will deviate from the direction of motion of the atom is

$$\varphi = \frac{E \sin \theta}{mv_c},$$

and changing the speed of the atom is

$$v_1 - v_1' = \frac{E \cos \theta}{mc}.$$

In the next approximation (we neglect $v_1 - v_1'$ in comparison with $v_1$ and $v_1'$) we obtain the difference between the energy of the emitted photon and the level energy:

$$\varepsilon' - E = \frac{Ev_1 \cos \theta}{c}.$$

The coincidence of $\varepsilon'$ and $\varepsilon$ occurs only when the photon is emitted backward, at $\theta = \pi$ (see (7)). Only in this single case does the recoil during radiation exactly compensate for the retardation during absorption. (Make sure this is an exact result and not an approximation.)

In each individual act of radiation, the change in the speed of the atom depends on the angle of departure of the photon and, according to (10), is in the range from $-E/mc$ to $E/mc \approx 3$ cm/s. Due to the randomness of the departure angle, the average value of the change in velocity during radiation can be considered zero.

The largest angle of deviation of the atom velocity during radiation, according to (9), is

$$\varphi_{\max} = \frac{E}{mv_1c}.$$

At a speed of about $10^3$ m/s this is approximately $3 \cdot 10^{-5}$ rad, and at a speed $10$ m/s it is approximately $3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ rad.

But this is for the time being. After decreasing the speed by $\Delta V = c \frac{\Gamma}{E} = 6$ m/s (see (8)), the resonance will stop. For further braking, adjustment becomes necessary. Analyzing the resonance conditions (6), two tuning methods can be proposed.

The first method is to smoothly increase the energy of photons in accordance with the decrease in the speed of atoms. To do this, you need a laser with variable energy of the photons it emits. There are such lasers.

The second way is to influence the energy $E$ itself. In particular, energy levels change slightly in an external magnetic field. In a field that is constant in time and varies smoothly along the beam axis, for an atom with any speed there is a place with a suitable field value. Both methods are actually implemented.

Let's take a closer look at the first method. Let us choose the initial photon energy such that it corresponds to resonance at a speed $V$ exceeding, say, the speed of 90% of the beam atoms. Let us begin to gradually increase the photon energy up to a value equal to the energy of the $E$ level, corresponding to resonance with almost motionless atoms.

First, atoms are decelerated in the speed range from $V - \frac{1}{2} c \frac{\Gamma}{E}$ to $V + \frac{1}{2} c \frac{\Gamma}{E}$. Due to the slowing down of atoms, this range will gradually become almost empty - the velocities of almost all atoms will certainly become less than $V$.

As the photon energy increases, the atoms are slowed down to be in resonance again, but atoms of the beam will be added to them, initially having a suitable speed. Thus, all atoms with velocities less than $V$ must achieve resonance when their deceleration begins.

Let us estimate the braking time from speed $V$ to almost zero speed. The number of absorption-emission cycles during this time is $N = V/(E/mc)$

$E/mc$ — decrease in speed in the act of absorption (see (5)); We neglect the change in speed during the emission of a photon excited by an atom.) The duration of the cycle $\Delta t$ is determined by the lifetime $\tau$ in the excited state, since at high intensity of the laser beam absorption occurs quickly. Thus, the total braking time is

$$t = N \cdot \Delta t = N \tau = \frac{mcV}{E} \tau.$$

At an initial speed of $V = 3 \cdot 10^3$ m/s $t = 10^{-3}$ s. (Recall that in the experiment conducted with sodium, $m = 22 \text{ ГэВ}/c^2$, $E = 2,1 \text{ эВ.}$)

The distance at which braking occurs estimates the values ​​of $l = Vt/2 \approx 1$ m. This distance is significant not only for choosing the dimensions of the vacuum chamber. The dynamism of the laser beam is limited. A noticeable angular deviation of the velocities from the axis of the atomic beam leads to the departure of atoms from the decelerating beam (Fig. 5). With a beam diameter of the order of uniform centimeters per meter length, a deviation of the order of $10^{-2}$ rad is sufficient. Deviations of this order are achieved during separation due to sideways radiation for atoms with velocities of $10-3$ m/s. The almost complete loss of atoms in this case limits the possibilities of “single-beam” braking. The temperature range indicated above corresponds to the temperature range from 0.1 to 0.01 K. The actually obtained temperature of 0.05 K is consistent with our rough estimates.

Even slow atoms, due to the dispersion of speed and direction of movement, will scatter relatively quickly. “Ultra-cold” atoms need to be somehow collected and kept in a compact group. A trap for atoms, even two traps - one inside the other, was also built from light.

An external “large” trap, which also provides additional cooling, is a kind of development of a braking beam. It is formed by the intersection of six laser beams with photon energy $\varepsilon_0 = E - \frac{\Gamma}{2}$, corresponding to the lower boundary of the level (Fig. 6).

The law of conservation of energy during absorption in this case leads to the equality:

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} - \frac{\Gamma}{2} = \frac{mv_1^2}{2}.$$

At kinetic energy atoms $\frac{mv^2}{2} > \frac{\Gamma}{2}$ the absorption of a “braking” photon is possible, but the absorption of an “accelerating” one does not occur. For in order for an atom that has absorbed a photon to be able to absorb a photon flying towards the bottom, this photon must have an energy at least slightly above the lower boundary of the level. The atom will therefore be replaced, and the direction of its copy during absorption and emission will change quite noticeably. This will lead to a confusing trajectory and, as a result, to a rather long wandering of atoms in the area of ​​intersection of six rays. In practice, for an area with a volume of several cubic centimeters, the delay time is approximately 0.5 s. This is more than enough for the kinetic energy of many atoms to become less or equal to half the level.

It is clear that when, during the deceleration process, the energy of atoms becomes less than $\Gamma/2$, absorption stops. Thus, the limiting temperature is determined by the width of the level: $T_{\text{np}} = \Gamma/2k$ (see (1)). In fact, the temperature reached $2,4 \times 10^{-4}$ K is almost no different from the limiting one.

The six-arm trap is called the “molasses trap.” In fact, it is inconceivable to hold atoms for a long time. They buy it while wandering and will definitely leave it, slowing down to energy $\Gamma/2$ or to a speed of about 0.5 m/s. That’s why we need an additional “real” trap.

It is necessary to create a small hole inside the “molasses trap” in which the slowing down atoms would roll down. But here, for a convincing elementary consideration, too extensive a digression would be required. Therefore, I will simply say that such a pit is a region with a volume of about $10^{-9}$ cm$^3$ near the focus of another laser beam with a photo energy that is noticeably less than the resonant energy. By moving the lens, you can move the focus and transport the atoms captured in its vicinity.

The energy depth of the pit is in temperature units only $5 \cdot 10^{-3}$ K. Therefore, the accumulation of residual gases with atoms at a temperature of 300 K literally embroiders a sodium atom from the pit near the focus. When the vacuum quality is achieved, the holding time is approximately 10 s.

The history of the creation of a light refrigeration unit covers the time from 1968, when a trap at the focus of a laser beam was always proposed, until 1986, when it was possible to trap 500 sodium atoms in it and hold it for several seconds.

Modern capabilities “give us amazing power over atoms,” as one of the creators of the installation, Steven Chu, put it. It seems that atoms and photons will again serve as inventions and create something no less beautiful.
