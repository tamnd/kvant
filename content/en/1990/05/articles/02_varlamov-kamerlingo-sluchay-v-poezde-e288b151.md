Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. VARLAMOV,
Dr. C. CAMERLINGO (Italy)

Recently the author of these lines had the opportunity to return by express train from Venice to Naples. The train was moving very fast (at a speed of about 150 km/h), outside the small window there were landscapes that seemed to have come out of the paintings of Renaissance masters. In full agreement with their pictures, the area was cold, and the food was either flying around the place or diving into another tunnel. In one of the particularly long tunnels near Florence, our ears suddenly became blocked, just as airplane passengers experienced when taking off or landing. Judging by external signs, the same fate befell our fellow passengers - everyone in the carriage was turning their heads, trying to get rid of the unpleasant feeling. When the meal finally escaped from the leaking tunnel, it went away by itself, and only one of us, who was not used to such misfortunes on the railway, was left with the question of the origin of this effect. Since it was clearly related to the pressure drop, we began to expect to discuss the possibility of a physical cause for the phenomenon. At first glance it seemed very striking that as we delved deeper into the task, this message seemed less and less certain. Soon we had an explanation ready, which we want to offer you.

Consider a train with cross-sectional area $S_n$ moving at a constant speed inside a long tunnel with cross-sectional area $S_0$. Let's immediately switch to the frame of reference associated with eating. We will assume that the air flow is established, and we will neglect the viscosity of the air. In this case, the movement of the tunnel walls relative to the food can be ignored - due to the lack of viscosity, it does not affect the air flow. We will also assume that the train is sufficiently long so that we can neglect the edge effects at the front and rear cars, and we will assume that the air pressure in the tunnel is steady and available near the skin of the entire train.

So, by abandoning minor details, we moved from the real movement of eating to the requested physical model, which we can already try to describe mathematically. So. There is a pipe (formerly a tunnel), in which a cylinder (formerly a tunnel) with streamlined ends is located. Air is forced through the pipe: far from the food (section $A-A$ to figure), the air pressure $p_0$ is equal to atmospheric pressure, and the speed of the air flow $v_n$ is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the speed with which the food moves to our reference system. Let's consider some section $B-B$ (just in case, away from the ends of the path, so that our assumptions are actually fulfilled). Let us denote the air pressure in this section by $p$, and the air flow speed by $v$. These quantities can be related to $v_n$ and $p_0$ using Bernoulli's equation:

$$p + \frac{\rho v^2}{2} = p_0 + \frac{\rho v_n^2}{2},$$

where $\rho$ is the air density. Let us immediately emphasize that we write the Bernoulli equation in the form of the so-called incompressible fluid approximation. This imposes additional restrictions that are worth discussing in more detail. Can the air density in the problem under consideration be considered tractable? The question is not easy,

We will present only suggestive physical messages. If the flow rate is not too high (what this means will become clear later) and we have neglected the viscosity of the air, then the change in air temperature can be neglected. Then the density will change to the same extent as the air pressure. We expect to get a small change in pressure, which will be determined by the overall density of the air. The latter can be found from the Mendleev-Clapeyron equation applied to air far from the air:

$$\rho = \frac{p_0 \mu}{RT}.$$ (2)

It is not difficult to see the limit where our assumption about the constancy of density $\rho$ depends is not fulfilled: if the speed of air flow in any of the sections of the pipe becomes comparable to the root-mean-square speed of thermal motion of molecules, then there is no longer any possibility of constancy of $\rho$ in different sections. Indeed, it is precisely this speed that determines the characteristic time for the establishment of the average gas density in the maximum volume, therefore, at constant flow rates, it, the density, simply will not have time to be established when moving from one section to another. However, as we will see below, this limitation may not be sufficient. For now, we will assume that the density $\rho$ is constant.

Equation (1) contains two unknown quantities, $p$ and $v$, so one more correspondence is necessary to determine $p$. It is given to us by the invariability of the mass of air flowing through any section per unit time (with flow in a pipe, the mass can neither appear nor disappear):

$$\rho v_n S_0 = \rho v \left( S_0 - S_n \right).$$ (3)

(This correspondence is often spoken of as the conditions of flow continuity.) Using (3) to get rid of the speed $v$ in (1) and substituting expression (2) for the density $\rho$, we find the air pressure in the tunnel:

$$p = p_0 \left( 1 - \frac{\mu v_n^2}{2RT} \left( \frac{S_0}{S_0 - S_n} \right)^2 - 1 \right).$$ (4)

The combination of parameters $\mu v_n^2 / RT$ included in this expression is very indo and dimensionless. Consequently, the quantities $\sqrt{RT / \mu}$ have the dimension of speed. In it, up to a coefficient, one can immediately see the root-mean-square speed of thermal motion of molecules. However, when analyzing the aerodynamic problem under consideration, another characteristic of the gas will be important for us - the speed of sound propagation in it, which the authors have long wanted to introduce. It, like the speed of thermal motion of molecules, is determined by the temperature and molecular mass of the gas, and the numerical value of the speed of sound $v_{3B}$ also depends on the so-called indicator
