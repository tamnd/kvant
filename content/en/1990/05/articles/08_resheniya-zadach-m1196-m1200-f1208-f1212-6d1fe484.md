Let us first prove the following lemma: a circle inscribed in angle $XBY$ and tangent to its sides at points $T$ and $T'$ will be external to triangle $XBY$ (Fig. 1) if and only if its perimeter $P_{XBY}$ is equal to $2BT$.

If the circle touches the line $XY$, then the equality $P_{XBY} = 2BT$ immediately follows from the theorem on tangents drawn from one point (see Fig. 1). Conversely, if $P_{XBY} = 2BT$, then the outer circle of the triangle $XBY$ touches the straight line $BX$ at a point located from $B$ at a distance $P_{XBY}/2$, i.e. at the point $T$, and therefore coincides with the given circle.

Let it now be known that $AM + AN = CM + CN$ (Fig. 2). Then the perimeters of triangles $ABN$ and $MBC$ are equal (their difference is $(AB - BM) + AN - CM + (BN - BC) = AM + AN - CM - CN = 0)$. Therefore, the outer circle $\omega$ of triangle $ABN$ will also touch the segment $MC$, and therefore (see Fig. 2),

$$2MT = 2BT - 2BM = P_{BMC} - 2BM = CB + CM - BM.$$

On the other hand, $\omega$ is an outside circle for the triangle $AMO$, therefore,

$$2MT = P_{AMO} = OM + MA + AO.$$

Equating the difference of the right-hand sides of the equalities to zero, we obtain:

$$CB + (CM - OM) - (BM + MA) - AO = CB + CO - AB - AO = 0,$$

Q.E.D.

It is similarly proved that the second equality of the problem implies the first and that both of them are equivalent to the third equality $BM + OM = BN + ON$. Note that for the existence of an “objective” out-of-shape circle of the quadrilateral $OMBN$ (Fig. 2), in addition to the well-known condition $OM + BN = ON + BM$, there are two more equivalent ones: $AM + MC = AN + NC$ and $AO + BC = CO + BA$; this fact was used in solving problem M1025 («Kvant», 1987, No. 5, p. 26).

M1198. Let's use a word to describe a line of 10 digits 0 and 1. We will consider two words to be synonyms if one can be obtained from several other operations of the following type: several consecutive digits are deleted from the word, the sum of which is even, and the same digits are written in their place, but in reverse order. $$\begin{align*}

Let us show that in our problem each word is reduced to the form

The number of words of the form (**) is equal to the number of pairs of integers $(a, b)$, where $a \geq 0, b \geq 0, a + b \leq 9$, i.e. $1 + 2 + ... + 9 = 45$ (Fig. 1).

And for a zero on the segment being turned over, this follows from the fact that the number of units of this segment of the word from the chosen zero has the same parity as to the right of it (Fig. 2). M1199. Prove that if the equation $ax^2 + (c-b)x + (e-d) = 0$ has a root greater than 1, then the equation $ax^4 + bx^3 + cx^2 + dx + e = 0$ has at least one root.

Let the root of the equation $ax^2 + (c-b)x + (e-d) = 0$ be equal to $x = t^2$, where $t > 0$; Then

$$at^4 + ct^2 + e = bt^2 + d.$$

Let's put $f(x) = ax^4 + bx^3 + cx^2 + dx + e$. From the previous equality it follows that the numbers

$$f(t) = (at^4 + ct^2 + e) + t(bt^2 + d),$$

$$f(-t) = (at^4 + ct^2 + e) - t(bt^2 + d)$$

have different signs (since $t > 1$, the sign determines the second term - large in modulus). Therefore, on the interval $[-t, t]$ the equation $f(x) = 0$ must have a root. (We use here the fact that a continuous function that takes values ​​of different signs at the ends of a segment must vanish at some point.)

D. Fomin

M1200*. For which k can be placed on the circle a) 10; b) 100; e) n duet so that each of them intersects exactly with the others?

The fact that this task is not as simple as it might seem at first glance is evidenced by the answer:
a) $k$ - any natural number less than 10, except 3 and 7;
b) $k$ is a number less than 100 for which $k+1$ is not divisible by 8 (i.e. $k \neq 7, 15, \dots, 95$);
b) $k$ is a number less than $n$ for which the numerator of the irreducible fraction equal to $\frac{k+1}{n}$ is odd.

We will solve the problem immediately in the general case c).

Let us give examples showing that any of the values ​​of $k$ indicated in the answer are possible. Let first $\frac{k+1}{n} = \frac{1}{d}$, i.e. $k=n/d-1$, where $d$ is some divisor of the number $n$ ($1 \leq d < n$). Let us place $d$ points on the circle (say, dividing it into equal parts) and cover each point with a group of $k+1$ small arcs so that the arcs of different groups do not intersect. Each of the $(k+1)d=n$ arcs intersects the $k$ others.

Let, in general, $\frac{k+1}{n} = \frac{2l+1}{d}$, i.e. $k+1=q(2l+1)$, $n=qd$, where $l, d, q$ are integers ($0 \leq 2l < d$). Let's divide the circle into $d$ equal parts and take arcs consisting of $l$ such parts, each of them in the number of $q$ copies. Each of the $qd=n$ selected arcs has common points (including ends) with $q-1+2lq=k$ others - with $q-1$ others of “its own group” and with all $q$ others, separated by 1, 2, ..., $l$ parts in one direction or the other. (We can, of course, enlarge each arc slightly - then they can all be distinct, and all pairwise intersections can be arcs rather than individual points. Figures a) and 6) show two examples for $n=10$, corresponding to $k=4$ and $k=5$.)

Let us now prove that if $n$ arcs are placed so that each intersects only $k$ others, then in the irreducible representation of the fraction $\frac{k+1}{n}$ the numerator is odd (in other words, $k+1$ is not divisible by $2^{r+1}$ unless $n=2^r m$, where $m$ is odd).

Note that if one of the arcs entirely contains an arc, it can be compressed to the size of this smaller one: it is clear that they intersect the same set of arcs. Let's mark the points where the arcs begin and end (we go around the circle, to determine, counterclockwise); if for some of these points there are no arcs starting (or no ending) at it, you can remove the segment adjacent to it - cut the arcs; in the end we get a reduced system of arcs.

Let $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_d$ be the points where the arcs begin and end, and let $q_i$ arcs begin at the point $M_i$, consisting of $l_i$ parts, i.e. ending at the point $M_{i+1}$ (hereinafter we assume that the distance index is “modulo $d$”, i.e. index $d+1$ replace with 1, $d+2$ with 2, ..., 0 with $d$, 1 with $d-1$, etc.). Obviously, $l_2 \geq l_1$ - otherwise the arc of the second group,

F1208. Many of you, probably, have noticed that the moment you step on wet sand, it brightens. This is due to the fact that the sand becomes dry. But as soon as you remove your foot, the trail left by your nose immediately fills with water. Explain this phenomenon.

To explain what happens to the sand on the river bank, let's start with... balls.

However, the new balls can be placed on a plane so that each of them touches six other balls. In the holes between the balls of the first layer, you can put balls of the second layer, each of which will touch three balls of the lower layer and six of its own layer, etc. The arrangement obtained in this way is called close packing of balls. If you break the tight packing, the gaps between the balls will increase, and the volume of the entire system will increase. This means that if forces act on a system of balls that lead to disruption of close packing, the volume of the system increases due to an increase in the spaces between the balls.

Any granular medium behaves similarly. Take a chenault (or coffee), for example, fill a glass with it, shake the glass slightly to spread the beans into the tightest possible pack, and then press down on the chennot. Pressure will lead to a violation of the tight packing, i.e., to an increase in the volume occupied by the grains, and some of the grains will spill out. If you now lightly move around the glass so that the grains are again “packed” more tightly, the glass turns out to be not filled to the top.

Let us denote by $\alpha$ the proportionality coefficient between the power dissipated by the resistor and the temperature difference between the resistor and the surrounding air. At temperature $t_3=80^\circ\text{C}$ the resistance of the resistor is $R_1=50$ Ohm (see Fig. 1). Then from the equality

$$\frac{U_1^2}{R_1} = \alpha (t_3 - t_0)$$

we get

$$\alpha = \frac{U_1^2}{R_1 (t_3 - t_0)} = 1, 2 \frac{B^2}{O\text{м} \cdot K}.$$

Spontaneous current fluctuations at a voltage across the resistor $U_2=80$ V will occur as a result of fluctuations in the resistance value of the resistor. When the temperature of the resistor becomes $t_1=100^\circ\text{C}$, its resistance jumps from $R_1=50$ Ohm to $R_2=100$ Ohm. As a result, the thermal power generated by the resistor decreases, and the resistor begins to cool (heat removal from the resistor is more intense than consumption). When the temperature of the resistor drops to $t_2=99^\circ\text{C}$, its resistance decreases abruptly from 100 Ohms to 50 Ohms. The thermal power released at the resistor increases, the resistor heats up again (poisoning of the heat conductor is faster than the removal). At temperature $t_1=100^\circ\text{C}$ is again proportional and the process repeats.

Period of current oscillations in the circuit

$$T = \tau_1 + \tau_2,$$

where $\tau_1$ is the heating time of the resistor from $t_2$ to $t_1$, $\tau_2$ is the cooling time from $t_1$ to $t_2$. Let us write the corresponding heat balance equations:

$$\frac{U_2^2}{R_1} \tau_1 = \tau_1 \alpha (t_1 - t_0) + C (t_1 - t_2),$$

$$\frac{U_2^2}{R_2} \tau_2 = \tau_2 \alpha (t_1 - t_0) - C (t_1 - t_2)$$

(since the temperature of the resistor changes very little, we can assume that the heat output is constant and equal to $\alpha (t_1 - t_0))$. Substituting the numerical data, we find

$$\tau_1 = \tau_2 \approx 0,1 \text{ с} \Rightarrow T \approx 0,2 \text{ с}.$$

The maximum current value is very high,

$$I_{\text{max}} = \frac{U_2}{R_1} = 1,6 \text{ A},$$

minimal value -

$$I_{\text{min}} = \frac{U_2}{R_2} = 0,8 \text{ A}.$$

F1211. In a sealed capillary tube there are two so many rods, separated by a drop of HgI₂ electrolyte solution. Inner diameter of the tube d=0.3 mm. The tube is connected in series with a resistor with a resistance of R=390 kOhm to a battery with EMF €=10 V. After what time will the droplet move one scale division (see figure)?

The flow of electric current in an electroplate is associated with mass transfer. In this case, the reduction of the metal rod from the electrolyte solution occurs at the catode, and the oxidation of the rod occurs at the anode, i.e., the transition into the electrolyte solution. In accordance with Faraday's law of electrolysis, the mass m of the rod released at the cathode during time t is equal to

$$m = \frac{1}{F} \frac{M}{n} I t,$$

(1)

where $F = 9,65 \cdot 10^4$ C/mol is the Faraday number, $M = 0,201$ kg/mol is the molar mass of the rod, $n = 2$ is the valency of the rod, $I$ is the flowing current. Since the resistance of the metal rod and electrolyte and the internal resistance of the source are negligible compared to R, the flowing current is equal to

$$I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}.$$

(2)

The release of the rod at the cathode and its dissolution at the anode lead to the mixing of electrolyte droplets towards the anode. The distance l over which the droplet will move is related to the mass released at the cathode of the rod by a very significant ratio

$$m = \rho \frac{\pi d^2}{4} l,$$

(3)

where $\rho = 13,6 \cdot 10^3$ kg/m³ is the density of the rod.

From equalities (1) - (3) the time required for a drop of electrolyte to move to dissolution l is found:

$$t = \frac{\pi d^2 F n \rho R l}{4 M \mathcal{E}}.$$

Substituting the values ​​given in the conditions and $l = 1$ cm, we obtain

$$t \approx 100 \text{ ч}.$$

Such “routine clocks” have found application in electronic technology as small-sized time-consuming devices and operating time counters. They can also be used as coulometers to measure the charge flowing through a section of a circuit over a long period of time.

F1212. The refractive index of the atmosphere of planet X decreases with height h above its surface according to the law n=n₀−αh. Radius of the planet R. Nauti, at what height h₀ above the surface of the planet is the optical channel through which light rays will go around the planet, remaining at a constant height.

In the atmosphere, where the refractive index n decreases with height, light does not travel rectilinearly. The rotation of the front of the light wave, and, consequently, the bending of light rays, occurs due to the fact that the speed of light in the medium v=c/n is lower, the higher the refractive index.

Let us denote by Δh the width of the optical channel along which light rays will go around the planet, remaining at a constant height (see figure). Let's distance the two extreme rays. A beam remaining at a constant height h₀ will go around the planet in time

$$t = \frac{2\pi(R+h_0)}{v_1} = 2\pi(R+h_0) \frac{n_0-\alpha h_0}{c}.$$

Another ray of the same light channel, separated from the first by a distance Δh << h₀, must go around the planet at a height of h₀+Δh in the same time (only in this case, the front of the light wave propagating along the channel will be perpendicular to the circle of radius R+h₀):

$$t = \frac{2\pi(R+h_0+\Delta h)}{v_2} = 2\pi(R+h_0+\Delta h) \frac{n_0-\alpha(h_0+\Delta h)}{c}.$$

Equating the propagation time and taking into account that Δh << h₀, we find

$$h_0 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{n_0}{\alpha} - R \right).$$

Note that the phenomenon considered is called circular refraction. As observations show, it is realistically possible, for example, in the atmosphere of Venus.
