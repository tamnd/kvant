About the electronic resistance of conductors
As you certainly know, the electronic resistance of a conductor depends on the materials from which it is made, the size and shape of the conductor. So, for example, for a homogeneous conductor of constant cross-section $S$ and length $l$ the resistance

$$R = \rho \frac{l}{S},$$

where $\rho$ is the specific electronic resistance. Sometimes it is more convenient to talk not about the resistance of a conductor, but about its inverse value - electronic conductivity.

The first physicist who tried to figure out the significance of the passage of direct electron current through conductors was a modest schoolteacher from Cologne, Georg Simon Ohm (1789-1854). Ohm published the results of his first experiments in 1826.

Of course, Ohm did not have modern high-precision electronic measuring instruments and reliable current sources at his disposal, so Ohm had to solve a number of complex practical problems along the way.

Thus, as current sources in his first experiments, Ohm used voltaic columns - an interlaced layer of two dissimilar metals (for example, silver and zinc), separated by paper soaked in a raster of salt. At the same time, he noticed that the current strength in the galvanic circuit noticeably kills over time. It is clear that in such conditions it was almost pointless to establish such quantitative patterns. When Ohm became acquainted with the works of Seebeck (Thomas Johann Seebeck (1770-1831) - German physicist), who discovered the thermoelectric effect in 1821, he began to use a thermal element in his first, which gives a very stable current. Having learned about the experiments of Oersted (Hans Kristin Oersted (1777-1851) - Danish physicist), who discovered the effect of electric current on a magnetic needle in 1820, Ohm decided to characterize the strength of the current by the angle of deflection of the magnetic needle located near the current-carrying conductor. To do this, the conductor was placed in the plane of the magnetic meridian (see Fig. 1), in the absence of current in the conductor, the magnetic needle 6 was located above it and was parallel to it, and the slightly solid wire 7, to which the arrow was suspended, was not deformed. When current flowed through the conductor, the magnetic needle left the plane of the magnetic meridian and twisted the suspension. Ohm turned head 8, where the upper end of the suspension was fixed, so that the arrow was again parallel to the current-carrying conductor, and measured the angle of rotation. This angle was taken as a characteristic of the magnetic action of electric current.

To study the conductivity of various metals, Ohm took wires of the same cross-section, but made of different materials, and connected them one by one into the circuit. As an eton, he chose copper wiring of a certain length, taking its conductivity as 1000 conventional units, and measured the angle of rotation of the head at which the magnetic needle becomes parallel to the current-carrying conductor. Then he included wiring from other metals in the circuit and shortened them until the angle of rotation of the head became the same as in the case of this wiring. Based on the length obtained, it was possible to judge the conductivity of the corresponding materials. Thus, Ohm found that the conductivity of gold is 574 conventional units, silver - 356, zinc - 333, etc.

Then Ohm examined wires made of the same metal, but of different thicknesses, and treated it in the same way as when determining the conductivity of various metals. He found that the resistance of wires made of the same materials is the same if the ratios of their lengths to the relative areas of their cross sections, that is, if the ratios $l/S$ for these wires are the same. As a result, it was found that the resistance $R$ is directly proportional to this ratio: $R \sim l/S$. The input of the proportional coefficient $q$ depends on the nature of the materials; you can write correspondence (1) for the wiring resistance. Let us now show how, based on this correspondence, you can obtain the formulas known to you for calculating the total resistance of a system of conductors connected in series or parallel.

made of any homogeneous material with a resistivity $\varrho$. Let's denote its length by $l$. Let us imagine this conductor consisting of several parts connected in series, for example three. Let their lengths be $l_1, l_2$ and $l_3$ (Fig. 2). It's obvious that
$$l = l_1 + l_2 + l_3.$$
Let's multiply both sides of this equality by the ratio $\varrho/S$:
$$\varrho \frac{l}{S} = \varrho \frac{l_1}{S} + \varrho \frac{l_2}{S} + \varrho \frac{l_3}{S}.$$
But $\varrho l/S = R$ is the resistance of the entire conductor, $\varrho l_1/S = R_1$, $\varrho l_2/S = R_2$ and $\varrho l_3/S = R_3$ are the resistances of its first, second and third parts, respectively. Thus,
$$R = R_1 + R_2 + R_3.$$ (2)
This is the required formula for calculating the total resistance when connecting conductors in series.
Now imagine the same conductor consisting of several, for example again three, parallel connected parts with cross sections $S_1, S_2$ and $S_3$ (Fig. 3). Similar to the previous case,
$$S = S_1 + S_2 + S_3,$$
or, after multiplying by the common factor $1/(\varrho l)$,
$$\frac{S}{\varrho l} = \frac{S_1}{\varrho l} + \frac{S_2}{\varrho l} + \frac{S_3}{\varrho l}.$$

In accordance with formula (1),
$$\frac{S}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R}, \frac{S_1}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R_1}, \frac{S_2}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R_2} \text{ и } \frac{S_3}{\varrho l} = \frac{1}{R_3},$$
we get it in a poseable way
$$\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}.$$ (3)

Using this formula, you can find the total resistance when connecting conductors in parallel.
Formulas (2) and (3) are derived here only for the particular case of a specific type of conductor - the same material, the same cross sections in the first case and the same lengths in the second. However, they are also applied to the most general cases.
