ABOUT NEWTON
ATTRACTION OF ELLIPSOIDS

V. SHAPIRO

From the editor.
This mathematical article uses concepts that are familiar to you from your physics course: force, potential, equivalent surface... Of course, all of them can be given an exact mathematical definition. But even without this, their supposed meaning is completely clear. Just in case, here are a few facts to help: the force (and potential) that several masses (or charges) create is equal to the sum of the forces (and potentials) created by each of them; if in some regions of space the potential is equal to a constant, then the force field is zero; an equivalent surface is a surface whose potential at all points is equal to a certain constant; the force vector at each point of such a surface is redistributed to it. Finally, the author freely uses (in the spirit of Newton!) the concepts of infinite small nodes, areas, volumes, etc.

Newton on the assumption of a homogeneous sphere

Isaac Newton published his main mathematical achievements in 1687 in “Mathematical Principles of Natural Physiology.” Throughout this treatise, he repeatedly returns to the theory of the acceptance of stationary and rotating bodies and the question of the shape of the earth's surface. Among other things, Newton gives a leganton proof of the following fact.

Theorem XXX from Section XIII “On the agreeable forces of spherical bodies”:

“If equal centripetal forces are directed to individual points of a spherical surface, decreasing in relation to the squares of the distances, then a particle placed inside this surface does not experience any pull from such forces in any direction.”

In other words, a homogeneous mathematical sphere does not accept internal points.

Newton argues this way. Let $P$ be an arbitrary point inside the sphere (Fig. 1). Let's take a small telephone with center $P$ and show that the forces from which two infinitesimal parts of the surface, carved on it by an angle, act on $P$ cancel each other out. Indeed, the force with which it acts on $P$ of each of the parts is proportional to the mass of the part, i.e., its area, and inversely proportional to the square of the distance to the point $P$. The bases of an infinite narrow end with a vertex $P$ can be considered flat. As can be seen from figure 1, which depicts a section of a sphere by a plane passing through the center $O$ and the axis of the ends, this axis makes equal angles to the base. Consequently, the ends are similar, and the ratio of the areas of their bases is equal to the ratio of the squares of the lengths of their axes. Therefore, the forces of acceptance of the two parts in question are mutually destroyed. Newton concludes:

“From this reasoning it follows that the acceptance of the entire spherical surface, as a state of opposite elements, is destroyed; therefore, $P$ this acceptance of the movement does not work out in any direction.”

Let us make two remarks about this respectful argument. Firstly, it is also suitable for a homogeneous spherical layer, which can be imagined as consisting of an infinite number of sphere materials. Secondly, mass can be replaced by electric charge, since the Coulomb force is also inversely proportional to the square of the distance. This means that a uniform charging sphere does not create an internal electric field.

Attraction field of a homogeneous sphere in the external region

To find out what the gravitational field of the inner sphere would be, Newton became interested in the question of what gravitational force the sphere creates in the outer region of space. Due to the symmetry of the problem, this force will depend only on the distance to the center of the sphere (and, of course, on its mass). But how exactly?

Let's forget for a moment that we are studying the forces of attraction and talk about the flow of liquid. Let us imagine that at the origin of coordinates there is a source from which there is an incompressible fluid along the radii of spherical symmetrical distances. Due to incompressibility, the same amount of liquid flows through each sphere centered at the source per unit time. This amount is proportional to the area of ​​the sphere, i.e., the square of its radius, and proportional to the speed of the fluid. This means that the speed of the current is inversely proportional to the square of the distance to the source.

We made an important observation: the velocity field of a spherical homogeneous incompressible fluid is the same as the force field of an exact mass (or electric charge). This means that the force field of an exact mass has the property of incompressibility: the magnitude of its flow through the boundaries of any region that does not contain mass is equal to zero - as much as it flows in, it goes up. The same is true for any distribution of masses (and charges), or the force that several masses create is equal to the sum of the forces created by each of them. We are now in a position to prove the following theorem:

the force of attraction of a homogeneous sphere of mass M coincides with the force created by the exact mass M placed at the center of the sphere.

Indeed, as we have already established, the force field of a sphere is spherical, symmetrical and incompressible. But we know that the only radial velocity field of an incompressible fluid is inversely proportional to the square of the distance to the center, i.e., it coincides with the field of the exact mass. That this mass coincides with the mass of the sphere follows from a comparison of the fluxes through the surfaces enclosing a given sphere.

This completes the study of the attraction of a homogeneous sphere. If we tried to calculate the forces directly, that is, by interpreting the contributions of incompressibly small pieces of the surface, we would certainly encounter great difficulties. Now our task is to generalize the results obtained to more complex surfaces.

On the free distribution of the sphere

The free distribution of a sphere on a closed conducting surface is the distribution that is obtained if the charge is given the opportunity to distribute independently in the absence of external forces. For example, on a sphere such a distribution will be uniform. Free distribution has two important properties.

Every surface is equivalent to the free distribution of a sphere on it.

This is very useful: the potential difference on the surface would lead to a redistribution of the sphere on it.

The force field of the free distribution of a sphere inside a closed surface is equal to zero (and the potential is equal to constant).

Indeed, the path potential, equal to a certain constant on the surface, is different from this constant in the internal region. Then at some point $P$ it reaches a minimum or maximum value. The distance is small equivalent to the surface surrounding this point. The lines of force pierce it and converge at point $P$, which contradicts the incompressibility of the flow. So, the potential is equal to a constant, and the force field is zero.

As you can see, Newton's theorem that the free distribution of charges on a sphere does not create an internal force field is generalized to arbitrary surfaces. Now we will deal with the explicit form of the free distribution for the surface, next in complexity to the sphere - for ellipses.

About homeods

An ellipse is a surface obtained from a sphere by stretching or sloping along three perpendicular axes with tracks, generally speaking, of different coefficients. What is obtained when describing the extension from a spherical layer is called a homeod (Fig. 2). The surfaces of the ellipses limiting the homeod are prepared, and the center of the preparation coincides with their common center.

Let's try to calculate the attraction of an infinite thin homeod at some internal point $P$. You probably agree that this attraction turns out to be zero; and to prove this, one must verify that the attractive forces of the two infinite small primids shaded at figure 2 cancel each other out. These forces are proportional to the masses (i.e., volumes) of the primids and inversely proportional to the squares of the extensions up to the point $P$. The volume of a shaded infinite small solid angle can be calculated as the product of the area $S$ of the perpendicular section of the infinite small solid angle and the length $h$ of the segment of the axis of this angle contained in the homeode. The area of ​​$S$ is proportional to the square of the distance $r$, so the force of attraction of the right prim on figure 2 is proportional to $r^2 h/r^2 = h$. Likewise, the force of attraction of the left primid is proportional to $H$. It remains to note that $h = H$ - after all, the corresponding segments lie on the same line and the lengths of their inverse images in the spherical layer are the same (Fig. 3). So, we have proven the following Newton's theorem:

the force of attraction of a homogeneous infinite thin homeod at any of its internal points is zero.

(From here, as in the case of a sphere, it follows that the attraction of a real “thick” homeod is also equal to zero.)

As we already know, a free distribution of charges is a distribution that creates a zero field at any internal point. On the other hand, since the bounding spaces of homeods are prepared by ellipses,

the density of free charge distribution at each point is proportional to the length of the radius vector drawn from the center to this point. Combining these two remarks, we arrive at a description of the free distribution of charges on the ellipsis:

Arnold's theorem

In order to generalize the homeod theorem, we will have to tinker a little with the formulas. Recall that the ellipsid is obtained by stretching the sphere along three coordinate axes, which is proportional to the square of the distance $r$, therefore the force of attraction of the left primid is proportional to $H$. It remains to note that $h = H$ - after all, the corresponding segments lie on the same line and the lengths of their inverse images in the spherical layer are the same (Fig. 3). We are going to prove that such a charged surface does not accept its internal points. But which points are called internal?

Recall that $f(t)$ is a polynomial of $n$ degree, having exactly $n$ roots. Such a polynomial (up to the constructed polynomial) can be written in the form

$$f(t)=(t-a_1)(t-a_2)...(t-a_n),$$

where $a_1, \dots, a_n$ are its roots. Differentiating this equality, we get:

$$f'(t)=(t-a_2)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)+$$

$$+(t-a_1)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)+\dots+$$

In part,

$$f'(a_i)=(a_i-a_1)(a_i-a_2)...$$

$$...(a_i-a_{i-1})(a_i-a_{i+1})...(a_i-a_n).$$

All terms, except $i$, contain the set $(a_i-a_i)$ and form zero.

We see that equality (1) is equivalent to the identity:

$$\frac{1}{(a_1-a_2)(a_1-a_3)...(a_1-a_n)}+\frac{1}{(a_2-a_1)(a_2-a_3)...(a_2-a_n)}+\dots+\frac{1}{(a_n-a_1)(a_n-a_2)...(a_n-a_{n-1})}=0. \quad (2)$$

It's hard to believe, but this identity holds for any pairwise distinct numbers $a_1, \dots, a_n$. And it is truly proven by the Olympics!

Consider a polynomial of $(n-1)$ degree:

$$g(t)=\frac{(t-a_2)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)}{(a_1-a_2)(a_1-a_3)...(a_1-a_n)}+\frac{(t-a_1)(t-a_3)...(t-a_n)}{(a_2-a_1)(a_2-a_3)...(a_2-a_n)}+\dots+\frac{(t-a_1)(t-a_2)...(t-a_{n-1})}{(a_n-a_1)(a_n-a_2)...(a_n-a_{n-1})}.$$

At $t=a_i$, all terms except the $i$th become zero, and the $i$th become 1. Therefore

$$g(a_1)=g(a_2)=\dots=g(a_n)=1.$$

This means that a polynomial of $(n-1)$ degree $g(t)-1$ has $n$ roots, which is possible only if $g(t)-1=0$ for all $t$. In part, the leading term of the polynomial $g(t)$ is equal to zero. But this is the right part of identity (2)! Thus, this identity, and with it Arnold’s theorem, is proven.

Field of a homogeneous homeon in the external region

Let's return to the homogeneous homeon. We already know that it creates a null field in its internal region. What will the field be like in the outer region of space? The answer to this question was found in 1809 by the Scottish mechanic and mathematician James Ivory. He managed to describe the equivalent surfaces of the free distribution of charges on an ellipiside - it turned out that these were also ellipisides (in the case of a sphere, concentric spheres are, of course, equivalent).

Consider the ellipiside $M$ given by the equation

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1.$$ (3)

A confocal ellipiside is any ellipiside $M$ given by the equation

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1,$$ (4)

where $t$ is some number $(t > -a^2, t > -b^2, t > -c^2)$. Each $t$ corresponds to its own ellipiside, and for $t \neq s$ the ellipisides $M_t$ and $M_s$ do not intersect, and the entire family $\{M_t\}$ fills the regions of space.

The name “conofocal ellipisides” is suggested by the analogy with a plane. The episide $M_d$ on the plane can be defined as a set of points, the sum of the distances from which to two given points - foci - is equal to some constant $d$.

In other words, if you fix the ends of length $d$ at the foci, place a pencil in the thread and pull it, then the pencil will draw an ellipiside $M_d$. For different $t$ we get different ellipisides with fixed foci. It is not difficult to verify (do it!) that in fitting coordinates the family of confocal ellipses is given by the equation

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1.$$ (5)
