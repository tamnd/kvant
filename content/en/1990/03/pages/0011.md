AT THE BOTTOM, when the overload is maximum, we get up and again, with a sinking heart, go up, a little higher than last time. The center of gravity of the system describes a figure-of-eight spiral (Fig. 11) when swinging.

Rocking of the oscillatory system due to changes in its parameters (on a swing, this is the distance from the suspension point to the center of gravity) is called parametric variation. Getting up at the bottom point, we do positive work against the sum of gravity and the centrifugal force of inertia (we are considering movement in a non-inertial reporting system). And at the top point the centrifugal force is zero, and only the projection $mg \cos \alpha$ remains from the force of gravity. Therefore, the negative work when squatting (with the same amplitude) is less according to the model. The total work done per cycle is positive, and the energy of the system increases.

Now let’s try to estimate the energy balance of a skier, as we just did for a swing.

A scorpion lies in wait for us here. At first glance, everything is very similar. When moving in an arc, the skater is also affected by the force of gravity and the centrifugal force of inertia (Fig. 12). The angle between them changes so that their equal force is minimum at the beginning of the arc ($F_{p1}$) and reaches a maximum at the end ($F_{p2}$). By squatting, the liar performs negative work, while standing up, he performs positive work. However, when he extends at the beginning of the arc, less force is applied to him than when he bends at the end. This means that the total work done during the bending-extension cycle (depreciation-push) will be negative. Paradox?! It would seem natural to do positive work and increase energy.

There is no mistake here. It’s just that until now we didn’t particularly care about the energy balance and thought only about how to reduce losses and gain speed. In this theoretical race for seconds, it was not easy to go too far. It's time to put everything in its place. Let's write down the law of conservation of energy on any section of the route:

$$\Delta E = mg \Delta h + \frac{\Delta (mv^2)}{2} = A_{tp} + A_{b} + A_{лыжн}.$$

On the left side is the change in the potential ($mg \Delta h$) and kinetic ($\Delta (mv^2)/2$) energy of the flyer, on the right is the work done by the flyer itself, the forces of friction and air resistance. What are the correspondences between these terms?

Let's start with the left side of the equation. The average speed of a slalomist on the track does not change much. Therefore, the second term is essential, i.e. $\Delta (mv^2)/2) \approx 0$. But the first one, on the contrary, is great. To gain the total speed for a slalomist $v \sim 10$ m/s, it is enough to lose only $\Delta h = v^2/2g = 5$ m of height. (In large mountains the differences are usually hundreds of meters.)
