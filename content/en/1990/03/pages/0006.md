Fig. 3. Options for choosing a route on the slaom course. The red curve is the optimal trajectory.

Fig. 4. Beads on wires. The time of sliding along the soul AB is less than along the chord that binds it.

that wiring? First of all, because he himself chooses the route of movement in the corridor specified by flags (for sledders - by a chute). Scientifically speaking, the slope of a mountain is a two-dimensional space where a sports wall (hereinafter if we consider it a material point) will have to find the optimal trajectory, while the movement of a bead is purely one-dimensional.*)

The type of optimal trajectory is determined by a combination of a number of factors. First of all, it is advisable to take the shortest route, deviating as little as possible from the slope line (Fig. 3). At the same time, we will benefit not only due to the reduction in distance, but also due to the increase in the average steepness of the route - the rounder the slope, the greater the rolling force and the less frictional force. In posture slalomists try to walk as close to the flags as possible, hitting them with their shoulders and body.

You can estimate the loss of time due to the removal of the path. Let the deviation from the optimal curve be only ±10 cm. Then the removal of each of the arcs forming it will be of order. On a slam track with 50 gates at an average speed of 10 m/s, the loss will be quite noticeable:

$$\Delta t \approx (50 \cdot 0,1 \text{ м}) / (10 \text{ м/c}) = 0,5 \text{ с.}$$

At the same time, in downhill or giant slalom, where the gate is smaller and the average speed is higher, this factor has an insignificant effect.

However, strange as it may seem, “going to the flag,” that is, straightening sections of the path between the flags, was also impossible. Firstly, you will have to slow down in order to fit into a more circular turn, and secondly, the time it takes to move in a straight line is not necessarily the shortest.

Let's look at a simple example. The bead rolls without initial speed from point $A$ to point $B$, first along an arc of a circle, and then along the chord contracting it (Fig. 4). If the angular size of the arc is small, then the time of movement in the first case is equal to a quarter of the oscillation period of a mathematical high-speed magnet with length $R$ (friction is neglected):

$$T_1 = \frac{\pi}{2} \sqrt{R/g}.$$
