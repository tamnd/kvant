The proof of the superposition principle is indeed quite complex. However, it can be bypassed. How to do this is shown in the following note - “Field lines and Gauss’s theorem” using the example of an electric field. Try reading this note, each time replacing the word “charge” with the word “mass”, and the words “electric field” with the words “gravitational field”. If you find it difficult to understand the details, it is recommended to understand at least the general meaning.

Let us now find out what the gravitational field is inside a thin sphere, i.e. at $r < R$. It turns out that inside the sphere the field direction is zero. To prove this, let us take an arbitrary point, for example, the point $A$ on figure 1, ​​and show that those included in $g_A$ from two opposite sections of the surface of the sphere, cut off by a narrow cone, cancel each other out. Indeed, from the similarity it follows that the linear dimensions of the selected areas are related as $r_1/r_2$, therefore, the ratio of their areas $S_1/S_2$, equal to the ratio of masses $M_1/M_2$, is $r_1^2/r_2^2$. Then from formula (3) we obtain that the directions created by these sections at point $A$ are equal in magnitude. And since their directions are opposite, they add up to the field.

Interesting result, isn't it? If inside the Earth, say, there existed a concentric empty cavity (as, for example, in the case described in the science fiction novel by V. A. Obruchev “Plutonia. An extraordinary journey into the bowels of the Earth”), then in this cavity there would be complete insomnia. Push off a little from the surface and fly evenly and straight to the opposite wall. So traveling on such Plutonia is extremely difficult.

Now we can answer the very first question - what is the gravitational field in a shaft dug along the diameter of the Earth. For simplicity, we will consider the Earth to be a homogeneous sphere with radius $R$. Consider a point at a distance $r < R$ from the center of the Earth. Having mentally drawn a sphere with radius $r$, we will divide the Earth into two parts - external and internal. As we have already found out, the field created at the rasterized point by the entire outer spherical layers is equal to zero. Therefore, the Earth's field at a given point coincides with the field created by the internal part, i.e., a ball with radius $r$. To use formula (3), we need to find the mass $M_r$ of this ball. It is obvious that the ratio of the mass $M_r$ to the mass $M_3$ of the entire Earth is equal to the ratio of their volumes, i.e. the ratio of cubes of radii: $M_r/M_3 = r^3/R^3$. Hence,

$$g_r = G \frac{M_r}{r^2} = G \frac{M_3}{r^2} \frac{r^3}{R^3} = g_0 \frac{r}{R},$$

where $g_0$ is the acceleration of free fall on the Earth's surface.

So, we know $g_r$ at any distance from the center of the Earth, both at $r > R$ and at $r < R$. This dependence is depicted graphically in figure 2.
