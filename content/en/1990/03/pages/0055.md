Mathematically, we construct an expression for the property of continuity of merging lines of the electric field is the Gauss theorem. In order to formulate and prove it, we must first move from the qualitative language of plum lines to precise quantitative representations. Let's start by slightly paraphrasing the property of continuity of lines.

Consider an arbitrary closed surface. If there are no charges inside the surface, then the number of lines leaving it is exactly equal to the number of lines entering. It is convenient to take into account incoming lines along with outgoing ones, but assign them a minus sign. Then we can say that the total number of plum lines emerging from the “empty” surface is zero. If there is some charge inside the surface, then, obviously, the total number of lines emerging from the surface will be proportional to the magnitude of this charge. This is the qualitative formulation of Gauss's theorem. But let's move on.

Let us introduce the scalar quantity $\Phi$ - it is called the flow of the direction vector through some small horse:

$$\Phi = ES \cos \alpha.$$  (1)

Now it is clear that Gauss’s theorem can be formulated as follows: the flow of the electric field direction vector through any closed surface is proportional to the total charge contained inside this surface. To prove this theorem, and in turn calculate the coefficient of proportionality, let us first consider a simple but very important property of the quantity $\Phi$.

Let us write formula (1) in the form $\Phi = (E \cos \alpha)S = E_nS$, where $E_n$ is the projection of the vector $\vec{E}$ onto the direction of the normal $n$. If the field is created by several charges, then according to the principle of superposition $\vec{E} = \vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2 + \ldots + \vec{E}_k$. But the projection of the sum of vectors is equal to the sum of the projections: $E_n = E_{1n} + E_{2n} + \ldots + E_{kn}$. From here we obtain that the total flow of the directional vector is equal to the sum of the flows created by individual charges: $\Phi = \Phi_1 + \Phi_2 + \ldots + \Phi_k$. Therefore, we can talk about the contribution to the total flux from each individual charge.
