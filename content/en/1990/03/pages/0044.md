Puc. 4.

Fig. 5.

Electrical charges can be induced, but the conductor as a whole remains electrically neutral (Fig. 3). Is it possible to really charge it so that the total charge is not equal to zero? It turns out that it is possible, and even quite simple. To do this, it is enough to replace the conductor in the presence of a charged body for some time, and then remove both the replacement and the charged body (Fig. 4). In this case, the sign of the charge of the conductor will be opposite to the sign of the charge of the body. Indeed, during substitution, free electrons, under the influence of a positive charge on the body, come from the ground and remain on the conductor, charging it as a whole negatively.

The magnitude of the induced charge can be significantly increased, bringing it practically closer to the magnitude of the induced charge, if the charged body is placed inside a long metal cylinder (or ball) with a slight deflection. In this case, the influence on other bodies is almost excluded, therefore, a charge will be induced on the inner surface of the cylinder (or ball), practically equal in magnitude to the charge of the ball, but opposite in sign, and on the outer surface - the same in magnitude and sign. If charged -

touch the inner wall of the cylinder with a body, charges of opposite sign are neutralized, and charges of the same sign will remain on the cylinder as were on the charged body (Fig. 5). In this way, it is possible to practically completely transfer charge from one body to another.

Now let's try to answer the second question posed at the very beginning. Let us have: a charged ball $A$, an uncharged ball $B$, a hollow metal ball and an electric scoop on which we want to accumulate a charge greater than the charge of the ball $A$.

First of all, let’s charge the ball $B$ according to the indication (naturally, with a charge of the opposite sign). Let's place ball $B$ inside the replaced ball for a while, and then remove both the replacement and ball $B$. A charge will remain on the ball, equal in magnitude to the charge of the ball $B$, but opposite in sign, i.e., coinciding with the sign of the charge of the ball $A$. Now let's transfer this charge to the electroscope. If you perform such an operation many times, then on the electric scoop, it will indeed be possible to obtain a charge of the same sign as the original one, but significantly larger in magnitude.

Finally, try answering two more questions.

1. An uncharged conducting ball $C$ was connected to the left side of the ball $B$ using a long flexible conductor (see Fig. 2). As a result, positive charges appeared on the ball. Why?

2. The electric jack has a slight positive charge. If a highly electrified stick carrying a large negative charge is brought close to a ball of electric juice, the leaves of the electric juice will first fall off and then disperse again. How can this be explained?
