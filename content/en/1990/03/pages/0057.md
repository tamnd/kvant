Another example: let’s find the directionality of the field of an infinite charged plane with the surface of a ball $\sigma$ (Fig. 5). From the symmetry it is clear that the vector $\vec{E}$ is everywhere redefined by the plane. Let us choose a closed surface in the form of a cylinder located symmetrically relative to the plane. The flux of the directionality vector through the side surface of the cylinder is equal to zero, and through each base with area $S$ it is equal to $ES$, i.e.
$$\Phi = 2ES.$$
But according to Gauss's theorem
$$\Phi = \frac{\sigma S}{\varepsilon_0}.$$
Equating the rules of the part of both equalities, we obtain
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}.$$
Finally, one last example. It concerns one very important property of conductors. Let us understand that the statistical charges of a conductor are always

are located on its surface. The proof is very simple. Since the direction of the field inside the conductor is zero (otherwise the movement of free charges would be possible), then the flow of the directional vector through any closed surface drawn inside the conductor is zero. And this means that the charge inside any however small surface in the thickness of the conductor is also zero. Consequently, all the charges of the conductor are actually located on its surface.

And now - important. The proof of the electron density of the volume of a conductor is based on Gauss’s theorem, which, like the property of continuity of field lines, is true only if $1/r^2$ is in Coulomb’s law. Conclusion: the validity of Coulomb's law can be verified experimentally. To do this, it is enough to verify the electron density of the conductor thickness.

You see how many interesting things can be told by just one theorem - Gauss's theorem.
