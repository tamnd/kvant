M1187. We will say that two quadrangles - paper and cardboard - fit together if the cardboard one can be placed on the paper one so that all its vertices fall on the sides of the paper one (one on each) and at the same time,

Let KLMN be a cardboard quadrilateral whose vertices K, L, M, N lie respectively on sides AB, BC, CD, DA of a convex paper quadrilateral ABCD.

Obviously, angles AKN and LKB of two paper triangles with vertex K add up to angle NKL if and only if $\angle NKL=90^\circ$. Thus, if the quadrangles fit together, then the KLMN cardboard quadrangle is necessarily a polygon.

M1188. a) Given 101 rectangles with integer sides not exceeding 100. Prove that among them there are three rectangles $A, B, C$ that can be placed inside each other: $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$.

b) Prove that among 1989 rectangles with integer sides not exceeding 100, there are 40 rectangles such that the first can be placed in the second, the second in the third, ..., the 39th in the 40th.

For each rectangle with sides $a$ and $b$ ($a \leq b$) we assign a point ($a; b$) on the coordinate plane. All possible points ($x; y$), where $x$ and $y$ are natural, $y \leq x \leq 100$ (see figure), can be included in 50 chains - “corners”, shown in figure in red - so that the points in each chain will correspond to rectangles that can be included in each other *).

Now both tasks can be solved without difficulty:

a) rectangle $2 \cdot 50 < 101$, at least 3 points out of 101 corresponding to rectangles lie in the same chain;

b) rectangle $39 \cdot 50 = 1950 < 1989$, at least 40 points out of 1989 corresponding to rectangles lie in the same chain.

N. Sedrakyan

*) Note. Among all rectangles ($x, y$), $y \leq x \leq 100$, one can specify a maximum of 50 pairwise incomparable ones: (100; 1), (99; 2), ..., (51; 49), (50; 50). There is a general Dilworth theorem: any finite set, where a particular order between some elements is indicated, can be covered with $k$ ordered chains, where $k$ is the maximum of pairwise incomparable elements (see the book “Correspondence Mathematical Optimization.” M.: Nauka, 1987, p. 111).

M1189. There are n straight lines on the plane, no three of which pass through the same point and no two of which are parallel. Prove that in each of the parts into which these lines divide the plane, you can put an integer different from 0 and not exceeding modulo n, so that on any side of any of these lines the sum of the numbers is equal to 0.

Let us give the shortest solution to the problem. It is based on the following fact: each of the parts into which the data straight lines divide the plane can be painted with black or white paint so that the areas that have a common section of the face have a different color. (This coloring can be obtained “by induction”: when adding a line in one of its bounded half-planes, all colors are replaced by opposite ones.)

Each intersection point of a straight line serves as the vertex of four angles - two white and two black. Let's write the numbers +1 (in white) and -1 (in black) in them. Obviously, the sum of these numbers in each half-plane bounded by any of their given lines is equal to 0.

Now, for each area, we sum up the numbers that are written in the corners - these will be the required integers, non-zero: since each convex area has no more than n sides, then it has no more than n corners.

Thus, we have proven the following theorem: if in each region into which n straight lines divide a plane, an integer number is written equal in absolute value to the number of its angles, and the signs of the numbers in neighboring (along a segment or ray) regions are different, then on each side of any of the n straight lines the sum of these numbers is equal to 0.

N. Konstantinov

M1190*. a) Prove that if there are 3n stars in a table of 2n×2n cells, then you can cross out n rows and n columns so that all the stars are crossed out.
b) Prove that in a table of 2n×2n cells you can arrange 3n+1 stars so that after crossing out any n rows and n columns, at least one star remains.

a) Cross out the n lines with the most asterisks. There cannot be less than 2n asterisks in them - this would mean that in one of these n lines there is no more than one asterisk, then in the remaining n lines there is no more than one asterisk, so there are less than 2n+n=3n asterisks in total. So, at least 2n stars have been crossed out; The remaining (no more than n) asterisks can be removed by crossing out the corresponding columns.
b) Place the stars as shown in figure: 2n - diagonally, another n - at the intersection of the i-th row with the (i+1)-th column (i=1, 2, ..., n) and the last one - at the intersection of the (n+1)-th row with the 1st column. Let's assume that we managed to remove all these asterisks by crossing out n rows and n columns. Let k≤n-1 of them rows (and thus n-1-k columns) come to the bottom (n-1) stars, i.e., to the last n-1 rows and columns. Then among the first n+1 lines n-k are crossed out. The remaining lines are divided into several zones: from p1,..., pr lines (r≥1), p1+...+pr=(n+1)-(n-k)=k+1. (We assume here that the first line follows the (n+1)th line.) In these zones there will be (p1+1)+(p2+1)+...+(pr+1)=k+r+1>k+1 stars, standing in different columns. There are n-(n-1-k)=k+1 columns left. Thus, by crossing out columns, all the asterisks cannot be removed.

F1198. A pipe with radius $R$ is filled with sand to a height $H$ ($H > 200R$). Sand density $ρ$. Find the minimum force of sand pressure on the bottom of the pipe. It is known that this pitch on a horizontal surface forms a hill with a limiting angle at the base of $\gamma_0$ (Fig. 1), and this angle is small ($\gamma_0 \sim 0,1$ rad). The coefficient of static friction between sand and pipe material is rsgen $\mu$.

Let us first find the coefficient of sand-sand friction $\mu'$. Let us consider sand on the slope of a hill with a base angle $\gamma$ (Fig. 2). Three forces act on it: gravity $mg$, normal slope reaction force $N$ and static friction force $F_{tp}$. From the condition that the vector sum of these forces is equal to zero (equilibrium condition), we easily find

$$N = mg \cos \gamma, F_{tp} = mg \sin \gamma = N \text{tg} \gamma.$$
On the other hand, $F_{tp}_{\max} = \mu' N$. Hence,

$$\mu' = \left( F_{tp} / N \right)_{\max} = \text{tg} \gamma_0 \approx \gamma_0,$$

where $\gamma_0 \ll 1$ is the limit (maximum) angle at the base.

F1199. It is known from experience that the speed of waves on the ocean surface, the length of which is $\lambda = 10$ m, is equal to $v = 4$ m/s. Suppose that in the ocean at great depth there is an interface, above which there is less salty water, and below which there is more salty water, so that the difference in the density of water is $\Delta \rho = 1$ kg/m$^3$. There may be waves along this boundary (so-called internal waves). Find the speed of such waves with a length of $\lambda = 10$ m. Consider the amplitude of the wave to be small.

The movement of fluid in both surface and internal waves is caused by boundary forces. These forces are naturally proportional to the density at the interface, i.e. $\Delta \rho$ for internal waves and $\rho$ for surface waves ($\rho = 10^3$ kg/m$^3$ is the density of water in the upper layers of the ocean). In addition, in the case of internal waves, twice the mass of water is set in motion, since water is moving on both sides of the interface. Then, for the acceleration of identically located parts of the liquid in both waves, we obtain

$$\frac{a_{\text{пов}}}{a_{\text{вн}}} = \frac{F_{\text{пов}}/m_{\text{пов}}}{F_{\text{вн}}/m_{\text{вн}}} = \frac{2\rho}{\Delta \rho} \approx 2000.$$

On the other hand, if the motion in a surface wave occurs, for example, $k$ times faster, then all the velocities in it will be $k$ times greater, and any change in motion will take $k$ times less time. This means that the acceleration, which is the ratio of the change in speed to the time of that change, will be $k^2$ times greater. From here

$$k = \sqrt{\frac{a_{\text{пов}}}{a_{\text{вн}}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{\Delta \rho}}.$$

F1200. There are two identical glasses on the table, one of which is filled with hot tea. Tea temperature $t_0$. The tea needs to be cooled to a temperature of $t_k$. This can be done in two ways:

1) immediately pour the tea into a second glass and wait until it cools to a temperature of $t_k$;
2) cool until the tea cools to a certain temperature $t_1$ such that after pouring into the second glass the temperature immediately turns out to be $t_k$.

Which way is the fastest? It is known that the heat transfer of a glass is proportional to the temperature difference between the glass and the environment; heat transfer between the tea and the glass occurs very quickly. The heat capacity of a glass is $C_0$, the heat capacity of tea is $C$.

Let us denote the temperature difference between tea and the environment by $T$:

$$T = t_{\text{чай}} - t_{\text{окр.ср.}}$$

and for brevity we will call $T$ temperature. Then the equation for the change in temperature $T$ of tea with time $\tau$, according to the conditions of the problem, can be written in the form

$$dT/d\tau = -\alpha T,$$

(*)

where $\alpha > 0$ is a constant proportionality coefficient. It also follows from the conditions of the problem that if before pouring tea from one glass to another the temperature was $T$, then after pouring it will become $CT/(C+C_0) = \beta T$, where $\beta$ is a constant coefficient.

Now consider processes 1) and 2). For the first process, we write the initial condition:

$$T^{(1)}(0) = \beta T_0$$ (immediately after transfusion)

and the equation for temperature change over time:

$$dT^{(1)}/d\tau = -\alpha T^{(1)}$$.

For the second process, we introduce a new variable $T' = \beta T^{(2)}$ and write the corresponding equalities:

$$T'(0) = \beta T_0,$$

$$dT'/d\tau = \beta dT^{(2)}/d\tau = \beta (-\alpha T^{(2)}) = -\alpha T'$$.

The same equation and the same initial conditions correspond to the same solutions. Therefore, at an arbitrary moment of time $T$

$$T'(\tau) = T^{(1)}(\tau).$$

Let at some moment of time $T_k$ the tea temperature in the first process reach $T_k$, i.e.

$$T^{(1)}(\tau_k) = T_k.$$

Let's find what the temperature will be in the second process if at time $T_k$ we pour tea from one glass to another:

$$T^{(2)}(\tau_k)_{\text{после}} = \beta T^{(2)}(\tau_k)_{\text{до}} = \beta T'(\tau_k)/\beta = T'(\tau_k) = T^{(1)}(\tau_k) = T_k.$$

So, we have found that the general methods of cooling tea require the same time. Therefore, you can pour tea from one glass to another at any time in the range from 0 to $T_k$ (see figure).

F1202. During an electrical discharge in rarefied neon (Ne) at room temperature, a very small part of neon atoms decays into electrons and consons (the mass M atoms of neo is 4×10⁴ times greater than the mass m of the electron). The length of the free gap of electrons (i.e., the average distance that an electron travels without collisions) l=0.1 mm. The gas is in an electric field with intensity E=10 V/cm. Estimate the “temperature” of electrons corresponding to their average kinetic energy. Boltzmann constant k=1.38×10⁻²³ J/K, electron charge e=1.6·10⁻¹⁹ Cl.

Since, according to the conditions of the problem, only a small part of neon atoms is incinerated, we can assume that the vast majority of new electrons with atoms are elastic. From the laws of conservation of energy and momentum it is easy to obtain that during any elastic collision with a neon atom at rest, the electron loses energy

$$\Delta W_1 = \frac{4m}{M} \frac{mv^2}{2},$$

where v is the speed of the electron before the collision (we taught that m≈M). With oblique elastic removal, the loss of electron energy will be of the same order.

Let us assume that between each two impacts the electron, under the influence of an electric field, moves uniformly accelerated with acceleration a=eE/m for a time τ=l/v, and immediately after the first collision the direction of its speed can be any. Then, during the time between impacts, the electron gains energy

$$\Delta W_2 = eE \frac{a\tau^2}{2} = \frac{(eEl)^2}{2mv^2}.$$

The speed of the electron, and therefore its kinetic energy, will not change if $\Delta W_1=\Delta W_2$, or

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{eEl}{4} \sqrt{\frac{M}{m}}.$$

Corresponding "temperature" of electrons

$$T = \frac{mv^2}{3k} = \frac{eEl}{6k} \sqrt{\frac{M}{m}} \approx 4 \cdot 10^4 \text{ K.}$$
