WHAT DOESN'T THINK
SKIER

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. ABRIKOSOV

In the world of winter sports, alpine skiing, sledding and bobsleigh stand apart. When you watch such sports conditions only on TV, some bewilderment arises: is it even possible to compete to see who can slide down the hill the fastest? and isn't the spread of results just a matter of chance? After all, we all solved the problem of a body rolling down an inclined plane (Fig. 1). The system of equations describing this movement makes it possible to find the acceleration of the body at the initial stage, the speed of steady motion, and the time of movement along the track from start to finish. This is where a paradox arises, justifying the bewilderment of the TV fan: the results do not depend on the behavior of the athlete. But we are not trying to take this factor into account in the original equations. What if you try?

It immediately comes to mind to pay attention to the force of friction and the force of air resistance. The friction coefficient is related to the choice of oil. For sledders and sledders (and giants too), the role of arodynamics cannot be underestimated. It is important not only to adopt and maintain the correct stance, but also to select the material and sew the jumpsuit from it well. An example is the legendary victory of the French team in downhill. The French were the first to realize that the fluttering bib number was an unacceptable Russian speed of 100 km/h. Under a hail of jokes, they glued numbers to their costumes with plasters. And we had fun at the finish line. Nowadays, in pursuit of hundredths of a second, even speed skaters wear “super-streamlined” overalls, and ice skaters are sometimes blown in wind tunnels. Curved poles allow you to take a more advantageous stance. New mats and coatings for skis and alloys for sled runners are being tested. Described by L. Cassil in the book “The White Queen’s Move,” the life of a false magician seems like innocent fun when the fight for Olympic gold is going on.

But the equipment of the participants is more or less equivalent, and the individual qualities of the athlete come to the fore - the will to win, physical and special training. We explained how the laws of mechanics allow you to apply your physical data and turn your will to victory in precious seconds of results. To understand the basics of skiing technique, let's take a break from downhill skiing (where aerodynamics, the ability to properly guide skis and successfully set a trajectory are so important) and move on to slalom disciplines. Here the athlete has to fight for success on his own, at times showing acrobatics.

We assume that we are talking about uniform sliding around a circle. In connection with the lie, the reporting system, along with real forces (gravitational forces, friction, ground reaction, air resistance), needs to enter the centrifugal inertial force $F_{\pi6}$, directed from the center of the arc and equal to

$$F_{\pi6} = mv^2/R$$
where $v$ is the speed of the flyer, $R$ is the radius of the arc (Fig. 2). The center of gravity of the flyer in this system is motionless; This means that at any moment of time the applied forces are equal to zero. Therefore, the reaction force of the snet must be directed towards the center of the arc - after all, this is the only force that can compensate for $F_{\pi6}$. For lugers, this is ensured by the inclination of the gutter. And for better grip on the snow, the surface of the slide has metal edges, and when turning, the lie “turns” - the athlete tilts the lie “on the edge”, so that they catch on the snow with metal edges - like horse-drawn horses. Horizontal compensation of the net reaction force imparts to the athlete a centripetal (in the inertial reporting system) rooting. To improve feel on hard and icy slopes, edges need to be sharpened regularly, especially before competitions.

By the way, what stress does the athlete experience? Let's figure it out. The average speed of a slalom skier is about 10 m/s with an arc radius of about 5 m, and $F_{\pi6} = mv^2/R \approx m \cdot 20 \cdot m/c^2$, i.e., equal to approximately twice the weight of the slalom skier. This force must be added vectorially to the component of the gravity force $mg \cos \alpha$ (usually $\alpha < 30^\circ$, $\cos \alpha > 1/2$) perpendicular to the slope. So the total overload exceeds 2g, and it falls mainly on the “outer” leg (an attempt to stand on the inner leg, as a rule, ends in a fall). Well, the nature of the load is similar to that of a test on a vibration stand. Therefore, alpine skiers engage in general physical training even in the summer. For example, they squat with a shitanka.

Scientifically speaking, the slope of a mountain is a two-dimensional space where a sports wall (hereinafter if we consider it a material point) will have to find the optimal trajectory, while the movement of a bead is purely one-dimensional.*)

The type of optimal trajectory is determined by a combination of a number of factors. First of all, it is advisable to take the shortest route, deviating as little as possible from the slope line (Fig. 3). At the same time, we will benefit not only due to the reduction in distance, but also due to the increase in the average steepness of the route - the rounder the slope, the greater the rolling force and the less frictional force. In posture slalomists try to walk as close to the flags as possible, hitting them with their shoulders and body.

You can estimate the loss of time due to the removal of the path. Let the deviation from the optimal curve be only ±10 cm. Then the removal of each of the arcs forming it will be of order. On a slam track with 50 gates at an average speed of 10 m/s, the loss will be quite noticeable:

$$\Delta t \approx (50 \cdot 0,1 \text{ м}) / (10 \text{ м/c}) = 0,5 \text{ с.}$$

At the same time, in downhill or giant slalom, where the gate is smaller and the average speed is higher, this factor has an insignificant effect.

However, strange as it may seem, “going to the flag,” that is, straightening sections of the path between the flags, was also impossible. Firstly, you will have to slow down in order to fit into a more circular turn, and secondly, the time it takes to move in a straight line is not necessarily the shortest.

Let's look at a simple example. The bead rolls without initial speed from point $A$ to point $B$, first along an arc of a circle, and then along the chord contracting it (Fig. 4). If the angular size of the arc is small, then the time of movement in the first case is equal to a quarter of the oscillation period of a mathematical high-speed magnet with length $R$ (friction is neglected):

$$T_1 = \frac{\pi}{2} \sqrt{R/g}.$$

Braking mechanism
or What are good skis

In alpine skiing, and in many other sports, the progress and development of technical athletes go side by side with the improvement of equipment. Just as it was impossible to even dream of six-meter jumps before the advent of fiberglass poles, it was impossible to imagine stylish six-meter skiers standing on prized hickory skis with Kandahar bindings.

In order to “run fast”, skis must not only glide well, but also hold onto the slope as tightly as possible and not slide laterally. Indeed, from the formula for the centrifugal force it is clear that the speed with which one can go through a turn is proportional to the square root of the force $F$ of the lateral adhesion of the ski to the snow -

$$v = \sqrt{FR/m}.$$

However, there is more to it than that. Slipping is the main cause of loss of speed (precious energy is spent on “shoving” snow from the slope). Typically, skiers use side slides when they need to slow down, overcome a round section where a beginner is unable to cope, or simply smooth out a slope after training. But if an athlete “sickly turns”, the stopwatch will immediately record this. Hence the desire to place the ski as close to the slope as possible and crash the edges into the snow. (And on the sliding surface that opens at the same time, advertising for a false company will be especially effective. True, it appeared there quite recently.)

At the origins of the development of alpine skiing are F. Nansen. The great polar explorer, political figure, Nobel laureate of the world was also the author of the first book about alpine skiing.

During Nansen's time, he wore soft leather boots on hard, unedged skis. And the first actual mountain prem was “telemark” (Fig. 5). This beautiful turn required dexterity and was fraught with a fall.

AT THE BOTTOM, when the overload is maximum, we get up and again, with a sinking heart, go up, a little higher than last time. The center of gravity of the system describes a figure-of-eight spiral (Fig. 11) when swinging.

Rocking of the oscillatory system due to changes in its parameters (on a swing, this is the distance from the suspension point to the center of gravity) is called parametric variation. Getting up at the bottom point, we do positive work against the sum of gravity and the centrifugal force of inertia (we are considering movement in a non-inertial reporting system). And at the top point the centrifugal force is zero, and only the projection $mg \cos \alpha$ remains from the force of gravity. Therefore, the negative work when squatting (with the same amplitude) is less according to the model. The total work done per cycle is positive, and the energy of the system increases.

Now let’s try to estimate the energy balance of a skier, as we just did for a swing.

A scorpion lies in wait for us here. At first glance, everything is very similar. When moving in an arc, the skater is also affected by the force of gravity and the centrifugal force of inertia (Fig. 12). The angle between them changes so that their equal force is minimum at the beginning of the arc ($F_{p1}$) and reaches a maximum at the end ($F_{p2}$). By squatting, the liar performs negative work, while standing up, he performs positive work. However, when he extends at the beginning of the arc, less force is applied to him than when he bends at the end. This means that the total work done during the bending-extension cycle (depreciation-push) will be negative. Paradox?! It would seem natural to do positive work and increase energy.

There is no mistake here. It’s just that until now we didn’t particularly care about the energy balance and thought only about how to reduce losses and gain speed. In this theoretical race for seconds, it was not easy to go too far. It's time to put everything in its place. Let's write down the law of conservation of energy on any section of the route:

$$\Delta E = mg \Delta h + \frac{\Delta (mv^2)}{2} = A_{tp} + A_{b} + A_{лыжн}.$$

On the left side is the change in the potential ($mg \Delta h$) and kinetic ($\Delta (mv^2)/2$) energy of the flyer, on the right is the work done by the flyer itself, the forces of friction and air resistance. What are the correspondences between these terms?

Let's start with the left side of the equation. The average speed of a slalomist on the track does not change much. Therefore, the second term is essential, i.e. $\Delta (mv^2)/2) \approx 0$. But the first one, on the contrary, is great. To gain the total speed for a slalomist $v \sim 10$ m/s, it is enough to lose only $\Delta h = v^2/2g = 5$ m of height. (In large mountains the differences are usually hundreds of meters.)
