There are charges: on the side closest to the ball $A$ they are negative, and on the far side they are positive. This can be clearly seen if the ball of electric juice touches the corresponding areas of the surface of the ball $B$. It is not difficult to explain this distribution of charges. The fact is that the free electrodes, which were equally distributed throughout the entire ball $B$, under the influence of the charges of the ball $A$ come into motion and are redistributed throughout the ball so that there are more of them on the left than on the right. Thus, on the left side of the ball a negative charge is induced, i.e., induced - $q_B$, and on the right side - a positive charge + $q_B$. This is the phenomenon of electrostatic induction. Let us note by the way that in our case the magnitude of the induced charge $q_B$ is, of course, less than the magnitude of the induced charge $q_A$, since the magnitude of the induced charge will also spread to other bodies surrounding it, where electric charges are also induced.

Electric force always acts between charged bodies. This will happen with balls $A$ and $B$. More precisely, in this case two forces will arise - the attractive force $F_{пр}$ between the ball $A$ and the left part of the ball $B$ and the repulsive force $F_{от}$ between the ball $A$ and the right part of the ball $B$ (Fig. 2). Since the negative charge is close to the ball $A$, the force $F_{пр}$ is greater than the force $F_{от}$, and as a result the balls will attract each other under the net force $F = F_{пр} - F_{от}$.

What will change if the ball $B$ is first charged with some charge $q_B'$, also positive?

Of course, all the previous arguments about the induction of charges remain in force, but now we must also take into account the interaction of charges $q_A$ and $q_B'$. Another force $F'_{от}$ appears - the force of repulsion of charges $q_A$ and $q_B'$. The final result will depend on the correspondence between the magnitudes of the force $F$ and $F'_{от}$, or rather, on the correspondence between the magnitudes of the charges $q_B$ and $q_B'$, since the greater the magnitude of the charge, the greater the force of the electric interaction.

If the magnitude of the induced charge $q_B$ turns out to be greater than the magnitude of the charge $q_B'$ on the ball, then as a result, one-dimensionally charged balls will still attract. When is this possible? We have already said that the magnitude of the induced charge on the conductor is less than the magnitude of the influencing charge. Therefore, only if the charge $q_A$ is much larger than the charge $q_B'$, it may turn out that the magnitude of the charge $q_B$ will be greater than the charge $q_B'$.

So, two one-dimensionally charged bodies can attract each other, but for this to happen, the charge on one of them must be much greater than the charge on the other.
