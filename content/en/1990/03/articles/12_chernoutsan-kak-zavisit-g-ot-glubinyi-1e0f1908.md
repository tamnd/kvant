How does g depend on depth?

Question: What is it - big, green, lives three meters underground and eats stones?
Answer: Big Green Stone Eater.

From MIPT folklore
How will the force of gravity change as the body plunges into an imaginary shaft, digging through the Earth along its diameter?

What gravitational force would act inside a “hollow” planet?
Would it be possible to walk on its inner surface? Let's try to answer these and similar questions. And not, of course, in order to develop instructions for drillers of ultra-deep wells or for astronauts, but in order to better understand the law of universal gravitation.

The law of universal gravitation, discovered by Newton, states that any two material particles (exact masses) are attracted to each other with a force proportional to the masses of these particles $m_1$ and $m_2$ and inversely proportional to the square of the distance $r$ between them:

$$F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2},$$

Let, for example, we need to calculate the gravitational force acting on the exact mass $m$ from a body of arbitrary shape. Let us mentally divide the body into exact masses $m, m_2, \dots, m_n$, calculate the forces acting on mass $m$ from all exact masses, and then add these forces vectorially. What will happen in the end? Two facts immediately strike the eye. First, the force acting on the exact mass $m$ is proportional to the magnitude of that mass. Secondly, the strength of the essential depends on what kind of body interacts with our exact mass - what its shape and size are, what mass it has.

Who acts on mass $m$ and how in our example? Directly the masses $m_1, \dots, m_n$ through the space separating them? Or is there a non-actor “intermediary”? According to modern concepts, such a material mediator is the gravitational field created by the masses $m_1, \dots, m_n$ in the entire surrounding space. This field is named and acts on the mass $m$, it is located in it. If, for example, you change any of the masses or somehow shift them, then the field will also change.

To quantitatively characterize the gravitational field at a given point, a special physical quantity is introduced - the direction of the gravitational field. Let's define it like this. Let us write the expression for the gravitational force acting on the exact mass $m$ in the form

$$\vec{F} = m\vec{g},$$ or $$\vec{g} = \frac{\vec{F}}{m},$$ (2)

where $\vec{g}$ (a value independent of $m$!) is the direction of the gravitational field. It has a simple physical meaning: any precise mass will move with such rooting if it is placed at a given point in the field and released. In particular, when the exact mass interacts with the Earth, $\vec{g}$ is the free fall root you know.

What and how does $\vec{g}$ depend on? Like gravity, the direction of the field is subject to the principle of superposition:

$$\vec{g} = \vec{g}_1 + \vec{g}_2 + \dots + \vec{g}_n,$$

where $\vec{g}_1, \dots, \vec{g}_n$ are the directions of the fields created at a given point in space by individual masses $m_1, \dots, m_n$. From expressions (1) and (2) it follows that any exact mass $M$ creates a field around itself, the direction of which is directed towards this point and depends on the distance $r$ to it according to the law

$$g = G \frac{M}{r^2}.$$

What kind of gravitational field does a physically symmetrical body create around itself? The answer to this question was known to Newton from the very beginning - the force of gravity towards the Earth is calculated using the same formula (1), where $r$ is the distance to the center of the Earth. However, Newton understood that such a statement required proof. The counterbalance took a lot of time and effort, he proved that the force of attraction to a thin sphere of bodies located outside it is equal to the force of attraction to a material point of the same mass located in the center of the sphere. And since the ball can be divided into thin spheres, the same result remains valid for the ball. Let us now find out what the gravitational field is inside a thin sphere, i.e. at $r < R$. It turns out that inside the sphere the field direction is zero. Now we can answer the very first question - what is the gravitational field in a shaft dug along the diameter of the Earth. For simplicity, we will consider the Earth to be a homogeneous sphere with radius $R$. Consider a point at a distance $r < R$ from the center of the Earth. Having mentally drawn a sphere with radius $r$, we will divide the Earth into two parts - external and internal. As we have already found out, the field created at the rasterized point by the entire outer spherical layers is equal to zero. Therefore, the Earth's field at a given point coincides with the field created by the internal part, i.e., a ball with radius $r$. To use formula (3), we need to find the mass $M_r$ of this ball. It is obvious that the ratio of the mass $M_r$ to the mass $M_3$ of the entire Earth is equal to the ratio of their volumes, i.e. the ratio of cubes of radii: $M_r/M_3 = r^3/R^3$. Hence,

$$g_r = G \frac{M_r}{r^2} = G \frac{M_3}{r^2} \frac{r^3}{R^3} = g_0 \frac{r}{R},$$

where $g_0$ is the acceleration of free fall on the Earth's surface.

So, we know $g_r$ at any distance from the center of the Earth, both at $r > R$ and at $r < R$. This dependence is depicted graphically in figure 2.
