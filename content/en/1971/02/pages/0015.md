Reader
From what you said, I understood that temperature is what equalizes when two bodies come into contact. At school we were told that temperature is a quantity proportional to the average kinetic energy of molecules:

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2} kT,$$

where $k \approx 10^{-21} \text{ } \partial x/\partial p \text{ — постоянная Больцмана.}$

Temperature equalization occurs because during a collision, energy is transferred from particles with higher energy to particles with lower energy.

Author
What do you think is the potential energy of interaction between particles in a crystal at absolute zero temperature?

Reader
We offer this energy by turning the crystal into gas. This means that for each atom there is an energy equal to approximately $kT_k$, where $T_k$ is the boiling point.

Author
Absolutely correct assessment. So, in your opinion, a crystal located at absolute zero temperature can heat a gas having a temperature below the boiling point of the crystal?

Reader
Of course not. I did not mean total, but kinetic energy, which is higher the higher the temperature.

Author
Fine. Consider, for example, a piece of ice. Above it, of course, there is water vapor. Due to temperature equalization, ice and steam have the same temperatures at equilibrium. This means that the average velocities of molecules in vapor and ice crystal are the same.

Reader (puzzled).

No, this is something strange. After all, in melted ice the molecules are almost immobile, but they move around at enormous speeds. I remember well that when a liquid evaporates, it cools. There is even a way to obtain very low temperatures - evaporation of liquid gel. This is understandable. Only the fastest molecules can fly beyond the boundaries of molecular acceptance in a liquid. The remaining slow ones correspond to a lower temperature.

Author
But wouldn’t some of the energy be spent on pre-molecular absorption in the liquid? Will the molecules fly out of the liquid into vapor at the same speed with which they began their journey?

Reader
Honestly, it's good to argue. I never thought that in a dispute the truth is actually born! I think I understood everything completely! Indeed, those molecules that were washed away quickly will evaporate, so first the liquid cools. Pre-acceptance, the molecules lose their speed, like a package flying towards the Moon. Therefore, gas also does not consist of record-breakingly fast molecules, and the average speeds of molecules in gas and liquid coincide. Thanks to evaporation, the overall temperature is established. If the escaping, formerly fast molecules are carried away, as during evaporation under pumping, a noticeable decrease in the temperature of the liquid can be achieved.

Author
What about the speeds of molecules in a crystal?

Reader
In a crystal, molecules move with the same average speeds as in a pair above it, but they do not perform a free translational motion, slightly distorted by rare molecules, as in a gas, but oscillate around standard places in the crystal lattice...
