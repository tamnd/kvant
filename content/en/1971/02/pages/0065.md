To the objectives of the IV International Physical Olympiad

1. In case a) the carriage moves relative to the ringer uniformly with speed $v_0$. The friction force $F_{TP} = kmg$ acts on the block from the side of the carriage, giving it acceleration $a = \frac{F_{TP}}{M} g$. Therefore, the speed of the block increases with time $v = at = k \times \frac{m}{M} gt$. It is clear that the block cannot move faster than the carriage, therefore, if the carriage does not have time to reach the leading edge of the block, then at moment $t_0 = \frac{v_0 M}{kmg}$ the speed of the block becomes equal to $v_0$ and the sliding of the carriage along the block will stop. Then the carriage and the block will move uniformly at a speed of $v_0$. Let's check whether the carriage will have time to reach the edge of the block. By the moment $t_0$ it will have passed the path relative to the block

$$ S = v_0 t_0 + \frac{at^2}{2} = \frac{Mv_0^2}{2kmg} \approx 25 \text{ cm}. $$

$S < L$, that is, the carriage does not reach the edge of the block.

In case b) you can not write down the equations of motion of the bar and carriage, but use the law of conservation of momentum for the bar-carriage system. If u is the speed of the bar, and v is the speed of the carriage, then $mv - Mu = mv_0$.

Given that $v + u = v_0$, we find that $v = v_0$ and $u = 0$. After the carriage is released, the block remains motionless, and the carriage will move at a speed of $v_0$. The front edge of the block remains $t = \frac{L}{v_0} = 5c$.

It is not difficult to report that the unit cell of NaCl needs to be assigned only four sodium atoms and four chlorine atoms (for example, those that form a small cube with an edge $\frac{1}{2} a$, one of the vertices of which coincides with the vertex of the unit cell). Indeed, the position of the other two atoms can be found if such a unit cell (with edge a and 8 atoms) is attached to the one drawn on figure.

The mass of the unit cell is equal to $(4A_{Na} + 4A_{Cl}) m_H$, where $A_{Na}$ and $A_{Cl}$ are the atomic weights of sodium and chlorine, and $m_H$ is the mass of the hydrogen atom.

On the other hand, it should be equal to $\rho \cdot a^3$. That's why

$$ \rho a^3 = 4 (A_{Na} + A_{Cl}) m_H, $$

where

$$ m_H = \frac{\rho a^3}{4 (A_{Na} + A_{Ce})} \approx 1.67 \cdot 10^{-24} e. $$

3. Let us denote by q the charge induced on the surface of the ball. If we ignore the distortion of the field due to the conductor, then we can assume that the field outside the sphere is the same as it would be if all the charges were located in the center of the sphere. This means that the potential between the ball and the sphere is what it would be if the sphere were not charged, and the ball had a charge q. But in this case, the potential of the sphere would be equal to $\frac{q}{R}$, and the potential of the ball would be $\frac{q}{r}$, and therefore $\Delta \varphi = \frac{q}{r} - \frac{q}{R} = q \frac{R-r}{Rr}$.

In our case, the potential of the ball $\varphi_2$ is equal to

$$ \varphi_1 + \Delta \varphi = \frac{Q + q}{R} + q \frac{(R-r)}{Rr} = \frac{rQ + Rq}{Rr}. $$

But the ball is grounded and $\varphi_2 = 0$, therefore

$$ \frac{rQ + Rq}{Rr} = 0; $$

from here

$$ q = -Q \frac{r}{R}. $$

Now it is not difficult to find the potential of the sphere

$$ \varphi_1 = \frac{Q + q}{R} = \frac{Q}{R} \cdot \frac{R-r}{R} = \frac{3}{4} e. $$

(since $1 \text{ кул} = 3 \cdot 10^9 \text{ CGSE}).$

By definition of capacity $C = \frac{Q}{\varphi_1} = \frac{R^2}{R - r} = 40 \text{ cm}.$
