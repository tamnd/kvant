Reader
Does temperature determine the distribution of particles by energy level only in quantum mechanics?

Author
Of course not. This is also true in classical mechanics. For example, the energy distribution of molecules of a “classical” ideal gas is as shown in figure 4. It is determined by the formula
$$\frac{n}{n_0} = e^{-\frac{E}{kT}}.$$
Here $n_0$ is the number of particles in the state from which we start, there is no energy of particle $E$; $n$ is the number of particles with energy $E$; $e = 2,718$ is the base of natural logarithms, $k$ is Boltzmann’s constant, $T$ is the gas temperature.

Reader
If we transform this formula, continuing the rhymes of both sides of the equality, then it will take the form
$$\ln \frac{n}{n_0} = -\frac{E}{kI}.$$

From here
$$T = -\frac{E}{k(\ln n - \ln n_0)}.$$

If we supply energy to a gas, we will increase its temperature, transferring more and more of it from lower energy levels to upper ones. In doing so, we can ensure that $n$ becomes $n_0$ and $\ln n$ greater than $\ln n_0$. This will mean that the gas temperature is negative! It seems to me that we have reached the point of absurdity. Therefore, it makes no sense to talk about the negative temperature of an ideal gas.

Author
If we consider only a few energy levels, then we can make it so that the upper ones will have more gas molecules than the lower ones. However, it is impossible to ensure that the energy distribution of molecules is the same as shown in figure 5: the dependence of the number of a part on energy is exponential and there is more of a part at very high levels than at lower ones.
