⟦folio 53⟧

numbers taken from the book “The Flight of Lightning.” The average number of thunderstorms on the globe is 16,000,000. Every day, about 100 lightning flashes on the ground.

An electric discharge moves in the air at a speed of 100 to 100 thousand kilometers per second. Studying the dynamics of such a process is a complex task. You can read about how it was solved and how the development of lightning was recorded on the pages of the book.

One of the most interesting questions of atmospheric electricity is the question of the distribution of charges on clouds. It turns out that in most cases the lower part of the clouds is negatively charged. B. Schonland describes in detail how the electric field changes during the development of lightning, how charges are restored from the cloud to the ground. There are pages in the book (unfortunately, there are very few of them) devoted to the most challenging and still most understood phenomenon - ball lightning.

The reader learned about different types of discharge in air, how gas initiation and electrification of clouds occur, and the role that water drops and ice crystals play in these processes. The final pages of the book are devoted to various phenomena that accompany thunderstorms. The author talks in detail about “atmospheric” (radio waves emitted during thunderstorms), explains why the charge on the surface of the earth remains constant.

The book is written in good language. A large number of seriously meaningful physical facts and, what is also important, a lot of useful historical information are concentrated in a small volume. The presentation is fully accessible to schoolchildren. It's just a pity that the book was published as a bit of a bummer.

Yu. M. Brook

ONCE AGAIN ABOUT THE PYTHAGOREAN THEOREM

Let us consider a vessel that has the shape of a straight line, at the base of which lies a right triangle ABC (see Fig.). Let's fill this vessel with gas and give it the opportunity to rotate around the vertical axis OO' (plane ABC is horizontal). Space is homogeneous, and no matter what position this vessel occupies, it will be in the same conditions. Therefore, we can assume that he is finished.

Consequently, the gas pressure forces on its side faces will mutually balance. But the forces F₁ and F₂ tend to rotate this vessel in one direction, and the force F₃ in the other. Therefore, the sum of the torques of the forces F₁ and F₂ must be equal to the torque of the force F₃.

$$F_1 \cdot \frac{AB}{2} + F_2 \cdot \frac{BC}{2} = F_3 \cdot \frac{AC}{2}.$$  (1)

But F₁ = P(AB \cdot h), F₂ = P(BC \cdot h), F₃ = P(AC \cdot h), where P is the gas pressure and h is the height of the vessel.

$$P(AB \cdot h) \cdot \frac{AB}{2} + P(BC \cdot h) \cdot \frac{BC}{2} = P(AC \cdot h) \cdot \frac{AC}{2},$$

where

$$AB^2 + BC^2 = AC^2$$

This proof can be read in the small brochure by B. Yu. Kogan “The Application of Mechanics to Theometry” (Nauka publishing house, 1965).
