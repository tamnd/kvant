pulse particles $p$ and coordinates $x$ we find out the values ​​of these quantities with some errors $\Delta p$ and $\Delta x$.
The properties of these two quantities are that it is impossible to make the product of these errors (sometimes called “uncertainty”) less than $\hbar$ - Planck’s constant divided by 2π

$$\left( h = \frac{h}{2\pi} \approx 10^{-32} \frac{\partial \hbar}{\partial e_k} \right): \quad \Delta p \Delta x \geq \hbar.$$

Author
In an object consisting of identical atoms, all of them are also active, and therefore their position cannot be changed with an accuracy greater than the distance between them. If $\Delta x \sim a$, what is the smallest possible value of momentum?

Reader
Of course, $\frac{\hbar}{a}$! If I were to say that the momentum, for example, is less than 0.1 $\frac{\hbar}{a}$, then this would mean changing it with an accuracy of 0.1 $\frac{\hbar}{a}$, which is impossible!

Author
This means that the lowest possible kinetic energy...

Reader
...equal to $\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{(mv)^2}{2m} = \frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}$, that is, the smaller the mass, the greater! I wonder what temperature this energy corresponds to in classical mechanics? The average distance between atoms and electrons in metals is $a \approx 10^{-10}$ m, the mass of an electron is $10^{-27}$ e, the mass of a helium atom is $10^{-23}$ e. From the formula

$$\frac{3}{2} kT = \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}$$

we find that for electrons the temperature is

$$T = \frac{\hbar^2}{3kma^2} = \frac{10^{-64}}{3 \cdot 1,38 \cdot 10^{-21} \cdot 10^{-30} \cdot 10^{-20}} \approx 2,5 \cdot 10^6 \text{°K}.$$

Big million degrees! For helium we would get temperatures $1^\circ \text{K}$ in the same way.

Author
Exactly the numbers that were discussed earlier. Now it is no longer difficult for you to find out what a substance will be like at zero temperature—solid or not. But first, think about why it is generally difficult for the parietal to harden at low temperatures.

Reader
Obviously, because at low temperatures the kinetic energy of the particle becomes small.

Author
What does small mean? Compared to what?

Reader
Apparently, in comparison with the potential energy of interaction between particles. When potential energy becomes significantly greater than kinetic energy, it forces atoms to occupy certain positions in the crystal lattice. Therefore, in order to say what a substance will be like at zero temperature, you need to know how the potential energy of interaction of particles depends on the distance between them.

Now it is clear to me that a liquid that does not freeze at absolute zero temperature should be sought among substances with light atoms and weakly interacting—further, increased kinetic energy of atoms and placed potential interaction energy $U$. Helium is the lightest inert gas, the atoms of which weakly interact with each other (as, indeed, with other atoms - this is their “inertness”). Obviously, it satisfies the general conditions.
