Let us now move on to the paradox of S.I. Vavilov. Imagine two completely identical correct sharply limited light points of width $a$, intersecting at a small angle $\alpha$. In the region $ABCD$ interference will occur (Fig. 2).

To observe the interference pattern, you can install a screen perpendicular to the drawing plane, passing through points $A$ and $C$. The interference pattern will consist of rectilinear alternating light and dark stripes filling the screen from point $A$ to point $C$ (Fig. 3).

The distribution of the energy flux density in the interference carten corresponds to the graph on figure 1. If the common beams have the same initial phases of light oscillations, then the difference in the phases of the light waves of the first and second beams at points lying on the straight line $BD$ will be equal to zero. It thus corresponds to the middle of the central light stripe. In the middle of the adjacent, dark stripe, the phase difference should be equal to $π$, in other words, the light vibrations in the common beams should be in antiphase. The phase difference $\Delta φ$ is equal to the path difference $\Delta L$ of both waves to a given location, divided by the wavelength and multiplied by $2π$:

$$\Delta φ = 2\pi \frac{\Delta L}{\lambda}.$$ (1)

An increase in the length of the beam traversed by the wave by $\lambda$ corresponds to a phase delay of $2π$. From formula (1) it follows that in the middle of the dark stripe closest to the center of the interference carten $\Delta L$ should be equal to $\frac{\lambda}{2}$.
