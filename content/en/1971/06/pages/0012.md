But then a shortage of electrons is detected on the neighboring atom, that is, the hole moves to the neighboring atom (Fig. 1, b). Of course, in reality it is electrons from filled atoms that jump to neighboring unfilled ones, but it is more convenient to talk about the movement of a hole. There is still only one hole, but it reflects the movement of many electrons. The movement of a hole is similar to the movement of a positively charged particle, because if, under the influence of an electric field, electrons move in one direction, then their “lack” moves in the other.

It will be useful to highlight the difference between the behavior of metals and semiconductors in an electric field. Current in a metal is created only by free electrons. Metal is structured in such a way that it always contains one or another amount of them. In a semiconductor, at sufficiently low temperatures, there are no free electrons. These free electrons

can be created. But at the same time, by tearing an electron away from the atom and presenting it with freedom, we also create a hole. Subsequently, these electrons and holes move independently of each other. It turns out that the current in a semiconductor consists of the electron current and the hole current.

It may happen that a free electron and a hole meet, that is, that an electron, while moving through a crystal, encounters an atom that just lacks an electron. It will occupy this free space, and at this moment both the electron and the hole will disappear. In this case, a recommendation is said to have occurred. You have the right to ask, why then create electrons and holes if sooner or later they will simply destroy each other? First, in the presence of an electric field, electrons and holes, as particles with different charges, move in opposite directions, and we can detect the current they create even before they recombine. Secondly, we do not knock out electrons from every atom, but, for example, from every thousandth. In this case, we create a significant current (since there are approximately $10^{23}$ atoms in each cubic centimeter of the crystal) and leave enough freedom for the movement of electrons and holes.

Semiconductor crystals are grown extremely pure. This is also done so that nothing interferes with the movement of electrons and holes.
