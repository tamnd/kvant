Fig. 7. In the diagram, close-packed molecules are represented by circles. The triangles indicate two possible positions for the molecules of the next layer.

Intelligent blocks of a crystal - atomic or molecular cells - are not creaks, but rather look like balls. Certain crystals have a close-packed structure, which means that these spherical blocks are not arranged directly on top of one another, but rather like cannonballs (or biliary balls) in a paramide in which each ball fits into a depression between its nearest neighbors. It is clear that each subsequent layer in such a paramid can fit tightly to the previous one with two possible positions of the balls. On figure 7 these positions of the balls of the upper layer are indicated by the symbols ▲ and ▼. When one layer is stacked on top of another, close packing conditions require that all the beads of that layer be located either at the positions marked ▲ or at the positions marked ▼. The next layer can again be adjusted so that its balls are located in either one or another position. Let the layers be arranged so that their balls are arranged sequentially, now in one position, now in another. This layer sequence is indicated by the symbol ▲. However, crystals are often packaged in much more complex ways. For example, one type of silicon carbide crystal is characterized by the following arrangement:

▲▲▼▼▲▼▼▼ and so on.

Here the group consisting of a heel of layers is repeated. In some types of silicon carbide crystals, a group of 29 layers is repeated.

How can information about the lower location of the balls in the layer be transmitted over such a large distance? How does each ball in the thirtieth layer end up in the same position as the corresponding ball in the first layer? Classical crystallography could not offer any explanation and there were reports of unknown long-range forces between molecules. Crystal growth theory using dislocation theory offered a simple, although incomplete, answer. The presence of a series of screw dislocations can lead to the appearance of a step height in a multilayer. This step becomes the only repetition in the structure of the crystal, which is constantly reproduced as it grows. The theory does not explain only one significant spectrum of implementation: in the case of silicon carbide, only 14 variations of the crystal structure pattern have been found, while the theory leads to the appearance of an almost unlimited number of such structures.

How does the initial screw dislocation occur? The answer to this question is not clear yet. Almost all crystals begin to grow from a small foreign core. Some of these nuclei may have surface inhomogeneities on which a screw dislocation will arise, propagating throughout the growing crystal. Other crystals may acquire dislocations as they grow. Dislocations can also occur on trapped parts of the dirt.
