The property of a semiconductor diode to pass current in only one direction is used, for example, to create rectifiers - devices that convert alternating current into direct current.
The simplest circuit of such a rectifier is shown in figure 7. To analyze the operation of this circuit, the only important thing for us is the fact that with one direction symbol the resistance of the diode ($R_D$) is practically equal to zero (when it passes a huge current), and with another direction symbol the diode practically does not conduct current and, therefore, its resistance is very high.

Now suppose that the diode allows current to pass only from left to right, that is, it is open when the input direction is greater than zero, and closed when it is negative.
At $U_{bx} > 0$ total circuit resistance
$$R_D + R \approx R,$$
and the output direction will be practically equal to the input.
At $U_{bx} < 0$ the diode resistance is infinite ($R_D \gg R$), and therefore the direction of $R$ is practically zero. the output direction is zero. Thus, our system converts an alternating direction into a constant direction.

Transistor
Now we can consider a transistor - a system that combines the two transitions just discussed. A $p-n-p$-transistor is a small block made, for example, of thermium, composed of three sections: $p$-region, $n$-region and again $p$-region (Fig. 8). Each transition in the transistor behaves as described above. In particular, in each transition there should be a potential drop - a drop from the $n$-region to each of the $p$-regions, so that the overall potential distribution will be as shown in figure 8.
