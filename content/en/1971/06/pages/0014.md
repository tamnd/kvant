As soon as a transition is formed, the situation changes. Electrons from the $n$ region moving towards the interface have the opportunity to jump to the $p$ region. Similarly, holes from the $p$-area will be counted into the $n$-area. Both of these processes act in the same direction: they tend to charge the $n$-type term positive, and the $p$-type term negative. In fact, the $n$-region, the total charge of which was equal to zero, now becomes positively charged, donating negatively charged electrons to the $p$-region. Positively charged holes coming from the $p$-region only contribute to this. Thermal $p$-type acquires a negative charge both due to the arrival of electrons and the departure of holes.

However, all this process must be left behind or later. When the $n$-type thermal is charged positively, and the $p$-type thermal is charged negatively, then a capacitor is formed, and an electric field appears at the boundary, directed from the $n$-region to the $p$-region (Fig. 5). This means that, for example, electrons from the $n$-region will no longer be able to go to the $p$-region. A strong electric field at the boundary will cause them to turn back. The same can be said for holes. The resulting electric field will reduce their chances of moving into the $n$-region. So, an equilibrium will occur in which the $n$-type thermal will be positively charged, and the $p$-type thermal will be positively charged.

If we take the potential of the electric field at the boundary to be zero, then the potential of the $n$-region is greater than zero, and the potential of the $p$-region is less than zero (Fig. 6), as will be the plate of a charged capacitor. The potential changes sharply in the region where the electric field exists, i.e., at the interface between $p$- and $n$-type thermals. The potential difference in the $p$-$n$ transition is equal to the energy that an electron needs to have to move from the $n$ region to the $p$ region, or a hole to transition from the $p$ region to the $n$ region. As for holes in the $n$-region and electrons in the $p$-region, if they are lucky enough to accidentally get to the interface, the electric field will only push them into another region.
