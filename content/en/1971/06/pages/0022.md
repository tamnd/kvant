Perfectly regular crystals rarely occur in nature. They usually contain at least one dislocation. This is a necessary condition for growth with slight supersaturation. In the general case, the crystal has a lot of dislocations. The presence of various dislocations complicates the growth picture. So, for example, two closely spaced dislocations, one of which is right-handed and the other left-handed, can lead to a step fixed at both ends (Fig. 3, a). As new creeps sit on such a step, it takes on a semicircular shape (Fig. 3, b). As a result, we arrive at a quantity-shaped step (Fig. 3, θ). This is a fully built-up crystalline surface on such a crystal. However, in the meantime, a new step is formed at the top of the original step, which serves as the core for building a new row (Fig. 3, e). As this process is repeated, the crystal will develop into a series of close-packed circles rather than spirals, as it did with a single screw dislocation. You can see a photograph of such a crystal on figure 4. The steps here are rectangles, not circles, for the reason that the conditions of this crystal at the periphery and in the center are slightly different.

Fig. 3. Closed loops can arise from steps that begin and end on the surface of the crystal (figure a) and which are caused by the proximity of two dislocations: right and left. Such a step is fixed at both ends and can only be raised by unfurling. Figure b shows a swelling step beginning to turn out. The next figure shows the moment of growth when the left and right protruding parts came together. The last figure shows how the internal parts have separated from the filled layer. A dent is visible in this step, which will be filled as soon as the area formed on the top of the loop grown from the first area begins to fill with molecules.
