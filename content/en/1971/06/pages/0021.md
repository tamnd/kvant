Once Frank understood the picture of a crystal with a dislocation, he immediately realized that the presence of a dislocation constantly creates an open step on the surface. Atoms and molecules can always easily attach themselves to it and continue to build a crystal without starting a new row. This step, as new building units are added, spreads across the surface of the crystal. However, it bends (Fig. 2). To understand the reason for this, consider a skater's chain, which turns around one of its ends. If they want to maintain formation, then those skaters who are close to the second end of the rotation will have to run faster than those who are close to the center of the rotation. After all, they must run in a circle with a large radius. In our case with a step on the surface of a crystal, the problem is that its outer edge moves no faster than the area in the center. The bricks are laid at a more or less constant speed along the entire length of the step. In this case, as construction proceeds, the rotation of the outer part of the step around the center does not occur quickly enough and the step bends, forming a spiral.
