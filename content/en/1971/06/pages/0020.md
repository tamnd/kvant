The growth of crystals should proceed approximately as shown in figure 1, 6. A cube falling onto the surface of a crystal binds firmly to it only when at least three of its six faces adhere to cubes already sitting on the crystal. Moreover, in order to guarantee the growth of any layer, it must consist of a certain minimum number of cubes.

In the case where too many of the cubes that make it up are located at the corners or on the edges, they will not be able to hold new cubes that fall to the surface.

The rate of transition of molecules from a solid to the surrounding vapor depends on the intensity of molecular movement - this depends on temperature. The rate of return from vapor to a solid depends on the concentration of molecules in the vapor, that is, on the vapor pressure. For each temperature there is a certain “saturated vapor pressure” at which the rate of transition from solid to vapor and back is balanced. Under these conditions the crystal will not grow. It can only if the steam is too long.

This model of crystal growth further assumes that once a layer on the surface is filled, further crystal growth is hindered: a higher degree of oversaturation is needed to begin to fill a new layer.

It was at this point that the classical theory of crystal growth failed. Calculations have shown that the formation of islands of condensation centers large enough for the construction of new words to begin and the crystal to continue to grow requires pressure exceeding the saturated vapor pressure by values ​​of 25 to 50 percent. However, in 1931, physicists M. Volmer and W. Schulze were able to grow crystals with a steam supersaturation of only one percent. The theory predicted that with such a degree of supersaturation, the crystal could grow at a rate 10^1000 times less than the rate at which it actually grows. This is apparently one of the most record-breaking discrepancies between theory and experiment.

How does such a relatively easy construction of new crystalline planes begin and continue? Physicists continue calculations again and again, but within the framework of the old model, they could not come to results that make any sense, as is now clear. This continued until 1949, when the English physicist F. Frank proposed a new model. Frank noted that the growth of a crystal can be quite simply understood if we assume that during the building process its crystal planes are not arranged independently of one another, but form a spiral structure. This would be possible if the crystal had such a deviation from the ideal structure, which is called a “screw dislocation.”

Let us imagine a crystal lattice of two adjacent sections, in which, throughout the entire depth of the crystal, the crystal elephants of one of the parts (the right one at Fig. 2) are located below the corresponding words of the other part. Why do we call such a structural disturbance a screw dislocation? Let the tiny observable walk around the inner edge of the cut along the surface of the crystal, starting its path at its upper end. He will end up at a lower level. And this would happen with each subsequent round, thus moving our observed elephant into the depths of the crystal. The model we described is called a screw dislocation, because the spiral path into the depth of the crystal always makes it turn to the right.
