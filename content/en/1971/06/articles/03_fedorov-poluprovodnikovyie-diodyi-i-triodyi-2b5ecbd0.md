Conductors, semiconductors and dielectrics

Before talking about the semiconductor diode and transistor, we will talk about what semiconductors are, what their internal structure is and what properties they have.

Of the large number of semiconductors known in nature, silicon and germanium are the most studied. These are elements numbered 14 and 32 in the periodic table.

Strictly speaking, semiconductors are dielectrics, only very weak dielectrics. Weak in the sense that they are easy to convert into semiconductors. There are several ways to do this. The main ones are: irradiation, bombardment of semiconductors with fast particles, heating and adding appropriate impurities to the semiconductor.

As you know, dielectrics do not conduct electric current, that is, they are insulators. If a potential difference is created at the ends of a dielectric sample and the current is changed, it will be equal to zero. This is explained by the fact that inside the sample there are no free loaded particles, for example, electrons, which, under the influence of an electric field, would move from one end of the sample to the other and thus create a current. The dielectric atoms firmly hold all their electrons and even if they release them from themselves, they have only exchanged with their neighbor for another electron.

It's a different matter in metal. The bulk of electrons there behave similarly to electrons in a dielectric. But some of them, those that are located far from the positively charged nuclei and are therefore weakly connected with them, during the formation of a crystal receive such freedom that they can be throughout the entire crystal. These electrons are called free electrons, or conduction electrons. If you place a metal in an electric field, then these

free electrons (because they have a negative charge) move in the direction opposite to the direction of the electric field. The electric field, like wind, blows them towards the boundary of the sample. A galvanometer connected in this way will register an electric current.

So, a dielectric does not conduct electric current because there are no free charge carriers in it that can move freely along the sample under the influence of an electric field.

But we ourselves can create such charges. One of the ways is to act on the atom of the dielectric with some suitable “tool”, for example, a quantum of light - a photon. We irradiate the dielectric with ordinary light, or even better, RENTen rays, since they have higher energy, and knock out electrons from the atoms that form the crystal. In this way, free electrons can be created in the dielectric and turned into a conductor. It is clear that the energy required to release an electron depends primarily on what atoms the crystal is made of and how these atoms are located in the crystal. In other words, this energy is different for different crystals. The minimum energy required to remove an electron from an atom and convert it into a free one is called the energy gap of a given dielectric. If radiation falls on the crystal, the energy of the quanta of which is lower than the minimum energy required to release an electron, then none of the electrons will be knocked out and the dielectric will remain an insulator.

The difference between dielectrics and semiconductors lies precisely in the size of the energy gap. Dielectrics with a sufficiently small energy gap are called semiconductors. The energy gap of semiconductors is approximately 10 times smaller than that of pronounced dielectrics, such as, for example, diamond. The energy gap width for diamond is 7 38, while for thermanium we have 0.72 38, and for silicon 1.1 38*). Thus, in order to turn a semiconductor into a conductor, it is enough to impart it to electrons relatively small small small small small smallest.

Now imagine that we are heating a dielectric. As the temperature increases, the average energy of the electrons will increase, and many of them will have enough energy to bridge the energy gap. As a result, there are more and more free electrons, and the appearance of free electrons occurs more often the higher the temperature, and, of course, most often in those crystals that have a smaller energy gap, that is, in semiconductors. That is why semiconductors are called semiconductors, because at sufficiently high temperatures (for example, room temperature ~300°K) they are conductors, and when the temperature drops they turn into dielectrics. The transistor example transmits work in freezing cold, since the semiconductors used in its transistors turn into real insulators.

Semiconductor diode
