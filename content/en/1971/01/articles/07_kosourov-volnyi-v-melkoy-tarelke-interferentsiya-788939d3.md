There is no more universal phenomenon in nature than the propagation of waves. Waves on the surface of water, sound, light, radio, transfer of deformation from one part of a solid body to another - all these are wave processes. Quantum mechanics shows that the movement of microparticles is controlled by wave laws. All these waves have different physical natures, different propagation speeds, different frequencies and wavelengths. But regardless of this, the movement of any waves has a lot in common. Having studied the laws of propagation of waves of one nature, it is possible to practically use a large number of them without changing them to waves of another type. It is most convenient to study waves on the surface of the wave.

What is a wave? Throw a stone into the ore. On the calm horizontal surface of labor a running circle will appear. The points on the water surface that the wave should reach begin to oscillate relative to their equilibrium position. This position corresponds to a horizontal surface. The farther a point is from the place where the stone fell, the more late it “learns” about the fall of the stone. Disturbance spreads at a certain speed. The points to which the disturbance must simultaneously be located are in the same stage of oscillatory motion (in the same phase). The speed of movement of this phase is the speed of the wave. It changes in the direction normal to the wave front.

For waves of any nature, it is always possible to indicate physical objects that, under the influence of the wave, will experience a disturbance, that is, a deviation from their equilibrium values. For sound, these are periodic increases and decreases in pressure. For radio waves and light - rapid changes in the direction of the electric and magnetic fields.

The properties of all media without exception are such that a disturbance that arises in a certain area spreads, being transmitted from point to point with a finite speed, which depends on the nature of the disturbance and the properties of the medium.

For a wave to occur, a source of disturbance is required, that is, an external cause that causes a disturbance in propagation in a certain region of the medium. A small source, such as, for example, a stone thrown into water, emits in a homogeneous medium (that is, in a medium in which the speed of the wave does not depend on the direction of its propagation) spherical waves (circular on the surface of the wave), propagating along radii. Such wave sources are called point sources.

One of the basic principles of elementary wave theory is the principle of wave independence or superposition principle. He argues that the disturbance caused by a wave at an observed point does not depend on the fact that other waves simultaneously pass through this point. The principle of superposition gives a simple rule for finding the total action of waves from several sources: the total amount of work is equal to the sum of the oscillations caused by each source separately.

A characteristic feature of wave processes is wave interference. Interference is called a set of phenomena that arise in a medium during the propagation of waves from two or more sources that oscillate in accordance (synchronously). It turns out that at some points the oscillations caused by the simultaneous action of two sources will be more confluent or weaker than the oscillations caused by each source separately. It may happen that coordinated waves will cancel each other out.

At first glance, the phenomenon of interference contradicts the principle of superposition. Before discussing this, we decide to look at the interference with our own eyes. An experienced eye can easily see interference when waves from two stones thrown into ore intersect. However, to study interference

First of all, you need a vessel with water. So that the waves reflected from its walls do not mask the waves coming from the sources, a vessel with flat walls is needed. In this regard, it is well treated almost to the very top. Running onto the walls, the waves quickly attenuate and are almost not reflected. An electric bell with an unscrewed cap can serve as a wave generator. Attach a piece of wire to the bell hammer and place a cork ball on its end, which will be the exact source of the waves. When testing wires, make sure that the wires are well insulated.

The bell must be secured above the plate so that by turning the bell the cork ball can be immersed in the water at the edge of the plate. It is best to power the call through an author-former. This allows you to adjust the amplitude of vibrations. The author-former from the children's railway is very convenient. A transformer from a burning device will also work. Turning on the device, we will see circular waves on the surface of the water with a distance between adjacent humps—the wavelength—of about 1 cm (Fig. 1 on page 36).

The best way to observe waves is by looking at the shadow on the bottom of the plate in direct sunlight or the light of a bright lamp. Each wave, acting like a cylindrical lens, produces a light stripe on the bottom of the plate, repeating the shape of the wave front. However, waves traveling at a speed of about 10 cm/sec merge for the gaze fixed on a stationary plate. They are visible only near the source, where their amplitude is large. To increase them over the entire surface of the water, you need to quickly turn your head. In the same way, with rapid head movements, you can see the spokes of a rolling wheel. It is very effective to observe waves on the frosted glass of a camera, especially large-format ones. Holding the camera in your hands and showing it slowly, it is easy to ensure that the waves are visible over the entire surface. At the same time, it seems that they are running very slowly. You can look at the reflection of the wave surface in a mirror. Slight movements of the mirror also make waves visible across the entire surface. But it is most convenient to observe waves under stroboscopic lighting. If you illuminate the installation with short flashes of light with a frequency equal to the frequency of the wave source, then from one flash to another the waves will move by one wavelength, and the wave pattern of the waves will seem motionless. This kind of lighting is extremely easy to achieve. It is enough to turn on a small light bulb parallel to the windings of the bell magnet. From a distance of 0.5-1 m it will illuminate the installation well and evenly, and the motionless shadow pattern of the waves will be clearly visible. The best way to take photographs is to use direct sunlight.

Now attach a fork with pieces of cork at the ends to the bell hammer according to the wiring. The distance between the ends of the fork is 2-3 cm. If the ends are lowered into the water at the same time, you will get two sources of waves that oscillate not only synchronously, that is, in time, but also in phase, that is, waves from both sources will arise at the same moment in time. The picture will be approximately the same as in figure 2. From the wave sources, areas of large amplitudes diverge upward, separated by areas of “silence”. The central region of large amplitudes is located perpendicular to the line connecting the sources. Both areas of silence and areas of large amplitudes pass between the sources.

A study of the interference pattern with a ruler will show that the distance between the two maxima on the line connecting the sources is equal to half the distance between the two waves, i.e., half the length of the wave.

Now it is completely clear why the distance between the maxima on the line connecting the sources is equal to half the wavelength. Indeed, at the midpoint of this wavelength, the waves arrive in the same phase and reinforce each other. If you move half a wavelength from this point, then the path of one wave will increase by half the wavelength, and the path of the other will decrease by the same amount. The path difference between the waves at this point (the difference in the path traveled by the waves from the sources to the point) will be equal to one wavelength, and the waves will again be mutually amplified, etc., through each wavelength.

The maximum corresponding to zero path difference is called zero maximum or zero order interference. Maxima with a path difference of one wavelength are maxima of the first order of interference, etc. The maximum order of interference is determined by an integer close to 2d/λ, where d is the distance between the sources and λ is the wavelength.
