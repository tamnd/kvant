AUXILIARY CIRCLE

The solution to many geometric problems begins with drawing auxiliary lines that help establish a connection between data and unknown elements of the figure. Recalling a successful auxiliary formation is often difficult. Therefore, you should approach each solved problem and try to find out why certain auxiliary lines lead to the goal. Is it possible to use the found example to solve some other problems?

At the next meeting of the mathematical circle, we will consider one of the interesting examples of solving geometric problems, which consists of introducing an auxiliary circle into the drawing.

Example 1. The altitudes AP, BQ and CR are drawn in an acute triangle. Prove that $\angle ABQ = \angle APR$).

Solved and e. Let H be the point of intersection of the altitudes of triangle ABC (Fig. 1). Since $\angle APB$ and $\angle CRB$ are straight lines, a circle can be described around the quadrilateral BPHR by taking BH as the diameter. Having constructed it, we replace that $\angle ABQ = \angle APR$ (as inscribed angles based on the same arc).

Example 3. Prove that the square bicsectrix of a triangle is equal to the difference between the product of its enclosing sides and the product

segments of the third side into which it is divided by a bicector.

Solved and e. We describe a circle around the triangle $ABC$ and extend the bicsectrix $CD$ of the triangle until it meets the circle at the point $E$ (Fig. 3). Let $BC = a$, $AC = b$, $AD = m$, $BD = n$, $CD = l$, $DE = x$.

According to the conditions, $\angle ACE = \angle BCE$, in addition, $\angle AEC = \angle ABC$, are like inscribed angles based on the same arc. Consequently, the triangles $ACE$ and $BCD$ are similar and the equality $$\frac{l + x}{b} = \frac{a}{l},$$ is true, hence $l^2 = ab - lx$. Chords $AB$ and $CE$ intersect at point $D$. Therefore the equality $lx = mn$ holds. Therefore, $l^2 = ab - mn$.

Example 5. Construct an equidistant triangle so that its vertices lie respectively on three given parallel lines.

Fig. 5.

There are several known solutions to this interesting problem using expressions around a point, similarity and algebraic methods. A very short solution is obtained by constructing an auxiliary circle.

Solution. Let $ABC$ be the original equidistant triangle whose vertices lie respectively on the parallel lines $a, b$ and $c$ (Fig. 5). The circle circumscribed around it intersects the line $c$, in addition to the point $C$, at some point $D$. Then, based on the property of inscribed angles, we have $\angle ADC = \angle ABC = 60^\circ$.

The following construction follows from this. From an arbitrary point $D$ of line $c$ we draw rays that form angles $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ with line $c$. Let one of the rays intersect line $a$ at point $A$ and line $b$ at point $B_1$, and the other ray intersect line $b$ at point $B$. Then the segment $AB$ will be the side of the triangle $ABC$.

Let us plot on the line $c$ the segment $DC$ equal to the segment $AB_1$, so that the points $C$ and $A$ lie on the same side of the line $BD$. Then the point $C$ is a triangle of triangle $ABC$, since triangles $ABB_1$ and $BCD$ are equal, which means that $AB = BC$ and $\angle ABC = 60^\circ$.

Let's consider another difficult problem, in which the most difficult thing to solve is to guess how to describe a circle around the squares.

Example 6. On sides AC and BC of triangle ABC, squares ACA₁A₂ and BCB₁B₂ are constructed outside it. Prove that lines AB₁, A₁B and A₂B₂ intersect at one point, and determine the angles between these lines.

Solution. If ∠ACB = 90°, then the validity of the theorem is obvious. Lines AB₁ and A₁B are redefined and each of them intersects line A₂B₂ at point C at an angle of 45° (Fig. 6).

If ∠ACB ≠ 90°, then the circles circumscribed about the squares have, in addition to the point C, another common point C₁, which turns out to be the intersection point of the lines AB₁, A₁B and A₂B₂ (Fig. 7). Indeed, ∠A₂C₁C = 90°, like a hanging one, resting on a semicircle. Similarly ∠CC₁B₂ = 90°. Therefore, ∠A₂C₁C + ∠CC₁B₂ = 180°, i.e. rays C₁A₂ and C₁B₂ form a straight line.

In the same way, it is proved that the point C₁ lies on the lines AB₁ and A₁B.

Considering the obtained inscribed angles, we find that the straight lines AB₁ and A₁B are mutually redefined and each of these straight lines makes an angle of 45° with the straight line A₂B₂.
