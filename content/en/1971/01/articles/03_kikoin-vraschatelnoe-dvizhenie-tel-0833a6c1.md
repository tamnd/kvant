It is well known that any body can perform two types of movement - translational and healing. The first of them is studied in sufficient detail in the school physics course. But the medical movement was “unlucky.” Due to lack of time, very little attention is paid to it. Meanwhile, the healing movement is found in both nature and technology no less often than the progressive one. In many cases, the actual motion of bodies is a combination of both types of motion. In other cases, translational movement or even resting bodies have healing details. This primarily includes all devices with wheels - from wheelbarrows and carts to airplanes and helicopters. Just try to find a machine that doesn’t have anything in it! Such a common device as a watch is, one might say, “chocked” with wheels interlocking with each other.

Rotational motion is not uncommon in nature. It is enough to recall the complex healing movements of the planets, their satellites, the healing of the Sun and, probably, other stars. Let us note by the way that when applied to planets, the word “rotation” is used in relation to two different types of their movements. They say, for example, that the Earth is healed around the Sun, but they also talk about the Earth being healed around its axis. So, are these the same movements? Can it be said that both are caused by the same principles? It is known that the Earth moves around the Sun along a trajectory close to a circle because it is affected by the force of gravity towards the Sun. But can it be said that the same or a similar force forces the Earth to make healing movements around its axis? Obviously not. In fact, the Earth's movements can be considered as a combination of translational and linear motion - translational motion around the Sun and linear motion around an axis passing through both earth's poles.

This article talks about the medical movement of bodies, what distinguishes it from translational movement, and how it is similar to it.

Cinematika of the medical movement

First of all, let’s fill the reader with what they already know about the medical movement from the school physics course. Let's start with a definition.

Rotational movement of the body is the type of movement in which all points of the body move in circles, the centers of which lie on the same straight line - the axis of healing.

If the axis of healing is fixed, then the body as a whole does not move, and the only thing that changes over time is the angle of rotation, which here plays the same role as the movement of some point of the body during its forward movement.

When a body is rotated through an angle φ, each point passes through a certain arc of a circle. If we denote the length of the arc by S, then there is a simple relationship between the values ​​of S and φ

$$S = \varphi r,$$

where $r$ is the distance of the point from the rotation axis. Points located at different distances $r$ from the axis of rotation describe arcs of different lengths, but the angle $\varphi$ is the same for the entire body.

The task of the mechanics of rotational movement is to be able to achieve a certain angle of rotation of the body at any time, if the initial angle of rotation is known. To do this, you need to know the rapid change in the angle of rotation, that is, the corner speed, which is usually denoted by the letter $\omega$. For a body rotating at a constant angular velocity,

$$\omega = \frac{\varphi - \varphi_0}{t},$$

where $\varphi - \varphi_0$ is the change in the rotation angle over a period of time of $t$ seconds.

If the angular velocity is constant, then the value of the angle of rotation at any time can be easily found:

$$\varphi = \varphi_0 + \omega t;$$

if the initial rotation angle $\varphi_0$ is zero, then

$$\varphi = \omega t.$$

This expression is similar to the formula $S = vt$ for uniform translational motion. Consequently, the angular velocity in rotational motion plays the same role as linear velocity $v$ for translational motion.

Angular velocity, like linear velocity, may not be constant. Then, in order to find the speed at any time, you need to know the angular acceleration, which, like linear acceleration $a$, is defined as a rapid change in angular velocity. In uniform rotation

$$\alpha = \frac{\omega - \omega_0}{t},$$

where $\omega$ and $\omega_0$ are the values ​​of angular velocity at the end and at the beginning of a long time period of $t$ seconds.

It is easy to find the connection between angular velocity and acceleration and their linear “twins” $v$ and $a$. Since $\omega = \frac{\varphi}{t}$ and $\varphi = \frac{S}{r}$, then $\omega = \frac{S}{rt}$.

But $\frac{S}{t} = v$ therefore

$$\omega = \frac{v}{r} \text{ и } v = \omega r.$$

In the same way, since $\alpha = \frac{\omega - \omega_0}{t}$, and $\omega = \frac{v}{r}$ and $\omega_0 = \frac{v_0}{r}$, then $\alpha = \frac{v - v_0}{rt}$. But $\frac{v - v_0}{t}$ is equal to the linear acceleration of $a$, so

$$\alpha = \frac{a}{r} \text{ и } a = \alpha r.$$

Thus, the numerical values ​​of the linear quantities $S$, $v$ and $a$ for each point of the rotating body are equal to the corresponding values ​​of the angular quantities (they relate not to individual points, but to the entire body), multiplied by the radius $r$ of rotation of this point.

Dynamics of rotational motion

The dynamics of translational motion studies, as is known, the reasons for the appearance of accelerations in bodies and makes it possible to determine their magnitudes and directions. Rotational dynamics do the same for angular acceleration. If the acceleration of the translational motion of a body is caused by a force applied to it, then the reason for the acceleration of the angular acceleration of the body is the torque of the force applied to it. Readers are familiar with it from the 8th or 9th grade physics course. Let us recall that the numerical value of the moment of force about any axis is equal to the product of the force applied to the body by the length of the perpendicular drawn from the axis to the line of action of the force. For example, if a force $F$ is applied to the rim of a flat disk, shown in figure 2, the vector of which lies in the plane of the disk, then the moment of this force relative to the axis perpendicular to the plane of the disk and passing through the point $O$ is equal to $Fr$. If the axis passed not through the point $O$, but through the point $O_1$, then the moment of the same force relative to the new axis would be different: it would be equal to $Fr_1$. If the same force is applied as shown in figure 3, then its moment relative to the axis passing through point $O$ is equal to zero, since the line of action of the force passes through this point. Such a force cannot cause rotation around an axis and cannot change angular acceleration. But the same force can cause rotation of the disk around an axis passing through point $O_1$.

The moment of force is also a vector quantity. And the direction of the force moment vector is determined using the same expression. Namely, we agreed to consider the direction of the moment of force to be that in which the gimlet (screw) moves along the axis if its hand rotates in the same way as a body would rotate under the influence of a force applied to it. For example, the vector moment of force $F$ relative to the axis passing through the point $O$, at figure 2 is directed perpendicular to the drawing plane “from us”.

Momentum, angular momentum

So, for rotational motion, Newton’s second law is written as $M = I\alpha$, where $M$ is the moment of force, $I$ is the moment of inertia and $\alpha$ is the angular acceleration of the body.

No angular acceleration is expressed by the formula

$$\alpha = \frac{\omega - \omega_0}{t}.$$

Therefore, the formula of Newton’s second law can be written as

$$M = \frac{I\omega - I\omega_0}{t}.$$

In this form, this formula means that the moment of force is equal to the change in value $I\omega$ unit time. But, as is known, Newton’s second law can be written in the form

$$F = \frac{mv - mv_0}{t},$$

where $mv$ is a quantity called the momentum or impullas of the body. It is clear that implulys $mv$ also has an analogue in the rotational motion diaphragm and this analogue is the value $I\omega$. This follows from the fact that the moment of inertia $I$ plays the same role in rotational motion as mass does in translational motion, and the angular velocity $\omega$ replaces the linear velocity $v$. The quantity $L = I\omega$ is therefore called the angular momentum of the body or the angular momentum. This is also a vector quantity (the same as $\omega$, $F$, $\alpha$, $mv$, $M$), directed in the same way as the angular velocity vector. Similar to the other values ​​we have given that relate to rotational motion, the momentum of the impullas is different relative to different axes of rotation.

If the body has such a shape or size or such a radius of rotation that we can talk about the rotation of a point (for example, a ball on a thin rod, a planet as it rotates around the Sun), then the moment of impullas is easy to associate with impullas. Indeed, the moment of inertia $I$ is equal to $mr^2$, and the angular velocity $\omega$ is equal to $\frac{v}{r}$. That's why

$$L = I\omega = \frac{mr^2v}{r} = rmv.$$

The moment of impuls of a point, therefore, is equal in magnitude to the product of the radius $r$ of the circle along which the point moves, and the impuls $mv$. It is clear that for a body of arbitrary shape such a simple correspondence is impossible.

Kinetic energy of rotating bodies

A rotating body is a moving body, since all its parts, except the point located on the rotation axis, move. Therefore it has kinetic energy. To express the kinetic energy, let's start again with the rotation of a small ball on a thin rod, that is, with the rotation of a point. In this case, the kinetic energy can be written in the general form

$$K = \frac{mv^2}{2},$$

where $m$ is the mass of the ball, and $v$ is its linear speed. But since $v = \omega r$, the expression for $K$ can be written in the form

$$K = \frac{m\omega^2r^2}{2}.$$
