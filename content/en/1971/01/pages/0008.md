Now all our difficulties will immediately disappear. In the formula

$$M = mr^2\alpha$$

$m$ is the mass of the ball, and $r$ is the distance from the ball to the axis of rotation. Since for a given ball and rod neither $m$ nor $r$ change, the product $mr^2$ is a constant quantity and can be denoted by one letter. Let us choose the letter $I$ for this value. Then the formula of Newton's second law takes the form

$$M = I\alpha.$$

The similarity between this formula and the formula is striking

$$F = ma.$$

Only the force $F$ is replaced by the moment of force, and together with the linear acceleration $a$ the formula includes the angular acceleration $\alpha$, as it should be, since the furnace is not about translational, but about rotational motion. The role of mass in this formula is played by a completely new, unusual quantity, equal to the product of the mass by the square of its distance to the axis of rotation.

Just as the acceleration of a translationally moving body depends, in addition to force, also on the mass of the body being accelerated, so the acceleration of a rotating body depends not only on the moment of force, but also on the value $mr^2$, which is, so to speak, the rotational “double” of the mass. This quantity is called the moment of inertia of the body relative to the axis. For a rotational movement, it is not only mass that is important. It is also important how far this mass is removed from the axis of rotation. It is clear that a small mass at a large distance from the axis of rotation can receive the same angular acceleration at a given moment of force as a large mass located near the axis.

All this applies as if to a ball rotating on a thin rod. But in reality, the formula turns out to be true even when a disk, wheel, cylinder, and in general any “real” solid body, which also has a certain moment of inertia, rotates. To obtain the moment of inertia of a body relative to each axis, you need to mentally divide it into many small parts (in the limit of infinitely small), for each of them determine the product of its mass by the square of the distance from this axis and add all the products. This will be the moment of inertia of the whole body. It is clear that the moment of inertia of a body is greater, the greater the mass of those parts of it that are located away from the axis. In other words, the moment of inertia of a body depends on how the mass of the body is distributed over its volume. For different axes the moment of inertia of the same body will be different.
