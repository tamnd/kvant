Then the speed of the planet will be dimensionless. Speed ​​is a number that shows how many times $v_0$ is contained in the speed of the planet:

$$\beta_1 = \frac{v_1}{v_0} \quad \text{и} \quad \beta_2 = \frac{v_2}{v_0}.$$

According to the previous speeds $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are related by the relation

$$\beta_1 = \frac{1}{\beta_2}.$$

What does it mean? If a particle moves at some point in the orbit with a speed of $\beta$, then at a point symmetric relative to the center of the orbit, its speed will be equal to $\frac{1}{\beta}$

We described the trajectory of the planet, specifying its position (coordinates) at each moment in time. You can do it differently - set the speed of the planet. The curve that we get in this case is called the velocity graph (we can say that this is a trajectory in velocity space). Since the product of the segments $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ has been constructed, the speed graph of the planet is a circle (Fig. 8) (remember the theorem: “A point is given inside a circle. Then for all chords passing through this point, the product of the lengths of the segments on which this point divides the chords are the same”).

If we replace the speed $\beta$ with $\frac{1}{\beta}$ at each point of the godgraph, then the godgraph will not change, but simply the surface speed at $180^\circ$. This property is called relative surface motion invariance.

If the planet's trajectory is circular, then $\beta = 1$ (prove it!). In this case, the godgraph is a circle with its center at the origin.

Circulation period

In order to calculate the orbital period - the time of a complete revolution of a planet around Coln or an artificial satellite around the Earth, it is necessary to calculate the time of passage of each section of the trajectory (separately, since the speed changes all the time) and then sum it over all sections. Of course, this cannot be done simply, but all difficulties can be circumvented.

From Kepler’s second law we know that in time $\Delta t$ the radius vector of the planet “sweeps” an area equal to $\frac{L}{2m} \Delta t$ (recall that $L = \text{const}$). This means that the period of revolution can be calculated by dividing the area of ​​the ellipse by the constant speed of “sweeping”

$$T = \frac{S_{\text{эллипса}}}{L/2m}.$$

Since the ellipse is obtained from a circle sloping in one direction $\frac{b}{a}$ times$^*$), then the area of ​​the ellipse is equal to

$$S_{\text{эллипса}} = S_{\text{крута}} \cdot \frac{b}{a} = \pi a^2 \frac{b}{a} = \pi ab.$$

From these formulas we get

$$T = \frac{2\pi ab}{L}.$$

But $b = \frac{L}{\sqrt{2Em}}$, therefore

$$T = \pi a \sqrt{\frac{2m}{E}}$$

$^*$) See the article by I. N. Bronstein “Ellipse” («Kvant» No. 9, 1970).
