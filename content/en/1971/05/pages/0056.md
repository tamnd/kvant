Let us assume that the body can only move downwards along the plane, since of the two forces acting along it, $P\sin\alpha$ is greater than $F\sin\alpha$. The friction force, therefore, can only be directed upward along the inclined plane.

In our example, we must, however, allow for one more possibility: the force $F$ may be so great that the body will not slide along the plane at all, but will turn away from it. However, comparing the “pressing” force $P\cos\alpha=17,4$ and force $F\cos\alpha=8,7$ we see that the first is greater than the second, and therefore the body cannot be torn off the plane.

It must be said that the difference between these forces $P\cos\alpha-F\cos\alpha=8,7\pi$ gives the desired pressure force of the body on the plane and the support reaction $N$, equal in magnitude to it.

Let us now write Newton's second law, remembering that forces directed opposite to the positive direction of the axis are considered negative. For the $y$ axis we obtain.

$$F\cos\alpha+N-P\cos\alpha=0$$

(1)

(there is no acceleration in this direction, therefore the sum of forces is zero). And for the axis $x$ we get:

$$P\sin\alpha-F\sin\alpha-f_{\text{тр}}=ma.$$

(2)

In problems of this type, mistakes are sometimes made when determining the magnitude of the friction force. To get used to problems with horizontal moving bodies, they often, without thinking twice, calculate the friction force as the product of the friction coefficient and the weight of the body.

In fact, $f_{\text{тр}}=k\cdot N$, where $N$ is the normal pressure force (support reaction), that is, the force acting on the body perpendicular to the sliding plane (or equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force of pressure of the body on the plane).

In our problem, we need to take into account both the angle of inclination of the plane and the force $F$, which somewhat reduces the force of normal pressure:

$$f_{\text{тр}}=k(P\cos\alpha-F\cos\alpha)=k(P-F)\cos\alpha=0,2(20-10)0,87=1,74\pi$$

Now from equation (2) it is easy to find the acceleration of the body:

$$a=\frac{(P-F)\sin\alpha-f_{\text{тр}}}{m}=\frac{(20-10)0,5-1,74}{2}=1,63\pi/cek^2.$$

3. The rocket is equipped with a spring scale on which a block rests. Before departure the scales show 10 $N$. In flight, the scales first show 30 $N$, then 10 $N$, 5 $N$, 0 $N$. What can be said about the acceleration and direction of the rocket's motion at these moments in time?

Let's look at the strength first.

Two forces act on the block: gravity $P$ and support reaction $N$ (Fig. 12). When the rocket has no vertical acceleration (for example, before takeoff), these forces are equal. In our case $P=N=10$ $N$.

If the rocket (and therefore the block) moves accelerated, upward, to the right, then these equal forces must be directed upward ($N>P$). If the acceleration is directed downward, then $P>N$.

What do the scales show? Of course, not gravity, as is sometimes thought. They all record the force with which the block presses on them. This force is applied to the scales and, in accordance with Newton’s third law, is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the reaction of the support $N$ applied to the block
