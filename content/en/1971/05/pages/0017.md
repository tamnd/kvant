Please note that in formula (10) we have chosen the constant $C$ so that the potential energy vanishes at infinity. Why is this convenient and when? Let us remember that potential energy characterizes the action of bodies on each other—interaction; if at infinity the force becomes zero, then the further the bodies are separated from each other, the weaker their mutual influence. This is precisely why the choice of constant in our and similar ($F(\infty) = 0$) cases is accordingly.

From formula (10) it follows that the type of potential energy significantly depends on the sign of $\alpha$ (see Fig. 106). If $\alpha > 0$, then the force is $F = \frac{\alpha}{r^2} > 0$, and we are dealing with repulsion (like charges, $\alpha = q_1 q_2 > 0$). If $\alpha < 0$, then the force is $F = -\frac{|\alpha|}{r^2} < 0$, which corresponds to attraction (opposite charges, $\alpha = -q_1 |q_2|$). Here, please, it is worth returning once again to the analogy with a sled going down an ice slide. Notice that if you put a sled on a slide that has a potential energy interwash profile, they point in the direction of the force. Such a set of rules makes it possible to immediately determine whether attraction or repulsion is taking place. Indeed, if the sank is shown to the point $r = 0$ (for example, the lower curve at Fig. 106), then attraction takes place, if on the contrary (for example, the upper curve), then repulsion occurs.
