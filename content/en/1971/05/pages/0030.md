This example is also used to obtain an image of an impossible cube. If you make a cage like the one shown in figure 3, then, positioning it so that the back edges of the cage cover the slits in the front ones, you will see an impossible cube: the back edges of the cage will seem to be pushed forward. (A photograph can also be obtained from this position.) The physiologist Gibson, using this idea, came up with a number of interesting illusions in which he used rectangular cardboard with slits in some of them, so that the most distant of the rectangular ones would appear to be located in the foreground. On figure4 the two sets of squares, although seemingly identical, are arranged differently in space, and in the third row the jack is actually close to the six, and the cards are therefore the same size. Such a photograph can be obtained by making appropriate slits in a nearby card and in the middle rectangular column, through which you can see the more distant six (Fig. 5).

Another series of the same kind of optical illusions can be obtained using the so-called artificial Ames room, the geometry of which is shown in figure 6. This room is made somewhat unusually. The floor in it is inclined and rises to the right of the observer, the rear wall moves away as one moves to the left, the windows on this wall are of different sizes and are shaped like a trapezoid.
