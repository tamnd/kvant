WRITTEN EXAM
IN MATHEMATICS
AT MSU MEKHMAT IN 1970

N. N. Kolesnikov

In «Kvant» No. 2 for 1970, an article by N. S. Bakhvalov and N. N. Kuznetsov about the written exam in mathematics at the mexmate of Moscow State University in 1969 was published. Without repeating the comments made there about the nature of this exam, I would like to emphasize some of its features in 1970.

The tasks proposed to applicants once again confirm the position that their successful solution was achieved by any applicant, according to the conditions of the school program, and not just by a graduate of mathematics schools. You will see that these problems do not contain “pitfalls” or “traps”, but require a clear understanding of the main sections of the mathematics program for applicants to universities and certain skills in technical calculations and solving simple equations, inequalities and geometric problems.

Naturally, such “standardization” of problems implies increased requirements for the evaluation of their solutions, requirements for the ability to bring a solution to the end, and not limit oneself to hints at a solution. A satisfactory mark (tri) was given in 1970 for completing the correct solution of any two or three problems, good (four) for any four problems, excellent (five) for five problems. 4 astronomical hours were allotted to solve the problem (a set of five problems - variants).

Let's move on to analyzing one of the options for the written exam in mathematics, briefly focusing on the most typical errors encountered in the works of those admitted to the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics.

How much time does racer A spend on a lap?

3. This problem can be solved in different ways by introducing certain unknowns.

Let $t_A, t_B$ and $t_C$ be the times that racers $A$, $B$ and $C$ spend on a lap, respectively (we take the length of the path to be one). Then the speeds of each of them will be $\frac{1}{t_A}, \frac{1}{t_B}, \frac{1}{t_C}$.

Note that at any but identical absolute speeds, the distance between the riders would remain unchanged throughout the entire movement. The distance between the racers is reduced due to the excess speed of one over the speed of the other. The speed of the racer $A$ relative to $B$ is $\frac{1}{t_A} - \frac{1}{t_B}$, $A$ relative to $C$ is $\frac{1}{t_A} - \frac{1}{t_C}$, and the speed of $B$ relative to $C$ is $\frac{1}{t_B} - \frac{1}{t_C}$.

Now it is easy to translate the conditions of the problem into equation language. Since the rider $A$ first agreed to $B$ when the latter completed his first lap, then $A$ agreed to $B$ in $t_B$ hours. But for this $A$ to cover $1/3$ of the circle with a relative speed of $\frac{1}{t_A} - \frac{1}{t_B}$, this gives the equation

$$\left(\frac{1}{t_A} - \frac{1}{t_B}\right)t_B = \frac{1}{3}.$$

The initial distance between racers $A$ and $C$ was $2/3$, racer $A$ covered it at a relative speed of $\frac{1}{t_A} - \frac{1}{t_C}$ in $t_B + \frac{1}{6}$ hours; we got the second equation:

$$\left(\frac{1}{t_A} - \frac{1}{t_C}\right)\left(t_B + \frac{1}{6}\right) = \frac{2}{3}.$$

In addition, it is known that

$$t_B + \frac{1}{24} = t_C.$$

From equation (1) it immediately follows that $t_B = \frac{4}{3}t_A$, and from equation (3) $t_C = \frac{4}{3}t_A + \frac{1}{24}$. Excluding now from

5. Let $B'$ be the base of the perpendicular dropped from the point $B$ to the plane $P$ (Fig. 4). According to the conditions

$$\Rightarrow BCB' = \varphi, OB = OA = r, CA = 2r, \\

Finding the length of the segment $AB$ can be found, for example, from the right triangle $AB'B$, the sides of which are easy to calculate. Note that $CB = CA = 2r$ are segments of tangents to the ball drawn from one point. It follows that

$$BB' = CB \sin \varphi = 2r \sin \varphi.$$

Side $AB'$ of triangle $AB'B$ is equal to the perpendicular $OO'$ dropped from $O$ to $BB'$ ($OA \parallel BB'$, as perpendiculars to one plane; $BB' \perp AB'$ since $AB'$ lies in the plane $P$, perpendicular to $BB'$; $OO' \perp BB'$ by construction; therefore $OO'B'A$ is straight). $OO'$ is from right triangle $OO'B'$:

$$OO' = \sqrt{r^2 - (BO')^2},$$

$$BO' = |BB' - B'O'| = |BB' - OA| = |2r \sin \varphi - r|,$$

$$OO' = AB' = \sqrt{\sin \varphi - \sin^2 \varphi} \cdot 2r.$$

Finally, $AB$ is found from triangle $AB'B$:

$$AB = \sqrt{(AB')^2 + (B'B)^2} = 2r \sqrt{\sin \varphi}.$$

We are now convinced that the difficulties in solving this stereometric problem (as well as others proposed in 1970) do not exceed those encountered in solving school problems. However, every year a lot of applicants for the written exam in mathematics simply do not attend to solving stereometric problems, apparently believing that they are inherently impossible to solve.

Among the errors encountered in the work of the applicants, it should be noted that it is not clear what an angle of a straight line with a plane is. Thus, in the problem analyzed, this angle is often taken to be $\Rightarrow BCA$.

It is also common when deciding to use the conditions that $\Rightarrow CB'A$ is direct, although in the general case this is not at all true.
