At the political school, the Sorbonne, the College de France, he listened to lectures by famous scientists: Cauchy, Fourier, Laplace, Monge, Poisson, Legacdre, Sturam, Poncelet, Binet and others, who were pioneering new paths in mathematical analysis, mathematical physics and mechanics. Having studied the conditions and results achieved by the French mathematical school, Ostrogradsky himself began to deal with important and pressing issues of that time, often the work of his Parisian colleagues. In 1826, the Russian scientist presented his first scientific work to the Paris Academy of Sciences - “Metoir on the propagation of waves in a cylindrical pool”, highly appreciated by Ksshi and published in the proceedings of the Academy. The scientific significance of this work can be judged by the fact that back in 1816 the Academy announced a special competition to solve it.

In 1824-1827, Ostrogradsky presented several more themes. These works enhanced the scientific reputation of the young scientist and won him the friendship and respect of many French mathematicians.

But Mikhail Vasilyevich is inexorably drawn to his homeland, where his successes were well known. It is not for nothing that young people who went to study abroad were given parting words by their relatives and friends with the words: “Become Ostrograd.”

In 1828 he left for Russia. This trip was difficult. On the way, he was robbed, and he had to walk from Frankfurt am Main to St. Petersburg. The “Russian pedestrian”, which was also acquired from abroad, looked very suspicious, and the momentary authorities, which I am currently restoring, established secret police surveillance over it. Ostrogradsky probably did not know about this until the end of his days.

Immediately after Ostrogradsky’s arrival in St. Petersburg, his fruitful work at the Academy of Sciences and numerous teaching activities began. The Academy of Sciences highly appreciated Ostrogradsky's scientific work: in August 1830 he was elected extraordinary, and a year later - ordinary academician in applied mathematics. From that time on, his life was full of creative successes, and his activities were marked by the conferment of a number of honorary scientific titles. Thus, in 1834 he was elected a member of the American Academy of Sciences, in 1841 - a member of the Turin Academy, in 1853 - a member of the Roman Lynch Academy and in 1856 - a corresponding member of the Paris Academy.

Ostrogradsky's scientific interests were determined early, even before leaving for Paris. In an announcement to the council of Kharkov University, Ostrogradsky wrote back in 1820 that he wanted to “improve himself in the sciences, applied mathematics.” And indeed, he devoted much of his work to mathematical physics and mechanics, laying the foundation of these sciences together with Laplace, Poisson, Fourier, Cauchy, Jacobi, and Hamilton.

Ostrogradsky wrote 15 works on mathematical physics. Most of them relate to problems of body propagation, control theory and hydrodynamics. His work on the theory of tellots was of greatest scientific importance. These studies, in addition to containing important results, relate directly to the theory of body propagation, and have enormous general mathematical significance, since, on the one hand, they laid the foundations for a number of important theories that are developing in our time, and on the other hand, they obtained theorems that are one of the central results of mathematical analysis.
