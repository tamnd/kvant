Dobino "two differences". The same, but neighbors in the chain must differ in two characteristics (Fig.1).
Next, you can try playing “tri differences”.

Treasure hunters. Two players each think of one cube (“treasure”) and try to find the treasure hidden by their opponent, pointing alternately at one or another cube. The opponent only reports how many differences they have from what was planned. The one who quickly agrees wins.

Three villages, two villages. Games that develop the concept of intersection of a set. “Two villages”: draw two circles on the floor - “villages” - and write names on them, for example, “red squares” on one and “small fat ones” on the other. You need to place all the red squares in the first circle, and all the small thick cubes (Fig.2) in the second circle.
We need to tell you how to do this. A similar game “tri villages” is even more interesting.

Names of the villages. One player places the cubes into “three villages”, but does not write their names on the circles, and the other must guess them (Fig.3).

Games for seniors

These games can be played by two people; the one who needs fewer questions to answer correctly wins.

Selection rules. One of the players comes up with some rule for selecting cubes, for example: “select only yellow ones,” and the other takes random cubes and receives the answer “yes” when the cube falls under the intended rule, and “no” otherwise. You need to guess the rule. The rules gradually become more complex, creating sums and intersections of features.

Settlement of villages. One player comes up with the name of the villages, and the other tries to populate them with cubes, receiving only “yes” or “no” instructions from the first.

Treasure dumpers (folded version). To guess the “treasure”, 3-4 “test” cubes are immediately laid out on the table and you get an answer as to how many signs of each test differ from the intended one. “Treasure” must then be indicated the first time.

Guessing the law. They come up with a rule according to which you can lay out a goal from the cubes. For example, “every even cube is large” or “after the squares there are thin circles.” The guesser begins to build the goal scientifically, cube by cube, each time receiving the answer “yes” when he places them without breaking the rule, and “no” when the rule is broken. We need to guess the law.
