A small horizontal mirror will cast light into the eyes of observed $O$ only if the distances from it to the observed and to the source are the same (at point $M$). If the mirror is tilted at an angle $\alpha$ to the observed side, then in order for the reflected light to enter the eyes, it must be slightly away from the observed one (point $N$). The mirror, inclined at an angle $\alpha$ in the opposite direction, should be at point $N'$.

The inclined positions of the mirror are similar to the extreme positions of the waves, at which the light reflected from them still enters our eye. The distance between $N$ and $N'$ therefore determines the length of the light path. At all points between $N$ and $N'$ we will find a section of waves that are inclined enough to reflect the rays into our eye.

Let us now consider the angles between the rays of light. From the drawing it is clear that $\beta + \alpha = \gamma + \delta$, $\beta - \alpha = \varepsilon = \delta$, hence $\gamma = \alpha + \beta - (\beta - \alpha) = 2\alpha$. Thus we come to the conclusion that the angle at which we see the large spot of light is simply equal to the angle between the two observed slopes. It is not difficult to calculate the linear size of the major axis of the spot $NN'$.

The short axis of the reflected light spot is easily found in a similar way. If you move the mirror from the point $M$ in a direction perpendicular to $NN'$, then in order for the reflected light to enter the eyes, the mirror must be rotated through a certain angle around an axis parallel to $NN'$ (Fig. 2). Assuming that the limiting angle of rotation of the mirror is still equal to $\alpha$, we find that the width of the light strip is $PP' = 2htg\alpha$, and, therefore, the short axis subtends the angle $\beta = \frac{2htg\alpha}{\sqrt{l^2 + h^2}}$.

The ratio of the two visible stripes of the spot will be equal to $\beta/2\alpha$ or, assuming that the spot is not large and the angle $\alpha$ is small, equal to $\beta/2\alpha = \sin\omega$, where $\omega$ is the angle at which we look at the water.

The smaller this angle, the more elongated the spot. If the gaze glides over the surface, the spot of light will stretch and narrow indefinitely.

When observing light paths on the surface of the sea, the angle $\omega$ is usually small - the light paths reach the horizontal (see photograph on figure 3), so we can only talk about the width of the path. And although the formulas we obtained are not literally applied in this case, using them, you can not only qualitatively determine the origin of the roads, but also understand the dependence of their width on the wind force and the height of the Sun above the horizon: with an increase in $\alpha$ and $h$, the width of the road returns.
