We will only point out that the peculiarities of metals are declared by the weak connection of some electrons with atoms and the easily mobile “gas” of free electrons formed due to this. Therefore, the movement of electrons in the field of an electromagnetic wave in metals is completely different from the movement in dielectrics, where each electron “knows” its atom. Free electrons easily absorb and reflect back most of the light falling on the surface of the metal. This is why metals reflect radio waves and even visible light well. Typically, electromagnetic waves passing inside the metal are strongly absorbed. It is interesting that sometimes the opposite picture is observed: substances that absorb light at Fig. 8. If the lenses of binoculars or cameras are biased, this means that light is reaching the eye or photographic plate: part of it is reflected. They remove shine by applying a very thin and transparent film to the surface of the lens. Its thickness and refractive index are selected so that the rays reflected from the two surfaces of the film (Fig. 8) are out of phase by 180° and, interfering, cancel each other out. This cannot be achieved for the entire region of visible light, but it can be achieved for that wavelength to which photographic film or the eye is most sensitive.
