How far can you travel by car, or a story about how instant success can turn into protracted failure

What can a machine do? She knows how to perform simple, mechanical operations: compare the contents of two cells of her memory, add, subtract, multiply, etc. And she does not care whether she is implementing a translation program or reading the trajectory of a satellite. But a machine can do a lot of such operations, and by combining them in different ways, the programmer can force the machine to perform some kind of operations, purposeful operations.

What operations must a machine perform to translate text from one language to another? First, she must “read” it. It's not that difficult to do. The text is entered in encoded form into memory cells, and the machine can look into its own memory. The machine must then recognize the words. This is done like this: each word (from one space to another) is compared with a dictionary that must be stored in the machine's memory. The machine must determine what form each word is in (morphological analysis) in order to then understand the relationships of all the words in a sentence in relation to each other (syntactic analysis). What then? Then you need to translate. Actually, the human translator continues all the described operations instantly and unconsciously, and the translation process itself begins from this moment. It is necessary to find words in the language in which the text is translated (this language is called the output language), equivalent to the words of the language from which the translation is carried out (input language). Then put these words into appropriate forms, that is, arrange them according to the law of the morphological and syntactic structure of the target language. Every step here brings difficulties. We'll have to figure them out.

The first machine translation enthusiasts expect a generally capable translation (well, maybe with a few exceptions). Let's take an English phrase:

I know his daughter.

The literal translation of this phrase turns out to be correct:

I know his daughter.

True, the English verb know is conjugated differently than the Russian verb to know: currently only in the third person singular it has the ending -s, and in all other persons it has the general form - know. How then can the machine understand that in the Russian form this verb should be given the form know, but not know and not knowing? This cannot be revealed from the dictionary entry for know, no matter what is written down: one form of know or all possible forms: know, know, knowe, we know, know and, finally, know (me). The clue, however, is contained in the English word I - i, which directly indicates the first person singular. This means that you need to put rules into the machine, according to which in order to translate the verb know (as well as other English verbs at present) the correct form of the Russian verb, it must take into account the subject, that is, translating each word separately using only a dictionary between language correspondences is not enough for correct translation.
