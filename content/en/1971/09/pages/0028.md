But there are many more languages, and not every pair of languages ​​can use one translator. It is clear that with the advent of powerful electronic computing technology, the hope began to shift the burden of translation onto the machine.

The beginning of this country's history

The idea of ​​automatic (machine) translation captured the masses (we are talking, of course, about the masses of scientists) in a consistent period, at the dawn of cybernetics, in the first years of the electronic era.

1946 There is a discussion between the American scientists W. Weaver and A. D. Booth about the limits of the use of automatic digital computers. The first believes that the basic "elements of language" can be discovered using decryption devices developed during the war to reveal the secret codes of the enemy, and machine translation can be built on the basis of these "elements". The other is more insular: it speaks of the fundamental possibility of only the last translation, and even then with a very small dictionary - the amount of computer memory was still very small. (The following year, Booth and his colleague Britten developed a program for such a translation.) But Weaver is optimistic. He shares correspondence with the “father of cybernetics” Norbert Wiener. Wiener was wary of Weaver's idea and pointed out the great difficulties that stand in the way of exploration. Weaver is not discouraged. His credo: “A sentence in one language is simply an encrypted phrase in another language.” Let's start decoding the machine. However, Weaver did not pay attention to one extremely important circumstance: with the help of formal decryption operations, easily carried out in a machine, it is possible to reach only the first analogous text in the original language. But the machine cannot understand the text in this language: after all, you need to know this language. During the First World War, codebreakers who famously deciphered all the most secret codes of American radio operators broke their teeth on one such code, which turned out to be simply plain text in an Indian language unknown to them. Thus, scientists have not yet had any information that natural human language can be “deciphered.”
