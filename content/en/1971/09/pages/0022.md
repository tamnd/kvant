S. A. Kheifets

The craving for something shiny is hidden in the depths of consciousness. A child who sees the light for the first time is drawn to the bright; the natives trade both food and water for shiny trinkets. People call a gifted, deep mind “brilliant.” We will not be interested in whether this attention to the shiny is connected with atavistic memories of the burning fire or with hypnosis, we will try to figure out why the shine appears - be it the shine of gold, amaz or cat's eyes in the dark.

We judge the rage of an object by the amount of light that was reflected from it and into our eyes *). It is clear that this amount depends on the proportion of reflected light and its direction. A white sheet of paper reflects better than black, and a mirror surface reflects better than a matte one.

The direction of light reflected by a surface is determined by the well-known law: the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. And the energy of reflected light also depends on the refractive index of the medium $n$. For a glass plate, the proportion of reflected energy will be approximately twice as large, since part of the transmitted light will be reflected from the second surface of the glass and return back. When the angle of incidence approaches $90^\circ$—a grazing ray—almost all the light is reflected (the reflection coefficient approaches 100%). Further, very black bodies, such as graphite, soot, velvet, begin to shine if you look at them at a small angle to the surface.

When light is normally incident on a glass plate, the fraction of reflected energy is close to 8.6%. Is it a little or a lot? It turns out, not much. Fresh snow reflects 80-85% of the incident light, and clouds reflect the same amount.
