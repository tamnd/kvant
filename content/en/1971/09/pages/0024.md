As the angle of incidence β increases, the intensity of the refracted beam decreases, and at $\sin \beta = \frac{1}{n}$ the refracted beam disappears. In this case, all the incident light is reflected from the boundaries - the so-called total internal reflection occurs. For glass $n = 1,52$, so that the beam, induzione at an angle to the normal $\beta \ge 36^\circ$, does not go out. For water $n = 1,33$, so that the fish can only see the sky through the “hole” with an opening angle of about 45°

The angle between the rays that entered the drop and those that exited is easy to determine if we take into account that the radius is perpendicular to the surface of the ball. If the incident rays are parallel, then, having passed through the drop, they begin to diverge. However, there is a minimum deflection angle. It is close to 42°. Near this direction, the refracted rays are thicker, that is, the bulk of the refracted rays go precisely in this direction. The dewdrop observed from this angle is quickly especially bright. At the same angle to the sun's rays a rainbow is visible.

Wherever there is a bright source of light (the Sun, a bright sky), you can always observe its spark-image in the dewdrop (Fig. 5).
