Author. Because I wanted to show: in non-relativistic quantum mechanics, the presence of “locked” electrons in the nucleus would lead to troubles. And this suggests that, they believe, they cannot be locked there. However, you are, of course, right: such reasoning is not proof.

Since we are talking about this, let's make a more accurate calculation. Using the uncertainty principles again, we obtain the order of the minimum impulse $p_{\min}$ of an electron “locked” in a nucleus of radius $R$, $p_{\min} \sim \frac{h}{R}$. According to the theory of relativity, $p = mv = \frac{m_0 v}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$ ($m_0$ is the rest mass of the particle, $v$ is its speed). “Our” impulse $p_{\min} \gg m_0 c$. This means that $v \approx c$ and $p \approx mc$ (m is the mass of the relativistic electron). This means that for an electron “locked” in the nucleus, $m \sim \frac{h}{R_c}$, which gives values ​​on the order of the mass of a proton at rest. Consequently, the proximity of the mass of the nucleus to the sum of the masses of only protons and neutrons means the absence of electrons “locked” in the nucleus: once they get into the nucleus, they are easily pulled out of there.

You can see the size of the region in which the particles are concentrated. This region can either strictly limit their movement, like the nucleus - the movement of protons and neutrons or atom - the movement of electrons, or be characteristic only from the point of view of the uncertainty principle.

Reader A. Petrov (Leningrad). If the average distance between particles is $a$, then $\Delta x \approx a$ and possibly even $\Delta x = L$, where $L$ is the size of the system. So, $p_{\min} \sim \frac{h}{L}$!

Author. Absolutely right. We estimate the characteristic energy, and it is determined by the average mass $a$. The smallest possible energy is $\frac{h^2}{2mL^2}$, and intermediate energy values ​​corresponding to $a < \Delta x < L$ are also possible.

Let me explain this remark in more detail. The average distance between electrons in a metal $a$ determines the characteristic distance in the uncertainty principle and the characteristic energy of zero-point vibrations, which we estimated. However, in principle, individual electrons can be in rare states, being at a distance from each other on the order of the size of the system $L$. In accordance with this, the minimum kinetic energy (zero-point energy), which has a small number of electrons, is of the order of $\frac{h^2}{2mL^2}$, that is, extremely small.

And here the cardial difference between Fermi and Bohemian particles is already evident.

Since basic particles can accumulate in unlimited quantities in any state, at very low temperatures they begin to accumulate precisely in a state with low energy - the so-called Bose-Einstein condensation begins in energy space.

What has been said is enough to obtain the basic characteristics of the Bose gas. Let's start with the distance of thermal radiation concentrated in the cavity of a heated body (and in equilibrium with this body). Since thermal radiation consists of quanta of electromagnetic waves (photons), the number of which per unit volume is determined by the temperature of the heated body emitting them, the only energy characteristic is temperature. Therefore, the average photon energy is on the order of $kT$. Here we are not given a distance stretching, but a warm stretching of energy. Let us adapt the uncertainty principle in a slightly different form by introducing the characteristic time $\Delta t$:

$$h \sim \Delta p \Delta x \sim \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} \Delta x \Delta t.$$

But the change in momentum over a given time is the product of the force and the time of its action: $\Delta p = F \Delta t$. This means that $\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} \Delta x = F \Delta x$ is the work of force on the path $\Delta x$, that is, according to the law of conservation of energy, the change in energy $\Delta E$. Thus,

$$\Delta E \Delta t \sim h$$
