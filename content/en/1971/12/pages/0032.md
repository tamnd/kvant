By taking some precautions, it is possible to obtain a smoke half-ring such as in figure 4. This is a brilliant illustration of the fact that the formation of a ring is not dependent on the presence of smoke. The best way to get a half ring is to blow the smoke very lightly into a paper tube, letting it flow down the bottom of the tube until it reaches the end. attributed to friction between the flowing air streams and the edge of the hole. However, most of the formation of outlets is due to the interaction with atmospheric air. To illustrate this point, I have come up with an outlet box in which the trenne with the edge of the hole is absent or, more correctly, is compensated by equalizing it over the entire cross-section of the exiting jet.

Approximately 200 holes with a diameter of 1.7 mm (Fig. 5) are drilled in the bottom of a cylindrical tin box. If the box is filled with smoke and a strong stream of air is released, a red outlet ring is separated from the surface, which is burnt on the sieve. You can simply cover the end of a paper tube with a piece of tightly stretched linen cloth and blow a smoke ring through it.

When experimenting with a box equipped with two round holes, I observe the merging of two rings moving side by side into one large ring. If the rings rotate at a high speed, they will bounce off each other, but if the rings are rotated slowly, they will connect. At the moment of connection, the emerging form is unstable. The connected rings abruptly change their horizontal position to a vertical position so quickly that it is difficult to notice, and then slowly lose the shape of the ring. The same can be demonstrated by using two paper tubes, holding them at different corners of the mouth and almost parallel to each other. In any case, the air in the room should be practically motionless.
