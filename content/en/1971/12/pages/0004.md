The last statement, however, needs more precise formulation. It should be added that the formula for potential energy is written in such a way that potential energy tends to zero when the distance $r$ between the planet and the Sun tends to infinity:

$$U(r) = -\gamma \frac{Mm}{r}$$

(γ is the gravitational constant, $M$ and $m$ are the masses of the Sun and the planet).

So, a planet cannot move away from the Sun and rotates in an ellipse if its total energy is negative.

And how will a comet move in the field of the Sun (or a spaceship in the field of the Earth) if its total energy is positive, that is

$$E = \frac{mv^2}{2} - \gamma \frac{Mm}{r} > 0$$

The energy equation (2) in this case formulaically coincides with the energy equation for the elliptical motion of the planet, only with a different sign $E$. The formula for angular momentum remains the same:

$$L = mv\rho$$

(ρ is the impact parameter, see below). Therefore, at $E > 0$ we should obtain the same formulas that we obtained by considering the elliptical trajectories of the planets ($E < 0$), only in some places there will be a “minus” sign along with the “flat” sign. The trajectory of movement in this case will be a hyperbola.

A hyperbola is defined as the locus of points, the difference in distances from which to two given points—foci—is constant. Let us remember that the ellipse is defined in the same way, only together with the “difference” the word “sum” was built.

Figure2 shows how both types of curves are constructed on a grid composed of two families of concentric circles centered at $F_1$ and $F_2$. Find out for yourself why ellipses and hyperbolas pass through the intersection points of circles.

Main theorem

Let us now check that if $E > 0$, then the comet or spaceship really moves along the hyperbola. For our purposes, since we will be talking about the atomic nucleus and Rutherfort's experiments, it is more convenient to talk not about a comet, but about a loaded particle that moves in the field of a heavy stationary charge.

This can be done because the law of the dependence of the interaction force on the distance is the same in both cases.

Let the heavy part be positively charged and located at the focus $F_1$ (Fig. 2). If the flying light part is negatively charged (its charge is $z_1e$), then it should fly along the trajectory indicated in the upper part of the figure by the sign “—” (we have not yet proven that this is a hyperbola!). The negatively charged part will fly along the same trajectory if the stationary part is negatively charged and is at focus $F_2$. This can be proven by using the mechanics equation - the total energy and momentum are the same in both cases. If the flying part is positively charged, then it will move along the trajectory marked by the “+” sign.
