Solution of the problem for arbitrary $n$

Let the numbers 1, ..., $n^2$ be arranged in the table $n \times n$. Let us choose the number $m$ in accordance with the lemma. Let's mark the cells containing 1, ..., $m$, and put asterisks in those of the remaining cells that are adjacent to the removed ones. By the lemma, the asterisks turn out to be no less than $n$. All numbers in cells with asterisks are greater than $m$. This means that most of them are $a \geq m + n$. A cell containing the number $a$ is adjacent to some offset cell containing the number $b \leq m$. Since $a \geq m + n$, $b \leq m$, then $a - b \geq n$. So, we indicated two adjacent numbers $a$ and $b$, the difference between which is greater than or equal to $n$. Thus, our problem is completely solved.

Exercises

1. In a rectangular table $m \times n$ $mn$ are randomly arranged in pairs of distinct integers. Accurately estimate the maximum difference between adjacent numbers.

2. Above, we called two table cells adjacent if they have a common side. Let’s give a new definition of “neighborhood”: we will now call two cells neighboring if they have either a common side or a common vertex (previously, a cell that was not on the edge of the table had four neighbors, now it has eight). How will the estimate of the maximum difference between neighboring numbers change with a new understanding of the term “neighboring”?

3. The last question does not apply to all table problems, but only to the lemma.

In the table, $n \times n$ are offset by $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ cells. Those of the remaining cells that have common sides with the removed ones are given an asterisk. Is it true that there are at least $n$ stars?

But it was still a colossal work, especially considering the state of mathematics at that time. It was necessary to solve the second part of the problem: the orbits are known from observations, now it is necessary to find their laws based on the results of experiments. That is, make a definition of an assumption about the form of orbital curves, and then test it on a huge amount of empirical material! If the results do not coincide, then come up with a new hypothesis and check again! After countless attempts, Kepler came to the following conclusion: the orbit is an ellipse, at one of the foci of which the Sun is located. He also found a law according to which the speed changes over the course of one year: the segment Sun - planet describes equal areas in equal periods of time. Finally, he found that the squares of the revolution time are related to the cubes of the axes of ellipses. Kepler's entire life was spent solving these problems.

To admiration for this wonderful man is added a feeling of admiration and awe, but this does not relate to the person, but to the mysterious harmony of nature that gave birth to us. Even in ancient times, people came up with curves that correspond to the simplest laws. Along with the straight line and the circle, they included the ellipse and hyperbola. We see the latter implemented in the orbits of celestial bodies, at least with a good approximation.

It seems that the human mind must freely formulate hypotheses before their actual existence is confirmed. The remarkable work of Kepler's life shows especially clearly that knowledge cannot grow out of the minds of practice. Such a calculation is only possible by comparing what is invented with what is observed.
