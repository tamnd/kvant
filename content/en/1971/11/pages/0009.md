In order for a molecule to be unable to leave the liquid, its average energy must be negative ($\bar{E} < 0$). Only in this case the molecule remains inside the potential well formed by its neighbors. If $\bar{E} > 0$, then the molecule will not stay inside the liquid and will leave it.

Since $\bar{E} = \bar{E}_k + \bar{U}$ and $\bar{U} < 0$, then in a liquid the average kinetic energy is less than the absolute value of the average potential energy: $\bar{E}_k < |\bar{U}|$, and only slightly less ($\bar{E}_k < |\bar{U}|$), so $|\bar{E}| \leq |\bar{U}|_0$ is the minimum (in absolute value) value of the potential energy. That is why the oscillations of the molecule in the hole do not last long. Due to the randomness of the movement of molecules, their energy continuously changes and becomes too more, then less energy $\bar{E}$.

As soon as the energy of a molecule exceeds the height of the potential barrier separating one well from another, the molecule jumps from one equilibrium position to another. This is precisely what determines the nature of the thermal motion of molecules in a liquid and a flowing liquid. The “settled life” time of a molecule is the time it remains inside a certain potential well. With increasing temperature, the average energy increases, and jumps occur more frequently.

Solids

The potential energy of interaction of a solid molecule with its nearest neighbors is similar to the interaction energy of liquid molecules (Fig. 12). Only the depth of the potential well here is somewhat greater, since the molecules are located closer to each other. Condition $\bar{E}_k < |\bar{U}|$ is also satisfied for solid bodies. But the kinetic energy of solid molecules is significantly less than that of liquid molecules. After all, solids form liquids when cooled. Accordingly, in solids the average kinetic energy of molecular motion is significantly less than the absolute value of the average potential energy:

$$\bar{E}_k \ll |\bar{U}|.$$

At figure 12, the average energy of a molecule inside a potential well is depicted by a straight line segment. The particle oscillates in the potential well. The height of the barriers between adjacent wells is large, and the molecules hardly move from one equilibrium position to another. To move molecules must receive energy that significantly exceeds the average energy. This event is unlikely. That's why solids, unlike liquids, retain their shape.
