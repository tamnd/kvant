Dispersion forces

It is well known that tensile forces also exist between nonpolar molecules. For example, atoms of noble gases do not have a dipole moment, but nevertheless interact with each other. The nature of these forces was clarified only after the creation of quantum mechanics.

The qualitative and roughly approximate appearance of these forces can be explained as follows. In atoms and molecules, electrins move around the nuclei in some complex way. And on average over time, the dipole moments of molecules may turn out to be zero.

But at each moment of time, electrins occupy a specific position. Therefore, the instantaneous value of the dipole moment is from zero. An instantaneous dipole creates a field that polarizes neighboring nonpolar atoms. Because of this, the interaction of instantaneous dipoles appears. The total interaction between nonpolar molecules is the average result of the interaction of all possible instantaneous

dipoles with dipole moments that they induce in neighboring molecules due to induction.

Quantum calculation leads to the conclusion that the interaction force in this case is proportional to the product of molecules $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ and inversely proportional to the seventh power of the distance:

$$F_{\pi} \sim \frac{\alpha_1 \alpha_2}{r^7}.$$

These forces are called dispersive for the reason that the dispersion of a substance (the dependence of the refractive index of light on frequency) is determined by the same properties of molecules as these forces.

Dispersion forces act between all atoms and molecules, since the mechanism of their appearance does not depend on whether they have permanent dipole moments or not. Typically, these forces exceed in magnitude both orientational and inductive forces. Only during the interaction of molecules with large dipole moments, for example, water molecules, the orientational force is greater than the dispersion force (three times for water molecules). When polar molecules (CO, HCl, etc.) interact, the dispersion forces are ten and hundreds of times greater than all others.

But the main thing is that all three types of tension decrease in the same way with distance as $\frac{1}{r^7}$. However, at large distances compared to the size of molecules, the finite speed of propagation of electromagnetic interactions begins to affect. Because of this, at distances of the order of $10^{-5}$ cm, the gravitational forces begin to decrease as much as $\frac{1}{r^8}$. This was discovered in Deryagin's experiments.
