Electromagnetic nature
intermolecular forces

It was almost impossible to begin a theoretical study of mechanical forces before the beginning of the 20th century. Simple and well-studied boundary forces during the interaction of bodies of columnar mass like a molecule could not play a noticeable role. It remained to propose that molecular forces are of an electromagnetic nature. Nothing was known about any other forces at that time.

Any atom, and especially molecules, is a complex system of a large number of loaded particles. It was not known how it worked. But the strength of their interaction undoubtedly depends on the structure of the molecules. However, the research began and began, naturally, with the simplest cases.

Orientation forces

For many molecules, such as water, the distribution of positive and negative charges is such that, on average, the centers of these charges do not coincide. Such a molecule can be approximately considered as an electric diopole: a combination of two exact charges +q and –q at a small distance l from each other (Fig. 1). The electrical properties of such a molecule are characterized by the diopole moment

$$p = ql$$

At first they were not able to calculate the values ​​of the diopole moments of molecules. Before the advent of quantum mechanics, this was generally impossible.

But, if we consider the diopole moments $p_1$ and $p_2$ of two molecules to be known, then the strength of interaction between the molecules depends. They will arise between two molecules, one of which has a diopole moment, and the second does not.

A diopole molecule creates an electric field that polarizes molecules with electric charges uniformly distributed throughout the volume.
