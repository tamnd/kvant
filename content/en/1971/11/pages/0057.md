It would seem (since the direction in which we project the forces and the point about which we calculate the moments can be arbitrary), by choosing different directions and different points, we can obtain as many angular equations for finding the forces applied to the body. However, this is not true. Most of the resulting equations turn out to be the following. For the case when the forces lie in the same plane, only three equations can be written.

Which equilibrium conditions are more convenient to use depends on the specific problem.

Problem 1. A pipe of mass $m$ lies on the ground. What minimum vertical reinforcement must be applied in order to lift it by one of its ends (Fig. 3)?

Solution problem in one line, if you use the equation of moments about the point $O$
$$mg\frac{l}{2}-Fl=0 \quad (l-\text{dлина трубы}),$$
whence $F=\frac{mg}{2}$. The correct choice of point allows us to exclude from consideration the unknown force $N$, which did not interest us in this case.

Problem 2. A ball of mass $m$ (Fig. 4) is suspended from the vertical main wall at point $A$ on a rope of length $l$. What are the tension forces of the rope $T$ and the pressure force of the ball on the wall $N$, if the radius of the ball is $R$?

Let us use the equation of moments about the center of the ball $O$. The moments of forces $mg$ and $N$ relative to point $O$ are equal to zero. Consequently, the line of action of the tension force $T$ also passes through the center of the ball.

As two other conditions, we can take, for example, the equality to zero of the projections of all forces on the horizontal and vertical directions:

$$T\cos\alpha=mg, \quad T\sin\alpha=N.$$

From the drawing it is found that $\sin\alpha=\frac{R}{l+R}$, and $\cos\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{(l+R)^2-R^2}}{l+R}$.

In some cases it is convenient to use only the moment equation.
