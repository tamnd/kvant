The difficulty is that the pushing forces, to a much greater extent than the attractive forces, depend on the individual molecules. Knowing how molecule A pushes molecules B and C away, we are not yet able to judge what pushing forces will act between molecules B and C. When molecules come together in a non-presidential manner, the individuality of their structure is more pronounced than when there are large distances between them.

Potential energy of molecular interaction

When studying the behavior of a large set of molecules, it is more convenient to use not the interaction force of molecules, but potential energy.

It is necessary to calculate the average characteristics of the system, and the concept of the average force of interaction between molecules is meaningless, since the sum of all forces acting between molecules, according to Newton’s third law, is zero. The average potential energy, as we will soon see, largely determines the state and properties of matter.

Since the change in potential energy is equal to the work done by the force, then from the known dependence of the force on the distance one can find the dependence of the potential energy on the distance*).

It should be taken into account that if the force changes with distance as $\frac{1}{r^n}$, where $n$ is a certain number, then the potential energy will be less according to the law $U \sim \frac{1}{r^{n-1}}$. This can already be seen from the dimension messages. Energy has the dimension of work, that is, the product of force and distance. Therefore, the dimension of energy is equal to the dimension of force multiplied by the dimension of distance.

*) See the article by R. G. Mints “Graphs of potential energy”, «Kvant» No. 5, 1971, pp. 8-16.
