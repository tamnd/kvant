From April 15 to April 20, the V Weighing Mathematical Optimization took place in Prgue. From these tasks that the jury members brought with them, it was necessary to display the most interesting and original tasks. This time, unlike previous years, the jury decided to give schoolchildren problems that would be close to serious mathematical science, and in the second round they even conducted a small experiment: to offer one more difficult problem, breaking it down into a series of gradually conditioned questions, in the solution of which it would be proposed to advance as far as possible.

Task 3 (8th grade). Let $a$ and $c$ be some two numbers in the top line, and $b$ and $d$ be the numbers below them:

$$\dots a \dots c \dots$$
$$\dots b \dots d \dots$$

The only condition that this number will satisfy is that $a+d=c+b$. Let's rewrite this condition as follows: $a-b=c-d$. It follows that the difference between the number in the top row and the number below it is the same for all columns; Let's denote this difference by $y$. The table can be written like this:

$$\begin{array}{c c c c c}
x_1 & x_2 & \dots & x_9 & x_{10} \\
x_1-y & x_2-y & \dots & x_9-y & x_{10}-y
\end{array}$$

$x_1, \dots, x_{10}$, $y$ are arbitrary numbers. This table can be determined in 11 questions. For example, in 10 questions you can find 10 numbers $x_1, \dots, x_{10}$ from the first line, and then, by asking one question “what number comes first in the second line?”, find $y$.

Task 6 (8th grade). We will prove the statement of the problem by contradiction. Let us assume that either the sum of two numbers out of 25 or their difference is contained among the remaining numbers.

Let's arrange these numbers in ascending order:

$$a_1 < a_2 < \dots < a_{24} < a_{25}.$$

Consider most of the numbers $a_{25}$ paired with any of the other numbers. Obviously, the sum of such numbers is not contained among the given numbers, since

$$a_{25} + a_i > a_{25} \quad (i=1, 2, \dots, 24).$$

Therefore, the differences of these numbers

$$a_{25} - a_{24} < a_{25} - a_{23} < \dots < a_{25} - a_1$$

must be contained among the given numbers. The only possible option:

$$a_{25} - a_{24} = a_1, a_{25} - a_{23} = a_2, \dots, a_{25} - a_1 = a_{24}$$

or, what is the same,

$$a_{24} + a_1 = a_{23} + a_2 = \dots = a_{13} + a_{12} = a_{25}.$$

($i=2,3,\dots,23$).

Therefore, the differences of these numbers must be contained among the given numbers. Let's write them in ascending order:

$$a_{24}-a_{23}<a_{24}-a_{22}<\dots<a_{24}-a_2.$$

From the equality $a_{23}+a_2=a_{25}$ it follows that $a_{24}-a_2<a_{23}$ and therefore the only possible option is:

$$a_{24}-a_{23}=a_1, \quad a_{24}-a_{22}=a_2, \dots, a_{24}-a_{12}=a_{12}, \dots, a_{24}-a_2=a_{22}.$$

But in this case, the difference $a_{24}-a_{12}$ is not contained among the remaining numbers, and the sum $a_{24}+a_{12}$ is not contained among the given numbers at all. We got a contradiction. Thus, the statement of the problem is proven.

The problem turns out to be true for $n$ numbers, where $n \geq 4$. The proof can be verified in a similar way, and for even $n$ we can obtain a contradiction at the very first step of our reasoning deniens. Try to write down this proof yourself.

Task 3 (grade 10). Here we are talking about a general orthogonal projection. The definition of the projection of a body onto a given plane is contained in the school textbook. We will need to determine the projection of the body onto a straight line.

The projection of a point onto a given line is called onto a given line, then its projection will be this point itself.

The projection of a body onto a given line is called onto a given line, then its projection will be this point itself.

Let $P$ be a plane and $l$ a line lying on $P$. From the theorem on three redistributions it follows that the projection of any point onto the line $l$ can be obtained by successively projecting this point onto the plane $P$ and projecting the resulting point onto the line.

According to the conditions of the problem, the projection of a body onto two planes is a circle. Let us assume that these planes intersect along the straight line $l$. From the above it follows that the projection of the body onto the straight line $l$ coincides with the projections of each of the circles onto $l$. From here we conclude that the projections of the circles $l$ coincide with each other.

It is clear that the projection of a circle onto the straight line $l$ lying in its plane is a segment equal to the diameter of the circle. Consequently, these circles have the same diameters and the same radii.

The case when these planes are parallel to each other is very convenient.
