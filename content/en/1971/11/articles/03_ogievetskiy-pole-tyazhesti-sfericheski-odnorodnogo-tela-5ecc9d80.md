Field of softness of a spherical-homogeneous body

I. I. Ogievetsky

Newton's law of universal gravitation equally determines the fall of an apple to the ground, the movement of the Moon around the Earth, and the orbits of the planets. It is based on the fact that the tension force between material points with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, located at a distance $l$ from each other, is equal to

$$F = \gamma \frac{m_1 m_2}{l^2},$$

(1)

where $\gamma = 6,67 \cdot 10^{-11} \frac{H \cdot m^2}{\kappa^2}$ is the gravitational constant (in the SI system). But the Earth, Moon and other real bodies are not exact, they have finite sizes. How to determine the tension force between stranded bodies?

The solution to this problem led Newton to the creation of the most important branch of mathematics - integral calculus. To find the force of constriction of a material point to a conjugate body, you should mentally divide this body into small (in the limit - infinitesimal) parts, calculate the contribution to the constriction force from each of these parts using formula (1) and then add (interpret) these contributions. Celestial bodies can be approximately considered spherical-homogeneous, that is, such that at any given point their density is entirely determined by the distance of this point from the center of the body. Newton discovered a remarkable fact:

$$F = \gamma \frac{4\pi R^2 \rho m}{d^2} = \gamma \frac{M \cdot m}{d^2},$$

(2)

where $M$ is the surface mass. In other words, the tensile force outside the spherical surface is the same as if the entire mass of the surface were concentrated at its center. This immediately implies a similar statement for a spherical homogeneous body.

*) This property is often used as very important, without proof. Use it in some articles «Kvant». Therefore, it is very useful to understand how it proves.
