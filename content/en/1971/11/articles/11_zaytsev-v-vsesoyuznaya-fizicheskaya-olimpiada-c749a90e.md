V All-Union Physical Olympiad

I. A. Zaitsev

From April 15 to 20, the 5th Weight-Union Physics Olympiad was held in Novosibirsk. According to the already established tradition, it took place in two rounds: theoretical and experimental. When selecting problems, the jury sought to ensure that when solving them there was enough knowledge assumed by a high school programmer, but it would require “physical thinking”, an understanding of what can be neglected, an understanding of how the process described in the task occurs, etc. The jury had the most difficulty with the problems of the experimental round. They should not take too much time to solve and they should be “olympiad”:

When making a decision, it was necessary to show resourcefulness and think about how best to set up the experiment in order to obtain the most accurate result possible. This, however, applied to both theoretical and experimental problems.

In total, the jury selected 14 theoretical and 10 experimental problems. Some tasks were repeated in different classes. Below we publish all the tasks that were offered at the Olympiad (some of them have already been published in «Kvant» No. 10, some we publish in this issue in “Problem «Kvant»”).

1. Two protons and two positrons, initially ending up at the vertices of the square on the $a$ (Fig. 1) side, are separated. The ratio of their masses is $M/m=2000$, and the charges are the same. Find the ratio of the velocities of protons and positrons after decomposition (at infinity). (10th grade).

Initially, all particles are subject to equal forces. But the masses of protons are 2000 times greater than the masses of positrons. This means that the acceleration of positrons will be 2000 times greater than the acceleration of protons. Therefore, positrons quickly spread out to infinity, and then protons will spread out, interacting only with each other. This makes it possible to consider protons as stationary when calculating the velocities of positrons.

Let's find the total potential energy of positrons before decay. If there were no protons, then the potential energy of interaction between two positrons would be equal to $\frac{e^2}{a\sqrt{2}}$ (i.e., the work that needs to be spent to merge two positrons). The field potential that each proton creates at the point where the positron is located is very high. Therefore, the total potential energy of positrons will be equal to

$$\Pi_1 = \frac{e^2}{a\sqrt{2}} + 2\frac{e}{a}e + 2\frac{e}{a}e = \frac{e^2}{a} \left( 4 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right).$$

(We can ignore the potential energy of interaction of protons, since it does not change - protons are motionless.) All this energy will turn into the kinetic energy of positrons as they disperse. It is clear that the velocities of positrons at infinity are the same. That's why

$$\frac{e^2}{a\sqrt{2}} = Mu^2. \tag{1}$$

Now let's consider the expansion of protons. Their potential energy before decomposition, very high, is equal to $\frac{e^2}{a\sqrt{2}}$, and the total kinetic energy after decomposition is equal to $Mu^2$ (and is the speed of protons). According to the law of conservation of energy

$$\frac{e^2}{a\sqrt{2}} = Mu^2. \tag{2}$$

Now dividing equation (2) by equation (1), we get

$$\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}} = \frac{M}{m} \left( \frac{u}{v} \right)^2,$$

where

$$\frac{u}{v} = \sqrt{\frac{m}{M} \frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}} + 1} \approx 0,007.$$
