2. One student came to take an exam with an automatic examiner. Five questions appeared on the machine’s screen, each of which required a “yes” or “no” answer.
After answering all the questions (to do this, you need to press the appropriate button), the machine, giving each correct answer 1 point, gives the overall score for the exam. The student carefully went through all the questions and was saddened to admit that he did not know the answer to any of them.
All his knowledge boiled down to the following:
1) the first and last questions require opposite answers;
2) on the contrary, the second and fourth questions should have the same answers;
3) at least one of the first two questions requires the answer “yes”, then the fifth requires the answer “no”.
In addition, student experience tells him that there are always more questions requiring a “yes” answer than questions requiring a “no” answer.
The picture turned out to be sad. But the student did not despair. He knew this subject poorly, but he was very good at solving logical problems. HOW IS IT EASIER TO SHARE?

What is the easiest way to divide a smaller number by a larger one if the larger number ends in 9?

Let you need to divide the number a by the number b, and a < b. Let b = 10c + 9. Now check that

$$\frac{a}{10c+9} = 10^{-1} \frac{a-p_1}{c+1} + \frac{a-p_1}{c+1} + 10p_1$$

where $p_1$ is the remainder when $a$ is divided by $c+1$, so $\frac{a-p_1}{c+1}$ is an integer.

Let $\frac{a}{b} = 0, \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \alpha_3 \dots$ be the decimal notation of the quotient. Then $\alpha_1 = \frac{a-p_1}{c+1}$ and $\alpha_1 + 10p_1 = 0, \alpha_2 \alpha_3 \dots$.

Denoting $\alpha_1 + 10p_1$ by $a_1$, we write the equality $\frac{a_1}{10c+9} = 0, \alpha_2 \alpha_3 \dots$, from which we similarly find $\alpha_2$ and so on.
