The technical speed of the source or receiver is not at all small compared to the speed of sound (for example, the speed of aircraft), and then the Doppler efficiency in both cases is significantly different.

Now that we have understood the Doppler efficiency for sound waves, it would seem that there is nothing simpler than writing similar formulas for light - it is enough to put the speed of sound $u$ together with the speed of light $c$ everywhere, and the answer is ready. However, here we are faced with an amazing obstruction: such formulas cannot be correct, since they contradict Galileo’s principle of relative.

According to this principle, it is impossible to distinguish uniform rectilinear motion from a state of rest, in other words, any physical phenomena must proceed in the same way and any physical experiments must give the same result in all coordinate systems moving relative to each other at constant speeds (that is, in inertial systems). We can consider, for example, the case of movement of a source and a stationary observable in a reporting system moving with the source, and then, according to our formulas, the change in the frequency of light in both systems would be different.

In the propagation of sound, this generality should not confuse us. After all, here, in addition to the source and receiver, the medium in which the sound propagates plays a role.
