Ohm's law
Y.A.SMORODINSKY

Simple at first glance

Typically Ohm's law is formulated this way. Let's take a conductor with current (Fig. 1). Let a potential difference $V_A - V_B$ be applied to its ends, and $V_A > V_B$. then the strength of the current flowing through the conductor will be determined by the formula

$$I = \frac{V_A - V_B}{R},$$

where $R$ is the conductor resistance. In addition, they add that if a conductor with a constant cross-section $S$ is uniform along the entire length $l$, then $R = \frac{1}{\sigma} \frac{l}{S}$, where $\sigma$ is called specific conductivity, and $\frac{1}{\sigma} = \rho$ is resistivity.

The graph of the dependence of the current on the applied potential difference is expressed as a straight line (Fig. 2).

Ohm's law looks very simple and very powerful. After all, it is clear that the greater the potential difference,

Experience has shown that Ohm's law is true for both very small and very large currents in metals. Such a large potential difference was applied to the wiring that over a length of 1 cm the potential changes by a million volts. And yet the current could be determined using Ohm's law. True, in fact, in such experiments very small deviations from this law were observed, but they could be explained by the fact that the conductor, despite all precautions, heated up, and its resistance changed due to changes in temperature. This seemingly simple law raises interesting questions.

In which direction does the current flow?

Why does current flow in the direction of decreasing potential, and not in the direction of increasing it? Let's take a battery and connect it to some resistance, for example, turn on a light bulb. According to Ohm's law, the circuit will consume current from the positive terminal of the battery through the light bulb to the imprinted terminal, reducing the potential difference between the terminals, which of course will be restored by the battery, but this consumes energy all the time (this will be discussed later).
