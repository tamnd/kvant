In the place where the current source is located, there is a sharp jump in the potential difference, which determines the electromotive force in the circuit. If you are only interested in the external circuit, then the last formula can be rewritten as follows:

$$IR = \varepsilon - Ir.$$

We see that the potential difference at the terminals of the current source is less than the electromotive force by values ​​$Ir$.

Looking now at Ohm's formula

$$X = \frac{am}{bm+x},$$

we see how well Ohm understood the role of the galvanic elements in an electrical circuit. He not only established that e.d. s. battery (composed of series-connected elements) is proportional to the number of elements, but also introduced the number of elements into the denominator of the formula. This means that he well understood the role of the internal resistance of the battery, which (as is very convenient for us now) is also proportional to the number of elements connected in series.

Ohm's local law

Let's write another formula for Ohm's law. To do this, we use the current density - the ratio of the current flowing through a very small area $S$ (located perpendicularly directed to the current) to the magnitude of this area

$$j = \frac{I}{S}.$$

Then from Ohm's law it follows that

$$j = \frac{V_A - V_B}{RS} = \sigma \frac{V_A - V_B}{l}.$$

If points $A$ and $B$ are located very close to each other, then the values

$$\frac{V_A - V_B}{l} = E$$

called the electric field intensity. Voltage $E$ is a vector directed towards the highest fast-musical potential. Since $j$ is also a vector and it is directed in the same direction, we can write Ohm’s law in vector form

$$\vec{j} = \sigma \vec{E}.$$

In this form, Ohm's law can be applied to any body at any point. This expression does not include any geometric dimensions of the body, but only the conductivity $\sigma$, determined by the substance in the place where we measure the current density. In this form, Ohm’s law is local in nature, that is, it includes quantities that relate only to one selected point (and not to the entire chain, as was the case in the formulas that we wrote above).

Elementary theory of Ohm's formula

We can now explain Ohm's law very simply. We carry current in metals using electrons that move under the influence of an electric field. Random thermal movement occurs in the conductor. Under the influence of an electric field, a more ordered movement is superimposed on it. (Usually its speeds are less than thermal ones.) The electric field acts on electrons with a force

$$\vec{F} = e\vec{E}.$$

Why don't electrons move at the same speed? Because they collide with atoms and lose their speed. This is explained in detail in the article by D. A. Frank-Kamenetsky (see «Kvant» No. 9, 1970).
