And although this venerable story may discourage us from ever trying to explain anything in our lives by the Doppler effect, nevertheless, apparently everyone has encountered this effect. When a buzzing train passes us or an airplane flies over us, we hear a sudden change in the tone of the sound. This is also Doppler effective.

Why does the frequency of the sound of a moving source change? Let's try to figure it out. Let's draw a picture of waves emitted by a moving source. The speed of sound propagation in the medium does not depend on the speed of the source, and the wave pattern will be a set of circles, the centers of which will be mixed in the direction of movement of the source (picture at the beginning of the article). Let now the observed stand, for example, behind the source, so that the source moves away from it with speed $v$. Let us assume that initially the distance between the source and the observed was equal to $u$, ($u$ is the speed of sound). then after 1 second the distance between them will be $u + v$ and at this distance all $\omega$ maxima created by the source per unit of time decrease ($\omega$ - sound frequency). Before observing for 1 second only those maxima that were initially at a distance of $u$ from it. Therefore, the frequency of the sound $\omega_1$ perceived by the observable will be less, and

$$\frac{\omega_1}{\omega} = \frac{u}{u + v},$$

from where $\omega_1 = \omega \frac{1}{1 + \frac{v}{u}}$

If the source approaches the application, then similarly we obtain

$$\omega_1 = \omega \frac{1}{1 - \frac{v}{u}}$$

If it is not the source, but the receiver of the sound that moves, the same change in frequency occurs qualitatively, but quantitatively the result will be somewhat different. If the applicator moves towards the source with a speed of $v$, then in 1 second it will pass not mismo $\omega$, but a mismo of a larger number of maxima $\omega_2$, and $\frac{\omega_2}{\omega} = \frac{u + v}{u}$, hence $\omega_2 = \omega \left(1 + \frac{v}{u}\right)$.

If the receiver moves away from the source, it will note a lower frequency

$$\omega_2 = \omega \left(1 - \frac{v}{u}\right).$$

This change in frequency when a source or application moves is called the Doppler effect. Considering $v$ positive in both cases when the distance increases, we can write

$$\omega_1 = \frac{1 \omega}{1 + \frac{v}{u}}, \quad \omega_2 = \omega \left(1 - \frac{v}{u}\right).$$
