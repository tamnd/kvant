⟦folio 28⟧

Until now, we have produced the rings from a round hole. Let's now try to form them from an elliptical and square hole. We see that in this case the ring does not retain the shape of the hole, but oscillates, trying to transform into a round ring, which is thus the only stable form of a closed cord.

Let us now consider the influence of foreign objects on the output loop. Bringing solid bodies from the side to the moving ring, we see that they push the ring away. But if the ring runs onto a stationary plane parallel to its plane, then, approaching it, it increases more and more in size, so to speak, spreads over the plane. If we let the peg run onto a knife, the plane of which passes through the axis of the peg, then the latter will be cut by the knife into two half-rings, the ends of which will slide along the surface of the knife; but, having passed this surface, the ends come together again, and the ring is restored.

In addition to smoke rings in the air, you can also observe air rings in the water. This interesting phenomenon, which at first glance seems paradoxical, is simply explained by the fact that, due to the centrifugal force, significant pressure is applied to the axes of the output ring. If, when forming a ring ring, we introduce several air bubbles into the water, they will immediately climb into the place of the liquid where the pressure is lowest, that is, on the axis of the ring, and will remain there all the time while the ring moves along the mass of water, despite the fact that air is 800 times lighter than water.

I will show here a device for the formation of air rings in water, which is a modification of the device of the professional Osborne Reynolds. There is (Fig.12) a large glass bathtub filled with water. A glass bent at a right angle is immersed in it.
