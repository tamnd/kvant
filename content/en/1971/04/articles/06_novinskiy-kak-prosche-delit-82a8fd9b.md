HOW IS IT EASIER TO SHARE?

What is the easiest way to divide a smaller number by a larger one if the larger number ends in 9?

Let you need to divide the number a by the number b, and a < b. Let b = 10c + 9. Now check that

$$\frac{a}{10c+9} = 10^{-1} \frac{a-p_1}{c+1} + \frac{a-p_1}{c+1} + 10p_1$$

where $p_1$ is the remainder when $a$ is divided by $c+1$, so $\frac{a-p_1}{c+1}$ is an integer.

Let $\frac{a}{b} = 0, \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \alpha_3 \dots$ be the decimal notation of the quotient. Then $\alpha_1 = \frac{a-p_1}{c+1}$ and $\alpha_1 + 10p_1 = 0, \alpha_2 \alpha_3 \dots$.

Denoting $\alpha_1 + 10p_1$ by $a_1$, we write the equality $\frac{a_1}{10c+9} = 0, \alpha_2 \alpha_3 \dots$, from which we similarly find $\alpha_2$ and so on.

What role fell to Poinsot in explaining the question of the motion of a solid body, the same role belongs to Helmholtz in explaining the question of the motion of a liquid.

Almost all of Helmholtz's works on mechanics are related to fluid mechanics. It can be said that modern hydrodynamics owes its development mainly to Helmholtz. Meanwhile, the most important work of the scientist in this area appeared in 1858*), 43 years after the formulas containing the reasons for the preservation of wines were found by Cauchy. But Cauchy considered the result he obtained only from the analytical side and did not foresee the mass of questions that could be solved with proper geometric illumination of the conclusions.

During the movement, either its ends will lie on the edges of the liquid (on the walls of the vessel or on the free surface), or it will remain closed.

In fact, in order to leave the walls of the vessel, the base of the vortex must be reduced in size to zero; and since the circulation of speed along the contour of the base must remain unchanged, the convergence would require that the speed of the round fluid at the bottom of the cord increase to infinity.

The hydrodynamic pressure of the fluid decreases as the speed increases. Therefore, when the vortex pressure on the wall of the vessel decreases, the pressure in this place will quickly decrease, and the remaining mass of the liquid will press on the particles of the end of the vortex cord and prevent them from moving away from the wall. The vortex cord, so to speak, sticks with its ends to the walls of the vessel. If the end of the cord lies on a free surface, then such suction can be seen by the funnel that forms on the free surface at the “bottom” of the cord.

If the ends of the vortex cord do not lie on the edges of the liquid, then they must be closed together, and thus a closed vortex cord is obtained - one in which, so to speak, both ends are sucked to each other.

The simplest type of closed vortex filament appears to be a vortex ring, shown at figure 7.

All parts of the liquid lying outside the ring swirl in a closed curve passing through the ring so that the circulation of velocity along all these curves is the same and equal to the circulation of velocity on the contour of the cross section of the ring. By crossing the vortex of the ring, we will receive various circulations for the trajectory of its part. Liquid point velocities

the largest on the surface of the ring. They decrease with distance from this surface inside the ring and are equal to zero on a certain center line. They also decrease as they move away from the ring in the mass of liquid surrounding it. For fluid points at a significant distance from the ring, the velocities are inversely proportional to the cubes of the distance from the ring.

We have seen that two straight parallel cords originating in a liquid mass, around which the liquid is round with equal directions of the vortex in opposite directions, will move in a direction perpedicular to the plane drawn through them. For the same reason, the vortex ring will not remain motionless, but will move in a direction perpedicular to the plane of the ring, in the direction in which the liquid flows out of the ring.

We see on figure 7 that particles of the liquid mass moving in the upper closed trajectories will move to the lower edge of the ring and move it to the right; in the same way, particles of the liquid mass moving along the lower closed trajectories will move to the upper edge of the ring and also move it to the right. All rings will move evenly to the right side, carrying behind them the round liquid around them. This movement will be faster, the greater the direction of the vortex and the smaller the size of the ring.

Since real liquids have viscosity and rub against the walls of the vessels in which they move, during their movement they are constantly filled with vortex cords. Helmholtz showed that a liquid mass in any imaginable movement can be considered as continuously filled with vortex cords, and as a means of studying the movement of these cords.

Brief information about the scientists mentioned in N. E. Zhukovsky’s lecture.

Euler is the greatest mathematician of the 18th century, a St. Petersburg academician, one of the creators of analytical mechanics. Laplace called Euler the father of modern mathematical analysis.

Poinsot is a French scientist, mathematician and mechanic. In 1834 he obtained an elegant geometric solution to some problems in the dynamics of rotation.

Helmholtz was an outstanding German physicist and mathematician of the 19th century. He became famous, in particular, for his work on acoustics (Helmholtz resonators) and thermodynamics. He is also the founder of the vortex theory.

Cauchy is a French scientist of the late 18th - first half of the 19th century, who was rightfully considered one of the creators of modern mathematics. He left over 800 memoirs on various branches of mathematics.

Shvedov V.F. is a professor at Odessa University, a well-known specialist in the hydrodynamics of viscous fluids (second half of the 19th century).

Tag is a professor of physics in Edinburgh (England, second half of the 19th century).

Reynolds is a professor at Owen College in Manchester (England, second half of the 19th century). He became famous for working on hydrodynamics.
