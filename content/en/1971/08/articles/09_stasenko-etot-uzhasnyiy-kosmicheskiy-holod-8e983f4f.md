In science fiction, almost anything is possible. For example, in Conan Doyle's "Maracot's Abyss" he uses a nice hypothesis that water at any depth does not exert any pressure, and nothing - it turned out to be an excellent novel with science, love, the ancient Greeks, and so on.

Unlike science fiction, every developed science prohibits more than it allows; but if she has already resolved something, then that’s for sure.

Much has been said about cosmic cold by both science fiction writers and engineers:

“...Clenching her teeth, closing her eyes, Svetlana furiously wounded her hand (the spaceship's flywheel)... she didn't have time to scream or move her hand. Death from cosmic cold was faster than thought" (B. Fradkin, The Astronomer's Secret 117-03, Molotov, 1956).

“...The cold of the world space turns out to be rather useful, ... since there is no need to take cooling substances and devices with you” (M. Valle, Flight into the world space, ONTI, 1936).

So, is it cold in space?

What does “cold” mean? Let's clarify this concept. Let's assume that when it's “cold,” a lot of tel is lost from us per unit time, for example, Q joules per second (rain/sec). And since one of us may be small in stature, others may be giants, it is better to see how much tella is consumed in a unit of time with a unit of area. As a measure of “coldness,” a tell per second per square meter of the surface of our body is used:

$$\frac{Q}{S} = q \left( \frac{\partial \chi}{\partial e} \cdot m^2 \right).$$

On Earth, we have learned to hardly think about why we are not, in fact, very hot; rather, living in a moderate order, we think about not being cold (we take care of fur coats, hats, etc.). And when it gets hot, we have ice cream, the depths of the sea, deep whirlpools, and the wind in our faces. The movement of water, air, ice - all this immediately and effectively provides a sufficient value of q. Molecules of the environment collide with the surface of any heated body and carry away the body. (With some ingenuity, we can even form a column of shivering air above us, like a marai above the horizon on a hot day.) What if it gets hot in space? In a vacuum there is no air, no water, it’s a shame to throw everything overboard the ship. There is only one thing left: radiation

This paradox - in order to cool quickly, you need to overheat - is associated with the well-known Stefan-Boltzmann law.

$$q = \sigma T^4. \tag{1}$$

Here $\sigma$ is a physical constant, the value of which can be found in the reference book: $\sigma = 5,7 \cdot 10^{-8} \text{bm}/\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{epa}d^4$. The equal sign occurs only for those that cool quickly and need to be overheated - it is associated with the well-known Stefan-Boltzmann law.
