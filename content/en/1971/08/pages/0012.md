absorb α-radiation. These rays turned out to consist of heavy atoms of matter carrying a positive electric charge; they emit from active substances at a speed of about 30,000 km per second. In all likelihood, the α-particle is a charged atom of helium *).

β-rays are more penetrating than α-rays and carry a negative electrical charge. They eject from radioactive matter at a speed significantly greater than the speed of emission of alpha particles. Some beta particles are emitted at speeds close to the speed of light. Their rest mass is only one thousandth of the mass of an α particle, and in fact they are identical to the electrons produced during a cathode discharge in a vacuum tube.

γ-rays have extraordinary penetrating ability, easily passing through 5-10 cm of iron. It has now been established quite reliably that γ-rays are waves similar in character to the well-extracted X-rays, but they have greater penetrating ability.

What all these types of radiation have in common is the ability to act on a photographic plate and cause phosphorescence **) in certain types of matter. But from a measurement point of view, their most important property is the ability to discharge onto electrified bodies. This property undoubtedly leads to the most revealing method for studying radioactive substances, and therefore we will consider it in more detail. Let's take an ordinary good insulated electric scooper and charge it so that its golden leaves spread widely. It is well known that under normal conditions and good insulation the leaves converge extremely slowly, and after a few minutes the leaves will seem almost motionless. Now let’s place a radioactive substance on the electrode of the electric juice. The sheets will immediately begin to quickly converge, which is due to the loss of charge on the electrified system. Regardless of whether the electroscope has a positive or negative charge, the loss of charge occurs at the same rate. The mechanism that causes the discharge of the electric juice has been studied in great detail, and it is now known that this effect is due to the fact that the radioactive radiation surrounding the electric juice has an impact, that is, a certain amount of carriers of negative and positive charges - ions - are formed in it. The ions are mixed in an electric field. If, for example, the electroscope is charged positively, then the negative ones will move towards the charged system. The effect of electric juice discharge is due to the attraction of a large number of negatively charged ions to a positively charged conductor. When the radioactive substance is removed, the rapid merging of the sheets stops immediately.
