⟦folio 9⟧

Some cosmic aspects of radioactivity

E. Rutherford

August 30, 1971 marks the 100th anniversary of the birth of Ernest Rutherford. It is difficult to name a person who has done more to advance our knowledge of the structure of the atom than Ernest Rutherford, the great experimenter of the 20th century.

While studying the radioactive decay of uranium, Rutherford discovered α- and β-rays and established that the first of them are a stream of fast helium nuclei, and the second are a stream of extremely fast electrons. He also showed that radioactive decay leads to the transformation of chemical elements. Through a series of brilliant experiments, Rutherford built a planetary model of the atom, being the first to realize that at the center of the atom there is a heavy nucleus around which electrons move. He created the basis of modern ideas about the structure of matter. In 1919, Rutherford carried out the first artificial nuclear reaction, during which one natural gas, nitrogen, was converted into another natural gas, oxygen.

The rapid development of nuclear physics, the founder of which was Ernest Rutherford, revolutionized natural science and led to the fact that the role of science in the life of society increased immeasurably.

We are printing today an abridged translation of a paper given by Ernest Rutherford at the Royal Astronomical Society of Canada on April 3, 1907. The paper was published in the journal of that society for May-June 1907.

The report is printed in abridged form. Translation from English and preparation of the publication was carried out by Yu. M. Tsipenok.

The topic of my speech in a completely natural way is divided into two parts: one is a consideration of the properties of radioactive substances themselves, the other is the prevalence of radioactive substances in the surface of the Earth and in our atmosphere.

A distinctive feature of radioactive substances in general is their ability to spontaneously and continuously emit radiation of a special type, releasing bodies, and in some cases also emitting light rays. In the case of radioactive substances such as uranium or thorium, these properties manifest themselves, if not indefinitely, then at least over a period of time varying over millions of years. In the case of radium, the duration of activity is shorter, but also changes over thousands of years. This learning is spontaneous and completely resistant to chemical or physical influence.
