Pierre Fermat (1601 - 1665). A French mathematician, a lawyer by profession, he studied mathematics “in his spare time.” Along with Descartes, he is the founder of analytical geometry. One of the creators of number theory; I worked a lot on prime numbers. Among his most interesting results is Fermat's little theorem.

first prove that the numbers $2^p$ - 1 with the values ​​$p$ indicated by them are real primes.

It is not at all difficult to calculate any of them, but to find out whether all these numbers are prime or not was far beyond the limits of human beings.

It remains unknown whether Mersenne was right or wrong.

No need, it was discovered that the cheerful Intaine Cataldi, a former professor of mathematics in Florence and Bologna, who first gave a method for extracting square roots, also studied Japanese perfect numbers to save his soul. His notes indicated the values ​​of the sixth and sevento perfect numbers, which he found almost a hundred years before Mersenne:
8 589 869 056 (sixth number),
137,438,691,328 (seventh number).

It turned out that both of these numbers coincide with those pointed out by Mersenne: $2^{16} \cdot (2^{17} - 1)$ and $2^{18} \cdot (2^{19} - 1)$.

It remained unproven whether these numbers were really perfect; This requires that the factors $2^{17} - 1$ and $2^{19} - 1$ be prime.

St. Petersburg academician, founder of modern mathematics, continuous calculator, friend of Lomonosov, the great Leonard Euler managed to find a new theorem about tanistic and mysterious perfect numbers. He proved that all even perfect numbers have the form indicated by Euclid. But what form odd perfect numbers should have and whether they can exist at all remains unknown to this day.

Euler proved that the first three numbers mentioned by Mersenne: $2^{17} - 1$, $2^{19} - 1$ and $2^{31} - 1$ are indeed prime.

The sixth and seventh perfect numbers discovered by Cataldi and later by Mersenne turned out to be correct. And the mysterious secret of how they managed to find them remained forever in history. Until now, only one “explanation” for this riddle has been proposed - it was still perfect: they were helped by the natural providence, it suggested to its chosen one the correct values ​​of two perfect numbers.

Thus, the eighth perfect number, which corresponds to $p = 31$ in Euclid's formula, is
2 305 843 008 139 952 128.

Again, for a century, this number remained the best known of the perfect numbers, but during this time the mathematician managed to find a new method by which one can determine whether the number $2^{p} - 1$, where $p$ is a prime number, is prime or not, without performing direct calculations. It turned out that not all of Mersenne's predictions were correct. He correctly predicted the value $p = 127$, but numbers with values ​​$p = 67$ and $p = 257$, Mersenne asked, were not perfect.
