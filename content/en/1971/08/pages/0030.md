Fig. 5. Inverter Posel.

A device that performs inversion_transformation. There is more than one such device. They are called inverters. figure 5 shows the Posel inverter, consisting of six hinged rods, $OA=OB$, $AM=AM'=BM=BM'$. This inverter works as follows: point $O$ is fixed; if point $M$ describes a certain line, then point $M'$ describes the line obtained by inversion

versions from the original one with the center of inversion at point $O$ and the inversion coefficient equal to $AO^2 - AM^2$. Following such an inverter on your own is not at all a difficult task, even for schoolchildren who do not attend the “skillful hands” club. If we add to this inverter a rod $O'M$ such that $O'M=OO'$ and a point $O'$ are fixed, then when rotating the rod $O'M$ the point $M'$ will describe a straight line. Such inverters are used in technology to convert circular motion into rectilinear motion.

Even simpler is the Garth inverter (Fig. 6). It consists of only four hinged rods. The figure that these rods form is called an antiparallelogram. A characteristic property of such a quadrilateral is the condition: $AB=CD$ and $BC=AD$. If some point $O$ of the segment $AB$ is fixed on the plane, mark points $M$ and $M'$ on the rods $AD$ and $BC$, lying on a straight line passing through the point $O$ and parallel $AC$, then when point $M$ moves along some figure, point $M'$ will move along the figure obtained from the previous one using inversion with the center at point $O$ and a coefficient equal to

$$\frac{AO \cdot BO}{AB^2} (AD^2 - DC^2).$$

Now try to solve several problems.

1. Three circles are drawn through two points, each of which passes through the points. What will they turn into during inversion with the center at one of these points?
2. What will three parallel straight lines become during inversion?
3. Construct a circle passing through two given points and tangent to a given line.
4. Prove that the Posel and Garth inverters actually perform the indicated inversion.
