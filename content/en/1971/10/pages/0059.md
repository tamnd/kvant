After all, one of the main problems facing modern science is to teach an electronic computer to process information written in human language, because only a machine can cope with the urgent tasks of automatic translation from one language to another, storing huge amounts of information in memory and quickly finding the necessary information in these arrays (information retrieval) and other tasks, without the solution of which further progress is unthinkable. And teaching a machine to understand human language is very useful. The machine understands only its perfectly precise, absolutely formatted programming language. Therefore, a linguist must accurately identify and write down in a formatted form understandable to machine languages ​​all the properties and patterns of human language necessary for understanding it by machine language. When solving this problem, a linguist is dealing with his own linguistic problem, only of enormous scale and more difficult inequality, and no one specifically organizes such data for him and directs him, directly or indirectly, to the correct method of solution, as is done in problem optimization.

Almost all optimization problems in linguistics and mathematics are “mathematical” to one degree or another. Take, for example, such seemingly purely linguistic tasks as translation from one language to another (see, for example, task 1). True, for these optimizations you are asked to translate only from an unfamiliar language, or into an unfamiliar language, or, even better, from an unfamiliar one to an unfamiliar one (!). However, this turns out to be a completely feasible task: the tasks are designed in such a way that a complete logical analysis of the data provides the possibility of such an unusual translation. It’s not for nothing that “table people,” as optimization organizers call them, that is, those participants who love and know how to organize data into tables, invariably add a lot of pride when solving problems of this and other types.

Other types of problems are even more “mathematical” (see, for example, problems II, III). They use other mathematical techniques: combinatorial, logical and others, but the general principle remains unchanged: the task should not require prior knowledge - everything should follow from the conditions, the solutions must be able to communicate everything “on the spot.”

These are the unusual optimizations that are happening at Moscow University. About 2,000 high school students are already studying there. Many of the signs are now being studied at the Department of Structural and Applied Linguistics and they themselves will create problems for optimization.
