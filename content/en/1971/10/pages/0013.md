However, ideal and fully built-up crystal lattices do not exist. They always contain a certain amount of vacancies, or “holes”, in which diffusing atoms can “jump”. When it jumps into a certain vacancy, the atom leaves behind a new vacancy.
A neighboring atom can move into this vacancy; intact, by continuous movements of atoms in the lattice, atom can pass through the crystal (Fig.2).

What makes an atom leave its place in the lattice and “scan” into a vacancy? The fact is that in fact, atoms in lattices are not in fixed positions in an array. As a result of thermal motion, atoms continuously vibrate relative to a certain average or, more precisely, upsaverage position over various possible locations of their location. The amplitude of these vibrations can vary from atom to atom and over time. From time to time, an atom is so far from its equilibrium position that it can jump to an adjacent vacant location. The tendency to this, of course, increases sharply if we heat the body, thereby causing the atoms to vibrate more strongly. Thus, the rate of diffusion increases very quickly with increasing temperature. For example, zinc diffuses into copper at 300°C almost 100 million times faster than at room temperature.

In the Roberts-Austin experiment, gold atoms at 200°C are energetically removed from the sausage to begin jumping into lattice vacancies (the same is the case for lead atoms). After all, if the experiment had been carried out for a long time, the gold atoms would have been evenly distributed throughout the lead cylinder. A simple analysis can be carried out. If you put several black balls into a box of white balls and shake the box for a long time, then the black balls turn out to be evenly distributed among the white ones.
