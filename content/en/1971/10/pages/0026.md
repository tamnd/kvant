The moment of friction forces is directed towards the vertical (the gimlet rule), therefore, the change in the angular momentum of the wolf is also directed towards the vertical, and the axis of the wolf tends to become perpendicular to the plane of rotation.

So, two moments of force act on the inclined fiber: the moment of a couple of forces - the reaction of support and gravity, and the moment of friction. The movement of a wolf will always occur in the presence of these two moments.

Let's now return to Thomson's wolf and try to explain his behavior. Since the Thomson fiber consists of a sphere with a middle apex, its center of gravity is located below the geometric center of the ball from which it is made. When spinning the wolf, we will involuntarily deviate its axis from the vertical position. But since the drag has a spherical shape, its support point changes as a result of such deviation. The axis of rotation of the wolf remains the same - vertical and will not coincide with its geometric axis. And since the center of gravity of the wolf lies below the geometric center of the ball, as a result of this

declination, its center of gravity will no longer lie on the axis of rotation (Fig. 7). He will take position $O'$ and will spin around the vertical axis together with the wolf. When rotating at a high angular velocity, the center of gravity of the wolf will rise in the same way as a ball on a thread rises, if the thread is untwisted as shown in figure 8.

The fiber does not hold it in a lateral position, but by inertia jumps over it, touching its lower plane, on which it rotates (Fig. 2). As soon as this happens, the fulcrum of the wolf moves from point $A$ to point $B$ (Fig. 9), and the drag, rotating around its axis, begins to presuppose near axis $BB'$. In other words, the Thomson wire will behave like an “ordinary” wire. Under the influence of the frictional moment, it will align its axis with the vertical axis $BB'$ and will continue to rotate upward with the ball.

From the above reasoning it is clear that we owe the columnar behavior of Thomson's wolf to the force of friction. Indeed, if there were no friction force, then the drag, having hit the plane, would return to the lateral position and rotate as long as it had a sufficiently high angular velocity. And then, under the influence of the moment of gravity, it would return to its original position (Fig. 2).
