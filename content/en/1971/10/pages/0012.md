The smell of perfume is known to exist at quite a distance. This is explained by the fact that perfume vapor diffuses very easily in the air. Drops of liquid dye in water diffuse easily throughout the vessel. Diffusion in a solid is much more difficult to observe. For this reason, the study of diffusion in solids has become one of the most interesting studies in physics today.

As in many other areas of a person, it reduces effectiveness; in this case, it reduces the precedence of knowledge. For centuries, workers welded metals and produced steel by heating solid iron in a carbon atmosphere, without having the slightest idea of ​​the diffusion processes taking place. Only in 1896 did scientific study of the problem begin.

The English metallurgist Sir William Roberts-Austin measured the diffusion of gold in lead in a simple experiment. He placed a thin disk of gold on the end of an inch-long cylinder of pure lead, placed this cylinder in a furnace where the temperature was maintained at about 200°C, and kept it in the furnace for 10 days. He then cut the cylinder into thin disks and measured the amount of gold that diffused into each lead medium. It turned out that by the “clean” end a completely measurable amount of gold had passed through the entire cylinder; lead diffused in the opposite direction into the depths of the gold disk (Fig. 1). Roberts-Austin discovered that heated metal diffuses into another when they are pressed closely together.
