They applied only a layer of copper on opposite sides of the brass stubble, and between the copper and brass on both sides of the brass stubble they placed molybdenum wire so that it would fix the boundary between the copper and brass. After this, the sample was heated to a high temperature. If copper atoms entered the brass rod as quickly as zinc atoms exited it, then the original size of the brass rod would not change. This would be indicated by the stationary position of the molybdenum wire. But in fact the molybdenum wire had shifted, and its dislocation indicated that the brass rod had contracted; that is, zinc atoms came out of stubble faster than copper atoms entered it. Under a microscope, it was possible to smooth out fairly large pores that had formed in the surface as a result of the appearance of a large number of vacancies arising where zinc atoms quickly escaped from the pores.

These results were published in 1947, and what is now called the “Kirkendyle effect” created a real sensation among metallurgists and all researchers who studied the properties of solids.

Old experiments were tested and new ones were carried out.

Since the Kirkendile effect was immediately discovered in many alloys, there was no doubt that it actually existed.

ASCHER'S DRAWING

If a plane is covered with a polygon so that two polygons have either a common side, a common vertex, or have no common points at all, then such a covering is called parquet.

In our magazine (see «Kvant» No. 3, 1970, p. 21) we presented a number of problems related to parquet floors.

For some parquets, it is possible to specify plane transformations that transform the figures that make up the parquet into each other so that the parquet itself does not change.

For the parquet shown on this figure, its transformations also do not change: rotation around the point by 60° and symmetry with respect to straight line l. What other transformations can you point out that do not change this parquet?

You see a completely different kind of parquet on figure by the Dutch artist Escher (see cover). This parquet is replaced by the fact that one of the transformations that does not change it is doubling all distances relative to the center (similarity transformation). Therefore, strictly speaking, figure depicts an infinite number of shrinking lizards. Try to come up with other parquets that do not change their similarity during transformations.

L. Limanov
