**M66**

Here are some examples where the sum of the squares $k$ of consecutive natural numbers is equal to the sum of the squares $(k-1)$ of the following natural numbers:
$$3^2+4^2=5^2, 36^2+37^2+38^2+39^2+40^2=41^2+42^2+43^2+44^2,$$
$$55^2+56^2+57^2+58^2+59^2+60^2=61^2+62^2+63^2+64^2+65^2.$$
Find a general formula covering all such cases.

Let us prove that for every natural number $k$ there is exactly one natural number $n$ such that
$$(n-k+1)^2+(n-k+2)^2+\ldots+(n-1)^2+n^2=(n+1)^2+$$
$$+(n+2)^2+\ldots+(n+k-1)^2.$$

(Note that on the right side of the equality $(k-1)$ is a summand, and on the left side there is $k$). Equality (1) is equivalent to the following:
$$n^2=[(n+1)^2-(n-k+1)^2]+[(n+2)^2-(n-k+2)^2]+\ldots$$
$$\ldots+[(n+k-1)^2-(n-1)^2]$$
$$n^2=k(2n-k+2)+k(2n-k+4)+\ldots+k(2n+k-2),$$
$$n^2=2k(k-1)n.$$

Thus, the stated statement is proven: equality (1) for natural $k$ and $n$ is satisfied if and only if $n=2k^2-2k$. (When moving from (3) to (4), we took advantage of the fact that the sum of the $(k-1)$ terms of the arithmetic progression $(2n-k+2)+(2n-k+4)+\ldots+(2n+k-2)$ is equal to $(k-1)\frac{(2n-k+2)(2n+k-2)}{2}=2(k-1)n$. Thus, we obtained an exclusion formula. It can be written, for example, like this:
$$(2k^2-3k+1)^2+(2k^2-3k+2)^2+\ldots+(2k^2-2k)^2$$
$$=(2k^2-2k+1)^2+(2k^2-2k+2)^2+\ldots+(2k^2-k-1)^2,$$
where $k$ is an arbitrary natural number. The examples given in the conditions are obtained from it with $k$ equal to 2, 4 and 5.

This formula (or others equivalent to it) has been found by a lot of people - most of them either give a proof close to ours, or use the formula for the sum of squares of natural numbers in their calculations (see Lemma 1, on page 17).

M68

The grid of lines depicted on figure 5 (see also the cover of “Kvat” No. 2, 1971) consists of concentric circles of radii 1, 2, 3, 4, ... with a center at the point $O$, a line $l$ passing through the point $O$, and all possible tangents to the circle parallel to the line $l$. The entire plane is divided by these lines into cells, which are colored in a checkerboard pattern. In the chain of pink dots shown in figure, every two adjacent dots are opposite vertices of a dark cell. Prove that all points of such an infinite chain lie on the same parabola (the pose weight figure is, as it were, woven from yellow and dark parabolas).

Taking the straight line $l$ as the axis $Ox$, and the point $O$ as the origin of coordinates of the rectilinear system, as shown in figure 5. The scale unit is already specified in the conditions. The grid of lines discussed in the conditions is composed of circles of radius $n$ with center $O$ (the equation of such a circle $x^2 + y^2 = n^2$) and straight lines $y = m$, where $m$ and $n$ are all possible integers; $n > 0$. Thus, each point of intersection (or tangency) of these lines—each “node” $(x, y)$ of our grid—corresponds to a certain pair of values ​​$n = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and $m = y$. Note that when we move from one of the pink dots to the adjacent pink dot, both $m$ and $n$ change by one, so their difference $n - m = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} - y$ remains constant; this difference for all pink dots is 6.

The equation $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} - y = 6$ is equivalent to the following: $x^2 + y^2 = (y + 6)^2$; $y = \frac{x^2}{12} - 3$. Thus, all pink points lie on the parabola $y = \frac{x^2}{12} - 3$.

In another similar chain of points, which we drew with the head color, when moving from a point to the neighboring one, $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and $y$ change by one so that their sum remains constant: $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} + y = 4$. This equation, after transforming the points, becomes the equation of the parabola $y = -\frac{x^2}{6} + \frac{3}{2}$.

In the same way, it can be shown that any infinite chain of points in which neighboring points are opposite vertices of the same cell (or one segment) lies on the parabola $y = -\frac{x^2}{2p} + \frac{p}{2}$, where $p$ is some non-zero integer. Conversely, any of these parabolas contains such an infinite chain of points (these are the points of intersection of the parabola with all possible lines $y = m$).

Note that if $p < 0$, that is, if the branches of parabolaOLS go upward, then if $p$ is even, the parabola goes along the dark cells, and if $p$ is odd, along the yellow ones; if $p > 0$, then vice versa. Moreover, through each intersection of lines and circles of our grid (excluding tangent points lying on line $x = 0$), two parabolas pass; one goes along the yellow cells, the other goes along the dark ones; one is directed with branches up, the other - down.

The solution to this problem is the addition of many honors. Some people use the following geometric definition of parabola in their solution: a parabola is a set of points equally distant from a given point and a given line. Essentially, the solution to problem M68 is very close to proving the equivalence of this definition of parabola and the one known from the school course (a parabola is the graph of a square triangle). If we choose a coordinate system so that a given point is the nacal coordinate, and a given line is the equation $y = c$, then the condition that the point $(x, y)$ is equally distant from the given point and the given line is written by the equation $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = |y - c|$, which is equivalent to the equation $y = -\frac{x^2}{2c} + \frac{c}{2}$.

The number 76 has this curious property: the last two digits of the number $76^2 = 5776$ give the number 76 again.

a) Are there any other two-digit numbers like this?

b) Find all three-digit numbers $A$ such that the last three digits of $A^2$ make up the number $A$.

b) Is there an infinite sequence of digits $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$ such that for any $n$ the square of the number “$a_n a_{n-1} \dots a_2 a_1$” is “...$a_n a_{n-1} \dots a_2 a_1$”? (The quotation marks here denote the decimal notation of the number. We exclude the obvious answer $a_i = O$ for all $i > 1$.)

The answer to this problem follows: for any $n > 1$ there are exactly two such $n$-digit numbers $n > 1$, the square of which is $A$ (not counting the very long ones: 0 ... 0000 and 0 ... 0001), - for $n = 2$ these are 76 and 25, for $n = 3$ - 376 and 625, with $n = 4$ - 9376 and 0625, with $n = 5$ - 09376 and 90625, etc., so there are two infinite sequences of numbers discussed in point c): one starting with the numbers 6, 7, 3, 9, 0, 1, ..., the other - with the numbers 5, 2, 6, 0, 9, 8, ...*).

Let's start with task a). The easiest way to find the two-digit numbers we need, which indicate pluralities, is as follows. If $A^2$ turns out to be the same two digits as $A$, then $A^2 - A = A$ (A - 1) is divisible by 100 = 25 4, and since the numbers $A$ and $(A - 1)$ are coprime (do not have common factors greater than 1), then one of them must be divisible by 25, and the other by 4. Let's try to see if each of the numbers is suitable 25, 50 and 75 for role $A$ or for role $A - 1$ (both of these numbers are double digits). To do this, you just need to check which of its neighboring numbers are divisible by 4. This will only be 76 and 24, so $A$ can only equal 25 and 76.

Problem 6 can be solved similarly), but we must immediately understand: if the square of the number $B = \langle a_{n-1} a_{n-2} \dots a_1 \rangle$ turns out to be the digits “... $a_{n-1} a_{n-2} \dots a_1$”, then it is possible, in a unique way, to choose the digit $a_n$ so that the square of the number $A = \langle a_n a_{n-1} \dots a_1 \rangle$ turns out to be “... $a_n a_{n-1} \dots a_1$”.

Let $B^2 = \langle ... b_n a_{n-1} a_{n-2} \dots a_1 \rangle$. Then $(n \geqslant 2 = > 2n > n + 1)$

$$A^2 = (10^n a_n + B)^2 = 10^{2n} a_n + 2 \cdot 10^n a_n B + B^2 = \langle ... c_n a_{n-1} a_{n-2} \dots a_1 \rangle,$$

where $c_n$ is the last digit of the number $2a_n a_1 + b_n$. We must choose $a_n$ so that $c_n$ equals $a_n$, that is, so that $2a_n a_1 + b_n = a_n = (2a_1 - 1)a_n + b_n$ is divisible by 10. Obviously, this can be done in a unique way. In the functions $a_1 = 5$ and $a_1 = 6$ that interest us, the $b_n \rightarrow a_n$ functions that we must indicate are especially simple: if $a_1 = 5$, then $a_n = b_n$, and if $n = 6$, then $a_n = 10 - b_n$ with $b_n > 0$ and $a_n = 0$ with $b_n = 0$.

A heavy hoop with lightweight knitting needles is located in a vertical plane and can be rotated around a horizontal axis passing through the center. A small ball is fixed in the thickness of its rim, having the same mass as the hoop itself. What will be the period of the wheel hoop around the axis? How will it change if: a) this hoop is moved to the Moon? b) place it in a liquid in which he will marvel without tremium?

Will engage in analogies. Readers can get acquainted with him by reading the article by Ya. A. Smorodinsky “Similar movements” in the magazine for this year. Let's use the analogy with a mathematical matrix of length $R$.

If the maximum angle of deviation of the matrix and the ball attached to the hoop is $\alpha_0$ (Fig. 7), then they are at a height of $h=R(1-\cos \alpha_0)$ from the equilibrium position. At the deflection angle $\alpha$, the kinetic energy of the matrix (and the hoop with the ball) is equal to the difference between the potential energies at angle $\alpha_0$ and angle $\alpha$. For the mathematical matrix we get

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = mgR(\cos \alpha - \cos \alpha_0).$$

Therefore the speed matrix

$$v = \sqrt{2gR(\cos \alpha - \cos \alpha_0)}.$$

Just like the matrix, the potential energy of the hoop with the ball changes. The potential energy of the hoop itself does not change, but the kinetic energy in this case is equal to the sum of the kinetic energies of the ball $E_{\text{ш}} = \frac{mv^2}{2}$ and the hoop $E_0 = \frac{mv^2}{2}$ (for the kinetic energy of the hoop, see the article by A.K. Kikon “Rotational motion of bodies”, «Kvant» No. 1, 1971). Therefore, in the case of a hoop with a ball, it follows from the law of conservation of energy that

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{mv^2}{2} = mgR(\cos \alpha - \cos \alpha_0),$$

and ball speed

$$v = \sqrt{gR(\cos \alpha - \cos \alpha_0)}.$$

At any angle of deviation from the equilibrium position, the speed of the ball is $\sqrt{2}$ times less than the speed of the mathematical matrix. Hence,

and the period of oscillation of the hoop with a ball is $\sqrt{2}$ times greater than the period of oscillation of the matrix and is equal to

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{2R}{g}}.$$

You can approach the solution of this problem anyway. If the mathematical matrix is ​​deviated from the equilibrium position by an angle $\alpha$, then it is returned to the equilibrium position by the component of the gravity force, tangent to the trajectory of the matrix (Fig. 8):

$$F = mg \sin \alpha.$$

Since the angle $\alpha$ is small, then $\sin \alpha \approx \alpha \approx \frac{x}{R}$, where $x$ is the displacement of the matrix from the equilibrium position. From here

$$F \approx mg \frac{x}{R}.$$

Therefore, for the tangential component of the matrix acceleration we can write the equation

$$mg \frac{x}{R} = ma$$ or $a = \frac{g}{R} x = cx,$

where $c = \frac{g}{R}$ is some constant coefficient for a given matrix. The matrix oscillation period is equal to $2\pi \sqrt{\frac{R}{g}}$, that is, $2\pi/\sqrt{c}$.

We will obtain a similar result for a weight attached to a circle and oscillating along a horizontal plane. The equation of motion of such a weight is $kx = ma$, and the period of oscillation is $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$.

In our case, the force returning the ball to the equilibrium position is equal, as in the case of the mathematical matrix, $mg \frac{x}{R}$, and the acceleration $a$ is imparted to both the ball and the hoop, that is, mass $2m$. Therefore $mg \frac{x}{R} = 2ma$; this means $c = \frac{g}{2R}$ and the period of oscillation of the system is $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{2R}{g}}$.

If a hoop with a ball is moved to the Moon or placed in a vessel with liquid, then its period of oscillation will change in exactly the same way as the period of oscillation of a mathematical matrix: in the first case it will increase by $\sqrt{6}$ times ($g_{\text{л}} = \frac{1}{6} g_3$) due to a change in the acceleration of free fall, and in the second case it will decrease by $\sqrt{1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_T}}$ times ($\rho$ - the density of water and $\rho_T$ - the density ball materials) due to the influence of the Archimedean buoyancy force.

Having tilted the block and holding it at a certain angle to the vertical, we apply a certain force $F$ to the block, directed along the block (Fig. 10). $$F \sin \alpha < kmg + kF \cos \alpha,$$

$$F < \frac{kmg}{\sin \alpha - k \cos \alpha}.$$

20 helium contained in a cylinder under the piston is very slowly transferred from state I ($P_1 = 4,1$ amm, $V_1 = 32$ l) to state II ($P_2 = 15,5$ amm, $V_2 = 9$ l). Each gas with the highest temperature access is simpler if the graph of pressure versus volume is a straight line (Fig. !i)?

Let us plot the isotherm grid on the graph—hyperbola $PV = \text{const} \frac{m}{\mu} RT$. The higher the gas temperature, the farther the vertex of the hyperbola is from the origin. Therefore, it is clear that during process I-II the gas reaches such a high temperature at which the corresponding hyperbola does not intersect the line I-II, but only touches it.

Since the point with coordinates $P$ and $V$, corresponding to the maximum temperature, lies on both the hyperbola and the straight line, its coordinates must satisfy two equations: the equation of the hyperbola

$$PV = \frac{m}{\mu} RT$$

and the equation of the line

$$P = \alpha V + \beta$$

(α and β are constants. We will define them a little later). Substituting $P$ from the second equation into the first, we obtain a quadratic equation for $V$:

$$\alpha V^2 + \beta V - \frac{m}{\mu} RT = 0.$$

Since a hyperbola and a straight line must have only one common point, this equation can only have one root, that is, its determinate must be equal to zero:

$$\beta^2 + 4\alpha \frac{m}{\mu} RT = 0.$$

Solving the last equation for $T$, we find

$$T = -\frac{\beta^2 \mu}{4\alpha m R}.$$

In this expression you need to substitute the values ​​of the coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Since points I and II belong to the same line $P = \alpha V + \beta$, then $P_1 = \alpha V_1 + \beta$ and $P_2 = \alpha V_2 + \beta$. Solving this system of equations, we find

$$\alpha = -\frac{P_2 - P_1}{V_1 - V_2}, \quad \beta = \frac{P_2 V_1 - P_1 V_2}{V_1 - V_2}.$$

$$T = \frac{(P_2 V_1 - P_1 V_2)^2 \mu}{4 (P_2 - P_1) (V_1 - V_2) m R}.$$

Substituting the numerical values ​​of the quantities included in this expression, we obtain

$$T \approx 490^\circ \text{ K}.$$
