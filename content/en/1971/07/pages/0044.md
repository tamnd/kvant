First, an image practically never radiates equally in all directions; This is due, in particular, to the finite dimensions of the line and mirrors. A flat mirror, for example, does not change the luminous intensity: the luminous intensity of the image is equal to the luminous intensity of the source. However, it must be taken into account that the image does not emit light in all directions. Indeed, point $A$ (Fig. 2) is illuminated by both the lamp and its image in the mirror, but point $B$ cannot be illuminated by the image. The luminous intensity of the image in this direction is zero.

Let's look at another example.

Source $O$ illuminates screen $P$ through diverging lens $J$ (Fig. 3). The source image $O'$ is closer to the screen than the source; if its luminous intensity is the same as that of the source, then the screen is illuminated brighter than the lens would be. When there was no lens, all the light that came in the $AOB$ cone hit the screen. If there is a lens, some of this light passes through a lot of the screen, and the illumination of the screen should decrease.

Obviously, this is due to the fact that the luminous intensity of the image $O'$ is less than the luminous intensity of the source $O$. Indeed, the luminous intensity is the ratio of the luminous flux to the solid angle, and we see that after the lens the light spreads to a larger solid angle, and

$$\frac{\Omega}{\Omega'} = \frac{(O'J)^2}{(OJ)^2}.$$

Therefore, $$\frac{I_0}{(OJ)^2} = \frac{I_1}{(O'J)^2}.$$ If the illumination of the screen without a lens was $E_0 = \frac{I_0}{(OC)^2}$, then with a lens

$$E_1 = \frac{I_1}{(O'C)^2} = \frac{E_0 (O'J)^2 (OC)^2}{(OJ)^2 (O'C)^2} < E_0.$$

The ratio of the luminous intensity of the image and the source is always equal to the ratio of the squares of their distances from the optical element creating the image. Let us prove this, for example, for an image in a volgut mirror.
