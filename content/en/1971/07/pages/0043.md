About problems in photometry

V. E. Belonuchkin

Photometric problems usually cause great difficulties in constructing them into instruments.

To a large extent, this is due to the inability to correctly apply the law of processing squares.

In order to clearly understand the meaning of a particular law, one must understand from what messages and under what assumptions it was derived, when it is valid and when it is not, how it must be clarified if it cannot be applied immediately.

What is the law of processed squares?

Let's remember its content: illumination is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the light source.

What is illumination? As is known, this is the ratio of the luminous flux to the area on which it falls; if light comes from point O (Fig. 1) and spreads evenly in all directions, then, for example, to the area $P_1$ and $P_2$, which are visible from point O at the same solid angles, there will be equal light fluxes. And the areas are proportional to the squares of the distance from point O. It should be noted that this reasoning is valid for an exact source that uniformly emits light in different directions, that is, equal luminous fluxes are emitted into any two identical solid angles.

If the light source has such properties, then the law of processed squares is simply a specific expression of the law of conservation of energy: due to the rectilinearity of the distance of light, the same energy (the same luminous flux) falls on the area that illuminates a given solid angle, regardless of the distance from the source this area is located.

Therefore, a comprehensive characteristic of a point light source is luminous intensity—the luminous flux emitted by the source into a unit solid angle. If an image is obtained using any optical element (lens, mirror), then it, naturally, is also a light source. What can be said about its luminous power?
