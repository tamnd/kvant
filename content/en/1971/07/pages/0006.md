Hooke and Edmond Halley. Halley said that he was able to obtain Kepler's third law by combining the law of gravity and Newton's laws of motion, but only for the case of circular orbits. Movement in a circular orbit can occur if an external force acts on the planet, directed towards the center of the circle and equal to $Mv^2/R$. This force is created by the attraction between the planet and the Sun. Pozny $\gamma \frac{M_C M}{R^2} = \frac{Mv^2}{R}$. If we remember that the speed of the planet’s motion in orbit is $v = \frac{2\pi R}{T}$, then, substituting this expression into the previous formula, we obtain Kepler’s law:

$$\gamma \frac{M_C}{R} = \frac{4\pi^2 R^2}{T^2},$$

or $\frac{R^2}{T^2} = \text{const.}$

When Halley finished his story, Wren offered a prize to anyone who could prove that the law of gravity decreasing in inverse proportion to the square of the distance is also true for motion in elliptical orbits.

Halley turned to Newton. Newton replied that he had already done this, and in November 1684 the manuscript with the calculations was in the hands of Halley. It took several years to prepare the manuscript and print it. Finally, in September 1687, Newton's book was published. It was called “Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy.” The laws applied to the study of the planets of the solar system have provided important insights. Thus, Newton “weighed” the Sun, expressing its mass in units of earth masses:

$$\gamma \frac{M_C M_3}{R^2} = M_3 \frac{v^2}{R_3} = M_3 \frac{4\pi^2 R^2}{R_3 T_3^2},$$

$$M_C = \frac{4\pi^2 R_3^3}{\gamma T_3^2},$$

$$\gamma \frac{M_3 M_\pi}{R^2} = M_\pi \frac{v^2}{R_\pi} = M_\pi \frac{4\pi^2 R_\pi^2}{R_\pi T_\pi^2},$$

$$M_3 = \frac{4\pi^2 R_\pi^3}{\gamma T_\pi^2},$$

where $R_3$ and $R_\pi$ are the radii of the Earth and Moon orbits, respectively.

The result was very interesting. The Moon is a satellite of the Earth, and the Earth is a satellite of the Sun. The mass of the satellite decreases each time. In the first equation, when the law of gravity is applied to the Sun and Earth, the mass of the Earth is reduced, and in the second, the mass of the Moon is reduced. Thus, Newton expressed the masses of the Earth and the Sun in terms of quantities easily accessible by astronomical changes - orbital radii and periods of revolution. The value $\gamma$ remained unknown to him. But if we take the ratio of the solar and terrestrial masses, then the constant $\gamma$ is reduced and the formula is obtained

$$\frac{M_C}{M_3} = \frac{R_3^3 T_2^2}{R_3^3 T_3^2} = \left[ \frac{\text{расстояние Земля — Солнце}}{\text{расстояние Луна — Земля}} \right]^3 \times \left[ \frac{1 \text{ месцц}}{1 \text{ год}} \right]^2.$$

Thus, Newton was able to express the ratio of the masses of the Sun and the Earth in terms of quantities known to astronomers.

To calculate the mass of the Sun in more familiar units, say kilograms, it was necessary to estimate the mass of the Earth. Newton knew only its volume. The average density of the Earth's substance in his time was not known. The only thing that was certain was that the density of the continents was greater than the density of water. But how many times does the average density of the Earth exceed the density of ocean water? Newton concluded that the average density of the Earth lies between 5 $e/cm^3$ and 6 $e/cm^3$. Only ninety years later, Sir Henry Cavendish “weighed” the Earth, changing the values ​​$\gamma$ using elegant round scales. The Earth's density turned out to be 5.5 $e/cm^3$.

How to determine the mass of the Moon? After all, it is abbreviated in all formulas. Nevertheless, Newton found a way to estimate this value.
