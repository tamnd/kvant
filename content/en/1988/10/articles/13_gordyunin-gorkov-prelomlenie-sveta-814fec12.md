Refraction of light

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. A. GORDYUNIN,
P. L. GORBKOV

Let us recall the basic laws of the geometry of light rays.

In a homogeneous, open-air medium, light propagates in a straight line. An optical characteristic of a medium is its refractive index $n > 1$, which indicates how many times the speed of light in the medium is less than the speed of light in a vacuum. A medium with a higher refractive index is called optically denser (or simply denser).

When a light ray falls on the interface between two media, it is divided into two rays (in the general case of different intensities) - reflected and refracted (rays $1'$ and $2$ on figure 1), lying in the same plane with the parasitic one. In this case, the angle $\gamma$ between the normal to the interface and the reflected beam (reflection angle) is equal to the angle $\alpha_1$ between the normal and incident beam (incidence angle). The angle of incidence $\alpha_1$ with the angle of refraction $\alpha_2$ (i.e., the angle between the normally and refracted ray) are related by a relationship called Sneglius’s law:

$$n_1 \sin \alpha_1 = n_2 \sin \alpha_2.$$

This law obviously contains the fact that light rays are reciprocal: if a ray falls from the first medium at an angle $\alpha_1$, then it is projected in the second medium at an angle $\alpha_2$, and if the ray falls from the second medium at an angle

α₂, then it will move in the first medium at an angle α₁.

If a ray passes from a less dense medium to a more dense one (Fig. 1, a), it is pressed against the normal (α₂ < α₁), and if from a more dense medium to a less dense one (Fig. 1, b), it moves away from the normal (α₂ > α₁). The largest angle of incidence (90°) has a gliding ray, so a refracted ray in a denser medium (n₂ > n₁) cannot make an angle with a normal angle greater than α₀ = arcsin (n₁/n₂). On the contrary, when passing from a more dense medium to a less dense medium, rays incident at angles greater than α₀ will be completely reflected. This is why this angle is called the angle of total reflection. Mathematically, this manifests itself in the fact that equation (*) has no solutions for α₁ for α₂ > α₀ and n₂ > n₁.

These phenomena can be clearly imagined as follows. If you cover the entire interface between the media with an opaque screen, leaving only a small hole, and illuminate the screen with diffused light containing rays from all directions, then in a denser medium all the rays passing through the hole will be concentrated in a cone with an angle of 2α₀ (Fig. 2, a). It is often called the cone of total reflection. Outside this cone it will be dark. If, at a certain distance h from the flat boundary in a denser medium, an accurate light source is placed, then the observer, looking from a less dense medium, will see not the entire interface illuminated, but only a circle with radius (Fig. 2, b)

$$R = h \mathrm{tg} \alpha_0 = \frac{hn_1}{\sqrt{n_2^2 - n_1^2}}.$$

So, from the law of refraction of light rays (*) two fundamental facts follow - the presence of total reflection and non-rectilinear detuning of light in an inexpensive medium. The distance of the second fact is more detailed.

It is easiest to study the path of a ray in a layered medium, in which the refractive index varies along one coordinate axis (for example, X). Such a medium can be represented as a set of thin plates with certain refractive indices nᵢ. Matching is performed at the interface boundaries.

n₁ sin α₁ = n₂ sin α₂ = ... = nₙ sin αₙ, since the angle of refraction in each is the angle of incidence for the next layer (Fig. 3, a). In other words, a match is made along the selected direction.

n(x) sin α(x) = const.

This important and red law (it is called the generalized Snell's law) indicates that the presence of intermediate words does not affect the relationship between the angle of incidence from the first medium and the angle of refraction in the latter. If, of course, the ray generally should reach the last environment, and does not turn, having experienced complete reflection (Fig. 3, b). In fact, when calculating various optical systems, one may not take into account the presence of thin layers between media - layers of glue between lenses in complex lenses, glass walls of vessels in which water is poured, etc.

Task 1. A ray of light falls on a plane-parallel plate with a thickness of $H=1$ cm from a stack with a refractive index of $n=1,73$ (Fig. 4). Due to multiple reflections from the edges of the plate, a number of light spots are formed on the screen E. Find the distance between the spots if the angle of focus is $\alpha=60^\circ$, and the beam above is perpendicular to the refraction of the screen. The plane of the beam coincides with the plane figure.

After a single refraction on both faces of the plate, the beam exits the plate parallel to the parachute beam. Since during further reflections and refractions the angles of emphasis on the face are the same, all rays hitting the screen are parallel to the parachute one. We find the distance $d$ between these rays, equal to the distance between the spots on the screen, from figure:

$$d = AC \cos \alpha, \ AC = 2H \ tg \beta,$$

$$tg \beta = \frac{\sin \beta}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2 \beta}}, \ sin \beta = \frac{\sin \alpha}{n},$$

where

$$d = \frac{H \sin 2\alpha}{\sqrt{n^2-\sin^2 \alpha}} \approx 0,58 \text{ см.}$$

Task 2. Estimate the error when fixing the angular position of a star visible from the Earth at an angle $\beta=45^\circ$ above the horizon. The refractive index of air at the Earth's surface is $n=1,0003$.

The position of the star, visible from Earth, differs from the true one due to the transformation of atmospheric rays. The thickness of the atmosphere, i.e. the height at which there is practically no air and therefore the refractive index is equal to unity, is several tens of kilometers. This is much less than the radius of the Earth, so in this case the atmosphere can be considered flat. Its refractive index subsequently changes from unity at the upper layers to a value of $n>1$ at the Earth's surface. Parallel rays come from the star, indicating the upper layers of the atmosphere at an angle $\pi/2-\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the true angular position of the star above the horizon. And we see the star at an angle $\beta>\alpha$ (Fig. 5). According to the law of refraction in an elephant medium

$$\sin \left( \frac{\pi}{2} - \alpha \right) = n \sin \left( \frac{\pi}{2} - \beta \right),$$

or

$$\cos \left( \beta - (\beta - \alpha) \right) = (1 + (n-1)) \cos \beta.$$

Given that $n-1 \ll 1$ and therefore $\beta-\alpha \ll \beta$, the approximation has:

$$\cos \beta + (\beta-\alpha) \sin \beta = \cos \beta + (n-1) \cos \beta.$$

Thus,

$$\beta-\alpha = (n-1) \text{ ctg } \beta = 3 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ rad} \approx 1'.$$

Problem 5. If you look at a capitular glass tube from the side, then the apparent internal radius will be equal to $r$. What is the true value of this radius? Stack refractive index $n$. The outer radius of the capital is much larger than the inner one.

Let $OC$ be the line connecting the eye and the center of the tube, $A$ the extreme point of the internal diameter of the capitlara (Fig. 8). The distance from the image (minimal) of point $A$ to the axis $OC$ is equal to the visible radius of the internal channel $r$.

We know that all rays emanating from the point $A$ at small angles will converge at one point after refraction. Since we are not interested in the exact location of this point, but only its distance from $OC$, it is enough to consider the path of only one ray $ABD$, the one that at the exit from the capitular is parallel to the $OC$ axis - after all, the image of the point $A$ will lie on its continuation (see Fig. 8). The distance between the rays $BD$ and $OC$ is $r$, the required distance is $AO = r_0$. Since the outer diameter is large compared to the inner diameter, the angle of incidence $\alpha$ will be small.

From triangle $OEB$

$$r = R\alpha n$$

(R is the outer radius of the tube). From the triangle $OAB$, taking into account the smallness of $\alpha$,

$$r_0 = R\alpha.$$

Thus,

$$r_0 = \frac{r}{n}.$$
