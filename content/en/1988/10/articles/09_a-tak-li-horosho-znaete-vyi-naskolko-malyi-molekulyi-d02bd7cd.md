F1120. To compare the thermal conductivity of different materials, it was proposed to use the following method. Two identical cylinders made of the materials under study are placed on a hot plate (see figure). One piece at a time is placed on the cylinders. Where the melt is most likely to appear, that cylinder has better thermal conductivity. Is this method correct?

Unfortunately, this simple method of comparing thermal conductivities cannot be considered correct. And here's why.

The time it takes for the upper part of the cylindrical sample to heat up to the jump melting temperature, of course, is shorter, the greater the thermal conductivity. However, this time also depends on the thermal capacity of the materials of the cylinder - the greater it is, the longer the time (large amount of heat) will be required to heat the sample to the appropriate temperature. Thus, a cylinder made of materials with high thermal conductivity will heat up more slowly than a cylinder made of materials with low thermal conductivity and slightly less thermal conductivity. This is exactly what happens, for example, in the case of high-voltage and iron.

It turns out that the proposed method makes it possible to compare other thermal characteristics of materials, namely, their thermal diffusivity. This is also an important physical quantity that characterizes the rate of change in the temperature of a substance. The coefficient of thermal conductivity is proportional to the ratio of the coefficients of thermal conductivity and thermal conductivity.

A. I. Buzdin

F1121. A metal ball with radius $q$, distant from other objects, then through a resistor with resistance $R$. A beam of electric currents hits the ball, the speed of the shaft from the ball was $v$. per second

Let's consider two solutions to this problem.

The first solution is a general one.

The total energy brought by electrons per unit time is

$$W = n \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

Current $I = ne$ flows through the resistor (the number of

the number of electrons is equal to the number of electrons falling on the ball during the same time), so that energy is converted into heat in the resistor (per unit time)

$$P = I^2 R = (ne)^2 R.$$

By virtue of the law of conservation of energy, the amount of heat released per ball per unit time is equal to

$$Q = W - P = n \frac{mv^2}{2} - (ne)^2 R = \frac{nmv^2}{2} \left(1 - n \frac{2e^2R}{mv^2}\right).$$

Ball potential (more precisely, the potential difference between the ball and the ground)

$$\varphi = IR = neR.$$

On the other side,

$$\varphi = k \frac{q}{Q}.$$

Thus, the charge of the ball

$$q = \frac{\varphi_Q}{k} = \frac{neR_Q}{k} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 neR_Q.$$

Note that electrons hit the ball if their kinetic energy is greater than the work expended on overcoming the repulsion from the ball:

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} > e\varphi.$$

Substituting $\varphi = neR$, we transform this condition in the form:

$$n < \frac{mv^2}{2e^2R}.$$

In this case, the expression in brackets for defining $Q$ is positive: $Q > 0$. Thus, it is clear that the selection of numbers in the conditions cannot be arbitrary.

If initially the number of electrons hitting the ball was greater than $n$, then the potential of the ball gradually increases, so that at some point the initial part of the electrons stopped hitting the ball (due to electron repulsion). If initially the potential of the ball was greater than or equal to $\varphi$, then the electrons did not hit the ball, and the charge flowed off it until the regime described in the problem was established.

The second solution allows you to feel more deeply what the electronic power of the source is.

Let's imagine our system as an electrical target consisting of a source with EMF $\mathcal{E}$ and zero internal resistance and two series connecting “resistors”. The resistance of one of them is the effective resistance of the ball $R_{\text{шар}}$, the other is the known resistance $R$. We will call a current source a device that accelerates electrons (for example, the well-known electron gun). Here the electron gains energy $mv^2/2 = e\mathcal{E}$. Hence the emf of the source is equal to

$$\mathcal{E} = \frac{mv^2}{2e}.$$

The goal is closed through grounding (we can assume that

The accelerating device receives exactly those electrons that went to ground).

Now our task is to determine the effective resistance of the ball $R_{\text{ш}}$, since the required amount of heat released on the ball can be found from the formula

$$Q = I^2 R_{\text{ш}} = (ne)^2 R_{\text{ш}}.$$

Let's write Ohm's law for our circuit:

$$\mathcal{E} = I(R + R_{\text{ш}}),$$

from where, knowing $\mathcal{E}$, $I$ and $R$, we find $R_{\text{ш}}$:

$$R_{\text{ш}} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{I} - R = \frac{mv^2}{2ne^2} - R.$$

Substituting $R_{\text{ш}}$ into the expression for $Q$, after simple transformations we obtain the already familiar expression

$$Q = n \frac{mv^2}{2} \left(1 - n \frac{2e^2R}{mv^2}\right).$$

F1122. Famous cases of observing the world of the sea in the desert. At what distance from what is being observed does such a mirage appear? Consider that the speed of light in the ground layer in the desert changes according to the law $c(z) = c_0(1 - az)$, where $c_0$ is the speed of light at the surface of the earth, $z$ is the height above the surface.

A change in the speed of light in the air (and hence its refractive index) with altitude leads to the bending of light rays (to refraction).*) This phenomenon causes the appearance of a sea mirage in the desert.

Let the observed height of which $h$ stand on the ground (see figure). Let's consider the light rays coming out from the point $A$. A ray making an angle $\alpha_0$ with the vertical touches the surface of the earth and then goes up. Angles $\alpha > \alpha_0$ correspond to rays that also go upward. The rays coming at angles $\alpha < \alpha_0$ rest against the ground. Using the property of reversibility of light rays, we obtain that up to the angle $\alpha_0$ the observer sees the earth, and starting from $\alpha_0$ the sky. But since the beam comes to the observed space, it seems to him that he sees the head (after all, a person recognizes its propagation of light as rectilinear).

Now let's find the required distance $H$.

According to the generalized law of light refraction for a layered medium,

$$\frac{\sin \alpha_0}{\sin \beta_0} = \frac{c(h)}{c_0},$$

where $c(h)$ is the speed of light at height $h$, $\beta_0$ is the angle of the beam under study with the vertical at the surface of the earth.

*) You can read more about this phenomenon in the article by A. A. Varlamov and A. I. Malyarovsky “A speaking tube the length of the equator?” («Kvant», 1985, No. 2) and in the article by S. A. Gordinin and P. L. Gorkov “Refraction of Light” in this issue of the magazine.
