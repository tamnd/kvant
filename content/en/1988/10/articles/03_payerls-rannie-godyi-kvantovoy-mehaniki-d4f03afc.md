P. PEIERLS

It may seem immodest that I will teach by telling a story about myself. The community doesn’t do that, but it talks about my personal impressions, and therefore I must first introduce myself. I entered the university in 1925. Now I would like to be able to say that I chose physics because it was an interesting subject and was rapidly developing. However, this would not be fair. Actually I wanted to be an engineer. That was the time when innovation and new automation were developing, and it was natural that the boy wanted to become an engineer. But I want to become an engineer. But the report told me that I was not fit for this, that I would not be a good engineer. Therefore, I chose, as it seemed to me, the closest thing to my dream - physics.

I went to university in Berlin, the city where my home was. My parents thought I was too young to go far. There I attended lectures by Max Planck. These were the worst lectures I have ever given. He read from his

books on theoretical physics literally. If you had a copy, you could follow the text line by line. Planck was very famous, but at that time we did not yet know why. For the first time, new words to Planck's ideas, Bohr's atom and all sorts of such things were presented at the lectures of Walter Bothe (later he became a nuclear physicist). There it became clear to me that something new and very interesting was happening in physics.

After a year, I decided that I was old enough to leave Berlin. I moved to Munich, where the best teacher in theoretical physics, Arnold Sommerfeld, worked at that time. It was a wonderful time for theoretical physics. Quantum mechanics was created, and now it is very difficult to imagine how quickly everything happened - in fact, in two years.

It was during this period that I entered the university and within a year I could read works on quantum mechanics. But I was late to its formation. If life could be repeated, I would like to be born a year or two earlier. Felix Bloch later explained to me that not every person is capable of creating new theories and that we appeared just in time to apply them. I think he was right. This was the best time to take some problem in which the solution of strange physics led to contradictions, and apply new methods to it.

So, I came to Sommerfeld. Sommerfeld was short in stature, but had enormous services. We sometimes called it "the top half and then some." Sommerfeld looked quite important and had the meaning of Geheimrat - Privy Councilor. This can be compared to the modern meaning of academician, and he liked to be called that. One American student did not know this at first and addressed Sommerfeld simply as “Herr Professor.” A week or two later everything was explained to him, and at the next meeting he already turned to Sommerfeld

...Sommerfeld had a small hut for rest in the mountains, where he sometimes invited graduate students and teachers. There he gave me the opportunity to speak at my first seminar. The works of Dirac and Yurdan on the theory of transformation had just appeared. Sommerfeld said: “We have not yet had time to understand these works, and perhaps you can explain them to us.” It was still a difficult task for a student who had spent only a year at the university. However, I took it up with satisfaction. I don’t know what other seminar participants learned, but I myself learned a lot. He and I were very prepared. He is still one year older than me. It's not that big of a deal now, but I can still learn a lot from him.

I spent a year and a half in Munich and would have happily stayed longer, but Sommerfeld was invited to America for six months to a year. On Sommerfeld’s advice, I went to Leipzig to work for Heisenberg.

Heisenberg was completely unimpressed by Sommerfeld. There was no trace of Geheimrat there. In appearance, in any case, he was a very modest man. Typically, there was a seminar once a week, and there was always tea before the seminar. The professor himself went to the pastry shop and chose suitable products. At least that's how I remember it. True, later one of our colleagues, who was then an assistant to Heisenberg, assured me that it was his task to follow the hedgehogs. This makes sense, since he was from Vienna and knew only about such things. I probably remember the period when he was not in Leipzite.

Heisenberg loved the game of ping-pong and was a very good player. In our free time we all played. Once a Chinese physicist came and was able to beat Heisenberg. It became a sensation. I later heard that when Heisenberg was traveling on a steamboat from America to Japan, he spent the entire trip practicing so that such terrible news would not happen again.

Heisenberg did not like pure mathematics and saw it only as a necessary tool. His method was as follows. Reflecting on

problem, he first guessed what the solution would be, and then selected a mathematical method that would give this particular solution. A good method if you have the same powerful intuition as Heisenberg. For others, this approach is a little dangerous.

In Leipzig I managed to write my first work. It concerned the so-called anomalous Hall effect.

When a current flows through a piece of metal and the whole thing is placed in a magnetic field, a transverse voltage appears. It is clear that this happens due to the deflection of electrons in a magnetic field. But in some metals the effect has the opposite sign. Now we explain this by the fact that in such substances the current is carried not by electrons, but by holes. But at that time there was no clarity on this issue, and Heisenberg simply told me that Bloch had developed the electronic theory of metals, and could I apply it to this problem. To my great satisfaction, it turned out that all this really could be done, and I solved the problem.

I spent a year in Leipzig. Heisenberg was invited to America and took a leave of absence. On his advice, I moved to Zurich to work for Pauli. I wrote my dissertation with him. I must say that I am very grateful to this system of vacations and invitations to America, thanks to which I got such a wonderful combination of teachers.

As you know, Pauli was also famous for making very unkind remarks to everyone. One of the sharpest attacks was made by Pauli in a conversation with Ernst Stiokelberr. He said: “Paulie, don’t talk so fast, I can’t think as fast as you.” Pauli replied: “It’s okay that you think slowly. I object to you posting faster than you can think.”

Someone showed him the work of a young theorist, knowing that the work was not very good, but still wanting to get to know Pauli to me; he read the work and said sadly: “This is not even wrong.”

Pauli had this habit: going to the movies in the evening, to a concert or something like that. He returned at about 11 o'clock and immediately sat down to work. Pauli worked quite a long time and therefore got up late. One day he was invited to a meeting at 9 o’clock in the morning, but he refused, saying: “No, no, I can’t stay without sleep for that long.”
Once Pauli was in a foreign city and asked the local physicist how he could find a cinema. He explained and the next day asked Pauli if he managed to get there. Pauli replied: “You express yourself quite clearly if you are not talking about physics.”
I spent three years with Pauli, and I had to listen to similar things more than once. But it was no longer very difficult. Nobody showed up for Paulin for a long time, they believe it because he was just as critical of himself and his own ideas. One time he explained to me why he was doing this. In his opinion, there are truly sensitive people with whom you can live only by stepping on their “great power” a sufficient number of times. True, I don’t think this was the real reason.
...We have Landdu twice in Zurich. The first time he came was in January 1930. At that time there were no diplomatic relations between Switzerland and the USSR. He was given permission for two weeks, so they extended the term for another two weeks. Everyone tried very hard to achieve this. But in the end he was forced to leave. Then he joked: “Lenin was in Switzerland for several years, but the revolution did not begin here. Obviously, they are rich that I can do this.” A year later he arrived with a Rockefeller scholarship, and then there were no more problems: he could stay as long as he wanted. We worked together. Landdu was still very young, but already a very thorough physicist. When some work appeared that interested him, he did not read it, but immediately began to calculate it himself. And if he agreed with what was written there, then he considered the work good. He liked to organize everything. For example, he divided physicists into different classes: his first class included Bohr and Sommerfeld; Einstein was in a special class, by himself. Landdu modestly placed himself in the second class. He treated other issues of life in the same “systematic” way.
Landdu really disliked beards, saying that it was a relic of Victorian times, especially on young people. Among us there was one physicist who did not wear a beard, but had very long sideburns. Landdu also considered this bourgeois and, calling his wife, asked: “When will you convince your husband to shave off those funny sideburns?” He argued that in the West, in Zurich, there are more beards than in Russia, in Leningrad. We made a bet and counted on the street how many beards we met. Then, when I arrived in Leningrad, we carried out the same calculation and found that there were more beards in Leningrad.

I won the bet, and Landu tried to explain this by saying that collectivization was underway and many peasants moved to the city.

Landu was convinced that only young theorists could do useful things. True, he later changed this idea. One day we were talking, and the name of a theorist came up that he had not heard of. Upon learning that he was 27 years old, Landu said: “So young and already so unknown!”

Besides Landu, there were other people in Zurich, including George Gamow. Gamow was already replaced by then; he was also a great comedian and loved all sorts of jokes. One day we went to the mountains and came to the top of a peak with a rather interesting name. There, Gamow pulled out a piece of paper from his pocket - it was a letter to the journal Nature about some kind of nuclear reaction, which he did not quite finish. Gamow sat on the top and finished his task.

Having written the last lines, he put the name of the peak where they were written, and thanked his companions for the opportunity to work there.

...In those days, just like now, physicists loved to travel, attending conferences at the SSSSI. The travel back then, however, was not paid, so you often had to sit all night in the corner of a third-class railway carriage. But when I received an invitation to the congress of the Physical Society in Odessa, at least within the country I was united with great comfort, as a guest. I was called there by Yakov Ilyich Frenkel, who read my works.

One of his favorite places to travel was Konenhagen, where Niels Bohr worked. He was a wonderful person. Bohr hated to squeeze people, but at the same time he could not

allow something to be said that is contrary to the truth. And these two qualities made a strange combination. Thus, Bohr once said: “I say this not to criticize, but it is complete erwunda.” He also once said that clarity and truth are additional concepts, and in his works he rather approached the boundaries of truth.

Bohr's writing process was quite complex. It began with Bohr dictating, and one of the guests had to write everything down. Then, when editing began, expressions were changed so that everything written was unconditionally correct. There were many changes, pages were rewritten, then retyped by machine, then corrected again, etc. Finally, everything was sent to the journal of the Danish Academy, where Bohr's work, of course, was very much appreciated. After which work began on proofs, the number of which sometimes reached 16.

Obviously, Bohr treated this way not only with words. One day he came to see the new building that was being built for the institute. The master, who knew him well, said: “Professor Bohr, do you see this steppe? If you want to move it again, then decide quickly, because in three hours the concrete will stop.”

As befits a professor, Bohr was rather absent-minded. I remember that during the conversation he smoked cigars all the time. He smokes and suddenly asks if there are any matches. They give him matches. He is trying to light a cigar without interrupting the conversation, and this is quite difficult. Then he would put the matches in his pocket, and five minutes later he would ask the same question again, and everything would start all over again. I kept the finished piece of chalk for a long time: he somehow held the cigar and the chalk in one hand.

but also about how to find work for scientists from Germany and Austria. This is not an easy time for scientists in general. There was an economic crisis, universities were not bursting, and space became available when someone retired or died. A doctoral dissertation did not at all guarantee a place for scientific research. I had a Rockefeller fellowship for a year, and I could, coming from Corixa, spend half my time in Rome and half in Cambridge. Hans Bethe, who spent the winter in Cambridge and the summer in Rome, had already done this before me. I did the opposite and still thought I had found the best solution.

In Rome I had the opportunity to meet Enrico Fermi, who was also a wonderful physicist. When he was asked about a problem, he almost always took a book from the shelf in which this problem had already been solved by him. Mostly they were simple - Fermi did not like complex problems. But here the question arises: what are called simple problems? Didn't they become simple after Fermi solved them?

My greatest impression of Fermi came later, already in Los Alams, during the testing of the atomic bomb. Everyone, of course, wanted to know how powerful the bomb was. There were a lot of tools to determine it, but it took some time. And Fermi prepared small pieces of paper, and when the blast wave came to us (we were about 15 kilometers from the explosion site), he released these pieces. Based on the distance they flew, he was able to quickly determine the power of the explosion. I don’t know what struck me most then: the idea of ​​the method or the fact that he precisely determined the moment when it was necessary to let go of the pieces of paper. I'm sure that in his place I would either release the pieces too early, or forget to release them at all.
