If you think the title of this article is a joke, you are wrong. Such cities exist. But don’t rush to imagine how space rockets throw them straight into the fiery star. This is wrong. However, things still cannot be done without space, but more on that a little later. As you will soon learn, the temperature on the surface of the Sun is high enough to melt a device made of any refractory metal. And rockets are not cheap yet. This means that you need to be able to measure temperature from a distance; non-contact methods are needed. The need for such methods also arises in more earthly matters. Metallurgists must know the temperature of the liquid metal in the furnace and the temperature of the red-hot molten metal, which quickly does not exist in the rolling mill. An aviation engineer needs to measure the temperature of the blades of a rapidly rotating gas turbine. There are many such examples when it is impossible to “scratch” the measured object. Luckily, you don't have to do this, and here's why.

All bodies constantly emit energy into the surrounding space in the form of thermal radiation. By its nature, warm radiation is electromagnetic waves, i.e., interconnected oscillations of electric and magnetic fields that propagate in a vacuum at the speed of light $c=300\ 000$ km/s. By analogy with water waves, the distance between two adjacent “crests” of oscillations is called the wavelength. Warm radiation emitted by heat carries information about body temperature. At high temperatures, we see part of the thermal radiation in the form of a glow (such a glow is emitted by all hot bodies - an ingot of metal, the Sun, and stars). Why only part? The fact is that warm radiation is a “mixture” of vibrations with different wavelengths - from $\lambda \approx 0,1$ µm to $\lambda \approx 100$ µm. And the human eye reacts to electromagnetic vibrations with wavelengths lying in the narrow visible range: 0.4-0.7 microns. At temperatures below 600 °C, bodies do not glow, but they emit thermal radiation, only the wavelengths of this radiation lie in the so-called infrared region invisible to the eye: 0.7-100 microns. A person can identify infrared radiation by its effect on the skin. For example, a well-heated dog “bursts with heat”, burns the face and hands at a distance of 2-3 meters - this is the effect of infrared radiation. If the body temperature exceeds 3000 °C, then wavelengths of 0.1–0.4 μm appear in warm radiation; This is the so-called ultrafilt range (also invisible).

Now it remains to find out how the temperature is “encrypted” in warm radiation and how to extract it from there. To do this, we need to know how the radiation spectrum changes - the dependence of the emitted power on the wavelength. It is the spectrum that contains all the information about temperature. It is clear that first of all it is necessary to somehow “separate” the warm radiation, to separate radiation with a certain wavelength from the “mixture”. The first spectrum of solar radiation was studied by the great English physicist Isaac Newton. He passed a thin ray of the Sun through a glass prism, and since light with different wavelengths is refracted in the material of the sign in different ways, on the screen behind the sign a radig (the colors of the rainbow) was obtained.

In our time, to repeat Newton's experiment, a common incandescent lamp can be used as a source of study. The visible part of the thermal radiation from the source appears on the screen in the form of a radial stripe. If an opaque plate with a narrow slit is placed directly in front of the screen, then radiation whose wavelengths lie in a narrow range will fall on the screen. By moving the slit, we can see radiation of any color radium - red, orange, yellow, green, etc.

Now we need to change what energy comes at different wavelengths. For spectral measurements, photoelectric receivers, or photodetectors, are used - devices that can convert radiation incident on them, including invisible radiation, into electric current. The photodetector is placed behind the output slit of the spectra and connected to some kind of recording device (recorder). A special electric motor evenly applies the photodetector together with the slit, and all sections of the spectrum pass in succession in front of the photodetector, and the current generated, proportional to the radiation power, is recorded using a recorder. As a result, the recorder displays the dependence of the radiation power on the wavelength, i.e., the radiation spectrum.

I feel that the impatient reader is already asking: what about the thermometer for Coln? A little more patience - we just have to figure out how the temperature of the body and the spectrum of its thermal radiation are related.

If the spectrometer is applied to the filament of an incandescent lamp, then the result of the measurements will be a curve with a maxim - like curve 1 on figure 2. The emission spectrum of any condensed (solid and liquid) substances and dense gases has the same form. When body temperature changes, the general appearance of the spectrum remains the same, but the maximum in the spectrum will shift. However, one of the replacement properties of thermal radiation is that the wavelength $\lambda_m$ corresponding to this maximum and the temperature of the radiating body $T$ are related by the simple relation $^*$

$$\lambda_m T = A, \quad A = 3000 \text{ мкм} \cdot \text{К}.$$

After the name of the first creator of this correspondence, it is called Wien’s displacement law, and the constant value $A$ is called Wien’s constant (the exact value of this constant is $A = (2897,5 \pm 0,3) \text{ мкм} \cdot \text{К})$.

Well, now we know everything in order to measure the temperature of the Coln surface using the radiation spectrum. The maximum emitted energy in the solar spectrum occurs at wavelength $\lambda_m \approx 0,5 \text{ мкм}$ (curve 2 at figure 2). Substituting this value into Wien's law, we obtain the surface temperature Colnа:

$$T = \frac{3000}{0,5} \text{ K} = 6000 \text{ K} \approx 5700 \text{ °C}.$$

So, you are convinced that you can “ruffle” the Sun only from a distance. But it’s better not from the surface of the Earth. When passing through the earth's atmosphere, part of the solar radiation is absorbed by water vapor, oxygen, and carbon dioxide, and absorption occurs in narrow wavelength regions. As a result, the spectrum of solar radiation reaching the Earth's surface is greatly distorted compared to the original one (curve 3 at figure 2). To obtain an undisturbed spectrum, the equipment is placed in high packages, reaching the upper rarefied layers of the earth's atmosphere. The best conditions for measurements are, of course, on board orbital stations, located above the Earth's atmosphere. And “degreemeters” for the Sun - spectrometers - actually fly in space.

Now let's turn from heavenly problems to earthly ones. What is permitted to Jupiter is not permitted to the bull, said the ancient Romans. This also happens in science - the method of measuring temperature according to Wien's law is suitable for estimating the temperature of the Sun and stars, but is not suitable, say, in metallurgy. At temperatures higher than metal processing, usually not exceeding 2000 °C, the maximum radiation is in the invisible infrared region, and this is a conventional measurement. In addition, the error in determining the wavelength corresponding to the maximum is up to 10%, and the temperature value is determined with the same error. But metallurgists need 1% accuracy. Therefore, to measure temperature they use special devices - optical pyrometers. The simplest of them is a visual brightness pyrometer. Temperature in this device is measured by the brightness of thermal radiation - with increasing temperature, the brightness increases, everything is clear here. But the pyrometer is “physical”, which means that measurements are carried out “by eye”; So the report is highly accurate? Indeed, a person can determine the temperature by the brightness of the radiation only very approximately. However, when comparing the brightness of a person, the eye works extremely accurately - it notices differences corresponding to fractions of a degree. The operation of the device is based on this property of the eye. The pyrometer (Fig. 3) consists of a telescope $T$, in the body of which a filter F made of red glass and a special electric lamp L are placed. The operator observes the image of the litts lamp against the background of the heated surface P of the body, the temperature of which must be determined. The lamp is connected to battery B and rheostat R. If no current flows through the lamp, the dimming is visible as a temperal strip against the background of the luminous surface of the body. By adjusting the current through the litts of the lamp, and therefore its brightness, with a rheostat, the operator ensures that the brightness of the litts and the heated surface is equal. At this moment, litts becomes no difference against the background of the surface, it seems to disappear (this is where another name for the device comes from - a pyrometer with a disappearing filament). Now all that remains is to measure the lamp current using an amperor, and using a previously made calibration curve (i.e. The temperature of the heated surface being studied is determined from the curve expressing the dependence of the lamp current on the surface temperature.
