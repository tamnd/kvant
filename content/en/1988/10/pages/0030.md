M1108. In a convex zero-line ($n > 4$), no three diagonals pass through the same point inside the polygon. What is the maximum number of diagonals in it that can be checked so that all the parts into which they split the zero-leaf turn out to be triangles?

Answer: $[3n/2] - 4$ (where $[x]$ is the integer part of $x$). The divisions given at figure 1 show that the maximum number $f(n)$ of diagonals satisfying the conditions of the problem is at least $3m - 4 = [3n/2] - 4$ for $n = 2m$, and at least $3m - 3 = [3n/2] - 4$ for $n = 2m + 1$. Now it remains to prove that

$$f(n) \leq [3n/2] - 4.$$

For this we need the following Lemma. If some set of diagonals of a $n$-gon ($n \geq 5$) divides it into triangles, then at least one of these diagonals does not intersect the others. (We do not take into account intersections at the vertices of the polygon.)

To prove Lemma, we note that no diagonal can intersect with more than one other diagonal: otherwise, one of the pieces of the partition adjacent to the section of the diagonal between neighboring intersection points would not be a triangle, since the sum of its angles would be greater than $180^\circ$ (Fig. 2). Let us now assume that any diagonal intersects with one of the others, and only with one. Let $AC$ and $BD$ be such intersecting diagonals (Fig. 3). Since $n \geq 5$, at least one of the sides of the quadrilateral $ABCD$, for example $AB$, is a diagonal (and not a side) of the given $n$-gon. The diagonal $AB$ is included in our set, since otherwise the broken line $AOB$, where $O$ is the intersection point of $AC$ and $BD$, will not be a triangle. Therefore, there is a diagonal intersecting $AB$. But this diagonal must also intersect $AC$ or $BD$, which contradicts the remark made above.
