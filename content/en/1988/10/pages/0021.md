(The clause “practical” is associated with changes as a result of the collision of the speed of movement of the entire Earth and the speed of its rotation, which are negligible even with the collision of the Earth with a great astronomer.)

Let us now estimate these energy costs in order of magnitude.

The energy consumption $E_1$ for the destruction of rocks is proportional to the volume of the crater, i.e. $E_1 \sim R^3$. What should it (volume) be multiplied by to get destruction? It is not difficult to understand that the energy of rock destruction is the volume multiplied by the rock strength limit $\sigma_n$, i.e. $E_1 \sim \sigma_n R^3$.

Let's estimate the energy $E_2$ that goes into removing rocks from the crater. The movement of most of the mass during the formation of a crater occurs over a distance on the order of the radius $R$. For such a movement of masses in the field, the initial velocity of decomposition must also be equal in order of magnitude to $u_0 \sim \sqrt{gR}$. The total mass of rocks ejected from the crater is $m_\kappa \sim \rho R^3$. Therefore, the cost of selection increased - $E_2 \sim m_\kappa u_0^2 \approx \rho g R^4$.

Take my word for it: the energy costs of sound waves $E_3$ are always small compared to $E_1$ and $E_2$. The physical reason for this is that in any supersonic collision, a shock wave will first appear. What is it? This is a strong rolling, propagating in materials at a speed greater than the speed of sound, and the greater the stronger the rolling, the greater the density. It is the shock wave that produces both destruction and acceleration of matter. Only when the main energy of the shock wave is spent, when the rolling in the wave becomes weak and the speed becomes equal to the speed of sound, does the shock wave transform into ordinary activity, sound. Bolna is impacted approximately within the volume of the crater, and the sound escapes with little attenuation over long distances (across the entire planet).

So, the main primary energy costs are $E_1$ and $E_2$. Let us now write an approximate energy balance equation for the padenite metric (but will allow us to determine the order of magnitude of the crater radius):

$$E_0 \approx E_1 + E_2, \text{ т. е. } mv^2 \sim \sigma_n R^3 + \rho g R^4.$$

Note that kinetic energy is missing a factor of 1/2. This is because our goal is to obtain an order-of-magnitude estimate only. The coefficients in front of the terms on the right side of the equality can only be obtained by an exact solution of the problem.

For the same reason, the above equation must be written somewhat differently. Judge for yourself. Which one? - The one that is larger. And this, in turn, depends on the radius of the crater, which is still unknown.
