F1122. Famous cases of observing the world of the sea in the desert. At what distance from what is being observed does such a mirage appear? Consider that the speed of light in the ground layer in the desert changes according to the law $c(z) = c_0(1 - az)$, where $c_0$ is the speed of light at the surface of the earth, $z$ is the height above the surface.

A change in the speed of light in the air (and hence its refractive index) with altitude leads to the bending of light rays (to refraction).*) This phenomenon causes the appearance of a sea mirage in the desert.

Let the observed height of which $h$ stand on the ground (see figure). Let's consider the light rays coming out from the point $A$. A ray making an angle $\alpha_0$ with the vertical touches the surface of the earth and then goes up. Angles $\alpha > \alpha_0$ correspond to rays that also go upward. The rays coming at angles $\alpha < \alpha_0$ rest against the ground. Using the property of reversibility of light rays, we obtain that up to the angle $\alpha_0$ the observer sees the earth, and starting from $\alpha_0$ the sky. But since the beam comes to the observed space, it seems to him that he sees the head (after all, a person recognizes its propagation of light as rectilinear).

Now let's find the required distance $H$.

According to the generalized law of light refraction for a layered medium,

$$\frac{\sin \alpha_0}{\sin \beta_0} = \frac{c(h)}{c_0},$$

where $c(h)$ is the speed of light at height $h$, $\beta_0$ is the angle of the beam under study with the vertical at the surface of the earth.

*) You can read more about this phenomenon in the article by A. A. Varlamov and A. I. Malyarovsky “A speaking tube the length of the equator?” («Kvant», 1985, No. 2) and in the article by S. A. Gordinin and P. L. Gorkov “Refraction of Light” in this issue of the magazine.
