1. The forces acting on the pipes are shown in figure 17. It is clear that
$$Q_1 = mg - \mu N,$$
$$Q_2 = mg + \mu N,$$
$$F_1 = \mu (mg - \mu N),$$
$$F \leq \mu Q_2 = \mu (mg + \mu N).$$
Let us assume that the left pipe does not come off the floor, i.e. $Q_1 > 0$, and the right pipe does not slip relative to the floor, i.e. $F < \mu Q_2$. Let $a$ be the projection of the acceleration of the centers of mass of the pipes onto the X axis. According to Newton’s second law
$$\mu (mg - \mu N) - N = ma,$$
$$N - F = ma.$$
In a short time $\Delta t$, the work of forces $N$ and $\mu N$ to move a small section of the right pipe (area $K$) goes to change the kinetic energy of this pipe:
$$Nv\Delta t - \mu Nv\Delta t = \Delta (mv^2),$$
where $v$ is the speed of the pipe axis. From here
$$Nv\Delta t - \mu Nv\Delta t = 2mv\Delta v.$$
Since $\Delta v/\Delta t = a$, then
$$N - \mu N = 2ma.$$

From equation (1), (2) and (3) we obtain
$$a = \frac{\mu (1 - \mu)}{2\mu^2 - \mu^3} g,$$
$$N = \frac{2\mu}{2\mu^2 - \mu^3} mg,$$
$$F = \frac{\mu (1 + \mu)}{2\mu^2 - \mu^3} mg.$$
It is easy to verify that for $\mu = 2$ the values ​​$N$ and $F$ are positive, i.e., they make sense, and the conditions $Q_1 > 0$ and $F < \mu Q_2$ are satisfied. So, our assumption about the characteristics of the movement of pipes turned out to be correct. At $\mu = 2$

2. Let's assume that the piston is fixed, and let's see how the pressure in each part of the cylinder changes when the amount of heat $Q$ is imparted to the gas:
$$\Delta p = \frac{vR\Delta T}{V},$$
$$Q = \frac{3}{2} vR\Delta T,$$
where
$$\Delta p = \frac{2}{3} \frac{Q}{V}.$$
Thus, when the same amount of heat $Q$ is reported, the internal energy of the gas to the left and right of the piston changes by
$$\Delta U_\pi = Q - A \text{ и } \Delta U_\pi = Q + A,$$
where $A$ is the work done by the expanding gas. Obviously $\Delta U_\pi > \Delta U_\pi$. In addition, the initial internal energy of the reference gas was 3 times greater than that on the left. Thus, the internal energy of the gas on the right side of the cylinder is always greater than on the left.
On the other hand, since $U = \frac{3}{2} vRT = \frac{3}{2} pV$, then in the final state, when the pressures and volumes are the same, the internal energies of the gas in the cylinder shell must be the same. A contradiction arises. How to resolve it?
It turns out that, in fact, between the volumes $V_\pi$ and $V_\pi$ there will always remain a neometric difference $\Delta V$, which ensures the finite difference in internal energies
$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} p\Delta V.$$
