M1086. With a number cut, perform two operations: “increase by 2 times” and “increase by 1”.
For what is the smallest number from the number 0 to get: a) the number 100? b) number n?

a), 6) It is convenient to solve the problem from the end, that is, to look for the shortest way to obtain the number 0 from an arbitrary natural number n using two operations - subtracting 1 (B) and halving (D). Let r(n) be the number of operations B and D in such a shortest algorithm. If n is odd, then only operation B can be applied to it, so

$$r(2k+1)=1+r(2k).$$

Let us show by indication of k that r(2k)=1+r(k), i.e., an even number in the shortest algorithm must be divided in half. For k=1 this is clear. Let this be proven for all numbers less than k. If we first apply the operation B to the number 2k (subtract 1), then the number of operations before obtaining 0 will be no less than

$$1+r(2k-1)=2+r(2k-2)=3+r(k-1)$$

(according to indication assumption). If we first divide 2k in half, and further along the optimal path, then the number of operations will be 1+r(k)≤2+r(k-1). So the second way depends.

To find out how many operations are required in the shortest algorithm for a given number n, we represent n as a sum of powers of two: $n=2^{k_1}+2^{k_2}+...+2^{k_m}$, where $k_1$ is non-negative, and $k_2$, ..., $k_m$ are positive integers ($k_1, k_1+k_2,...$ are the numbers of double notation digits of n in which there is a unit). Then

$$r(n)=k_1+r(n/2^{k_1})=k_1+1+r(2^{k_2}+...+2^{k_m})=...$$

$$...=(k_1+1)+(k_2+1)+...+(k_m+1)=$$

$$=(k_1+k_2+...+k_m)+m=[\log_2 n]+m$$

([x] is the integer part of x), since $k_1+k_2+...+k_m$ is the exponent of the most powers of two, not exceeding n. Note that m is equal to the number of entries of the number n. In particular, for n=100 we obtain the answer to problem a): $r(100)=9$.

The margin shows the transformation of the number 100 into 0 in decimal and double notation. Since $100=2^6+2^5+2^2$, we get $r(100)=6+3=9$.

F1097. To reduce the proportion of reflected light from the surface of the stack, a thin film is applied to it, the refractive index of which is less than the refractive index of the stack (coating of optics). What is the smallest thickness of a film with a refractive index n=4/3 that should be placed on the surface of the stack so that when light containing radiation of two wavelengths with $\lambda_1=700$ nm and $\lambda_2=420$ nm is applied (normal to the surface), the reflected light is maximally attenuated for both wavelengths?

Let a plane light wave with a wavelength in vacuum $\lambda$ be incident on a film of thickness $h$. After partial reflection from the upper and lower surfaces of the film in the opposite direction (see figure), waves 1 and 2 follow. If the light wave is incident normally to the surface of the film, then the difference in the paths of reflected waves 1 and 2 is equal to twice the thickness of the film. In order for the reflected light to be as attenuated as possible, waves 1 and 2 must be in antiphase. For the condition to be met, the difference between waves 1 and 2 must be equal to an odd number of half-wavelengths in the film:

$$2h = (2k-1) \frac{\lambda}{2n} (k=1, 2, 3, ...),$$

where $\lambda/n$ is the wavelength in the film. From here

$$h = \frac{(2k-1)\lambda}{4n}.$$

As we can see, $h$ depends on the wavelength. The maximum attenuation of light at wavelengths $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ will be at the following film thicknesses:

$$h_1 = \frac{\lambda_1}{4n}, \frac{3\lambda_1}{4n}, \frac{5\lambda_1}{4n}, ... = \frac{700}{4n}, \frac{2100}{4n}, \frac{3500}{4n}, ...$$

$$h_2 = \frac{\lambda_2}{4n}, \frac{3\lambda_2}{4n}, \frac{5\lambda_2}{4n}, ... = \frac{420}{4n}, \frac{1260}{4n}, \frac{2100}{4n}, ...$$

($h_1$ and $h_2$ - in the direction). From a comparison of these two sets, we conclude that the smallest agreement values ​​of $h_1$ and $h_2$ are $\frac{2100}{4n}$ nm ≈ 394 nm.

The acceleration of the ball by the spring continues until the spring is in an equilibrium state. If by this moment the ball is picking up speed v, then the speed of the free (not fixed to the wall) end of the spring is the same. The kinetic energy of a spring, in which in an equilibrium state one end is accumulated and the other has a speed v, can be represented in the form $aMv^2/2$, where M is the mass of the spring, uniformly distributed along its length, a is a numerical coefficient, does not depend on the speed of the end of the spring.

Let us write the law of conservation of energy for all three cases (for balls with masses m, 2m, 3m), considering the initial state of the spring to be the same each time and equal to $x_0$:

$$\frac{kx_0^2}{2} = \frac{m}{2} v^2 + \frac{1}{2} aMv^2,$$

$$\frac{kx_0^2}{2} = \frac{2m}{2} \left(\frac{2}{3} v^2\right) + \frac{1}{2} aM \left(\frac{2}{3} v^2\right),$$

$$\frac{kx_0^2}{2} = \frac{3m}{2} u^2 + \frac{1}{2} aMu^2.$$

Solving these equations together, we find the speed u that a ball of mass 3m gains when fired:

$$u = v \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}.$$

F1100. A fairly long capital, immersed in a vessel with water, is hermetically sealed at the top. In this case, the liquid level in the capital falls to $\Delta h=4$ cm. What is the relative humidity of the air at the surface of the water in the vessel if the ambient air temperature is 20 °C?

When the upper end of the capital is closed, the water vapor present in the water-free upper end of the capital becomes saturated and the partial pressure of the steam increases. Because of this, the water level in the capital decreases (since at the water level in the vessel the pressure remains constant).

Let us write down the equilibrium conditions for water in capital, located at the level of the free surface of water in the vessel, before and after closing the capital:

$$p_{\pi} + p_{\beta} - \frac{\sigma}{2r} + \rho gh = p_{\pi} + p_{\beta},$$

after closing -

$$p_{\pi} + p_{\beta} - \frac{\sigma}{2r} + \rho g (h - \Delta h) = p_{\pi} + p_{\beta}.$$

Here $(p_{\pi} + p_{\beta})$ is the pressure at the surface of the water in the vessel, equal to the sum of the partial pressures of steam and dry air; $(p_{\pi} + p_{\beta} - \frac{\sigma}{2r})$ is the pressure above the curved surface of water in the open capital ($r$ is the radius of the capital, $\sigma$ is the surface tension of water); $p_{\pi}$ - saturated water vapor pressure at a temperature of 20 °C; dry air pressure $p_{\beta}$ in the capital can be considered unchanged, since the capital is quite long.

From equations (1) and (2) we obtain:

$$p_{\pi} - p_{\beta} = \rho g \cdot \Delta h,$$

or $$\frac{p_{\pi}}{p_{\beta}} = 1 - \frac{\rho g \cdot \Delta h}{p_{\beta}}.$$

But $\frac{p_{\pi}}{p_{\beta}}$ 100% is the desired air humidity at the surface of the water in the vessel. Thus,

$$\varphi = \left(1 - \frac{\rho g \cdot \Delta h}{p_{\beta}}\right) 100\%.$$

Taking the values ​​$p_{\pi} = 2,33 \cdot 10^3$ Pa from the table and substituting $\rho = 10^3$ kg/m$^3$, $g = 9,8$ m/s$^2$, $\Delta h = 4 \cdot 10^{-2}$ m, we get $\varphi \approx 83\%$.

L. G. Markovich

F1101. A light horizontal rod 2a long can be freely manipulated around a vertical axis passing through its middle. Identical massive balls are mounted on a rod and can move along it without friction and elastically reflect from the stop at its end (Fig. 1). First, the balls are fixed at distances a/2 from the axis. The rod is pressed up to angular velocity $\omega_0$, after which the balls are simultaneously released. What trajectories will the balls move along? How long will it take the rod to make a full revolution? Construct a graph of dependence $\omega(t)$. The dimensions of the balls are much smaller than the length of the rod.

Due to the symmetry of the initial location and initial velocities, the balls will always be at symmetrical points and have symmetrical velocities. Then it is easy to show that the balls will move freely, except for moments of reflection from the stops.

Indeed, suppose that the ball $A$ is acted upon by a force $F$, perpendicular to the rod (there are no longitudinal forces, since there is no friction). Then, for reasons of symmetry, the ball $B$ should be acted upon by a force $-F$ (Fig. 2). But this means that from the side of the balls a pair of forces will act on the rod, spinning it. Neglecting the mass of the rod in comparison with the masses of the balls, we conclude that these forces should be negligible (in the limit, no forces are required at all to rotate a weightless rod). Thus, we come to the conclusion that between collisions with stops the balls will move uniformly and rectilinearly.

Let us now analyze the collisions of balls with stops. Firstly, during a collision, the $X$-component of the momentum (Fig. 3) is preserved, since otherwise the balls at the moment of impact will be acted upon by forces perpendicular to the rod, which is impossible due to the previous reasoning. Secondly, due to elasticity, energy is conserved. Therefore, when the balls collide with the stops, the law of equality of angular incidence and reflection is satisfied.

From the above reasoning and the geometry of the problem, it is clear that the trajectories of each ball will be an equilateral triangle on the side $l=2a\cos 30^\circ=a\sqrt{3}$ (see Fig. 3).

The rod will make a full revolution in the time $T$ that it takes for the ball to make such a triangle with a speed equal to the initial speed $v_0=\omega_0 a/2$. Thus,

$$T=3l/v_0=6\sqrt{3}/\omega_0.$$

Let's find the dependence $\omega(t)$. It is clear that $\omega=v_t/r$, where $v_t$ is the component of the ball’s velocity perpendicular to the radius, i.e. $v_t=v\cos \alpha=va/2r$. Hence,

$$\omega=va/2r^2=\omega_0/(1+(2x/a)^2)$$

(see Fig. 3). If we start from the moment when $\alpha=0$, then $x=v_0t$,

$$\omega=\omega_0/(1+(\omega_0 t)^2)$$ at $-\sqrt{3}/\omega_0 \leq t \leq \sqrt{3}/\omega_0$.

At moment $t=\sqrt{3}/\omega_0$ there will be a strike against the stop, and the cycle will repeat (see Fig. 4).
