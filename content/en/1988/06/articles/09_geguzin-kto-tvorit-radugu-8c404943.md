Who creates radioga?

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
Y. E. GEGUZIN

Radguu are created by water drops: in the sky - jinks, on the watered asphalt - droplets, splashes from a water jet. Radguy can also match the dewdrops that cover the low-mown grass on an autumn morning.

The “geometry” of radiga in the sky was described a long time ago. Usually two multi-colored concentric arcs are visible in the sky - one bright and the other paler. Each arc is part of a circle, the center of which lies on a straight line drawn through the sun and observed by the eye. This straight line is a kind of axis, and a radig is curved around it. The eye is observed to be at the top of the cones, at the base of which there are multi-colored arcs (Fig. 1). The generatrices of these cones with the axis respectively make angles of 42° and 51°. The sun shines from behind, and the lower it descends to the horizon, the higher the top of the radig rises. At the moment when the sun touches the horizon, you can see a semicircular radius - it has never been larger. If the sun rises above the horizon by more than 42°, the top of the bright radius will go beyond the horizon.

Everything happens as if the bending ones are straight, like a rocker arm, fixed at the point $O$, where the observing eye is located, and at the ends of the rocker arm are the sun and the top of the beam. This means that each observing person has “its own” radig, depicting around “its own” axis, the same one that passes through his eye... And one more thing: it is impossible to reach the radig, as well as to the horizon. And it is also impossible to approach it, because this would mean changing the entire geometry of the radius, in particular the angle at the apex of the cone. And its observance is the first requirement of both physics and radig geometry.

For geometric information, it is worth noting the data on the order of colors in radii. As you know, radiet contains “all the colors of radiga” - from red to violet. The order of the colors is reversed, and they face each other with red stripes. That's all the geometry of the radig, at least the one that corresponds to the drops in the sky.

Now about the physics of radiga. Its history goes back to 1637, when the French philosopher and natural scientist Renedecart first understood the role of the drop in the emergence of radigue. He confirmed his discovery with a calculation that required enormous labor: he traced the path of ten thousand parallel solar rays in a spherical drop. The first of them touches the surface of the drop, and the ten passes through its center, i.e., the distance between the outer rays is equal to the radius of the drop.

Descartes' idea was simple and natural. He believed that the sun's rays, moving EMPLYING in a drop and reflecting once from its surface, can enter the eye while observing (Fig. 2). Having traced such a path of ten thousand rays, he was convinced that all the rays, the numbers of which are approximately between 8500 and 8600, would emerge from the drop in almost the same direction, at an angle of $42^\circ$ to the radig axis. Consequently, among others, this direction is highlighted with shining brightness, and a hundredfold amplified beam will be perceived by the observed...

Everything that has been said about the ten thousand rays concerns the main radig, the one to which the number $42^\circ$ refers. If we consider a more complex path of rays in a drop - two assumptions with two rather than one reflection - we get an explanation of the second radig, to which the number $51^\circ$ refers.

...The appearance of colors is a natural consequence of the dependence of the refractive index on the wavelength of light. What happens in a drop is the same as in a glass prism, which splits white light into “all the colors of radigue.” The “physics” of the radiga remains unchanged under different “geometries” - for the radiga on wet asphalt and on mown grass covered with dew.

It is also worth mentioning the efficiencies associated with the small size of the droplets. Those drops that mainly create radiation have a diameter of 0.08-0.20 mm. With such dimensions, it must be taken into account that light has a wave nature. However, the changes associated with this change in the elementary theory of Descartes, who considers a ray rather than a wave, turn out to be not very significant.

If the drops that created the radiga remained in the sky without changing, the radiga could be observed for no more than 2 hours 48 minutes: it is during this time that the sun passes an arc path across the sky at $42^\circ$.
