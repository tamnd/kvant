F1102. A laser beam falls on a translucent plane-parallel plate, one surface of which is painted so that it can scatter light in all directions. The following picture is visible on the plate: a light dot in the center, a dark circle with a sharply alternate border, and a light halo around it. Explain the phenomenon.

The laser beam hits point $O$ of the painted surface (see figure). Rays of scattered light emanating from point $O$ at an angle $\alpha$, changing $\alpha_0$ - the angle of total internal reflection), exit the plate and create an image of a luminous point $O$. Rays emanating from point $O$ at an angle greater than $\alpha_0$ again fall on the painted surface and illuminate it, creating a luminous halo. In this case, the radius $R$ of the unilluminated region is equal to $2d$ tg $\alpha_0$, where $d$ is the thickness of the plate.

A. M. Shalazin

Topology is not interested in the properties of figures associated with length, area, volume and similar quantitative characteristics. She studies the most profound properties of figures and bodies, which remain unchanged under the most deformation conditions, but without breaks or deformations. If bodies and figures were cut, torn and glued together, then any body of any complex structure could be transformed into any other body with any structure, and all the original properties would be irrevocably lost. After a little thought, you will understand that topology is the study of the simplest, and at the same time the most profound properties that a body has *).

Although back in the eighteenth century much mathematics was accomplished by solving individual topological problems, the beginning of systematic work in the field of topology was laid by August Ferdinand Möbius, a German astronomer who taught at the University of Leipzig in the first half of the last century. Before Mobius, everyone thought that any surface had two sides, like a sheet of paper. It was Moebius who made a disconcerting discovery: if you take a strip of paper, twist it half a turn, and glue the ends together, you will get a one-sided surface with not two sides, but one single side!

It's hard to believe that this could even exist, but the one-sided surface actually exists - a real, tangible thing that anyone can build in an instant. The fact that the Möbius strip has only one side is not compatible, and it retains this property, no matter how you stretch and deform it.

But let's get back to our story. I started Mathematica at the University of Chicago, completed my doctorate in topology, and was able to introduce Mobius into the society without much difficulty. There weren't very many of us - just two chains of flowers, mostly Chicago topologists, but not some chains of society working at universities in neighboring cities.

We held monthly meetings that were purely academic in nature, but once a year - on November 17 (Mobius's birthday) - we gathered for a banquet and invited as a guest some famous topologist who gave a lecture.

This year we decided to send the anniversary of the patron of our society to the “Purple Hats”, where the prices were quite reasonable, and after the lecture you could go down to the hall and watch the Varte program. We were lucky with our guests: our invitation was accepted by the famous Professor Slipenarsky, the first topologist in the world and one of the greatest mathematical geniuses of our century.

Slipenarsky and I went to the Purple Hats together in a taxi, and on the way I asked him to outline in general terms what he was going to talk about at the lecture. The topic of the lecture, “Zero-Sided Surfaces,” also caused much anticipation among members of the Mobius Society, and even Professor Robert Simpson from the University of Wisconsin notified the board in writing of his intention to attend the banquet. We arrived on time. After our guest of honor had been introduced to Professor Simpson and another member of the society, we sat down at table. After my brief opening remarks, Sleipenarski entered, thanked those present with a smile for their applause, and cleared his throat.

It is hardly possible to present in any detail the brilliant, but understandable only to specialists, report of Sleipenarsky. Its essence boiled down to the following. About ten years ago, Sleipenarski came across a statement in one of the lesser-known works of Moebius that struck his imagination. According to Möbius, there was theoretically no reason why a surface could not lose both its sides: in other words, become “null-sided.”

Of course, the professor appeared, such a surface cannot be visualized visually, just like the square root of minus units or a hypercube in four-dimensional space. But the abstractness of a concept does not mean that it is meaningless or cannot find application in modern mathematics and physics.

We should not forget, the professor continued, that those who have never seen a Möbius strip cannot imagine even a one-sided surface. Many people with a well-developed mathematical imagination refuse to believe in the existence of a one-sided surface, even when they have a Mobius strip in their hands.

I looked at Professor Simpson, and it seemed to me that at these words he smiled slightly.

For many years, Sleipenarski continued, he persistently sought to construct a zero-sided surface. By analogy with known types of surfaces, he was able to study many properties of a zero-sided surface. Finally, augmented day has arrived. Sleipenarski paused to see what impression his words made on the listener, and looked around at the frozen audience. His efforts were successful, and he built a zero-sided surface.

Like an electrical discharge, his words stung those sitting at the table. Each one met, looked at his neighbor in surprise and settled down in a similar manner. Professor Simpson shook his head furiously. When Sleipenarsky went to the back of the dining room, where the blackboard was prepared, Simpson turned to his neighbor on the left and whispered: “This is nonsense!
