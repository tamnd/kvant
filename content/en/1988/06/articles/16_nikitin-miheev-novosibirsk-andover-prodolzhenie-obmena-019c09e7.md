Novosibirsk - Andover.
Continuing the exchange

In the ninth issue of the magazine «Kvant» for 1987, an article by V. G. Kharitopov was published about the first student exchange between Novosibirsk Physics School No. 165 and Phyllis Academy (USA). In September 1987, the second exchange between schools took place. The Soviet delegation was represented by ten students: Elena Abrosova from Ust-Kamenogorsk, Svetlana Aikina from Novokuznets, Eduard Gunchenko from Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky, Valeria Gurova from the Chita region, Maxim Drovyshev from the Alma-Ata region, Sergey Erokov from Novokuznets, Denis Malkhin from Omsk, Alexey Melnikov from Krasnoyarsk, Ogilko Sergey from the Tselinograd region and Tislenok Ilya from Tomsk and two leaders: the director of the school, candidate of physics and mathematical sciences A. A. Nikitin and the deputy director for educational work Yu. V. Mikheev. In this issue they share their impressions of the trip.

The Soviet delegation arrived in Andover on September 15. That same day, the school took pictures on the steps of the art gallery, after which a picnic was organized on the lawn near the cafeteria. On September 16, Phyllis Academy Principal Donald McNemar held a traditional reception for the entire school on the cafeteria lawn, and from there everyone went to the church building for the busy grand opening. This was the first of our own, which made a very strong impression on us. First, everyone except the seniors (graduation class students) entered the hall and sat down in the side seats. Then the seniors began to enter and greeted the hall enthusiastically. The noise was incredible, the students were shouting, kicking their feet, whistling, and clapping. This went on for several minutes, but as soon as the director stood up and raised his hand, there was instant silence. In his speech, the director noted that the opening of the school took place on a significant day, which coincided with the celebration of the 200th anniversary of the American Constitution. Then he told how many students started studying at the Phyllis Academy, and especially noted that this year students from 29 countries of the world will study, including 5 Chinese schoolchildren and 10 schoolchildren from the Novosibirsk Physics and Music School on exchange with the Phyllis Academy. Then the floor was given to A. A. Nikitin. His speech in English was met with thunderous applause. After this, the president of the student body spoke about the program for the subject school year, under his leadership the whole hall performed the traditional song of the Phyllis Academy with many movements, and then the school symphony orchestra performed magnificently.

Workdays began on September 17. Each of our students chose one of the math courses, one art-related course, and two of the following three courses: physics, chemistry, programming.

Mathematics courses are designed for all students, from those who have only the most basic skills to those who have already studied mathematics at Phyllis Academy for two or three years. Particularly noteworthy are the introductory course of calculus of functions (Precalculus), calculus of functions (Calculus), probability theory, mathematical statistics, vector analysis, theory of functions of a complex variable. The right to choose individual courses must be “earned” by studying the preliminary definition of the course, so the number of students in groups ranges from 20-22 to 4-5. The complexity of individual courses studied in the last, twelfth grade is quite high. For example, the course on the theory of functions of a complex variable is close to the first and second years of our universities. There are textbooks available for each of the courses, many of them prepared by Phyllis Academy teachers. A quick glance at the textbook gave us the impression that American teachers pay very little attention to the logic of proofs; most of the topics are studied at the level of intuitive reasoning and taught how to use them in solving a problem. However, as we were able to see in the busy classes, the logic and methodology of solving the problem are given a lot of attention in the lessons. The pace of studying the material is high, and much is given as

homework, and classes turn more into conversations and consultations on homework. Students ask questions at any time, the teacher interrupts his reasoning and answers in detail and in detail until all the students understand everything, and then continues the explanations. The teacher himself primarily works at the blackboard, students are called to the blackboard extremely rarely, and knowledge testing is carried out only in writing.

Classes in other subjects are conducted in a similar manner. Programming courses (Computer) are designed for only one trimester; the algorithmic languages ​​Logo and Pascal are studied with practical work on personal computers Apple, Macintosh and IBM.

Approaches to learning vary. In one of the courses, the principles of programming were examined using the example of a ready-made program playing “tic-tac-toe.”

In another course, on the contrary, they began studying by drawing up simple programs, drawings, and then began to use these programs, introducing the concepts of procedures, variables, cycles, transition conditions, and so on. Theoretical classes alternated with practical work on a computer.

We did not notice that computers were widely used in lessons in other subjects. They are mainly used when working with texts: typing and editing. In mathematics, several classes were conducted with a program that allows you to build graphs of elementary functions, determine their zeros, calculate the product and depict tangents to the graph, find the values ​​of certain intervals, depict rotation functions about various axes and calculate their volume.

The structure of the physics course at Phillips Academy is close to being adopted in our country, studying starting from mechanics and building up to the basics of atomic physics in three terms. There are two levels of study: one for those who wish to gain a general introduction to physics, and the second for those who wish to gain in-depth knowledge. With an outwardly identical structure, these levels differ in the use of mathematical apparatus, and therefore, to choose a deep physics course, you must first study, at a minimum, Calculus.

Improvement in language was important for our students. Most of the teachers and students at Phillips Academy showed a noticeable interest in the representatives of the Soviet Union, so if difficulties arose with the language, then try to speak more slowly and use some other words. In English lessons, a lot of attention was paid to jokes, jokes, and sometimes discussions with American schoolchildren about sports, education, cultural life, and so on were eliminated.

Very soon we found out that studying at Phillips Academy is quite difficult for both our students and American ones. Some students (though there are not many of them) are forced to drop out of one of the chosen courses after one or two weeks, leaving only four. Our students used a large instrument, simple and familiar, but after two weeks they had to make a lot of effort to cope with the entire volume of work, but after some time, when the guys began to perceive American speech quite freely, it became easier to study.

only studies and sports. There were many different meetings. Meetings with clubs were especially busy: Russian, international, political. I also remember our trips to Boston, where we visited a small observation deck, a science museum, a baseball game, and Harvard University. We also visited Georgetown University in Washington, as well as two parts of the school: Phillips Exeter Academy near Andover and St. Albany in Washington. Visiting these schools allowed us to understand that the US school system of living and studying is not “standardized”.
It was very uninviting to see strict uniforms in Exeter and ritual patterns in the dining room of St. Albany. Phillips Academy students are free to communicate with each other and with teachers, can neglect their clothing, and are not bound by any rituals during meals.

Our delegation was supposed to spend the last few days in the USA in Washington. In this regard, we were very often told that our delegation was lucky that we could see the most beautiful city in the USA, and very often we were asked whether we would meet with the subject R. Reagan. However, only on the evening of October 15 we were informed that tomorrow at one o'clock in the afternoon our delegation would be received by the United States. On the morning of October 16, we flew to Washington, checked into the hotel and went to the Soviet settlement in Washington, where we were very warmly greeted by Ambassador Yu. V. Dubinin. And despite the fact that the meeting was very short, after it the cauterization immediately took place, which covered the members of the delegation before the meeting with the US subject. To Chaudi we were taken to a small presentation reception room, where there were already many telephone operators, photojournalists and journalists. At exactly one o'clock Ronald Reagan came pouring out. Sveta Aikina handed him a badge associated with the exchange between Novosibirsk and Andver, the US representative read out a prepared message in which he noted that the exchange

among young people will contribute to better relations between our countries. The reporters asked questions that were not related to the issue of exchanges, the president answered two questions and asked the reporters to leave. Academy Director Phillips then spoke briefly about our exchange. After listening to his speech, Reagan said that he had a very busy schedule, but nevertheless stayed and answered questions. When asked how he felt about the changes that had taken place in our country, the president replied that he was following them with interest, he liked perestroika and openness, that he had a good relationship with M. S. Gorbachev and that in the near future there would be meetings between the leaders of the USSR and the USA. Instead of saying that in the Soviet Union they know more about the USA than in the United States about the USSR, he said that now in the USA interest in the USSR is growing, and the ongoing cultural and scientific exchanges will help the peoples of our countries learn more about each other. At the end of the meeting, the President took a photo with the delegation.

The remaining days we got acquainted with the capital of the United States, its museums and attractions.

During our group's stay in the USA, there were many meetings, many other relationships were formed, and many impressions were accumulated. But despite all this, we experienced an incomparable feeling when on October 22 we arrived at Sheremetvo in Moscow, dear to us all.
