Vaporization. Vapor properties

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences A. A. VARLAMOV

Evaporation. Saturated and unsaturated pairs

As is known, the process of vaporization - the transition of a substance from a liquid to a gaseous state - can occur in two ways: evaporation and boiling. Let's start with the first one.

In a liquid, as in a gas, particles are in constant thermal motion. But if in a gas (under normal conditions) the kinetic energy of the movement of molecules significantly exceeds the potential energy of their interaction, then in a liquid these quantities turn out to be of the same order. Therefore, the liquid molecule performs only warm vibrations around some equilibrium positions, sometimes “jumping” to others. The average energy of such vibrations is determined by the temperature of the liquid; however, a liquid always contains a certain number of molecules with kinetic energies significantly greater than the average. When such high-energy molecules find themselves in the near-surface layer, they can, in principle, overcome the attractive state of the surrounding molecules, go beyond the liquid and form a gaseous phase - vapor - above its surface. The process described is called evaporation. It is important that evaporation occurs to some degree at all temperatures. Any liquids and even solids will evaporate (for them the evaporation process is called sublimation).

If the liquid is in a closed state, then, for example, with the process of evaporation, i.e., the departure of fast-chemical molecules from the liquid, the reverse process also occurs - the return

molecules from vapor to liquid, i.e. condensation. In the final estimate, dynamic equilibrium is established between liquid and vapor - a state in which the number of parts leaving the liquid per unit time is, on average, equal to the number of parts returning to it. Vapor that is in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid is called saturated.

Problem 1. Are the temperatures of the liquid and the saturated vapor in dynamic equilibrium with it equal?

In response to such a question on the exam, you can often hear that the temperature of the vapor is higher than the temperature of the liquid, since the “hottest” molecules, the kinetic energy of which in the liquid was noticeably higher than the average, evaporate—fly out of the liquid.

This is not true, and here's why. Escaping from the liquid, “hot” molecules have to do work against the attractive forces acting on them from the surrounding molecules. This work is done by reducing the kinetic energy of “hot” molecules, as a result of which they “cool” when turning into steam. Moreover, they are “cooled” precisely to the temperature of the liquid. This is understandable - the temperatures of the liquid and the saturated steam in equilibrium with it must be equal, otherwise one of the most important conditions for the thermodynamic equilibrium of the system would be violated - the equality of the temperatures of its individual elements.

What properties does saturated steam have? At a given temperature, it is characterized by a certain pressure $p_H(T)$, which, unlike the case of an ideal gas, does not depend on the valued volume. The volume of the vessel in which the pairs are located determines its mass. It turns out that, despite the existing interaction between the molecules of saturated steam (it is this that leads to steam condensation), its state can be described with fairly high accuracy using the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation.

Therefore, for the mass of steam occupying volume $V$ at temperature $T$, we have

$$m = \frac{p_H(T)V}{RT},$$

where $M$ is the molar mass of the substance under consideration, $R$ is the universal gas constant.

Problem 2. Compare the densities of water and saturated water vapor at temperature $t=100^\circ C$.

The saturated vapor density is determined from the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation, taking into account the fact that at $100^\circ C$ the saturated vapor pressure is equal to normal atomospheric pressure ($10^5$ Pa):

$$\varrho_n = \frac{p_H M}{RT} = 0,58 \text{ кг/м}^3,$$

where $M=18 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ кг/mоль}$, $R=8,31 \text{ Дж/(моль·К)}$. The density of water at the same temperature is $\varrho_B = 0,98 \cdot 10^3 \text{ кг/м}^3$. Thus, the density of saturated water vapor is approximately 1700 times less than the density of water.

In the case where the substance evaporates, it is sufficiently intensively removed from the surface and therefore evaporation prevails over condensation, forming steam that is unsaturated. Unsaturated steam is also formed after complete evaporation of a liquid in a closed vessel, if its initial mass was less than that required for the formation of saturated steam. When describing the properties of saturated steam, you can also use the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation. In addition, unsaturated steam obeys a well-known law for various isoprosses of an ideal gas (the same cannot be said about saturated steam).

The pressure and density of saturated steam is less than saturated steam can be converted to saturated steam by isothermal rolling or isochoric cooling.

Problem 3. A window was opened in a hot kitchen. It's bitterly cold outside. What will an observer see looking at the window opening from the kitchen? From the street?

An observer on the street will see steam pouring upward from the open window. Indeed, hot air from the kitchen will exit through the window and rise upward. The unsaturated water vapor contained in the air immediately cools, becomes saturated and begins to condense, forming many tiny droplets that we see. This is what they conventionally talk about - “water vapor”, although in fact it is not steam, but fog. Water vapor is produced and cannot be visible by itself.

More unexpected will be the spectacle that the viewer will observe inside the kitchen. He will see steam pouring out of the flooded kitchen through the window from the street and falling down. What's the matter here? Cold air from the street, replacing the burning air that came out through the window, does not immediately mix with the air inside the kitchen and, being much colder, falls down. The water vapor entering it from the surrounding warm air cools, becomes saturated and partially condenses. It seems to the observer that the clouds of this fog are falling down.

Humidity
To quantitatively characterize the content of water vapor in the air, special physical quantities are introduced - absolute humidity and relative air humidity. Absolute humidity is usually called the pressure of unsaturated water vapor entering the air. The ratio of this pressure to the pressure of unsaturated water vapor at the same temperature is called relative humidity.

Let's consider several specific tasks.

Problem 4. It is necessary to supply $V=10000$ m³ of air into the room at a temperature of $t_1=18$ °C and a relative humidity of $\varphi_1=50\%$, taking it from the street at $t_2=10$ °C and a relative humidity of $\varphi_2=60\%$. How much more quickly can additional evaporate into the supply air? The saturated vapor pressure of $t_1$ is $p_{\text{н1}}=2,1 \cdot 10^3$ Pa, and $t_2=1,2 \times 10^3$ Pa.

From the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation, determines the mass of saturated steam located in the volume $V$ under indoor and outdoor conditions:

$$m_{\text{н1}} = \frac{Mp_{\text{н1}}V}{RT_1}, \quad m_{\text{н2}} = \frac{Mp_{\text{н2}}V}{RT_2}.$$

Knowing the relative humidity of the air in each of the two cases, we will find the true masses of water contained in the air:

$$m_1 = \varphi_1 m_{\text{н1}}, \quad m_2 = \varphi_2 m_{\text{н2}}.$$

The difference between these masses

$$\Delta m = m_1 - m_2 = \frac{MV}{R} \left( \frac{\varphi_1 p_{\text{н1}}}{T_1} - \frac{\varphi_2 p_{\text{н2}}}{T_2} \right) = 21,7 \text{ кг}$$

and determines the amount of water that should be additionally evaporated into the supplied air.

Problem 7. Determine the most permissible bubble size at which water overheating by $\Delta t = 1^\circ C$ can occur. Atmospheric pressure $p = 10^5$ Pa, saturated vapor pressure is greater than the boiling point of water $t_\kappa = 100^\circ C$ with an increase in temperature by $1^\circ C$ increases by $\Delta p = 10^3$ Pa, the heating surface coefficient of water under these conditions is $\sigma = 0,6$ N/m.

If the liquid is thoroughly cleaned of impurities and heated in a vessel with fairly smooth walls, then it can actually be overheated above the boiling point at a given nomatospheric pressure, sometimes even quite significant.

It is clear that in order for the boiling point to increase, it is necessary that too large bubbles do not arise. We find restrictions on the radius of the bubble, which becomes only at $101^\circ C$, and before this temperature it will collapse due to surface tension:

$$\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma}{r_{\max}}$$

(we have neglected the pressure of so much liquid here; judge for yourself at what heights this can be done), where

$$r_{\max} = \frac{2\sigma}{\Delta p} = 1,2 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ м.}$$

Thus, the size of the roughness on the walls of the vessel should not exceed 0.01-0.1 mm, otherwise steam bubbles of an undesirable size will appear on them and the liquid cannot be overheated.
