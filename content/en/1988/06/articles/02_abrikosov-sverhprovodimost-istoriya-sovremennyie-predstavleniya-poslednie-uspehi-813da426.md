SUPERCONDUCTIVITY:

history, modern ideas, recent successes

Academician A. A. ABRIKOSOV

The phenomenon of superconductivity was discovered by the Dutch physicist Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911. Kamerlingh Onnes was the first to obtain liquid helium, and he used it to create crystals - devices that can be maintained at very low temperatures. In particular, he decided to check the correctness of the idea that existed at that time about the behavior of electrical resistance at low temperatures. Measuring the resistance of mercury, Kamerlingh Onnes discovered that it abruptly vanishes at a temperature of about 4 K. This phenomenon was called superconductivity, and the temperature of transition to the superconducting state was called critical. Currently, many superconductors are known with a wide variety of critical temperatures, from fractions of a degree to approximately 100 K. But more on the latter later.

Post-graduate research into superconductors reveals many of the remarkable properties. Thus, it turned out that superconductivity is destroyed by a magnetic field. The critical field at which this occurs depends on temperature. It was further discovered that superconductivity disappears even when a sufficiently large current is passed through the sample. Finally, the so-called Meissner effect was discovered, the essence of which is as follows. If you place a metal in a not very strong magnetic field and the temperature is high, then when the metal transitions to a superconducting state, the confluent field lines will be pushed out of it. Subsequent studies have shown that in fact, with such a transition, a small layer, $10^{-5}—10^{-6}$ cm thick, will appear at the surface of the superconductor, in which currents circulate, completely shielding the internal regions of the sample from the external field. The thickness of this layer is called the penetration depth.

In 1937, P. L. Kapitsa discovered the phenomenon of superfluidity of a liquid gel - its ability to flow through narrow capillaries without any friction. Four years later, L.D. Landdu was able to explain this phenomenon. In Landdu's theory, the so-called superfluidity criterion was derived, according to which it arises when moving at a speed exceeding a certain critical one. I will describe this quality. Inhibition of the gel means a change in its energy and momentum. However, the liquid gel is a quantum liquid and can change energy and momentum by absorbing and emitting certain quanta called quasiparticles. These quasiparticles behave in the volume of the body like real particles, however, with an uneducated connection between energy and momentum. The difference between them and ordinary particles - electrons, photons - is that quasiparticles cannot exist outside the body. In liquid helium, particles can also appear only when the flow rate of the gel is above a certain final value.

It would seem that from here it is easy to move on to an explanation of superconductivity as the superfluidity of a charged electron liquid in metals. However, the properties of the quasiparticles generated in the electron liquid turned out to be different from the properties of the quasiparticles in liquid helium. So, for them the critical speed is zero, and, therefore, the flow of current without resistance turns out to be non-existent. What is the difference between these two liquids - liquid helium and e-liquid? First of all, it lies in the fact that the intrinsic moment of rotation, called spin, is equal to zero for gel atoms, and $\hbar/2$ for electrons, where $\hbar$ is Planck’s constant. Therefore, the question immediately arose whether electrons could combine into pairs. For such pairs, the total spin was either zero (electron spins are directed in opposite directions) or $\hbar$. Similar pairs of quasiparticles in an electron liquid can be directed to quasiparticles of a liquid gel, and one could hope to cause the phenomenon of superconductivity by analogy with superfluidity. However, electrons are single-film charged particles; due to the Coulomb interaction, they are bounced off, and it would seem that there is no reason for pairing.

It was only in 1950 that an experiment was carried out to change the critical fields $B_{\kappa p}$ and temperatures $T_{\kappa p}$ of mercury samples of different isotopic states, which shed light on the possible causes of pair formation. We found out that the values ​​of $T_{\kappa p}$ and $B_{\kappa p}$ depend on the mass of the isotope according to the law

$$T_{\kappa p}, B_{\kappa p} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}.$$

But the mass of the nuclei forming the critical lattice is manifested only in their movement. Thus, it became clear that this movement is essential for superconductivity. Based on this fact, the English physicist G. Freilix and, independently of him, the American physicist J. Bardeen proposed a concept that explains the nature of the forces of attraction between electrons. The fact is that the nuclei, or rather the ions that form the critical lattice of a metal, are also a quantum system, and in this system there are also quasiparticles corresponding to lattice flasks. They are called phonons. Electrons can exchange phonons, and this bond leads to an attraction that can overcome direct Coulomb repulsion.

However, even after this idea was expressed, it remained unclear how such an attraction would make it possible to form pairs of electrons. According to quantum mechanics, for this to happen, the attractive forces must be sufficiently large and

Cooper, who drew attention to the fact that we are talking about the formation of pairs not from two isolated electrons, but in the presence of the entire set of other electrons. A year later, in 1957, Bardin, Cooper and R. Schrieffer, and independently of them, Academician N.N. Bogolyubov, built a microsop theory of superconductivity, which connected together all the known experimental facts about the properties of superconductors.

I will not present this theory here due to its complexity. I will note only a few important circumstances. First of all, if the system is at $T=0$, then energy can only be transferred to it by breaking the pair. This requires the expenditure of finite energy, which is denoted by $2\Delta$. In this regard, the electronic heat at low temperatures behaves like $e^{-\Delta/T}$. Second: I have already noted that for the connection of electrons into pairs, the entire electronic collective exists. But the state of this system depends on temperature. Therefore, the binding energy of the $2\Delta$ pair depends on temperature and at $T=T_{\text{кр}} \Delta(T)$ it becomes zero—the superconductor becomes a normal metal.

The third property is due to the fact that pairs have a finite size, on the order of $10^{-4}-10^{-5}$ cm. The question arises: how do they not interfere with each other? After all, the average distance between electrons in a metal is of the order of $10^{-8}$ cm. This paradox is a manifestation of the quantum properties of matter. However, among the authors of the microsop theory of superconductivity, Schrieffer, for comparison, likened the electrons in a superconductor to a dance in a modern disco. The two dance, and although there are many other dancers between them, they do not lose touch with each other. Therefore, it is more accurate to speak not about pairs, but about the pair correlation of electrons in a superconductor.

I have already told you that when the external magnetic field is compared with the critical one, the superconductor jumps into the normal state. This statement, strictly speaking, is valid only for a cylindrical sample in a longitudinal field and, moreover, not for all superconductors. Indeed, why shouldn't a massive superconductor be broken down into thin elephants of normal and superconducting metal and maintain superconductivity until the city of large fields? After all, the critical field for a thin layer is higher than for a massive superconductor. The separation into elephants does not occur because in all pure superconductors consisting of one metal, there is a special surface energy that arises at the boundaries between the normal and superconducting phases. This energy, like surface tension, tends to reduce the surface area of ​​the boundaries. The microfurnace theory explained its origin. It turned out that it is related to the finite size of Cooper pairs. If you reduce this size, the surface energy can change sign and become negative. Thus, a natural division of superconductors will arise into superconductors of the first kind - with positive surface energy, and superconductors of the second kind - with negative surface energy. It should be noted that type II superconductors are much more common than type I superconductors. Moreover, any superconductor of the first kind can be converted to the second kind. To do this, it is enough to introduce a certain amount of atoms into it or otherwise spoil the crystal lattice. Electrons begin to scatter on these defects. Now I will talk a little about the properties of type II superconductors. Since the surface energy in them is negative, nothing is transferred to the endless splitting of their volume into normal and superconducting regions. Therefore, superconductivity in them will be gradually displaced with increasing external magnetic field, starting from a certain field value $B_{kp,1}$. The transition to the normal state occurs in the upper critical field $B_{kp,2}$. The physical meaning of $B_{kp,2}$ is as follows. In a magnetic field, electrons, being charged particles, move along spiral trajectories, and the radius of the spiral is inversely proportional to $B$. If the radius of the spiral becomes smaller than the size of the pair, then the pair can no longer exist and falls apart. If the external magnetic field is below $B_{kp,2}$, but above $B_{kp,1}$, then it partially penetrates the superconductor. This occurs due to the appearance of output currents in the superconductor. It turns out that these come out as quantum objects. Each of them carries a magnetic flux quantum. If you want, we can say that the number of lines of force passing in each current is strictly defined. The flux quantum is very small and equal to $\Phi_0=\pi h/e=2\cdot 10^{-15}$ Wb.

As the external field decreases, the outputs diverge and in the field $B_{kp,1}$ completely disappear from the superconductor.

A metal cylinder cooled to a low temperature is in a superconducting state. When the magnetic field is turned on, circular points are induced on the surface of the cylinder, which create a magnetic field in the cylinder with inductance $B_{tok}$, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to inductance $B_{беншен}$ of the external field. These circular dots are superconducting and do not fade over time. Therefore, at the point of the superconductor the total inductive value is zero: $B_{беншен} + B_{tok} = 0$. Indical field lines do not penetrate into the superconductor.

The greater the inductiveness of the external field, the more current must flow across the surface to ensure that the interior of the metallic superconductor is shielded from the external field. At a certain critical value of the internal field $B_{kp}$, the field penetrates the internal bond, superconductivity is destroyed - the metal goes into the normal state. An external magnetic field is not necessary for such a transition. The current flowing along the surface of the superconductor itself creates a magnetic field, and when $B_{tok}$ reaches a value corresponding to $B_{kp}$, superconductivity is destroyed. The value of $B_{kp}$ increases with decreasing temperature, but even close to $T=0$ the value of $B_{kp}$ for pure superconducting metals is not large - no more than ten shares of dough.

In superconducting alloys, as well as in a number of superconducting compounds, at certain inductions $B_{kp,1}$ the field begins to penetrate the internal bond. In this case, separate clusters of magnetic induction lines appear. Each such clot is surrounded by ring-shaped currents that fill the outlets into liquid or gas. These clumps are called outputs. Inside, superconductivity is destroyed, but in the space between the exits it is preserved. With increasing magnetic field induction, the number of outputs increases.

At some inductions $B_{kp,2}$ the outputs begin to break, the field is “filled” with the sample, and superconductivity is completely destroyed. Superconductors with such properties are called type II superconductors.

Next, the attention of Bednorz and Mühler was attracted by oxides containing copper in a state with an intermediate valence: part Cu⁺⁺, and part Cu⁺⁺⁺. Such oxides were studied by French physicists. They included the rare element lanthanum as a structural element. If we take the compound La₂CuO₄, then the copper in it is only divalent, and this substance behaves as an insulator. Mühler and Bednorz began to replace trivalent lanthanum with divalent elements in order to take away some electrons from copper and thereby partially convert copper into the trivalent state. As a result, the first high-temperature superconductor La₂-xBaₓCuO₄, where x=0.1-0.2, was obtained. At first, Bednorz and Mühler did not believe anything, and their article was published by the leading American physics journal “Physical Review Letters”. Then they sent it to the German journal Zeitschrift für Physik, where it was published in the fall of 1986. Initially the article did not arouse interest. But then Japanese experts decided to check the message and were convinced that Mueller and Bednorz were not mistaken. After this, American physicists pointed out research, and from the beginning of 1987 a real “superconducting boom” broke out. There are now several thousand articles on this topic. It was natural to try to increase the critical temperature by replacing the elements with their chemical analogues. Replacing Ba with Sr resulted in Tₖp=45 K. An American group from Houston led by P. Chu subjected these samples to rolling and found that the critical temperature increases rapidly during rolling, although in conventional superconductors under similar conditions Tₖp tends to decrease slightly. Then they decided to try to create a “chemical” scatium by replacing lanthanum atoms with smaller intria atoms. As a result, a compound with a fantastically high critical temperature Tₖp~93 K was obtained.

The idea of ​​a "chemical scathium" helped make the discovery, but in the end it turned out to be wrong. Very carefully studied indicators that a high-temperature superconductor are the “1 - 2 - 3” phase: Y Ba₂Cu₃O₇-x, where x is less than one. As for theoretical explanations, there are more questions than answers. For example, according to the Bardeen-Cooper-Schriefer theory, the correspondence $2\Delta(0)/T_{\kappa p}=3,5$ is obtained. For new superconductors, different changes give this value values ​​from 3 to 12. Further, these substances have a number of special features, but it is not very clear which of them are of fundamental importance. For example, what role does the complexity of crystals play, do the existence of lanthanum or indium atoms play a role in the mechanism of superconductivity, or do they play a role in the process of the mechanical truss that holds the crystal lattice together? What is the role of oxygen? It is known that in the intrium compound there is an elephant, consisting of a chain Cu - O - Cu - O, and there are planes in which two oxygen atoms come to each copper atom. When oxygen is removed, it first leaves the chain, and the substance loses superconductivity. But in the lanthanum compound there are no such chains.

So, it’s not very clear what to cling to. It has been experimentally established that in new superconductors, electrons are combined into Cooper pairs. But what is the mechanism of attraction? The mechanism of transfer of phonans - quanta of the flask lattice - is followed by an isotopic coefficient, i.e., a change in $T_{\kappa p}$ with the transition to another isotope. Changes were made to the samples by replacing the isotope $O^{16}$ with $O^{18}$. For the lanthanum compound, the coefficient is observed, although it is less than predicted by the VKS theory. But in a 90-degree sodium superconductor, as well as in the same substance with european sodium, this coefficient is practically absent. Hence the conclusion is drawn that, in addition to the long-known phonon mechanism, there is another mechanism for transmitting interaction between electrons. In principle, quasiparticles of other types can exist in a substance—for example, those associated with the excitation of electrons remote from conducting words. They are called plasmens. There are others: the substance may be close to the transition to a magnetically ordered state. This state has its own quasiparticles - magnons. But even if there is no real ordering, it can arise in the form of fluctuations and the interaction of electrons is created. Theories have emerged that take advantage of this circumstance.

I cannot list all the theories - there are a lot of them. I will just note the very interesting concept of two-electron centers. It is known that oxygen leaves very easily from new superconductors, while in ordinary oxides it is bound very poorly. There is a concept according to which two electrons can immediately leave the oxygen atoms for the copper; this makes the oxygen neutral and thus makes it easier to escape from the lattice. And this circumstance that the electrons are either in a collectivized metallic state or find themselves localized in pairs on oxygen leads to their attraction.

So, now is the time for research and search both for the mechanism of high-temperature superconductivity and for methods of practical application of new superconducting materials. Research is being carried out by large groups of people, and it is possible that they are increasing their success. However, the discovery of high-temperature superconductors has already accomplished one important thing: it has destroyed the long-standing prejudice that superconductivity is explained by three temperatures. This encourages people to take further positions, and even if it is not possible to “tame” the discovered superconducting ceramics, other classes of superconductors with higher $T_{\kappa p}$ and more suitable for practical use will certainly be found.
