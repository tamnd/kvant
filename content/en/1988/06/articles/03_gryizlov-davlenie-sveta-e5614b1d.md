LIGHT PRESSURE
S. V. GRYZLOV

Medieval scientists observing the movement of comets tried to understand why, depending on the position of comets relative to the Sun, the shapes of their tails changed. In 1604, Johannes Kepler proposed that the shape of comets' tails is determined by the action of light pressure. Since then, many researchers have tried to understand this pressure (among them was one of the creators of the wave theory of light, O. Fresnel). But all experiments were inconclusive. And for almost 300 years, light pressure remained just a hypothesis.

In 1865, J.C. Maxwell created the electromagnetic theory of light. According to this theory, light waves are of an electromagnetic nature, that is, light phenomena can be considered as a special case of electromagnetic phenomena. From the basic equations of Maxwell's theory, the existence of light pressure followed as an absolutely exact fact. Maxwell calculated this pressure. According to calculations, it turned out that on a sunny noon, on a surface that fully reflects light rays, a pressure equal to only 4.7 × 10⁻⁶ N/m² is created.

For a long time, students argued about the correctness of Maxwell's equation. Attempts were again made to detect the effect of light pressure. And again - flawless.

The difficulty in measuring the pressure of light was not only and not so much that the force of light pressure is very small. The main complications arose due to the fact that the experimenter interfered... with the light itself. Moreover, the “effectiveness” of the interference it created was many times greater than the pressure of light.

In 1899, the remarkable Russian physicist Pyotr Nikolaevich Lebedev first measured the pressure of light. He was able to quickly overcome difficulties that prevented others from exploring.

The main part of Lebedev’s device were flat, lightweight wings made of various (mostly) metal materials. The surface of one wing was blackened, while the others were mirrored. The light was practically completely reflected from the mirror surface, and the blackened surface absorbed the light. As a result, the force of light pressure on the mirrored wings turned out to be almost twice as great as the force of pressure on the black ones. Due to this, a torque of forces arose, the wing system began to turn, twisting the thread. The angle of twist of the thread was used to estimate the light pressure.

What kind of interference occurred during the measurement?

The first was that the light falling on the wings also heated the surrounding air. The resulting convection air currents, like the wind, set the wings in motion. And when the scientist took measurements, it was unknown why the wings were mixed - either from light pressure, or from air flows.

This was not the only obstacle. Under the influence of light, another effect occurs, which is called radiometric. Its essence is that different sides of the wing were heated differently (after all, the light falls only from one side) and gave different amounts of energy to the air molecules in contact with them. The side where this energy is greater (the warmer side), the molecules left with greater speed, i.e., with greater momentum. And according to the law of conservation of momentum, when flying away, they repel the winger with greater force than the molecules of the opposite “cold” side. It turned out that radiometric forces act in the same direction as the light pressure, and their magnitude, as already mentioned, is several orders of magnitude greater than the light pressure.

To bring these “harmful” forces to a minimum, first of all require the creation of a vacuum. The fewer molecules remain in the vessel, the weaker the interference.

It was not without reason that Lebedev chose thin metal films as the materials for the winger. Firstly, such films conducted heat well, and the temperature difference on different sides of the wing became smaller—the radiometric effect also decreased. In addition, their small mass determined the small moment of inertia of the entire moving system, which in vacuum conditions also makes it possible to increase the accuracy of the experiment.

Many other different devices and devices were invented by P. N. Lebedev. He went through many options for the experimental conditions and experienced various changes until his work was brought to success.

As a result of his experiments, he changed the light pressure; the value he obtained coincided with the value predicted by Maxwell's theory with an accuracy of 20%. Lebedev showed that the light flux has not only energy, but also momentum.

For a long time it was not possible to use light pressure due to its low power. Only the advent of lasers - devices capable of concentrating light energy into a very narrow and powerful beam - made it possible to apply Lebedev's discoveries.

For example, you can hold small particles in the air, balancing the force of gravity with the force of light pressure, and mix them. Under the influence of light pressure, particles with different masses will accelerate differently. Therefore, it is possible to sort very small particles using special traps.

Light pressure can be used to separate a mixture of two gases. Such separation can be realized if the frequency of laser radiation (with which the mixture is irradiated) coincides with the frequency of the transition of atoms of one of the gases from an unexcited state in air.

By absorbing a photon, an atom of such gas receives an impulse in the direction of the laser beam. During the reverse transition of an atom from an excited state to a non-excited state, the momentum vector of the emitted photon has an arbitrary direction. During subsequent absorptions and emissions, the “emission” pulses cancel each other out, and the “absorption” pulses add up, and ultimately the resonant atom receives a common impulse directed along the laser bow. Thus, the laser beam, passing sequentially through the chambers, in which there is a mixture of gases, carries with it atoms of one of them into the second chamber.
