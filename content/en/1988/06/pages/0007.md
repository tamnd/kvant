she. With a sufficient concentration of defects, a type I superconductor necessarily transforms into a type II superconductor.

Now I will talk a little about the properties of type II superconductors. Since the surface energy in them is negative, nothing is transferred to the endless splitting of their volume into normal and superconducting regions. Therefore, superconductivity in them will be gradually displaced with increasing external magnetic field, starting from a certain field value $B_{kp,1}$. The transition to the normal state occurs in the upper critical field $B_{kp,2}$. The physical meaning of $B_{kp,2}$ is as follows. In a magnetic field, electrons, being charged particles, move along spiral trajectories, and the radius of the spiral is inversely proportional to $B$. If the radius of the spiral becomes smaller than the size of the pair, then the pair can no longer exist and falls apart. If the external magnetic field is below $B_{kp,2}$, but above $B_{kp,1}$, then it partially penetrates the superconductor. This occurs due to the appearance of output currents in the superconductor. It turns out that these come out as quantum objects. Each of them carries a magnetic flux quantum. If you want, we can say that the number of lines of force passing in each current is strictly defined. The flux quantum is very small and equal to $\Phi_0=\pi h/e=2\cdot 10^{-15}$ Wb.

As the external field decreases, the outputs diverge and in the field $B_{kp,1}$ completely disappear from the superconductor.

A metal cylinder cooled to a low temperature is in a superconducting state. When the magnetic field is turned on, circular points are induced on the surface of the cylinder, which create a magnetic field in the cylinder with inductance $B_{tok}$, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to inductance $B_{беншен}$ of the external field. These circular dots are superconducting and do not fade over time. Therefore, at the point of the superconductor the total inductive value is zero: $B_{беншен} + B_{tok} = 0$. Indical field lines do not penetrate into the superconductor.

The greater the inductiveness of the external field, the more current must flow across the surface to ensure that the interior of the metallic superconductor is shielded from the external field. At a certain critical value of the internal field $B_{kp}$, the field penetrates the internal bond, superconductivity is destroyed - the metal goes into the normal state. An external magnetic field is not necessary for such a transition. The current flowing along the surface of the superconductor itself creates a magnetic field, and when $B_{tok}$ reaches a value corresponding to $B_{kp}$, superconductivity is destroyed. The value of $B_{kp}$ increases with decreasing temperature, but even close to $T=0$ the value of $B_{kp}$ for pure superconducting metals is not large - no more than ten shares of dough.

In superconducting alloys, as well as in a number of superconducting compounds, at certain inductions $B_{kp,1}$ the field begins to penetrate the internal bond. In this case, separate clusters of magnetic induction lines appear. Each such clot is surrounded by ring-shaped currents that fill the outlets into liquid or gas. These clumps are called outputs. Inside, superconductivity is destroyed, but in the space between the exits it is preserved. With increasing magnetic field induction, the number of outputs increases.

At some inductions $B_{kp,2}$ the outputs begin to break, the field is “filled” with the sample, and superconductivity is completely destroyed. Superconductors with such properties are called type II superconductors.
