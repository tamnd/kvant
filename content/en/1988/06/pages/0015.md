⟦folio 13⟧

NUMBERS AND FUNCTIONS

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences A. S. YARSKY

“Algebraic function”, “algebraic equation”, “algebraic curve”... Among the almost boundless variety of branches of modern mathematics, there is no such thing where the algebraic side of the mathematical object under consideration is not subject to separate study. There is often even talk of a kind of “algebraic experiment” in mathematics.

The set of numbers and the set of functions are endowed with a single algebraic classification: “rational”, “algebraic”, “transcendental”. For numbers, this classification was developed back in the 17th century (although it was only in 1844 that the first proof of the transcendence of a certain set of numbers, which belonged to J. Liouville, appeared) and was gradually transferred to an increasingly wider set of mathematical objects.

The generality of this classification makes it possible, in particular, to look at some properties of functions and numbers from a unified point of view, to more clearly see their similarities and thereby more deeply understand their differences.

§ 1. Integers and functions

Let's consider two sets - the set $Z$ of numbers and the set $R[x]$ of polynomials in one variable $x$ with coefficients in the set $R$ of real numbers. At first glance, these two sets of differences solve everything: elements $Z$ are numbers, and elements $R[x]$ are functions, all elements $Z$ can be arranged in the form of a sequence $0,1,—1,2,—2,....$, and the operation cannot be performed with elements $R[x]$ - there are “too many” of them for this... So, it’s easy to see the difference between $Z$ and $R[x]$. It is more difficult to discover those properties that make these two sets similar, to discover the analogy that provides the basis for “calling different things by the same names” (which, according to A. Poincaré, is what mathematics is about).

1. The sum, difference, product of integers (polynomials) is an integer (polynomial).

2. Any integer (polynomial) can be divided with a remainder by a non-zero integer (non-zero polynomial).

Let's consider the second property - divisibility. The algorithm for dividing polynomials with a remainder - division by the "corner" - is very similar to the division by the "corner" of integers. For example:

$$\frac{x^3+2x^2-x-1}{x^3+3x^2-x^2-x+2}$$

From here

$$x^3+2x^2-x-1=(x^2-x+2)(x+3)-7.$$

In general, if $P, Q \in R[x]$ and $Q$ are non-zero polynomials, there is also $P_1, R \in R[x]$ such that

$$P=P_1Q+R,$$

and the degree of the remainder $R$ is strictly less than the degree of the divisor $Q$. The quotient $P_1$ and remainder $R$ are uniquely determined by the choice of dividend $P$ and divisor $Q$.

Let us recall a few definitions. The greatest common divisor (GCD) of the polynomials $P$ and $Q$ is the polynomial $D$ dividing $P$ and $Q$ and divisible by any other common divisor.*) Let us denote the GCD $P$ and $Q$ by the symbol $(P, Q)$.

*) In fact, we have defined the GCD only up to the constructed polynomial.
