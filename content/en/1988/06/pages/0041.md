⟦folio 39⟧

(It must be said that Professor Simpson was considered a recognized authority on topology in the Midwest and was the author of several important papers on topology and field theory, in which he attacked Sleipenaarski's basic theories.)

We arrived on time. After our guest of honor had been introduced to Professor Simpson and another member of the society, we sat down at table. I drew Sleipenarski's attention to the tradition of preparing our banquets with small details, in a “topological spirit.” For example, the middle napkin pegs were made in the shape of Möbius strips. Specially baked bagels were served with coffee, and the coffee pot was made in the shape of a “Klein bottle.”

After my brief opening remarks, Sleipenarski entered, thanked those present with a smile for their applause, and cleared his throat.

It is hardly possible to present in any detail the brilliant, but understandable only to specialists, report of Sleipenarsky. Its essence boiled down to the following. About ten years ago, Sleipenarski came across a statement in one of the lesser-known works of Moebius that struck his imagination. According to Möbius, there was theoretically no reason why a surface could not lose both its sides: in other words, become “null-sided.”

Of course, the professor appeared, such a surface cannot be visualized visually, just like the square root of minus units or a hypercube in four-dimensional space. But the abstractness of a concept does not mean that it is meaningless or cannot find application in modern mathematics and physics.

We should not forget, the professor continued, that those who have never seen a Möbius strip cannot imagine even a one-sided surface. Many people with a well-developed mathematical imagination refuse to believe in the existence of a one-sided surface, even when they have a Mobius strip in their hands.

I looked at Professor Simpson, and it seemed to me that at these words he smiled slightly.

For many years, Sleipenarski continued, he persistently sought to construct a zero-sided surface. By analogy with known types of surfaces, he was able to study many properties of a zero-sided surface. Finally, augmented day has arrived. Sleipenarski paused to see what impression his words made on the listener, and looked around at the frozen audience. His efforts were successful, and he built a zero-sided surface.

Like an electrical discharge, his words stung those sitting at the table. Each one met, looked at his neighbor in surprise and settled down in a similar manner. Professor Simpson shook his head furiously. When Sleipenarsky went to the back of the dining room, where the blackboard was prepared, Simpson turned to his neighbor on the left and whispered: “This is nonsense! Lubo Slaippi is completely out of his mind, or he just decided to play a trick on us.”

It seems to me that the idea of ​​a prank occurred to many of those present. I saw some of them smile in disbelief as the professor drew complex diagrams on the blackboard.

After some appearances (I omit them completely for fear that they would be completely incomprehensible to most readers), the professor announced that he would like to conclude the lecture by constructing one of the simplest zero-sided surfaces. By this time, everyone present, not excluding me, was exchanging ideas with smiles. The smile on Professor Simpson's face was somewhat tense.
