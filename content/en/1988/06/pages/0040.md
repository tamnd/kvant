Gini Bublik is usually called a torus. The straw through which you drink a cocktail is also a torus, only elongated. From a topological point of view, a bagel and a straw are no different.

Topology is not interested in the properties of figures associated with length, area, volume and similar quantitative characteristics. She studies the most profound properties of figures and bodies, which remain unchanged under the most deformation conditions, but without breaks or deformations. If bodies and figures were cut, torn and glued together, then any body of any complex structure could be transformed into any other body with any structure, and all the original properties would be irrevocably lost. After a little thought, you will understand that topology is the study of the simplest, and at the same time the most profound properties that a body has *).

Although back in the eighteenth century much mathematics was accomplished by solving individual topological problems, the beginning of systematic work in the field of topology was laid by August Ferdinand Möbius, a German astronomer who taught at the University of Leipzig in the first half of the last century. Before Mobius, everyone thought that any surface had two sides, like a sheet of paper. It was Moebius who made a disconcerting discovery: if you take a strip of paper, twist it half a turn, and glue the ends together, you will get a one-sided surface with not two sides, but one single side!

It's hard to believe that this could even exist, but the one-sided surface actually exists - a real, tangible thing that anyone can build in an instant. The fact that the Möbius strip has only one side is not compatible, and it retains this property, no matter how you stretch and deform it.

But let's get back to our story. I started Mathematica at the University of Chicago, completed my doctorate in topology, and was able to introduce Mobius into the society without much difficulty. There weren't very many of us - just two chains of flowers, mostly Chicago topologists, but not some chains of society working at universities in neighboring cities.

We held monthly meetings that were purely academic in nature, but once a year - on November 17 (Mobius's birthday) - we gathered for a banquet and invited as a guest some famous topologist who gave a lecture.

This year we decided to send the anniversary of the patron of our society to the “Purple Hats”, where the prices were quite reasonable, and after the lecture you could go down to the hall and watch the Varte program. We were lucky with our guests: our invitation was accepted by the famous Professor Slipenarsky, the first topologist in the world and one of the greatest mathematical geniuses of our century.

Slipenarsky and I went to the Purple Hats together in a taxi, and on the way I asked him to outline in general terms what he was going to talk about at the lecture. Slipenarsky only smiled in response and advised him to be patient. The topic of the lecture, “Zero-Sided Surfaces,” also caused much anticipation among members of the Mobius Society, and even Professor Robert Simpson from the University of Wisconsin notified the board in writing of his intention to attend the banquet. Professor Simpson did not deign to be present at any event this year!
