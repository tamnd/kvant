Slapenarsky took out a pack of blue paper, a reaper and a tube of glue from his jacket pocket. He cut out a figurine from paper that strangely resembled a paper doll: five long protrusions, or appendages, resembling a head, arms and legs. Then he folded the figure and began to carefully glue the ends of the shoots together. The procedure was very delicate and required great care; the protrusions were very cleverly intertwined. Finally, there were only two loose ends left. Slapenarsky dripped glue onto one of them.

“Gentlemen,” he said, holding the intricate blue paper structure in front of him and turning it so that everyone could see, “you will now see the first public demonstration of Slapenarski’s surface.”

With these words, the professor pressed one of the free ends to the other.

There was a loud bang, as if an electric lamp had burst, and the paper figurine disappeared!

We froze for a moment, and then, as one, we all burst into laughter and applause.

Of course, we were convinced that we had become victims of a subtle hoax. But one cannot help but admit that everything was executed magnificently. Like other participants at the banquet, I believed that Slapenarsky showed us an ingenious chemical trick, and that the paper was impregnated with a special composition that made it possible to set it on fire with friction or some other method, after which it instantly burned, leaving no ashes.

Professor Slapenarsky seemed puzzled by the others' laughter, and his face took on a color indistinguishable from his beard. He smiled shyly and sat down. The apploidism gradually died down.

We all crowded around our guest and vying with each other to jokingly congratulate him on his replacement open. The senior official reminded us that tables are available for those who would like to order drinks and watch the cooking program.
