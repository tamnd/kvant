One of the possible designs of a droplet generator is shown in figure 1. The microelectrodriver 1 is fixed to the base 3 with a rigid crimp 2 and two bolts. A piece of eraser 4 is mounted on the motor shaft, through which a steel wire 5 is passed. The ends of the wire, when the shaft rotates, are alternately immersed in a liquid linted into a shallow jar (lid) 6, and, leaving it, splashes drops. It is clear that the diameter of the droplets is determined by the diameter of the wire, the depth of immersion in the liquid and the speed of its rotation. To be able to change this speed, connect the microelectrodriver to the battery through an improvised rheostat consisting of a stylus or a piece of low-voltage conductor. Drops falling from the ends of the rotating wire fly not only in the direction shown in figure, but also in other directions, therefore, the drop generator must be limited from the sides by a U-shaped protective screen made of cardboard. Placing a sheet of black paper in front of the open part of the screen, adjust the rotation speed of the splashing wire (it should be no more than a few revolutions per second) and determine the place where the drops fall. To observe a rainbow, it is enough to place a plate with drops under straight rays and select the required angle φ between these rays and the direction of observation (Fig. 2; the rainbow is shown schematically by a curved line). As follows from the rainbow theory, this angle for water should be equal to φ_b=42°, and for glycerin - φ_r=27°.

If everything is done well enough, you will see a beautiful bright rainbow and, perhaps, next to it a second, much fainter one. The phenomenon of the present is so impressive that it is really worth the effort to see it.
