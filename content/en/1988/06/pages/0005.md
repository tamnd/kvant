In 1937, P. L. Kapitsa discovered the phenomenon of superfluidity of a liquid gel - its ability to flow through narrow capillaries without any friction. Four years later, L.D. Landdu was able to explain this phenomenon. In Landdu's theory, the so-called superfluidity criterion was derived, according to which it arises when moving at a speed exceeding a certain critical one. I will describe this quality. Inhibition of the gel means a change in its energy and momentum. However, the liquid gel is a quantum liquid and can change energy and momentum by absorbing and emitting certain quanta called quasiparticles. These quasiparticles behave in the volume of the body like real particles, however, with an uneducated connection between energy and momentum. The difference between them and ordinary particles - electrons, photons - is that quasiparticles cannot exist outside the body. In liquid helium, particles can also appear only when the flow rate of the gel is above a certain final value.

It would seem that from here it is easy to move on to an explanation of superconductivity as the superfluidity of a charged electron liquid in metals. However, the properties of the quasiparticles generated in the electron liquid turned out to be different from the properties of the quasiparticles in liquid helium. So, for them the critical speed is zero, and, therefore, the flow of current without resistance turns out to be non-existent. What is the difference between these two liquids - liquid helium and e-liquid? First of all, it lies in the fact that the intrinsic moment of rotation, called spin, is equal to zero for gel atoms, and $\hbar/2$ for electrons, where $\hbar$ is Planck’s constant. Therefore, the question immediately arose whether electrons could combine into pairs. For such pairs, the total spin was either zero (electron spins are directed in opposite directions) or $\hbar$. Similar pairs of quasiparticles in an electron liquid can be directed to quasiparticles of a liquid gel, and one could hope to cause the phenomenon of superconductivity by analogy with superfluidity. However, electrons are single-film charged particles; due to the Coulomb interaction, they are bounced off, and it would seem that there is no reason for pairing.

It was only in 1950 that an experiment was carried out to change the critical fields $B_{\kappa p}$ and temperatures $T_{\kappa p}$ of mercury samples of different isotopic states, which shed light on the possible causes of pair formation. We found out that the values ​​of $T_{\kappa p}$ and $B_{\kappa p}$ depend on the mass of the isotope according to the law

$$T_{\kappa p}, B_{\kappa p} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}.$$

But the mass of the nuclei forming the critical lattice is manifested only in their movement. Thus, it became clear that this movement is essential for superconductivity. Based on this fact, the English physicist G. Freilix and, independently of him, the American physicist J. Bardeen proposed a concept that explains the nature of the forces of attraction between electrons. The fact is that the nuclei, or rather the ions that form the critical lattice of a metal, are also a quantum system, and in this system there are also quasiparticles corresponding to lattice flasks. They are called phonons. Electrons can exchange phonons, and this bond leads to an attraction that can overcome direct Coulomb repulsion.
