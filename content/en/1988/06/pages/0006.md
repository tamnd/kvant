Cooper, who drew attention to the fact that we are talking about the formation of pairs not from two isolated electrons, but in the presence of the entire set of other electrons. We can say it differently: pairs are formed not from electrons, but from quasiparticles of the electron liquid. These pairs began to be called Cooper's after the name of their discoverer.

A year later, in 1957, Bardin, Cooper and R. Schrieffer, and independently of them, Academician N.N. Bogolyubov, built a microsop theory of superconductivity, which connected together all the known experimental facts about the properties of superconductors.

I will not present this theory here due to its complexity. I will note only a few important circumstances. First of all, if the system is at $T=0$, then energy can only be transferred to it by breaking the pair. This requires the expenditure of finite energy, which is denoted by $2\Delta$. In this regard, the electronic heat at low temperatures behaves like $e^{-\Delta/T}$. Second: I have already noted that for the connection of electrons into pairs, the entire electronic collective exists. But the state of this system depends on temperature. Therefore, the binding energy of the $2\Delta$ pair depends on temperature and at $T=T_{\text{кр}} \Delta(T)$ it becomes zero—the superconductor becomes a normal metal.

The third property is due to the fact that pairs have a finite size, on the order of $10^{-4}-10^{-5}$ cm. The question arises: how do they not interfere with each other? After all, the average distance between electrons in a metal is of the order of $10^{-8}$ cm. This paradox is a manifestation of the quantum properties of matter. However, among the authors of the microsop theory of superconductivity, Schrieffer, for comparison, likened the electrons in a superconductor to a dance in a modern disco. The two dance, and although there are many other dancers between them, they do not lose touch with each other. Therefore, it is more accurate to speak not about pairs, but about the pair correlation of electrons in a superconductor.

I have already told you that when the external magnetic field is compared with the critical one, the superconductor jumps into the normal state. This statement, strictly speaking, is valid only for a cylindrical sample in a longitudinal field and, moreover, not for all superconductors. Indeed, why shouldn't a massive superconductor be broken down into thin elephants of normal and superconducting metal and maintain superconductivity until the city of large fields? After all, the critical field for a thin layer is higher than for a massive superconductor. The separation into elephants does not occur because in all pure superconductors consisting of one metal, there is a special surface energy that arises at the boundaries between the normal and superconducting phases. This energy, like surface tension, tends to reduce the surface area of ​​the boundaries. The microfurnace theory explained its origin. It turned out that it is related to the finite size of Cooper pairs. If you reduce this size, the surface energy can change sign and become negative. Thus, a natural division of superconductors will arise into superconductors of the first kind - with positive surface energy, and superconductors of the second kind - with negative surface energy. It should be noted that type II superconductors are much more common than type I superconductors. Moreover, any superconductor of the first kind can be converted to the second kind. To do this, it is enough to introduce a certain amount of atoms into it or otherwise spoil the crystal lattice. Electrons begin to scatter on these defects. The nature of electron movement changes, and the size of the pairs becomes smaller
