SUPERCONDUCTIVITY:

history, modern ideas, recent successes

Academician A. A. ABRIKOSOV

The phenomenon of superconductivity was discovered by the Dutch physicist Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911. Kamerlingh Onnes was the first to obtain liquid helium, and he used it to create crystals - devices that can be maintained at very low temperatures. In particular, he decided to check the correctness of the idea that existed at that time about the behavior of electrical resistance at low temperatures. Measuring the resistance of mercury, Kamerlingh Onnes discovered that it abruptly vanishes at a temperature of about 4 K. This phenomenon was called superconductivity, and the temperature of transition to the superconducting state was called critical. Currently, many superconductors are known with a wide variety of critical temperatures, from fractions of a degree to approximately 100 K. But more on the latter later.

Post-graduate research into superconductors reveals many of the remarkable properties. Thus, it turned out that superconductivity is destroyed by a magnetic field. The critical field at which this occurs depends on temperature. It was further discovered that superconductivity disappears even when a sufficiently large current is passed through the sample. Finally, the so-called Meissner effect was discovered, the essence of which is as follows. If you place a metal in a not very strong magnetic field and the temperature is high, then when the metal transitions to a superconducting state, the confluent field lines will be pushed out of it. Subsequent studies have shown that in fact, with such a transition, a small layer, $10^{-5}—10^{-6}$ cm thick, will appear at the surface of the superconductor, in which currents circulate, completely shielding the internal regions of the sample from the external field. The thickness of this layer is called the penetration depth.
