However, in the fall of 1986, a message appeared from physicists G. Bednorz and A. Mühler (Switzerland) about the discovery of superconductivity of the La - Ba - Cu - O compound with a critical temperature in the region of 30 K. These authors did not immediately approach their goal opening. The fact is that even before them, the superconducting oxide Ba - Pb - Bi - O with a critical temperature of 14 K was known. The strange thing about this compound was that the density of free electrons carrying current was 10²¹ cm⁻³, which is an order of magnitude less than that of ordinary metals. At the same time, according to the Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) theory, the value of the critical temperature increases with the number of free electrons. It is clear that a decrease in the number of electrons should lead to a decrease in Tₖp. And 14 K was a relatively high critical temperature.

Next, the attention of Bednorz and Mühler was attracted by oxides containing copper in a state with an intermediate valence: part Cu⁺⁺, and part Cu⁺⁺⁺. Such oxides were studied by French physicists. They included the rare element lanthanum as a structural element. If we take the compound La₂CuO₄, then the copper in it is only divalent, and this substance behaves as an insulator. Mühler and Bednorz began to replace trivalent lanthanum with divalent elements in order to take away some electrons from copper and thereby partially convert copper into the trivalent state. As a result, the first high-temperature superconductor La₂-xBaₓCuO₄, where x=0.1-0.2, was obtained. At first, Bednorz and Mühler did not believe anything, and their article was published by the leading American physics journal “Physical Review Letters”. Then they sent it to the German journal Zeitschrift für Physik, where it was published in the fall of 1986. Initially the article did not arouse interest. But then Japanese experts decided to check the message and were convinced that Mueller and Bednorz were not mistaken. After this, American physicists pointed out research, and from the beginning of 1987 a real “superconducting boom” broke out. There are now several thousand articles on this topic. I can't tell you all the details of this race, but I will note the main points.

It was natural to try to increase the critical temperature by replacing the elements with their chemical analogues. Replacing Ba with Sr resulted in Tₖp=45 K. An American group from Houston led by P. Chu subjected these samples to rolling and found that the critical temperature increases rapidly during rolling, although in conventional superconductors under similar conditions Tₖp tends to decrease slightly. Then they decided to try to create a “chemical” scatium by replacing lanthanum atoms with smaller intria atoms. As a result, a compound with a fantastically high critical temperature Tₖp~93 K was obtained.

The idea of ​​a "chemical scathium" helped make the discovery, but in the end it turned out to be wrong. Very carefully studied indicators that a high-temperature superconductor are the “1 - 2 - 3” phase: Y Ba₂Cu₃O₇-x, where x is less than one. Subsequent attempts to replace Y with other elements have shown that superconductivity with Tₖp~90 K is observed in compounds with the “1 - 2 - 3” structure, where osmium and almost all rare earths, including lanthanum, can replace intrium.

Now I will talk about some of the features of these compounds and attempts to theoretically explain the high Tₖp. First of all, about the structure. In the general typical compounds La - Ba - Cu - O and Y - Ba - Cu - O, it corresponds to the so-called Slonsky periodic ones.
