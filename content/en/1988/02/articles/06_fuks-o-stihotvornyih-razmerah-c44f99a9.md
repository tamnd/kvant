Determination of size. We postulate that the text (verses) in question is divided into lines, the lines are divided into words consisting of words (we ignore words like “in” or “to”). The syllables in each line are numbered consecutively, starting from the first. One of the words of each word is stressed (this is the syllable on which the stress falls when reading the word in person).

As is always the case with axioms, this statement only approximately reflects reality. Firstly, there are words with two (or more?) stresses: self-zoning, doubly periodic, etc. However, these are long, awkward words that are rarely found in poetry. Secondly, in some words, such as or, through a period, etc., the stress may move to another syllable. This often happens in poetry, an example is the mention of the 26th line from “Eugene Onegin” - Or they shone, my reader; Remember again: Through the forests, Through the seas, the sorcerer carries the boytyrs. Such a transfer of stress is also possible in colloquial speech - say: “A river flows, there is a lot of space through it,” and you will hear that the stress in the words falls on the second syllable. Thirdly, and finally, some groups of words, for example, “homework” in the phrase “Homework assignment,” form so-called rhythmic groups that should be equated with words. For details, see the article by A. N. Kolmogorov and A. V. Prokorov “Model of the rhythmic line of Russian speech, adaptations to the study of the metrics of classical Russian stixa” in the collection “Russian verse composition” (M.: Nauka, 1985). We ignore all these anomalies here, introducing the following rule: all the rules formulated below have exceptions, it is only necessary that these exceptions be few.

For example, iambic, discussed above, is (2,2)-meter.

A work is of (n, k)-size if each line is of (n, k)-size. When reading a poem, strong syllables are emphasized with more or less distinct stress. But let us emphasize once again: one should not confuse, let alone put aside, strong and stressed syllables. A strong syllable does not have to be stressed (the first syllable of the word is from the second line of “Eugene Onegin”), but the stressed syllable does not have to be strong (the word is not in the same line). The rule placed within the framework precisely indicates when a strong syllable must be stressed, that is, when a syllable with a certain number in a line is actually stressed when read correctly.

In more detail, (n, k)-meter is called complex size with the kth strong beat.

Let us note that our terminology is not entirely consistent with that adopted in poetry. In particular, what we call size is more often called meter, and size also includes the number of strong syllables in a line, or, as they say, the number of moans. For example, iambic is meter, and iambic pentameter is meter.

Let's start with $n=1$. Obviously, any text is subject to (1,1)-meter: all syllables are strong. Reading such poems means chanting (shai-bu-shai-bu!). Poems of this kind exist, for example: No-eats-nebet-men-shoi-sy-nok (Nekrasov), Ko-siko-ca-no-ka-ro-ca (Tvardovsky). But they are rare.

The sizes with $n>3$ are more interesting, especially with $n=4$ and 5.

Combination of sizes. Before turning to them, let's ask ourselves: can the same string be subordinated to two different sizes? Our definition does not exclude this possibility. For example, a line is composed of only monosyllabic words, subject to any size.

In practical terms, this means that different two-syllable meters, as well as different three-syllable meters, cannot be combined. It would seem that it is easier to combine a two-syllable meter with a three-syllable one, although in this case gray restrictions on the length of words arise.

Rule 3. Prove that if a line is subordinate to both a trochee and a dactyl, then the 4th syllable is part of either a monosyllabic word or a word consisting of at least 4 words.

Nabokov gives an example of a line subordinate to iambic and amphibrach: Mysterious and unearthly. But a line is not yet a poem. Moreover, the strong syllables of two-syllable and three-syllable meters combined in one poem would be completely different, and this poem (if it existed) could be read in two completely different ways. One can say that you will not find such a poem in any positive collection.

Another thing is the combination of bisyllabic and tetrasyllabic meters, and the reason is that 4 is divisible by 2. For example, what does it take for a line subject to iambic to also obey, say, (4,2)-meter? It is necessary that in a word containing more than one even syllable, the stress falls on a syllable whose number is not divisible by 4. Eliminate the last “not”, and you get the conditions of iambic (4,4)-meter. Since there may not be very many words containing several four-syllable words in a poem, this condition of presentation

Four-syllable sizes. Now we can turn to their consideration. Each four-syllable meter we will assign a two-syllable meter in accordance with it: for (4,1) and (4,3) meter, this is, by definition, considered to be trochee, for (4,2) and (4,4) meter - iambic. Further, if a line obeys a four-syllable meter, we will speak of its meter as a non-independent four-syllable if it also obeys a conjugate two-syllable meter, and as an independent one otherwise.

Let's start with the independent four-syllable meter. I must admit that I do not know of any convincing examples of Russian poetry subordinated to an independent four-syllable meter. Probably, there are none - one can come to such conclusions by reading the article by poet L. E. Lyapina “Russian Songs” in the already cited collection “Russian Versification”. (Peonies are the scientific name for four-syllable meters; and five-syllable meters are scientifically called pentons.) Why is this so? In search of an answer, let's try to construct a line subject to an independent four-syllable meter:

I wrote forty articles in the magazine «Kvant». This line obeys (4,3)-meter, but does not obey trochee. Reading it causes obvious difficulties: the stress in the words forty and the log of each station is not in place. What's the matter? The fact is that, as has already been said, approximately a third of the words in a poem should be stressed; Therefore, strong words have not been achieved, and it comes to adding at least weak additional stress to them. For political reasons, these stresses want to be placed in the middle of the interval between strong syllables, and this means that the four-syllable size tends to be dependent.

Non-independent four-syllable meter is a very common phenomenon. If you doubt its existence (as do neokoto poets - polemics with them constitute the main content of the above-read article by L. E. Lyapina), ask any student of a music school - and he will tell you that the four-syllable meter is found very often in music, in particular in songs:

Was I in the field and not a grass...
Oh how I love my cows...
(all this is a non-independent (4.3)-meter; it, like the three-beat one, is very characteristic of Russian song). If this doesn’t convince you, look through Pushkin. Here is a sample of (4,2)-meter from his youthful poems:

In the ovens of Helikon
I was once undressed;
In the name of Apollo
Named Tibullus...

The famous “Winter Road” by Pushkin (On the winter, boring road...) also seems to me to be written in four-syllable meter; however, the composer Sviridov suggested reading these verses as two-syllable.

Moreover, often (one might say, as a rule) only part of the poem is subordinated to the four-syllable meter, and transitions from a four-syllable rhythm to a two-syllable one and back can be unbearable or figurative. This can be easily seen, for example, in Nekrasov’s pose “Who Lives Well in Rus'” (non-independent (4.2) size).

Answer: (5,3)-size.

Indeed, suppose that the given poem obeys (n, k)-meter. In all 20 lines, the 3rd and 8th syllables are stressed (in their own words). There are 7 lines in which the word containing the 3rd syllable has more than 3 syllables. This means that either the 3rd syllable is strong, or n≥5. The same is true for the 8th syllable. Further, the 3rd and 8th syllables are simultaneously strong only in the five-syllable size. It is easy to check that the poem really obeys the (5,3)-meter and does not obey other five-syllable meters. We exclude sizes with n>5 from consideration (see below).

To be convincing, I’ll add that out of our 20 lines, 5 obey the (3,3)-size, 3 obey the (4,1)-size, 1 obeys the (4,4)-size, all 20 obey the (5,3)-size, 10 obey the (5,5)-size (this is the cost of our definition, see p. 18) and none obeys any other (n, k)-size with n, not greater than 5. A machine would give us such information if we resorted to its help.

But where is the drawn-out melodiousness of “Merchant Kalashnikov” in “Dragon”? The main reason for its absence is that in almost every line of "Dragon", in addition to the main stresses on the 3rd and 8th syllables, there is a fairly distinct additional deletion either on the 6th syllable. Additional stresses can be traced in other poems by Gumilyov, written in five-syllable size, as well as in Akhmatova and Tsvetaeva. In a certain sense, here too one could talk about the lack of independence of the (five-syllable) meter, but it is impossible to clarify this meaning while remaining within the framework of force-botonics.

Tsvetaeva’s poems, for example, are difficult to approach with the definition of meter that was given at the beginning of the article and which we use all the time. Many of her poems are written in individual meter with fixed but not arithmetic progression numbers of strong syllables, say 3, 6, 8, as in the poem.

Four, five, what's next? Next - 6. Are there six-syllable meters? May be. So, although with some naturalness, one can call the six-syllable size of individual places in “Merchant Kalashnikov”:

They buried him across the Moscow River...
Between Tula, Ryazan, Vladimir...

But the number 6 has too many divisors, and the six-syllable meter will most likely break down into two or three syllables (the lines above are no exception). Almost any poem written in a three-syllable meter will be subordinated to a six-syllable one, but this does not affect the sound of the verse.

In general, speaking about (n, k)-meters with large n, one should, firstly, keep in mind that very far apart strong syllables are unlikely to be able to create a certain rhythm of the verse, and secondly, take into account the following, purely arithmetic circumstance.

Exercise 4. Let m be the number of words in the line. a) If n≥k>m, then the string is completely subject to (n, k)-size (there are no strong words - compare with the case n=k=1, p. 21). 6) If k≤m<k+n, then the condition of subordination to the (n, k)-size does not depend on n and the property that the k-th syllable is deleted.

Therefore, it is advisable to assume (this can be added to our definition) that the number of words in the lines of a poem subordinate to (n, k)-size (at least in most lines) is not less than k+n. (For example, it makes no sense to look for (5,5)-dimensions in Gumilyov’s “Dragon” - see p. 23.)

In practice, all this means that there are no (n, k)-dimensions with n greater than 5, so we can end our conversation. Beyond its borders there were numerous deviations from the syllabote, which are rich in Russian life. But these digressions are also beyond the scope of our arithmetical approach to poetic meters.
