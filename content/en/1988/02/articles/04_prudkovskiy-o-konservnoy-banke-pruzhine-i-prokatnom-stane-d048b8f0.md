ABOUT THE CAN,
SPRING
AND ROLLING MILL

Doctor of Technical Sciences
V. A. PRUDKOVSKY

Let's assume that you have had to open cans more than once. And from time to time you wondered: couldn’t the metal sheet from which the jar is made be made thinner? Then, for example, from the materials for one tin can, two or even three could be made. What's the matter? Why don't metallurgists save metal? Let's try to figure it out.

Tin cans are made from thin steel sheet - tin. Tinplate is produced by cold rolling. It must be said that more than 500 years have passed since the earliest reliable mention of the use of rolling for deforming metal. In 1486, the great artist, architect, scientist and engineer Leonardo da Vinci depicted a rolling mill for producing thin strips of gold and silver for jewelry. Such a mill had two smooth rolls with a diameter of about 40 mm, which were turned by hand. The essence of the rolling process has not fundamentally changed since then (figure 1, a). A metal strip is pulled into the gap between the rollers rotating in different directions, the thickness of which ($h_0$) is greater than the size of this gap ($S_0$). Under the influence of the rolls, the strip undergoes plastic deformation and changes in shape and should emerge from the rolls with a thickness seemingly equal to the size of the gap between them (while increasing in length and remaining the same width $B$). However, in fact, only the metal of the rolling strip ($h_1$) is always larger than the contamination between the rolls. Why?

The deformation of the strip will occur as a result of the forces applied to it by the rolls. Each roll acts on the strip, and according to Newton’s third law, the strip acts on the roll with the same force; it is called the resistance force of the deformed or simply the resistance of the deformed. Under the influence of this force, elastic deformation of the rolls and other parts of the rolling mill occurs, the initially established gap between the rolls increases, and the thickness of the rolling strip increases. Well, okay, you say, then you need to make the gap between the rolls less than the required thickness of the rigid and get the required thickness. Logical! Although this is only one way.

But is it really that simple? The thickness of a rigid “normal” tin can is 0.2-0.25 mm. What gap should be installed between the rolls to obtain this sheet thickness? What gap needs to be set to get a sheet with a thickness of, say, 0.1 mm?

Let's first see how the rolling mill (figure 2) is built. The rollers (1), the deformation strip, cannot appear in the air. They are mounted on bearings (2) together with support rolls (3) in a frame called the rolling mill frame (4). The required gap between the rolls during rolling is set using screws, which are called push vintimi vintimi (5).

The deformation resistance force acting on the work rolls from the side of the rolled strip causes elastic deformation not only of these rolls, but also of the support rolls, as well as pressure screws, mill screws and other elements of the rolling mill. In this case, the rolling mill behaves like a circle, although not of a very ordinary design.

It is known from a physics course that every spring has rigidity, which determines its degree of deformation under the influence of applied forces. What does the rigidity of a rolling mill depend on if we consider it?

On the other hand, based on experiments on strip rolling in rolls, we found the values ​​of its resistance to deformation. Now we can answer the question posed earlier: what gap $S_0$ should be installed between the rolls in order to obtain a strip thickness equal to $h_1$? Let us assume that we want to obtain a strip with a thickness of $h_1$ on a rolling mill, the rigidity of which is determined by the value of tg $\alpha$ from figure 3. To do this, we must first find from the graph of the dependence $F_c(h)$ for the resistance to deformation of the strip, corresponding to its rolling from a thickness of $h_0$ to $h_1$. Next, it is necessary to equate this force with the elasticity of the mill (according to Newton’s third law) and, taking into account the rigidity of the mill, determine the required gap value $S_0$.

Figure 5 makes it clear how this operation is carried out. On figure the dependence graphs of $F_y(S)$ and $F_c(h)$ are given together. From point $A$ to figure 5, $\delta$ restores the perpendicular to the intersection with the curve $L_2$ (point $B$). From point $B$ we draw a horizontal line to the intersection with the perpendicular, restored from point $K$ (figure 5, $a$), the corresponding gap $S_1$ between the rolls, which will be equal to the thickness of the strip $h_1$ ($S_1=h_1$). Through the point $D$ of the intersection of the horizontal $BD$ and the vertical $KD$ we draw a straight line $L_1$, parallel to $L_0$ and defining the arm of the rolling mill with a given rigidity tg $\alpha$. Then the point $E$ of intersection of the straight line $L_1$ with the axis $S$ gives us the required value $S_0$ for the gap between the rolls, which should be set before rolling in order to obtain a given strip thickness equal to $h_1$.

Let us now assume that we need to obtain a strip of thickness $h_2$ (see figure 5, $\delta$). Carrying out similar reasoning, we find ourselves at point $E'$, which corresponds to a negative gap between the rolls. Consequently, we cannot carry out such a rolling process and this rolling mill allows, within the limit of obtaining the minimum possible strip thickness, equal to $h_{np}$. Thus, each rolling mill has its own minimum strip thickness, depending on the properties of the rolled metal and the strip size. It is impossible to obtain a strip with a thickness less than this limit on this mill.

As you will agree, tin cans are by no means the main products made from steel strip. For example, for picture tubes of color TVs, a strip of special steel 0.15 mm thick is needed with permissible deviations from this thickness of ±3 mm. Of course, the technology for producing such a strip differs significantly from technological strips for the production of cans. Rolling mills are also of great importance.

When making capacitors and gaskets in magnetic heads for small-sized equipment, foil (the thinnest tape) of aluminum, tantalum and beryl bronze with a thickness of 1-3 microns is required. How to get such a ribbon? It should be noted that, as a rule, it is quite narrow, 400-600 mm in width (while the strip for cans reaches 1.5 m in width). Of course, for a narrower band the resistance of the deformed band is less than for a wide band. To roll such a narrow and thin strip, it uses sharply rigid twenty-shaft rolling mills; rolling is carried out in two work rolls 400–1200 mm long, each of which rests on a straight line of support rolls. In general, sheet rolling mills are determined by the length of the roll (strip width); so when they say “mill 1700”, this means that the length of the working part of the rolls is 1700 mm.

There is another way to reduce the maximum possible strip thickness. It consists of a change in the shape of the curve $L_2$ (see figure 5, 6), i.e., a decrease in the resistance force of the deformed. This can be achieved in various ways: changing the friction conditions between the rolls and the strip using lubricants, tensioning the strip during rolling, reducing its width, and others.

A certain heat treatment of the metal (annealing) is carried out, which reduces the resistance of the deformed material, which, by the way, is widely used in the production of film tapes. However, all measures related to increasing the rigidity of the mill and reducing the resistance of the deformed one, as a rule, are associated with additional costs. Thus, a strip for kinesops costs significantly more than a strip for the production of cans. The cost of the thinnest ribbons of some metals is sometimes 20 times more than the cost of gold of the same mass. So you need to think: what is more profitable - rolling thicker and cheaper tin or thinner and more expensive one. Obviously, in each specific case you need to use the real possibilities.

Not long ago, at the Karaganda Metallurgical Plant, a new mill was put into examination, which makes it possible to produce tin plate with a thickness of 0.18 mm. Thin sheets of aluminum alloys are beginning to be used for cans. The resistance of aluminum deformed during rolling is lower than during rolling. So the issue with cans will be resolved. But the point, as we have already said, is not only about cans, although sometimes you cannot do without them. The point is different. The problem of tool deformation during metal forming is a very serious problem. In our case, the tool was rolls, and rolling is one of the metal forming processes. There are a lot of such processes, and they are gradually replacing metal cutting, as they are more economical and low-waste. However, the dimensional accuracy of a product during pressure processing, especially in a cold state, significantly depends on the rigidity of the tool and the processing units themselves. The problem of increasing rigidity is still far from being solved and is waiting in the wings.
