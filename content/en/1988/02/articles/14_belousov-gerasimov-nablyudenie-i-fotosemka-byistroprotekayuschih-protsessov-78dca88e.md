Observation and photography of fast processes occurring

S. M. BELOUSOV, R. N. GERASIMOV

The book by J. Darius “Inaccessible to the Eye” pushed us to this work, in which we read about high-speed photography and saw interesting photographs.

We decided to assemble a setup that would allow us to take photographs of rapid processes. And what

This article, written by Leningrad schoolchildren, talks about the extensive and interesting work carried out by the authors - students of the 9th grade of Physics School No. 45 at Leningrad State University - on the basis of the Research Institute of Physics of Leningrad State University.

If these processes could also be observed directly with the eye, we installed a stroboscopic one. The block diagram of our installation is shown in figure 1.

The process of falling drops on the surface of water was taken as the object of study.

The next drop falling into the water interrupts the laser beam, darkening the photodiode. An electrical pulse from the photodiode reaches a timer (adjustable delay), which is needed so that different phases of the process under study can be observed. The delayed pulse enters the forming amplifier, opens, amplified many times, and goes to ignite the flash lamp.

In order for the drop not to be smeared in the photograph, a fairly short exposure time is needed. This time (τ) can be easily estimated based on the following messages. A drop falling into water from a height of H=20 cm has a speed at the water surface

Lograph S1-64. The current pulse that occurs when the drop passes through the laser beam leads to the launch of sweep A. After a time $T$, determined by the position of the “sweep duration A” and “sweep delay B” knobs, the oscilloscopes generate a delayed sweep trigger pulse (sweep B). This pulse was supplied to the input of the external trigger of the G5-63 pulse generator. The pulse generated by the generator was fed to the flash lamp ignition circuit. The delay time was set with a multi-turn potentiometer in the range from 0 to 400 μs and was absent with an accuracy of 1 μs.

We photographed a drop falling into water from different heights. The most successful and complete was a series of photographs of drops falling from a height of 37.5 cm. The photographs of this series proceed sequentially, like stages of an originating process. In the photographs you can see a ruler with millimeter divisions; it sets the scale. All photographs are taken to the same scale. The time $t$, which is given in the captions to the photographs, is absent from the moment the drops of water touch. Photography was carried out using a Zenit camera on Aerophoto film (1400 units).

Here is a brief description of the photograph.

Falling into water, the drop oscillates and because of this has a non-spherical shape. A drop hovering above the water is captured in photos 1 and 2. A small droplet is visible above the drop in photo 1. This is the so-called Plato ball, which occurs when a drop is separated from the dropper.

Hitting the water, the drop squeezes the liquid underneath it up along its edges into a thin cylinder. Photo 2 shows the beginning of this process. The liquid at the edges of an ever-increasing cylinder is energetically more favorable than forming drops. From the symmetry messages it is clear that the drops should be distributed evenly along the edges of the cylinder. That's why they get red photos like photo 3, where the cylinder looks like a crown.

Under the influence of gravity and surface tension, the crown begins to fall off (photo 4). When the crown falls off completely, a water hole will form in its place; after a while, a water hole will form.

The ka collapses, and a stolnik begins to grow from its center (photo 5).

The column grows and tires and at some point reaches its highest height (photo 6).

Under the influence of surface forces, a bridge is formed on the column, and the column breaks up into drops that fall back into the liquid. The successive stages of column disintegration into droplets are shown in photos 7-9. During stroboscopic observation, we noticed that depending on the thickness and height of the column, it breaks up into a different number of drops. (The collapse of the column is due to the fact that when drops form, their total surface energy turns out to be less than that of the column.) It turns out that the number of drops on which the column disintegrates does not depend on the tension surface coefficient, but is determined only by the ratio of the diameter to the height of the column.

After the drops from the disintegrated column have fallen into the water, a water hole forms again and the column is drawn in from the center (photo 10-12).
