However, there was one difference between the electric charge and the “magnetic charge” of the pole of a magnet. When electrifying a body, we obtain an isolated electric charge of a definable sign. And as a result of magnetization, it is impossible to obtain an isolated magnetic stripe - you necessarily get two opposite poles at once. A magnetized body cannot have an isolated “magnetic charge” of a certain sign.

But the differences were not very confusing. The tradition of Newtonian mechanics was too weak. Electrical directions and potential

Coulomb's law determined the force of interaction between charges. But how does this interaction take place? One could simply say that two charges act on each other at a distance according to Coulomb’s law, and not go into thinking about what invisible threads connect the charges, what invisible springs attract unlike charges and expand like ones. This is what Newton did in his time, studying theogeny: “I still could not deduce the reason for these properties of the gravitational force from the phenomenon, but I will not invent hypotheses... It is enough that gravity actually exists and acts according to the laws we have set forth and is quite sufficient to explain all the movements of celestial bodies and the sea.” But in order to simplify the calculation of the force of interaction between inaccurate charged bodies, it turned out to be convenient, purely formally, to introduce a certain characteristic of the space surrounding the charge - the electrical directivity $\vec{E}$ - the force with which the

The intensity of the electric field is a vector. Generally speaking, it changes from point to point - both in absolute value and in direction.

We can attribute to each point in space surrounding a given source of electric force (a precision charge, a charged body or a system of charged bodies) another characteristic - electric potential - the potential energy of interaction of a test unit charge placed at this point with the source of force. When moving a test unit charge from one point to another, the electric force does work that is equal in absolute value to the difference in the electrical potentials of two points. In physics, along with measurable quantities, there are quantities that are very convenient, but not directly measurable. These quantities can be changed (transformed) in such a way that the physical consequences do not change. For example, the coordinate values ​​of a point $x$, $y$, $z$ depend on the choice of the origin and how the coordinate axes are oriented. But the directly measured distance between points does not depend on the choice of the origin of coordinates or on the direction of the coordinate axes. An equally convenient quantity, but depending on the choice of the beginning of the cut-off value, is the electric potential.

So, there are two characteristics of the space surrounding a charged body. They talk about what would happen if we placed a test unit charge at different points in space. One - electrical directionality - speaks of the electrical forces that would act on this charge at various points in space; the other - electric potential - talks about the potential energy that such a charge would have at different points.

Both characteristics are related to each other. The mathematical description of the properties of the electric force leads to some convenient technique - the calculation of the electric force acting on a given charge at a distance, but somehow transforms the space surrounding it. It turns out to be convenient to attribute the action of a given charge on other charges to a special state of space surrounding a given charge—its electric field. Electric potential is a scalar characteristic of this field. Electrical voltage is a vector characteristic.

As we will soon see, the electric field is not just a convenient mathematical device, but a physical reality.

Electromagnetic induction

If electric current has a magnetic effect, then could a magnet be a source of current? Is it possible to “cook magnetism into electricity”? This is the question asked by the English physicist M. Faraday (1791-1867), and his search for an answer led him to the discovery of the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction - the emergence of an electric current in a conducting circuit when the magnetic flux through the circuit changes.

Studying this phenomenon in different aspects, Faraday introduces the concept of “magnetic curves”. “Under the magnetic curves of the Japanmayu there are lines of magnetic forces, although distorted by the proximity of the poles; these lines are drawn with iron filings; Magnetic arrows would be located tangentially to them,” this is how Faraday defined “magnetic curves.” Today we call them magnetic induction lines. Electromagnetic field

So, in the middle of the 19th century, two opposing approaches to the description of electromagnetic phenomena emerged. The question was raised about whether magnets or charged bodies act directly at a distance or whether there is some medium that transmits the effect of one magnet (or current-carrying wire) to another, or one electrically charged body to another.

“When we observe that one body acts on another at a distance, then before accepting that this action is direct and indirect, we usually investigate whether there is any material connection between the bodies; and if we find that bodies are connected by threads, stubbles, or any mechanism capable of giving us an account of the observable actions of one body on another, we prefer to explain the actions by means of these intermediate links, rather than admit the concept of direct action at a distance.

So, when we, holding a wire, make a bell ring, the successive parts of the wires are first stretched and then set in motion, until, finally, the bell rings at a distance as a consequence of a process in which all the intermediate parts of the wire took part one after another.

We can make the bell ring at a distance in another way: for example, by pumping air into a long tube, at the other end of which there is a cylinder with a piston, the movement of which is transmitted to the bell. We can also use a wire, but instead of holding it, we can connect it at one end to an electric battery and at the other to an electromagnet, and thus make the bell ring by electricity.

Here we have indicated three different ways to set the bell in motion. But all these methods have in common that there is a continuous connecting line between the caller and the bell and that at each point of this line a certain physical process takes place, the consequence of which the action is transmitted from one end of the line to the other. The transfer process is not instantaneous, but gradual; so that after an impulse is given at one end of the connecting line, a certain period of time passes during which this impulse travels its way until

The works of Faraday and Maxwell affirmed the physical reality of the electromagnetic field. The space surrounding the charges seemed to be waiting. It turned out to be in a special state and had special properties. The discovery of electromagnetic induction makes it possible to approach these properties. Electrical and magnetic phenomena turned out to be truly interconnected. But this connection was revealed only in non-standard processes, through changes in electrical and magnetic characteristics over time.

A charge at rest is surrounded only by an electric field. The moving charge is surrounded by both an electric field and a magnetic field. If the electric current is constant, the magnetic field is constant. Such a field, in turn, acts only on moving charges—currents. Thus, the direct current field carries out only the magnetic action of currents. It's a different matter when the current is alternating. In this case, the magnetic field also turns out to be variable. And the law of electromagnetic induction determines the electrical action of such a field - it (the field) turns out to be a source of an electric field that moves charges in a wire, causing an electric current in this wire.

So, an electric field can exist outside of direct connection with charges - its source can be an alternating magnetic field. This electric field acts on charges. This means that it can be characterized by the force acting on a unit test charge, i.e. by electrical directivity. And this force will do work on a test electric charge moving in such an electric field. But unlike the case of an electrostatic field - a field of stationary charges - in the electric field of an alternating magnetic field, the work of the electric force depends on the path along which the test charge moves. In this field, work along a closed loop is not zero.
