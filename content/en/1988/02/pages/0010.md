The works of Faraday and Maxwell affirmed the physical reality of the electromagnetic field. The space surrounding the charges seemed to be waiting. It turned out to be in a special state and had special properties. The discovery of electromagnetic induction makes it possible to approach these properties. Electrical and magnetic phenomena turned out to be truly interconnected. But this connection was revealed only in non-standard processes, through changes in electrical and magnetic characteristics over time.

A charge at rest is surrounded only by an electric field. The moving charge is surrounded by both an electric field and a magnetic field. If the electric current is constant, the magnetic field is constant. Such a field, in turn, acts only on moving charges—currents. Thus, the direct current field carries out only the magnetic action of currents. It's a different matter when the current is alternating. In this case, the magnetic field also turns out to be variable. And the law of electromagnetic induction determines the electrical action of such a field - it (the field) turns out to be a source of an electric field that moves charges in a wire, causing an electric current in this wire.

So, an electric field can exist outside of direct connection with charges - its source can be an alternating magnetic field. This electric field acts on charges. This means that it can be characterized by the force acting on a unit test charge, i.e. by electrical directivity. And this force will do work on a test electric charge moving in such an electric field. But unlike the case of an electrostatic field - a field of stationary charges - in the electric field of an alternating magnetic field, the work of the electric force depends on the path along which the test charge moves. In this field, work along a closed loop is not zero. Therefore, it is impossible to associate the magnitude of the electric potential with each point in space—the work done to move a unit test charge from a certain initial point to a given one.
