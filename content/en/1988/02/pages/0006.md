Oersted describes the deflection of a magnetic needle under the influence of direct current in a straight wire. The force did not act along the straight line connecting the wire and the magnet. The magnetic force of the current turned out to be not repulsive or attractive, but “turning”.

Oersted's research was continued and developed by the French physicist and mathematician A. M. Ampere (1775-1836). He constructed the first theory of electrodynamics (Ampere's term), conceived as a continuation of Newton's work. But Newton’s description of action at a distance was already far from the first crack—the uniformity of such a description was being violated. A little more time will pass, and the very idea of ​​​​action at a distance will be rejected in the works of Faraday and Maxwell.

One could simply say that two charges act on each other at a distance according to Coulomb’s law, and not go into thinking about what invisible threads connect the charges, what invisible springs attract unlike charges and expand like ones. This is what Newton did in his time, studying theogeny: “I still could not deduce the reason for these properties of the gravitational force from the phenomenon, but I will not invent hypotheses...
