It is now clear: in order to determine the size of the gap required to obtain the rigidity of a given thickness, you need to know the value of the rigidity of the rolling mill. How to find her? There are, of course, calculation methods, but for an existing mill it is easier to change it.

Using pressure screws, we set some gap $S_0$ between the work rolls. Now, using a spacer device, we will each time increase this gap by values ​​$\Delta S$ and measure the elastic force of the mill with the diameter. At figure 3, the results of several measurements are presented in the form of a graph of the dependence of elastic forces $F_y$ on the size of the gap between the work rolls. This dependence is depicted by a straight line, since the deformations of the rolling mill parts are elastic and Hooke’s law is valid.

The tangent of the angle $\alpha$ characterizes the rigidity of the mill. The larger tg $\alpha$, the greater the rigidity. Of course, ideally the angle $\alpha$ should tend to $\pi/2$, but... all bodies are deformed.

Now let's see what happens in the real rolling process. We have already said that, according to Newton’s third law, the rolls act on the strip being rolled with the same magnitude of forces as the strip acts on the rolls. We called this force the resistance of the deformed. But how to find its magnitude? Obviously, for a rolling mill with a certain rigidity, its elastic deformation will depend on the resistance of the deformed strip being rolled. If strips of aluminum, copper and steel are rolled on the same mill, then the elastic deformation of the mill will be different in each specific case. When rolling aluminum it will be less, when rolling copper it will be more, and when rolling steel it will be even more. Why? Let us recall the distance diagram of metals. Diagrams are also drawn for aluminum, copper and steel. With their help, you can calculate the deformed resistance force for each specific metal or alloy. Thus, this force is mainly dependent on the material of the rolled strip.

But when rolling, what happens is in the basic state of the metal between the rolls, and not the distance. Let's assume that we rolled a strip of thickness $h_0$ and width $B$ by values ​​$\Delta h$ and recorded the force $F_c$ that we needed for this. At figure 4, the results of several wholesales are presented as a graph of forces $F_c$ versus strip thickness $h$. This dependence is no longer linear, since plastic deformation of the strip occurs. The force $F_c$ is, with some assumptions, nothing more than the resistance of the deformed strip.
