Two more balls, the radii of which we must find, touch the ball $Q$ externally and are located on opposite sides of it: the smaller one is closer to the vertex $C$, the larger one is further away from it. (Note that only the smallest ball $O_2$ fits inside the cube. The middle ball $O_1$ and the large ball $O_3$ “crawl out” beyond its boundaries and can touch not the faces of the cube, but their extensions.)

Let's find the radii of these three balls. Let us draw an auxiliary plane through the vertical edges $AA_1$ and $CC_1$. In this plane lie the centers of all the balls, their points of contact with each other and with the plane $ABCD$. Consider marking the balls $O_2$ and $Q$ (Fig. 5) with this plane. In triangle $O_2KQ$, only the length of leg $KO_2$ is unknown. But $KO_2=MN$, and we find the distance between points $M$ and $N$ from the projection onto the base plane $ABCD$ (Fig. 6);

$$MN=AC-AM-NC=a\sqrt{2}-R\sqrt{2}-x\sqrt{2}=\sqrt{2}(a-R-x).$$

Using Pythgor's theorem for the triangle $O_2KQ$, we obtain the equation

$$(R+x)^2=(R-x)^2+2(a-R-x)^2,$$

which has two roots (recall that by condition $R=0,5$, $a=1,5$): $(3-\sqrt{5})/2$ and $(3+\sqrt{5})/2$.

What does having two roots mean? Draw a section and projection for the balls $O_3$ and $Q$ similar to those shown in Figures 5 and 6. Make sure that the radius of the large ball $O_3$ is found from the equation, which differs from the one written only by the signs inside the parentheses on the right side. This difference is eliminated when squared. This means that the resulting two roots are the long radii of the balls $O_2$ and $O_3$, respectively.
