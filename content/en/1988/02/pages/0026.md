Four, five, what's next? Next - 6. Are there six-syllable meters? May be. So, although with some naturalness, one can call the six-syllable size of individual places in “Merchant Kalashnikov”:

They buried him across the Moscow River...
Between Tula, Ryazan, Vladimir...

But the number 6 has too many divisors, and the six-syllable meter will most likely break down into two or three syllables (the lines above are no exception). Almost any poem written in a three-syllable meter will be subordinated to a six-syllable one, but this does not affect the sound of the verse.

In general, speaking about (n, k)-meters with large n, one should, firstly, keep in mind that very far apart strong syllables are unlikely to be able to create a certain rhythm of the verse, and secondly, take into account the following, purely arithmetic circumstance.

Exercise 4. Let m be the number of words in the line. a) If n≥k>m, then the string is completely subject to (n, k)-size (there are no strong words - compare with the case n=k=1, p. 21). 6) If k≤m<k+n, then the condition of subordination to the (n, k)-size does not depend on n and the property that the k-th syllable is deleted.

Therefore, it is advisable to assume (this can be added to our definition) that the number of words in the lines of a poem subordinate to (n, k)-size (at least in most lines) is not less than k+n. (For example, it makes no sense to look for (5,5)-dimensions in Gumilyov’s “Dragon” - see p. 23.)

In practice, all this means that there are no (n, k)-dimensions with n greater than 5, so we can end our conversation. Beyond its borders there were numerous deviations from the syllabote, which are rich in Russian life. But these digressions are also beyond the scope of our arithmetical approach to poetic meters.
