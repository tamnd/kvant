When it comes to electric current, we often think of current in wires, bringing light from light bulbs, friends' voices on the phone, world events on TV. However, in physics, the “quiet” current of wires came much later than the current of an electric discharge. The first experiments to study currents were carried out specifically with discharges. While studying the discharges of lightning or a Leyden jar - the prototype of the modern capacitor - Franklin and the Italian physicist G. Beccaria (1716-1781) observed the magnetic effect of electric current. Iron objects located next to the conductors through which the electric discharge passed became magnetized. It was clear that electrical and magnetic phenomena were somehow connected.

The analogy in the behavior of strips of magnets and electric charges was obvious - like poles, like like charges, repelled, opposite poles attracted. Experimental methods developed by Coulomb allowed him to measure the force of interaction between two magnets. It turned out to be inversely proportional to the square of the distance; as in the case of the force of the electrical interaction of two charges, Coulomb postulated that the magnetic force is proportional to the product of the “magnetic charges” of the stripes. However, there was one difference between the electric charge and the “magnetic charge” of the pole of a magnet. When electrifying a body, we obtain an isolated electric charge of a definable sign. And as a result of magnetization, it is impossible to obtain an isolated magnetic stripe - you necessarily get two opposite poles at once. A magnetized body cannot have an isolated “magnetic charge” of a certain sign.

But the differences were not very confusing. The tradition of Newtonian mechanics was too weak. It seemed natural that between two small bodies all forces, regardless of their nature, act uniformly - along a straight line connecting the bodies; and with distance all forces decrease equally - inversely proportional to the square of the distance between the bodies. An attractive study is the only one of all distances - gravitational, electric, magnetic. Then the difference in forces would appear only in different places of their influence: gravitational force is proportional to the product of body masses, electric force is proportional to electric charges, magnetic force is proportional to magnetic charges.

At the beginning of the 19th century, thanks to the invention of the electric battery by A. Volt (1745-1827), it became possible to study the effect of direct current. Instead of short-term electric discharge currents, laboratories received a continuous electric current in the wires connecting the battery poles. For twenty years, having a continuous current at their disposal, physicists searched for the magnetic strength of the current. The force acting in a straight line between a magnet and a current.
