How are all these pillar structures connected? Complex numbers help to find an explanation. (We rely here on the reader being familiar with complex numbers and their geometric interpretation.) Let the points O, A and B be assigned the numbers zero, a and b. We will define the desired straight line l by its point z, |z|=1 (Fig. 6). Since 2OC = |a/z + a/ē|, 2AC = |a/z - ē/ē|, area S_AOC = |a²/z² - ē²/ē²|/8 and similarly S_BOD = |b²/z² - ē²/ē²|/8. The numbers under the model sign in the last two equalities are minima, so the equality S_AOC = S_BOD is equivalent to the condition ē²/ē² - ē²/ē² ± (b²/z² - b²/ē²), or (b²±a²)/z² = (b²±a²)/ē².

This means that z² = ±t(b²±a²), where t is some positive real number, i.e. z₁,₂ = r√b²±a² and z₃,₄ = ir√b²±a², where r = 1/|√b²±a²|. The numbers b¹+a² and b²−a² define, respectively, the line containing the median of the triangle OAB₁ and the line parallel to AB₁ from the first construction (see Fig. 2); the known square root is answered by drawing the bicsectrix of the angles AOM and AOM'. The second construction (see Fig. 4, 5) is obtained if we factorize the radical expressions: b²−a² = (b−a)(b+a), b²+a² = (b−ia)(b+ia). The numbers b±a and b±ia are the points P₁, P₂, Q₁ and Q₂, and the geometric mean √uv of the two numbers u and v specifies the bicsectrix of the angle between the vectors u and v. For example, z₁ = r√b²+a² lies on the bicsectrix of the angle Q₁OQ₂.

M1069. Some cities only allow paired volumes of squares (if two families exchange squares, they do not participate in other exchanges on the same day). Prove that any complex volume with squares of several families can be completed in two days. (It is assumed that both before and after each exchange, the family lives in a separate square.)

Let's represent each square as a point on the plane, and moving from one square to another as an arrow connecting the corresponding points. It is clear that at any point one arrow begins and one ends. Therefore, if we leave any vertex and move along the arrows, we will always hit new points until we return to the beginning of the path (this is inevitable, since the number of points is finite). Let's do the same starting from other points. We will find that all the arrows form several non-intersecting closed wheels - cycles (Fig. 1). Therefore, it is enough to prove the statement of the problem for the “cyclic exchange” square.

Suppose we need to carry out an exchange A₁→A₂→...→Aₙ→A₁ - to cross the tenants of square A₁ into square A₂, A₂ - into A₃, etc. On the first day we will make paired exchanges A₁↔A₁−1, A₂↔A₁−2, ..., i.e. A₁↔A₁−k (we assume that A₀=A₁); the residents of the square Aₙ and, for even n, Aₙ/2, do not move at all yet. On the second day - exchanges A₁↔Aₙ, A₂↔A₁−1, ..., i.e. A₁↔A₁−k. As a result, the residents of square Aₙ will move to square Aₙ+1−(n−k)=Aₙ+1 for k=1, ..., n−1, and from square Aₙ to Aₙ+1−n=A₁, which is what we need.
