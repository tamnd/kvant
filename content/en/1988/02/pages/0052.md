Math 6-10

The notes published below are united by a common theme: they talk about the sum of the angles of a polygon - convex and arbitrary, flat and “spherical”. Along the way, Euler's theorem on the map on the plane (and on the sphere) and the more famous Euler's theorem on the polygon, which follows from it, are discussed. Although the first note is addressed to students in grades 6-8, the second is deactylassic, and the third is deactylassic, we still recommend reading them back to back. All three notes were prepared by N.V. Vasiliev and V.L. Gutenmakher.

Sum of angles
Sum of triangle angles

One of the first geometric facts we learned was the theorem that in any triangle the sum of the angles is 180°. This fact can be verified experimentally: cut out an arbitrary triangle from paper, tear off two corners from it and attach to the third - together they form a reversed angle of 180°. The proof of theorem (Fig. 1) is based on the same idea - to collect three angles at one vertex: if a straight line is drawn through the vertex of a triangle, parallel to the opposite side - the base, then the two angles formed will be equal to the angles at the base.

About Euclid's "fifth postulate"

In this argument of ours, we used Euclid’s “fifth postulate” - an axiom that states that there is only one straight line parallel to a given point and passing through a given point. Pairs of crosswise angles of parallel lines are equal*), and this implies the theorem on the sum of the angles of a triangle. Of course, from the theorem on the sum of the angles of a triangle, in turn, one can derive the “fifth postulate” - they are equivalent to each other.
