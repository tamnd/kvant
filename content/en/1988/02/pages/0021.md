A work is of (n, k)-size if each line is of (n, k)-size. When reading a poem, strong syllables are emphasized with more or less distinct stress. But let us emphasize once again: one should not confuse, let alone put aside, strong and stressed syllables. A strong syllable does not have to be stressed (the first syllable of the word is from the second line of “Eugene Onegin”), but the stressed syllable does not have to be strong (the word is not in the same line). The rule placed within the framework precisely indicates when a strong syllable must be stressed, that is, when a syllable with a certain number in a line is actually stressed when read correctly.

In more detail, (n, k)-meter is called complex size with the kth strong beat.

Exercise 1. a) Prove that if in a line subject to (n, k)-size, a strong syllable belongs to a word containing no more than n syllables, then this syllable is stressed. b) If there are no words in a line containing more than n syllables, then the condition of subordination of this line to (n, k)-size is equivalent to the fact that all strong syllables are stressed.

Let us note that our terminology is not entirely consistent with that adopted in poetry. In particular, what we call size is more often called meter, and size also includes the number of strong syllables in a line, or, as they say, the number of moans. For example, iambic is meter, and iambic pentameter is meter.
