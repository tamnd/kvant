If the potential in it is equal to zero, then, by determining the work of the electric force when moving a unit charge from this point to any other point, we thereby determine the potential of any point in space.

There is an element of production in the definition of potential. After all, we did not calculate the potential at the initial cutoff point - we simply set it equal to zero. If we set it equal to zero, and some value $\varphi_0$, then the potentials of all other points in space would change by values ​​$\varphi_0$. It turns out that the potential is defined in an ambiguous way - it is determined up to the choice of the potential value at the cutoff point, and we can choose this value as we want - the work of the electric force does not depend on it.

In physics, along with measurable quantities, there are quantities that are very convenient, but not directly measurable. These quantities can be changed (transformed) in such a way that the physical consequences do not change. For example, the coordinate values ​​of a point $x$, $y$, $z$ depend on the choice of the origin and how the coordinate axes are oriented. But the directly measured distance between points does not depend on the choice of the origin of coordinates or on the direction of the coordinate axes. An equally convenient quantity, but depending on the choice of the beginning of the cut-off value, is the electric potential.

So, there are two characteristics of the space surrounding a charged body. They talk about what would happen if we placed a test unit charge at different points in space. One - electrical directionality - speaks of the electrical forces that would act on this charge at various points in space; the other - electric potential - talks about the potential energy that such a charge would have at different points.

Both characteristics are related to each other. The mathematical description of the properties of the electric force leads to some convenient technique - the calculation of the electric force acting on a given charge at a distance, but somehow transforms the space surrounding it. It turns out to be convenient to attribute the action of a given charge on other charges to a special state of space surrounding a given charge—its electric field. Electric potential is a scalar characteristic of this field. Electrical voltage is a vector characteristic.

As we will soon see, the electric field is not just a convenient mathematical device, but a physical reality.
