So far we have only considered a variation of the Ramsey game, which Frank Harari, the famous graph theorist, called the evasion game. As he noted, there are probably at least three other types of play. For example, in cerra available (played on the same graph as the Sim game), the first player to complete the construction of a one-armed triangle wins. The other two games continue until all the lines have been colored, and the winner is the player who has completed either the most or the fewest number of single-arm triangles. In addition to the number $R(3,3)$, the value of which underlies the game Sim, only five non-trivial classical Ramsey numbers for 2-colorings are known:

1. $R(3,4)$ is equal to 9. For any 2-coloring of the graph $K_9$ we will necessarily obtain a red triangle ($K_3$) or a blue tetrahedron ($K_4$). Nobody knows who wins if the game matches Ramsey's game.

2. $R(3,5)$ is equal to 14.

3. $R(4,4)$ is equal to 18. Thus, for any 2-coloring of the graph $K_{18}$ one will necessarily obtain a one-valued tetrahedron ($K_4$). (This is an incomplete Ramsey game, but it is not easy to play due to the difficulty of recognizing tetrahedra.) The indicated property of 2-colorings of the graph $K_{18}$ corresponds to the fact that in any team of 18 people there are either four pairwise acquaintances or four pairwise strangers.

Another classical Ramsey number is known, but for 3-colorings. This is the number $R(3,3,3)$, and it is equal to 17. Thus, any 3-coloring of the graph $K_{17}$ will necessarily result in a one-valued triangle. In fact, you will get several single-arm triangles, but the exact number of them is unknown***.
