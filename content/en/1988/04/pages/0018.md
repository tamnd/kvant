Suppose that we randomly color the lines of the graph $K_n$ red or blue. We can color all the lines red, color all the lines blue, or use both colors. This coloring is called a 2-coloring of a graph. 2-coloring is, of course, a simple way to partition all pairs of our $n$ vertices into two disjoint classes. Similarly, a 3-line coloring partitions these pairs into three disjoint classes, and in general, a $r$-coloring partitions pairs of vertices into $r$ pairwise disjoint classes.

A subderf of a complete graph is any graph contained in the complete graph in the sense that all its vertices and lines belong to this complete graph. It is easy to see that every complete graph is a subgraph of every complete graph with a large number of vertices. Many simple graphs have names. figure 2 shows four families of graphs: chains, cycles, stars, and wheels. Note that a wheel with four vertices is simply another image of the graph $K_4$.

Let us now consider a problem involving six pencils of different colors. We will assign each color to any type of graph we like. For example:

1. red: pentagon (five-point cycle),
2. orange: tetrahedron,

Fig. 2.

3. yellow: seven-pointed star,
4. green: 13-vertex chain,
5. blue: eight-vertex wheel,
6. purple: bow tie (two triangles with one common point).

Let us now ask an interesting question. Are there complete graphs such that any 6-coloring of them will necessarily result in a subgraph of one of the types listed above? In other words, no matter how we color such a complete graph with six pencils, we will certainly get either a red pentagon, or an orange tetrahedron, or a yellow seven-vertex star, etc. Ramsey's theorem states that all complete graphs, starting from a certain finite size, have these properties. The smallest graph from this infinite family is called the Ramsey graph for a given set of subgraphs. The number of its vertices is called the Ramsey number for this set of subgraphs.

Each Ramsey graph has both a game and a puzzle associated with it. The game in our example is as follows. Two players take turns taking any of the six pencils and coloring one line in the Ramsey graph. The first one to finish coloring one of the given subgraphs is considered the loser. Since the graph is a Ramsey graph, the game cannot end in a draw. Moreover, the Ramsey graph is the smallest complete graph in which nothing is returned.

The puzzle concerns a complete graph that has one less vertex than the Ramsey graph. Obviously, this is the largest complete graph on which the game can end in a draw. Such a graph is called a critical Ramsey graph for a given set of subgraphs. The puzzle consists of finding a coloring of the critical graph such that none of the given subgraphs arise. This coloring is called a critical coloring.
