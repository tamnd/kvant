I. Langmore’s student K. Blodgett made a major contribution to the development of methods for transferring such films to solid-state substrates, which is why films on substrates are also called Langmore-Blodgett films or L-B films.

Of course, as a result of working Langmore installations, everything is not so simple. First, it is necessary to strictly control the process of collecting single molecules “scattered” on the surface of water (this state is called a “two-dimensional gas” of molecules). As the film forms, the “packing density” of the molecules changes: first they are packed less tightly (“two-dimensional liquid”), and then more densely (“two-dimensional solid”); the surface tension in the layer changes accordingly. At this stage, do not press too hard with the barer, otherwise the molecules will begin to build up on top of each other in layers. The film is applied in the “solid body” mode.

Secondly, as molecules are transferred to the substrate, holes will form in the molecular layer on the surface of the water. To avoid this, use a barer to suppress the film, constantly reducing its area; this process is monitored by an electronic system. The movement of the substrates is automated, the temperature, the degree of acidity of the medium, etc. are controlled.

Naturally, when creating “building blocks” of electrons, first you need to have L-B films of conductors, semiconductors and dielectrics. Recently, films have also been removed. The general scheme of work is as follows: the desired molecule is selected, for example, a dye, then a hydrophobic tail is chemically “sewn” to it - and now you can apply the film to the substrate using the described method.

Using the Langmore technique, one can realize an idea that is very attractive from an electronic point of view—the idea of ​​molecular architecture. Indeed, we can transfer onto the substrate first a layer of molecules of substance A, then a layer of molecules of substance B, then substance B, etc. However, this requires several Langmore baths filled with different substances, or one multi-section bath. Such installations have already been created. So, we are now able to send various molecules onto each other, strictly controlling the process and not making a mistake in a single layer. This means that you can create molecular electron elements with a size of about 10 nm, filled in on our graph (see Fig. 1) for the year 2000. How to create? Not much on this matter. Well, at least this: let's try to create an element by analogy with the well-known electron tube - a triode. The operation of a triode is familiar to you: electrons from the cathode, carried away by the electric field, fly to the anode, and their flow is regulated by changes in the electrical potential on the grid located in the middle. What would a “film” analogue of a triode look like? Let's do this: prepare an electrode - it will be an analogue of the cathode; then we apply one layer of dielectric molecules on it; on a dielectric - one layer of conducting molecules - this is an analogue of a grid; then - again one layer of dielectric molecules; and finally, on top there is another electrode - the anode. The role of vacuum, which ensures the flow of electrons from the cathode to the anode in the triode, in our “device” is played by thin dielectric layers through which electrons can pass using a tunneling mechanism (the quantum effect of “leakage” of electrons through a dielectric layer 1-10 nm thick). So the triode with a size of only 10-20 nm is ready. So far this triode is only a fantasy, but a whole series of similar MDM (metal-dielectric-metal) and MIS (metal-dielectric-semiconductor) elements using Langmore films have already been created.
