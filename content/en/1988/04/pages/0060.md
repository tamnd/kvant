Thus, the tension surface coefficient (characteristics in this case of one of the surfaces of the soap film) receives another interpretation - this is the force acting on a single linear element of the surface boundary and perpendicular to this element:

$$\sigma = \frac{F_{\text{H}}}{\Delta l}$$

$$T = 2 \frac{F_{\text{H}}}{\Delta \varphi} = \sigma \frac{l}{\pi}$$

We dwelled on this problem in detail because it shows where the distortion of surface tension forces comes from. It turns out that for carrying out quantitative calculations it is convenient to imagine that the presence of an energy surface is equivalent to that of the liquid surface being in a stretched state. In this case, it is assumed that the forces stretching the film are applied to its linear boundary. If this boundary is drawn mentally, for the film into two parts, then the parts will interact with each other with forces perpendicular to the boundary. It would seem that all this is very similar to stretching a rubber film. And yet, there is a fundamental difference. It consists in the fact that as the surface of the liquid film increases, the surface forces remain constant, while as the stretch of the rubber film increases, the tension in it increases. So, there really is an analogy, but you need to use it carefully. However, when solving problems, the “power language” often turns out to be more familiar, and it is also worth mastering. Left to itself, the system tends to a state with a minimum surface energy, while a film with a large surface tension coefficient will tend to contract, while stretching the other film and moving the film. The force acting on the right side of the liquid will be equal to $F_2=2\sigma_2l$, and on the left side - $F_1=2\sigma_1l$ (we taught that films have two surfaces). In order for the bar to be in balance, a force must be applied to it

$$F=F_2-F_1=2(\sigma_2-\sigma_1)l$$
