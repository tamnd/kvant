Inequalities and graphs

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. A. PETROV

In each unit of practice in the written exam in mathematics, you are required to solve an inequality. This task has the highest number of incorrect answers. Errors occur due to odd or incomplete use of the properties of the corresponding functions.

A universal “crib” by which you can read all the properties of a function is its graph. Therefore, when solving more or less complex inequalities, it is useful to resort to the consistent use of graphs of elemental functions. This technique allows you to automatically, without straining your memory, transfer to equivalent inequalities; at the same time, it automatically takes into account (it is most often forgotten to take into account) the range of permissible values. In addition, geometric illustration, as always, contributes to a more conscious understanding of the essence of the transformations used. Let's look at specific examples.

Example 1. Solve inequality
$$2^{\sqrt{\log \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} tg x}-1}<1.$$ (1)

Solution. Let us denote the exponent by $t$. Then inequality (1) takes the form
$$2^t<1.$$ (1')

Let us depict the graph of the function $y=2^t$ (Fig. 1). Let us mark on the axis $Oy$ the values ​​that interest us. They fill the beam (- $\infty$; 1). Let's project this ray onto the graph, and then project the separated part of the graph onto the $Ot$ axis. Thus, we have found a solution to the auxiliary inequality (1'): $t>0$. Therefore, inequality (1) is equivalent to inequality
$$\sqrt{\log \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} tg x}<1.$$ (2)
