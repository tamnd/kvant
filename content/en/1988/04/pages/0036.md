F1089. When flying from the Mir station to the Salyut-7 station, which were in the same orbit, our cosmonauts slowed down their ship, moved to a lower orbit, and at a time t=30 hours ago, Salyut-7, which was flying ahead of Mir at a distance of L=3000 km. Considering the orbits to be loupe, determine how many kilometers the projected orbit is below the main one. The height of both orbits is much less than the radius of the earth R₃=6400 km.

Let us write down the difference in the angular velocities of the spacecraft in the intermediate orbit and the station:

$$\omega_1 R_1 t - \omega_2 R_2 t = (\omega_1 R_1 - \omega_2 R_2) t = R_3 t (\omega_1 - \omega_2) \approx L \Rightarrow (\omega_1 - \omega_2) = L/R_3 t$$

(we taught that the heights of the orbits are compared to the radius of the earth, i.e. $R_1 \approx R_2 \approx R_3$). At the same time, taking into account that $\omega = \frac{v}{R} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R^3}} = \sqrt{\frac{g R_3^2}{R^3}}$ (M is the mass of the earth), we can write:

$$\omega_1 - \omega_2 = \sqrt{g R_3^2} \left( \sqrt{\frac{1}{R_1^3}} - \sqrt{\frac{1}{R_2^3}} \right) =$$

$$= \sqrt{g R_3^2} \left( \sqrt{\frac{1}{(R_2 - \Delta R)^3}} - \sqrt{\frac{1}{R_2^3}} \right) \approx$$

$$\approx \sqrt{\frac{g}{R_3}} \left( \sqrt{\frac{1}{(1 - \Delta R/R_3)^3}} - 1 \right) \approx \sqrt{\frac{g}{R_3}} \frac{1/(1 - \Delta R/R_3)^3 - 1}{\sqrt{1/(1 - \Delta R/R_3)^3} + 1} \approx \sqrt{\frac{g}{R_3}} \frac{3 \Delta R/R_3}{2}$$

(2)

Equating expressions (1) and (2), we find $\Delta R$:

$$\Delta R = \frac{2 L}{3 t} \sqrt{\frac{R_3}{g}} \approx 15 \text{ км.}$$

M. V. Semenov

F1090. Look at the sky on clear frosty night. Which stars twinkle more noticeably: those located high above the horizon or low, and why?

The twinkling of stars is caused by turbulent air flows in the earth's atmosphere, which arise due to uneven cooling of the atmosphere. Basically, these are upward currents that arise due to the difference in temperature of the earth's surface and the adjacent layers of air. These flows lead to the fact that the density of the atmosphere in a given place is constantly changing. Accordingly, the refractive index changes, and therefore the rays of light coming from the stars are refracted in one direction or the other - the stars twinkle. This effect is especially noticeable in close lands; therefore, “low” stars, the rays of which, on the way to the observed one, travel a greater distance to nearby earth than the rays of “high” stars, flicker more noticeably.

A. S. Butov
