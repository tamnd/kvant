The characteristic radius of interaction between liquid molecules is approximately equal to the average distance between them, so that each molecule actually interacts only with its closest neighbors. Molecules near the free surface of a liquid are in a special position compared to bulk molecules, since they interact with molecules of both the liquid and its vapor (and this interaction is much weaker). We can say that the molecules of the surface layer account for a different share of the potential energy of the system than the bulk molecules.

It is clear that when changes occur in the shape of a liquid at a given volume and a fixed temperature, the internal energy corresponding to bulk molecules does not change, and the contribution of surface energy will be greater, the larger the free surface area of ​​the liquid.

The liquid surface coefficient $\sigma$ is called the relative work $\Delta A$, which must be done to increase the surface area of ​​the liquid by values ​​$\Delta S$, to values ​​$\Delta S$, i.e.

$$\sigma = \frac{\Delta A}{\Delta S},$$

or we can say that the coefficient of liquid surface area per unit surface area.

Objects in whose behavior the role of the liquid surface is most clearly manifested are soap films.

Consider the following situation. A loop of thread of length $l$ (Fig. 1, a) floats on the soap film. The part of the film inside the thread is carefully pierced. What shape does the thread form? What is the tension of the thread at the equilibrium position if the surface coefficient of liquid tension of the soap distance is $\sigma$?

Obviously, after piercing, the remaining film will behave in such a way as to minimize the size of its surface. This will lead to the fact that for a given thread length ($l$), the hole forms a figure of maximum area. As you know, such a figure will be a circle (Fig. 1, b). The radius of the circle will be found from the correspondence

$$2\pi r = l, \quad t. e. \quad r = \frac{l}{2\pi}.$$

To determine the tension of the thread, consider a small element of its length $\Delta l = r\Delta \varphi$ (Fig. 2). It is under the influence of two tension forces $T_1$ and $\vec{T}_2$ ($T_1 = T_2 = T$), acting from the sections of the thread adjacent to the element and directed tangentially, and the force $2\vec{F}_h$ of the liquid surface of the soap film ($\vec{F}_h$ is the force from one surface of the film). From the equilibrium condition of the thread element we obtain (the projection of the force in the direction towards the center of the circle):

$$2T \frac{\Delta \varphi}{2} - 2F_h = 0$$
