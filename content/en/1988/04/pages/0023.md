INTERVIEW WITH PROFESSOR RONALD GRAHAM

— Professor Graham, when and how did you first become interested in mathematics? What influenced your choice: family, teacher, books, modern?

— I first felt a strong interest in mathematics in elementary school, in the third or fourth grade. I especially remember how, having become familiar with the algorithm for extracting square roots - you know that old interctional algorithm - I became interested in whether it was possible to do the same for cube roots and for higher powers. And I found a similar solution. I was supported by a teacher who was kind to my hobbies.

Later, when I was in junior high school, two other teachers helped me. Seeing that I could solve any problem in algebra, the teacher once told me: “Here is a problem that you cannot solve.”
In the problem I decided on the growth rate of the mouse population, depending on their size, i.e. I would have to deal with a differential equation. Well, the teacher turned out to be right - I couldn’t solve the problem. Then he gave me an analysis textbook and said: “If you want to know the solution, read this book.” I did just that. Then mathematics captured me.

Before that, I was fascinated by stars and was planning to become an astronomer. Only later did I learn that stargazing is not the whole of astronomy. But this science still interests me. In addition to a diploma in mathematics, I have another one - in physics.

There was little they could do to help me at home. The fact is that in our family I was the first to graduate from college.

— How did you become a research mathematician?

“As far as I remember, in high school and during my junior year at university I didn’t set such a goal for myself—I was just interested in doing mathematics.” Then, in 1950, I began training under a special program that gave me the right to enter the university even before graduating from high school. To participate in this program, you must pass certain test exams. In mathematics, I was the best in the United States.
