First, let's figure out how molecules are located on the surface of water. Films using the Langmuir method can be obtained from so-called amphyllic organic molecules, i.e. molecules containing hydrophilic (“water-loving”) and hydrophilic (“water-unloving”) parts. These are precisely the molecules of fatty acids (oils). figure 2 shows the location of the stearic acid molecule on the surface of water. The hydrophilic “head” of the molecule is immersed in water, and the hydrophilic “tail” is located in the air and prevents the ULE molecules from going into the water.

To obtain a monolayer of such molecules on the surface of water, proceed as follows. The oil is dissolved in a volatile organic solvent, such as acetone, so that the relative concentration of oil molecules is low (about 0.01%). A drop of such a solution is placed on the surface of the water in a special installation called a Langmuir bath (Fig. 3). The drop quickly “scatters” over the surface of the water, the acetone evaporates, and single amphyllic oil molecules remain scattered on the surface of the water.

These molecules must be collected into a single oil layer. To do this, a barer is moved along the surface of the water in the Langmuir bath (see Fig. 3), with the help of which all the molecules move to one edge of the bath and are pressed so that a film is formed on the surface of the water, the molecules in which are located in one layer (monomolecular film). Now this film must be transferred to a specially treated substrate (silicon or glass plate). They do it like this. The substrate, located in the bath (Fig. 3), begins to be lifted very slowly, at a speed of ~1 cm/h. The substrate is well wetted by water, and the hydrophilic “heads” of the molecules, not wanting to part with the water, “stretch” behind it (Fig. 4, a) and ultimately end up “sticking” to the substrate. So exactly one layer of molecules grows on the substrate, oriented in a certain way - the “head” on the substrate, the “tail” outward. Let's start lowering the substrate back into the bath. Now the substrate is not wetted with water (Fig. 4, b), behind it the film of oil disappears downwards - a second monomolecular layer is formed, “linked” to the first “tails”. The hydrophilic “heads” of the molecules stick out into the bathtub, and if we try to lift the substrate again, the water, which again wets it well, will “reach out” for it, and the “heads” of the molecules follow the water. So the re-raised substrate will be “dressed” in three monomolecular layers oriented in a certain way (Fig. 4, c). We can get as many of these words as we want, and we will always know exactly how many molecular layers are located on the substrate.
