Another application of Langmuir films is molecularly ordered lubrication. When we drop oil into a bearing, the space between the stewing parts is filled with hundreds and thousands of layers of oil molecules, which reduce the trene - the dry trene is replaced by liquid. However, such a lubricant is not suitable for modern high-precision devices. For example, when reading information from magnetic recording disks used in modern computers, the following scheme is used: a reading head moves above a fast rotating disk. The distance between the head and the disk surface should not exceed 20-30 nm. How can we reduce trene here? Specialists from the Japanese company Sony covered a magnetic disk with a single-layer Langmuir film and showed that such a “lubricant” makes it possible to increase the wear resistance of the head by almost 100 times and long-term preservation of recordings on the disk.

But still, the main thing that excites researchers is the creation of fundamentally new electronic elements based on Langmuir technology. It is very promising here to introduce large organic molecules into Langmuir films, which are already “able” to perform any complex operations. Nature itself has developed and created thousands of such small “windows” for nss - these are protein molecules. For example, when a rhodopsin protein molecule absorbs a light quantum, it changes its state—a proton moves, which can be detected. If you incorporate rhodopsin molecules into a film using Langmuir technology, you will get a very sensitive light sensor. Molecular sensors have already been created that allow recording changing concentrations of gases; the creation of an “artificial nose”, which is a “mozanka” of molecules sensitive to various gases, is always discussed.

The creation of molecular electronics elements requires solving a number of problems. For example, how to raise wiring to such an element (input-output devices)? How can we observe what we have achieved as a result of molecular construction? Indeed, the wavelength of visible light is ~0.5 µm, and the size of the elements is about 10 nm, i.e. 50 times smaller. This means that it is impossible to see a single element even with the best optical microscope.

To control test samples made using Langmuir technology, more complex observation methods are used - for example, RENTenostructural analysis. The RENTen study with a wavelength of $\lambda=0,154$ nm is commonly used. The radiation is scattered by molecular elements, creating a diffraction pattern containing alternating maxima and minima, in which the image is protected. From the position of the maxima in the scattering angle (2θ), at the first stage of processing the results using the Wulff-Bragg formula (2d sin $\theta=n\lambda$), the thickness of molecular films (d) and the distances between neighboring molecules are calculated, and then, using computer calculations, it is possible to clarify the location of smaller details in the molecular elements - groups of atoms and even individual atoms.

So, Langmore's technique allows, in principle, to use molecules as building materials for electronic circuits. One can hope that molecular electronics elements created using this method will be put into practical use in 5–10 years.
