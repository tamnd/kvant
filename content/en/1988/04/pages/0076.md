9. The largest number $a, b, c, d$ must be less than the sum of the other three, and not all numbers $a, b, c, d$ must be the same. Proof. Let, for definition, $a \geq b \geq c \geq d$. Our conditions take the form $a < b + c + d, a > d$. If the first condition is not satisfied, then there is no closed broken line at all on the side $a, b, c, d$; if all four numbers are the same, then the broken line will be a rhombus and will not have a self-intersection. It remains to show that if our conditions are satisfied, then a closed self-intersecting broken line on the side $a, b, c, d$ exists. Here is its construction (Fig. 4). The first of our inequalities shows that $b + c > a - d$. Let's take a segment $AB$ of length $a$ and from its end $A$ draw a segment $AD$ of length $d$. Let us further draw the segment $DE \perp DB$, whose point $E$ lies on the segment $AB$. The angle $BAD$ can be made so small that the sum of $DE + EB$, which is close to $a - d$, is less than $b + c$. Moreover, it will be $DB < b + c$. This means that on $DB$ we can construct a triangle $DCB$ with lateral sides $b$ and $c$; we will construct it as shown in figure 4. The result will be a closed broken line $ABCD$ with lower side lengths, and it is easy to understand that it has self-intersection: since $b > c$, then the angle $CDB$ means, in the absence of self-intersection, the point $C$ should fall into the triangle $DEB$, which would be acceptable to the impossible inequality $b + c = DC + CB \leq DE + EB < b + c$.
