What can we say about $R$ (3, 3, 3) - the number of vertices of the minimal complete graph such that any 4-coloring of which produces a single-label triangle? This number is unknown, although the highest estimate - 64 - was found for it by Jon Folkman, a brilliant combinator who died in 1964 at the age of 31. The best lower estimate, 51, comes from a thesis by Fan Chung, a young Chinese woman at Bell Labs.

There are many fascinating generalizations of Ramsey's classical theory. We have already looked at the most obvious; finding what are called generalized Ramsey numbers for $r$-colorings of complete graphs such that given subgraphs distinct from complete x necessarily arise. The pioneers in this territory were Vaclav Chvatal and Harari; For the last five years, Barr has been excavating on it. Consider, for example, the problem of finding the numbers of vertices of minimal complete graphs in which a mono-named star with $n$ vertices appears. For 2-colorings, Harari was the first to solve this problem. In 1973, Barr and Robert solved it for an arbitrary number of colors.

Another generalized Ramsey problem is to find Ramsey numbers for 2-colorings of complete graphs such that a certain number of “broken” single-label triangles appear. (Triangles are said to be disjointed if they do not share any vertices.) In 1975, Barr, Erdős and Spencer proved that if the disjoint of a triangle is $d>2$, then the corresponding Ramsey number is 5d. For colorings with more than two colors, the problem remains unsolved.

For wheels, the problem is not solved in the general case, even for two colors. the number of vertices for a wheel with four vertices, i.e. for a tetrahedron, is, as we have seen, 18. Wheel

It would be very interesting to get such a coloring at least for $K_{17}$.

In 1968, Ronald Graham, one of America's leading combinators**, found an ingenious solution to a Ramey-type problem posed by Erdős and Hajnal. What is the smallest graph not containing $K_6$ such that any 2-coloring of which produces a one-label triangle? Graham found a unique solution, which is the reconstructed graph shown in figure 3.
