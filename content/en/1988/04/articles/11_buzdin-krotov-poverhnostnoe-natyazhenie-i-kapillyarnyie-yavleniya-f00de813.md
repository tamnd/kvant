Surface tension and capillary phenomena

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. I. BUZDIN,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. S. KROTOV

Last year, university entrance exams in physics were conducted according to a new program, which included a number of questions that were not previously available. Thus, a new topic has appeared: “Properties of the surface of liquids. Surface tension. Wetting. Capillary phenomena" (we have given the verbatim wording), which makes it possible for the examination committee to propose tasks on this range of issues.

Since this type is practically absent from textbooks for university preparation in recent years, and the topic contains a large number of subtleties, when writing this article we sought to forestall the possibility of unexpected exam situations. This is partly why we will pay more attention to the discussion of individual theoretical issues, and not just deal with solving standard problems.

Surface tension

To begin with, it is worth saying a few words about the specification of surface tension forces acting at the boundary of a liquid and its vapor.

As you know, liquid molecules are so close to each other that the interaction between them is not taken into account. Therefore, when estimating the internal energy of a liquid-vapor system, not only kinetic energy plays a significant role

⟦folio 57⟧

the movement of molecules, but also the potential energy of their interaction.

The characteristic radius of interaction between liquid molecules is approximately equal to the average distance between them, so that each molecule actually interacts only with its closest neighbors. Molecules near the free surface of a liquid are in a special position compared to bulk molecules, since they interact with molecules of both the liquid and its vapor (and this interaction is much weaker). We can say that the molecules of the surface layer account for a different share of the potential energy of the system than the bulk molecules.

It is clear that when changes occur in the shape of a liquid at a given volume and a fixed temperature, the internal energy corresponding to bulk molecules does not change, and the contribution of surface energy will be greater, the larger the free surface area of ​​the liquid.

The liquid surface coefficient $\sigma$ is called the relative work $\Delta A$, which must be done to increase the surface area of ​​the liquid by values ​​$\Delta S$, to values ​​$\Delta S$, i.e.

$$\sigma = \frac{\Delta A}{\Delta S},$$

or we can say that the coefficient of liquid surface area per unit surface area.

Objects in whose behavior the role of the liquid surface is most clearly manifested are soap films.

Consider the following situation. A loop of thread of length $l$ (Fig. 1, a) floats on the soap film. The part of the film inside the thread is carefully pierced. What shape does the thread form? What is the tension of the thread at the equilibrium position if the surface coefficient of liquid tension of the soap distance is $\sigma$?

Obviously, after piercing, the remaining film will behave in such a way as to minimize the size of its surface. This will lead to the fact that for a given thread length ($l$), the hole forms a figure of maximum area. As you know, such a figure will be a circle (Fig. 1, b). The radius of the circle will be found from the correspondence

$$2\pi r = l, \quad t. e. \quad r = \frac{l}{2\pi}.$$

To determine the tension of the thread, consider a small element of its length $\Delta l = r\Delta \varphi$ (Fig. 2). It is under the influence of two tension forces $T_1$ and $\vec{T}_2$ ($T_1 = T_2 = T$), acting from the sections of the thread adjacent to the element and directed tangentially, and the force $2\vec{F}_h$ of the liquid surface of the soap film ($\vec{F}_h$ is the force from one surface of the film). From the equilibrium condition of the thread element we obtain (the projection of the force in the direction towards the center of the circle):

$$2T \frac{\Delta \varphi}{2} - 2F_h = 0$$

(we learned the small element and put sin $\varphi = \varphi$). To find the force $F_h$, let’s give the film (mentally) the opportunity to contract further, so that the element $\Delta l$ moves by amounts $\Delta s$. The energy of the film will decrease by $\Delta E = 2\sigma \Delta l \cdot \Delta s$ due to the work of force

film tension $\Delta A=2F_{\text{H}}\Delta s$. Where do $\Delta E=\Delta A$, and $F_{\text{H}}=\sigma \Delta l$ come from.

Thus, the tension surface coefficient (characteristics in this case of one of the surfaces of the soap film) receives another interpretation - this is the force acting on a single linear element of the surface boundary and perpendicular to this element:

$$\sigma = \frac{F_{\text{H}}}{\Delta l}$$

Since $T \Delta \varphi=2F_{\text{H}}$, the rope tension will be equal to

$$T = 2 \frac{F_{\text{H}}}{\Delta \varphi} = \sigma \frac{l}{\pi}$$

We dwelled on this problem in detail because it shows where the distortion of surface tension forces comes from. It turns out that for carrying out quantitative calculations it is convenient to imagine that the presence of an energy surface is equivalent to that of the liquid surface being in a stretched state. In this case, it is assumed that the forces stretching the film are applied to its linear boundary. If this boundary is drawn mentally, for the film into two parts, then the parts will interact with each other with forces perpendicular to the boundary. It would seem that all this is very similar to stretching a rubber film. And yet, there is a fundamental difference. It consists in the fact that as the surface of the liquid film increases, the surface forces remain constant, while as the stretch of the rubber film increases, the tension in it increases. So, there really is an analogy, but you need to use it carefully. However, when solving problems, the “power language” often turns out to be more familiar, and it is also worth mastering. Let us also consider the tasks in this regard.

Problem 1. Films of two liquids are separated by a movable bar

Fig. 3.

length $l$ (Fig. 3). The surface tension coefficient of one liquid is $\sigma_1$, the other is $\sigma_2$. How much force must be exerted on the bar so that it is at rest?

Let, to define $\sigma_2>\sigma_1$. Left to itself, the system tends to a state with a minimum surface energy, while a film with a large surface tension coefficient will tend to contract, while stretching the other film and moving the film. The force acting on the right side of the liquid will be equal to $F_2=2\sigma_2l$, and on the left side - $F_1=2\sigma_1l$ (we taught that films have two surfaces). In order for the bar to be in balance, a force must be applied to it

$$F=F_2-F_1=2(\sigma_2-\sigma_1)l$$

Problem 2. The outer radius of a soap bubble is $R$, the thickness of its wall is $h$. What is the air pressure of the bubble? What is the pressure in the thickness of the soap film? Assume that the film is thin ($h \ll R$). Bladder air pressure $p_0$. The surface tension coefficient of the soap bar is $\sigma$.

The well-known spherical shape of soap bubbles is due to the action of surface tension forces. Indeed, if the potential energy of a soap film in a gravity field can be transformed in comparison with its surface energy (this is the case for thin films), then the latter will be responsible for the observed geometry. But with a constant volume of air containing the inner bubble, the figure with the smallest surface area will be a ball. Consequently, in the equilibrium state the film will have the shape of a spherical shell.

Divide (mentally) a spherical shell into two equal halves and distance one of them (Fig. 4). Let us write down the equilibrium conditions for the outer and inner hemispherical surfaces. The external one (radius $R$) is acted upon by external pressure forces $F_1=p_0\pi R^2$ (show this yourself), directed downward, force

surface tension $F_2 = \sigma \cdot 2\pi R$, directed downward, and counteraction to them, directed upward from the pressure of the rolled liquid (curved liquid film) $F_3 = p_2\pi R^2$.
Film equilibrium means that
$$F_1 + F_2 - F_3 = 0,$$
i.e.
$$p_0\pi R^2 + 2\pi R\sigma = p_2\pi R^2,$$
from where we obtain the desired pressure in the thickness of the film:
$$p_2 = p_0 + \frac{2\sigma}{R}.$$
The pressure force of the liquid film directed downward and equal to $F_4 = p_2\pi(R - h)^2$, the surface tension pressure $F_5 = \sigma \cdot 2\pi(R - h)$ directed downward, and the air pressure pressure inside the bullet $F_6 = p_1\pi(R - h)^2$ directed upward act on the inner surface. The equilibrium condition for the inner surface gives that
$$F_4 + F_5 - F_6 = 0,$$
where does the air inside the bullet come from:
$$p_1 = p_2 + \frac{2\sigma}{R - h},$$
or
$$p_1 = p_0 + 2\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{R - h} \right).$$

Capillary phenomena

Let us now consider how the surface of the liquid will behave near the walls of the vessel in which it is poured.
Obviously, taking into account the walls of the vessel, two more terms will be added to the general expression for the energy of the liquid - vapor - vessel wall system. This is the energy of interaction of liquid molecules with molecules of the walls (at a given temperature, the greater the number of surfaces in contact, the greater the

flowing liquid and vessel) and the energy of interaction between vapor molecules and walls. The equilibrium position of the surface of the liquid and its vapor near the walls of the vessel will be established such that the total potential energy of the system takes on a minimum value.
In most cases, this leads to the fact that the surface of the liquid near the walls is curved.

Thus, if the interaction between liquid molecules and the walls of the vessel turns out to be the broadest in comparison with other molecular interactions—the liquid wets the vessel—the interface between the liquid and vapor near the walls “creeps” upward, and a certain angle $\theta$ with the vertical (Fig. 5) is formed. The angle $\theta$ is called the contact angle. At a fixed temperature it will always be the same for a given liquid and wall materials. The stronger the interaction of liquid molecules with molecules of the walls of the vessel, compared with the interaction of liquid molecules, the smaller the angle $\theta$. In the limit, when $\theta \rightarrow 0$, they say that the liquid completely wets the materials of the walls of the vessel. On the contrary, when the attraction of liquid molecules to each other transforms over the attraction of liquid molecules and the walls of the vessel, we have the case of a non-displacing liquid. In this case, the angle $\theta$ is obtuse. In the limit, when $\theta \rightarrow \pi$, we speak of complete non-displacement, and the liquid boundary near the walls of the vessel behaves as if the liquid were repelling from the vessel.

Let us illustrate this with the following problem.

Problem 3. In a thin horizontal cylindrical tube there is a drop: a) wetting; b) non-displacing liquid. What will happen to the drop if

Since the coefficient of the application surface depends on temperature (decreases with its increase), when one end of the tube, for example the left one, is heated, the forces of the application surface on the right will begin to dominate, and the drop will creep - to the right if the liquid wets, and to the left if it does not wet the tube material.

Let us now consider a standard exam task from previous years.

Problem 4. What is the height of the liquid rising between two vertical parallel glass plates, the distance between them d (Fig. 6)? Surface coefficient σ, liquid density ρ, complete wetting.

Let the width of the plate be L, then along the perimeter of the upper boundary of the liquid along the glass plates there is a slur hoot pressure $F_H=2\sigma L$ (coefficient “2” appears due to the fact that there are two plates), directed vertically upward, since the wetting is complete. This slurhoot is the liquid between the plates, which means it is equal to the slurry of this liquid:

$$F_H=\rho g L d$$

(Ldh is the volume of liquid rising between the plates). Using the previously obtained expression for $F_H$, we finally find the height of the liquid rise:

$$\Delta p=\rho g h,$$

or, substituting the found expression for h,

$$\Delta p=\frac{2\sigma}{d}.$$

Now it is not difficult to solve the last problem.
