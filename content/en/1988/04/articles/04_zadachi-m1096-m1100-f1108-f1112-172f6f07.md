M1078. The function $f$ defined on the set $N_0$ of all non-negative integers and takes a value in this set. Prove that the equality $f(f(n))=n+1987$ cannot hold for all $n \in N_0$.

Let us assume that the statement of the problem is false and the function $f$ satisfying $f(f(n))=n+1987$ for $n \in N_0$ exists. Then
$$f(n+1987)=f(f(f(n)))=f(n)+1987,$$
and therefore
$$f(n+1987k)=f(n)+1987k$$
(*)
with all $n, k \in N_0$. Now consider an arbitrary integer $r$, $0 \leq r \leq 1986$, and divide the number $f(r)$ by 1987 with a remainder:
$$f(r)=1987p+q, 0 \leq q \leq 1986.$$
By conditions $f(f(r))=r+1987$, and by (*) $f(f(r))=f(q)+1987p$. Since $r \leq 1986$, only two cases are possible:
1) $p=0$, i.e. $f(r)=q$, and $f(q)=r+1987$;
2) $p=1$, i.e. $f(r)=q+1987$, and $f(q)=f(f(r))-1987=r$.
In both cases, obviously, $f(r) \neq f(q)$, and therefore $r \neq q$. Thus, the set $0, 1, ..., 1986$ can be divided into pairs $(a, b)$ so that in each pair there are $f(a)=b, f(b)=a+1987$. But this set has an odd number of elements. The resulting contradiction shows that a function with the required properties does not exist.

F1088. The weightless leaf is fixed in a weightless suspension at a speed of f = 10 rpm so that it can freely swing relative to the upper step (Fig. 1). A monkey with mass m = 30 kg begins to climb down the leaf. At what distance l from the suspension will it be when the vertical position of the leaf ceases to be stable?

Let the leaf deviate from the vertical by a small angle a. At this moment, the monkey’s movement will be considered as a movement in a circle with radius ~lα (Fig. 2). Centripetal acceleration of a monkey -

$$a = (2\pi f)^2 l\alpha.$$

This acceleration can be imparted to her by the reaction force T from the side of the leaf - this is the only force that has a projection F on the horizontal plane. If F > ma, then the monkey (with the leaf) will return to the vertical - the vertical position of the leaf will be stable.

From the considered 丝 (Fig. 2) it is clear that F = mga. From the condition

$$mg\alpha > m(2\pi f)^2 l\alpha$$

we find:

$$l < g/(2\pi f)^2 = 10 \text{ m}.$$

When l is greater than 10 m, the equilibrium is unstable.

F1090. Look at the sky on clear frosty night. Which stars twinkle more noticeably: those located high above the horizon or low, and why?

The twinkling of stars is caused by turbulent air flows in the earth's atmosphere, which arise due to uneven cooling of the atmosphere. Basically, these are upward currents that arise due to the difference in temperature of the earth's surface and the adjacent layers of air. These flows lead to the fact that the density of the atmosphere in a given place is constantly changing. Accordingly, the refractive index changes, and therefore the rays of light coming from the stars are refracted in one direction or the other - the stars twinkle. This effect is especially noticeable in close lands; therefore, “low” stars, the rays of which, on the way to the observed one, travel a greater distance to nearby earth than the rays of “high” stars, flicker more noticeably.

A. S. Butov

F1091. A ball with mass m and charge q suspended on an inextensible thread of length l is located in the center of the hoop, along which charge Q (Fig. 1) is uniformly distributed. Hoop radius R; the charges q and Q are single-valued. Define

Let the ball move a small distance $\Delta x$ ($\Delta x \ll R$) from the center of the hoop (the vertical displacement is proportional to $\Delta x^2$, and we will neglect it, assuming that the movement is proportional in the plane of the hoop). Let's see what force $F_3$ acts on the charge q of the ball from the side of the hoop charge Q. From the symmetry messages (see Fig. 2) it is clear that the direction of the field created by the charge Q at point A is directed along the radius of the hoop. The force $F_3$ is also directed.

To find $F_3$, let's calculate the force $f_3$ acting on the charge $q$ from the side of the small element of the hoop located at a distance $r$ from the charge $q$ (see Fig. 2). The charge of this element is

$$\Delta Q = \frac{Q}{2\pi R} R \cdot \Delta \gamma = \frac{Q}{2\pi} \Delta \gamma \approx \frac{Q}{2\pi} \Delta \varphi$$

(φ≈γ, since Δx≈R). Let us express $r$ in terms of $R$ and Δx (using the cosine theorems):

$$r^2 = R + (\Delta x)^2 - 2R \cdot \Delta x \cdot \cos \gamma \approx R^2 + (\Delta x)^2 - 2R \cdot \Delta x \cdot \cos \varphi \approx R^2 - 2R \cdot \Delta x \cdot \cos \varphi.$$

Thus,

$$f_3 = -k \frac{\Delta Q \cdot q}{r^2} \approx -k \frac{Qq}{2\pi} \frac{1}{R^2 - 2R \cdot \Delta x \cdot \cos \varphi} \Delta \varphi.$$

The total force $F_3$ is equal to $\int_0^{2\pi} f_3 \cos \varphi d\varphi$, i.e.

$$F_3 = -k \frac{Qq}{2\pi R^2} \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\cos \varphi}{R^2 - 2R \cdot \Delta x \cdot \cos \varphi} d\varphi \approx -k \frac{Qq}{2\pi R^2} \left(1 - \frac{2\Delta x}{R} \cos \varphi\right)^{-1} \cos \varphi d\varphi \approx -k \frac{Qq}{2\pi R^2} \left(1 + \frac{2\Delta x}{R} \cos \varphi\right) \cos \varphi d\varphi = -k \frac{2Qq \cdot \Delta x}{2\pi R^3} \cos^2 \varphi d\varphi = -k \frac{Qq \cdot \Delta x \cdot \pi}{2\pi R^3} = -k \frac{Qq}{R^3} \Delta x.$$

Now let's write down the equation of motion of the ball:

$$m(\Delta x)'' = -\left(m g \sin \alpha + k \frac{Qq \cdot \Delta x}{R^3}\right),$$

where α is the angle of deviation of the bottom of the pendulum from the vertical. Since we are interested in small vibrations, sin α≈Δx/l, and

$$m(\Delta x)'' = -\Delta x \left(m \frac{g}{l} + k \frac{Qq}{R^3}\right).$$

This is the equation of harmonic oscillations with frequency

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{l}} + k \frac{Qq}{R^3 m}.$$
