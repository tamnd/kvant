TEN DIGITS

Candidate
physical and mathematical sciences
A. P. SAVIN

Literacy begins with the ability to write and count. Already at 3-4 years old, climbing the stairs, we confidently count the steps: “One, two, three, four, heels, ....” And in first grade we write numbers in a notebook:

1234567890

These numbers are called *Arabic*, although the Arabs only transmitted the Indian tenth number system with its numbers to Europe. We read about this in the “Book of Abacus” by the illustrative mathematician Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci): “The nine Hindu signs are as follows: 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1. Using these signs and the sign 0, which is called “sifr” in Arabic, you can write any number you want.” Fibonacci, who published his book in 1202, learned a lot from his acquaintance with the mathematical works of the Arabs. It is curious that the very order of the numbers when listing them: 9, 8, 7, ... reflects their borrowing from the Arabs, since the Arabs write from right to left, and not from left to right.

You probably already realized that the word “digit” comes from the Arab name for zero, and in Russia for a very long time the word “digit” has been familiar with the zero sign. Look at a piece of text from Magnitsky’s “Arithmetic” (1703).

This text is translated into modern Russian as follows: “Numbering is a counting or a way of representing all perfect numbers using ten signs, which are depicted like this: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 0. Of these, nine are significant, and the last 0 (which is called zero, or nothing) in itself means nothing when standing alone.”

Although in the 16th century there was already a highly developed printing industry in Europe, the numbers in the books of that time, as we see, are very similar to handwritten ones. Many artists worked on creating a variety of typographic figures - on the shape of letters and numbers - trying to give them the appearance accepted by the eye (each character had to be quite different from the other). But the history of numbers does not end there. Quite recently, a number of pages used this entry:

123456789

How are these numbers calculated better? And the fact that the tails of four numbers go up, and the tails of odd numbers go down. Now it is more difficult to confuse, say, the numbers 2 and 5. True, this innovation has not been widely adopted, but this is the intention of the numbers, and each of you is familiar:

0123456789

We see such numbers on microcalculators and hand-held electronic watches. Using a set of seven segments, it is possible to depict each of the ten numbers in a fairly “recognizable” way.

We see another image of numbers related to the needs of technology on the back of each postal envelope:
