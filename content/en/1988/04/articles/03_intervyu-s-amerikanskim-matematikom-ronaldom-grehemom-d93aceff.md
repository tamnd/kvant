INTERVIEW WITH PROFESSOR RONALD GRAHAM

— Professor Graham, when and how did you first become interested in mathematics? What influenced your choice: family, teacher, books, modern?

— I first felt a strong interest in mathematics in elementary school, in the third or fourth grade. I especially remember how, having become familiar with the algorithm for extracting square roots - you know that old interctional algorithm - I became interested in whether it was possible to do the same for cube roots and for higher powers. And I found a similar solution. I was supported by a teacher who was kind to my hobbies.

Later, when I was in junior high school, two other teachers helped me. Seeing that I could solve any problem in algebra, the teacher once told me: “Here is a problem that you cannot solve.”
In the problem I decided on the growth rate of the mouse population, depending on their size, i.e. I would have to deal with a differential equation. Well, the teacher turned out to be right - I couldn’t solve the problem. Then he gave me an analysis textbook and said: “If you want to know the solution, read this book.” I did just that. Then mathematics captured me.

Before that, I was fascinated by stars and was planning to become an astronomer. Only later did I learn that stargazing is not the whole of astronomy. But this science still interests me. In addition to a diploma in mathematics, I have another one - in physics.

There was little they could do to help me at home. The fact is that in our family I was the first to graduate from college.

To participate in this program, you must pass certain test exams. In mathematics, I was the best in the United States. — One of our readers sent us the following problem related to the Erdsch conjecture: $n$ of infinite arithmetic progressions cover the first $2^n$ natural numbers; prove that they cover the entire natural series. What can you say about this task?

— This statement, or its variant with the replacement of $2^{2n}$ by $2n!$, is relatively easy to prove by induction. But the fact is that the Erdsch conjecture itself (in which $2^n$ appears instead of $2^{2n}$ - and this estimate is already exact!) was proven by Crittenden and Van den Eijden (1969). The proof is difficult; it cannot be discussed briefly.

When solving such problems, the first stage is very important - to make sure whether the hypothesis is valid: you either try to build countermeasures, or prove some special cases, weaker statements or consequences of the hypothesis; This will happen until you finally gain confidence in the validity of what you are proving.

— The tasks on arithmetic progressions are associated with the name of Van der Waerden. Are there any good problems on this topic?

— Here is an interesting problem related to Van der Waerden’s theorem: Let us denote, among its number, by $W(k)$ the smallest natural number such that for any partition of the numbers from 1 to $W(k)$ into two classes, although in one of them there is a $k$-term arithmetic progression. (Van der Waerden proved that $W(k)$ exists for any $k$.) It is easy to see that $W(2)=3$, $W(3)=9$; it is also known that $W(4)=35$, $W(5)=178$, but $W(6)$ has not been found even with the help of a computer. I propose the hypothesis that $W(k)$ grows no faster than the “tower” of $k$ two $2^{2n}$. But there are reasons to doubt even this estimate: there are some similar combinators of the problem associated with the name Ramsey, where functions are found that grow even faster. You know, there is the Guinness Book of Records, widely known in the West. So, there is “the largest number actually used in a mathematical proof.” It was invented by me in connection with one problem of Ramsey's theory.

There is a Sekersch conjecture that $S(k)=2^{k-2}+1$. Apparently, hope for its proof in the general case has not yet increased?

- Yes, finding the value of $S(k)$ or even giving a good estimate of it is apparently a very difficult task. The problem can be expressed: to prove that for a large number of points $N$ among all $k$-gons with vertices at these $N$ points there must be at least $C_k$ convex, where $C_k>0$ depends only on $k$, and tries to estimate $C_k$. And here’s another version of this problem: find from what number of $n=f(k)$ points you can always choose an “empty” convex $k$-gon (i.e., one in which there are no longer any of these $n$ points). It turns out that such a $f(k)$ exists for $k=3$, 4 and 5, but for $k\geq 7$ it simply does not exist. For $k=6$ - he doesn’t know anything.

In general, Erdsham calls this problem “a problem with a happy lump”: the fact is that he and Sekersh became infatuated with his spiranate Esther Klein, and it all ended with... that the spirant became Sekersh’s wife.

— Do you think that computational science, information science, will absorb mathematics?

- This is a rather difficult question. Most of the most talented scientists in the United States have used computers from the very beginning of their scientific careers. If they are interested in graphs or numbers, then a wide variety of experiments can be carried out on the computer. When playing computer games, schoolchildren in the United States begin to use computers at the age of 6-7 years. Then the range of mathematical studies can be expanded using the same computers.

The situation on the labor market is such that the demand for specialists in computational methods is enormous. Therefore, many talented young people consider computers in the context of their future profession.

Both young and established mathematicians come to the conclusion that computational experiment is a source of important and interesting problems.
