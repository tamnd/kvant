Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
I. D. NOVIKOV

We live in an expanding universe. This fact was predicted theoretically by the Soviet mathematician A. A. Friedman and confirmed in the late 20s by the American astrophysicist E. Hubble.

The non-stationary nature of the world around us has a certain significance for the physical processes in the Universe. In this article we will discuss some general patterns of tell processes in the macrocosm. The application of the laws of thermodynamics discovered in the past to the entire Universe led to conclusions, sometimes to paradoxes. And we'll look at some of them here. But before turning to these problems, let us briefly recall the essence of Friedman's theoretical prediction.

The main idea of ​​Friedman's work is fundamentally simple and consists of the following. On a very large scale (as is now known, on a scale of more than many millions of light years), matter in the form of a galaxy, their accumulation, is uniformly distributed in space. Mutual acceptance of gigantic masses will inevitably lead to their movement and mutual mixing. If const' < 0 ($E < 0$), then the geometry turns out to be similar to the geometry on the sphere, parallel lines intersect, and the space itself turns out to be closed, having a finite volume. The curvature of space is given by the “rainbow of curvature of the world” $l$, and the total volume of the closed Universe in order of magnitude is $V \sim l^3$. The change in $l$ over time is described, like the change in other distances in the Universe, by law (3).

The question naturally arises: what happened before that? However, there is no answer yet, and specialists are considering various possibilities here.

All of the above allows us to imagine the evolution of the Universe in the past as follows. Before the singular state, the Universe rolled up, the density of matter increased, and ultimately a superdense singular state. In this state, we still know the laws of nature very poorly, or rather, we only agree on some of them. In this state, it is believed that enormous gravitational repulsion forces will arise, which stop the rolling of the Universe and cause it to begin to expand. It is in this expanding Universe that we live today.

But it turned out that the situation is not so simple at all, and such a scenario of evolution in the countryside can be realized in reality. The difficulties that arise in such a theory go back to the middle of the last century.

In 1850, the German physicist P. Clausius and in 1851, independently of him, the English physicist W. Thomson discovered the law known as the second law of thermodynamics. In Thomson's formulation, this law reads as follows: in nature, a process is impossible, the only result of which was mechanical work performed by cooling the heat reservoir. It followed that the complete conversion of heat into mechanical energy was impossible. This means that if you isolate any system, then in the end all types of energy in this system will turn into heat, and the heat will be evenly distributed throughout the entire system - thermodynamic equilibrium will occur, as they say.

In practice, we know very well the manifestation of this law. In mechanical systems it is trivial to convert mechanical energy into heat. In heat engines, we can, however, convert warm energy into mechanical work. But to do this, it is necessary to maintain a difference in the temperature of the heater and refrigerator, otherwise the machine will not work, and energy must be spent on this, and part of the expended energy also turns into heat. This is how continuous accumulation of heat occurs, the transition of all types of energy into heat. Clausius later gave a mathematical expression for the second law of thermodynamics.

Thomson and Clausius understood the critical significance of the law of thermodynamics they discovered for the evolution of all cultures. Indeed, everything in the village must be considered as an isolated system, and for it the exchange of energies with “other systems” is impossible. This means that in the village all types of energy must ultimately turn into heat, the heat will be evenly distributed throughout the village. Although the law of conservation of energy is not violated, the energy does not disappear anywhere and remains in the form of thermal energy, but it turns out to be “powerless”, deprived of the possibility of transformation into mechanical work, and all macroscopic movement in the village will stop. This gloomy state resulted in “heat death” in the village. The reader will probably agree that the name of this very well characterizes the subjective state itself. But the village in which we live is clearly not in a state of “heat death”! From here, theological conclusions were drawn: either the village was essentially short-lived and had not yet reached the state of “thermal death,” or “someone” was involved in the evolution of the village, preventing it from developing to the state of “heat death.”

Let us trace how these predictions were determined by science. The thermodynamic ideas of Clausius and Thomson were developed by the original physicist L. Boltzmann. He showed what the meaning of the second law of thermodynamics is. Heat is essentially the chaotic movement of atoms or molecules that make up material bodies. Therefore, the transition of the energy of mechanical movement of individual parts of the system into heat is indicated by the transition of organized movement into chaotic, increased disorder in the system. The same can be said about other types of motion of matter. Such an increase in disorder is inevitable due to statistical laws, unless the system is influenced from outside and does not contribute to the preservation of order.

Boltzmann showed that the method of disorder in a system is a quantity introduced by Clausius - entropy. The greater the chaos, the greater the entropy. The transition of certain types of motion of matter into heat is marked by an increase in entropy. If all types of energy have turned into heat, and the heat meter has been distributed throughout the system, then this state of maximum chaos no longer changes over time and corresponds to maximum entropy.

But such an interpretation means that the second law of thermodynamics is not always fulfilled absolutely exactly, and deviations from it are possible. Indeed, the meaning of this law is that an isolated system moves into an increasingly probable state of chaotic movement of the particles that make it up. But during the transition, deviations and fluctuations are possible. So, for example, in some small volume of gas, atoms, during collisions with the contents, can accidentally acquire equally directed impulses, i.e., they can accidentally begin to move in one direction. This will no longer be thermal (chaotic) movement, but the direction of movement of some volume of gas as a whole. The thermal motion of particles accidentally changed into the direction of mechanical motion.

But, of course, such cases are very rare and unlikely. And the larger the volume in the gas we take, the less likely it is that such incidents can happen to it. In general, with the exception of very rare and small fluctuations, the entropy of an isolated system always increases, and the system reaches the most probable state of maximum entropy, in which it must exceed an unlimited time. But still, although rarely, sometimes in one place of the system, sometimes in another, according to the law, chance will find deviations from this state, usually very small.

It was on this path that Boltzmann sought a way out of the gloomy conclusion about the “thermal death” of the Universe.

The sleepless Universe, he said, eternally remains in the most probable state of thermodynamic equilibrium with maximum entropy. But in any volume, rare deviations from this state—fluctuations—are possible. True, in large volumes noticeable fluctuations are very rare. But if we have infinite time, then we can prove how large the random fluctuation is in huge volumes. According to Boltzmann, this is the kind of gigantic fluctuation in which we live.

Boltzmann's fluctuation hypothesis was the only (from the point of view of physics of that time) attempt to refute the conclusion about “thermal death” until the discoveries of Friedmann and Hubble.

These discoveries radically changed our understanding of the state towards which the evolution of processes in the Universe is directed. First of all, we found out that gravity plays a certain role in the evolution of the Universe. In the discussion of “heat death”, gravity was generally ignored. Meanwhile, this cannot be done in any way.

How does this affect the processes occurring in the Universe? Let's try to understand this using the following example: consider a gas ball, between the particles of which gravitational forces act. Let us assume that at first the substance of the ball was cold and was scattered in space, the mutual gravitation between particles was extremely small, and the gravitational energy of the substance was practically equal to zero. Let the matter gradually, under the influence of albeit weak gravity, gather into a ball, and this ball is removed further and further by gravity. It is clear that the substance of the ball is gaining more and more speed and, consequently, more and more kinetic energy of motion. This positive energy will increase due to gravity. But due to the law of conservation of energy, the total energy of the system must be conserved. This means that the increase in positive energy of motion is accompanied by a decrease in the potential energy of gravity, i.e., an increase in its absolute value (after all, $E_\pi < 0$). Now it is clear that as the system scans, the positive part of its energy can increase due to gravity. This is the circumstance in the past, when gravity was not paid due attention and was not taken into account. Since the positive part of the energy of an isolated system can increase, then the increase in energy (which necessarily occurs) does not necessarily lead to the fading of processes.

Thus, the conclusion about “heat death” as it was made in the middle of the last century, when the fact of the non-existential nature of the Universe was not taken into account, is incorrect.

In a pulsating Universe, in each cycle there is an increase in energy, an accumulation of heat (according to the second law of thermodynamics, which, of course, is not violated). The accumulation of thermal energy occurs on a large scale, for example, during the glow of stars processing nuclear energy reserves for study. We will assume that in the signal state the energy cannot decrease sharply.

Entropy increases from cycle to cycle. At first glance, this should lead to the fading of the pulsations, to a decrease in their amplitude. It would seem that the picture should be similar to the damping of oscillations of a pendulum, when the tension in its suspension gradually transfers the energy of oscillations into the body. In fact, the picture of pulsation in such a model of the Universe looks completely different - the amplitude of the pulsation of the Universe increases!
