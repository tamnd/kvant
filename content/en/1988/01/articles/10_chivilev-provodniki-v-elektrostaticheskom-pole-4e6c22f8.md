Conductors in a power plant field

“I want to tell you a new and terrible experience, which I do not advise you to repeat... Suddenly my right hand was struck with such force that my whole body was shaken, as if struck by lightning. ...In a word, I thought that the end had come... For the sake of the French crown, I would not agree to once again subject myself to such terrible cooperation...” These are the words from the memoirs of the Leiden professor Muschenbreck, given in the book by V. Kartsev “The Adventures of the Great Equations.”
Muschenbrek carried out an experiment on electricity in 1745 and obtained the simplest capacitor, later called the Leyden jar. During the experiments, the professor was subjected to “such terrible cooperation” as a result of the discharge of a capacitor through the human body, which, as is known, is a conductor.

The fact that conductors exist in nature enriches the world around us with a variety of electrical phenomena, some of which are far from dangerous. Conductors are important in the study of electromagnetism.

Let us consider in detail the case when a charged stationary conductor is in an external power field (created by the construction of stationary charges). In a conductor, sooner or later all the charges will swap, and equilibrium will occur (since otherwise we would get a perpetual motion machine as a result of the non-transfer of heat during the movement of charges). For such a charged conductor placed in an external electric field, the following statements will be true.

1. Field inside the conductor. At any point inside the conductor, the direction of the electric field is zero. Indeed, if this condition were not fulfilled, the free charges in the conductor would begin to move under the influence of field forces, and the equilibrium would be disrupted.

2. Charge distribution in a conductor. In order to answer the question about the distribution of charge in a conductor, we need to clarify some properties of the power lines of the power plant field. For example, that the line of force of an electric field (including a power plant) is an imaginary line in space, drawn so that the tangent to it at each point coincides with the direction vector of the electric field at this point. The experience of studying power fields gives reason to conclude that the lines of force of these fields are continuous and not closed, they can only begin on positive charges and end up only on negative ones and cannot begin (end) at a point in space where there are no charges. When graphically depicting the field of some systems of charges, the number of field lines starting or ending on any charge is proportional to the model of this charge. It follows from this that lines of force necessarily leave (or enter) any charge.

After what has been said about lines of force, let us return to the question of charge distribution in a conductor. Let us mentally select an arbitrary sufficiently small volume ΔV inside the conductor (Fig. 1). Let's assume that this volume has a charge (for definition, positive). Then lines of force will emerge from the selected volume, i.e., an electric field will exist near it. But there is no field inside the conductor. Therefore, the allocated volume must be neutral. And since we took this volume at an arbitrary place inside the conductor, we can say that the entire “inside” of the conductor is neutral and, therefore, the entire charge of the conductor is on its surface.
