Heinrich Hertz and electromagnetic waves

The outstanding German physicist Heinrich Hertz was born in 1857 in Hamburg into the family of a lawyer. Already in his school years, the extremely crisp, healthy boy was discovered to be multi-talented - he had an amazing memory, excellent abilities in drawing, languages, technical creativity (for example, he personally made an excellent spectroscope and other physical and astronomical instruments), showed a keen interest in the exact sciences and wealth. In 1880, Hertz graduated from the University of Berlin, completing a brilliant doctoral dissertation on theoretical electrodynamics. His scientific supervisor at that time was the first physicist in Europe, G. Helmholtz (1821-1894), for whom he worked as an assistant for the next three years.

Helmholtz, who for his share of life borrowed from many problems of physiology, physics and technology, at this time was especially interested in electrodynamics. He developed his own version of theoretical electrodynamics, which competed with the previously proposed theories of W. Weber (1804-1891) and J. C. Maxwell (1831-1879). In 1879, the Berlin Academy of Sciences, on the initiative of Helmholtz, nominated the competition for the task: “To establish experimentally whether there is a connection between electrodynamic forces and dielectric polarization.” The solution to this problem, according to Helmholtz, should be the answer to which of the electrodynamic theories corresponds to the truth. Of course, he realized that victory would be his own child. It is not surprising that Helmholtz suggested taking on this task to Hertz, who he considered the most talented of his many students - especially since a year earlier he had already received a prize from the University of Berlin for experimental research in electrodynamics.

Hertz tried to solve the problem using electrical vibrations, which arose during the discharge of capacitors and induction coils. However, the young scientist was unable to obtain any definitive results. And it was clear why: significant higher-frequency - or, as they said then, faster - vibrations were required than those that physicists were able to excite at that time.

In 1883, Hertz received a position as an assistant professor at the University of Kiel, and in 1885 he became a professor of experimental physics at the Technical High School in Karlsruhe.

According to the scientist, it is known that, having failed in Berlin with the Helmholtz problem, he still retained a reliable path to its solution, and since then everything connected with electrical vibrations has invariably attracted his close attention. This persistent interest played a major role in the fact that the observation made in the fall of 1886 not only served as a reason for Hertz to return to the distinguished “until better times” problem and solve it, but also became the starting point of his path to a replacement discovery - the experimental detection of electromagnetic waves.

What kind of observation was this?

In Karlsruhe, Hertz put at his disposal a physics room with a rich collection of scientific instruments. Along with other devices from this arsenal, the scientist, in demonstration experiments at the lectures, used a pair of induction coils with a finely adjustable gap between the metal balls at the ends of the winding (Fig. 1) using a micrometer. “I was disgusted,” Hertz later said, recalling how he put off lecture demonstrations with these coils, that in order to excite a spark in one of the coils it was not necessary to connect a powerful battery to the other. Narratively, all that was needed was a small Leyden jar or even a discharge from a small inductor - if only a spark would leak out [through the opening gap of the primary coil." Let me point out that this conclusion was by no means made “right away.” To establish himself in it, Hertz brought the width of the spark gap of the secondary coil to hundredths of a millimeter, began to work in a darkened room, and looked for beautiful and amazing sparks through a magnifying glass.

Having carried out a series of essentially simple, but deeply thought-out experiments, Hertz came to a firm conviction: the sparks in the secondary coil are excited by a spark discharge in the primary coil not due to a simple induction effect, but because the primary discharge generates precisely those very “very fast electrical vibrations”, the inaccessibility of which at one time did not allow him to solve the problem of the Berlin Academy in a competition.
