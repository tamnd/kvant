Truly dimensionless numbers, i.e. those that are not combinations of dimensional quantities, are also often found in physics. it is difficult to find formulas that do not include the transcendental numbers $e$ and $l$, not to mention simply the wide variety of numerical factors included in any formula.

Let's return to physical quantities that have one dimension or another. The task of a physical theory is to connect various physical quantities with correspondences that can be verified experimentally. For example, wanting to check how the path $s$ traversed by a particle moving with constant acceleration $a$ changes over time, we must compare our changes with the formula

$$s(t) = v_0 t + \frac{at^2}{2} = v_0 t \left( 1 + \frac{at}{2v_0} \right),$$

where $v_0$ is the initial velocity of the particle. This is a very simple formula. But it (like all formulas in physics!) requires that all the quantities included in it have the corresponding dimensions: if $s$ changes in centimeters, and $t$ - in seconds, then $v_0$ - in cm/s, and $a$ - in cm/s². But we want not only to check this formula, but also to find out: how does acceleration affect the nature of the particle’s motion? The second form of notation is convenient for this (when we put $v_0 t$ out of brackets). It shows: at first (at $t \ll 2v_0/a$) the role of acceleration is insignificant - the particle is in practical motion with a constant speed $v_0$, but then (at $t \gg 2v_0/a$) the particle “forgets” what its initial speed was - its movement is determined by acceleration. As you can see, it is impossible to unambiguously answer the question of whether the acceleration of a particle is greater or less. The answer depends on what stage of the movement we are interested in. And we found this out by comparing the dimensionless ratio $at^2/2v_0 t = at/2v_0$ with unity. Strictly speaking, when discarding the first or second term, it would be necessary to take into account the accuracy with which our device changes the path taken, and allow ourselves to ignore the corresponding term only if it turns out to be less than the relative error of the change. We will no longer go into such subtleties, although they are very important when analyzing real experiments.

When solving real problems, we often have to simplify; without this, the problem simply cannot be solved. Simplification is always based on the preference of small terms in comparison with large ones. But in order to discard something, it is necessary to compare terms, and only values ​​of the same dimension can be compared. The above formulates the first (basic) rule for using dimensional quantities. Many physicists are so afraid of breaking this rule that, when starting to solve a problem, they immediately non-dimensionalize the quantities involved.

Let's return to the motion of a particle with constant acceleration. Let's change the time in units of $2v_0/a$ (i.e., instead of time $t$, we will introduce “dimensionless time” $\tau = at/2v_0$), and the distance traveled in units of $2v_0^2/a$ (i.e., we will introduce “dimensionless length” $\sigma = \frac{a}{2v_0^2} s$). Then formula (1) will be greatly simplified:

$$\sigma = \tau(1 + \tau).$$
