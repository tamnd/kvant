But now imagine yourself as a climber who needs to climb up such a rope. This often comes to be done in mountaineering practice. The one who goes first makes a path along a difficult climb, using the entire arsenal of climbing equipment. Then he secures the rope, and all other parts of the climb climb along it. Naturally, the rope stretches under the load created by the climber. The further the climber is from the anchorage point, the greater the absolute elongation of the rope will be. The sensations of someone climbing such a rope will be approximately the same as those of a climber “suspended by an elastic band.” Believe me, this is not very pleasant, especially when there are sharp stones around! It would be nice to have a rope in which the dependence of deformation on load is depicted by the curve $a$ on figure 2. Then, at low loads, the rope would hardly stretch. This would allow one to climb along a fixed rope, like a tightrope in a gym. At high loads, on the contrary, we “benefit” from a rapid increase in deformation. In fact, if the area of ​​loads under which $e$ grows rapidly is approximately the same as the loads after the climber’s roof, then the rope “works” like rubber, “taking the fish onto itself.”

There are no materials in nature that have such characteristics. However, the combination of various materials makes it possible to construct a rope that will provide climbers with the necessary safety and convenience when climbing. The curve $6$ on figure 2 is a schematic characteristic of a real rope.

A cross-section of such a climbing rope is shown in figure 3. Its outer braid is made in a special way. Sufficiently rigid and strong nylon threads (sometimes they are replaced with lavsan threads) will be located at an angle to the axis of the rope. Under load, these threads change their direction and gradually position

almost parallel to the axis. The inner part of the rope is a combination (bundle) of several of the same braids, but smaller in diameter. The threads inside each such braid are very strong; they almost do not change their length until they break. The number of threads is selected so as to withstand a load of approximately $3 \cdot 10^3$ N. When the climber is tired, as soon as the load on the rope exceeds this value, the internal and strong threads are broken, and the entire rope as a whole is stretched. When they break, the rigid threads become the first “dampers” of the kinetic energy of the falling body. Existence, part of this energy goes into work spent on breaking the threads. With a rope that can be used to climb up after the roof, you can quite safely INSTRUCT the greenhouse, but climbing it will, as we now understand, be inconvenient.

The main conclusion we came to is this: an ideal climbing rope should not only be very strong, but also be easily deformable. Unfortunately, the industry so far produces little such rope. And many sports climbers have to use a common nylon rope (about thirty years ago, when there were few such ropes, climbers used strong sisal ropes). The strength of the nylon rope is satisfied, but the deformation is low. It does not exceed 15-20% at loads of the order of $10^4$ N. What does this mean? Do climbers deliberately put their lives in danger by using unsuitable equipment?
