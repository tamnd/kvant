Smoothly. So we'll try to figure out how to do this.

We can assume that when falling off, Alexey’s speed at point $A_1$ is zero and his fall almost to point $A_2$ is simply a free fall (the rope is not stretched!). At point $A_2$ its speed is $v_0=gt$, where $t$ is the time of fall. During this time, Alexey will fly a distance of $gt^2/2=2l_0$; so $t=\sqrt{4l_0/g}$, and $v_0=g\sqrt{4l_0/g}=2\sqrt{gl_0}$.

The speed $v_0$ is damped more or less smoothly somewhere near the point $A_2$ due to the distance of the rope. At the moment when the rope stretched and the distance began, Alexey’s speed was $v=v_0$, and when the elongation of the rope became maximum and the fall stopped, $v=v_\kappa=0$. If the version for which the speed will be “repaid” is designated $\Delta t$, then the average force acting on the climber for this version is $F_{cp}=\frac{M(v_0-v_\kappa)}{\Delta t}$. The value $\frac{v_0-v_\kappa}{\Delta t}=\frac{v_0}{\Delta t}=a$ has the meaning of the average acceleration with which “braking” occurs. Obviously, the higher the initial speed and the lower the braking version, the greater the force $F_{cp}$. But then from what we said, it follows that the rope connecting Alexey and Victor must be such that the version $\Delta t$ before Alexey stops is large enough. What does it mean - a sufficiently large version of braking? And this means that, in any case, the average force $F_{cp}$ acting on Alexey from the side of the rope must be less than the maximum number that the human body can withstand - $F_{\max}=5\cdot10^3$ N. So, the inequality must be fulfilled

$$F_{\max}\geqslant F_{cp}=\frac{Mv_0}{\Delta t},$$

hence $\Delta t\geqslant Mv_0/F_{\max}$ (note, by the way, that $a=\frac{v_0}{\Delta t}\leqslant\frac{F_{\max}}{M}=\frac{5\cdot10^3\mathrm{H}}{10^2\mathrm{kg}}=50\mathrm{m/c^2}$, which means that $a$ can be greater than $g\approx10\mathrm{m/c^2}$. For our specific example it is easy to obtain that $\Delta t\geqslant0,2$ s.)

What can provide the necessary time $\Delta t$, and therefore the safety of the rope to stretch well. To simplify the calculations, we will assume that such braking when stretching the rope is a process of uniformly slow motion. Then the path taken by Alexey before his arrest (stopping) is equal to the lengthening of the rope $\Delta l=a(\Delta t)^2/2$. Substituting $a=v_0/\Delta t$ here, we get $\Delta t=2\Delta l/v_0$. Combining this last formula with the inequality for $\Delta t$ written above, we arrive at the inequality

$$\Delta l\geqslant\frac{Mv_0^2}{2F_{\max}}=\frac{M\cdot4gl_0}{2F_{\max}}=\frac{2Mgl_0}{F_{\max}}.$$

This is an estimate for the distance at which the speed must be reduced so as not to harm the climber. (For $l_0=2,5$ m and $M=100$ kg $\Delta l\geqslant1$ m.) Let us now divide the part of the inequality for $\Delta l$ by the length of the rope before stretching $L+l_0$. The ratio $\varepsilon=\Delta l/(L+l_0)$ is called relative strain or simply strain. If the right side of the inequality

$$\varepsilon\geqslant\frac{2Mgl_0}{(L+l_0)F_{\max}}$$

Substitute the numerical values ​​for our specific situation, it turns out that $\varepsilon\geqslant0,13$.

It can now be argued that Alexey will not build during the fall if the deformation of the climbing rope is more than 13%. In fact, a good perfect climbing rope has a deformation under loads of the order of $10^4$ N of about 40-45%.

It would seem that everything is clear now. We will take with us to the mountains a rope with the necessary characteristics for safety, and Victor can, without hesitation, tightly close it.
