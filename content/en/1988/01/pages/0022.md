⟦folio 20⟧

Let's say a few more words about energy transformations during the rise and lift Alexei.

When Alexey rose higher than Victor, who was belaying him, with each step he increased his potential energy relative to the belay point (or relative to Victor). After release, this potential energy turns into kinetic energy of the free-flying climber. After reaching the full length of the free rope, Alexey begins to stretch it. Its kinetic energy now turns into the energy of deformation of the rope. The situation could be worse for the movement of a climber on a tension spring or elastic band if the rope were perfectly elastic. If this were so, the climber, after stopping while climbing, would begin to move upward again. However, such a flask process is not actually implemented. Under load, the individual fibers from which the rope is made interact with each other - rub against each other. In addition, when the fibers are stretched, heat is released. The oscillations that we might think about actually die out before they even begin. The kinetic energy of the fallen Alexei turns into heat, which is released in the rope. Well, what if the elastic properties of the rope are such that it cannot provide swimming and slow down for a fallen athlete? But then such braking can be carried out by the teammate, who insures his companion! To do this, Victor just needs to “etch”, release some part of the rope under tension. The reliability of proper belay, it turns out, does not lie in securing the rope as tightly as possible if the roper falls, but in being able to adjust its tension correctly and in a timely manner. As we have already understood, in the case of a good climbing rope, the kinetic energy of the fall is converted into the warm and elastic energy of the rope. For a “fluffy” rope (strong, but inextensible), the same kinetic energy largely turns into heat when the rope rubs against the hands of its belayer. This heat can be quite high, so one of the basic rules in mountaineering is “Belay in your hands!”

Quite simple reasoning shows that this rule is not in vain. Since we only want to obtain an estimate of the amount of heat generated, we will use it conditionally that all the energy of the climber’s fall is converted into heat due to the friction of the rope on the hands of the belayer. The kinetic energy of the serving climber at the moment when the rope or his companion belaying him begins to “work” is equal to

$$E_{\kappa} = M v_0^2 / 2 = 2 M g l_0.$$

Substituting $l_0 = 2,5$ m, $M = 100$ kg, $g \approx 10$ m/s², we get $E_{\kappa} \approx 5 \cdot 10^3$ J. This is the energy that should turn into heat Q. Is it a lot or a little? Let's see how much water could be boiled by applying $Q = 5 \cdot 10^3$ J to it. The heat required to heat water from $t_1 = 20^\circ\text{C}$ to $t_2 = 100^\circ\text{C}$ is $Q = cm$ ($t_2 - t_1$). The heat capacity of water is $c = 4,2$ J/(g °C), so $m = Q / (c(t_2 - t_1)) \approx 15$ g. Not so little!

Of course, we have now made a rather rough estimate. In reality, you need to keep in mind that not only the belayer’s hands heat up, but also the rope itself. But more important than others. The human word conducts heat relatively poorly (a physicist would say: it has flat thermal conductivity). Therefore, when belaying the head with your hands, a thin layer of hand tissue is heated and “burned.” The word “burned” here could be calculated. But in order not to actually burn your hands, you need to belay in your hands. The thermal conductivity of the materials from which the gloves are made is even less than that of the climber’s body.

These are the physical basis of belaying in the mountains. Knowing them is useful, of course, not only for climbers. And now you yourself will probably definitely answer the question that is in the title of this article. Happy climbing!
