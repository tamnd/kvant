DOES A CLIMBER NEED
PHYSICS?
Candidate of Technical Sciences
A. V. GELLER

People who conquer mountain peaks are usually romantics. And the mountains themselves with their white peaks are so majestic that any words about the physical obligations of certain phenomena that arise in the mountains, about calculations and justifications for some specific situations sometimes seem ridiculous. Something like entering a formula for poetry or an algorithm for creating a painting.

And yet, we will try to solve one such problem and use its example to show how physics helps climbers. While still on the plain, sitting at your desk, you can solve security issues in the mountains. So we're going to the mountains. Of course, we must carefully select and prepare the necessary equipment, stock up on folding and ice hooks, good ropes...

A climbing rope, like most other pieces of equipment in modern sports, is a complex engineering “structure”. In this article we will tell you why climbers need special ropes and how they “work” in the mountains. But first, let’s discuss a typical and, alas, common situation that arises when climbing or during training.

figure 1 schematically shows the movement of a two-link. The first climber, let's call him Alexey, climbed the rock to a height of $L=5$ m higher than the second, whose name is Victor. Alexey scored a cry at point A, which is the belay point, and then rose another $l_0=2,5$ m. Suddenly Alexey fell. Victor closed his end of the rope very firmly, and Alexey hung on it.

The question that interests us is: what properties should a rope have in order for Alexey to remain unscathed?

Of course, the first thing (while we are still at home) is to look at some reference book that lists the characteristics of sports equipment. So we learned that for a rope with a diameter of 10 mm, it is strong, i.e., the force that needs to be applied to tie it is equal to $P=1,5 \cdot 10^4$ N. It is also known that the maximum number that a human body can withstand is $F_{\text{max}}=5 \cdot 10^3$ N. We will also need the mass of a climber with a backpack $M$. Let's assume that $M=100$ kg is a completely reasonable estimate.

It would seem that for Alexei’s safety it is enough for the rope to be strong. Then, when he fell, he stopped intact, since the rope would not break. But remember that a person who falls from a height of 10 m (the height of the third floor) most often breaks if he falls on solid ground. If he falls on a haystack or in a deep snowdrift, he will most likely get off with just a mess. The fall of a climber hanging from an inextensible rope will also result in a blow at the end of his flight, even if he does not have to reach the base of the rock. Therefore, the conclusion is drawn: the speed that a person gained when falling must be extinguished
