FLEXAGONS, FLEXORS, FLEXMANS

A. A. PANOV

We invite you to a short excursion into the mysterious world of flexagons and flexes - paper toys with the amazing ability to suddenly change their shape and color. You can put together any of these toys in the shortest possible time.

But first of all, about the title of the article. English to flex familiar to fold, bend, bend.

Now prepare a sheet of paper or thick landscape paper, pencils, scissors, glue - and start dividing.

Hexaflexaon

Flexaon is a polygon glued together from paper, which, by bending and folding, can transform into more and more new states.

Hexaflexaon is a flexaon shaped like a regular hexagon. Let's cut out a strip of paper 2.5 cm wide, consisting of ten regular triangles, and cut out both sides of it - straight and reverse, as in figure 1.

We fold the strip several times along the boundaries of the triangles and continue making the flexaon, folding the strip as in figure 2.

Now all that remains is to bend the wizz blue triangle, glue two unpainted triangular surfaces together - and the flexaon is ready. However, his one is red, the other is blue. To bring to light its third, green surface, you need to act as follows. First, the flexaon is placed on the plane of the table so that it rests on the three lower points. These points need to be pressed closer to each other, and at the moment they are completely pressed together, the flexaon itself will turn inside out, exposing its green surface. Now all that remains is to move the upper points of the flexaon to the sides, and the flexaon is ready for a new transformation. Thus, sequentially moving from one state to another, the flexaon will alternately present to us all its three surfaces. Figure 3 gives some insight into the manufacture of flexaon.

The model we made is the simplest of the extensive family of hexaflexaons, described in Martin Gardner’s book “Mathematical Puzzles and Entertainment” (M., Mir, 1971). This interesting book outlines the history of the discovery of hexaflexaons and describes in detail how to build models with a large number of surfaces.

Double swivel joint

The double joint is a design element that serves as the basis for another range of flexo models. Let's cut out of paper two rectangles measuring 2.5 × 5 cm and two strips measuring 1 × 7 cm. On the edges of the end of each strip we will cut out a 1 × 1 cm square. We will color the strips with rectangles, as in figure 4, where their straight and reverse sides are presented.

Glue both strips to one of the rectangles, connecting unpainted squares with unpainted ones, and wrap both strips onto the red side of the rectangle. The result should be what is shown in figure 5.

Flexes by Robert Connelly and Klaus Steffen

It must be said that a ring of tetradra as a bendable polyhedron raises a number of objections. First of all, there is a hole in it. Secondly, there are edges, each of which has four faces. So it is not clear whether this ring should be called a polyhedron.

To avoid any doubts, when searching for flexes, one could limit oneself to only convex polyhedra, that is, polyhedra lying on one side of each of their faces. But there is the famous Cauchy theorem that any convex polyhedron is inflexible (see, for example, the article by N.P. Dolbilin “Rigidity of convex polyhedra”, «Kvant», 1988, No. 5). It was proven in 1813. Although this theorem does not exclude the existence of non-convex flexes, many mathematicians believed that such flexes also do not exist. Therefore, the flexor, discovered by Robert Connelly in 1977, is a sensation. At one time, the construction proposed by Connelly was discussed in the article by V. Zalgaler “Continuously flexible polyhedron” («Kvant», 1978, No. 9) and seemed to me then so complicated that I didn’t even begin to understand it. However, Klaus Steffen soon managed to come up with a flexor so simple that it can be glued together in literally a matter of minutes. How to do this is shown on page 4 of the cover «Kvant» of No. 5 for this year.

Flexmas

Flexms are creatures that inhabit the world of flexagons and flexes. You've probably already noticed the image of flexan (p. 13). To use with flexmans, cut out a square of thick paper with a side of 15-20 cm. It needs to be diagonally with the mushroom up and along the dashed line (figure 16, a) with the mushroom down, and then folded to form a triangle. Now you will need to perform four identical operations. The result of the first of them - sibb along the dashed line figure 16, b - is depicted on figure 16, c, the final result - on figure 16, d. There remain four more identical operations - bending small triangles, and before us is a flexman.

The most useful property of flexans is their ability to walk on inclined planes. It is necessary to place the Flexan on a fairly gentle inclined plane, and it immediately begins to descend along it in small steps. Each of the flexans has a unique characteristic or, in any case, a unique gait. I think that getting to know him will make you feel comfortable.
