In the second half of the 18th and early 19th centuries, the remarkable humanist teacher Juhann Heinrich Pestaloschi (1746-1827) lived in Switzerland. Nowadays, Pestaloshi is considered throughout the world to be the founder of the theory of primary education of children, the idea of ​​​​connecting education with productive labor. We could talk about him for a long time - but Pestaloshi is not our hero here. Pestalosi tested and implemented his ideas in the excellent primary boarding school he founded for poor children. Pestaloshi's staff traveled throughout Switzerland, selecting prospective students and persuading (often the hardest) their parents to send their children to school.

In 1814, one of Pestaloshi's last employees met a young shepherd, Jacob Steiner, the son of a poor peasant, in the mountains of Switzerland. Jacob at that time barely knew how to read and write; as a student he acquired some knowledge of mathematics and astronomy, which was especially fascinating to him at that time. This knowledge and interests of the young shepherd amazed Pestaloshi’s employee, who began to convince Steiner the father to refuse the services of valuable help and send his son to school. Euler established that the three midpoints of the sides of an arbitrary triangle, the three bases of its altitudes and the three points bisecting the segments of the altitudes from the point of their intersection (the orthocenter of the triangle) to the vertices belong to one circle. Let us now continue the life of Steiner's description. His greatest success during the Berlin period of his life was his acquaintance with the amateur mathematician, wealthy industrialist, talented engineer and railway magnate August Leopold Kreple (1780-1855). Kreple was not a prominent scientist, although he is a member of the Berlin (Prussian) and corresponding member of the St. Petersburg (Russian) Academies of Sciences. But a large entrepreneur is obliged to be a good breaker in people - and Kreple proved that he possesses these abilities to the fullest.

Constructions using one ruler

Propositions 1-3 of Euclidean “Principles” assert the possibility of unlimited continuation of a given line segment, drawing a line through two known points and drawing a circle with a given center (point) and radius (segment). Together with the assumptions implied by Euclid, but not explicitly formulated, about the possibility of indicating the point of intersection of two given (for example, each with two properties points) lines, two known circles (defined by properties by centers and radii) and given straight lines and circles, these statements describe the entire array of “Euclidean-solvable” construction problems: these problems must coincide with the listed construction problems, i.e., be solved using a ruler and a compass.

In 1797, Lorenzo Mascheroni’s (1750-1800) book “Geometry of the Compass” was published in Italy, in which it was established that all construction problems solvable by a compass and a straightedge are also solvable using a compass alone (with the natural limitation that a straight segment, of course, cannot be constructed with a compass - however, knowing two of its points, you can use a compass to indicate an unlimited number of others points of the segment). Much later, a book by Georg Mohr (1640-1697), published in 1672 (125 years before Mascheroni!) in Dutch and Danish, was found, in which the same theorem was proven.

Steiner became interested in constructions that could be realized with the help of a single ruler (see problems 1-3 below). Shortest road networks

Steiner often asked his students problems that required them to identify the best figure or configuration in one way or another. Here is one of his favorite tasks:

There are three villages: A, B, C. It is required to connect them with a minimum length network of roads.

It is more or less clear (although this still needs to be proven) that the solution here is given either by two (except for the linear) sides △ ABC, or by segments AP, BP, CP, where the sum of the distances from P to the vertices of △ ABC is the smallest possibility (Fig. 3, a, b). Moreover, in the case of Figure 3.6, the segments AP, BP, CP should form identical angles at point P, or if, say, ∠APB≠∠APC, then you can find point Q such that QA=PA, and QB+QC<PB+PC. It follows that the solution to Steiner's problem is given by figure 3,6 if each angle △ ABC is less than 120°, and figure 3, a if ∠B≥120°.

In the case when the number of villages is n>3, the “minimal” system of roads connecting them may turn out to be related to figure 3,a, i.e., it consists of any roads that connect our villages - such a road system is called a core system. By brute force (if necessary, with the help of a computer; now computers are actively involved in solving the “general” Steiner problem with a large number of “villages”), you can always find the shortest spanning road system. But more often, a better network of roads is similar to Figure 3.6, i.e., containing additional “nodes” of the network in which three roads converge, pairwise forming angles of 120° with each other - such nodes are called Steiner points, and the road networks containing them are called Steiner networks (Fig. 4). Alas! We do not have any general methods for finding minimal Steiner networks connecting n points; It is also unknown when the “absolutely minimal” road network will be a skeletal network and when it will be a Steiner network. True, it is assumed that the minimum spanning road network cannot exceed the minimum Steiner network: in the worst case, it turns out to be only (2/3)√3 times (i.e., approximately 15%) in length - but this hypothesis has so far been proven only for the case n≤5.

This is how difficult Steiner’s “promising” problem about the shortest network of paths connecting n points turned out to be (Steiner himself, for the case n>3, has only isolated examples of such networks - but even today we know almost as little as he knew!).
