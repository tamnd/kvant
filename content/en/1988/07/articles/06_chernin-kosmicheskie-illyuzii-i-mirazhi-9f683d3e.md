SPACE ILLUSIONS
AND MIRAGES
Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. D. CHERNIN

Cosmic sources of study, called quasars, were discovered a quarter of a century ago, in 1963. But their nature still remains not fully understood. There is no end in sight to the surprises they present to physicists and astronomers. Amazing phenomena have recently been discovered that have never before been observed in the sky: doubling of images and movement at superluminal speeds.

In this article we will not touch upon the history of the discovery of quasars, hypotheses and theories put forward to explain their properties.*) We will provide only the most general information about quasars, and then discuss in detail what mirages and illusions are in the world of quasars and how they should be understood.

The most powerful emitters in the Universe

Each quasar is a bright “lantern” that shines not only in the visible optical range of electromagnetic waves, but also in all other ranges - from radio waves to gamma rays. The size of the emitting region is comparable to the size of the Solar System. But in the Solar System there is only one star - the Sun, and a quasar in the same volume contains an energy generator thousands of times more powerful than the entire Galaxy with its billions of stars. How does this generator work? This is the most important question in the physics of quasars, to which it is still possible to find an answer.

Quasars are different in such data of the Universe, where neither stars nor entire galaxies can be smoothed out even with the help of the largest telecopes. The most distant quasar currently known is located at a distance of 13 billion light years from us. This means that the light from it takes 13 billion years to reach us and, therefore, we see it as it was 13 billion years ago. At that time, neither the Earth nor the Sun existed (according to modern estimates, the age of the Solar System is about 5 billion years).

The distances to quasars and other distant cosmic bodies are judged by the spectra of their radiation. In the spectra of quasars, emission lines of ordinary atoms are visible - such as hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, etc. - but they are all shifted towards the red end of the spectrum, that is, in the region of long wavelengths. This shift - the famous red shift - is due to the fact that the sources of radiation are moving away from us. The extension itself is a manifestation of the general distribution of the Universe. According to the laws of cosmology, the farther a quasar or galaxy is from us, the greater the escape velocity.

If a light source emits waves with a period of $T_0$ and at the same time moves relative to us with a speed of $v$, then the waves of light visible to us will have a period

$$T = T_0 \frac{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta}{\sqrt{1 - (v/c)^2}}.$$  (1)

Here $c$ is the speed of light, $\vartheta$ is the angle between the direction of speed and the line of sight (the prime that connects us and the source; when the source moves directly towards us, $\vartheta = 0$, when the line is straight away from us - $\vartheta = \pi$). For the most distant quasars, the period $T$ is 4-5 times longer than the period $T_0$. The change in period, and with it the frequency and wavelength of light due to the relative motion of the source and receiver, is called the Doppler effect. It creates a red shift in the spectra of expanding galaxies and quasars.

More than 2,500 quasars are now known. They are scattered across the celestial sphere more or less evenly. On average, the distance between them, if you change it along an arc in the sky, is several degrees (to be clear, let’s say that the angular diameters of Lunya and the Sun are about 0.5 degrees).

But two quasars very close to each other have long been known, which are also so similar to each other that they are called twin quasars. They are only six arcseconds apart, which is thousands of times less than the average distance between quasars; their brightness is practically the same; the spectra of these quasars are the same - in general cases the same emission lines with the same intensity are visible. Gravity lens

Rays, as is known, propagate rectilinearly if there is no pre-filling. The space environment is so rarefied that it practically does not cause pre-filling. And yet the paths of light can be bent. Such curvature is created by the gravitational fields of celestial bodies.

There's nothing particularly surprising about this. Even in Newton's theory of gravity, it was taken for granted that a ray of light, considered as a stream of particles - light corpuscles moving at the speed of light, deviates from a straight path when it passes close to the gravity of a body. Particles of light, like all particles and bodies of the Universe, experience attraction to a gravitating body.

Considerations about the bending of a ray of light by a gravitational field remain valid in the relativistic theory of gravity, which is called the general theory of relativity. According to this theory, the angle of deflection of the beam passing at a minimum distance $r$ from a body of mass $M$ is equal to (see Fig. 1)

$$\alpha = \frac{4GM}{c^2r}.$$ (2)

At the suggestion of A. Einstein, the first attempts to observe this effect were made for starlight passing near the Sun. Measurements can be taken during a total solar eclipse, when the Moon covers the solar disk. Many observations have been made since the total solar eclipse of 1919, with results that are in perfect agreement with the prediction of general relativity.

In 1936, Einstein published a short note in which he predicted a new effect associated with the bending of light in a gravitational field - the gravitational lensing effect. If there is a gravitating body on the path of light from some distant source to the observed, then under certain conditions more light will come to the observed than in the absence of this body. The gravitational field of a body is also capable of directing rays to be observed that would otherwise pass through the mismo observed. The gravitating body, like an ordinary lens, seems to focus the rays.

A gravitational lens is both similar and different from a regular light lens. In the lens itself, the deflection of the beam is less, the closer to the center of the lens. Therefore, at some points lying on the axis of the lens - at its focus - not only the ray located along this axis, but also all the rays passing through the lenses come to its focus. In the field of the gravitating center, the picture is different: the closer its path to the center, the greater the deviation of the beam. This can be seen from formula (2).

How are the quantities related to each other, the given relative positions of the source, the gravitational body and the observer? From figure 2 it is clear that

$$AS' = AS + SS'.$$

Since the relative position of the source, the gravitational body and the observer? From figure 2 it is clear that

$$AS' = L\beta, \quad AS = L\varphi, \quad SS' = l\alpha.$$

Substituting these three expressions into the first equality, we get:

$$L\beta = L\varphi + l\alpha.$$

Let us substitute $r=DC = (L-l)\beta$ into formula (2) and, taking this into account, rewrite the correspondence between distances and angles in the form

$$\beta^2 - \varphi\beta - \frac{l}{L} \frac{4GM}{c^2(L-l)} = 0.$$

This quadratic equation is called the equation of the gravitational lens created by the gravitational center.

Let's consider the possibility of solving this equation. First of all, turn to the case when the gravitational center lies strictly on the straight line $OS$ (i.e., the angle $\varphi$ is equal to zero). Then the solution to the equation has the form

$$\beta = \beta_0 = \pm 2 \left( \frac{l}{L} \frac{GM}{c^2(L-l)} \right)^{1/2}.$$

The picture has, very indo, axial symmetry, so that the observed appearance is not the point $S'$, but a ring obtained by rotating this point around the axis of symmetry - the straight line $OS$ (or the straight line $OA$ coinciding with it at $\varphi=0$); the angular radius of the ring is $\beta = \beta_0$.

If the gravity center does not lie on the straight line connecting the source and the point of observation, then there is no axial symmetry in the picture. Equation (3) at $\varphi \neq 0$ has two solutions:

$$\beta_{1,2} = \frac{1}{2} \varphi \pm \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} \varphi^2 + \frac{l}{L} \frac{4GM}{c^2(L-l)}}.$$

This means that the lens creates two images, the directions of which are given by the angles $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ (the first of these angles is positive and the second negative). The path of the corresponding rays is shown in figure 3. The minimum distances from the attracting center are different for two solutions:

$$r_1 = (L-l)\beta_1, \quad r_2 = (L-l)\beta_2.$$

Obviously, the angles of deflection are also different for them.

Several examples of doubling and tripling the images of cosmic bodies are now known. In all cases we are talking about quasars. These mirages undoubtedly create the effect of a gravitational lens.

Why is it that gravitational lenses are detected specifically by quasars and do not affect the light of stars or galaxies? The fact is that quasars, as we said, are visible from afar, on average from much greater distances than galaxies, not to mention stars. Therefore, the probability that light from them will meet on the way to the observed massive (but weak in brilliance) object is quite high. In addition, a quasar is a very small in apparent size, almost an exact source, and because of this, several separate images can appear from it, which will not blur and merge, as would be the case for extended sources - galaxies.

Moreover, luminous clouds and jets of matter emerge from this area from time to time. However, the appearance of a jet, which right “before everyone’s eyes” increases in size, and the bright spot at its end quickly moved away from the center of the quasar. How an illusion is born

The most important thing in the theory of relativity is the statement that light in emptiness propagates at a certain finite speed, which does not depend on whether the light source moves relative to us or accumulates. We will now use this fact to vaguely obtain formulas that describe our moving flashlight example. (And we only need to add that we will be talking about the propagation of light in emptiness in the absence of strong gravitational fields on its path. We said that gravitational fields are capable of bending the path of light; they are also capable of changing its speed. But if there are no very massive adjacent bodies between us and the source, the speed of light is a simple $c$; so we will assume.) Let the first of two signals be emitted by a flashlight at some moment, which we will choose as the beginning of the time recording ($t=0$). The distance from us (from the observer) to the source at this moment is $R$; This means that the signal will be received by us at time $t_0=R/c$.

Let, further, the second signal be emitted at the moment of time $t_1=s/v$, when the body, moving rectilinearly with a speed $v$, has passed a distance $s=S_1S_2$ (see Fig. 4). At moment $t_1$ the distance to the source is

$$R_1 = \frac{R - s \cos \vartheta}{\cos \alpha} \approx R - s \cos \vartheta$$

(we taught that the angle $\alpha$ is very small, since the distances $R$ and $R_1$ are much larger than the segment $s$, and $\cos \alpha \approx 1$). The second signal reaches us in time

$$\frac{R_1}{c} = \frac{1}{c} (R - s \cos \vartheta)$$

and will be accepted at the moment

$$t_2 = t_1 + \frac{R_1}{c} = \frac{s}{v} + \frac{R}{c} - \frac{s}{c} \cos \vartheta.$$

Time passed from receiving the first signal to receiving the second signal

$$\Delta t = t_2 - t_0 = \frac{s}{v} (1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta).$$

From here we can express the distance $s$ traveled by the flashlight in terms of the time interval $\Delta t$, the speed of movement $v$ and the angle $\vartheta$:

$$s = \frac{v \cdot \Delta t}{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta}.$$

The apparent movement of the body $\tilde{s}$ is represented by the projection of the segment $s$ onto the picture plane:

$$\tilde{s} = s \sin \vartheta = \frac{v \sin \vartheta}{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta} \Delta t$$

(we taught again here that the angle $\alpha$ is very small). To find the “apparent” speed $\tilde{s}$, you need to divide the apparent displacement $\tilde{s}$ by the time interval $\Delta t$:

$$\tilde{v} = \frac{\tilde{s}}{\Delta t} = \frac{v \sin \vartheta}{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta}.$$

Let us assume that $v = 0,8$ $c$ and $\cos \vartheta = 0,8$; then $\sin \vartheta = 0,6$, and $\tilde{v} = \frac{4}{3}$ $c$. At a higher, but sub-light, speed of real movement of the source, the speed of visible movement turned out to be superfree.
