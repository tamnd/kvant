The word “molecule” appeared almost 350 years ago. It appeared in the book of the French philosopher Pierre Gasendi, published in 1647. According to Gasendi, a molecule (“mass”, from the Latin “moles” - mass) is several atoms combined into one group. Differently united atoms, i.e., different molecules, are “bricks” from which nature builds various bodies. Through this M.V. Lomonsov, developing the doctrine of the structure of matter, wrote that molecules can be homogeneous (from identical atoms) and heterogeneous (from different ones), that molecules interact with each other and are in constant chaotic motion.

These provisions formed the basis of the molecular-kinetic theory of the structure of matter. Over time, they were confirmed by numerous experiments. We want to talk about some of them.

In 1911, the French physicist Dunuite conducted an experiment that showed that in their movement, gas molecules constantly collide with each other, and between collisions their movement is rectilinear.

Since the air has been pumped out of the tube, the sodium molecules in compartment II move practically without collisions and, once they hit the cold walls, settle on them. And only those molecules get into compartment III whose speed when leaving compartment I was directed along the straight line $O_1O_2$, and these molecules settle in compartment III on the wall of the tube strictly directing the responses $O_1$ and $O_2$.

The fact that molecules move in a otic manner would seem to indicate that the speeds of molecules can be very different and it is impossible to indicate either their direction or a certain value. However, in 1860, the English physicist Maxwell theoretically showed that there is an exact law that obeys the speed of random, thermal motion of molecules. According to this law - it is called the law of molecular velocity distribution (or simply Maxwell's distribution), at a given temperature there is a certain velocity value that characterizes the movement of most molecules of a given gaseous substance. And only a small fraction of molecules have speeds much greater or much less than this most probable speed. (In this case, it is assumed that all gas molecules are exactly the same and the temperature is constant throughout the entire volume.) The law of the distribution of molecules by speed allows us to determine the proportion of molecules whose speed lies close to the highest value. The closer this value is to the most probable speed, the greater the proportion of molecules moving at this speed.

Although the meaning of what has been said is clear, it must be borne in mind that it is pointless to be interested in the exact value of the speed of the molecule being determined or how many molecules have a specific speed. The fact is that when considering, say, a gas in a state, we are dealing with a huge number of objects (after all, in one cubic centimeter of air in a room there are approximately $2,5 \cdot 10^{19}$ molecules). And there is no way (and no need) to discuss the characteristics of each object - a molecule - separately. In a number of cases, to describe the behavior of such a huge number of parts, it is necessary to use the average values ​​of the quantities characterizing the movements of the part - say, average speed or average energy.

Knowing the speed at which cylinders 3 and 4 rotate, you can use the distance between tracks 5 and 6 to determine the time it takes the molecules to travel the distance from slot 2 to the surface of cylinder 4. And knowing this distance, you can determine the speed of the molecules.

It is important to note that the layer formed by the molecules is not of constant thickness. Near the middle the layer is thickest, and at the edges its thickness decreases (Fig. 5). Since the thickness of the middle layer is determined by the number of molecules illuminated on the surface of the cylinder, it can be used to judge the number of molecules impinging on the wall at one speed or another. After all, the fastest molecules fall close to track 5, and the slower ones settle further away from it. (Isn’t it true that the “profile” of the median plaque is similar to the Maxwell distribution curve?)

Stern performed the first experiment to determine the speed of molecules in 1920, and a quarter of a century later, the idea of ​​the experiment was used to experimentally test Maxwell’s law. In these experiments, the number of molecules whose velocities lie in a certain velocity range was measured. The measurements completely confirmed the maximum distribution law.

Perhaps the reader has a question: do the collisions of molecules with each other have no effect on the distribution? In Stern’s experiment, hot molecules flew without colliding with each other, but perhaps if the molecules did not hit the cold wall they were stuck to, but one as hot as themselves, and bounced off from it, then over time, as a result of collisions with each other, they would equalize their speeds?

Of course, collisions affect the speed of molecules. But the change in speed occurs in such a way that in any period of time the number of molecules whose speed decreases is equal to the number of molecules whose speed is noticeable, and the distribution of molecules by speed remains unchanged - the same as at figure 2. Unless, of course, the temperature of the gas is constant.

What if the gas heats up? Higher temperatures correspond to higher molecular speeds. If a higher temperature is established in the entire volume occupied by the gas, then the distribution curve will shift towards higher velocities. In this case, as is easy to understand, the area under the curve remains constant - after all, it corresponds to the total number of molecules in the volume.

And in conclusion, we invite you to think: what phenomena in life, in the surrounding nature, indicate that molecules move randomly, that between collisions their movement is rectilinear, and that the speeds of molecules at the same temperature are not the same.

There is only one hardware store in Monrovia, the capital of Liberia. When I told the dark-skinned clerk how many galls of paint I needed, he whistled in surprise:

- It’s as if you were planning to carve out a mountain, mister!
“No,” I assured him, “not a mountain, just an island.”

The clerk smiled. He thought I was being crazy, but I was really going to paint the target island in five colors: red, blue, green, yellow and purple.

Why did I come across this? To answer this question, I will have to go back several years.

The fact is that the topic of my doctoral dissertation was the problem of four colors. The four-color hypothesis states that for the correct coloring of any map, in which any two neighboring countries that share a common segment of the border will be painted in different colors (two countries are not considered neighboring if their borders have only one common point), four colors are enough. Countries on the map can be of any size and the most intricate shapes. Their number can also be arbitrary. The four-color hypothesis was first stated in 1860 by Mobius, and although better mathematical minds worked on its solution, this hypothesis could neither be proved nor disproved*).

Due to strange circumstances, the problem of four colors was solved for all surfaces except the sphere and the plane. In 1890, R. J. Heawood proved that seven colors are necessary and sufficient to color the surface of a torus (the surface of a donut), and in 1934, F. Franklin proved that six colors are sufficient to color maps on one-sided surfaces such as the Möbius strip and the Klein bottle.

On November 17, 1947, Stanislav Slapenarsky, a professor at the University of Vienna, who was giving a series of lectures at the University of Chicago, made a sensational announcement about his discovery of zero-sided surfaces**). This discovery had far-reaching consequences for the study of the properties of the Klein bottle and produced a genuine revolution in research on the problem of four colors.

Developing some of Slapenarsky’s ideas, I published my famous work in 1950, refuting Heawood’s “proof”, which believed that for the correct coloring of a map on a plane, a heel of colors is necessary and sufficient. According to the comprehensive conviction of topologists, four colors are enough to correctly color a plane or sphere.

Shortly after the publication of my work on the four-color problem, I happened to have breakfast at the university's Quadrangle Club with Professor Alma Busch. Alma is one of our leading anthropologists and undoubtedly the most beautiful woman in the entire university.

Alma has just returned from an expedition to a small island located several hundred miles off the coast of Liberia at the western edge of the African continent. She led a group of anthropology students who studied the rights and customs of the heel of the Lemen who inhabited the island.

“The island is divided into five areas,” Alma told me, placing a long black plastic mouthpiece on her sigret. “They all border each other.” This is important for understanding your rights there. The commonality of boundaries allows the elements to maintain a certain unity of culture. What's wrong with you, Marty? Why do you look so amazed?

I froze, and, without bringing the fork to my mouth, I slowly put it on the table.
