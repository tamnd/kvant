Introduction

We all literally come across the processes of evaporation and condensation at every step. And although a boiling kettle will immediately be imagined as large, of course, the most important example is the natural whirlpool of water, without which life on Earth would be at least different.

So, the solar body evaporates moisture at the earth's surface, convective points and diffusion allow parameters to reach high atmospheric layers. As you move upward, the air temperature drops, vapors condense and form clouds. The merging and enlargement of droplets inside the clouds is completed before jem - the cycle closes.

We will talk about one of the most important links in this chain - the formation of droplets when cooling water vapor, but first let us recall one important concept.

As you know, saturated vapors are those that are in equilibrium with the liquid phase. Each temperature $T$ corresponds to its specific value $p^0(T)$ of saturated vapor pressure (Fig. 1). Line $p^0(T)$ divides the plane $p, T$ into areas corresponding to the liquid and gaseous states.

Equilibrium between a liquid and a gas is an example of so-called dynamic equilibrium. Through the interface there is a constant exchange of molecules between the phases: there are, as it were, counter processes of evaporation and condensation. If the vapors are saturated, then the flows of particles in both directions are the same and the amount of substance in both phases is unchanged.

The concentration of saturated vapor is determined by the rate of evaporation per unit surface area of ​​the liquid, i.e., the density of saturated vapor is a measure of the volatility of a substance.

Now we can move on to the main topic of our article - the effect of formation on the equilibrium of the liquid and gas phases. It turns out that surface curvature leads to a change in saturated vapor pressure. Let's look at an example.

Let there be two identical almost spherical droplets under the cap. We already know that every immutability of such a system is deceptive. In fact, there is steam under the hood, and the molecules that leave one drop easily reach another. Is dynamic equilibrium stable in this case?

(here $\sigma$ is the surface tension coefficient, and $S = 4\pi r^2$ is the surface of a sphere with radius $r$). Not difficult

The idea of ​​this perpetuum mobile is this. A capillary made of non-wettable material is immersed in a vessel containing a highly volatile liquid. The liquid level in the capillary will be lower than in the main reservoir by amounts

$$h = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho g r},$$

where $\rho$ is the density of the liquid, $r$ is the radius of the capillary, $g$ is the free pressure acceleration. Liquid vapors directly above its surface have a pressure of $p^0(T)$, and above the menscus in the capillary their pressure will be greater by amounts

$$\Delta p = \rho^0 g h,$$

where $\rho^0$ is the density of saturated vapors (according to the Clapeyron-Mendleev equation, $\rho^0 = \frac{p^0(T) M}{RT}$).

According to the inventor's plan, excess vapor will result in increased condensation in the capillary, and as a result, a liquid cycle will arise in the system.

This reasoning contains the only weak point: the author did not take into account the effect that we discussed in the previous section. The law of conservation of energy states that there is no directed circulation in the system. We come to the conclusion that the equilibrium vapor pressure above the convex surface of the liquid is equal to

$$p_0(r) = p^0(T) + \Delta p = p(T) + \frac{2\sigma}{r} \frac{\varrho^0(T)}{\varrho_{\text{ж}}}.$$

(Note that the free pressure acceleration $g$ was not included in the final answer, although it was present in the intermediate expressions.)

Analysis of the eternal digger is a technique that allows us to achieve the goal in the shortest way. But the resulting formula has a universal character: for a given liquid, the vapor pressure depends not only on temperature, but also on the curvature of the surface.

Let the interface now have a concave shape. Having installed a wetted capitular in our perpetuum mobile, we immediately obtain a result in which the addition $\Delta p$ has the opposite sign:

$$p_0(r) = p^0(T) - \frac{2\sigma}{r} \frac{\varrho^0(T)}{\varrho_{\text{ж}}}.$$

For not too small radii of curvature, our correspondences are equivalent to the more accurate equation

$$p(r) = p^0(T) e^{\frac{2\sigma}{r} \frac{\varrho}{r} RT},$$

received in 1871 by Lord Kelvin. The radius of curvature is taken with a “+” sign for a convex surface and with a “-” sign for a concave surface.

You can find the value of the radius of curvature characteristic of a given liquid, at which the correction to the pressure is comparable to the pressure at the surface:

$$r_0(T) = \frac{2\sigma}{p^0(T)} \frac{\varrho^0}{\varrho_{\text{ж}}} = \frac{2\sigma M}{\varrho_{\text{ж}} RT} = \frac{2\sigma V_{\text{ж}}}{RT}$$

(here $V_{\text{ж}}$ is the molar volume of the liquid phase). The value of $r_0$ depends only on temperature, or a function of temperature is σ and $V_{\text{ж}}$.

The real significance of an imaginary surface

Another example of the influence of interfaces on the properties of a liquid-vapor system can be found where this interface has not yet formed, namely, in supersaturated vapor.

If saturated steam comes into contact with a liquid, cooling or rolling immediately leads to condensation. The states of such a system are described by the points p⁰(T) of the phase diagram (see Fig. 1). However, when there is no liquid phase, by isothermally compressing the vapors or by cooling them at a constant volume, it is possible to supersaturate them. The formula (*) allows you to better understand what this means.

Vapors whose pressure exceeds p⁰(T) will be saturated with respect to droplets of radius

$$r(p) = \frac{2\sigma}{p - p^0(T)} \frac{Q^0}{Q_{ж}} = \frac{p^0(T)}{p - p^0(T)} r_0(T).$$

Such drops are called critical nuclei. For example, that smaller droplets will readily evaporate.

The number of molecules inside the embryo is

$$N(r) = \left( \frac{p^0}{p - p^0} \right)^3 N(r_0).$$

If the degree of supersaturation of water vapor is 10% ($\frac{p - p^0}{p^0} = 0,1$), then there are $N(r) \sim 10^5$ molecules. In the gas phase, the same number of molecules occupies a volume several thousand larger than in the liquid phase. It is easy to see that the formation of a critical nucleus here is very difficult, since it is unlikely that the vapor molecules will suddenly want to be “consumed” so much. Therefore, supersaturated steam can “get stuck” in its energetically unusable, so-called metastable state for quite a long time.

Conclusion, but not the end

Before we put an end to it, let's clarify one more question. So far we have discussed how droplets begin to form. But when does it end? Why are all the dewdrops so similar in the morning? What stops their growth? After all, we are convinced that the larger the radius of the drop, the more willingly it “drinks.” Apparently there is some additional factor that determines the maximum size.

You don't need to look too far. The fact is that we have not yet taken into account the effect of gravity on dewdrops. However, the solid shape of large drops seems to hint that this is not always acceptable.

Let's try to estimate to what extent gravitational energy can be neglected. For a spherical drop it is equal to

$$E_g = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \varrho g$$

and, being proportional to $r^4$, must inevitably exceed the energy surfaces $E_s = 4 \pi r^2 \sigma$ as $r$ increases.

To find the limiting value of the radius, let’s equate the energy of a dewdrop with the total energy of two “half” drops into which it could break up:

$$E(1) = 4 \pi r^2 \sigma + \frac{4}{3} \pi r^4 \varrho g = 2 E\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)$$

($r/\sqrt[3]{2}$ is the radius of a “half” drop). It follows that

$$r_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{3^3 \sqrt{2} \sigma}{\varrho g}} \approx 0.5 \text{ см.}$$

At first glance, this is a lot of auto for dewdrops. But, of course, we did not teach all the factors. In particular, the size of a drop depends on the surface on which it is formed and how wet this surface is with water. And, besides, drops that are too large simply cannot stay on the leaves and blades of grass and roll off them. However, in terms of the order of magnitude, the given estimate is completely reliable.
