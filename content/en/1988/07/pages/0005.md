Soon Jacob found himself in the town of Iveren near Bern, in a boarding school run by Pestaloshi, where Pestaloshi accepted him for free: after all, Steiner had nothing to pay for his studies, food, or accommodation.

Steiner spent 4 years with Pestaloshi. First he studied at school, and then he taught mathematics there. However, Pestalosi quickly realized that Steiner's talent deserved better use. Following Pestaloshi's insistent advice, Steiner moved to Germany in 1818, to the city of Heidelberg, the largest university center closest to Iveren.

Pestalosi hoped that Steiner would graduate from Heidelberg University and then choose his own path; however, these hopes were not fully realized. Pestaloshi gave Steiner a certain amount of money for travel and for the first establishment in a foreign city. But then Steiner had to provide himself with his daily bread. Steiner did not have any profession other than that of a mathematics teacher. He was forced to overload himself with partial lessons, for which he was paid little: after all, he did not have any service education. These lessons, deeply hated by Steiner, did not give him the opportunity to systematically attend university. During his three years in Heidelberg, he managed to take only a few university courses (which, however, was also quite a lot, given his level of preparation). In addition, in Heidelberg, Steiner acquired his first mathematical acquaintances, or the Swiss’s outstanding talents immediately caught the eye.

In 1821, friends informed Steiner about an opening in Berlin for a vacancy as a mathematics teacher in one of the capital's gymnasiums, and he immediately set off on his journey. The position of a gymnasium teacher would give him a regular income. But to take up his position, he still had to pass exams, and their results could not particularly inspire the leadership of the gymnasium.

First of all, the candidate for the position of teacher was asked if he was familiar with the gymnasium course. Steiner answered this question briefly: “No, I don’t know.” What else could he say? After all, in the Russian gymnasium they studied Latin and ancient Greek, which the son of a Swiss peasant was no more familiar with than you and I, my reader. But things weren’t going too well with the math exam either: Steiner showed extensive and deep knowledge in geometry, but more than modest knowledge in algebra and trigonometry, and he had serious problems in the field of mathematical analysis. However, the young Swiss's conspicuously outstanding geometric abilities and the flattering characteristics he presented did their job: Steiner was allowed to teach mathematics for two years in all grades except graduation. During this time, he had to pass exams for the gymnasium course (Steiner never took them) and an additional exam in mathematics (Steiner took them much later).

In high school, Steiner tried for 14 years. This became possible only after the opening of a real school in Berlin, in which the number of hours was increased in mathematics and natural sciences, but ancient languages ​​were not taught (and therefore, their knowledge was not required from the school teachers). However, despite the fact that the organizer and director of the school was also a student of Pestalosi, Steiner at first was listed only as an assistant teacher.
