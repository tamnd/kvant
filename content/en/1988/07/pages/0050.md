Although the meaning of what has been said is clear, it must be borne in mind that it is pointless to be interested in the exact value of the speed of the molecule being determined or how many molecules have a specific speed. The fact is that when considering, say, a gas in a state, we are dealing with a huge number of objects (after all, in one cubic centimeter of air in a room there are approximately $2,5 \cdot 10^{19}$ molecules). And there is no way (and no need) to discuss the characteristics of each object - a molecule - separately. In a number of cases, to describe the behavior of such a huge number of parts, it is necessary to use the average values ​​of the quantities characterizing the movements of the part - say, average speed or average energy.

An experiment to determine the speed of movement of gas molecules was first carried out only at the beginning of the 20th century by the German physicist Stern. The experimental diagram is presented in figure 3. When thin silver-plated platinum wire 1 is heated (the wire glows when an electric current passes through it), silver molecules evaporate from its surface. Some of the molecules, through a narrow gap 2 in the thin cylinder 3 surrounding the wiring, enter inside the large cylinder 4 surrounding the wiring and the small cylinder. The air has been pumped out of the large cylinder, so the silver molecules move there practically without collisions and, when they hit the cylinder wall, settle on it (the surface temperature of the large cylinder is quite low). The settling molecules form a narrow strip of plaque 5. If the entire system accumulates, then this milk is formed just directing itself 2, creating, as it were, its image. But if both cylinders (3 and 4) rotate around a common axis, passing through wire 1, then the plaque deposit will no longer form a guide 2 itself.
