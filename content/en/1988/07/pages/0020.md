⟦folio 18⟧

A gravitational line can not only frame an image, but also create two images of the same object. Let's see how this happens.

figure 2 shows the course of rays propagating from the source (point $S$) to the observed one (point $O$) mismo of the gravitational center (point $D$). The beam coming from the source passes at a minimum distance $r=DC$ from the gravitational center, is deflected by it at an angle $\alpha$ and arrives at the observation point. (For simplicity, a simple example is drawn along with the rounded curve at point $C$ of the observing merger.) The observed view of the source is not at point $S$ (in the direction $OS$), but in the direction of the ray $OC$ - for example $OS'$. (Angles $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\varphi$ at figure should be considered small.)

How are the quantities related to each other, the given relative positions of the source, the gravitational body and the observer? From figure 2 it is clear that

$$AS' = AS + SS'.$$

Since the relative position of the source, the gravitational body and the observer? From figure 2 it is clear that

$$AS' = L\beta, \quad AS = L\varphi, \quad SS' = l\alpha.$$

Substituting these three expressions into the first equality, we get:

$$L\beta = L\varphi + l\alpha.$$

Let us substitute $r=DC = (L-l)\beta$ into formula (2) and, taking this into account, rewrite the correspondence between distances and angles in the form

$$\beta^2 - \varphi\beta - \frac{l}{L} \frac{4GM}{c^2(L-l)} = 0.$$

This quadratic equation is called the equation of the gravitational lens created by the gravitational center.

Let's consider the possibility of solving this equation. First of all, turn to the case when the gravitational center lies strictly on the straight line $OS$ (i.e., the angle $\varphi$ is equal to zero). Then the solution to the equation has the form

$$\beta = \beta_0 = \pm 2 \left( \frac{l}{L} \frac{GM}{c^2(L-l)} \right)^{1/2}.$$

The picture has, very indo, axial symmetry, so that the observed appearance is not the point $S'$, but a ring obtained by rotating this point around the axis of symmetry - the straight line $OS$ (or the straight line $OA$ coinciding with it at $\varphi=0$); the angular radius of the ring is $\beta = \beta_0$.

If the gravity center does not lie on the straight line connecting the source and the point of observation, then there is no axial symmetry in the picture. Equation (3) at $\varphi \neq 0$ has two solutions:

$$\beta_{1,2} = \frac{1}{2} \varphi \pm \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} \varphi^2 + \frac{l}{L} \frac{4GM}{c^2(L-l)}}.$$

This means that the lens creates two images, the directions of which are given by the angles $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ (the first of these angles is positive and the second negative). The path of the corresponding rays is shown in figure 3. The minimum distances from the attracting center are different for two solutions:

$$r_1 = (L-l)\beta_1, \quad r_2 = (L-l)\beta_2.$$

Obviously, the angles of deflection are also different for them.
