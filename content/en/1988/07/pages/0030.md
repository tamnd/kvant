The real significance of an imaginary surface

Another example of the influence of interfaces on the properties of a liquid-vapor system can be found where this interface has not yet formed, namely, in supersaturated vapor.

If saturated steam comes into contact with a liquid, cooling or rolling immediately leads to condensation. The states of such a system are described by the points p⁰(T) of the phase diagram (see Fig. 1). However, when there is no liquid phase, by isothermally compressing the vapors or by cooling them at a constant volume, it is possible to supersaturate them. The formula (*) allows you to better understand what this means.

Vapors whose pressure exceeds p⁰(T) will be saturated with respect to droplets of radius

$$r(p) = \frac{2\sigma}{p - p^0(T)} \frac{Q^0}{Q_{ж}} = \frac{p^0(T)}{p - p^0(T)} r_0(T).$$

Such drops are called critical nuclei. For example, that smaller droplets will readily evaporate.

The number of molecules inside the embryo is

$$N(r) = \left( \frac{p^0}{p - p^0} \right)^3 N(r_0).$$

If the degree of supersaturation of water vapor is 10% ($\frac{p - p^0}{p^0} = 0,1$), then there are $N(r) \sim 10^5$ molecules. In the gas phase, the same number of molecules occupies a volume several thousand larger than in the liquid phase. It is easy to see that the formation of a critical nucleus here is very difficult, since it is unlikely that the vapor molecules will suddenly want to be “consumed” so much. Therefore, supersaturated steam can “get stuck” in its energetically unusable, so-called metastable state for quite a long time.
