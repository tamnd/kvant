Its direct proof could be the discovery of the gravitational center itself in the right place on the celestial sphere - between two images of the quasar, but not at strictly equal distances from them. And this is a real success to do. The gravitational center turns out to be a galaxy of faint brilliance, located between nearby quasars and, moreover, somewhat closer to one of them than to the other. The distance to it turned out to be approximately equal to half the distance to the quasar. Further observational studies showed that the real picture is somewhat more complex. They found out that one of the images of twins is actually two very close images that merged in previous photographs. It is not difficult to prove that a gravitational image can arise in the case when the deflecting gravitational field is created not by an exact center, but by a volumetric body lying on the line of sight. The galaxy serves as such a body. Rays of light from a quasar can pass not only at the edge of the galaxy, but also through it. The gravitational mass, which determines the deflections of the rays, is less for light passing near the center of the galaxy, and more for light passing near its edge. The angle of deflection depends on the distribution of masses in the galaxy. The lens equation will now be more complex than the square one, and this creates great variety in the possible variations of the visible picture.

Several examples of doubling and tripling the images of cosmic bodies are now known. In all cases we are talking about quasars. These mirages undoubtedly create the effect of a gravitational lens.

Why is it that gravitational lenses are detected specifically by quasars and do not affect the light of stars or galaxies? The fact is that quasars, as we said, are visible from afar, on average from much greater distances than galaxies, not to mention stars. Therefore, the probability that light from them will meet on the way to the observed massive (but weak in brilliance) object is quite high. In addition, a quasar is a very small in apparent size, almost an exact source, and because of this, several separate images can appear from it, which will not blur and merge, as would be the case for extended sources - galaxies.

Faster than light?

This is, one might say, an exemplary quasar; it has all the typical properties of quasars and is also convenient for observation. In its very center, the area where the image is distributed, some violent processes are constantly taking place, which are manifested in changes in brightness. Moreover, luminous clouds and jets of matter emerge from this area from time to time. However, the appearance of a jet, which right “before everyone’s eyes” increases in size, and the bright spot at its end quickly moved away from the center of the quasar.
