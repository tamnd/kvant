Gravity lens

Rays, as is known, propagate rectilinearly if there is no pre-filling. The space environment is so rarefied that it practically does not cause pre-filling. And yet the paths of light can be bent. Such curvature is created by the gravitational fields of celestial bodies.

There's nothing particularly surprising about this. Even in Newton's theory of gravity, it was taken for granted that a ray of light, considered as a stream of particles - light corpuscles moving at the speed of light, deviates from a straight path when it passes close to the gravity of a body. Particles of light, like all particles and bodies of the Universe, experience attraction to a gravitating body.

Considerations about the bending of a ray of light by a gravitational field remain valid in the relativistic theory of gravity, which is called the general theory of relativity. According to this theory, the angle of deflection of the beam passing at a minimum distance $r$ from a body of mass $M$ is equal to (see Fig. 1)

$$\alpha = \frac{4GM}{c^2r}.$$ (2)

At the suggestion of A. Einstein, the first attempts to observe this effect were made for starlight passing near the Sun. Measurements can be taken during a total solar eclipse, when the Moon covers the solar disk. Many observations have been made since the total solar eclipse of 1919, with results that are in perfect agreement with the prediction of general relativity.

In 1936, Einstein published a short note in which he predicted a new effect associated with the bending of light in a gravitational field - the gravitational lensing effect. If there is a gravitating body on the path of light from some distant source to the observed, then under certain conditions more light will come to the observed than in the absence of this body. The gravitational field of a body is also capable of directing rays to be observed that would otherwise pass through the mismo observed. The gravitating body, like an ordinary lens, seems to focus the rays.

A gravitational lens is both similar and different from a regular light lens. In the lens itself, the deflection of the beam is less, the closer to the center of the lens. Therefore, at some points lying on the axis of the lens - at its focus - not only the ray located along this axis, but also all the rays passing through the lenses come to its focus. In the field of the gravitating center, the picture is different: the closer its path to the center, the greater the deviation of the beam. This can be seen from formula (2).
