The most powerful emitters in the Universe

Each quasar is a bright “lantern” that shines not only in the visible optical range of electromagnetic waves, but also in all other ranges - from radio waves to gamma rays. The size of the emitting region is comparable to the size of the Solar System. But in the Solar System there is only one star - the Sun, and a quasar in the same volume contains an energy generator thousands of times more powerful than the entire Galaxy with its billions of stars. How does this generator work? This is the most important question in the physics of quasars, to which it is still possible to find an answer.

Quasars are different in such data of the Universe, where neither stars nor entire galaxies can be smoothed out even with the help of the largest telecopes. The most distant quasar currently known is located at a distance of 13 billion light years from us. This means that the light from it takes 13 billion years to reach us and, therefore, we see it as it was 13 billion years ago. At that time, neither the Earth nor the Sun existed (according to modern estimates, the age of the Solar System is about 5 billion years).

The distances to quasars and other distant cosmic bodies are judged by the spectra of their radiation. In the spectra of quasars, emission lines of ordinary atoms are visible - such as hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, etc. - but they are all shifted towards the red end of the spectrum, that is, in the region of long wavelengths. This shift - the famous red shift - is due to the fact that the sources of radiation are moving away from us. The extension itself is a manifestation of the general distribution of the Universe. According to the laws of cosmology, the farther a quasar or galaxy is from us, the greater the escape velocity.

If a light source emits waves with a period of $T_0$ and at the same time moves relative to us with a speed of $v$, then the waves of light visible to us will have a period

$$T = T_0 \frac{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta}{\sqrt{1 - (v/c)^2}}.$$  (1)

Here $c$ is the speed of light, $\vartheta$ is the angle between the direction of speed and the line of sight (the prime that connects us and the source; when the source moves directly towards us, $\vartheta = 0$, when the line is straight away from us - $\vartheta = \pi$). For the most distant quasars, the period $T$ is 4-5 times longer than the period $T_0$. The change in period, and with it the frequency and wavelength of light due to the relative motion of the source and receiver, is called the Doppler effect. It creates a red shift in the spectra of expanding galaxies and quasars.

More than 2,500 quasars are now known. They are scattered across the celestial sphere more or less evenly. On average, the distance between them, if you change it along an arc in the sky, is several degrees (to be clear, let’s say that the angular diameters of Lunya and the Sun are about 0.5 degrees).

But two quasars very close to each other have long been known, which are also so similar to each other that they are called twin quasars. They are only six arcseconds apart, which is thousands of times less than the average distance between quasars; their brightness is practically the same; the spectra of these quasars are the same - in general cases the same emission lines with the same intensity are visible.
