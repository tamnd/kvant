How an illusion is born

The most important thing in the theory of relativity is the statement that light in emptiness propagates at a certain finite speed, which does not depend on whether the light source moves relative to us or accumulates. We will now use this fact to vaguely obtain formulas that describe our moving flashlight example. (And we only need to add that we will be talking about the propagation of light in emptiness in the absence of strong gravitational fields on its path. We said that gravitational fields are capable of bending the path of light; they are also capable of changing its speed. But if there are no very massive adjacent bodies between us and the source, the speed of light is a simple $c$; so we will assume.) Let the first of two signals be emitted by a flashlight at some moment, which we will choose as the beginning of the time recording ($t=0$). The distance from us (from the observer) to the source at this moment is $R$; This means that the signal will be received by us at time $t_0=R/c$.

Let, further, the second signal be emitted at the moment of time $t_1=s/v$, when the body, moving rectilinearly with a speed $v$, has passed a distance $s=S_1S_2$ (see Fig. 4). At moment $t_1$ the distance to the source is

$$R_1 = \frac{R - s \cos \vartheta}{\cos \alpha} \approx R - s \cos \vartheta$$

(we taught that the angle $\alpha$ is very small, since the distances $R$ and $R_1$ are much larger than the segment $s$, and $\cos \alpha \approx 1$). The second signal reaches us in time

$$\frac{R_1}{c} = \frac{1}{c} (R - s \cos \vartheta)$$

and will be accepted at the moment

$$t_2 = t_1 + \frac{R_1}{c} = \frac{s}{v} + \frac{R}{c} - \frac{s}{c} \cos \vartheta.$$

Time passed from receiving the first signal to receiving the second signal

$$\Delta t = t_2 - t_0 = \frac{s}{v} (1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta).$$

From here we can express the distance $s$ traveled by the flashlight in terms of the time interval $\Delta t$, the speed of movement $v$ and the angle $\vartheta$:

$$s = \frac{v \cdot \Delta t}{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta}.$$

The apparent movement of the body $\tilde{s}$ is represented by the projection of the segment $s$ onto the picture plane:

$$\tilde{s} = s \sin \vartheta = \frac{v \sin \vartheta}{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta} \Delta t$$

(we taught again here that the angle $\alpha$ is very small). To find the “apparent” speed $\tilde{s}$, you need to divide the apparent displacement $\tilde{s}$ by the time interval $\Delta t$:

$$\tilde{v} = \frac{\tilde{s}}{\Delta t} = \frac{v \sin \vartheta}{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \vartheta}.$$

Let us assume that $v = 0,8$ $c$ and $\cos \vartheta = 0,8$; then $\sin \vartheta = 0,6$, and $\tilde{v} = \frac{4}{3}$ $c$. At a higher, but sub-light, speed of real movement of the source, the speed of visible movement turned out to be superfree.
