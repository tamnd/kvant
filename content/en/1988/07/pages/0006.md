As a result, by selecting problems for strong schoolchildren, Steiner acquired a lifelong interest in elementary geometry. Let us consider several of Steyer's results in this area. Let's start with problems coming from the great Euler.

Euler established that the three midpoints of the sides of an arbitrary triangle, the three bases of its altitudes and the three points bisecting the segments of the altitudes from the point of their intersection (the orthocenter of the triangle) to the vertices belong to one circle. This circle is called Euler's circle or the circle of nine points of a triangle. It is remarkable that for each triangle its Euler circle touches the incircle and three outer circles (Fig. 1). This fact is often called Feuerbach's theorem after the person who first proved it; but few people know that Steiner, who was unfamiliar with Feuerbach’s result, proved this theorem just two years after its discoverer and immediately published his result (so many mathematicians became acquainted with it through the publication of Steiner, not Feuerbach).
