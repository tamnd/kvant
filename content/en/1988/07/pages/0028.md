Yes, there is such a mechanism. We will show that the rate of evaporation depends on the shape of the evaporating surface. On a surface with greater curvature (near a small drop), this process is more intense. In turn, the counter flow of molecules (condensation) is determined only by the vapor density under the hood and is the same for both droplets. Therefore, if the radius of the droplets is different, the droplets cannot be in equilibrium with the vapor at the same time. In this case, the larger drop turns out to be more stable and can gradually absorb the smaller one without even touching it. Such “feeding” at a distance could be called telekinibalism.

But it turns out to be useful to figure out exactly where the inventor made a mistake, what specific physical principle he did not take into account. Methodologically, this is reminiscent of proofs by contradiction, generally accepted in mathematics.

The idea of ​​this perpetuum mobile is this. A capillary made of non-wettable material is immersed in a vessel containing a highly volatile liquid. The liquid level in the capillary will be lower than in the main reservoir by amounts

$$h = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho g r},$$

where $\rho$ is the density of the liquid, $r$ is the radius of the capillary, $g$ is the free pressure acceleration. Liquid vapors directly above its surface have a pressure of $p^0(T)$, and above the menscus in the capillary their pressure will be greater by amounts

$$\Delta p = \rho^0 g h,$$

where $\rho^0$ is the density of saturated vapors (according to the Clapeyron-Mendleev equation, $\rho^0 = \frac{p^0(T) M}{RT}$).

According to the inventor's plan, excess vapor will result in increased condensation in the capillary, and as a result, a liquid cycle will arise in the system.

This reasoning contains the only weak point: the author did not take into account the effect that we discussed in the previous section.
