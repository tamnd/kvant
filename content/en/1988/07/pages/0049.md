⟦folio 47⟧

The word “molecule” appeared almost 350 years ago. It appeared in the book of the French philosopher Pierre Gasendi, published in 1647. According to Gasendi, a molecule (“mass”, from the Latin “moles” - mass) is several atoms combined into one group. Differently united atoms, i.e., different molecules, are “bricks” from which nature builds various bodies. Through this M.V. Lomonsov, developing the doctrine of the structure of matter, wrote that molecules can be homogeneous (from identical atoms) and heterogeneous (from different ones), that molecules interact with each other and are in constant chaotic motion.

These provisions formed the basis of the molecular-kinetic theory of the structure of matter. Over time, they were confirmed by numerous experiments. We want to talk about some of them.

In 1911, the French physicist Dunuite conducted an experiment that showed that in their movement, gas molecules constantly collide with each other, and between collisions their movement is rectilinear.

The experimental diagram is shown in figure 1.

Fig. 1.

air. The tube is divided by two partitions (1 and 2) into three compartments. The partitions have holes ($O_1$ and $O_2$) located on the axis of the tube. A piece of sodium metal is placed in compartment I. This part of the tube is heated. Sodium begins to evaporate, sodium vapor fills compartment I and through holes $O_1$ and $O_2$ penetrates into compartments II and III. Sodium plaque forms on the stacks of the tube, while in compartment II it covers all the walls except partition 1, and in compartment III it covers only a small area with responses $O_2$.

The results of this experiment are explained quite clearly. Molecules whose velocities have different directions enter compartment II through hole $O_1$. Since the air has been pumped out of the tube, the sodium molecules in compartment II move practically without collisions and, once they hit the cold walls, settle on them. And only those molecules get into compartment III whose speed when leaving compartment I was directed along the straight line $O_1O_2$, and these molecules settle in compartment III on the wall of the tube strictly directing the responses $O_1$ and $O_2$.

The fact that molecules move in a otic manner would seem to indicate that the speeds of molecules can be very different and it is impossible to indicate either their direction or a certain value. However, in 1860, the English physicist Maxwell theoretically showed that there is an exact law that obeys the speed of random, thermal motion of molecules. According to this law - it is called the law of molecular velocity distribution (or simply Maxwell's distribution), at a given temperature there is a certain velocity value that characterizes the movement of most molecules of a given gaseous substance. And only a small fraction of molecules have speeds much greater or much less than this most probable speed. (In this case, it is assumed that all gas molecules are exactly the same and the temperature is constant throughout the entire volume.) The law of the distribution of molecules by speed allows us to determine the proportion of molecules whose speed lies close to the highest value. The closer this value is to the most probable speed, the greater the proportion of molecules moving at this speed.
