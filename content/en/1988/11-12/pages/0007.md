The chief mathematician, the greatest, is Kolmogorov. And then I participated in the Moscow optimization in mathematics (most likely it was the spring of 1946, when I was in the 8th grade), and among those who welcomed the optimization students was Kolmogorov. A young-looking man stepped onto the podium. I was very impressed: both the manner of speaking guasavo, tilting my head to the side to the shoulder, and the text of the speech, which began with the phrase: “The President of the Moscow Mathematical Society, Pavel Sergeevich Aleksadrov, instructed me to welcome the participants in mathematical optimization.” If the greatest mathematician is Kolmogorov, then how great is the one who could entrust something to Kolmogorov himself?!

I met Andrei Nikolaevich when I was in my third year. He taught a special course on the mechanism of Moscow State University “Measure Theory”. They took this special course mainly for satisfaction, since there was no need to pass this course. However, during a break between lectures, I approached him. I can’t remember this: A.N. made some statements that I refuted. This impressed him, and he invited me to come to his dacha, asking if I was only ready to talk about mathematics or if I could also ride lies. That's how we met him.

I don’t even want to remember about kaataniya, because the lies simply didn’t stick to me. Before that, I skated only in felt boots, threading the toe of the felt boot into the loop, and I had never seen any other fastenings and did not know that such existed. Andrei Nikolaevich gave me boots, boots, and bindings (hard or semi-hard, I don’t remember now). The lies kept falling off me, and every time Academician Kolmogorov stood on his knees in the snow, straightening the fastenings of my lies. The first lies left me completely downhill, we returned and put on others. Most likely, I was not to blame for this, because with proper fastenings the lies should not have left. This all happened on January 22, 1950. From that time on I became a student of A.N.

Working with A.N. was very easy. All the time I felt that he was a man of genius. In my life there were three brilliant people with whom I had the opportunity to communicate: B. L. Pasternak, A. A. Zalizyank and A. N. Kolmogorov. A.N. had his own special style. In the winter of 1950, for the first time he took me to his dacha, and not without some ceremonies, he made me his student. Then he assigned the topic of my classes. He said that there are such special functions, they are called resource functions, and he almost never knows anything about them in our country. He gave me several shades of articles from foreign magazines and told me to look into it. And he said: “As a last resort, if nothing comes of you, he will give us competent abstracts.” At the same time, he gave me several copies of his works, one of which had a dedicatory inscription. This is a very early and unique feature from the 20s that I treasure.

It was very difficult to say how A.N.’s genius was manifested. But I can name two of its distinctive features. First. As a graduate student, I met with A.N. weekly. And every time, at every subsequent meeting, he remembered why we parted, and in general all my work (everything I had done before) he knew better and more accurately than I did. Although I only did this, and he, as you know, did many others. Second feature. G. I. Barenblatt often went to Komarovku at the same time as me, with whom A. N. studied hydrodynamics (and with me - mathematical logic). Barenblatt and I were indelibly impressed by the ease with which A.N. moved from hydrodynamics to mathematical logic and back. It was completely natural for him. And it seemed like a miracle to us.
