LIGHTNING IN CRYSTAL

Doctor of Technical Sciences
Y. R. NOSOV

Ask an electrical food specialist - an engineer, a scientist, a company manager: what is the most promising and promising in this area today? Eight out of ten will answer: “Optoelectronics!”

The long-standing idea of ​​​​using in computer science not only electrical signals (as offered by traditional microelectronics), but also light and optical signals - this idea turned out to be very fruitful. We found out that thanks to the merging of electronics with optics, the capabilities of computing and communication devices will increase immeasurably: their performance will increase hundreds and thousands of times, the devices will become more reliable, noise-resistant, and miniature.

All this was understood back in the sixties. So why, when the reader asks for the first time, does so much still remain just a promise? Here's the thing. In order to “bring light” to work, you need to learn to handle it as freely and simply as with an electric current. Learn to amplify and convert light signals, seamlessly transmit them from point to point, record and save. But first of all, you need to learn how to generate them. Whatever may be said about the importance of all elements of an optoelectronic system, it is the source of light signals, the emitter, that is the fundamental principle, the “alpha and omega” of optoelectronics. And of course, you need not just any emitter, such as, for example, a lighting lamp, but one that, in terms of miniaturization, efficiency, and durability, is comparable to a transistor and integrated circuits.

Obviously, a solution to the emitter problem could only be found in the field of semiconductors.

Let's fast forward thirty years ago. In those years, semiconductor science worked to meet the needs of transistor technology - the great hopes of electronics were connected with these devices. The first transistors were made from thermium, but it was clear that better results could be achieved with silicon and the then new semiconductor gallium arsenide, a two-component compound of arsenic and gallium (GaAs). The “silicon” direction quickly achieved success and since 1960 has become the basis not only of transistor technology, but also of microelectronics.

But they stubbornly refused to get transistors from Arsend Gall. And they were ready to write off the millions of dollars spent on creating perfect GaAs single crystals as a waste, justifying themselves by saying that “science requires sacrifices,” but... It is not always known where you will find it and where you will lose it. Suddenly, even in this “dead end” direction, hope began to shine, and it began to shine in the literal sense of the word.

In 1956, it was discovered that when an electric current passes through an arsenide-halide diode, it begins to emit! *) This is how LEDs appeared. Physicists and engineers have literally pounced on the interside effect. It was immediately established that the semiconductor crystal in the LED does not heat up - which means that luminescence, long known to scientists as “cold glow,” is responsible for the radiation.
