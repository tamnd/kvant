⟦folio 17⟧

And when theorists calculated quite accurately what the quantum structure of the original crystal should be, then the circle of semiconductors “suspected” of possible involvement in the laser effect narrowed sharply. The fate of the “butterfly” was predetermined... On the eve of 1963, almost simultaneously, the first semiconductor lasers were created in laboratories in the USA and USSR.

As in the experiments with LEDs, a $p—n$ junction was fabricated in an Arsend Gall wafer (again this semiconductor was a “pinoer”); the only difference was that during doping, much larger impurities were introduced, as a result of which more free electrons were formed. Then, pressing on the plate with the tip of a lancet, tiny square crystals were poked out of it. The plate is cut in order along crystallographic planes, so the lateral opposite faces of the crystal turn out to be plane-parallel and mirror-like; these two mirrors form the resonator necessary for lasing. Then the crystal of the lower face was attacked on a massive copper substrate (for a heat sink), a second, thinner electrode was attached to the upper face - the structure was ready for research.

When current was passed, the crystal began to generate infrared radiation, and, as befits an LED, weakly and in all directions. But as soon as the current was increased to a certain value (it was called the threshold current), the picture changed significantly - the radiation power increased sharply, and intense light escaped from the side faces from those places (strips) where the plane of the $p—n$ transition intersects the faces of the resonator. Studies of the radiation spectrum revealed that the band of generated waves had sharply compressed. There was no doubt left - it was a laser!

We quickly understood the mechanism of its operation. Just like in an LED, the applied extractor “drives” the electron onto the “energy slide”, only

The “hill” part is higher, and the number of electrons is much greater. “Pumped up” electrons collide near the $p—n$ transition (here an active zone is formed) and, “falling down the hill,” generate radiation quanta. But this is where the analogy with the LED ends. The light wave, propagating along the plane of the $p—n$-transition, is reflected from the mirror faces and, repeatedly passing through the active zone, forces the electrons to “fall off the hill”. It turns out that a huge number of electrons simultaneously and in strictly the same way perform the quantum transition prescribed to them (at figure 1 this transition is indicated by a black arrow). Therefore, the beam has a high degree of monochromaticity, as well as a complete definition of polarization. According to the mechanism of its occurrence, such radiation is called forced, stimulated, induced (these are all synonyms), while the radiation of an LED is spontaneous (random in direction and polarization and, to some extent, in wavelength).

They started talking about a new device. It seemed very quickly that big changes were about to begin in optoelectronics. But time passed, and the newly born laser did not find any real application in any equipment. And soon the effect was surprised by its size and went out of order. It also turned out that in terms of the degree of monochromaticity it was only 10-20 times better than an LED ($\Delta \lambda/\lambda \approx 0,005$ versus 0.05 for an LED) and thousands of times worse than a gas laser.
