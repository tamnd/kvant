Having accepted me into graduate school, Kolmogorov assigned me two difficult exams in algebra and the equations of mathematical physics and announced that the third exam should still be closer to logic. And he told me to take the theory of relay circuits. There was almost no mathematical literature on relay contact circuits at that time, but only technical literature. Therefore, preparing for this exam was the most difficult for me. I remember that there were two places where I couldn’t understand how a triode worked. And only after learning everything, I realized that I didn’t need to understand it. In addition to the three exams, graduate reports were also required. As one of them, A.N. gave me for translation the book of the Hungarian mathematician Rosa Peter, “Recursive Functions,” that had just been published in German. I didn’t know German, but I didn’t dare tell Kolmogorov about it. I got to work and... translated. The book turned out to be unimportant, but the preface that A.N. wrote was brilliant.

I have two works jointly with A.N., there is also a work on which only my last name appears, but which is completely copied by A.N. This is an article in the “Reports of the Academy of Sciences” about the proof of Gödel’s theorem. When A.N. induced a certain result in his student, which in fact was almost suggested to him, he created such an environment as if the student had figured it out himself, and suggested writing an article in a magazine about the result. This kind of psychological support for his younger partner is a very significant aspect of his activity. Whether it was instinctive or whether he was thinking about it, I don’t know.

Unfortunately, I probably cannot consider myself a full-fledged student of A.N., because I did not possess either of the two qualities necessary for this: I did not understand anything about music and did not play sports. However, he did not send me away. And sometimes he talked to me about art, it’s time

You hate me with your mischief in everything. Who today, for example, knows Dufresne’s chess textbook? Nobody. This is a famous textbook from the end of the last century. I had this textbook at home (I got it from my father), so I read it. And when A.N. and I started talking about chess, the first word he uttered was “Dufresne,” and he told me the contents of the textbook.

Another such case. For me it was necessary that on the coat of arms the right and left sides are considered not that we see rights and words, but vice versa. (This is because the coat of arms was drawn on a shield and viewed from the point of view of the one who is not counting.) I had barely learned this when A.N. mentioned it for some reason.

Let us remember ALBERT Nikolaevich SHIRYAEV, Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, leading researcher at the Mathematical Institute of the Academy of Sciences:

— I first heard about Andrei Nikolaevits, of course, at school: the mathematics teacher talked a lot about him. I was then studying at the 538th Moscow boarding school, and our teacher pushed us to participate in all sorts of clubs and competitions. While accepting prizes at one of the Olympiads, I saw A.N. for the first time. Then I entered the university. Although we had heard about A.N. before, what we had in common with him seemed something inaccessible, unreal.

At first I wandered around different departments, and a lot of time passed before I settled on the department of probability theory. The first time I had some contact with A.N. occurred when I was teaching his course “Analysis-3”. And my first conversation with A.N. took place when I finished my thesis. I did it between the 4th and 5th years, because I knew that at the beginning of the 5th year I would not have time for my thesis, since I was part of the country’s student alpine skiing team, and in early February 1957 I took part in the 2nd Winter University Games in Grenoble.
