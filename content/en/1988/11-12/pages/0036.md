M1140. Let's draw one or several intersecting curves on the plane (curves can have self-intersection points, Fig. 1). At each intersection point, you can perform a “reconstruction” in a number of ways (Fig.2). If you perform a restructuring at all intersection points, you will get several intersecting curves (Fig. 3).
a) Prove that the number of intersecting curves that can be obtained is not greater than the number of regions in which the original curves divided the plane (there are 7 such regions in figure 1).
6) Is it always possible to make rearrangements so that the result is one curve?
c) Let’s choose the direction of traversal on each curve and make adjustments in accordance with this direction so that the arrows “repel” each other (Fig. 4). Could the result be a single curve?

F1143. There are a very large number of cylindrical vessels with water, immersed one in the other so that each next one floats in the limit. The area of ​​the bottom of the smallest vessel is $s_0$ and much less than the area of ​​the bottom of the largest. The smallest vessel should have a volume of water $v_0$. How far will this vessel descend relative to the ground? (After adding water, all the vessels continue to float.)
G. V. Grigoriev

F1144. There are $N$ identical billiard balls on the plane. One ball was pushed, and after experiencing several impacts, it stopped at the same point from which it began to move. At what minimum $N$ is this possible? The collisions are considered to be absolutely elastic.
