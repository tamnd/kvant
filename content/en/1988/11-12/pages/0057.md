Each point to which the wave excitation reaches is, in turn, the center of secondary waves; the surface emitting these secondary waves at a certain moment in time indicates the position of the front of the actual propagating wave at that moment.

It is easy to imagine, for example, how plane and spherical waves propagate (Fig. 1). The departing secondary waves after time $\Delta t$ is for a plane wave a plane shifted by a distance $c\Delta t$, and for a spherical wave it is a sphere with radius $R+c\Delta t$, where $c$ is the speed of propagation of secondary waves, $R$ is the radius of the first initial spherical wave.

In fact, the Heugens principle in this formulation is simply a geometric sieve for constructing a surface that sends out secondary waves. This surface is identified with the wave front, and thus the direction of wave propagation is determined.

Geugens initially formulated his principle for light waves and applied it to derive the laws of reflection and refraction of light at the interface between media. First of all, the very fact of the presence of reflected and refracted waves followed directly from the Heugens principle, and this was already a great success. According to Geugens, each point of the boundary of the media, as the front of the incident wave reaches it, becomes a source of secondary waves that propagate into both boundary media. The result of the superposition of these secondary waves in the first medium from which the wave is incident is a reflection wave, and the result of the superposition of secondary waves in the second medium is a refraction wave.

Of course, based on the Heugens principle, we cannot answer the question about the intensity of reflected and refracted waves, since for this we need to know at least their physics

honest nature (which, in principle, Geugens does not “participate” at all). But the geometric laws of reflection and refraction are completely independent of either the physical nature of the waves or the specific mechanism of their reflection and refraction. They are the same for all waves.

Let $v$ be the speed of a plane incident wave, $\alpha$ be the angle of its incidence (Fig. 2). Then the front of the incident wave passes through the interface between the two media with a speed of $v/\sin \alpha$. Both reflections and refractions of the wave are generated by the incident wave, therefore their fronts run along the boundaries at the same speed, i.e.

$$\frac{v}{\sin \alpha} = \frac{v_1}{\sin \alpha_1} = \frac{v_2}{\sin \alpha_2}.$$

Angles $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ determine the directions of propagation of wave reflection and refraction fronts. But since in a plane wave the rays are perpendicular to the wave front, the same relationships hold for reflected and refracted rays.

The explanation of the laws of refraction and reflection is a strong argument in favor of the validity of the Heugens principle. However, naturally, it raised many doubts and questions. Why is there no backward wave (after all, secondary sources emit spherical waves that also propagate against the front)? Why does light pass through the hole in a straight line (after all, secondary waves should also propagate in the region of the geometric shadow)?
