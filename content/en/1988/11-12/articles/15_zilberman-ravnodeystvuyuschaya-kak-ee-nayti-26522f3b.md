Resultant - how to find it?

We often solve problems with “practical content” without realizing how complex they are. Further, a simple automatic machine contains thousands of parts, each of which is acted upon by many forces. Just listing them is difficult, but writing and solving so many equations... However, we manage to get around this difficulty by introducing the concept of resultant force. Let's talk about this in more detail.

To determine the resultant, you need to add all the forces acting on the body vectorially (this is not always easy, but more on that later). The resulting total vector will be equivalent to the original system of forces.

So it's equivalent? Imagine that you are being pulled with a force of 500 N to the left by your left hand and with the same force - to the right by your right hand. The sum of these forces is zero, that is, they seem to be absent at all. Does this make you feel better?

In fact, equivalence here is understood in a rather narrow sense - when replacing all forces from the resultant, the movement of the body should not change, but there is no talk about deformations, breaks, etc.

What difficulties may arise when combining forces? If they are all applied at one point, then there are none. And it’s easy to add, and it’s clear where to apply the total vector - the resultant. Here we need a special physical quantity - moment of force.
The moment of force $\vec{F}$ relative to point $O$ (Fig. 1) is equal to

$$M = Fr \sin \alpha,$$

where $r$ is the distance from point $O$ to the point of application of force $A$. You can do it differently - decompose the force $\vec{F}$ into two components - along $r$ and perpendicularly $r$. The torque is created only by the perpendicular component $\vec{F}_2$:

$$M = F_2 r.$$

Of course, this is just the previous formula, rewritten a little differently, but in many tasks it is more convenient to use it.

It is easy to see that when the force $\vec{F}$ is transferred along the line of its action, the moment of force does not change, therefore such transfer is permissible. (Note that the deformation of the body changes when the point of application of force is transferred. This is easy to understand with a simple example. Let’s pull a rubber band tied at one end to the wall, first by the middle, and then by the other end - it grows unequally.)

Now let's return to the equal force. If forces are applied at different points, but the lines of their action intersect at one point, everything is simple again. Let's transfer the forces along the line of their action to this point and add them up - everything, as in the first case. If the lines of action of the force do not intersect at one point, the task of finding the equal force condition becomes more complicated.

For simplicity, let us analyze the special case when all force vectors lie in the same plane (flat system of forces). You can work out solving the problem in several steps - adding the forces in pairs, as shown in figure 2. First, add the forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ - we get the vector $\vec{R}_1$, and then add it with the vector $\vec{R}_2$. The equal force $\vec{R}$ is applied at point $A$.

This way you can not get an answer in all cases. Problems arose if the force vectors were parallel. Let's look at an example: we need to find the equal force of parallel forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ (Fig. 3). It is clear that the model of the equal force is equal to the sum of $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$, but at what point should the equal force be applied? Here simple reasoning will help: no matter what point of application we take, the moment of the equal force relative to the axis passing through this point is still equal to zero. But when replacing forces with their equal forces, the moments should not change - this means that you need to take a point relative to which the total moment of the original system of forces is equal to zero. In our example, this point $O$ can be found from the condition

$$F_1 \cdot AO = F_2 \cdot OB.$$

If $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ are directed in different directions, then point $O$ turns out to be outside the segment $AB$, close to the force that is larger in magnitude. (Check this out for yourself.)

You have probably solved problems where you need to find the equivalent force of parallel forces. Thus, usually forces also applied to different parts of the body are considered parallel.
