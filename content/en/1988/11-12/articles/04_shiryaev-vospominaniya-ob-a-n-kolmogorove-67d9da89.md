Having accepted me into graduate school, Kolmogorov assigned me two difficult exams in algebra and the equations of mathematical physics and announced that the third exam should still be closer to logic. And he told me to take the theory of relay circuits. There was almost no mathematical literature on relay contact circuits at that time, but only technical literature. Therefore, preparing for this exam was the most difficult for me. I remember that there were two places where I couldn’t understand how a triode worked. And only after learning everything, I realized that I didn’t need to understand it. In addition to the three exams, graduate reports were also required. As one of them, A.N. gave me for translation the book of the Hungarian mathematician Rosa Peter, “Recursive Functions,” that had just been published in German. I didn’t know German, but I didn’t dare tell Kolmogorov about it. I got to work and... translated. The book turned out to be unimportant, but the preface that A.N. wrote was brilliant.

I have two works jointly with A.N., there is also a work on which only my last name appears, but which is completely copied by A.N. This is an article in the “Reports of the Academy of Sciences” about the proof of Gödel’s theorem. When A.N. induced a certain result in his student, which in fact was almost suggested to him, he created such an environment as if the student had figured it out himself, and suggested writing an article in a magazine about the result. This kind of psychological support for his younger partner is a very significant aspect of his activity. Whether it was instinctive or whether he was thinking about it, I don’t know.

Unfortunately, I probably cannot consider myself a full-fledged student of A.N., because I did not possess either of the two qualities necessary for this: I did not understand anything about music and did not play sports. However, he did not send me away. And sometimes he talked to me about art, it’s time

You hate me with your mischief in everything. Who today, for example, knows Dufresne’s chess textbook? Nobody. This is a famous textbook from the end of the last century. I had this textbook at home (I got it from my father), so I read it. And when A.N. and I started talking about chess, the first word he uttered was “Dufresne,” and he told me the contents of the textbook.

Another such case. For me it was necessary that on the coat of arms the right and left sides are considered not that we see rights and words, but vice versa. (This is because the coat of arms was drawn on a shield and viewed from the point of view of the one who is not counting.) I had barely learned this when A.N. mentioned it for some reason.

Let us remember ALBERT Nikolaevich SHIRYAEV, Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, leading researcher at the Mathematical Institute of the Academy of Sciences:

— I first heard about Andrei Nikolaevits, of course, at school: the mathematics teacher talked a lot about him. I was then studying at the 538th Moscow boarding school, and our teacher pushed us to participate in all sorts of clubs and competitions. While accepting prizes at one of the Olympiads, I saw A.N. for the first time. Then I entered the university. Although we had heard about A.N. before, what we had in common with him seemed something inaccessible, unreal.

At first I wandered around different departments, and a lot of time passed before I settled on the department of probability theory. The first time I had some contact with A.N. occurred when I was teaching his course “Analysis-3”. And my first conversation with A.N. took place when I finished my thesis. I did it between the 4th and 5th years, because I knew that at the beginning of the 5th year I would not have time for my thesis, since I was part of the country’s student alpine skiing team, and in early February 1957 I took part in the 2nd Winter University Games in Grenoble.

The defense objected at the end of February. After protection, A.N. came out into the corridor with me, talked to me about work, said a lot of things that I did not understand. And either someone told him about my participation in the games in Grenoble, or my tanned appearance spoke for itself, but A.N. invited me to his place in Komarovka to ride the lies.

A.N. inquired about my mathematical interests, but there was no further conversation then. Starting from the 4th year, I was in a special group, so I could not count on working at the Mathematical Institute. However, after the 5th year, my classmate Vitya Leonov and I, who unfortunately died early, learned that A.N. was taking us to the Mathematical Institute. We didn’t even immediately appreciate this happiness. And work immediately began: A.N. included us in research on nonlinear spectral analysis, nonlinear theory of random processes. These were real and fruitful years that I even gave up skiing. Regarding one of our works, which is now widely known and which Leonov and I reported, A.N. said with a smile that “the authors are filled with the importance of the problem being developed.”

When V. Leonov and I were enrolled in the Steklov Mathematical Institute, A. N. immediately clearly defined our responsibilities. In 1956, the journal “Theory of Probability” was created, and certain work for the journal was entrusted to us: editing, marking up other people’s works. This activity, although it was rough, was a good school for us. The next thing Leonov and I had to do was go to A.N.’s lectures, in part on random processes, and write them down. By the way, these recordings of his lectures are still useful to me; they remained unreleased. V. Leonov put a lot of effort into putting them in order, but when A.N. saw the text he received, his comment was very brief: “Wow, what a freak it turned out to be!”

As for scientific research on nonlinear random processes, A. N. noticed in one work of Cherenkov (the son of a famous academician) some rules for calculating the so-called seven-invariants of small orders. He decided that, apparently, there was some general rule (for all orders), somehow he appeared and instructed us to prove it. The situation was very difficult. Every Thursday A.N. began by asking what we had done about this. We were testing a fantastic amount of paper. And only a year later we proved what was needed using the most complex combinatorial methods. And then we found a proof that fit on two pages. A.N. wrote the introduction to this work.

Now about how we worked. A.N. knew so much that we quite quickly learned the rules: not to leave A.N. without a piece of paper, on which he wrote the landowners when talking. Then, smoothing out these pieces of paper, we smoothed them out like puzzles. It often occurred to me that he was saying something brilliant and to the point, but you didn’t understand anything. There were no tape recorders then, but recording everything was simply not physically possible. And every Thursday to A.N.’s question “What has been done?” I should have answered that it was nothing, and come not to sleep, not to eat enough. Although we had already begun to have families, we stubbornly hid this from A.N.

Then, two years later, A.N. also created a situation that was associated with shocks. For example, I remember well how one day they let me in at 9 pm and told me that A.N. was asking me to come to him in Komarovka. Around 11 o'clock I reached Komarovka. A.N. turned out to be sick. He said that tomorrow he would have a lecture where he would have to read Blackall's theorem on resurgent events. A.N. doesn’t want this lecture to be missed, and asks me to give it. And he suggested two options: either he immediately tells me something on the topic of the lecture, or he gives Bla-

Quite quickly, A.N. switched me to a new topic. I had to study books on radio engineering, which described the design of receivers and lamps, and understand the principles of radar. The customers came and taught the essence of the task. Its mathematical formulation, proposed by A.N., turned out to be surprisingly successful (A.N. was able to formulate problems so successfully that completely new directions were then expressed from them). While working on this problem, I wrote my doctoral dissertation and a book on statistical sequential analysis.

A.N. did not explain anything like that. I had to figure it out on my own. He gave us an almost engineering formulation of the problem: to minimize the average delay time for a given probability of false alarms. He guessed the answer, of course. A.N. only said that in a very simple situation the answer should be such and such, and it could not be obtained from any theory existing at that time: we simply had to invent it.

And now about general culture. Since we were bound by secretarial duties and lecture activities, we often had to be in Komarovka. We went hiking and listened to music. And in music it was impossible to be familiar with A.N.

A typical example. After some evening, when there was a lot of music, A.N. asked: “Well, what else should you play now? Charpentier or Couperin? I said: “Andrey Nikolaevich, maybe something easier?” A.N. immediately: “Now I’ll find you “Lonely Harmon”.” I had no idea that A.N. might know anything about this “Lonely Harmony.” And one day, while listening to music, I sat and tapped my fingers on the table. A.N. quickly placed a napkin. I understood everything.

My activities (first non-silent transformations, then sequential analysis) are an example of how A.N., with the help of very simple formulations, pushed people into an independent orbit, after which he believed that he had employees who were doing this and knew it better than him (although it was possible to know better than A.N. only the details, but not the general ideas).

About non-mathematical communication with A.N. I can only tell you individual episodes, individual touches. I remember such a characteristic episode. We were together in Hungary at a congress on information theory. We received 1300 forints. Since we were given food and drink there, each of us had a large sum of money. And the question arose: what to buy? A.N. immediately asked about the bookstore. We came there. He immediately saw the book figure by Michelangelo, which cost 1200 forints, bought it, spending everything he had, and said: “And you’ll already buy me a ticket for the tramway.”

I first became acquainted with old Russian cities through A.N. Sometimes we visited provincial Russian cities, in the outback. A.N. knew a lot about each city, and he always had a definite goal: he knew that here you need to see such and such a wall or tower. Apparently, in memory of these poses, A.N. gave me the book “Ancient Russian Cities” for my tollstudio fortieth birthday.

I have never seen A.N. desired. He never, in any form, sought sympathy for himself. And to the question: “How do you feel about all honor and respect?” - A.N. answered simply: “Calm down.”
