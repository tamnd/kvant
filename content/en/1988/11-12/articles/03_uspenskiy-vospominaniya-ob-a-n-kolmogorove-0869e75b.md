It is characteristic of the mathematical works of Andrei Nikolaevich Kolmogorov that he was a pioneer and discoverer in many areas of mathematics: he owns outstanding achievements in the theory of probability, the theory of functions, functional analysis, topology, the theory of differential equations, the theory of dynamical systems, the theory of turbulent fluid motion, etc. - it is difficult to indicate an area of mathematical analysis in which A. N. Kolmogorov would not have made a significant inclusion (sometimes two hundred years old) problems.

Andrei Nikolaevich completed his first significant work - an example of a Fourier series of a summable function, diverging almost everywhere - at the age of 19. (As a side note, I’ll mention that Fourier told me in 1965: “Oh, Kolmogorov! This is that wonderful young man who constructed a Fourier series that is divergent almost everywhere!” For Fourier, all of Kolmogorov’s work on probability theory, dynamic systems, turbulence and melley was worth less than this student work.) Andrei Nikolaevich himself always highly valued “sports-mathematical” He was proud of the achievements of this first work, but considered his most difficult sporting achievement to be his work on Hilbert’s 13th problem.

Andrei Nikolaevich usually tried to avoid technical specifications and generalizations of the constructed theory. But at the third stage of work, when it is necessary to comprehend the results obtained and see new paths, at the stage of creating functional generalizing theories, Andrei Nikolaevich has remarkable achievements.

Among the more than three hundred articles he published, there are studies on mechanics, geology, biology, crystallization of metals, the theory of arrows, the theory of versification, the theory of information transmission, the theory of automata, etc. In mathematics itself, Andrei Nikolaevich was also always interested in problems, the solutions of which bring us closer to the meaning of nature and to the mastery of its forces. He himself said about his applied activities as follows:

“Having studied with some success, and sometimes even usefully, a fairly wide range of practical applications of mathematics, I have remained basically a pure mathematician. Admiring the mathematicians who turned into great representatives of our technology, fully appreciating the importance of computers and cybernetics for the future man, I still think that pure mathematics in its traditional aspect has not lost its rightful place of honor among other sciences. The only thing that could be disastrous for it would be an excessively sharp stratification of mathematicians into two schools: one cultivates the abstract newest branches of mathematics, without clearly focusing on their connections with the real world that gave birth to them, others are busy with “applications”, without going up to an exhaustive analysis of their theoretical foundations... I want to support the legitimacy and dignity of the position of a mathematician who understands the place and role of his science in the development of natural sciences, technology, and all human culture, capable of developing his natural science in accordance with these general needs."

These words of Andrei Nikolaevich about the lawfulness and dignity of the position of a mathematician, published in 1963 on the cover of an issue of Ogonyok dedicated to him, are his credo and scientific conclusion. The relevance of his thoughts and warnings becomes more and more detailed as Lagrange’s prediction comes true: “Places in mathematics at the Academy will become what departments of Arabic at universities have already become...”

I met Andrei Nikolaevich when I was in my third year. He taught a special course on the mechanism of Moscow State University “Measure Theory”. They took this special course mainly for satisfaction, since there was no need to pass this course. However, during a break between lectures, I approached him. I can’t remember this: A.N. made some statements that I refuted. This impressed him, and he invited me to come to his dacha, asking if I was only ready to talk about mathematics or if I could also ride lies. That's how we met him.

I don’t even want to remember about kaataniya, because the lies simply didn’t stick to me. Before that, I skated only in felt boots, threading the toe of the felt boot into the loop, and I had never seen any other fastenings and did not know that such existed. Andrei Nikolaevich gave me boots, boots, and bindings (hard or semi-hard, I don’t remember now). The lies kept falling off me, and every time Academician Kolmogorov stood on his knees in the snow, straightening the fastenings of my lies. The first lies left me completely downhill, we returned and put on others. Most likely, I was not to blame for this, because with proper fastenings the lies should not have left. This all happened on January 22, 1950. From that time on I became a student of A.N.

Working with A.N. was very easy. All the time I felt that he was a man of genius. In my life there were three brilliant people with whom I had the opportunity to communicate: B. L. Pasternak, A. A. Zalizyank and A. N. Kolmogorov. A.N. had his own special style. In the winter of 1950, for the first time he took me to his dacha, and not without some ceremonies, he made me his student. Then he assigned the topic of my classes. He said that there are such special functions, they are called resource functions, and he almost never knows anything about them in our country. He gave me several shades of articles from foreign magazines and told me to look into it. And he said: “As a last resort, if nothing comes of you, he will give us competent abstracts.” At the same time, he gave me several copies of his works, one of which had a dedicatory inscription. This is a very early and unique feature from the 20s that I treasure.

It was very difficult to say how A.N.’s genius was manifested. But I can name two of its distinctive features. First. As a graduate student, I met with A.N. weekly. And every time, at every subsequent meeting, he remembered why we parted, and in general all my work (everything I had done before) he knew better and more accurately than I did. Although I only did this, and he, as you know, did many others. Second feature. G. I. Barenblatt often went to Komarovku at the same time as me, with whom A. N. studied hydrodynamics (and with me - mathematical logic). Barenblatt and I were indelibly impressed by the ease with which A.N. moved from hydrodynamics to mathematical logic and back. It was completely natural for him. And it seemed like a miracle to us.
