LET'S TALK A LITTLE
ABOUT THE WEATHER...

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. M. BUVNOV

In the old days of proper heating, it was prescribed to talk about the weather when meeting someone for the first time. Let's talk about it too, namely about that side of the issue that concerns the movement of the atmosphere as some physical object; after all, in the end, the weather anywhere on our planet is determined by the movement of atmospheric masses. However, such movement is so complex that accurate prediction of the behavior of the atmosphere over the long term is not possible. To obtain a sufficiently satisfactory weather forecast, the harmonious development of two directions is required. The first direction is to increase the frequency of changes in the basic parameters of the atmosphere (temperature, pressure, humidity, wind speed, etc.) and reduce the distances between points at which this information is available. The second direction, which is the subject of study of atmospheric physics, is the study of the basic physical laws that determine atmospheric processes.

Hadley model

When considering very complex objects (and the atmosphere certainly belongs to such objects), physicists suffer from constructive models and, within the framework of this model, consider the behavior of the object. The transition from object to model is the most important step of any research. On the one hand, the model must reflect the characteristics of the object that we are studying, i.e., satisfy experimental and natural data; on the other hand, it must be simple enough for understanding and theoretical description. What is the simplest model that satisfies these requirements when considering large-scale atmospheric movements?

First of all, the model must take into account the main factors determining these movements.

The first factor should be called the strength of gravitational interaction. This force largely regulates the gas composition of the atmosphere and determines the planet's ability to retain its atmosphere. The greater the mass of the planet, the easier it is for it to retain light gases; with a low mass, the rotating body may completely lose its atmosphere (perhaps this is why the Moon does not have an atmosphere).

The second important factor is the planet's exposure to solar radiation. The amount of energy supplied to the planet from the Sun depends on the time of day, as well as on the inclination of the orbit (winter - summer, equator - stripes). On average, every square meter of the earth's surface receives 1372 J of energy from the Sun every day. (Knowing the radius of the Earth, it is easy to calculate the total flow of energy coming from the Sun.) The rays falling on the Earth are partially reflected; The ratio of reflected radiation to radiating radiation is called surface albedo. Albedo surfaces, depending on the type of surface, take a value from 0.1-0.15 for the ocean to 0.6-0.8 for pure snow; The average albedo for the Earth is approximately 0.3. The more energy comes to a given place on the planet, the more it heats up. As the air heats up, it bursts and falls, and as a result, high temperatures appear in the atmosphere.

Cyclones and anticyclones

Every time, while serving as a weather forecast, we learned about the movement or interaction of cyclones and anticyclones, that is, vortexes formed, the dimensions of which correspond to kilometers. How to relate the presence of these formations to the general circulation of the atmosphere? It turns out that, depending on the basic definition of the parameters (the speed of rotation of the Earth, the temperature difference in the direction of the equator - stripes, etc.), a convective cell can be either stable - and in this case the Hadley circulation develops - or unstable, as is observed on Earth, where a wave movement is formed, consisting of a different number of vortices. Five to six large vortices are usually observed in the atmosphere. If all vortex movements are averaged over a sufficiently large period of time, then the averaging of the movement will be similar to the zonal circulation we considered.

The easiest way to consider the formation of large-scale vortices is in a laboratory experiment. Two metal cylinders are taken and installed in pine. The inner cylinder corresponds to the pole - it is cooled, the outer one corresponds to the equator - it is heated. There is liquid between the cylinders. If the system rotates at a constant angular velocity, then various modes of motion can be observed in it. At a low whirl speed, the particles move from the outer cylinder to the inner one in a spiral, so that the fluid, relative to the stationary one, is observed to be whirler than the cylinders (Fig. 4). If the fundamental scheme of the formation of cyclics and anticyclics is quite simple, then a detailed description of their movements is poorly developed, primarily because too many factors determine this movement. Until now, we have considered an idealized atmosphere, and now we will consider the possibility of the influence of materials on the general cyclulation of the atmosphere. Continents are higher than the oceans, and high mountain ranges especially change the movement of the atmosphere. In addition, the average temperatures of materials and oceans differ greatly, since the oceans are more inert, they warm up more slowly, but also cool more slowly. figure 7 presents the average annual and average surface temperature at latitude 55° in the northern hemisphere for different periods of the year. The difference in temperatures between materials and the ocean in winter is only slightly less than the difference between the temperatures of polar and tropical regions. This thermal deficiency and the influence of the relief lead to the fact that large vortices stop moving with the wave and the vortex may not change its position for several weeks. This state of the atmosphere is called a “blocking situation” and leads to numerous catastrophic consequences, primarily droughts.

Greenhouse effect and nuclear winter

The external factors that determine the movement of the atmosphere practically do not change over time, but changes occurring in the atmosphere, primarily caused by human influence, can fundamentally change the total effect of these factors. Let's look at this using the example of the so-called greenhouse effect and the phenomenon of “nuclear winter”.

A laser fiber-optic line connects in one second to transmit such an amount of digital information that all 30 volumes of BSE can be encoded! Well, why “lightning in a crystal”? Yes, because the current density in LEDs and lasers can be several, and sometimes tens of times, higher than the current density in a lightning discharge. This means that dozens of “microlightning” sparkle in crystals, only they are controlled by the will of man and do not serve destruction: their flashes breathe life into optoelectronics.

Anyone who today or tomorrow devotes himself to LEDs and lasers will be lucky enough to work on the subject of solid-state physical and technical influences.
