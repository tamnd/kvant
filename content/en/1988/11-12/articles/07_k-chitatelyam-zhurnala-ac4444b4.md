LIGHTNING IN CRYSTAL

Doctor of Technical Sciences
Y. R. NOSOV

Ask an electrical food specialist - an engineer, a scientist, a company manager: what is the most promising and promising in this area today? Eight out of ten will answer: “Optoelectronics!”

The long-standing idea of ​​​​using in computer science not only electrical signals (as offered by traditional microelectronics), but also light and optical signals - this idea turned out to be very fruitful. We found out that thanks to the merging of electronics with optics, the capabilities of computing and communication devices will increase immeasurably: their performance will increase hundreds and thousands of times, the devices will become more reliable, noise-resistant, and miniature.

All this was understood back in the sixties. So why, when the reader asks for the first time, does so much still remain just a promise? Here's the thing. In order to “bring light” to work, you need to learn to handle it as freely and simply as with an electric current. Learn to amplify and convert light signals, seamlessly transmit them from point to point, record and save. But first of all, you need to learn how to generate them. Whatever may be said about the importance of all elements of an optoelectronic system, it is the source of light signals, the emitter, that is the fundamental principle, the “alpha and omega” of optoelectronics. And of course, you need not just any emitter, such as, for example, a lighting lamp, but one that, in terms of miniaturization, efficiency, and durability, is comparable to a transistor and integrated circuits.

Obviously, a solution to the emitter problem could only be found in the field of semiconductors.

Let's fast forward thirty years ago. In those years, semiconductor science worked to meet the needs of transistor technology - the great hopes of electronics were connected with these devices. The first transistors were made from thermium, but it was clear that better results could be achieved with silicon and the then new semiconductor gallium arsenide, a two-component compound of arsenic and gallium (GaAs). The “silicon” direction quickly achieved success and since 1960 has become the basis not only of transistor technology, but also of microelectronics.

But they stubbornly refused to get transistors from Arsend Gall. And they were ready to write off the millions of dollars spent on creating perfect GaAs single crystals as a waste, justifying themselves by saying that “science requires sacrifices,” but... It is not always known where you will find it and where you will lose it. Suddenly, even in this “dead end” direction, hope began to shine, and it began to shine in the literal sense of the word.

In 1956, it was discovered that when an electric current passes through an arsenide-halide diode, it begins to emit! *) This is how LEDs appeared. Physicists and engineers have literally pounced on the interside effect. It was immediately established that the semiconductor crystal in the LED does not heat up - which means that luminescence, long known to scientists as “cold glow,” is responsible for the radiation.

We also figured out the mechanism of how the LED works. Used in it

Soon*, no one was surprised that Gallium phosphide LEDs intensely emit red or green light, depending on the type of impurities introduced into the crystal. The use of a triple compound of gall, arsenic and phosphorus allows you to obtain any glow in the range from dark red to orange and almost yellow. Silicon carbide appears in the yellow-green and blue regions of the spectrum, although very, very weakly. And only the blue color, like Maeterlinck’s accelerated blue bird, still eludes scientists. And the brightest turned out to be red LEDs - it was under the “almi sails” that optoelectronics confidently “floated” into technology and into our everyday life.

Many devices use sets of a large number of LEDs, placed in a certain order on a common panel. At the command received from the control device, the required number, letter, or graph is written out from individual luminous points. This leads to a natural thought: why cut a semiconductor wafer into separate crystals, and then put them back together to form LEDs? This is how sign indicators appeared - several crystals or one crystal with several points glowing independently of each other are placed inside a common plastic shell.

LEDs and indicators, which appeared in industry in the late 60s, have found wide application. Today their world production is approaching 10 billion(!) pieces per year. In electronic watches and calculators, on instrument panels of robotic kits and flexible production systems, in buttons and keys of a wide variety of radio and electronic equipment, on control panels of aircraft and submarines - wherever these bright red fireflies and numbers flash.

True, the capabilities of LEDs are limited; they only use devices for individual use, if necessary, from the indicator to the eye. But let’s already talk about installing brake lights on some brands of automatic vehicles from sets of super-intense LEDs. Of course, LED lighting in apartments is a long way off, although who knows what the next day has in store for us?

However, it’s time to translate both, let’s try to summarize a little. Everything we talked about here concerned, technically speaking, the use of LEDs in information control devices. With their help, information generated in the depths of a computer in the form of electronic impulses is transformed into the primo perceived and thus actually reaches a person. Are devices necessary or important? Without a doubt! But this is only part of the problems in which optoelectronics should (and can) help with information. After all, there are still problems of processing, transmission, and storage of information. What contribution can LED make here? Alas... The praises involuntarily fade away, a bright rainbow of colors shines...

Firstly, as already noted, the intensity of the LEDs is very low - what the eye senses is not always captured by the photodetector, and even if it is some distance away. Secondly, it turned out that the LED radiation is not monochromatic - we will talk about the quantitative assessment of this disadvantage below, but now it only indicates what radiation is

And finally, the main thing is that the LED shines almost evenly in all directions; it is impossible to concentrate its energy in one highly directional beam. The simplest task for electrical circuits - transmitting a signal from point A to point B and only there - cannot be solved by an LED. Most of the light energy is not only wasted for free, it “clogs” the surrounding space and causes information leakage. The LED - this careless trans-fat and that chatterbox, unable to keep his mouth shut - is clearly not a pair of information, where everything is precisely calculated and verified, and for each bit as much energy is allocated as necessary.

Fortunately, the LED as an emitter has an excellent alternative - it is a laser, characterized by high-intensity, almost monochromatic, highly directional radiation. Let's make a small digression here and talk about the quantitative content of these laser properties.

The directionality of the characteristic is the telescoping angle α, within which the generated emission rays lie; if the beam diverges, symmetrically moving away from a certain axis (direction of radiation), then to evaluate the divergence it uses a flat angle, measured as usual - in radians, degrees, minutes.

There are no strictly monochromatic waves in nature, therefore, to characterize the approximation of a real wave flow to an ideal one, they speak of the degree of monochromaticity. Quantitatively, this is the ratio of the wavelength band of the generated radiation Δλ to the wavelength at the center of the band λ₀; the smaller Δλ/λ₀, the more perfect the laser. For example, we point out that a typical gas - helium-neon - laser provides α < 1′ and Δλ/λ₀ < 0.000001. Such a flux wave is suitable for computational optoelectronics devices - this term has recently been used to characterize the direction that is associated with the processing of information presented in the form of optical signals.

Everything seems to be fine, but let’s not rush to a final conclusion. Let's imagine the same helium-neon laser with its almost half-turn glass discharge tube and a high-voltage power source weighing several kilograms. Now let’s put next to it an integrated circuit the size of a postage stamp, which contains about a million (!) transistors and requires only 5 volt power. Are these two products compatible? Agree, not very much. And this incompatibility was confirmed with complete certainty - numerous attempts to use lasers in microclectronic computing devices have actually been carried out. Here is the concept “you cannot harness an ox and a tremulous doe to one harness.”

Obviously, compatibility can be achieved only in one single option - if the laser, like the microcircuits, is semiconductor.

... The history of the creation of a semiconductor laser, characteristic for scientific discoveries of the 20th century. In 1960, solid-state (ruby) and gas (helium-neon) lasers were invented almost simultaneously, and soon after that, scientists predicted the possibility of creating a semiconductor laser. By analogy with other semiconductor devices, such a laser was predicted to have superior power, durability, resistance to external influences, low cost, high reproduction of parameters and the ability to change them over a very wide range. It was very tempting to create such a device, and in the leading physics laboratories of the world, the hunt for a semiconductor laser literally began - everyone wanted to cover the beautiful butterfly with their crown.

And when theorists calculated quite accurately what the quantum structure of the original crystal should be, then the circle of semiconductors “suspected” of possible involvement in the laser effect narrowed sharply. The fate of the “butterfly” was predetermined... On the eve of 1963, almost simultaneously, the first semiconductor lasers were created in laboratories in the USA and USSR.

As in the experiments with LEDs, a $p—n$ junction was fabricated in an Arsend Gall wafer (again this semiconductor was a “pinoer”); the only difference was that during doping, much larger impurities were introduced, as a result of which more free electrons were formed. Then, pressing on the plate with the tip of a lancet, tiny square crystals were poked out of it. The plate is cut in order along crystallographic planes, so the lateral opposite faces of the crystal turn out to be plane-parallel and mirror-like; these two mirrors form the resonator necessary for lasing. Then the crystal of the lower face was attacked on a massive copper substrate (for a heat sink), a second, thinner electrode was attached to the upper face - the structure was ready for research.

When current was passed, the crystal began to generate infrared radiation, and, as befits an LED, weakly and in all directions. But as soon as the current was increased to a certain value (it was called the threshold current), the picture changed significantly - the radiation power increased sharply, and intense light escaped from the side faces from those places (strips) where the plane of the $p—n$ transition intersects the faces of the resonator. Studies of the radiation spectrum revealed that the band of generated waves had sharply compressed. There was no doubt left - it was a laser!

We quickly understood the mechanism of its operation. Just like in an LED, the applied extractor “drives” the electron onto the “energy slide”, only

The “hill” part is higher, and the number of electrons is much greater. “Pumped up” electrons collide near the $p—n$ transition (here an active zone is formed) and, “falling down the hill,” generate radiation quanta. But this is where the analogy with the LED ends. The light wave, propagating along the plane of the $p—n$-transition, is reflected from the mirror faces and, repeatedly passing through the active zone, forces the electrons to “fall off the hill”. It turns out that a huge number of electrons simultaneously and in strictly the same way perform the quantum transition prescribed to them (at figure 1 this transition is indicated by a black arrow). Therefore, the beam has a high degree of monochromaticity, as well as a complete definition of polarization. According to the mechanism of its occurrence, such radiation is called forced, stimulated, induced (these are all synonyms), while the radiation of an LED is spontaneous (random in direction and polarization and, to some extent, in wavelength).

They started talking about a new device. It seemed very quickly that big changes were about to begin in optoelectronics. But time passed, and the newly born laser did not find any real application in any equipment. And soon the effect was surprised by its size and went out of order. It also turned out that in terms of the degree of monochromaticity it was only 10-20 times better than an LED ($\Delta \lambda/\lambda \approx 0,005$ versus 0.05 for an LED) and thousands of times worse than a gas laser. And in terms of angular divergence ($\alpha \approx 30^\circ$), the semiconductor laser was more similar

The most obvious thing was that the shortcomings of the semiconductor laser were fully developed and had a seemingly insurmountable physical justification. The electrons injected by the $p$—$n$ transition did not want to be retained in the thin active zone, but scattered throughout the crystal; The same thing happened with the light wave. Lost electrons and quanta, preventing them from being included in the laser effect, only uselessly heated the crystal. How to keep these restless carriers of electric current and light radiation in one part of the crystal? After all, you can’t put a shutter or screen inside - it’s still a monocrystal, in the lattice of which the atoms are arranged in perfect order. When we started chasing the “beautiful butterfly”, I somehow didn’t think about all this. And now it began to seem that the “butterfly” was destined for the fate of a preserved exhibit in the Kunstkammer of Physics Discs.

And yet - for the umpteenth time! — the resourcefulness of human size triumphed. Heterostructures solved the problem. If in a gall agrenium crystal some of the gallium atoms are replaced with aluminum atoms, then the structure of the crystal lattice will not change at all - the atoms of these two elements are so identical in physical properties. However, this results in the formation of a new semiconductor—gallium-aluminum arsenide, whose band gap is greater than that of pure gallium arsenide. The boundary between these two semiconductors inside a single single crystal is a heterojunction; in this region there is an energy barrier and at the same time an “optical barrier” is formed - the interface between two semiconductors with different refractive indices. An active zone with a high refractive index, “faceted” by heterointerfaces, has become an ideal trap for electrons and a light guide for light.

And the next word belonged to “Her Majesty of Technology”. Not much time passed, and we learned how to produce pairs of heterojunctions inside a single crystal, located parallel to each other at a distance, maintained with fantastic precision in several atomic layers. The threshold current reached tens of milliamps, the upper limit of the operating temperature reached 100 °C, and the durability (though in some samples) reaches years. This began the “Renaissance” of semiconductor lasers. Inventions and adaptations poured in as if from a year of plenty. Do you need even more power current? Please use a structure in which the active zone is “sandwiched” by heterojunctions not only from above and below, but also from the sides. The thinnest microponant of an active semiconductor is excited by a current of only 1 mA! Should there be a spectrum of radiation? And this is welcome. If one of the heterointerfaces is made wavy, then the selectivity of the resonant sharply increases and the degree of monochromaticity becomes the same as that of a gas laser.
