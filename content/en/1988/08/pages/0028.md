⟦folio 26⟧

The federal issue of our magazine featured three photographers of ski tracks. The photos were taken at the same time, and the ski tracks were located nearby. But they are so different from each other...
We invited readers to think about what explains such a variety of types of ski tracks.

Probably everyone who became interested in this issue realized that it is impossible to do without physics. And physics is always physics, both in March and in August. Therefore, we continue the conversation about ski tracks today, when the growing snow has already melted, and new snow is still far away.

LET'S TALK
ABOUT YESTERDAY'S SNOW

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. V. MITROFANOV

Perhaps some of you, while reading the terms of the problem, have already agreed on what the essence of the matter is. The answer is: the ski tracks are very different from each other because different numbers of skiers have passed on them at different times. Why did this have such a big impact on the appearance of the ski track? Let's try to figure it out.

Snow that has fallen on the ground is a very complex physical object that continuously changes over time. You know that long before the spring melt, the snow settles, compacts, can become denser or, conversely, more fishable, lose weight, “weather.” At the same time, many physical characteristics of snow change, for example, the coefficient of thermal conductivity, porosity, humidity, etc. Human intervention - say, snow compaction when laying ski tracks or a pedestrian road - also changes the appearance of the snow cover, giving the snow properties that distinguish it in some way from untouched snow.

The amazing variability of the state of snow in nature is due to the fact that snow does not simply exist on its own, but participates in direct and reverse physical transitions ice - steam and ice - water. These processes are especially active in the spring, when the snow is in conditions close to the so-called triple point of water.
