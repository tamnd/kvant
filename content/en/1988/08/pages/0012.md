Part of the beam conductively interacts with the particle: it will be reflected from the surface, refracted inside, etc. The rays from this part of the beam no longer retain their original direction and will either go completely to the side, or, passing through the lens, will fall into the focal plane, but not into focus. As a result, the total light energy collected at the focus will decrease by $\Delta\Phi$.
At first glance, it seems very clear that the light energy carried away by the scattered part of the beam is equal to

$$\Delta\Phi' = E_0 S,$$

(1)

where $E_0$ is the illumination of a single area in the incident beam, and $S = \pi r^2$ is the area of ​​the particle contour. However, we will see that in reality things are not so simple, and the total light energy dissipated by the part is different from $\Delta\Phi'$.

To characterize the scattering properties of a particle, we will use the dimensionless quantity

$$\dot{Q} = \frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta\Phi'},$$

(2)

where $\Delta\Phi$ is the energy “losing” as a result of dissipation. Finding the dependence of $Q$ on $r$ and on $\lambda$ and studying the distribution of scattered radiation in space is the main problem of scattering theory, the solution of which is associated with such brilliant names as P. Descartes, J. Tyndall, W. Rayleigh, L. I. Mandelstam and others. Or perhaps interest in this task... out of childish curiosity. Above us is projected a natural “ground” of various light scattering phenomena - an atmosphere that is an environment saturated with water droplets and dust particles. Dozens of questions have occupied us since childhood. Why are the clouds white and the clouds black? Why is the sun yellow and the sky blue? What is a rainbow? Why does everything look like fog to such countries? Serbian physics is born from such questions.

The complete theory of the scattering of an electromagnetic wave on a spherical particle turned out to be a “hard nut to crack”, which scientists discovered only at the beginning of our century. But some of its results can be obtained from simple physical considerations. First of all, physical intuition tells us that the entire scattering picture is determined not by the wavelength and radius of the particle separately, but by the correspondence between them. In other words, the dimensionless quantity $Q$ must depend on the dimensionless combination of $r$ and $\lambda$, namely, on the parameter $\varrho = r/\lambda$.

Although we do not yet know the explicit form of $Q(\varrho)$, we will still try to trace its behavior in limiting cases. If the particle size is infinitely small compared to the wavelength ($\varrho \rightarrow 0$), then there is no point in talking about light rays that hit the particle and dissipate it. The wave simply does not “replace” the particle, $\Delta\Phi \rightarrow 0$ and $Q \rightarrow 0$. The problem of the scattering of the infinite by a small particle was considered by the famous English physicist Lord Rayleigh, who showed in 1892 that, firstly, light is scattered in all directions evenly, and secondly -

$$Q \sim \varrho^4 \sim \frac{1}{\lambda^4}.$$

(3)

Using this relationship, the color of the sky was first discovered. After all, it is determined by the color of the sun's rays scattered by the atmosphere. From (3) it follows that the short-wavelength part of the spectrum (blue color with $\lambda_1 \approx 0,4$ µm) is scattered horizontally than the long-wavelength part (red color with $\lambda_2 \approx 0,7$ µm):

$$\frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \left( \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} \right)^4 \approx 9.$$

Posed, we see above us the bottomless blue of the heavens.

Now let us turn to the case when the particle is infinitely large compared to the wavelength ($\varrho \rightarrow \infty$). Here we are in the domain of geometric optics, and it seems very clear that scattered rays are those rays that fall into the contour of a particle.
