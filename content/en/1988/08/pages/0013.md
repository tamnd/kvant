Rays of light that have undergone diffraction propagate forward along the path of the incident rays, but still deviate slightly from their first initial direction. And if so, then in our strict experiment with a lens these rays will no longer be collected in the focal spot. And it turns out that the energy carried away by these rays is exactly equal to $\Delta\Phi'$. This results in a doubling of the amount of scattered light energy:

$$\Delta\Phi = 2\Delta\Phi', \text{ и } Q = 2.$$

Let us pay attention to the fact that an infinitely large (compared to $\lambda$) particle, unlike an infinitely small one, scatters light in the surrounding space no longer evenly: after all, only half of $\Delta\Phi$ is contained in the diffraction “tongue” directed in front of it.

The asymmetry of the spatial distribution of scattered light can be observed in the evening during thick fog. Here comes the automiles. The light from his headlights seems to make the fog glow. But this glow is not very visible from everywhere. The driver sees a rather dim cone of light. But a pedestrian looking from the front (not point-blank, of course, at the headlight, but at a slight angle to the direction of light) sees a bright luminous column of fog. The fact is that relatively large drops of fog scatter light slightly in the direction of distribution of the incident light beam. This effect is also known to astronomers: the recently discovered rings of Jupiter, consisting of dust particles, are clearly visible when illuminated by the Sun from behind, from behind Jupiter. In this case, the scattered light reaches an observer on earth.

So we got:

$$\lim_{Q \to 0} Q = 0, \lim_{Q \to \infty} Q = 2.$$

The general appearance of the curve $Q$, calculated using exact formulas, is depicted in figure 2 (blue curve). Here, the abscissa axis represents the value $X = K\varphi$, where $K$ depends on the relative refractive index of the particle and the environment. So, for a particle of water in the air (fog) $K = 4,1$, and for a particle of fat in water (milk) $K = 1,5$. The first thing that catches your eye when looking at the curve $Q(X)$ is its non-motonicity. Along with the “normal” areas of increase, there are also abnormal areas of decrease $Q(X)$. In general, the presence of peaks in various dependences describing the oscillatory process is associated, as a rule, with a good sign of the phenomenon of resonance. In our case, the oscillatory process is the propagation of an electromagnetic wave, and it resonates with a particle: when $\lambda \approx nr$, where $n$ is an integer, the scattering increases sharply.
