Racial steam diffuses to the surface of the snow, and the ice is frozen from deep water vapor, and not from water formed from the snow due to excessive pressure on the snow, as some of you might mistakenly think.

In the future, the fate of the ice flats is different from that of the snowdrifts neighboring it, where the snow sublimates faster under the influence of the sun and wind. It’s funny that there is a kind of greenhouse effect: the ice produces sunlight, and the heat exchange between the snow under the ice and the outside space is small - wind and convection under the ice crust are practically absent. On a sunny frosty day, the snow heats up under the ice, sublimates faster and may even begin to melt. Under the ice, the snow begins to disappear!
This is precisely what the rustle of falling floes of ice “tells” us on a fine spring day.

Let's now turn to the left photo, which shows the light beam. This tray is relatively new. In February or early March, shortly before the shooting, a lot of flatters drove along the lake. They thoroughly compacted the snow, which the blizzards had added a lot to. The snow on the flat is comparatively lighter, lighter. But why is the line lighter than the background and why is it higher than the general snow level?

One might have assumed that snow had fallen the day before, and the wind had blown it onto a larger field than nearby in the open; the next day the snow around melted or evaporated, but it remained on the bed, which is why the bed is white. But a line similar to the one that was formed appears light against the background of snow, regardless of whether the snow passed the day before or not. Light-colored flies are often found near cities in the spring, when the snow is melting with might and main; it happens that almost all the snow on the ground has melted, and traces of the snowflakes still stretch in white stripes along the ground...

Let's offer another explanation. A bright snowy snowy background is an “autograph” of an interesting physical process, which could be called radiation instability of the boundaries of dirty snow, if we use scientific terminology. The word “dirt” means dust, sugar, ash, the word pollution that enters the snow from the atmosphere. Dirt, unlike snow, absorbs visible radiation well: it is black! Dirt particles are in contact with snow. Therefore, dirty snow is better warmed by the sun than clean snow, and therefore evaporates (or melts) faster. But the more the layer of snow evaporates, the dirtier its surface will become: dirt does not evaporate, but accumulates on the “moving” border of the snow cover. And the dirty surface will be warmed up even more by the sun, etc. Therefore, if in any place of the snow cover the snow evaporates faster, then this place will be “blacker” than the areas adjacent to it, and in the future the snow evaporates faster from this place. Under the influence of sunlight, the initially flat snow surface becomes embossed with light (high) and dark (low) areas.

Subsequently, under the influence of the sun, the snow on such a bed evaporates faster than the surrounding one (a recessed bed is better protected from the cold wind than snow in an open place). Having become dark, the lizhne even evaporates even faster. And in the end, it stands out from the surrounding background thanks to the same processes that are “to blame” for the existence of white grass on dirty snow.
