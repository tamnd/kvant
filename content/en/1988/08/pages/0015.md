From here, for $\Delta x \to 0$, we move on to the differential equation:

$$E' = -Q \pi r^2 NE(x).$$

As is known, a differential equation of the form

$$y'(x) = ay(x)$$

has as its solution an exponential function

$$y(x) = ce^{ax}.$$

As applied to our case, we obtain that at the exit from a cove of length $l$ a parallel beam of light will create illumination

$$E(l) = E_0 e^{-Q \pi r^2 Nl}.$$

Real devices are built in such a way that it is not the illumination that changes, but the values ​​$D = -\ln(E(l)/E_0)$, called optical density:

$$D = -\ln \frac{E(l)}{E_0} = Q \pi r^2 Nl.$$

Let's transform this formula as follows:

$$D = \frac{Q \cdot \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 Nl}{\frac{4}{3} r} = \frac{QVlK \frac{1}{\lambda}}{\frac{4}{3} K \frac{r}{\lambda}} = \frac{Q}{X} \frac{3}{4\lambda} KlV.$$

Here $V = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 N$ is the total volume of fat globules per unit volume of milk. And $V \cdot 100\%$ is the percentage of fat in milk.

So $D$ is proportional to $V$. This means that by changing the optical density, the fat content of milk can be calculated. But alas! The accuracy of determining $V$ will be low. As can be seen from (4), the proportional density coefficient between $D$ and $V$ includes the function $z = Q/X$ (red curve at figure 2). In natural milk, regardless of the fat content, the average radius of fat particles can be different - from 0.5 to 1.5 microns. At $K = 1,5$ the value $X$ (for visible light) can vary, respectively, from approximately 1 to 3. Such a change $X$, as can be seen from figure 2, corresponds to a change $z$ approximately twofold. Now imagine that we took two milk samples of the same fat content (the same $V$), but due to some physiological characteristics of the cows, the particle size in one sample was 0.5 microns, and in the other - 1.5 microns. Then $D$ will differ by half. This is a huge mistake. To avoid this, before changing the size of the fat globules in the milk sample is standardized. The device is equipped with a gromogenizer - a device in which milk is pressed through a system of valves, where the fat globules are crushed and reduced to an average size of approximately 0.5 microns. This achieves an unambiguous relationship between $D$ and $V$. (Milk homogenization, by the way, is often carried out at dairies. Such milk is more resistant to separation, souring and is better absorbed.)

The problems, however, do not end there. The fact is that milk contains another insoluble component - protein. Protein particles have an average radius of ~0.05 μm, and light scattering from them can reach 20% of that from fat particles. In order to eliminate this interfering factor, milk is diluted with a weak alkali solution.
