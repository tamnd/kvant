The Poisson distribution is one of the most important in probability theory; it is used in many problems. Without going into details, we can say that it appears where randomly scattered points are considered, and we are interested in the number of points that fall into a given area. In this way, there can be isomarks in a well-mixed dough (or cherries in a compote), typos in a book, moments in the construction of telephone calls, drops reaching on dry asphalt, mutations in chromosomes exposed to irradiation, etc., etc. But, of course, for Poisson’s law to come into force, certain conditions must be met, otherwise natural and sufficiently general. If, for example, the biting of α-particles is considered, care must be taken that the intensity of the radiation does not change during the observation time; if telephone calls are registered, it is necessary that the callers do not agree with each other or with the owner’s telephone about the time of conversation, etc. These conditions and the consequences arising from them are discussed in more detail in the article “How the Poisson distribution arises.”
