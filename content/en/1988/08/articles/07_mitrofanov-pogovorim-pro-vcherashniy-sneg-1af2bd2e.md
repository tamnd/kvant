LET'S TALK
ABOUT YESTERDAY'S SNOW

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. V. MITROFANOV

Perhaps some of you, while reading the terms of the problem, have already agreed on what the essence of the matter is. The answer is: the ski tracks are very different from each other because different numbers of skiers have passed on them at different times. Why did this have such a big impact on the appearance of the ski track? Let's try to figure it out.

Snow that has fallen on the ground is a very complex physical object that continuously changes over time. You know that long before the spring melt, the snow settles, compacts, can become denser or, conversely, more fishable, lose weight, “weather.” At the same time, many physical characteristics of snow change, for example, the coefficient of thermal conductivity, porosity, humidity, etc. Human intervention - say, snow compaction when laying ski tracks or a pedestrian road - also changes the appearance of the snow cover, giving the snow properties that distinguish it in some way from untouched snow.

The amazing variability of the state of snow in nature is due to the fact that snow does not simply exist on its own, but participates in direct and reverse physical transitions ice - steam and ice - water. These processes are especially active in the spring, when the snow is in conditions close to the so-called triple point of water. Let us recall that at the triple point there are three forms of matter - gaseous (in this case water vapor), liquid

(water) and solid (ice) coexist in equilibrium. For water, the triple point (Fig. 1) is located in temperature near 0 °C (more precisely at $t_0=0,0078$ °C); pressure of saturated water vapor corresponding to the triple point, $p_0=610,6$ Pa (or, in other units, 0.006 atm, or 4.58 mm pt. art.).

The speed of phase transitions for snow depends on a number of factors, for example, how the sun is shining, how warm or frosty it is outside, what the humidity is, whether there is a warm wind blowing, etc. Small, very insignificant changes in the initial state of the snow can lead to a significant deviation in the speed of one or another process and ultimately have a strong impact on the characteristics of the snow cover. This is exactly what happens on the flat track.

When the trap became so compact that an ice crust formed on it, the trappers stopped using this trap and continued using a new one nearby. If the vapor pressure in the air is less than this value, then the snow will evaporate (solid-vapor transition), and more importantly, without even starting to melt! And here's what's interesting. The pressure of saturated vapor above a curved surface of a liquid differs from the pressure above a flat surface.*) The same is true for a solid body in equilibrium with vapor. Small snow particles sublimate faster than large ones. The thinner the edge of the snowflakes, the greater its curvature, and, therefore, the faster it evaporates. On embedded surfaces, the snow curvature is reversed, and there evaporation can be replaced by crystallization of moisture if the snow temperature is low. This process is similar to the condensation of a liquid in narrow pores or capillaries, only in this case the phase transition occurs bypassing the liquid state.

As a result, steam crystallizes at the intersection of the rays of snow curvature where different snow patches touch. Not only does the shape of the snow curve change (remember snow floes in the form of grains on the roads at the end of winter), but also individual snow patches freeze. Large dense areas of pre-compacted snow freeze faster. Snow hardens and can turn into ice in places where it has already become hard. In the future, the fate of the ice flats is different from that of the snowdrifts neighboring it, where the snow sublimates faster under the influence of the sun and wind. It’s funny that there is a kind of greenhouse effect: the ice produces sunlight, and the heat exchange between the snow under the ice and the outside space is small - wind and convection under the ice crust are practically absent. On a sunny frosty day, the snow heats up under the ice, sublimates faster and may even begin to melt. Under the ice, the snow begins to disappear!
This is precisely what the rustle of falling floes of ice “tells” us on a fine spring day.

Let's now turn to the left photo, which shows the light beam. This tray is relatively new. In February or early March, shortly before the shooting, a lot of flatters drove along the lake. They thoroughly compacted the snow, which the blizzards had added a lot to. The snow on the flat is comparatively lighter, lighter. But why is the line lighter than the background and why is it higher than the general snow level?

One might have assumed that snow had fallen the day before, and the wind had blown it onto a larger field than nearby in the open; the next day the snow around melted or evaporated, but it remained on the bed, which is why the bed is white. But a line similar to the one that was formed appears light against the background of snow, regardless of whether the snow passed the day before or not. Light-colored flies are often found near cities in the spring, when the snow is melting with might and main; it happens that almost all the snow on the ground has melted, and traces of the snowflakes still stretch in white stripes along the ground...

Let's offer another explanation. A bright snowy snowy background is an “autograph” of an interesting physical process, which could be called radiation instability of the boundaries of dirty snow, if we use scientific terminology. The word “dirt” means dust, sugar, ash, the word pollution that enters the snow from the atmosphere. Dirt, unlike snow, absorbs visible radiation well: it is black! Dirt particles are in contact with snow. Therefore, dirty snow is better warmed by the sun than clean snow, and therefore evaporates (or melts) faster. But the more the layer of snow evaporates, the dirtier its surface will become: dirt does not evaporate, but accumulates on the “moving” border of the snow cover. And the dirty surface will be warmed up even more by the sun, etc. Therefore, if in any place of the snow cover the snow evaporates faster, then this place will be “blacker” than the areas adjacent to it, and in the future the snow evaporates faster from this place. Under the influence of sunlight, the initially flat snow surface becomes embossed with light (high) and dark (low) areas.

Now let's look at the “black” marks (middle photo). It seems that this lie has never been “rigged.” Most likely, here two or three ice-catchers passed by and left a crushed, but not yet very dense trail. Subsequently, under the influence of the sun, the snow on such a bed evaporates faster than the surrounding one (a recessed bed is better protected from the cold wind than snow in an open place). Having become dark, the lizhne even evaporates even faster. And in the end, it stands out from the surrounding background thanks to the same processes that are “to blame” for the existence of white grass on dirty snow.

The “black” lie has one interesting feature, which not everyone will pay attention to. The hotter the sun gets, the hotter it becomes. The above can be formulated in the language of physics, using certain albeby and optical constants of water and snow.

Albebo of a matte surface is the total, or, as they say, integral, coefficient of reflection or scattering of light, i.e., the ratio of the reflected (or scattered) light flux to the incident one. Pure snow almost completely scatters the light falling on it. The albebo of snow is usually about 0.9, and sometimes (for the purest snow) it even reaches a value of 0.98.

Snow covered or washed away with water is no longer a matte surface, but smooth in the optical sense. Such a surface reflects light according to the law of geometric optics known to us: a small part of the incident light flux is reflected from the smooth surface of water (Fig. 2). With normal padding, the coefficient of light reflection from the water surface is close to 2%.

Qualitatively, what happens with a dark glass is similar to a simple experiment when a little clean water is poured into a glass of granulated sugar: with suitable lighting, the contents of the glass immediately darken.

Do not think that the formation of relief on a snow or ice surface is associated only with the presence of dirt. Let's imagine that for some reason the surface of pure snow has become a little uneven, with protrusions or depressions, even small ones. Then different parts of the surface are inclined differently in relation to the incident solar rays. Therefore, the surface of the snow in different places will not be equally illuminated by the sun. And if such or the sublimation of snow is determined mainly by the level of solar radiation, and not by other factors (say, a warm wind), then the sharper snow protrusions directed towards the sun will “bleed off” under the sun more slowly than the flat holes near the protrusions, i.e. the relief of the snow surface will become more pronounced than it was originally. Snow cones or sharp tongues form on clean snow. This usually happens when the sun is shining brightly, but there is no warm wind. Such conditions often occur high in the mountains, and in the spring, under the bright sun, amazingly beautiful ice teeth, called calgospores, “grow” on the glaciers, reaching heights of about a meter.

This type of surface relief formation is familiar to specialists in radiation physics of polymers. In polymer films irradiated with hard ultraviolet radiation, the effect of photo-etching of the surface is observed, and photo-etching of the first surface of smooth surfaces is accompanied by the growth of free “calgospores” - uneven heights, sometimes of a finite shape (a micrograph of such a surface is placed on the first page of the cover). Of course, for polymers the scale is smaller: the size of irregularities is on the order of micrometers. In this case, as for snow, there is radiation instability of the solid phase boundary.
