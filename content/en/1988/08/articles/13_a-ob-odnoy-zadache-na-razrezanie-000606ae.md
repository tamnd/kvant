When is it more difficult to remove the stopper from a thermok?
For the second experiment, you will need a thermos, preferably with a narrow stopper that tightly closes the neck, but does not fully fit into it. First, boil water in a kettle, pour it into a thermos, pour it out a little and tightly close the thermos with a stopper. After a few hours (from two to ten, depending on the design of the thermos), try to remove the stopper from the neck - it will stick so tightly and be drawn into the thermos that it will be difficult for you to remove it. (That’s why you need to take a cork that is high enough from the neck.) If you leave the thermos not empty, but with boiling water, then the effect of the cork coming out will either not be at all, or it will be very weak. Well, what if the thermos is filled with boiling water, say, a quarter or half? It would seem that the force of protrusion should be intermediate between the first two cases. It turns out that no, the effect will be only slightly stronger than in the case of a full thermos. Let's try to figure out what's what.

A distinctive property of a thermos is its low heat output. In a good liter thermos, the water cools down by only 2-3 degrees per day. Considering that the removed heat of water is 4.2 kJ/(kg K), we can estimate the amount of heat given off by a thermos per day: ≈10 kJ. An empty thermos flask has a mass of approximately 200 g. The specific heat of the thermos material (metal, glass) can be considered equal to 0.5 kJ/(kg K), so a heat transfer of 10 kJ corresponds to a decrease in temperature by approximately 100 °C. This means that the thermos simply reaches room temperature, i.e. 80 °C. Such a drop in temperature, according to Charles’s law, corresponds to a pressure difference of 0.2 atm, which, with a plug cross-section of ≈5 cm², gives a fairly noticeable force - approximately 10 N.

Since the removed thermal capacity of water is almost 10 times greater than the removed thermal capacity of glass, then already 100 g of water in a thermos reduces the temperature difference, and therefore the pressure, by 4 times; when the thermos is a quarter full, the pressure difference will be 7 times less, and when half filled, it will be 15 times less than in an empty thermos.

Explain why
Try to explain the last simple experiment yourself. Fill the thermos halfway with very hot milk, close the stopper and shake. You will see how the milk bubbles up from the stopper - air comes out of the thermos. Why?
