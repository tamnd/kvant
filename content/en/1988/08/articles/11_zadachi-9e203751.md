The watch you see on figure literally blew my mind. Sealed in a glass container - a sort of house “without windows, without doors” - they are tightly isolated from the outside world. On the dial there is the inscription “Lecoultra” - this is the name of the famous Swiss watch company. And this brand of watches is called “eternal”. It is impossible to start them, but they go on without stopping. And no batteries, wires, etc. Perpetual motion machine?!

But you and I are literate people and we know that there cannot be a perpetual motion machine. The energy must be supplied to the clock somehow. Solar battery? But the watch runs fine even in the dark.

I admit, I did not reveal the secret of the “eternal” watches from Lecoultra. It turns out that the whole point is in thermal distribution, which is what we are talking about. The clock contains a cylinder of ethyl chloride, which, by distribution or slope, reacts very sensitively to small fluctuations in room temperature. Changing the temperature by 1 degree winds it up for 28 hours!

Of course, this is an original application of thermal distribution. And although the phenomenon itself is probably known to everyone, it is not so easy to understand what causes it. In any case, there are some things that need to be sorted out. In this country we will talk mainly about the thermal distribution of solids.

You, of course, know that temperature is associated with the movement of atoms and molecules that make up bodies. This is usually explained using the example of liquids or gases. In particular, the pressure of gases on the walls of a vessel arises due to their bombardment by molecules. The higher the temperature, the more intense the movement of molecules and the higher the pressure. And vice versa. Car enthusiasts probably know that during the autumn-winter cold spell, the tires need to be pumped up - the pressure in them drops.

But the intensity of atomic and molecular motion when heated is a common property of all bodies, including solids. We just need to understand how exactly atoms can move in a solid body.

In ancient times, when the wise Greeks had just “invented” atoms, they imagined them in the form of balls. At first glance it seems that this is a very rough model. And this is really true. But it also turns out to be very fruitful. It is no coincidence that one of the famous physicists of our time, Nobel laureate Neville Mott, remarked on this occasion that “to explain the many tails of metals, the atomic model of Lucretius is sufficient”*).

Most solids (particularly metallic ones) are crystals. The atoms in them are located at the sites of a regular crystal lattice. The atoms in the lattice are packed quite tightly. There are no large gaps where they can penetrate during their movement. Therefore, practically the only way of movement available to atoms in a crystal is oscillations near their “native” nodes**).

Often, to describe such movement, an atom is represented as “dangling” in an external node. The higher the temperature, the faster the atom moves, and the more it stretches the grains in its movement. In other words, the amplitude of oscillations of atoms near their nodes returns at

Considering atoms in the form of oscillating outer solid balls, we can easily explain the tell distribution. When they oscillate, the balls move closer together, and since they are solid, the neighbors, when they stand together, begin to prevent each other from oscillating. But remember that when heated, the amplitude of oscillations should increase. And for this to happen, the atoms need to expand. And this is the tell distribution.

Let's turn now to the numbers. The magnitude of the tell distribution of solids is usually characterized by the tell distribution coefficient $\alpha$, which shows how much a rod of a single length is removed from a given material when heated by $1^{\circ}\text{C}$. For example, for platinum $\alpha=9,0 \cdot 10^{-6}$ deg$^{-1}$. This means that when heated to $1^{\circ}\text{C}$, a one-meter platium rod will be removed by 9 micrometers.

One might think that this is nonsense that is not even worth considering. But take your time. Due to seasonal temperature fluctuations, the famous Eiffel Tower - the “iron madame” - is 15 cm higher in summer than in winter. And all because iron has a relatively high tell distribution coefficient $\alpha=12,2 \times 10^{-6}$ deg$^{-1}$. How do you feel about the fact that your car's pistons will jam because of the telltale distribution? Or does the caliber of the gun barrel change? Or will the correspondence in size of the parts of some complex and delicate mechanism be slightly disrupted? Therefore, it is clear how important it is to have a material with the ability to distribute heat. Platinum (and even better, platinum-day alloys with $\alpha=8,3.10^{-6}$ deg$^{-1}$) looks very attractive in this sense. It is no coincidence that the length of the platyronide meter has a long service life. But it’s clear that they won’t go broke for long. Isn't there some kind of alloy that is “cheaper” and with an even lower thermal distribution coefficient?

In 1896, an alloy of iron and nickel with a surprisingly low thermal distribution coefficient of about $1,5 \times 10^{-6}$ deg$^{-1}$ was invented in France. It is called invar (from the Latin invariablis - unchanging). Invar was first used in 1899 to produce the length of the arc of the earth's meridian, determined by the Russian-Swedish expedition to the polar archipelago of Spitsbergen. When delivered to warmer Europe, the length of the Invar eton remained almost unchanged.

The mechanism of invarity was understood much later and turned out to be very simple. Here we will only try to explain the idea.*)

Even in the past, the phenomenon of magnetostriction was discovered, which consists of a change in the size of a body when it is magnetized, which usually occurs when a magnetic field is applied from the outside. However, there is a group of materials that are magnetized spontaneously (without an external magnet) when the temperature changes. These, in particular, include iron: when it cools, magnetization begins at a temperature of $769^{\circ}\text{C}$ and continues with further cooling. Such magnetization is also accompanied by a magnetostrictive effect. All that remains is to detail the material in which the tell distribution and magnetostriction would compensate each other. This is the secret of the Invars!
