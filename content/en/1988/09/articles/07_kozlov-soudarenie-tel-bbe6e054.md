Introduction

Some physical theories are built on the model adopted in mathematics - from a small number of physical statements that play the same role as axioms in mathematical theories, various logical consequences are strictly deduced. This article is devoted to one such theory - the theory of collision of bodies. The impact phenomenon is well described by a simple mathematical model. Immovable therefore, the laws of impact were established before the discovery of the basic principles of dynamics.

Galileo also carried out a series of experiments to clarify the laws of collision of bodies. These experiments, however, did not lead him to definite conclusions. A contemporary of Galileo, the Pryazhsky professor Marzi, in his work “De proportione motus” (1639), published some of the results of his studies of the phenomenon of impact. In particular, he knew that a body, having elastically hit an identical body at rest, loses its speed, imparting it to this body. The first detailed study of the laws of impact was undertaken in 1668 at the suggestion of the Royal Society of London. With outstanding mechanics and mathematicians Wallis, Rehn and Heugens presented their works in which they outlined the laws of motion of colliding bodies.

John Wallis limited himself, without elaborating on this, to considering an absolutely inelastic impact. He proceeded from hypotheses about the conservation of the total momentum of colliding bodies.

Christopher Wren outlined the rules for calculating elastic impact. Wren, like Wallis, did not provide any theoretical considerations, but to test his rules he performed a series of simple and convincing experiments. Newton referred to these experiments in his famous “Mathematical Principles of Natural Physiophy” (1687). Christin Huygen's competition meroir was the most complete study of the theory of impact. It outlined the derivation of correspondences to the impact theory, based on Galileo's principle of relative. The Royal Society of London published only the meroirs of Wallis and Wren. Conservation of Momentum

Let us consider a number of problems in mechanics related to the study of the collision of two bodies with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, moving along a straight line without the influence of any forces. Let $v_1$ and $v_2$ be the velocities of these bodies before impact (Fig. 1). We will consider the speed of an algebraic quantity: if a body moves to the right, then its speed is positive, and if it moves to the left, then it is negative. At the moment of impact, only internal forces act on the body; therefore their total momentum $m_1v_1+m_2v_2$ is conserved. Let $v'_1$ and $v'_2$ be the velocities of the bodies after the impact. From the conservation of the total momentum we obtain the relation

$$m_1v_1+m_2v_2=m_1v'_1+m_2v'_2.$$ (1)

From this equation alone, of course, it is impossible to find $v'_1$ and $v'_2$. This is not surprising: collisions are different. There are elastic and inelastic. For example, during an absolutely inelastic impact, the bodies come together and then move as one whole. In this case $v'_1=v'_2=v$ and from formula (1) we find the speed of the colliding bodies after the impact:

$$v=\frac{m_1v_1+m_2v_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$ (2)

Change in kinetic energy

In addition to momentum, kinetic energy plays an important role in mechanics, equal to $mv^2/2$, where $m$ is the mass of the body, $v$ is its speed. Let us consider an absolutely inelastic collision of two bodies. In this case

$$\Delta T=(\frac{m_1v_1^2}{2}+\frac{m_2v_2^2}{2})-\frac{(m_1+m_2)v^2}{2}$$

called lost kinetic energy.

The following statement is called Carnot's first theorem:

The lost kinetic energy is equal to the energy of a point with mass $\mu=$

$$\frac{1}{1/m_1+1/m_2},$$

which moves at a speed equal to the difference in the speed of the point before the impact:

$$\Delta T=\frac{\mu(v_1-v_2)^2}{2}.$$

Task 2. Prove the first Carnot theorem.

The mass $\mu$ is called in mechanics the reduced mass of the system. It is equal to half the harmonic mean of the numbers $m_1$ and $m_2$.

Task 3. Prove the inequalities

$$\mu \leq \frac{m_1+m_2}{4}, \mu \leq \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{m_1m_2}.$$

Let us introduce the lost velocities: $u_1=v-v_1$, $u_2=v-v_2$, with the help of which the second Carnot theorem is formulated:

The lost kinetic energy is equal to the total kinetic energy of bodies with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, moving with lost velocities $u_1$ and $u_2$:

$$\Delta T=\frac{m_1u_1^2}{2}+\frac{m_2u_2^2}{2}.$$

Task 4. Prove Carnot's second theorem.

Adinabatic invariant

As a respectable application of formulas (5), consider the following problem. The ball moves between two walls, one of which is stationary, and the other approaches it with speed $V$ (Fig. 3; the ball starts moving from the stationary wall). The impact of the ball on the walls is absolutely elastic. After a collision with a stationary wall, the speed of the ball does not change, and after each collision with a moving wall, the speed of the ball (see formulas (5)) increases by $2V$. Consequently, the energy of the ball also increases. Let $v_0$ be the value of the ball’s speed at the initial moment of time (i.e., the moment of the “zero” impact on a stationary wall), $l_0$ be the distance between the walls at this moment. Similarly, by $v_n$ and $l_n$ we denote the speed of the ball and the distance between the walls at the moment of the $n$ impact of the ball on a stationary wall. Let's put $I_n = v_n l_n$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$).

Problem 9. Prove that $I_n = I_0$.

Let's define the problem. Let us introduce a function $I(t)$ equal to the product of the velocity $v(t)$ of the ball and the distance $l(t)$ between the walls at an arbitrary time $t$. Let $t_0, t_1, \dots$ be the moment of impact of the ball on a stationary wall. According to the statement of Problem 9, $I(t_n) = I_0$ for all $n \geqslant 0$. However, with other values ​​of $t$ we get $I(t) \neq I_0$.

Problem 10. Prove the inequality

$$\left| \frac{I(t) - I_0}{I_0} \right| \leq \frac{V}{v_0 + V}.$$

In Huygen's temugar "On the motion of bodies under the influence of impact" the law of conservation of momentum is derived from the principle relative to the Galilean matrix. We will reproduce Huygen's reasoning for the case of an absolutely inelastic impact.

Let us turn to the derivation of formula (2) for an abscilton inelastic impact.

Geugen's reasoning involves the analysis of several cases.

a) Let us first consider the simplest situation when two identical bodies with masses $m$, moving towards each other at speeds $v$ and $-v$, collide. Due to the assumption of the inelastic nature of the impact, the bodies stick together, forming one body with mass $2m$. According to Symmetrine, this body will be rummaged after the impact.

b) Let us consider a more complex case when two identical masses $m$ collide, having arbitrary velocities $v_1$ and $v_2$ until the moment of impact. Let us move on to the new inertial reporting system $S'$, which moves relative to the original system $S$ with a constant speed $v = (v_1 + v_2)/2$. In the system $S'$ the picture of the body’s motion will be as in case a): bodies of the same mass collide with equal velocities $(v_1 - v_2)/2$ and $(v_2 - v_1)/2$. According to the relative principle and the conclusion of point a), after an impact a body with mass $2m$ will dig into the system $S'$. Consequently, relative to the system $S$ it will move at a speed $(v_1 + v_2)/2$. So, formula (2) is established in the case when $m_1 = m_2$.

c) Let now there be three identical bodies of each mass $m$ (Fig. 6). At the initial moment, they will all dig around, with bodies 1 and 2 touching. Let's assume that bodies 1 and 2 are separated in different directions (for example, as a result of an explosion). Due to symmetry, their speeds will be equal in magnitude. The first body will continuously move at speed $-v$, and the second will move to the right with speed $v$ and will soon collide with body 3. According to the result of point 6), the body “2+3” with mass $2m$ will, after the impact, move to the right with speed $v/2$. If the distance

It is stated that after an absolute inelastic impact, a body of mass $3m$ will fall off (in accordance with formula (2)). Indeed, we can imagine a new reporting system $S'$ in which the clock runs “backward.” Relative to the system $S'$, all bodies will move in the opposite direction with the same absolute velocity, so we only have to use the relative principle and the result of point c).

e) Now let us turn to the case when bodies with masses $m$ and $2m$ move with arbitrary velocities $v_1$ and $v_2$. As in the reasoning of point 6), let us move on to the new reporting system $S'$, which moves relative to $S$ at a speed $v_1 + 2v_2/3$. It is at this speed that their common center of mass moves (check it out!). In system $S'$ the center of mass of body $m$ and $2m$ accumulates, body $m$ approaches it with speed

$$v_1' = \frac{v_1 + 2v_2}{3} - v_1 = -\frac{2}{3}(v_1 - v_2),$$

and the body $2m$ - with speed

$$v_2' = \frac{v_1 + 2v_2}{3} - v_2 = \frac{1}{3}(v_1 - v_2).$$

We see that the relative speeds $v_1'$ and $v_2'$ differ in sign and $|v_1'| = |2v_2'|$. Therefore, according to point d), after a collision, a body of mass $3m$ will dig into the system $S'$, and relative to the original system $S$ it will move at a speed

$$v = \frac{mv_1 + 2mv_2}{3m} = \frac{v_1 + 2v_2}{3}.$$

Thus, formula (2) is established in the case when $m_2 = 2m_1$.

Reasoning similarly, we can justify formula (2) for the case of a rational ratio of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$. Its justification for irrational relations $m_1/m_2$ is already formulaic in nature and is associated with the construction of the concept of a real number. The situation here is the same as with the derivation of the formula for the area of ​​a rectagon from the sides $a$ and $b$: for commensurate $a$ and $b$ the rectagon can be divided into identical squares, and if $a$ and $b$ are immeasurable, then it is necessary to use the approximation of irrational rational numbers.

From a mathematical point of view, Goygenas's deceptions may not be considered completely rigorous. But we did not strive to give a rigorous proof of the formula for absolute inelastic impact. We had a goal: to show that Geugenas's anden necessarily lead to the law of conservation of momentum. We emphasize that we proceeded only from Galileo’s principle of relative relativity and did not use the basic principles of Newton’s dynamics (for example, the law of equality of action and reaction). Geugenas's dissection shows that the law of conservation of momentum follows from the symmetry (or invariance) of the laws of mechanics of Galileo's relative transformation. This reflects a general fact: every symmetry of the laws of mechanics leads to its own conservation law. For example, the invariance of relative rotations of space is the law of conservation of angular momentum, and the invariance of relative shifts of the time axis is the law of conservation of energy. A general theorem relating symmetries to conservation laws was discovered by the German mathematician Emmy Noether (1882–1935).
