A dispute that lasted centuries

The dream of flight—of flapping flight—is as old, if not as old as the world, then, in any case, as old as the history of developed human consciousness. The ancients, having before their eyes the example of birds, could not imagine any other way of flight. Such is the legend of Icarus.
This is also the first attempt preserved in history to comprehend flight from the point of view of the scientific knowledge of that time. Leonardo da Vinci did it. It took hundreds of more years of observations and experiments to come to that very important idea for us that it is possible to fly on a fixed wing. Now we have gone so far along this path that we have left behind the feathered flyers in almost all respects, although not a single flying machine “controls its body” perfectly, like a bird.

Cruise flight

We will call cruising a horizontal flight in a mode that provides the most convenient focus in the field of package-space technology M.K. Tikhonravov in his book “The Flight of Birds and Machines with Flapping Wings.” Moreover, exactly the same attempts were repeated in the 30s of our century and, at least in projects, live indestructibly to this day. The legend of a man's flapping flight has been alive since the times of the ancient Greeks; only its content has changed. In the bird's mesmerizing lightness, the ancient myth-makers saw hope for the realization of flight, but today's mythmakers - they are artificial, because man flew - is a fool, a perfection that has not been achieved by us, firmly believing that the creation of nature is always and in everything above the timid created mind.

Safety issues aside, flapping flight still attracts a huge number of enthusiasts today. Its apparent ease encourages amateurs to build a model and submit ingenious projects, in particular the confidence that they know the secret that finally allows a person to make flapping flight.

No less impressive is the fact that in terms of load per unit of power (see note 1), birds are many times greater than airplanes. It is very true that an apparatus capable of transferring 1000 N for each kilowatt of power is unequally beneficial to another, which transfers (all other things being equal) only 100 N. Consequently, as the identity of flapping flight states, in terms of energy, a bird is many times more perfect than an airplane.

But, let’s not forget, “other things being equal”! What is their equality?

Like any energy process, the flight of a bird (or an airplane) obeys the law of conservation of energy, which for any object moving at a constant speed $v$ in a resisting medium can be represented as $N\eta_{\text{дв}}=F_cv$, where $N$ is the power consumed during movement, $\eta_{\text{дв}}$ is the efficiency of the propulsion device, $F_c$ is the resistance force of the medium (at a given speed). So, the criterion of energy perfection is $K_s$. It is quite natural that the magnitude $K_s$ cardinally affects the range of horizontal flight $L$. According to Wrege's formula,

$$L = -\frac{K_s}{C_y} \frac{\lg(1-\bar{m}_{\text{rop}})}{\lg e},$$

where $C_y$ is the specific fuel consumption, $\bar{m}_{\text{rop}} = m_{\text{rop}}/m_0$ is the relative mass of fuel, $m_0$ is the starting mass of the object$^3$).

It is clear from the formula that absolute dimensions and mass do not affect the flight range. Thus, comparing the miniature size of birds with the distances covered by them is more a matter of emotion than of scientific argumentation. When assessing the actual factors at play, comparison here does not reveal any advantage of birds.

Flight data is free research material. It is enough to study the distance traveled and the relative mass loss well (and this does not seem difficult) to obtain a reliable assessment of the energetic perfection of the object.

Unfortunately, this data on birds is usually insufficient. In the information about the mentioned flight of plovers, neither the weight of the birds nor the wind speed are indicated; Moreover, there is no indication that the birds did not feed along the way. The flight of lapwings across the Atlantic is accompanied by a strong tailwind - up to 95 km/h; weight loss remained unchanged. Complete and reliable experimental data are available only for one case: the migration of flocks of American moths Dendroica striata from Massachusetts (USA) to Bermuda (a distance of 1300 km). Flight speed was controlled by radio; the wind on the route was taken into account. The weight loss averaged 0.165 from the starting value.

Correspondence of data on aircraft: the relative weight loss of the obsolete LI-2 aircraft is 0.182 with a flight range of 2090 km, the ANT-25 aircraft, which flew from Moscow to St. Petersburg (USA) in 1937, is 0.542 with a range of 13,000 km. Using the known values ​​of the specific heat of combustion of aviation fuel and the “fuel” (fat) of birds, as well as the thermal efficiency, from these data it is possible to determine $K_s$: for poultry $K_s=10,6$, for the LI-2 aircraft $K_s=8,7$, for ANT-25 $K_s=13,9$. These data confirm the lack of energetic superiority of birds. It is interesting to note that the relative mass loss of 0.165 for the ANT-25 aircraft corresponds to a range of only 3300 km.

Takeoff, landing, on-site deposit

Yes, the deciders of the flight machine will say, but a bird takes off and lands vertically, and for airplanes that lack this ability, we are forced to build expensive runways.

Here, as they say, nothing can be said: we can create a device that takes off like a helicopter and flies horizontally like an airplane only at the cost of enormous fuel consumption on takeoff. It is necessary, however, to make some clarifications.

From Newton’s second law it follows that the lifting force $P$ of any diapasic support body (wing, main rotor) is equal to the product of the second mass of the air grown downwards $m_s$ by the vertical speed of the grown jet. Denoting the area swept by the supporting body (Fig. 2) through s and keeping in mind that the speed of the regrown jet $v_2$ in the injection mode is twice the speed in the plane of the supporting body $v_1$, we find the lifting force equal to the apparatus: $P=G=m_s v_2=2 m_s v_1=2 \rho s v_1^2$ (ρ is the air density at the ground)$^4$.

Not all of the energy expended goes to retracting the jet; we take into account its losses (due to swirling of the jet, due to the friction of the supporting organ against the air), we enter the “relative efficiency” $\eta_0$ and find the power required to support units of weight:

$$\frac{N_{\text{потр}}}{G} = \frac{1}{\eta_0 \sqrt{2 \eta}} \left(\frac{G}{s}\right)^{1/2}.$$

It is obvious that the determining factor for the occurrence of vertical takeoff and hovering is the so-called load on the swept area $G/s$. This leads to important conclusions that are fully confirmed by practice.

Under the condition of geometric and kinematic similarity (which coincides satisfactorily within one biological species), the swept area changes proportionally to the square of the linear dimensions, the mass - proportionally to the cube; the load on the area is therefore proportional to the linear dimension. This explains the fact mentioned above: only relatively small birds - no larger than a pigeon - have vertical take-off and the ability to “quiver” flight (hover). Larger ones - for example, crows - are able to carry out precise take-off (from a standstill), use a powerful push with their legs; even larger ones - loon, pelican, flamingo - take off like an airplane, with a running start. Finally, the condor, albatross and other large birds can only begin flight by throwing themselves from emerging objects - trees, rocks, coastal dunes.

In addition - and this should not be forgotten by enthusiasts who dream of performing a vertical take-off on an artificial aircraft - this can only be achieved if the wings move in a horizontal plane to the take-off mode - otherwise it is impossible to obtain a sufficiently large swept area. In birds, the transition from an almost vertical max in cruising flight to an almost horizontal plane during takeoff and landing is carried out without difficulty due to flexibility and the ability to greatly change the inclination of the body. It is not possible to build such a full-scale artificial apparatus with the current level of technology.

Thus, the main source of the “ability” of insects and small birds that generates our feelings to take off vertically and perform a fluttering flight is the simple construction of a small load on the area swept by the wing.

We see that promises of an energy-dependent artificial flapping flight apparatus that does everything that a bird “can do” must be treated with great caution.
