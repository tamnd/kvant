Liquid nitrogen and copper nut
V.V. UTESHEV

Once in the laboratory we observed how liquid nitrogen boiled into a round copper nut floating in it. The boiling process proceeded very interestingly and not at all as one might expect. Liquid nitrogen began to boil immediately after the nut was immersed in it, but a few seconds later, just before completion, boiling was suddenly heard sharply.

It is clear that the reason for boiling is the large difference in the initial temperatures of the nut and nitrogen. But the question arises: why does the nut cool down not monotonously, not smoothly, as one might assume, but has two modes of intensity, and the change of modes occurs very quickly (in a jump)?

Any boiling process is always associated with heat transfer. In our case - from the nut to the nitrogen. Therefore, the answer to the question about the boiling characteristic can be found by analyzing the heat transfer mechanism.

As is known, heat from one body to another can be transferred in different ways, for example, through thermal conductivity or convection. In convection, heat is transferred by moving and moving large masses of matter heated to different temperatures.

In contrast to convection, with thermal conduction the transfer of heat is not accompanied by the movement of matter.

In nature, convective phenomena occur literally at every step. Sometimes they want to actively use them, sometimes, on the contrary, they want to get rid of them. The maximum temperature relieves the nest from convective currents and thereby retains heat well. True, we can say that there is also thermal conductivity of air. But, as experience shows, it plays an insignificant role in heat transfer (compared to convection).

In technology, especially in all kinds of cooling systems, convection most often plays a positive role, and all kinds of devices strengthen it. In conventional automation, for example, a radiator is used for this purpose. When automatic air moves, the process of artificial air convection - flowing through the radiator, the moving masses of air “collect” heat with the heating of the engine.

The most intense convective events on Earth occur in the atmosphere. Air temperature at different altitudes varies within very wide limits. A large area of ​​air has less density and therefore rises, while colder areas, on the contrary, fall, resulting in convection. It is curious that in zero-gravity the temperature difference between different air does not lead to natural convection, therefore, for example, a match in a spaceship cannot burn in the usual way. It lights up and immediately goes out, since there is no convective flow of oxygen necessary for combustion. However, you can create artificial convection, say, by blowing lightly towards a match, and thus maintain combustion.

In physics, to quantitatively characterize heat transfer processes, a special physical quantity is introduced - the thermal energy flux density (tell). This is the energy passing through a unit area per unit time. For convection, the heat flux density $W$ is directly related to the substance flux density $q$, i.e., with the mass of substance passing through a unit area per unit time: $W \sim q$. With thermal conductivity, it is renamed no, and the heat flux density is determined by how quickly the temperature of the body changes from one part of it to another: $W \sim \Delta T / \Delta x$ (here $T$ is temperature, $x$ is coordinate).

Now let's return to the case with the nut and liquid nitrogen. First, let's explain it theoretically.

Heat in the nut is distributed according to the principle of ordinary thermal conductivity. In nitrogen, heat is transferred by convection due to the moving masses of evaporated nitrogen. But none of these heat transfer methods explain the original characteristics of liquid nitrogen. What's the matter?

It turns out that the most interesting process occurs at the point of contact of the nut with liquid nitrogen, where the temperature changes abruptly. At this contact, the energy flux density can be represented in the form: $W = a \Delta T$, where $a$ is a certain effective heat transfer coefficient, and $\Delta T$ is the same temperature jump between the nitrogen and the nut. The boiling fasteners described at the beginning of the movement correspond to different values ​​of $a$, and the transition from one value to another occurs abruptly at the moment when the temperature of the nut reaches a certain certain value.
