FRIEDMAN
(on the 100th anniversary of his birth)

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. Y. FRANKEL

It is difficult to determine to which field of science the activities of the remarkable Soviet scientist Alexander Alexandrovich Friedman can be attributed. If we turn to the biographical directory of Physicists, we will find in it a separate article about Alexander Alexandrovits. But an equally informative article is available in a similar reference book “Mathematics and Mechanics”. Finally, in the third similar collection of biographies, “Astronomers,” we will also find the name of Friedman, and at the same time we will learn that one of the criteria for the reliability of the Moon is named in his honor. Who was this compatriot of ours, whose life is rightly called a feat, and whose discovery in the field of cosmology is one of the most revolutionary in science, close in scale to what was made by Copernicus? He was “one in three persons,” or rather, even in four: a physicist, a mechanic, a mathematician, and an astronomer (astrophysicist). The model of the time-varying, expanding Universe that he created back in 1922 is now in the center of attention of astrophysicists; it received experimental confirmation in a number of discovery works, the first of which was carried out by the American astronomer E. Hubble four years after Friedman’s death, and one of the most significant - the discovery of electromagnetic radiation arbitrary in the Universe (referred to as relict radiation) - about a quarter of a century ago. Perhaps, it was from the moment of this discovery made by the American physicists R. Wilson and A. Penzinas that the paradoxical conclusions from Friedman’s theory, which at one time seemed to him to be partly a curiosity, acquired the force of practical unrefuted facts, and the science of the Universe began to develop rapidly.

A. A. Fridman was born in St. Petersburg on June 16, 1888. His father, also Alexander Alexandrovich, was a ballet dancer, conductor, composer, and studied at the St. Petersburg Conservatory with N. A. Rimsky-Korsakov. Mother, Lyudmila Ignatevna Voyachek, daughter of the famous St. Petersburg composer, organizer and conductor I. K. Voyachek, was a pianist. The musical “gene” did not manifest itself in Alexander Fridman: he simply loved music, nothing more. In 1897, he entered the preparatory class of the 2nd St. Petersburg Gymnasium, located not far from house No. 35 on the Moika River embankment, where his family lived.

The hypnasia had a strong teaching staff. Suffice it to say that two of the teachers were authors of gymnasium textbooks. Information on the history of the gymnasium - the oldest in St. Petersburg - can be found in its materials, which form a special fund of the State Historical Archives of Leningrad. In this background, of course, there is information about students and teachers, and materials from meetings of the Pedagogical Council of the gymnasium, and even conduit pages (remember “Conduit and Schwambrania” by Lev Kassil!), in which the work of class guards about the misdeeds and pranks of gymnasium students was punctually entered.

If in the first grade Alexander Fridman studied more than average (in most subjects, including arithmetic, he got C grades), then starting from the third grade he -

Another young man studied in the older class at the 2nd gymnasium, whose name is probably known to the reader of “Kwan” - this was the future famous mathematician, Academician Vladimir Ivanovich Smirnov, Friedman’s postgraduate friend, adviser to his works, author of a five-volume course of higher mathematics. Another unusually gifted young man studied in the same class with Smirnov - Mikhail Petelin, also Fridman’s comrade and adviser. A rare combination of talents, not least, it seems, manifested thanks to the pedagogical abilities of the teacher - mathematicians Ya. V. Iodinsky and P. N. Genzel and physicist I. V. Glinika.

In 1903 (Friedman was then in the sixth grade), I. V. Glinika organized extra-class physics classes in the physics room of the gymnasium, and soon after that a physics circle that met three times a week. The most proactive participants supervised the practical classes of their comrades. Then the circle began to be somewhat solemnly called the “Society of Physics Amateurs of the 2nd St. Petersburg Gymnasium.” Gymnasium students read abstract reports at its meetings, organized their own circle physics library, and decided to publish a leading journal, which contained reviews of the latest achievements in physics made by students.

Friedman was very fond of the gymnasium physics circle and, according to the recollections of one of his university comrades, attended its meetings when he had already become a student.

I tried in vain to find Alexンドra Friedman among the mischievous people whose descriptions of their exploits filled the daily notes in the confectionary department of the 2nd gymnasium. Apparently, he was a rather quiet rightist at first, then, together with Tamarkin, he became seriously interested in mathematics, and even later, in the very last grades, in the revolutionary movement. Here he showed himself to be an experienced conspirator: the elementary school did not even suspect that one of the students was a member of the Central Committee of the St. Petersburg Social Democratic Student Organization. Friedman also had a “party” nickname - “Lilac”.

One day, Friedman and Tamarkin, then eighth grade students, were unusually excited in class and talking. They were even marched out of class. However, when the teacher found out the reason for such excitement, he did not let the “case” proceed. It turned out that it was that morning that the friends learned that their first mathematical work - a paper on number theory - had been accepted for publication by a famous German mathematical journal and received the approval of the greatest mathematician - D. Hilbert.

Friedman graduated from high school with a gold medal.

Moreover, it can both increase and decrease. Our Universe can expand for a long time (the name “expanding Universe” is clearly associated with the name of Friedman and corresponds to the thread of the phase he is experiencing). Whether this expansion will be limitless or will be replaced by compression depends on what the average density $\rho$ of materials in the Universe is. If $\rho$ is less than some critical value $\rho_k \approx 10^{-29}$ g/cm$^3$, then the expansion will continue. If $\rho > \rho_k$, then it will be replaced by compression; The universe is brought to a “point”, which is called the point of singularity. After this, a “Big Explosion” will occur - and again the substance, concentrated to the required densities, begins to move back towards expansion. The “scenario” of what will happen after the Big Bang has already been reconstructed in many details by theoretical physicists and astrophysicists. It is quickly outlined in the book “The First Three Minutes” by American physicist and Nobel Prize winner Steven Weinberg. It is interesting to note that the very idea of ​​the division (or, better yet, the rebirth) of the Universe in the Great Explosion was first formed by the American physicist of Russian origin G. A. Gamow, who in the 20s studied with Friedman at Petrograd University and considered himself his student.

Alexander Alexandrovich Friedman was the first to obtain formulas for determining the growth of the Universe and, based on very inaccurate data, estimated it at 10 billion years. According to current estimates, it is 15-20 billion years. Friedmann's cosmological work, published in 1922 in a leading German physics journal, aroused objections from Einstein, who, as stated, believed the Universe to be Stationary and therefore considered Friedmann's conclusions about the possibility of its evolution in time erroneous. Einstein wrote a short note about this in the same magazine. Friedman wrote a letter to Einstein at the end of 1922, in which he defended his views. The contents of the letter were brought to the attention of Einstein by Friedman’s Petrograd colleague, Professor Yuri Aleksandrovich Krutkov, who was working abroad in the spring of 1923. In May of this year, he met with Einstein several times, and on May 18 he made the following note in his diary: “He defeated Einstein in the Friedman dispute. The honors of Petrograd have been saved!” Einstein sent (all to the same journal) another note in which he rejected his ideas of growth against Friedman's conclusions and recognized his results as outstanding and shedding new light on the gaps of cosmology. The great scientist had to make mistakes before, but later, in 1931, he admitted that he had never made a more annoying mistake in his life than in evaluating Friedman’s work.

Friedman did not have to wait until the triumph of his theory, in which he “at the tip of his pen” discovered the expansion of the Universe and, in general terms, predicted the possible paths of its evolution. As mentioned at the beginning of the article, the evidence came later. Four years after the death of Alexander Alexandrovich, the American astronomer E. Hubble, based on studying the spectra of distant galaxies, showed that galaxies are moving away from us at a speed of $v$, proportional to the distance separating him from us $r$: $\vec{v} = H\vec{r}$. The coefficient of the proportional $H$, which does not depend on $\vec{r}$ (and depends, generally speaking, on time), is called the Hubble constant. According to modern data, $H$ is taken equal to 75 km/(s · Mpc). Note that the Hubble constant, together with the universal gravitation constant $G$, determines the critical density $\rho_k$: $\rho_k = 3H^2/8\pi G \approx 10^{-29}$ g/cm$^3$.

In 1965, R. Wilson and A. Penzas in the USA discovered relict radiation - a direct trace of the Great Explosion. Friedman's work received new and powerful confirmation.

We will not reproduce here the formulas and reasoning that Friedman gave to his conclusions. Let us point out, however, that in 1934 it turned out to be possible to draw a number of important conclusions not in the complex language of the general theory of relativity, which Aleksandr Aleksadrovich used in his works, but in the simpler and more familiar Newtonian language. The remarkable Soviet theoretical physicist Ya. B. Zeldovich spoke about this very clearly on the pages of «Kvant» (No. 3, 1984), who did an extraordinary amount both for the deep development of Friedman’s ideas and for their propaganda.

In the first half of the 20s, Friedman continued to work simultaneously in the field of atmospheric physics and in the field of relativity theory. In July 1925, being the director of the Main Geophysical General Laboratory, he, together with the professional aeronaut pilot P.F. Fedosenko, made a geronic flight into the atmosphere in a hot air balloon; A national altitude record was set (7400 m). Friedman wrote down his impressions of the flight. Fedosenko did the same. Wonderful articles! From them - especially, of course, from Fedosenko's article - we learned that the courageous observer pilot of the First World War, Alexander Friedman, remained the same brave man, becoming a world famous scientist.

In the summer of 1925, for the first time in many years, he went to Crimea for three weeks. Returning home - he arrived in Leningrad on August 17 - Aleksadr Aleksadrovich fell ill with typhoid fever and from this absurd accident died at the age of 37 on September 16, in the prime of his creative powers and talent. Newspapers responded to this sad event with a number of publications. In particular, “Evening Red Gazeta” - the predecessor of “Evening Leningrad” - on September 18 published an interview with the doctor who treated Fridman.
