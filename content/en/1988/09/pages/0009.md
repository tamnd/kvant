⟦folio 7⟧

Moreover, it can both increase and decrease. Our Universe can expand for a long time (the name “expanding Universe” is clearly associated with the name of Friedman and corresponds to the thread of the phase he is experiencing). Whether this expansion will be limitless or will be replaced by compression depends on what the average density $\rho$ of materials in the Universe is. If $\rho$ is less than some critical value $\rho_k \approx 10^{-29}$ g/cm$^3$, then the expansion will continue. If $\rho > \rho_k$, then it will be replaced by compression; The universe is brought to a “point”, which is called the point of singularity. After this, a “Big Explosion” will occur - and again the substance, concentrated to the required densities, begins to move back towards expansion. The “scenario” of what will happen after the Big Bang has already been reconstructed in many details by theoretical physicists and astrophysicists. It is quickly outlined in the book “The First Three Minutes” by American physicist and Nobel Prize winner Steven Weinberg. It is interesting to note that the very idea of ​​the division (or, better yet, the rebirth) of the Universe in the Great Explosion was first formed by the American physicist of Russian origin G. A. Gamow, who in the 20s studied with Friedman at Petrograd University and considered himself his student.

Alexander Alexandrovich Friedman was the first to obtain formulas for determining the growth of the Universe and, based on very inaccurate data, estimated it at 10 billion years. According to current estimates, it is 15-20 billion years. Friedmann's cosmological work, published in 1922 in a leading German physics journal, aroused objections from Einstein, who, as stated, believed the Universe to be Stationary and therefore considered Friedmann's conclusions about the possibility of its evolution in time erroneous. Einstein wrote a short note about this in the same magazine. Friedman wrote a letter to Einstein at the end of 1922, in which he defended his views. The contents of the letter were brought to the attention of Einstein by Friedman’s Petrograd colleague, Professor Yuri Aleksandrovich Krutkov, who was working abroad in the spring of 1923. In May of this year, he met with Einstein several times, and on May 18 he made the following note in his diary: “He defeated Einstein in the Friedman dispute. The honors of Petrograd have been saved!” Einstein sent (all to the same journal) another note in which he rejected his ideas of growth against Friedman's conclusions and recognized his results as outstanding and shedding new light on the gaps of cosmology. The great scientist had to make mistakes before, but later, in 1931, he admitted that he had never made a more annoying mistake in his life than in evaluating Friedman’s work.

Friedman did not have to wait until the triumph of his theory, in which he “at the tip of his pen” discovered the expansion of the Universe and, in general terms, predicted the possible paths of its evolution. As mentioned at the beginning of the article, the evidence came later. Four years after the death of Alexander Alexandrovich, the American astronomer E. Hubble, based on studying the spectra of distant galaxies, showed that galaxies are moving away from us at a speed of $v$, proportional to the distance separating him from us $r$: $\vec{v} = H\vec{r}$. The coefficient of the proportional $H$, which does not depend on $\vec{r}$ (and depends, generally speaking, on time), is called the Hubble constant. According to modern data, $H$ is taken equal to 75 km/(s · Mpc). Note that the Hubble constant, together with the universal gravitation constant $G$, determines the critical density $\rho_k$: $\rho_k = 3H^2/8\pi G \approx 10^{-29}$ g/cm$^3$.

In 1965, R. Wilson and A. Penzas in the USA discovered relict radiation - a direct trace of the Great Explosion. Friedman's work received new and powerful confirmation.
