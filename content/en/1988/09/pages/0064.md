⟦folio 62⟧

The collisions of molecules lead to a constant change in the distribution function $f(v)$, but after equilibrium is established, collisions of molecules no longer affect this function. The explicit form of the equilibrium function $f(v)$ was found theoretically in 1860 by the outstanding English physicist Maxwell.*)

As follows from the theory (attention! this is the first main statement of the note), the function $f(v)$ in equilibrium is completely determined by the temperature of the system $T$. This fact, if you think about it, seems to countries. It means that the system will always come to the desired state on its own, regardless of what happened to it at the initial moment of time, as long as its temperature was set. You can “uplift” the gas as much as you like, but when left to itself, it will always return to the same state and begin to forget about what happened to it in the beginning.

figure shows graphs of the function $f(v)$ for two temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$ ($T_2 > T_1$). Try to explain on your own the fact that the graph “mixes” when the temperature changes.

So, temperature plays a fundamental role in determining the thermal equilibrium of bodies.

From what has been said, another extremely important point becomes clear. Since we know what fraction of molecules are moving at what speed, we can calculate the average characteristics of molecular motion. In particular,

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2} kT.$$

Based on this formula, it is often said that temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the constant motion of molecules. This is not entirely true. The fact is that the coefficient “$^3/2$” in the expression for energy does not make sense at all. The number “3” is due to the fact that in three-dimensional space, in order to determine the position of the center of mass of molecules, it is necessary to know its three coordinates. Or, to put it another way, each molecule has three degrees of freedom of its constant movement. But, in addition to constants, molecules can have other degrees of freedom: rotational, vibrational, etc.

It turns out that, knowing the velocity distribution function of molecules, it is possible to show (attention! this is the second main statement of the note) that for any degree of freedom there is an average energy $^1/2 kT$. (This is where the coefficient “$^3/2$” in the expression for the average kinetic energy of constant motion of molecules comes from.) In other words, temperature is not a measure of the kinetic energy of constant motion of molecules, but a measure of the energy that comes to any one degree of freedom of the system.

E. E. Gorodetsky
