Note that when heated, another process occurs - a decrease in the density of water (associated with its distribution). This is explained, generally speaking, to play the opposite role. But the effect caused by it, due to the small coefficient of thermal distribution of water, turns out to be much weaker than the first and practically does not manifest itself.

F1117. Light from an exact source A is output through an opaque wall using a light guide - a thin probe rod with a section radius R (Fig. 1); the inner part of the rod with radius r (R—r≤r) is made of materials with refractive index $n_1$, and the outer region is made of materials with refractive index $n_2$ ($n_2<n_1$). Determine the diameter of the light spot on the screen located at a distance L from the right end of the light guide. The light source will be located at the center of the left end of the light guide.

How will the spot size change if the source is removed along the axis of the light guide at a distance l from the end?

Fig. 1.

Obviously, those rays from the source will come to the screen, which undergo complete reflection at the contact surface of the core and cladding and appear, as it were, captured by the fiber core.

Light rays pass through the fiber core (Fig. 2) if the angle of incidence is $\alpha \geqslant \alpha_0$, where $\alpha_0$ is the limiting angle of total reflection at the boundary of two media with refractive indices $n_1$ and $n_2$ ($\sin \alpha_0=n_2/n_1$).

After refraction at the end of the light guide opposite from the source, the light rays diverge in the form of a finite beam. In accordance with the laws of geometric optics, the angle of refraction is determined by the conditions

$$\frac{\sin \beta}{\sin (90^\circ-\alpha)}=n_1.$$

The light ray will experience the greatest deviation at the end of the stick if the angle $\alpha$ is minimal, i.e. $\alpha=\alpha_0$. Then for the maximum refraction angle $\beta_{\max}$ we obtain an expression that is valid for $n_1^2-n_2^2<1$:

$$\sin \beta_{\max}=n_1 \cos \alpha_0=\sqrt{n_1^2-n_2^2}.$$

Thus, knowing the height of the truncated cone (light beam) $L$, the radius less than the base $r$, the angle $\beta_{\max}$ between the axis of the cone and its generatrix, it is easy to find the radius of the large base, or, which is the same, the radius of the light spot on the screen:

$$R_1=r+L \ tg \beta_{\max},$$

or

$$R_1=r+L \frac{\sqrt{n_1^2-n_2^2}}{\sqrt{1+n_2^2-n_1^2}}.$$

If $n_1^2-n_2^2\geqslant 1$, then the light beam comes out at any angle $\beta\leqslant\pi/2$ and the size of the light spot will be unlimited.

In the case when the source is removed at a distance l from the end of the light guide, rays that hit the left end at angles $\gamma\leqslant\gamma_{\max}$, where $tg \gamma_{\max}=r/l$, can participate in the formation of the light spot. Therefore, the emerging beam cannot have a divergence angle greater than $\gamma_{\max}$, i.e. $\beta\leqslant\gamma_{\max}$ (as when passing a plane-parallel plate). If $\beta_{\max}\leqslant\gamma_{\max}$, then formula (2) is also valid for this ray. If $\beta_{\max}>\gamma_{\max}$, then in formula (1) instead of $\beta_{\max}$ you should put $\gamma_{\max}$:

$$R_1=r\left(1+\frac{L}{l}\right).$$
