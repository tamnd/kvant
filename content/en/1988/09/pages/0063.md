In this note we will touch upon only two main points of the modern understanding of thermal phenomena from the point of view of the molecular structure of matter.

Let's highlight a small, but still macrosopic part of some body. Small - this means that the size of the selected area is much smaller than the size of the entire body. We need this so that in the case when the temperature changes along the body, it can be considered the same throughout our region. On the other hand, we said that the area should be macroscopic. This means that, despite its small size, the number of parts in it must still be very large.

The molecules filling the selected area move at the highest speeds, collide with each other, some of them leave the area, and others come in their place - in general, a complex, one might even say, existing life is going on. Understanding this crazy molecular “dance” by observing each molecule separately is an exercise of complete inaction, and unnecessary: ​​we would still not know what to do with such gigantic information. Fortunately, it is the complexity and intricacy of molecular motion that allows us to approach the description of the situation from a completely different angle - from the point of view of probability theory.

The predictions of this theory are very specific. She cannot, for example, imagine who will win the ten-kilometer cross-country ski race at the upcoming 1992 Olympic Games and in what time. However, looking at the results of all previous games, we can more or less reliably say that 1-2% of the race participants will show a time varying from 29 minutes, and 60-70% will fit within the interval from 29 to 31 minutes, etc. Of course, at school modern levels and distributed results will be different. It is quite true that modern rank determines the average level of results (and in this sense, as will be seen later, it is similar to temperature).

The theory of probability, knowing almost nothing about individual events, reliably represents the outcome of a large number of similar or repeating events. It is clear, therefore, that chaotic molecules moving and interacting with each other are an ideal object for such a theory (it is clear why the number of molecules in the selected region should be large).

The discussion question is: what fraction of all molecules filling the selected region has a speed close to some given speed $v$ (or, in other words, has a speed in the range from $v$ to $v+\Delta v$)? In order to formulate the question more precisely, we denote the number of parts that have speed in a given interval by $\Delta N(v)$, and the total number of parts located in the selected area by $N$. Then we need to find the ratio $\Delta N(v)/N$.

If the permissible speed spread $\Delta v$ is not too large, then it is clear that $\Delta N(v)$ should be proportional to $\Delta v$:

$$\frac{\Delta N(v)}{N} = f(v)\Delta v.$$

The quantity $f(v)$ is called the velocity distribution function of molecules and is the most important characteristic of any macrosoppech system. Knowing $f(v)$, we can estimate what percentage of all molecules move at a speed close to such and such a speed, and how many - to such a speed, etc.
