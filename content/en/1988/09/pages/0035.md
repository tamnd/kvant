Consequently, the second body will not change its speed, and the first will move behind the second, lagging behind it at a speed equal to their relative speed before the impact.

In practice, however, there are often cases when at the moment of impact the bodies do not stick together, but their total energy is not conserved. In this situation, Newton’s hypothesis is usually accepted, replacing correspondence (3) with the following relation:

$$e(v_1 - v_2) = -(v_1' - v_2').$$

Here $e$ is a certain dimensionless coefficient, contained in the range from 0 to 1 and determined by the general one from experiment. The number $e$ is called the recovery coefficient. At $e=0$ an absolutely inelastic impact is obtained, and at $e=1$ the impact will be absolutely elastic.

Let $u_1$ and $u_2$ again denote the lost velocities.

The amount of lost energy in the general case is calculated using the generalized Carnot theorem:

$$\Delta T = \frac{1-e}{1+e} \left( \frac{m_1 u_1^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 u_2^2}{2} \right).$$

For $e=0$ this statement is known to us as Carnot's second theorem; at $e=1$ there is no loss of energy at all
