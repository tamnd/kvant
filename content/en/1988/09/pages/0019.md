GEOMETRY OF A SHEET OF PAPER

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
D. V. FUCHS

1

Take a sheet of paper in your hands and, without creasing, bend it. You will have in your hands a piece of surface, the shape of which depends on how you bend the sheet. Samples of the surfaces that can be obtained are shown in figure 1.

One thing is that not every surface can be represented as a curved sheet of paper. For example, it is well known that a sheet of paper cannot be given a spherical shape: if you press a sheet of paper against a globe, folds will certainly appear on the sheet. A sheet of paper can be rolled into a tube or into a pound without creasing, but you cannot avoid folds by folding it in four like a handkerchief (Fig. 2).

Definition. Surfaces that can be thought of as a curved sheet of paper are called unfoldable.

It’s not a definition, but nonsense; nothing worthwhile can come out of it, the Pedant will say, and he’ll be right. It’s not that he’s right that this definition is somehow bad, but that he’s not worth reading further: we won’t have any solid definitions or evidence. It’s better to say that all our proofs (as in one ancient Indian geometric tract) will essentially be a state of one word: look!

Still, not for the Pedant (he has already slammed the magazine), but for the Thoughtful Reader, why: the physical properties of a sheet of paper, essential for our definition, are its flexibility and inextensibility (incompressibility). The second means that a line broken on a sheet can change its shape when bent, but retains its length; the first means that there are no other restrictions on the nature of the bending.
