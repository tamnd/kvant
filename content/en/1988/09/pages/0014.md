So, the criterion of energy perfection is $K_s$. It is quite natural that the magnitude $K_s$ cardinally affects the range of horizontal flight $L$. According to Wrege's formula,

$$L = -\frac{K_s}{C_y} \frac{\lg(1-\bar{m}_{\text{rop}})}{\lg e},$$

where $C_y$ is the specific fuel consumption, $\bar{m}_{\text{rop}} = m_{\text{rop}}/m_0$ is the relative mass of fuel, $m_0$ is the starting mass of the object$^3$).

It is clear from the formula that absolute dimensions and mass do not affect the flight range. Thus, comparing the miniature size of birds with the distances covered by them is more a matter of emotion than of scientific argumentation. When assessing the actual factors at play, comparison here does not reveal any advantage of birds.

Flight data is free research material. It is enough to study the distance traveled and the relative mass loss well (and this does not seem difficult) to obtain a reliable assessment of the energetic perfection of the object.

Unfortunately, this data on birds is usually insufficient. In the information about the mentioned flight of plovers, neither the weight of the birds nor the wind speed are indicated; Moreover, there is no indication that the birds did not feed along the way. The flight of lapwings across the Atlantic is accompanied by a strong tailwind - up to 95 km/h; weight loss remained unchanged. Complete and reliable experimental data are available only for one case: the migration of flocks of American moths Dendroica striata from Massachusetts (USA) to Bermuda (a distance of 1300 km). Flight speed was controlled by radio; the wind on the route was taken into account. The weight loss averaged 0.165 from the starting value.

Correspondence of data on aircraft: the relative weight loss of the obsolete LI-2 aircraft is 0.182 with a flight range of 2090 km, the ANT-25 aircraft, which flew from Moscow to St. Petersburg (USA) in 1937, is 0.542 with a range of 13,000 km. Using the known values ​​of the specific heat of combustion of aviation fuel and the “fuel” (fat) of birds, as well as the thermal efficiency, from these data it is possible to determine $K_s$: for poultry $K_s=10,6$, for the LI-2 aircraft $K_s=8,7$, for ANT-25 $K_s=13,9$. These data confirm the lack of energetic superiority of birds. It is interesting to note that the relative mass loss of 0.165 for the ANT-25 aircraft corresponds to a range of only 3300 km.

Takeoff, landing, on-site deposit

Yes, the deciders of the flight machine will say, but a bird takes off and lands vertically, and for airplanes that lack this ability, we are forced to build expensive runways.

Here, as they say, nothing can be said: we can create a device that takes off like a helicopter and flies horizontally like an airplane only at the cost of enormous fuel consumption on takeoff. It is necessary, however, to make some clarifications.

From Newton’s second law it follows that the lifting force $P$ of any diapasic support body (wing, main rotor) is equal to the product of the second mass of the air grown downwards $m_s$ by the vertical speed of the grown jet. Denoting the area swept by the supporting body (Fig. 2) through s and keeping in mind that the speed of the regrown jet $v_2$ in the injection mode is twice the speed in the plane of the supporting body $v_1$, we find the lifting force equal to the apparatus: $P=G=m_s v_2=2 m_s v_1=2 \rho s v_1^2$ (ρ is the air density at the ground)$^4$.

Not all of the energy expended goes to retracting the jet; we take into account its losses (due to swirling of the jet, due to the friction of the supporting organ against the air), we enter the “relative efficiency” $\eta_0$ and find the power required to support units of weight:

$$\frac{N_{\text{потр}}}{G} = \frac{1}{\eta_0 \sqrt{2 \eta}} \left(\frac{G}{s}\right)^{1/2}.$$
