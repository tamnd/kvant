⟦folio 13⟧

It is obvious that the determining factor for the occurrence of vertical takeoff and hovering is the so-called load on the swept area $G/s$. This leads to important conclusions that are fully confirmed by practice.

Under the condition of geometric and kinematic similarity (which coincides satisfactorily within one biological species), the swept area changes proportionally to the square of the linear dimensions, the mass - proportionally to the cube; the load on the area is therefore proportional to the linear dimension. This explains the fact mentioned above: only relatively small birds - no larger than a pigeon - have vertical take-off and the ability to “quiver” flight (hover). Larger ones - for example, crows - are able to carry out precise take-off (from a standstill), use a powerful push with their legs; even larger ones - loon, pelican, flamingo - take off like an airplane, with a running start. Finally, the condor, albatross and other large birds can only begin flight by throwing themselves from emerging objects - trees, rocks, coastal dunes.

In addition - and this should not be forgotten by enthusiasts who dream of performing a vertical take-off on an artificial aircraft - this can only be achieved if the wings move in a horizontal plane to the take-off mode - otherwise it is impossible to obtain a sufficiently large swept area. In birds, the transition from an almost vertical max in cruising flight to an almost horizontal plane during takeoff and landing is carried out without difficulty due to flexibility and the ability to greatly change the inclination of the body. It is not possible to build such a full-scale artificial apparatus with the current level of technology.

The second important factor determining the occurrence of hovering and vertical take-off is the maximum available power available for a given animal.
