load per unit power:

$$\frac{G}{N} = 3600 \frac{K \eta_{\text{ДВ}}}{v}.$$

The dimension of this quantity is N/kW.

This dependence is universal: it is valid for any moving objects (a bird, an airplane, a train, a ship, a liquid pumped through pipes) regardless of the origin of the force that sets them in motion (muscles, a heat or electric engine, wind) and the device of the body’s movement (a wheel, a screw, a flapping wing, a pump). To solve the problem to the end in each specific case, it is enough to find the values ​​of “equivalent quality” $K_3 = K \eta_{\text{ДВ}}$, since in addition to $K_3$ the load on power $G/N$ depends only on the speed $v$. Thus, the criterion for energy accomplishment, which evaluates the rational choice of a technical solution or the natural structure of an object, is not the power load, which fundamentally depends on speed (the “equality of other conditions” is not observed!), but the dimensionless value $K_3$.

The reason for the large values ​​of $G/N$ in birds and insects now becomes clear. Its absence is illustrated by the graph on figure 1. Further, at a low level of energy performance (in Sarachia, for example, $K_3 = 2,8$, while in good aircraft $K_3 = 13$ and more), their specific loads are higher simply because the flight speed is low. In this sense, the point on the graph corresponding to the value of the Gossamer-Condor flylet that flew across the English Channel is particularly indicative. This device, built according to an airplane design - a fixed wing and a screw propulsion device, thanks to its very low flight speed ($v = 17,8$ km/h) has a power load of 4400 N/kW, unattainable for any birds.

The physical meaning of the above is extremely simple: when a certain power is expended, the greater the load transferred, the lower the speed. This is the “secret” of high power loads in flying animals.
