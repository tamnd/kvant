When the nut cools sufficiently and the heat flow is not enough to maintain the “coat”, the “coat” disintegrates (abruptly) and is replaced by a large number of individual bubbles of nitrogen gas. Now part of the heat will still leave the nut through the gas, and part - through liquid nitrogen, which conducts the heat of the gas. Thus, there is a certain paradoxical situation here when the heat flow increases with a decrease in the temperature difference. And this happens due to a strong jump in the coefficient α. (As the flow increases, a “coat” does not form because the temperature of the nut has already dropped sufficiently.)

Figures 1 and 2 show graphs of the dependence of $W$ on $\Delta T$ and on time $t$. In the circled region, α suffers a jump.

Note that the same unusual boiling can, in principle, be observed with other substances. For example, instead of liquid nitrogen, you can take ordinary water and throw a very heated copper nut into it. After immersing the nut in water, a “fur coat” also forms, but it disintegrates so quickly that it is almost impossible to notice the change in boiling modes. The difference in the duration of the boiling process is associated with the difference in heat capacity. Water has a huge heat capacity compared to liquid nitrogen and therefore boils in a significantly shorter time.

Instead of a copper nut, you can use some other heated body. Honey is good in that it has high thermal conductivity, as a result of which the nut has time to cool uniformly throughout the entire volume, creating the necessary conditions for the formation of a “fur coat.”

A completely similar phenomenon of changing heat transfer modes can be observed by throwing a few drops of water onto a hot frying pan (or stove). If the frying pan is hot, but its temperature is still not very high, the drops falling on the surface of the frying pan will be motionless and will evaporate quite quickly. If the frying pan is very hot, drops of water immediately after falling on its surface will gather into balls, which will “run” around the frying pan for quite a long time. This is due to the fact that a steam cushion will appear between the frying pan and the drops (the same as a “coat” in the case of a nut), which will impede heat transfer from the frying pan to the drop and thereby increase the lifetime of the drop.*)

So, heat transfer - such a seemingly simple and familiar phenomenon, actually hides great opportunities for observation and research.

*) The behavior of such “running” drops is described in detail in the article by M. Golubev and A. Kagalenko “A Drop on a Hot Surface” («Kvant», 1977, No. 12) and in the article by A. Lushkov and Yu. Luzhkov “Stars” from a water drop («Kvant», 1978, No. 7).
