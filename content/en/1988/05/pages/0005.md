— What difficulties did you encounter on your path to becoming a scientist?

— I felt very difficult due to insufficient mathematical education. I knew little special literature. After graduating from the Faculty of Physics, I was attracted to mathematical language for quite a long time. Therefore, I was not immediately perceived by mathematicians as a mathematician, but rather as a physicist, since my language was somewhat obscure to them. At first, a number of my questions on mathematical papers were rewritten by mathematicians, sometimes even without references. Among the mathematicians, it became clear to me that my work was not rigorous. I had to assume this point of view. I succeeded in this, oddly enough, when I proved my competence in modern other areas of mathematics, and not in those that I was mainly involved in at that time.

— Such an attraction is created that you all the time followed the line of most resistance: you went to the physics department, because physics was the worst for you, and then you began to study mathematics because you did not have enough mathematical language. What is this - a character trait?

— No, apparently, I became interested in mathematics at the Faculty of Physics because I simply had a great inclination for mathematics. As I said, this hobby happened in my second year. Later, however, I became interested in quantum mechanics and quantum physics. Please, you can say that I actually followed the line of my hobbies, the line of least resistance in my life. And maybe I actually got into the physics department through some trickery: I even had a C in physics on my school certificate.

— Your first works (as well as many of the external ones) relate to fundamental research and are of interest primarily to theoretical mathematicians. On the other hand, you are a famous applied mathematician. How do you reconcile “pure” mathematics with physics and applied issues?

— I think it turned out well that I went to the physics department.

I have a penchant for generalizations, for mathematical and general formulations. My physics class is a source of mathematical problems for me; this has had an overall positive impact on my work. During my student years, I set myself a number of problems related to theoretical physics. So far I have not been able to solve all the problems. I thought that this way of searching for mathematical problems on the border with theoretical physics was a good way. On the other hand, it makes it possible to evaluate the final results in terms of their applications to physics or to some other applied fields. In algebra, and especially in number theory, there are criteria by which work is generally assessed. For example, how long ago was the task set, who solved it and did not solve it, a sports element, or something. If you can also solve it, it means there is serious progress. But in mathematics related to applications, such a criterion, to a certain extent, is precisely the application, the breadth of possible applications. Here, intuition must be defined; one must feel exactly which problems solved by a given mathematician can be useful. Well, in this case, knowledge of physics and physical problems is essential. I will give an example of a mathematician like Ludwig Dmitrievich Faddeev. He also graduated from the Faculty of Physics. The problems that he began to occupy were read first, then received wide resonance thanks to his and not only his works. Faddeev is often one of the first to solve pressing problems in mathematics. These are problems such as the Kortever-Defries equation*) or, say, the inverse problem of scattering theory. Faddeev somehow senses exactly what problems will develop in the future. And this is due, to a large extent, to his physical image.

*) A differential equation that describes waves on shallow bodies of water, the solutions of which (the so-called solitons) have a very low mathematical property.
