What happens as the device descends? The speed of the apparatus will decrease under the influence of the resistance force $F_x$. But as it decreases, the density of the atmosphere quickly increases (see formula (1)). And, despite the drop in speed, the lifting force $F_y$ returns. And this lifting force can be used to delay the “fall” of the device. In this case, the aircraft actually becomes a glider, and the gravity force acting on it can be balanced by the lifting force:

$$Mg = C_y\rho v^2 S.$$

To use this capability of a winged vehicle, you need to fly at altitudes where the density of the atmosphere is not less than $\rho_{\min} \approx \frac{Mg/S}{C_y v^2}$ (the numerator of this fraction - the gravitational force per unit area of ​​the wing - is called the load on the wing, so we highlighted it). And using formula (1) you can determine the maximum altitude, no lower than which you can stay “on the wing” at a given flight speed $v$:

$$\frac{Mg/S}{C_y v^2} \approx \rho_0 e^{-\frac{h_{\max}}{h^*}} \Rightarrow \frac{h_{\max}}{h^*} \approx \ln \frac{\rho_0 C_y v^2}{Mg/S},$$

where

$$h_{\max}(v) \approx h^* \ln \frac{\rho_0 C_y v^2}{Mg/S}.$$

In other words, if we want the apparatus to “not fail” at a given altitude $h$, its speed must be no less than

$$v_{\min}(h) \approx \sqrt{\frac{Mg/S}{C_y \rho_0}} e^{\frac{h}{h^*}}.$$
