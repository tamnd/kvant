In this case, we take the radius so small that the sphere $S$, crossing the edges and faces of the polyhedron approaching the center vertex $O$, does not touch any other edges and faces of the polyhedron. It is clear that the faces of the polyhedron forming a polyhedral angle with vertex $O$ cut out on the sphere $S$ a convex (spherical) polygon $M$ whose angles are equal to the dihedral angles of the polyhedron. If you draw a sphere $S'$ with the same radius at the corresponding vertex $O'$ of another polyhedron, then a polygon $M'$ is cut out on it, the sides of which are equal to the corresponding sides of the polygon $M$. The equality of sides follows from the conditions of Cauchy's theorem: pairwise equal faces must converge at the corresponding vertices of polyhedra.

This is where Lemma 1 comes into play. We have assumed that Cauchy's uniqueness theorem is false; this means that at least one edge turns out to be offset with a “+” or “-” sign, and, in accordance with Lemma 1, if offset edges approach some vertex, then the number of sign changes for the edges when going around this vertex is at least 4.

It would seem that there is still a long way to go from this simple remark to the proof of the theorem. But here Cauchy finds an unexpected witty move, after which proof becomes a matter of technique. It turns out that the following statement is true.

Lemma 2. Let some edges on a closed convex polyhedron be offset by the + or - sign. Let us select all those vertices of the polyhedron to which at least one offset edge approaches. Then, among the selected vertices, such a vertex will always be found when traversing around the one encountered by a pair of neighboring edges and when traversing around the corresponding face and vice versa (Fig. 7).

$$\Gamma = \Gamma_3 + \Gamma_4 + \Gamma_5 + \Gamma_6 + \dots$$
