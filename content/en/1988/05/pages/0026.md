Well, let's fly fast, shall we? Stop! Introducing, we know that at high speeds a shock wave will “sit” on the blunt edges of our apparatus, behind which the impact (or even not the impact, but a mixture of fragments of its molecules) will heat up so much that the apparatus can freeze. This is the most difficult problem of reentry - the thermal barrier. How to answer this tello? You can use everything: the thermal conductivity of the device itself, which allows heat to flow “from nose to tail,” its possible melting (and, therefore, changing its shape, which makes the device even “dumber”), the warm radiation of “hot added” parts of the device, etc.

We will not discuss this problem as a whole here, but to estimate the permissible speed we will take the “worst” case, when all the body brought to the apparatus by air is removed from its surface only by radiation.

There is such a law - the Stefan-Bolshman law - according to which the power carried away by radiation from each square meter of area is proportional to the fourth power of temperature: $q = \sigma T^4 \frac{\Delta J}{\mathrm{m}^2 \cdot \mathrm{c}}$. The proportionality coefficient $\sigma$ is called the Stefan-Bolshman constant; it can be written out from a physics reference book: $\sigma = 5,67 \cdot 10^{-8}$ J/(m$^2$·s·K$^4$). This is a very sharp dependence: if the temperature is increased, for example, $3^4=81$ times - almost a hundred times! According to this law, it turns out that for effective cooling by radiation it is necessary to “get even hotter.”

So, let’s estimate the maximum speed at which the device can move without yet swimming. The mass of air incident per unit time per unit surface area of ​​the apparatus is equal to $\rho v$. The energy brought by this air is $\rho v \frac{v^2}{2}$. Almost all of this energy turns into heat. And all this heat, according to the proposal, leaves the apparatus due to radiation, which per unit time carries away energy $\sigma T^4$ per unit area. So, we require that the surface temperature does not exceed the melting temperature $T_{\pi \pi}$. Then we will have

$$\frac{v^2}{2} \rho v \leq \sigma T_{\pi \pi}^4.$$

Thus, the speed of the vehicle cannot be greater than the value $v_{\max}$ defined for each altitude:

$$v \leq v_{\max}(h) = \left[ \frac{2 \sigma T_{\pi \pi}^4}{\rho(h)} \right]^{1/3}.$$

The plot of $v_{\max}(h)$ on figure 8 is the EF curve.

So, if we want to use the “carrying and braking” properties of the atmosphere, i.e., land on the planet on a winged vehicle without consuming fuel for braking, then in the altitude-velocity coordinate plane our “trajectory” cannot pass above the CDAB curve (the wings will not support the vehicle) and below the EF curve (story!). Both of these curves are plotted for landing conditions on Earth. It can be seen that with increasing speed, the distance between these curves—the width of the “entry corridor”—reaches a minimum value (which means, especially at this height, you need to be careful!), but, fortunately, these curves are ignored! Wise Nature has provided the opportunity for the development of creatures to return to Earth on winged vehicles.
