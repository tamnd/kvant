Law of Conservation of Energy

Conservation laws in physics, and in nature in general, play a very special role, being, as it were, a universal law for all natural phenomena. Moreover, it is absolute legislation: everything that is consistent with it (i.e., with the laws of conservation) may or may not originate, but it is no longer consistent - it is completely prohibited.

Physics is often called an exact science. This is not entirely true. Physics is accurate only in the sense that it knows exactly how close its laws are. Thus, even such a fundamental law of nature as the law of universal gravitation has a complete definition of the limits of applicability. At very small distances it does not take into account the quantum nature of phenomena, and at very large distances it does not take into account the fact that the distance between two interacting masses cannot be overcome instantly.

The only exception in this sense is the laws of conservation - they are completely universal and, at the current level of knowledge, absolutely accurate. For example, the law of conservation of energy can be considered such a reliably established experimental fact that its further verification (again at the current level of knowledge) is apparently simply meaningless.
