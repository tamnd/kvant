⟦folio 41⟧

ARCHIMEDEAN POWER
IN LITERARY WORKS

Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences
S. A. TIKHOMIROV

Physics surrounds us everywhere - at home, on the street, in the field, in the forest, by the river. Writers, amazingly accurately noting all the origins around them, give bright, colorful descriptions of various natural phenomena. Reading a book, carefully following the plot, we sometimes skip these descriptions, considering them not interesting enough. Meanwhile, they seem to be very valuable and useful material not only for “lyricists”, but also for “physicists”. They often learn to be attentive and teach them to observe, notice, and see manifestations of physics in the life around them.

We invite you to look at some pages of literary works from this physical point of view. Let's start with an excerpt from A. P. Chekhov's story “Stel”:

“Yegorushka... ran and flew from a half-length bus. Having described an arc in the air, he fell into the water, sank deeply, but did not reach the day; some force, cold and spicy to the touch, picked him up and carried him back up.”

It is clear that we are talking here about the power of Archimedes, “cold and spicy to the touch.” Let's try to explain the various manifestations of Archimedean power found on the pages of fiction. Read them and answer the questions posed.

An excerpt from the story “Under Water” by B.V. Zhitkov. The lieutenant in command of the submarine, upon entering the port, frivolously decided to dig under the steamer, which was facing the boat. “...But at this time the boat’s progress immediately slowed down. Everyone stagger forward. The lieutenant shuddered. The miner looked at him questioningly.

- Ran aground? Right? - he asked the lieutenant.

The rudders were set to rise, the vitrat was working, the instruments showed that the boat was at the same depth. The lieutenant remembered that there was a Portuguese, sticky bottom, and realized that the boat’s belly was stuck in this sounding muck...”

Why couldn't the boat float? Does a body immersed in a liquid always experience a buoyant force?

K. G. Paustovsky, episode from the story “Kara-Vugaz”. “...Our cook asked to go for a swim, but the bay did not accept him. He threw his legs high, and despite all his efforts the cook could not plunge into the water. This amused the team and alleviated their bad mood somewhat. By evening the cook was covered with ulcers and claimed that the water pouring in was diluted aqua regia, otherwise it was sulfuric acid.”

Why couldn’t the cook swim in Kara-Vugaz Bay?

L.N. Tolstoy, lines from the story “Ice, Steam and Water.” “...It is easier to swim in cold water than in warm water. And in hot water even a tree drowns.”

Why does a tree drown in hot water?
