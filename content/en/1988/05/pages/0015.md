The question arises: from which developments can a convex polygon be obtained in this way? To do this, two conditions must be met:

(I) for development, Euler’s formula holds (B-P+G=2);
(II) the sum of the plane angles formed during gluing at one vertex does not exceed 360°.

The meaning of Aleksaidrov's theorem will amaze you with its simplicity: conditions (I) and (II) are necessary and sufficient for it to be possible to glue a convex polygon from development polygons (possibly, additionally development polygons).

Developing the ideas underlying this theorem, A. D. Aleksaidrov constructed the internal geometry of convex surfaces - one of the most important theories in modern geometry.

How many different convex polygons can be glued together from one net? Due to the fact that the faces of the polygon are not fixed, Cauchy's theorem does not answer this question. A.D. Aleksaidrov proves a theorem that, on the one hand, succeeds Cauchy’s theorem, and on the other hand, perfectly complements his own sufficiency theorem: if a convex polygon can be glued from a development, then only one.

Moreover, from this development it is impossible to obtain any other convex surface at all, not only a polygonal one, but also a curved one. This successful theorem of Aleksaidrov was obtained in 1941 by his young student S.P. Olovyanshinkov*.

As for the most complete generalization of Cauchy's theorem to the case of arbitrary, and not just polygonal surfaces, this question must remain unresolved for some time. Let an arbitrary closed convex surface be made of a thin, flexible, but inflexible material. Is it possible, while maintaining the convexity, to obtain from it a surface of a different geometric shape? If the original surface is a convex polygon, then this is impossible - this is the case of the Cauchy-Aleksaidrov-Olovyanshinkov uniqueness theorem.
