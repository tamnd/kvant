⟦folio 50⟧

The decrease in rope tension force with increasing angle of coverage is proportional to the magnitude of the tension force:

$$\Delta T / \Delta \alpha \sim T,$$

or $$\Delta T / \Delta \alpha = -\mu T.$$

The minus sign here means that as the angle of coverage increases, the tension in the rope decreases.

In physics, situations often occur when the rate of change of a quantity is proportional to this quantity itself. For example, there is a natural radioactive decay (“Physics 10”, § 92): the decrease in the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei per unit time is proportional to their number. Another example would be the discharge of a charged capacitor through a resistor: the decrease in charge on the capacitor is proportional to the current through the resistor, which, in turn, is proportional to the charge on the capacitor. In all these cases there is a very rapid change in the corresponding value. Indeed, if, for example, the rate of change in the speed (acceleration) of a body's movement is constant, then the magnitude of the speed is linearly calculated with time. If the acceleration is proportional to the speed, then it increases all the time and the speed calculation is much faster - according to the so-called exponential law.

The same dependence is obtained in our case for changing the tension force of the rope. (We emphasize once again that we are talking about the minimum possible difference in the tension force of the rope - when it is about to begin sliding along the support.) This issue was first considered by the mathematician, mechanic, physicist and astronomer Leonhard Euler (1707-1783). He showed that the rope tension $T$, depending on the angle of coverage of the support by the rope (angle of navigation) $\alpha$, changes according to the law

$$T = T_0 e^{-\mu \alpha},$$

where $e=2,72...$ is the base of the natural logarithm, $T_0$ is the initial tension of the rope (not yet on the support).

The angle $\alpha$ (measured in radians) is related to the number of turns $n$ of the rope

around the bollard with a simple ratio: $\alpha=2\pi n$. Then, if during one revolution the rope tension decreases by $k$ times, i.e.

$$\frac{T_0}{T_1} = e^{2\pi \mu n} = k,$$

after $n$ revolutions, the tension weakens $k^n$ times:

$$\frac{T_0}{T_n} = \frac{T_0}{T_1} \frac{T_1}{T_2} \dots \frac{T_{n-1}}{T_n} = k^n = c^{2\pi \mu n}.$$

With a coefficient of three $\mu=0,3$, for example, one turn of the rope includes the force of its tension almost 7 times, and if you make two turns, the tension weakens by about 40 times! As the number of revolutions increases, the tension in the rope (due to friction) becomes less and less and gradually disappears.

Returning to Jules Verne's hero Matif, we can now say that by winding a rope around an iron pipe, he made his task much easier. Ya. I. Perelman, using the data about “Trabokolo” contained in the novel and making the appropriate calculations, discovered that if Matifu managed to wrap the rope around the pipe in one go, then there could be a child in his place. In the same way, when mooring ships, sailors do not need much strength - they just need to show good looks and strength and manage to quickly wind the rope onto the bollard in time.

Let us note that each of you comes across this phenomenon almost every day when tying something - be it cords, scarfs or rope. After all, any knot is essentially a rope wound around a “support” (the same rope).

A. I. Buzdin
