Now let's write:

$$F_y = C_y \rho v^2 L^2, \quad F_x = C_x \rho v^2 L^2.$$ (2)

We said that $C_y$ and $C_x$ (let's call them aerodynamic coefficients) depend on the angle of attack. What about taking these dependencies openly? In some cases they can be calculated. For example, such a calculation can be carried out for the case when the apparatus flies very high, where there are already few molecules and they do not collide with each other when approaching the apparatus, but collide only with it, with the apparatus; this makes the distance very easy.

Let's consider the motion in the reference frame associated with the apparatus, which moves in the atmosphere with a speed of $\vec{u}$. In this system, the apparatus will end, and air molecules will not fly towards it. We will assume that $u$ is much greater than the thermal velocities of molecules; then we can neglect the static rate of motion of the molecules and assume that they all fly into the apparatus with a speed of $\vec{v} = -\vec{u}$.

Let us first consider the impact of a single molecule on the hard surface of the apparatus, inclined relative to the velocity vector by an angle $a$ (angle of attack). Under the assumption of an absolutely elastic impact, after the molecules collide, $v$ will not change, but the vector $\vec{v}$ will rotate by an angle $2a$ (figure 3). It is not difficult to find the change in the speed of a molecule as a result of an impact (see figure 3):

$$\Delta v = 2v_n = 2v \sin a.$$

And the change in momentum of this molecule, if its mass is $m$, is equal to

$$\Delta p_m = 2mv \sin a.$$
