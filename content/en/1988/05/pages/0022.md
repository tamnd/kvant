Shock shock

Note that in our consideration we actually represented the apparatus as a plate of area $S$, inclined at an angle $\alpha$ to the flow of molecules, and the speeds of the molecules were considered identical in magnitude and direction - the apparatus moved much faster than the thermal motion of the molecules themselves. What happened on the back leeward side of the plate did not interest us: the molecules did not get there, so this space could be filled, for example, with the heat of a wedge with the same angle $\alpha$ at the apex. Let now this wedge descend into the dense layers of the atmosphere and move more and more slowly, but still faster than the average thermal speed of molecules and, therefore, faster than sound. (It is clear that such an organized process as a sound effect cannot propagate faster than the molecules themselves, moving at thermal speed.) In order to slow down as much as possible, let’s begin to tilt the wedge plane at an increasingly greater angle relative to the flow (after all, the resistance force is $F_x \sim \sin^3 \alpha$). Then the molecules will bounce off the windward half-plane at an increasingly right angle, and a sloped layer will begin to form in front of the wedge surface. When the plane is positioned perpendicular to the flow, molecules striking the wedge far from its edge will not have time to escape from it, and a real pusher will begin; and the oncoming molecules (they know nothing about the obstacle - after all, the speed is greater than the speed of sound) will crash into this thickness of molecules in a big way. A sloped layer will appear near the plane $S$, separated from the undisturbed gas by the so-called direct shock wave (figure 6). In this jump, the flow speed drops sharply (that’s why it’s a jump) - from supersonic to slow subsonic.

Let the body move very quickly, well, for example, its speed $v$ (more precisely, $u=v$) is twenty times the speed of sound $c$. In other words, the ratio of the flow speed $v$ to the speed of sound $c$ is equal to $M=20$ (this ratio is called the Mach number), and each molecule in the oncoming undisturbed flow has a kinetic energy approximately equal to $\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{mc^2}{2} M^2$.
