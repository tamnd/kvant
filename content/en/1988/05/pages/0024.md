Entry Corridor, or Closure

At the beginning of the article, we discussed the fact that if a planet does not have an atmosphere or has one, but it is very rarefied, then for a soft landing, which requires braking, it will be necessary to expend almost as much energy as to escape from its gravitational field. But if there is an atmosphere of sufficient density, then you can save this energy (and therefore the associated mass of initially stored fuel) by using the drag force $F_x$ for braking, and the lift force $F_y$ for maintaining it in the atmosphere.

What does “sufficient density” of the atmosphere mean? And is it only the density of the atmosphere that should enter into our considerations? What about the size of the planet, its mass (on which the acceleration of gravity on the surface depends), the thickness of the atmosphere, its composition? What about the characteristics of the device itself?

This is what we are going to talk about now.

So, let an apparatus of mass $M$, having a wing area $S$, move with a speed of $v$ in the Earth’s atmosphere at an altitude of $h$, where the density of the atmosphere is $\rho$. The lift force will be $F_y = C_y \rho v^2 S$. Encircling the planet, the device moves in a search-disruption trajectory. For simplicity, let this trajectory be close to a circle with radius $R + h$ (R is the radius of the Earth); then the centripetal acceleration of the apparatus will be equal to $v^2/(R + h)$. This acceleration is determined by gravity and lift $F_y$:

$$Mg - F_y = \frac{Mv^2}{R + h}.$$

Considering that the “thickness” of the atmosphere is small compared to the radius of the Earth, we rewrite the last equation in the form

$$C_y \rho v^2 S \approx M \left( g - \frac{v^2}{R} \right).$$
