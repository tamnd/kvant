But the fact is that returning to Earth is no simpler than flying into space. In fact, how much energy does it take to lift someone’s weight (well, for example, himself) to a height h? Every literate schoolchild will say: mgh. And if you hide it from a height h, then this (potential) energy will turn into kinetic energy, and then turn into tello. But who will benefit from it, especially if h is equal to, say, the height of a ten-story building. After all, it is desirable to land softly, with almost zero speed, and this means that all the kinetic energy, into which potential energy is converted, needs to be put somewhere - for example, to spend on friction a rope thrown from the tenth floor.

In the same way, an aircraft, when landing on the Moon or on planets almost devoid of an atmosphere (Mercury, Mars), will have to dampen the speed and corresponding kinetic energy using the traction force of the rocket engines, directed in the opposite direction.

However, these explanations are correct if we mean the standard recommendation for school tasks: “neglect the sporting atmosphere.” But a parachute, a glider, an airplane with its engines turned off (which has become a glider) are recognized without wasting energy. Their speed is dampened by the aerodynamic force - the force acting from the air. Is it possible to use the Earth’s atmosphere for planning “free” recognition from space? This question is not as trivial as it may seem, if we remember the sad fate of the majority of meteorites, which never reach the surface of the Earth, and if they are added, then in a pitiful form. But we would like to land “entirely”, without expending either our mass or our energy.

So is it possible?
