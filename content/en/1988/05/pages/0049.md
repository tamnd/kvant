lots: $W_{\text{кон}} - W_{\text{нач}} = A - Q$.
Strictly speaking, the quantities $A$ and $Q$ can be both positive and negative:
$A > 0$, if external bodies do work on the system, and $A < 0$, if the system itself does work on external bodies;
$Q > 0$ if mechanical energy is converted into some other form, and $Q < 0$ if the transition is from a non-mechanical form of energy to a mechanical one.
The above equation turns out to be extremely effective in solving a wide variety of physical problems.

E. E. Gorodetsky

Lenz's rule

In physics, a situation often occurs when an external influence on an object leads to the appearance of a counteraction to this influence. Let's give specific examples.

Let us enclose the gas in a heat-conducting cylinder, closed with a light piston, which can mix freely in it. In equilibrium, the piston will take a position at which the gas pressure in the cylinder is equal to the external pressure. By mixing the piston, we reduce the volume of gas in the cylinder. At the same time, the gas pressure will increase, and this will serve as a transformation for further mixing of the piston. With an increase in the volume of gas, the pressure in the cylinder will drop, and again a force appears that causes the piston to move.

Put a kettle of water on the fire. As a result of an increase in the temperature of the bottom of the kettle in the kettle - water system, heat transfer processes will arise that tend to reduce the effective temperature - heat will be transferred from the kettle to the water.

Let's introduce a conductor into the power plant field. Under the influence of the field, the charges of the conductor are redistributed in such a way as to destroy the cause that caused their redistribution, i.e., the field inside the conductor.

We will move the permanent magnet out of the conductive bulb. An induction current will appear in the bulb, and it will begin to adhere to the magnet, trying to destroy the cause (removal of the magnet from the bulb) that led to the occurrence of the current.

All these examples suggest that an external influence, caused by a system from a state of stable equilibrium, stimulates processes in the system that seek to weaken the result of this influence. This is the formulation of the sight established by the French scientist Le Chatelier in 1884 and refined by the German physicist Braun in 1887. This sight allows you to predict in which direction processes will flow in a system that is out of balance. It is widely used in physics and chemistry.

The historical aim of Le Chatelier - Braun was formulated as a generalization of Lenz's rules, which states: the induction current resulting from electromagnetic induction is always directed in such a way that it tries to block the cause that caused it. This rule was formulated by the Russian physicist Lenz in 1833. The essence of Lenz's rules is illustrated by several examples.

Example 1. The south pole of a permanent magnet moves away from the conductive bulb (Fig. 1). Determine the direction of the induction current in the bulb. Where is the force acting on the flask directed?

To answer the first question, the reason caused by the induced emf in the flask is taken to be the reduced magnetic flux through the flask. According to Lenz's rules, the own magnetic field created by the induction current must be prevented from this decrease. Therefore, the magnetic lines of the own field go through the flask in the same direction as the magnetic field lines of the permanent magnet, i.e. to the left.
