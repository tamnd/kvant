And since it is true for $3^3=27$, we conclude by induction that it is also true for all natural $n>2$.

M1083. The greatest of non-
negative numbers $a_1$, $a_2, \dots, a_n$ equal to a.
a) Prove the inequality
$$\frac{a_1^2+a_2^2+\dots+a_n^2}{n} \leqslant \left(\frac{a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n}{n}\right)^2 + \frac{a^2}{4}.$$
b) When it reaches
equality?

a), b) Set $(a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n)/n=m$, then
$$\frac{a_1^2+a_2^2+\dots+a_n^2}{n} - m^2 \leqslant am - m^2 = \frac{a^2}{4} - \left(\frac{a}{2} - m\right)^2 \leqslant \frac{a^2}{4},$$
since $a_i^2 \leqslant a \cdot a_i$ ($i=1, 2, \dots, n$). This proves
inequality a).

Equality is achieved in it if and only if,
firstly, $a_i^2=a \cdot a_i$, i.e. for every $i$ either $a_i=0$ or $a_i=a$, and secondly, $m=a/2$. And this is possible only in two cases: 1) all $a_i$ are equal to 0; 2) $n$ is even and $n/2$ of the numbers $a_i$ are equal to 0, and the remaining $n/2$ are equal to $a$ ($a>0$).

In the same way, we can prove the following - more general - inequalities: $p_1a_1^\alpha + p_2a_2^\alpha + \dots + p_na_n^\alpha \leqslant \left(p_1a_1+\dots+p_na_n\right)^\alpha + (\alpha-1)\alpha^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}a^\alpha$ for $p_i \geqslant 0$ ($i=1, \dots, n$), $p_1+p_2+\dots+p_n=1$, $\alpha>1$, $0 \leqslant a_i \leqslant a$.

If the brake pads did not act on the wheels of the automatic, then in the absence of punctures between the tires and the highway, the automatic would go down the slope with acceleration. In this case, along the path S, it would “gain” kinetic energy ΔK, and according to the law of conservation of energy

$$\Delta K = mgS \sin \alpha,$$

where mgS sin α is the change in potential energy.

According to the conditions of the problem, the automat's density does not change. This means that the work A of the friction forces (between the brake drums and pads) along the path S is exactly equal to mgS sin α. Let's find a job A.

If the automatic vehicle has passed the path S, then each block has passed along the surface of the drum the path S₁ = Sr/R, and the friction force between each block and the drum has done work

$$A_1 = kNS_1 = kNS \frac{r}{R}.$$

The total work of friction forces is

$$A = 8A_1 = 8kNS \frac{r}{R}$$

(each of the four wheels has two pads).

Thus, according to the law of conservation of energy

$$8kNS \frac{r}{R} = mgS \sin \alpha.$$

From here comes N:

$$N = \frac{mgR \sin \alpha}{8kr} = 630 \text{ Н.}$$

F1095. The absent-minded hair-reductor did not notice how he accidentally ran into a vertical wall. Estimate at what minimum speed the tire deforms to the metal rim upon impact. It is assumed that you, having a good understanding of the phenomenon, can yourself set the quantities necessary for the solution, choose their sufficiently correct numerical values ​​and obtain a numerical result.

When the denser liquid is in the lower half of the tube, and the less dense liquid is in the upper (stable equilibrium position). According to the law of conservation of energy, it is at this moment that the kinetic energy of the liquid $K=(m_1+m_2)v^2/2$ (and therefore its speed) will be maximum, and $K=\Delta\Pi$ (ΔП is the change in the potential energy of the system). Let's find ΔΠ.

The center of gravity of the liquid occupying the lining (upper or lower) of the tube is located at a distance $y=l/8$ from the nearest horizontal side. The displacement of the center of gravity of the liquid upon transition to a stable equilibrium position is equal to

$$\Delta y=l-2y=l-2 \frac{l}{8}=\frac{3}{4} l.$$

Therefore, the change in potential energy of all systems is

$$\Delta\Pi=(m_1-m_2)g \cdot \frac{3}{4} l=\frac{3}{2}(Q_1-Q_2)gSl^2$$

(S is the cross-sectional area of ​​the tube).

Maximum kinetic energy value -

$$K_{\max}=(m_1+m_2)\frac{v^2_{\max}}{2}=(Q_1+Q_2)Slv^2_{\max}.$$

Thus, from the law of conservation of energy -

$$(Q_1+Q_2)Slv^2_{\max}=\frac{3}{2}(Q_1-Q_2)gSl^2$$

- find $v_{\max}$:

$$v_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}gl \frac{Q_1-Q_2}{Q_1+Q_2}}.$$

When a bicycle wheel hits a vertical wall, the gas in the chamber is compressed. At the same time, work is done

$$A \sim p \cdot \Delta V,$$

where $p$ is the excess air pressure in the chamber, $\Delta V$ is the change in chamber volume. The kinetic energy of the bicycle will be available to do this work, so

$$p \cdot \Delta V \sim \frac{mu^2}{2}.$$

From here we get for the speed of the bicycle

$$u \sim \sqrt{\frac{2p \cdot \Delta V}{m}}.$$

The excess pressure in the chamber can be estimated from the correspondence

$$p \cdot 2S \sim mg,$$

where $S$ is the contact area of ​​each wheel with the road. Hence,

$$u \sim \sqrt{\frac{g \cdot \Delta V}{S}}.$$

The volume of $\Delta V$, as seen from figure, can be estimated as

F1096. A wire stake with radius $R$ has a conductive jumper located along the diameter. The left and right semicircles include capacitors $C_1$ and $C_2$ (see Fig.ok). The ring is placed in a build-up of a magnetic field linear with time with induction $B(t) = B_0 t/T$, a variable of its plane. At some point in time, the jumper is removed and then the field is stopped changing. Start the charges installed on the capacitors.

A ring with a conductive jumper can be represented as two circuits - $AC_1B$ and $BC_2A$ - each with an area of ​​$\pi R^2/2$. The magnetic flux, arbitrary in each circuit, changes over time as

$$\Phi(t) = \frac{\pi R^2}{2} B_0 \frac{t}{T}.$$

In this case, the induced emf is maintained in each circuit

$$\mathcal{E} = - \frac{d\Phi}{dt} = - \frac{\pi R^2 B_0}{2T}.$$

This is the potential difference between the plates of each capacitor (see Fig.ok). Consequently, the charges on the capacitor plates when the magnetic field changes will be constant and equal, respectively,

$$Q_1 = \mathcal{E} C_1, \quad Q_2 = -\mathcal{E} C_2.$$

After the jumper is removed and the field stops changing, the charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ will be redistributed between $C_1$ and $C_2$ until the potential difference between the connecting plates of the capacitors becomes equal to zero, i.e. until the voltages on the capacitors are equal. The charges established on the plates will be determined by the conditions

$$\frac{Q'_1}{C_1} = \frac{Q'_2}{C_2},$$

$$Q'_1 + Q'_2 = Q_1 + Q_2$$

(the last equality follows from the law of conservation of charge). Solving (2) and (3) together, using expression (1) for $\mathcal{E}$, we find $Q'_1$ and $Q'_2$:

$$Q'_1 = \mathcal{E} C_1 \frac{C_1 - C_2}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{\pi R^2 B_0 C_1}{2T} \frac{C_1 - C_1}{C_1 + C_2},$$

$$Q'_2 = \mathcal{E} C_2 \frac{C_1 - C_2}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{\pi R^2 B_0 C_2}{2T} \frac{C_2 - C_1}{C_1 + C_2}.$$

G. V. Fedotovich
