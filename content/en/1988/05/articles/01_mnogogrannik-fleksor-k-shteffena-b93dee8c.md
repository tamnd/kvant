⟦folio none⟧

Is it possible to deform a manifold intermittently so that all its faces remain unchanged and only the two-dimensional angles change? In other words, is it possible to get rid of variety? It turns out that it is possible.

The first page of the cover shows, apparently, the simplest variety known in abundance - a flexor, which was proposed by Klaus Steffen, and a mirror image of this flex.

The development of this flex consists of two equal bases and a cover (see figure). The values ​​of a, b, c, d, e are good: a=12, b=10, c=5, d=11, e=17. After the base is cut out, you need to glue the edges together in pairs so that at one vertex (marked with a light circle) the base is convex in one direction, and at the other vertex (marked with a dark circle) - in the opposite direction, so that the entire base is not convex.

The second base is glued in exactly the same way, so both bases can be aligned with each other. After this, the bases are glued together so that each of them has two free ribs, and these four ribs are sealed with a lid. The resulting flexor manifold is intermittently redundant: it can be slightly squeezed and unclenched by changing the two-dimensional angles at the edges.

Cut→isolate→glue and you get the Steffen Flexor.
