CONVERSATION WITH AN ACADEMICIAN
V. P. MASLOV

Victor Pavlovich, when and how did you become interested in physics and mathematics? What will have the greatest impact - school, clubs, family?

I didn’t participate in krupki; at school I wasn’t particularly interested in mathematics or physics. On the contrary, I was quite weak in these subjects, especially in physics. And this was a time when many of my friends were interested in physics, then there was a fashion for physics. Maybe it was my backwardness in physics that played some role: I decided that I did not have enough education, that I needed to go to the physics department in order to take advantage of it.

You probably didn’t take part in the Olympics?

Once I took part in a math olympiad. I went more or less by accident, my friends just went there. It was the second round, I solved only one problem, after which I left. I received a certificate for this.

After graduating from school, you went to study at the Faculty of Physics of Moscow State University. Did you have other options for choosing your life path?

Yes, I was generally more interested in the humanities. But my stepfather, a famous historian, Professor Boris Fedorovich Porshnev, strongly dissuaded me from the humanitarian path. As a matter of fact, he persuaded me to go to the physics department.

Who do you consider to be your teachers? Which of your colleagues influenced you the most?

Euler's conjecture
The question of whether the shape of a polyhedral surface is uniquely determined by its edges, or whether the surface can somehow change, despite the immutability of its faces, interested mathematicians long before Cauchy.
The great Euler also pondered the question of uniqueness. In 1766, he expressed a hypothesis: “A closed spatial figure does not allow changes until it breaks.” Euler considered a “closed spatial figure” to be what is now commonly called a closed surface. Thus, Euler's assumption applied not only to polyhedral surfaces. But in relation to polyhedra, confidence in the validity of this hypothesis was most solid. This confidence was based on centuries of experience in dealing with polyhedra and, in its own way, achieved belief in an axiom.

Simple polyhedra
The question of the invariability of a polyhedral surface has proven to be a tough nut to crack, but before we can understand it, it is necessary to clarify some concepts. The conditions here by a polyhedron are not understood to be a body bounded by polygons, as is done in school, but a surface composed of these polygons; in this case we will propose that our polyhedron is glued together from a finite number of finite polygons so that to each side of any of its faces one and only one face is glued*). A polyhedron with such conditions is called closed. The condition of closure is a natural condition. Any of the polyhedra studied in the school course are recognized; paramids and Platonic solids are closed. A general plywood parcel box, but without a lid, cannot be considered a closed polyhedron. But once you nail the lid to it, you get a closed polyhedron.

We will also propose that our polyhedron is, as mathematicians say, a topological sphere. A topological sphere is a surface that differs from the usual “round” sphere of the present, just as the curved pore of a deflated ball differs from an inflated one. In other words, we propose that our polyhedron, if it were made of rubber, could be grown without tearing or gluing into a regular round sphere. The conditions call such a polyhedron simple. All convex polyhedra, including the “school” ones, are simple, just like both polyhedra in Figures 1, 2. On figure 3 there is an example of not a simple, but a triple-shaped polyhedron.

A polyhedron can be very “twisted”, which sometimes makes it difficult to figure out whether it is simple or not. It is all the more remarkable that you can tell about a polyhedron whether it is simple or not without even looking at it. Let's imagine that someone told us the number of all vertices B, the number of all edges P and the number of all faces Γ of an unknown polyhedron X. Then, subtracting the number φ(X), called the Euler characteristic of the polyhedron,

Flexibility of polyhedra

Let us now imagine that we have glued together a polyhedron from cardboard polygons using adhesive tape (of course, finite, closed, simple). It is clear that due to the flexible connection in each edge, any two connecting faces can be rotated relative to each other around a common edge, like the pages of a book, they could... if they were not connected to the other edges.

When the faces are glued together into a polyhedron, the question arises: is it possible to change and deform the polyhedron intermittently so that during deformation all its faces remain unchanged, and only other angles change? If such a deformation occurs for a given polyhedron, then we call the polyhedron flexible, and if not, we call it inflexible.

Thus, the bending of a polyhedron, if it is possible at all, is associated with a change in other angles. And, although each pair of edge-adjacent edges is itself free to choose its own different angle, it is highly likely that in the company of other edges this freedom is wasted. It is possible that this kind of “plausible reasoning” served as Euler’s support for the hypothesis that closed polyhedra are inflexible.

The question of the bendability of simple polyhedra was resolved only two centuries after Euler. Already in our time, just ten years ago, it was found out that regarding polyhedra Euler was right in his assumption... and wrong. Right because, as was established in 1975, “almost all” simple polyhedra are inflexible. However, “almost all” does not mean all polyhedra, but in some senses they are “an overwhelming large institution.” Two years later, in 1977, the American geometer R. Connelly constructed the first examples of flexible polyhedra and thereby disproved Euler's hypothesis (see «Kvant», 1978, No. 9, p. 14, as well as the first and fourth pages of the cover of this issue).

It is possible that the discovery of bendable polyhedra will not seem very surprising to someone, especially if he remembers the mechanics of musical instruments, for example, the button accordion. But this association does not diminish here. The fact is that the mechanisms “work” thanks to the creasing of the materials from which they are made. If we mehani are assembled from solid plates connected to each other by small door hinges, then it would not be possible to play the instrument. Such mechanisms, as is easy to understand, could neither be compressed nor expanded.

However, a bendable polyhedron is also unsuitable for mechanics, although for a different reason. The fact is that the currently known collection of bendable polyhedra has a somewhat unexpected property: the volume contained in the polyhedron remains constant during bending, i.e. the polyhedron “does not breathe.” A hypothesis arose that this is always the case - this volume does not change when the polyhedron is bent.

Note further that, due to Cauchy's theorem, a flexible polyhedron cannot be convex.

Indeed, the bendability of a given polyhedron means that there are other polyhedra glued together from the same faces in the same order, but not equal to this one due to slightly different other angles. Moreover, if the original polyhedron was convex, then the other polyhedron with only different angles must also be convex. And this

Cauchy's Lemma on Convex Polygons

To prove Cauchy's theorem, it is useful to carefully look at how things work with polygons. It is no coincidence that the title of Cauchy’s work on the theorem on polygons contains the word “polygon”. Let's imagine a flat polygon made up of rods with hinged connectors at their ends. In the case of a triangle, the lengths of the rods determine the angles between them (the third feature of triangles), and the structure is rigid. We encounter the practical application of this obvious geometric fact at every step: in rod structures that are subject to significant loads (place trusses, crane booms, structure floors, etc.), triangular elements are present to ensure rigidity.

In the case of a polygon with a large number of sides, the lengths of the sides do not determine its angles, and therefore, the polygon itself does not determine. However, Cauchy drew attention to one fact that turns out to be essential in the proof of his theorem.

Let $A = A_1A_2...A_n$ and $B = B_1B_2...B_n$ be convex $n$-gons, and let $A_1A_2 = B_1B_2, ..., A_{n-1}A_n = B_{n-1}B_n, A_nA_1 = B_nB_1$ be. Let us assign a “+” or “-” sign to each vertex $A_i$ of the first polygon, depending on whether it is $\angle A_i > \angle B_i$ or $\angle A_i < \angle B_i$. If $\angle A_i = \angle B_i$, then vertex $A_i$ remains unmarked. Before moving on to Cauchy's lemma, we note that unequal polygons have at least four marked vertices, otherwise (as is easy to prove) the polygons $A$ and $B$ are equal and there are no unmarked vertices at all.

Lemma 1. Let two convex polygons have pairwise equal sides, and let the corresponding sides be pairwise equal.

There are pairwise unequal angles. Then, when traversing the vertices of the polygons, the differences of the corresponding angles change sign at least four times.

It is clear that the number of sign changes when traversing the vertices of a polygon must be even and not equal to zero. Therefore, to prove the lemma it is necessary to show that the number of sign changes is not equal to two. Let us present the idea of ​​the proof.

Let us assume that the number of sign changes is two. Then the polygon $A$ is divided into two broken lines: one broken line $A_iA_{i+1}...A_j$ has vertices offset by a “+” sign, and none of the vertices are offset by a minus sign; the other broken line - $A_jA_{j+1}...A_i$, on the contrary, has vertices offset by the “-” sign, and none of the vertices are offset by the “+” sign (Fig. 4).

Therefore, the broken line $A_iA_{i+1}...A_j$ can be obtained from the corresponding broken line $B_iB_{i+1}...B_j$ of the polygon $B$, increasing the angles of the latter. Each is perfect next, that as a result of such a deformation of the broken line, the closing chord must increase, i.e. $A_iA_j > B_iB_j$. (I am not constructing a proof of this fact
