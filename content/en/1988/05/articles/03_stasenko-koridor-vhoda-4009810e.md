But the fact is that returning to Earth is no simpler than flying into space. In fact, how much energy does it take to lift someone’s weight (well, for example, himself) to a height h? Every literate schoolchild will say: mgh. And if you hide it from a height h, then this (potential) energy will turn into kinetic energy, and then turn into tello. But who will benefit from it, especially if h is equal to, say, the height of a ten-story building. After all, it is desirable to land softly, with almost zero speed, and this means that all the kinetic energy, into which potential energy is converted, needs to be put somewhere - for example, to spend on friction a rope thrown from the tenth floor.

In the same way, an aircraft, when landing on the Moon or on planets almost devoid of an atmosphere (Mercury, Mars), will have to dampen the speed and corresponding kinetic energy using the traction force of the rocket engines, directed in the opposite direction.

However, these explanations are correct if we mean the standard recommendation for school tasks: “neglect the sporting atmosphere.” But a parachute, a glider, an airplane with its engines turned off (which has become a glider) are recognized without wasting energy. Their speed is dampened by the aerodynamic force - the force acting from the air. Is it possible to use the Earth’s atmosphere for planning “free” recognition from space? This question is not as trivial as it may seem, if we remember the sad fate of the majority of meteorites, which never reach the surface of the Earth, and if they are added, then in a pitiful form. But we would like to land “entirely”, without expending either our mass or our energy.

So is it possible?

To answer this question, let's draw up a Plan. Now we will proceed according to plan: first, let us briefly recall how the atmosphere is structured, how the force with which a body moving in it is affected - the arodynamic force - depends on the density of the atmosphere and the speed of the body, how this body heats up and cools when moving in the atmosphere, and, finally, we will move on to the Conclusion.

Atmospheric density

So, first of all, decide about the Earth's atmosphere. True, there is no point in hoping to tell everything that is possible about the atmosphere of the Earth - about all its winds, thunderstorms, typhoons, clouds - this cannot be done even in a very thick book. But you can do what physicists do every time when everything is very complicated (and it’s almost always difficult) - you can build a model of the atmosphere. A good model should make sense of the meaningful features of the object or phenomenon that we intend to use to work with. And what is the most hasket for us in the atmosphere? Well, for example, its density - after all, we are going to move in the atmosphere, and quickly, and it is intuitively clear that the force of resistance to movement should depend on the density of the environment (after all

It is clear that the density of the atmosphere should decrease with height. The dependence of the density of the Earth's atmosphere on altitude above sea level can be approximately expressed by the so-called Bolytsman barometric formula

$$\rho \approx \rho_0 e^{-\frac{h}{h^*}}$$

It is clear that at an altitude of $h^*$ the density of the atmosphere is $e = 2,72$ times less than at sea level.

Aerodynamic force

A force acts on a body moving in the air. To verify this, it is enough, for example, to place your palm horizontally at the window of a moving bus or train carriage (carefully, of course!). When turning the palm around the horizontal axis, the force from the air will act on the hand either upward, when the wind hits the palm, or downward, when it hits the back side. In the first case, the angle of attack $\alpha$ (between the plane of the palm and the velocity vector $\vec{v}$ of the oncoming flow) is considered positive, in the second - negative (figure 1).

What does this power depend on?

We use dimensional theory to answer this question. First, we write down those characteristics of the process from which, as we expect, the said force will be generated.

Well, of course, it depends on the speed $v$ (completely different forces act on an outstretched hand when you are riding, say, on a metro ecalator and when you dry your hand from the window of a speeding automatic car). Further, if, for example, a palm is held in the air and in water, then (at the same speed of movement) in the second case the resistance is greater than in the first. This means that it is also important in what medium the movement occurs - the force of the palm depends on the density of the medium $\rho$. And from what else? Of course, it depends on the size of the body movement: try to wave your palm in the air without up and with up at the same speed - it will immediately become clear that it is more difficult to go up. Shock shock

Note that in our consideration we actually represented the apparatus as a plate of area $S$, inclined at an angle $\alpha$ to the flow of molecules, and the speeds of the molecules were considered identical in magnitude and direction - the apparatus moved much faster than the thermal motion of the molecules themselves. What happened on the back leeward side of the plate did not interest us: the molecules did not get there, so this space could be filled, for example, with the heat of a wedge with the same angle $\alpha$ at the apex. Let now this wedge descend into the dense layers of the atmosphere and move more and more slowly, but still faster than the average thermal speed of molecules and, therefore, faster than sound. (It is clear that such an organized process as a sound effect cannot propagate faster than the molecules themselves, moving at thermal speed.) In order to slow down as much as possible, let’s begin to tilt the wedge plane at an increasingly greater angle relative to the flow (after all, the resistance force is $F_x \sim \sin^3 \alpha$). Then the molecules will bounce off the windward half-plane at an increasingly right angle, and a sloped layer will begin to form in front of the wedge surface. When the plane is positioned perpendicular to the flow, molecules striking the wedge far from its edge will not have time to escape from it, and a real pusher will begin; and the oncoming molecules (they know nothing about the obstacle - after all, the speed is greater than the speed of sound) will crash into this thickness of molecules in a big way. A sloped layer will appear near the plane $S$, separated from the undisturbed gas by the so-called direct shock wave (figure 6). In this jump, the flow speed drops sharply (that’s why it’s a jump) - from supersonic to slow subsonic.

Entry Corridor, or Closure

At the beginning of the article, we discussed the fact that if a planet does not have an atmosphere or has one, but it is very rarefied, then for a soft landing, which requires braking, it will be necessary to expend almost as much energy as to escape from its gravitational field. But if there is an atmosphere of sufficient density, then you can save this energy (and therefore the associated mass of initially stored fuel) by using the drag force $F_x$ for braking, and the lift force $F_y$ for maintaining it in the atmosphere.

What does “sufficient density” of the atmosphere mean? And is it only the density of the atmosphere that should enter into our considerations? What about the size of the planet, its mass (on which the acceleration of gravity on the surface depends), the thickness of the atmosphere, its composition? What about the characteristics of the device itself?

This is what we are going to talk about now.

So, let an apparatus of mass $M$, having a wing area $S$, move with a speed of $v$ in the Earth’s atmosphere at an altitude of $h$, where the density of the atmosphere is $\rho$. The lift force will be $F_y = C_y \rho v^2 S$. Encircling the planet, the device moves in a search-disruption trajectory. For simplicity, let this trajectory be close to a circle with radius $R + h$ (R is the radius of the Earth); then the centripetal acceleration of the apparatus will be equal to $v^2/(R + h)$. This acceleration is determined by gravity and lift $F_y$:

$$Mg - F_y = \frac{Mv^2}{R + h}.$$

Considering that the “thickness” of the atmosphere is small compared to the radius of the Earth, we rewrite the last equation in the form

$$C_y \rho v^2 S \approx M \left( g - \frac{v^2}{R} \right).$$

From here it follows, first of all, that where there is almost no atmosphere, that is, the density and, consequently, an increase

What happens as the device descends? The speed of the apparatus will decrease under the influence of the resistance force $F_x$. But as it decreases, the density of the atmosphere quickly increases (see formula (1)). And, despite the drop in speed, the lifting force $F_y$ returns. And this lifting force can be used to delay the “fall” of the device. In this case, the aircraft actually becomes a glider, and the gravity force acting on it can be balanced by the lifting force:

$$Mg = C_y\rho v^2 S.$$

To use this capability of a winged vehicle, you need to fly at altitudes where the density of the atmosphere is not less than $\rho_{\min} \approx \frac{Mg/S}{C_y v^2}$ (the numerator of this fraction - the gravitational force per unit area of ​​the wing - is called the load on the wing, so we highlighted it). And using formula (1) you can determine the maximum altitude, no lower than which you can stay “on the wing” at a given flight speed $v$:

$$\frac{Mg/S}{C_y v^2} \approx \rho_0 e^{-\frac{h_{\max}}{h^*}} \Rightarrow \frac{h_{\max}}{h^*} \approx \ln \frac{\rho_0 C_y v^2}{Mg/S},$$

where

$$h_{\max}(v) \approx h^* \ln \frac{\rho_0 C_y v^2}{Mg/S}.$$

In other words, if we want the apparatus to “not fail” at a given altitude $h$, its speed must be no less than

$$v_{\min}(h) \approx \sqrt{\frac{Mg/S}{C_y \rho_0}} e^{\frac{h}{h^*}}.$$

Well, let's fly fast, shall we? Stop! Introducing, we know that at high speeds a shock wave will “sit” on the blunt edges of our apparatus, behind which the impact (or even not the impact, but a mixture of fragments of its molecules) will heat up so much that the apparatus can freeze. This is the most difficult problem of reentry - the thermal barrier. How to answer this tello? You can use everything: the thermal conductivity of the device itself, which allows heat to flow “from nose to tail,” its possible melting (and, therefore, changing its shape, which makes the device even “dumber”), the warm radiation of “hot added” parts of the device, etc.

We will not discuss this problem as a whole here, but to estimate the permissible speed we will take the “worst” case, when all the body brought to the apparatus by air is removed from its surface only by radiation.

There is such a law - the Stefan-Bolshman law - according to which the power carried away by radiation from each square meter of area is proportional to the fourth power of temperature: $q = \sigma T^4 \frac{\Delta J}{\mathrm{m}^2 \cdot \mathrm{c}}$. The proportionality coefficient $\sigma$ is called the Stefan-Bolshman constant; it can be written out from a physics reference book: $\sigma = 5,67 \cdot 10^{-8}$ J/(m$^2$·s·K$^4$). This is a very sharp dependence: if the temperature is increased, for example, $3^4=81$ times - almost a hundred times! According to this law, it turns out that for effective cooling by radiation it is necessary to “get even hotter.”

So, let’s estimate the maximum speed at which the device can move without yet swimming. The mass of air incident per unit time per unit surface area of ​​the apparatus is equal to $\rho v$. The energy brought by this air is $\rho v \frac{v^2}{2}$. Almost all of this energy turns into heat. And all this heat, according to the proposal, leaves the apparatus due to radiation, which per unit time carries away energy $\sigma T^4$ per unit area. So, we require that the surface temperature does not exceed the melting temperature $T_{\pi \pi}$. Then we will have

$$\frac{v^2}{2} \rho v \leq \sigma T_{\pi \pi}^4.$$

Thus, the speed of the vehicle cannot be greater than the value $v_{\max}$ defined for each altitude:

$$v \leq v_{\max}(h) = \left[ \frac{2 \sigma T_{\pi \pi}^4}{\rho(h)} \right]^{1/3}.$$
