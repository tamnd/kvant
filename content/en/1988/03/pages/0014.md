The title of this article seems a bit foreign, doesn't it? Weave something flexible. And here - straight! The title, however, does not talk about the process of weaving, but rather about the result. But can they be intertwined, hooked onto each other, cunningly arranged in relation to each other in a crossing straight line? At first glance, it seems that they cannot. However, where do we get this impression? In everyday life, we never deal with anything, let's put it straight: it is not so important that there are no infinitely thin objects - we are ready to neglect thickness - the essential absence of unlimited extended objects. Further, better than light - these examples of straightness - dissipating and weak, become imperceptible at a great distance. In practice, you only have to deal with straight segments. Any set of segments that do not intersect each other can be arranged randomly by dividing the segments so that they remain disjoint at all times. Experience convinces us of this, and it is not difficult to prove it. We depict them as straight lines as segments. That’s why we think that straight lines cannot be intertwined. Well, what is it really like?

First of all, let's more precisely formulate the questions that interest us. The first question is: can several non-intersecting lines be positioned differently? What does it mean to be “positioned differently”? Now we are not interested in either angles or distances between straight lines. We will assume that when moving straight lines, during which they remain non-intersecting, their relative positions do not change. If one set of straight lines cannot be obtained from another by such a movement, then the conditions are to assume that the straight lines in these sets are located differently.

The easiest for our spatial imagination are sets of parallel lines. It is clear that any two such sets consisting of the same number of lines have the same structure. Indeed, by rotating the entire space with straight lines of one set “frozen” into it, you can make them parallel to the straight lines of another set, after which, by moving the straight lines of the first set in turn so that they do not overlap and remain parallel, you can easily combine them with the straight lines of the second set.

Let us now turn to arbitrary sets of lines. Is it possible to turn an arbitrary set of disjoint lines into a set of parallel lines, to “comb” it, as it were? This problem has a simple and unexpected solution. Try to answer without looking at the subsequent text. Have you thought about it? Well, then read on. The answer is always possible. Let's prove it.

Let us take an arbitrary set of disjoint lines. Let us choose two planes parallel to each other, not parallel to any straight line in our set. Let us fix the points of intersection of the first plane with the straight lines: we will place hinges there.
