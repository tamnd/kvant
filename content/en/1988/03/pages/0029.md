F1084. If you press a nickel against the frozen glass for a short time, a circle will melt on the glass along the edge of the nickel. Why?

When we press a nickel against the glass, it heats up from our hand. It heats up and melts the frost. And the coin touches primarily at the edges, where there is a thickening. There is an air gap between the rest of the coin and the frost. The thermal conductivity of air is much less than the thermal conductivity of metal. Therefore, it first “warms up” and thaws along the ring, along the edge of the coin. After this, the coin “lies” on its entire surface, and melting occurs over the entire area of ​​​​the coin.

L. A. Ashkinazi

F1085. figure 1 depicts the process performed on an ideal gas and transferring it from state B. Find on the graph the sections of the process where the temperature of the gas increases (decreases).

Let us consider two fairly close points of the process $C$ and $D$ on the curve and write down an expression for the change in gas temperature during the transition from point $C$ to points $D$:

$$\Delta T_{CD} = T_C - T_D = \frac{M}{mR}((p + \Delta p)(V + \Delta V) - pV) \approx \frac{M}{mR}(p \cdot \Delta V + V \cdot \Delta p).$$

Let $\Delta T_{CD} \approx 0$ (this means that either points $C$ and $D$ lie on the same isotherm, or during transition $C \rightarrow D$ the gas temperature passes through an extreme value (maximum or minimum)). Then

$$\frac{p}{V} = -\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta V}.$$

(*)

From figure 1 it is clear that $\frac{p}{V} = \text{ctg } \alpha$, and at sufficiently close points $C$ and $D$

$$\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta V} = \text{tg } \beta' \approx \text{tg } \beta'' \approx \text{tg } \beta = -\text{ctg } \gamma.$$

Thus, from (*) it follows that

$$\text{ctg } \alpha = \text{ctg } \gamma, \text{ или } \alpha = \gamma.$$

Let us now assume that at some point on the curve $AB$ $\alpha < \gamma$. Then $\text{ctg } \alpha > \text{ctg } \gamma$, and repeating the above reasoning in reverse order, we obtain that $\Delta T_{CD} > 0$. Consequently, the temperature of the gas increases when passing through this point. And vice versa: if at some point $\alpha > \gamma$, then $\Delta T_{CD} < 0$, and the gas temperature in this case decreases.

Having found points $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$ on the graph, in which $\alpha = \gamma$ (see Fig. 2), we find out that in process sections $AM_1$ and $M_2M_3$, where $\alpha < \gamma$, the gas temperature increases, and in sections $M_1M_2$ and $M_3B$ ($\alpha > \gamma$) - decreases.

I. Yu. Poteryayko
