The diagram of the device constructed by I.K. Kikoin and his colleagues is shown at figure.

On axis 1, mounted on agate bearings (to reduce friction), a magnetized rod 2 is fixed. The counteracting moment is created by spiral springs 3. The axis with a rod attached to it supplies an arrow that slides along the dial, on which 100 divisions are marked. The device was portable and could simply be installed on a current-carrying busbar anywhere. The distance from the bus to the magnetized rod is always the same (several centimeters), so the angle of rotation of the arrow depends only on the current strength. Experience has shown that a slight inaccuracy depends on the current strength, observed only at the highest current strengths (starting from the 84th scale division). A particular difficulty was the calibration of the device. After all, it was impossible to compare its readings with a reference device, as is usually done - such devices simply do not exist. Therefore, a calibration method based on the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction was specially developed.

In addition to the portable kiloammeter, a stationary device was developed to measure current in individual electrical baths for measuring current in the main circuit and transmitting its readings to the control panel. To do this, it was necessary to “convert” the angle of rotation into an electrical signal, which was transmitted to the remote control.

Although the error in measuring the current strength was quite large - 3%, for technical measurements it was quite acceptable and satisfying to customers.

In addition to kiloammeters, I.K. Kikoin and his colleagues developed instruments for measuring power (wattmeters), energy meters and amperor (electric charge) meters, also necessary for electrolysis plants - all designed for currents of about 70 kA. It should be noted that not only was the idea of ​​the devices proposed and their design developed, but all these devices were manufactured in the workshops of the Institute of Metal Physics in Sverdlovsk, where I.K. Kikoin worked at that time, and supplied in the required quantities to electrolysis plants in the Urals and neighboring areas. This allows the Ural Aluminum Plant to begin producing products - the most important metal for the automotive industry.

Thus, work that began back in 1939 turned out to be in 1942 just the bones for the life of an important branch of the defense industry. On the day of the 25th anniversary of the Great October Socialist Revolution, it was announced that I. K. Kikoin, S. V. Gubar and V. S. Obukhov were awarded the State Prize. This was the first State Prize awarded to a physicist from the Urals.
