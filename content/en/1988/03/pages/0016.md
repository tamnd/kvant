Orientation and semi-orientation

An oriented line is a straight line with a direction marked on it. The direction is usually shown by an arrow. A primer can be oriented in two ways, two straight lines in four ways, a set of $n$ straight lines in $2^n$ ways. A semi-orientation of a set of lines is a pair of opposite orientations of this set. In other words, a semi-orientation of a set of lines is its orientation up to the simultaneous inversion of all arrows (“up to reverse”). figure 2 shows two orientations of a pair of lines, constituting one of its semi-orientations.

Each pair of non-perendicular straight lines can first be given a canonical semi-porentation (i.e., a semi-porentation that is determined by the pair itself, or more precisely, by the relative position of the lines included in it).

To do this, we somehow orient one of the lines of such a pair and rotate this line so that it becomes parallel to the other line. This rotation can be accomplished in two ways (Fig. 3); Let's choose the most economical one - the one at which the angle of rotation is the smallest (it is the only one, since the straight lines are not orthogonal). The result is an oriented straight line, it is parallel to the second straight line of our pair, which thereby acquires orientation (Fig. 4). Thus, choosing the orientation of one of the lines gives the orientation of the pair. If we choose the opposite orientation of this line, we will get the opposite orientation of the pair. Starting with another straight line, we get the same two opposite orientations of the pair. These two orientations constitute the communicated canonical semiporentation.

Isotopy, during which the angle between straight lines does not change, transforms canonical semiporentation into canonical semiporentation.
