About the time machine and the theory of relativity

In the eighth chapter of the physics course for grade 10, you became acquainted with the constructions of the special theory of relativity (SRT) and the main consequences from them. However, from the provisions of SRT, it states that the simultaneity of spatially separated events is relative, i.e., it depends on the reporting system in which the observation is carried out. This statement, like many others, refers to the theory of relativity, it seems to countries, even paradoxical, and in any case contradicts common sense. Why?

From the first steps of science fiction, one of the stated techniques of the genre was “time travel.” Many authors use it to create a red "time paradox", an absurd closure of a chain of events. Thus, the hero of one fantastic story is mixed up in the previous state and meets a handsome concha, who, as she knows, should become the author of replacement discoveries. This is exactly what happens, but only due to the fact that the heroine herself, having a good memory, promptly explains to him all the details of the next discovery, known to her from “future” textbooks and monographs. As you understand, no one opened anything. Simply because of the displacement in time, the effect (the hero of the story knows the details of the discovery) produced equal causes (the discovery itself), which led to the paradox.

To prevent such paradoxes from arising, it is impossible, as common sense suggests, to swap the past and the future.

- Stop! - you say. - In that case, something is wrong with the theory of relativity. After all, regarding simultaneity, I am aware that for different observers the order of events in time can be different.

Let's illustrate this with a specific example. Consider a very long platform A standing motionless on the rails, at the ends of which there are two light receivers, and two trains B and C, traveling to the right and left, respectively. At some point in time, an instantaneous flash of light is produced at a point located exactly halfway between the receivers. Let's call this event number 1. Then event No. 2 - the light ray is detected by the left receiver and event No. 3 - the light ray is detected by the right receiver. It is clear that in the reporting system associated with platform A, event #2 and #3 occur simultaneously. However, in the reporting system associated with train B, which is moving to the right, event No. 3 will occur more equally than event No. 2, since the right receiver in this system moves towards the light beam, and the left receiver runs away from the light beam. In the reporting system associated with train C going to the right, event #3 will occur later than event #2. The whole point here, of course, is that for all three observers light will propagate at the same speed (in accordance with the second step of SRT).

It turns out that with the advent of SRT, the very concept of “earlier-later” became relative. What was “earlier” in one reporting system may be “later” in another system.

“All this looks very strange,” you say. “As we have seen, changing events in places in time through services has consequences.”

It turns out that everything is not so strange. Depriving the absolute meaning of the concepts “earlier-later” and “simultaneously”, STR never leads to a violation of the principle-consequences of the connection between events.
