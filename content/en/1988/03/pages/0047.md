When is this possible and when is it not? Let's start from the other end - we will try to establish when a principle-and-effect connection can exist between two events.

In classical (pre-Einstein) physics, there was the following simple answer to this question: if one event occurs later than another, then it can be its consequence, regardless of where these events occur. The fact is that there was no reason to believe that the speed of transmission of information (signals) is limited in any way. Then, even if event $B$ occurred very far from event $A$ and not much later, it is possible, using a signal of sufficiently high speed, to transmit information about event $A$ to point $B$ even before the occurrence of event $B$. It turns out that any violation of the time sequence of events “earlier-later” could lead to a violation of principle-effect relationships. According to the theory of relative nikostics, a signal cannot propagate at a speed greater than the speed of light in vacuum $c$. Based on this, the conditions for the emergence of a fundamental connection between events also change. If a ray of light from point $A$ arrives at point $B$ before an event has occurred there, then event $A$ can affect event $B$. It is in this case that event $B$ is considered fundamentally related to event $A$. Let's write these conditions like this:

$$t_B - t_A \geq \frac{r_{AB}}{c},$$

where $r_{AB}$ is the distance between the points where events $A$ and $B$ occur. Condition

$$t_A - t_D \geq \frac{r_{AD}}{c}$$

means that event $A$ can be a consequence of event $D$, i.e. event $D$ is fundamentally related to event $A$, albeit in reverse order. If

$$|t_A - t_K| < \frac{r_{AK}}{c},$$

then the events, even without being simultaneous, are completely independent of one another; no information about one event can come to the present moment of the other.
