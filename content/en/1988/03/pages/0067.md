TWO GAMES

Last time we mentioned Dawson's chess, which is played on a 3x8 board with only pawns. Pawns move and behave according to the usual rules, and they must be taken. The one who cannot make the next move loses - all his remaining pawns are locked.

Let White's first move be 1. a2, then Black is forced to answer 1...ba, and White also 2. ba. The verticals “a” and “b” are “out of play”, and it’s Black’s move. If they play 2...f2, then White should play 3. ef or 3. gf. After the exchanges, from the three filled verticals “e”, “f” and “g”, only one “f” remains with the pawns stuck on it, and it’s White’s turn to move again. So, any move leads to a reduction in the number of verticals on which one can still move by 3, 2 or 1 (if the adjacent verticals are empty) and the transfer of the move to the partner. Therefore, the game can be reformulated as follows: White and Black take turns placing pawns on the 2nd rank on squares not bordering those already occupied; whoever cannot make a move loses. Further we will consider the game in this form.

It must be said that analyzing a game on a 3×n board for arbitrary values ​​of n is a very complex mathematical problem. Consider n from 1 to 8. For n=1 and n=2, White wins (1. a2). They also win with n=3 (1. b2, but not 1. a2? c2). When n=4, black wins (1. a2 c2; 1. b2 d2). It is easy to check that with the next three values ​​of n (n=5, 6, 7) they win again, and with n=8 they cannot avoid defeat. Due to symmetry, it is enough to space four “openings”: 1. a2 e2 2. g2 c2; 1. b2 f2! 2. d2 h2; 1. c2 e2 2. g2 a2; 1. d2 f2 2. a2 h2.

In principle, it is possible to derive recurrence relations to determine the results for various values ​​of n, but there is no simple formula or rule to determine the winner for a given n. Even more difficult is the analysis of Dawson’s “meager” chess formula (this is what the author of the game originally proposed): the one who makes the last move loses.

Let's consider another interesting chess-mathematical game related to the meaning of the “eight queens problem.” The two players take turns placing queens on the file "a", "b", etc. No two of them must be each other (the color of the queens does not matter). The player who cannot make another move loses.

In this game 5 moves were made - the beginner won (all fields of the “f” vertical are controlled by the already placed queens).

Readers are invited to consider the “game of queens” on the n×n board. We can hypothesize that in the first version of the game, white wins on odd boards, and black wins on even boards. In the second option, the game again ends in a draw (zero win). Moreover, if one of the players deviates from the arrangement of n queens that do not threaten each other on the n×n board, then he must lose.
