In the experiments, rue was chosen as a sample because at room temperature it is always in a liquid state. The Hall effect was not found in it. As it turned out later, even in solid ruthium this effect is very small and difficult to measure. Experiments were also carried out with whistles. Bismuth seemed very appropriate, since in solid height the Hall emf is several orders of magnitude greater than in other metals: in “ordinary” metals it is measured in microvolts, and in height it is measured in millivolts. However, no effect was found in liquid altitude. It is now known that the anomalously larger Hall effect in solid altitude is due to the characteristics of its crystal structure. In the liquid state this structure disappears. And experimenters who were going to measure a larger effect, seeing that it was 1000 times less than expected, came to the conclusion that there was simply no effect.

I decided to take metal as a sample, which is the simplest. This is silk metal. Silk metals are good because practically all theoretical predictions regarding their properties are well determined in experiments. For the experiment, I chose an alloy of sodium and potassium, which, at a known concentration, the component becomes liquid already at room temperature.

It seemed to me that at room temperature in previous experiments the connection was not only with the poor choice of samples, but also with the insufficiently accurate setup of the experiment. The fact is that when a current is passed through a liquid metal located in a magnetic field, forces begin to act on the metal from the side of the magnetic field, which lead to the mixing of individual layers of liquid metal relative to each other. (In solid metal, of course, this movement of layers does not occur.) As a result, output currents will arise that “lubricate” the Hall effect in the liquid metal. So it is necessary, if not to eliminate it, then to minimize these “parasitic” currents. To do this, we took a sample in the form of a thin layer of liquid metal. Careful measurements revealed the presence of the Hall effect. A year and a half later, Sommerfeld’s work was published, which described experiments and confirmed the modern theory of metals. Sommerfeld also referred to our experience. To tell the truth, I was extraordinary for a year - such an authority as Sommerfeld appreciated my work.
