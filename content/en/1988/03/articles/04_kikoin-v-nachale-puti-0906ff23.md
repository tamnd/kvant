AT THE BEGINNING OF THE JOURNEY

Isak Konstantinovich Kikoin graduated from secondary school No. 1 named after L. M. Pozemsky in Pskov in 1923. He always recalled his years as a scientist with gratitude. The school helped him find his calling and instilled in him a love for physics, a science that he devoted his whole life to.

In February 1983, sixth graders from Pskov School No. 1 wrote Isak Konstantinovich a letter asking him to tell about the things he had learned at school, about himself, about his work. Isak Konstantinovich readily responded to the request. At different times, he wrote four letters to schoolchildren.

We present excerpts from these poems to your attention.

...I was born on March 28, 1908 in the city of Žagre (this is in the Lithuanian SSR).

...The family adhered to the creative rule - to teach children to do as much work as possible around the house, to take care of all family members; a love of work and responsibility for the assigned work were instilled.

Throughout my life I have remained grateful to my parents for this. In my life I often encountered difficulties and remembered the behests of my father.

In our family, children were taught to write and read early. At three years old I already knew how to read and write. Every day I received a task from my father, for which I had to report to him. My father also asked me texts that I had to answer him by heart. Now I understand that these oral tasks contributed to the formation of a good memory in me, which I retained for the rest of my life.

...In 1916 we moved to Opochka, Pskov province (now region), where I went to school.

Considering it was not difficult for me, so much so that, having “skipped” the grade twice, in 1921 I found myself in the last grade. But in the same year, my father was transferred as a mathematics teacher at your school, which was then called the school’s 1st and 2nd students.

Naturally, I went to study at the same school. Thus, I spent the last two academic years in our school... I have the brightest and most pleasant memories of these powerful years.

Quite early, while still in elementary school, I diligently studied mathematics and physics; Probably, the influence of the father affected.

The school where you are now studying created exceptionally favorable conditions for in-depth study of many of these subjects.

We were taught mathematics by a first-class teacher, Dmitry Mikhailovich Lyapunov, to whom I am very grateful for the knowledge I received from him.

I was very lucky with physics too.

I was entrusted with managing the school's physics room and, together with my school friend, managing the school library (both were my social work).

...The physics room was well equipped with instruments, and I had the opportunity to carry out many experiments. True, many devices needed repair, and I gained experience in manufacturing the parts necessary for this.

The school library was extremely rich, containing several parts of thousands of volumes. There were a lot of books on physics and mathematics.

... Almost every day I happily spent time studying in the library.

...I graduated from school at the age of fifteen. By this time, I had already decided exactly in which higher educational institution I would continue my education. The fact is that shortly before this, an article by the outstanding physicist Academician Abram Fedorovich Ioffe appeared in one of the central newspapers about his main department of physics and mechanics at the Leningrad Polytechnic Institute. M.I. Kalinina. After reading this article, I firmly decided to enroll only in this faculty. But... youth were accepted into the vocational schools starting from the age of seventeen. Therefore, I decided to enter the Pskov Digging School - one of the fifth schools that existed in the country. It was the best of the secondary technical institutions in Pskov. Unfortunately, the wonderful environment in which the school was located is no longer there. I easily passed the exams and was accepted into the third year of the school (later it became known as the Land Management Technical School). However, the reason why I chose this school was that, given the relatively significant unemployment that then existed in the country, there were not enough land surveyors.

I will not dwell on the years spent at this school, but will limit myself to pointing out that my good physics and mathematics training helped me graduate from school with the highest grades.

...I was already 17 years old then, and I had the right to enter the Polytechnic Institute of my choice, the Faculty of Physics and Mechanics. ...Perhaps you will be interested to know how my entrance exams for this faculty went. In those days, the rules for passing exams for admission to universities changed frequently. In particular, in 1925, for those entering Moscow and Leningrad universities, the rules stipulated that persons who graduated from secondary schools or secondary technical educational institutions that year and wished to enter universities in the same year took exams in their region or provincial cities. Persons who graduated from secondary educational institutions before 1925 were examined at the admissions committees of colleges in the same way as now).

Thus, I have to take exams at home in Pskov. For the Pskov province, two places were allocated for the Faculty of Physics and Mechanics that I had chosen.

...At the beginning of the school year I come to Leningrad. I came to my faculty and read the list of pleasant students. My last name is not there!

...The next morning (it was August 28) I went to the office to find out the reason for this obvious misunderstanding. When I told my last name to the head of the offices, he ordered... to bring my personal file. He began to leaf through it and said: “You are not accepted because you did not appear for the exams.” In response to my explanation that I took the exams in Pskov to the provincial selection committee, the answer was: “You graduated from school in 1923, and not this year, 1925, and according to the existing rules you must take the exam here - at the institute.” In response to this, I said that this year I graduated from the Pskov Land Surveying School. This was followed by an answer that surprised me: “We know nothing about this.” I began to argue that my personal file should include a certificate of completion of the Land Surveying School in Pskov. Looking through the personal file again and finding this certificate, he categorically stated: “Since your certificate is not in the printing house, it cannot serve as an official document for us.” I explained to him the reason... and showed the printing house the certificate issued to me, emphasizing that the texts, seals and signatures in both documents exactly match.

...I headed to the first of the indicated audiences. There was a math exam going on. A respectable man in a naval uniform sat at the table. Later I found out that this was the famous professor R. O. Kuzmin, who lectured on higher mathematics at the Faculty of Karabakh (he wore a naval uniform). On the table in front of him was Bychkov's problem book in mathematics. The professor opened a scientific problem book and suggested solving two quadratic equations with two unknowns. I looked at the problem and immediately, without solving it, told him the answer. He checked the answer and asked how I solved the problem so quickly. I told him that the problem was simple and could be solved in a simple manner. I must admit to you that this was not true. The explanation was that I have a good memory, and I completed all the problems in this textbook... and memorized the answers.

Then Professor Kuzmin offered me another problem - three quadratic equations with three unknowns. I again gave him the answers, without solving this system, I said that such a problem could easily be solved in ume. This, of course, was also untrue. Then he closed the problem book and asked me to calculate the logarithm of a number, which I did successfully, in the normal way. After asking me a few oral questions on geometry, to which I answered correctly, he asked where I had studied and then gave me a grade of “V” (very satisfactory)... The math exam lasted about fifteen minutes. From there I ran to the classroom where the physics exam was taking place. This exam took 15-20 minutes and I also received a "B" grade.

The exams in chemistry and political education were also successful.

The last exam in literature remains. For this exam, we gathered all those who took the exam into one auditorium. The exam was taken by two school teachers (there was no such subject at the Polytechnic Institute). They wrote on the board topics for essays to choose from for the exam. ...I loved the works of N.V. Gogol, re-read them many times and enjoyed everything that had been written about Gogol himself and his work. I chose this topic. When I said that I had not yet finished my essay, she asked: “How much have you written?” Hearing that I had written about 12 pages, she said that this was quite enough, and added: “It’s not so important to us what you wrote, but how you wrote it.” As far as I later understood, they were interested in the literacy of the applicants.

The next day I was enrolled as a student in the Faculty of Physics and Mechanics of the Polytechnic Institute.

Many years later, my teacher, Academician A.F. Ioffe, while visiting me at home in Moscow, told my family that once upon a time he, as the dean of the faculty, was “everyone told” that a student appeared at his faculty who, within one day, passed the entrance exams to the institute and at the same time “very satisfactorily.”

Thus began the happy days of my youth.
