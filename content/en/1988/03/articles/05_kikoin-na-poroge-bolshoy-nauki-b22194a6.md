ON THE THRESHOLD OF BIG SCIENCE

In 1977, on the pages «Kvant», Academician N.K. Kioin spoke about the main stages in the formation of Soviet physics. In this story, a lot of space was devoted to the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology (LPTI), which at the end of the twenties became one of the main centers of physical science in the USSR.

As a second-year student at the Leningrad Polytechnic Institute, Isaac Konstantinovich began working at LFTN. We offer our readers from the article by N.K. Kioin “How Soviet Physics was Created” («Kvant», 1977, No. 10-12), in which he shares his memories of student life, closely connected with LFTN.

...Opposite our institute, across the street called “The Road to Sosnovka,” there was the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology (then it was called the Physics and Technology RENTENOVSKY INSTITUT). Its director was Academician A.F. Ioffe…. The bulk of the employees were young people who started working as students of the Faculty of Physics and Mechanics.

...Students were selected to work at the institute in this way. Faculty teachers - employees of the institute, including senior students who supervised the students' laboratory work, had instructions from A.F. Ioffe to keep an eye on students who showed interest and ability in experimental physics, and to invite them to work at the physics-technical institute. In 1927, as a second-year student, I was invited to work at the Institute of Physics and Technology. There, young people immediately approached modern science. We are not very friendly and did not take into account the fact that we had not yet “got through something” at our faculty. At scientific seminars, which took place regularly on Fridays, from 5 to 7 pm, scientific works just completed by the institute’s staff or published in foreign journals were discussed. We took part in the seminar, we had to listen and understand what was being said, and if we didn’t understand, then we could cope. At first we didn’t even dare to ask because we didn’t understand anything. But quite quickly the concept of a real person, apparently because we were led to a style of presentation and read a lot ourselves.

...When I started working at the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology, under the influence of lectures and seminars by Ya. I. Frenkel, who told us about modern ideas in metal physics, I decided to study the issues of this particular area of ​​physics and, above all, the Hall effect. I decided to check whether the Hall effect would arise in liquid metals. Earlier, at the beginning of the century, this issue was dealt with by such luminaries of science as Nernst and Drude. Having reviewed the literature on this issue, I came to the conclusion that the choice of liquid metals in previous experiments was made unsuccessfully. Firstly

In the experiments, rue was chosen as a sample because at room temperature it is always in a liquid state. The Hall effect was not found in it. As it turned out later, even in solid ruthium this effect is very small and difficult to measure. Experiments were also carried out with whistles. Bismuth seemed very appropriate, since in solid height the Hall emf is several orders of magnitude greater than in other metals: in “ordinary” metals it is measured in microvolts, and in height it is measured in millivolts. However, no effect was found in liquid altitude. It is now known that the anomalously larger Hall effect in solid altitude is due to the characteristics of its crystal structure. In the liquid state this structure disappears. And experimenters who were going to measure a larger effect, seeing that it was 1000 times less than expected, came to the conclusion that there was simply no effect.

I decided to take metal as a sample, which is the simplest. This is silk metal. Silk metals are good because practically all theoretical predictions regarding their properties are well determined in experiments. For the experiment, I chose an alloy of sodium and potassium, which, at a known concentration, the component becomes liquid already at room temperature.

It seemed to me that at room temperature in previous experiments the connection was not only with the poor choice of samples, but also with the insufficiently accurate setup of the experiment. The fact is that when a current is passed through a liquid metal located in a magnetic field, forces begin to act on the metal from the side of the magnetic field, which lead to the mixing of individual layers of liquid metal relative to each other. (In solid metal, of course, this movement of layers does not occur.) As a result, output currents will arise that “lubricate” the Hall effect in the liquid metal. So it is necessary, if not to eliminate it, then to minimize these “parasitic” currents. To do this, we took a sample in the form of a thin layer of liquid metal. Careful measurements revealed the presence of the Hall effect. A year and a half later, Sommerfeld’s work was published, which described experiments and confirmed the modern theory of metals. Sommerfeld also referred to our experience. To tell the truth, I was extraordinary for a year - such an authority as Sommerfeld appreciated my work.

...In 1930, when I had just graduated from college and received the title of engineer, on the recommendation of A.F. Ioffe, I was sent on a business trip to Germany to get acquainted with the physical laboratoriums of the West. I met about three locals in Germany and was able to get acquainted with the work of the physical laboratories of Leipzig, Monchen, and Hamburg. And I must say that I was very surprised when I became convinced that the level of our physicists, in particular my own level, was no lower than the level of physicists I met in laboratories abroad.

I'm on a business trip, I'm near my place of work at the laboratorium in Monchen, in the former laboratorium of Roentgen. There are works and university doctorates (that’s what they called students graduating from university who were preparing their dissertations). However, I noticed that doctoral students, preparing for final exams, read Ya. I. Frenkel’s book “Course in Electrodynamics,” published in German. I asked: “Who will you give the exams to?” They answered: “Sommerfeld.” That same Sommerfeld, the greatest world-class theorist about whom, when we were students, there was a saying “there is no Bohr except

I knew that there was a five-volume course on physics by Sommerfeld himself, and I asked why doctoral students study electrodynamics not according to Sommerfeld, but according to Frenkel.
