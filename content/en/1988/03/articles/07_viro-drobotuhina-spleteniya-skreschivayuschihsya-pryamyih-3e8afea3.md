The title of this article seems a bit foreign, doesn't it? Weave something flexible. And here - straight! The title, however, does not talk about the process of weaving, but rather about the result. But can they be intertwined, hooked onto each other, cunningly arranged in relation to each other in a crossing straight line? At first glance, it seems that they cannot. However, where do we get this impression? In everyday life, we never deal with anything, let's put it straight: it is not so important that there are no infinitely thin objects - we are ready to neglect thickness - the essential absence of unlimited extended objects. Further, better than light - these examples of straightness - dissipating and weak, become imperceptible at a great distance. In practice, you only have to deal with straight segments. Any set of segments that do not intersect each other can be arranged randomly by dividing the segments so that they remain disjoint at all times. Experience convinces us of this, and it is not difficult to prove it. We depict them as straight lines as segments. That’s why we think that straight lines cannot be intertwined. Well, what is it really like?

First of all, let's more precisely formulate the questions that interest us. The first question is: can several non-intersecting lines be positioned differently? What does it mean to be “positioned differently”? Now we are not interested in either angles or distances between straight lines. We will assume that when moving straight lines, during which they remain non-intersecting, their relative positions do not change. If one set of straight lines cannot be obtained from another by such a movement, then the conditions are to assume that the straight lines in these sets are located differently.

The easiest for our spatial imagination are sets of parallel lines. It is clear that any two such sets consisting of the same number of lines have the same structure. Indeed, by rotating the entire space with straight lines of one set “frozen” into it, you can make them parallel to the straight lines of another set, after which, by moving the straight lines of the first set in turn so that they do not overlap and remain parallel, you can easily combine them with the straight lines of the second set.

Let us now turn to arbitrary sets of lines. Is it possible to turn an arbitrary set of disjoint lines into a set of parallel lines, to “comb” it, as it were? This problem has a simple and unexpected solution. Try to answer without looking at the subsequent text. Have you thought about it? Well, then read on. The answer is always possible. Let's prove it.

Let us take an arbitrary set of disjoint lines. Let us choose two planes parallel to each other, not parallel to any straight line in our set. Let us fix the points of intersection of the first plane with the straight lines: we will place hinges there. We will also fix the points of intersection of the second plane with straight lines, but in such a way that they are not movable only on the plane, but could slide along straight lines. Orientation and semi-orientation

An oriented line is a straight line with a direction marked on it. The direction is usually shown by an arrow. A primer can be oriented in two ways, two straight lines in four ways, a set of $n$ straight lines in $2^n$ ways. A semi-orientation of a set of lines is a pair of opposite orientations of this set. In other words, a semi-orientation of a set of lines is its orientation up to the simultaneous inversion of all arrows (“up to reverse”). figure 2 shows two orientations of a pair of lines, constituting one of its semi-orientations.

Each pair of non-perendicular straight lines can first be given a canonical semi-porentation (i.e., a semi-porentation that is determined by the pair itself, or more precisely, by the relative position of the lines included in it).

To do this, we somehow orient one of the lines of such a pair and rotate this line so that it becomes parallel to the other line. This rotation can be accomplished in two ways (Fig. 3); Let's choose the most economical one - the one at which the angle of rotation is the smallest (it is the only one, since the straight lines are not orthogonal). The result is an oriented straight line, it is parallel to the second straight line of our pair, which thereby acquires orientation (Fig. 4). Thus, choosing the orientation of one of the lines gives the orientation of the pair. If we choose the opposite orientation of this line, we will get the opposite orientation of the pair. Starting with another straight line, we get the same two opposite orientations of the pair. These two orientations constitute the communicated canonical semiporentation.

Isotopy, during which the angle between straight lines does not change, transforms canonical semiporentation into canonical semiporentation. This suggests that we consider another type of isotopy - isotopy of semi-porentation pairs: we allow changes in the angle and distance between straight lines, but require that the semi-porentation does not change during the isotopy (semi-porentation can

Line engagement coefficient

Each pair of straight lines obtained has a characteristic, which can take values ​​+1 and -1. This characteristic is called the gearing coefficient. It preserves under isotopies, and therefore if two obtained pairs of straight lines have different coupling coefficients, then they are not isotopic. This is how the coefficient of engagement is determined. The most economical way to combine one straight line with another that intersects it is to move the line along a common perpendicular and rotate it through the smallest angle necessary to match the directions. In this case, the straight line will move either like the handle of the right gimlet or like the handle of the left one (Fig. 5); then, that the type of gimlet does not depend on which of the two straight lines we moved. In the first case, the gearing coefficient is -1, in the second - +1.

It is clear that reorientation of one of the straight lines of the pair entails a change in the gearing coefficient.

When the orientation of the pair is replaced by the opposite one, the coefficient of engagement does not change; therefore, it is a characteristic of the obtained pair and depends only on the obtained orientation. When reflected in a mirror, the coefficient of engagement of a pair of oriented straight lines changes (Fig. 6).

Let us now recall the accident that we encountered in our search for an isotopy between two pairs of skew lines that preserves the distances and angles between the lines (Fig. 7). Then we could not answer the question about the isotopicity of the sets shown in figure 8. But now we know that such pairs (with their canonical resulting matrices) are obtained from each other by reflection in the mirror and therefore have different coupling coefficients. This means that such pairs cannot be connected by isotopy, during which the distances and angles between the pairs do not change. And if two pairs have the same distances, angles and coefficients of engagement, then these pairs can be connected by such an isotope.

Straight threes

When we worked in pairs, the common perpendicular of a pair of intersecting lines played a dark role. Structurally speaking, one could do without it, but it is so naturally connected with straight lines, so reliably connects them into something whole and visible, that it would be strange not to use it. Now it would be nice to find an object that is also inherent in a trio of intersecting straight lines. We will not do this for a triple whose lines lie

The fact is that this arrangement is unstable: slightly rotate any of the straight lines, we get the depicted three, to which our designs are applied.

So, consider an arbitrary triple of pairwise skew lines that does not contain parallel planes in three. Through each of these lines we draw two planes parallel to the other two lines. This produces six planes. It is clear that they fall into three parallel pairs (each pair of skew lines is contained in two parallel planes). Intersecting, the planes form a parallelepid. Our straight lines are continuations of its three pairwise crossing edges (Fig. 9). Thus, any three pairwise intersecting straight lines that do not lie in three parallel planes are continuations of the edges of a certain parallelepid. This parallelepid is the object promised above, associated with the triple of straight lines. What is it replaced with? First of all, he is the only one.

In fact, a single plane passes through a line, parallel to another line that intersects it, and if these lines are a continuation of the edges of the parallelepid, then one of its faces lies in this plane. Consequently, the six planes we have constructed are uniquely determined by the original three straight lines, and any parallelepid whose edges lie on these straight lines is bounded by these planes and, therefore, is also uniquely determined.

We see that the load-bearing parallelepid connects straight lines no worse than an ordinary perpendicular connected straight lines in a palais. Here, as in the case of a general perpendicular and in the case of the resulting pair of non-perpendicular straight lines, something additional to it will naturally arise from the original geometric configuration, but canonically connected with it and therefore worthy of attention when studying the original object.

Mystery. On figure 10 there are shown, in accordance with what was proven above, two parallelepipeds with edges lying on three pairwise intersecting straight lines. What's the matter? Where is the deception hidden - in the evidence or in figure?

Will be engaged in the classification of triples up to isotopies. As was shown, no matter which three of pairwise intersecting straight lines we take, by slightly moving it, we can ensure that its straight lines turn out to be continuations of the edges of a certain parallelepid. A parallelepiped is determined (up to movements) by the lengths of its edges and the angles between them. By continuous deformation, we can first make all the angles right (we get a rectangular parallelepid), and then make all the edges the same length, for example, unit length (we get a cube; Fig. 11). This deformation is accompanied by isotopies of the triple of straight lines, which are continuations of the edges of the parallelepid. Thus, we managed to obtain the lines of our triple on the pairwise crossing edges of the unit cube. This is a remarkable achievement. Try it!

Doesn't work? Don't get upset, and it shouldn't work out. Like pairs of oriented lines, triplets of (unoriented!) lines have a characteristic called the coupling coefficient, which can take values ​​of ±1 and which is preserved under isoptics and changes when the triple of lines is reflected in a mirror. Here's its definition. In an arbitrary set of three pairwise crossing lines, we orient all the lines at random. Pairs of lines containing in our triple use linkage coefficients (equal to ±1). Multiplying them, we get a certain number (the same +1 or -1), which is called the linkage coefficient of the original triple of lines. It does not depend on the orientation of the lines: any line is reoriented, we place a sign on two of the three components, which will not change the product. The preservation of the coefficient of linkage of a triple of lines under isopia and its change when the triple is reflected in a mirror follow from the corresponding properties of the coefficient of linkage of a pair of oriented lines. Since the triples of lines on figure 12 are mirror images of each other, their linking coefficients are different, and these triples are indeed not isoptic.

Since every triple of pairwise skew lines is isopic to one of the two triples shown in figure 12, two triples are isopic if and only if they have the same linking coefficients.

So, already triplets of pairwise crossing lines can be arranged differently. This justifies the title of the article, as well as the fact that in what follows we will simply call sets of pairwise intersecting straight lines weave.
