We will consider several orthogonal projections of the tegradra and take advantage of the fact that the projection of a triangle along a side or along the median and the projection of a parallelogram along a side or diagonal are segments, the lengths of which are expressed in terms of the area of ​​the corresponding figure and the length of the side, median or diagonal (Fig. 1).

Let ABCD (Fig. 2) be given. Each of the secant planes passes through the midpoints of 4 of its edges and, therefore, intersects it along a parallelogram bounded by the midlines of the faces. The areas of sections parallel to the edges DA, DB, DC will be denoted by $s_a$, $s_b$, $s_c$, and the areas of faces ABC, BCD, CDA, DAB by $S_D$, $S_A$, $S_B$, $S_C$. Let us project the tegradr along the edge CD (Fig. 3). The result is a triangle ABC (we keep the same designations for the point projects as for the points themselves). Let us draw in it the middle line KL, parallel to AB (this is the project of the section $s_c$), and the median CM (this is the project of the triangular section CDM of our tegradr; M is the middle of the edge AB). In parallelogram CLMK (Fig. 3) the sides and diagonals are equal

$$CL = KM = CB / 2 = S_A / l,$$
$$CK = LM = CA / 2 = S_B / l,$$
$$KL = 2s_c / l, \quad CM = 2S_1 / l,$$

where $l = CD$, $S_1$ is the area of ​​$\triangle CDM$. Writing the “parallelogram equality” for CLMK (the sum of the squares of the diagonals is equal to the sum of the squares of the side), we get

$$2(S_A^2 + S_B^2) = 4s_c^2 + 4S_1^2.$$

The equality is proved in a similar way

$$2(S_C^2 + S_D^2) = 4s_c^2 + 4S_2^2,$$

where $S_2$ is the area of ​​the triangular section ABN passing through the middle N of edge CD. Finally, we orthogonally project the tegradr along its “midline” MN (Fig.4). The result is a parallelogram ACBD (diagonals AB and CD, intersecting, are divided in half). The diagonals of this parallelogram are the projects of triangular sections $S_1$ and $S_2$, i.e. $AB = 2S_2 / m$, $CD = 2S_1 / m$, where $m = MN$, and the middle lines are the projects of sections $s_a$ and $s_b$, and, since the sides are the middle lines, $AD = 2s_a / m$, $AC = 2s_b / m$. Let's use the parallelogram equality again:

$$2(4s_a^2 + 4s_b^2) = 4S_1^2 + 4S_2^2.$$

From (1), (2) and (3) the statement of the problem follows:

$$S_A^2 + S_B^2 + S_C^2 + S_D^2 = 4(s_a^2 + s_b^2 + s_c^2).$$

M1072. Factor the number 989·1001·1007+320 into prime factors.

Answer: 1009·997·991. You can guess this expansion using the following message: if $f(x)=(x-a)(x-b)(x-c)$, then $f(x)+f(-x)=-2(a+b+c)x^2+2f(0)$, i.e. for $a+b+c=0$ the sum $f(x)+f(-x)$ is constant. In our case, we can take $f(x)=(x-10)\times(x+2)(x+8)$, then the given number is $f(999)-2f(0)=-f(-999)=1009\cdot997\cdot991$. It is easy to check that all three factors here are prime.

S. V. Fomin

M1075. Find the most natural number in whose decimal notation each digit (except the outer ones) is strictly less than its two adjacent digits.

Answer: 96433469. Let $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ be consecutive digits of the number $N$ that satisfies the conditions of the problem. Let's consider the differences between neighboring numbers $d_i=a_{i+1}-a_i$, $i=1, \dots, n-1$. Condition $a_i<(a_{i-1}+a_{i+1})/2$ can be rewritten as $a_i-a_{i-1}<a_{i+1}-a_i$, or $d_{i-1}≤d_i-1$. F1084. If you press a nickel against the frozen glass for a short time, a circle will melt on the glass along the edge of the nickel. Why?

When we press a nickel against the glass, it heats up from our hand. It heats up and melts the frost. And the coin touches primarily at the edges, where there is a thickening. There is an air gap between the rest of the coin and the frost. The thermal conductivity of air is much less than the thermal conductivity of metal. Therefore, it first “warms up” and thaws along the ring, along the edge of the coin. After this, the coin “lies” on its entire surface, and melting occurs over the entire area of ​​​​the coin.

F1085. figure 1 depicts the process performed on an ideal gas and transferring it from state B. Find on the graph the sections of the process where the temperature of the gas increases (decreases).

Let us consider two fairly close points of the process $C$ and $D$ on the curve and write down an expression for the change in gas temperature during the transition from point $C$ to points $D$:

$$\Delta T_{CD} = T_C - T_D = \frac{M}{mR}((p + \Delta p)(V + \Delta V) - pV) \approx \frac{M}{mR}(p \cdot \Delta V + V \cdot \Delta p).$$

Let $\Delta T_{CD} \approx 0$ (this means that either points $C$ and $D$ lie on the same isotherm, or during transition $C \rightarrow D$ the gas temperature passes through an extreme value (maximum or minimum)). Then

$$\frac{p}{V} = -\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta V}.$$

(*)

From figure 1 it is clear that $\frac{p}{V} = \text{ctg } \alpha$, and at sufficiently close points $C$ and $D$

$$\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta V} = \text{tg } \beta' \approx \text{tg } \beta'' \approx \text{tg } \beta = -\text{ctg } \gamma.$$

Thus, from (*) it follows that

$$\text{ctg } \alpha = \text{ctg } \gamma, \text{ или } \alpha = \gamma.$$

Let us now assume that at some point on the curve $AB$ $\alpha < \gamma$. Then $\text{ctg } \alpha > \text{ctg } \gamma$, and repeating the above reasoning in reverse order, we obtain that $\Delta T_{CD} > 0$. Consequently, the temperature of the gas increases when passing through this point. And vice versa: if at some point $\alpha > \gamma$, then $\Delta T_{CD} < 0$, and the gas temperature in this case decreases.

Having found points $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$ on the graph, in which $\alpha = \gamma$ (see Fig. 2), we find out that in process sections $AM_1$ and $M_2M_3$, where $\alpha < \gamma$, the gas temperature increases, and in sections $M_1M_2$ and $M_3B$ ($\alpha > \gamma$) - decreases.
