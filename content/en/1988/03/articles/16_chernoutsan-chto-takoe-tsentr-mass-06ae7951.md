What is the center of mass

If you throw a stone at an angle to the horizon, it is known to travel in a parabola. What if you throw a long stick and twist it properly? Of course, different points of the stick move in different ways, describing rather complex trajectories, but the movement of the stick as a whole is somewhat similar to the movement of a stone: rise, top position, descent. Moreover, if we neglect air resistance, then the movement of one specific point of the stick will be no different from the movement of a stone flying freely along a parabola. This point is the center of mass of the stick.

The center of mass exists for any body, moreover, for any system of bodies. It has remarkable properties, some of which this article will introduce you to.

Let's start by determining the position of the center of mass. Let us consider a system of material points with masses $m_1, ..., m_n$. How, knowing their coordinates, find the coordinates of this “most important point”? The answers look like this:

$$x_{\mu m} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2 + ... + m_n x_n}{m_1 + m_2 + ... + m_n},$$

similar for coordinates $y_{\mu m}$ and $z_{\mu m}$. Why the center of mass is determined in this way will become clear later, when we consider its diametric properties. For now, let’s get familiar with expression (1) by discussing several related issues.

a) In the case of two points with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, the expression for $x_{\mu m}$ has a clear meaning: the center of mass lies

6) All materials of the point “participate” in a certain position of the center of mass absolutely equally. This means that if the location of the masses is symmetrical relative to some point, then this point will be the center of mass. For example, the center of mass of a homogeneous ball coincides with its center (the same for a cylinder, cube, etc.).

c) And one more thing. It turns out (try to prove this), the position of the center of mass will not change if we, having isolated some part of the distances of the system, concentrate the entire mass of this part at one point - its center of mass. For example, the center of mass of a wiring triangle coincides with the center of mass of a system of three points located in the middle of the side (the masses of the points are equal to the masses of the corresponding sides).

Let us now move on to the most important thing - the study of the physical properties of the center of mass.

Let the points move to $s_1$, ..., $s_n$ in a short time $\Delta t$. Then, as can be seen from (1), the displacement of the center of mass will be equal to

$$\vec{s}_{\mu M} = \frac{m_1 s_1 + m_2 s_2 + \ldots + m_n s_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \ldots + m_n}$$

(recall that the projections of the displacement vector on the axis are equal to the change in the corresponding coordinates). Dividing the displacement by $\Delta t$, we find the speed of the center of mass:

$$\vec{v}_{\mu M} = \frac{m_1 \vec{v}_1 + m_2 \vec{v}_2 + \ldots + m_n \vec{v}_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \ldots + m_n}$$

it costs nothing more than the full impulse of the system $\vec{P}$. Therefore, expression (2) can be written in the form

$$\vec{P} = (m_1 + m_2 + \ldots + m_n) \vec{v}_{\mu M}.$$

So, the first property: if the entire mass of the system is mentally concentrated at the center of mass, then the momentum of this imaginary point will be equal to the total momentum of the system. What follows from this? We know, for example, that the momentum of a closed system is conserved. This means that if the system is closed, the speed of its center of mass $\vec{v}_{\mu M}$ remains constant.

Let's look at an example. A uniform thin stick of length $l$ stands vertically on a smooth floor (Fig. 2). She is released, after which she falls flat. How to find out how much the lower end of the stick will shrink by the time it falls? A stick in a gravitational field is not a closed system; but, since only vertical forces act on it, the horizontal projection of its momentum does not change, in our case it remains equal to zero. This means that the center of mass will not mix in the horizontal direction, that is, the center of the stick will fall to the place where it stood, and its lower end will shift by $l/2$.

Let's continue our discussion of the properties of the center of mass. Let us consider a system of two point materials with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$. Let this system not be closed, that is, each body is acted upon by both internal and external forces.
