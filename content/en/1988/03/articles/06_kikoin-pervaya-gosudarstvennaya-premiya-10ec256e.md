FIRST STATE PRIZE

Isak Konstantinovich Kikopin was awarded several State Prizes. Some of the works for which he was awarded prizes were not published in print due to secrecy. The actual fact of awarding the prize was not always reported. For a number of years - these were the years of the most focused work - even the name of N.K. Kikopin was not mentioned in the press, his articles did not appear in scientific journals.
However, N.K. Kikopin and his employees S.V. Gubar and V.S. Obukhov received the very first State Prize for work that was published, and the award was announced in the press and on the radio. Professor A.K. Kikopin talks about this work.

This happened later in 1942, at the very height of the Great Patriotic War.

The work was published in the journal "Bulletin of Electrical Industry" under the title "New system of electrical measuring equipment for measuring high-power direct currents." What is this new system? Why was she needed?
And why did the measuring instruments of this system turn out to be so important that the work was then, during the war, awarded the State Prize? After all, measuring instruments are not tanks, not guns, and not mines or bombs!

Why do we need high currents?

In modern technology, alternating current is most often used and, moreover, of relatively low strength - usually a few amperes, cutting - ten amperes, even cutting - hundreds of amperes. There are, however, technical branches where constant current, and very high power, is required. This is primarily the metallurgy of non-ferrous metals. Such an important metal as aluminum is obtained exclusively by electrolysis, and at the same time a direct electric current of many decimal amperars (currently up to 150 kA) passes through a molten mixture of alumina (Al₂O₃) and cryolite (Na₃[AlF₆]). Further, at the time to which the described work relates, a current of up to 70 kA is used in the electrolysis of aluminum.

Just before the war, the Ural Aluminum Plant, the largest in the country, was built and put into operation in the Urals. There were two other factories - one in the Leningrad region, and the second in Ukraine. It was at the Ural plant that electrolytic baths were designed for currents of up to 75 kA. Naturally, to control the aluminum production process it was necessary to be able to measure these currents.

How do large currents change?

Everyone knows how the quantities characterizing electric current change (current strength, voltage, power, energy). In those days, mainly devices of the magnetoelectric system were used; There were also devices that used heating of wiring through which a variable current passes (telephone devices). In all cases, the non-existence of parts of the devices is made of thin-

thin wires through which too large points should not be passed. And if it is necessary to change strong points, then the devices are shunted - a resistance (shunt) is connected parallel to the current-carrying part of the changing device, a certain number of times less than the resistance of the current-carrying part of the device. If, for example, a current of 100 A flows in the circuit, and the device is designed for current changes of no more than 1 A, then the shunt resistance should be 99 times less than the resistance of the coil in the device. Then a current of 1 A will flow through the device, and through the shunt - 99 A. The greater the variable current, the less the shunt resistance should be. This is supplemented by an increase in the cross-sectional area of ​​the shunt conductor.

As long as we are talking about changing the descats and even a few soten amperes, problems with shuntimi do not arise. However, when it is necessary to change points of thousands of amperes, the shunt becomes a weighty and burdensome part of the device. Thus, the shunt to the device for changing the current of 10,000 A had a mass of 35 kg. In practice, as the variable current increases, the shunt mass increases in proportion to the square of the current. This means that if you need to change the current of 50 kA, the mass of the shunt should be 875 kg, and if it is also 100 kA - 3.5 tons!

Let's add to this that changing devices with shuntimi for electrolysis plants were not produced in our country. We bought them in Germany. But even abroad it was impossible to buy the 75 kA kilometer meters necessary for the Ural plant. The situation became especially aggravated with the beginning of the war. Two aluminum plants in the European part of the USSR found themselves in occupied territory. There was only one aluminum plant left in the Urals, which could not be started up without changing the equipment.

This prompted I. Kikon to take up this important problem. Its decision involved not only the Ural, but also other electrolysis plants, for example, the Solina Kama Potash Plant. The author of these lines has correspondence with this plant, from which it is clear how important the rapid development and production of new devices was for him.

What devices were proposed to replace shunt devices?

Ampere through which no current passes

The idea of ​​​​changes in very large direct currents, proposed by I.K. Kikonan, was extremely simple. Around any constant electric current there is a constant magnetic field at a point located at a given distance from the conductor with current, depending only on the strength of the current. This dependence is well known for the magnetic field around a cylindrical conductor. In this case, the field induction is inversely proportional to the distance from the conductor and directly proportional to the current strength.

At electrolysis plants, current-carrying wires are copper bars of straight cross-section, and even folded together. And in this case, the magnetic field induction is determined by the current in the tires, but its dependence on the current strength is somewhat more complicated - it deviates slightly from linear. This, however, does not prevent one from estimating the current strength from the magnetic field induction. It was proposed to evaluate the induction itself in the simplest way - by the deflection of the magnetic needle.

As is known, a magnetic needle (magnetized rod) in a uniform magnetic field is acted upon by a pair of forces, the rotational moment of which differs from the strength of the needle and is ultimately established along the lines of induction of the magnetic field. But you can make sure that the needle is not installed along the induction lines, but is rotated at an angle proportional to the current. To do this, a force must act on the arrow, the moment of which “unfolds” the arrow.

The diagram of the device constructed by I.K. Kikoin and his colleagues is shown at figure.

On axis 1, mounted on agate bearings (to reduce friction), a magnetized rod 2 is fixed. The counteracting moment is created by spiral springs 3. The axis with a rod attached to it supplies an arrow that slides along the dial, on which 100 divisions are marked. The device was portable and could simply be installed on a current-carrying busbar anywhere. The distance from the bus to the magnetized rod is always the same (several centimeters), so the angle of rotation of the arrow depends only on the current strength. Experience has shown that a slight inaccuracy depends on the current strength, observed only at the highest current strengths (starting from the 84th scale division). A particular difficulty was the calibration of the device. After all, it was impossible to compare its readings with a reference device, as is usually done - such devices simply do not exist. Therefore, a calibration method based on the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction was specially developed.

In addition to the portable kiloammeter, a stationary device was developed to measure current in individual electrical baths for measuring current in the main circuit and transmitting its readings to the control panel. To do this, it was necessary to “convert” the angle of rotation into an electrical signal, which was transmitted to the remote control.

Although the error in measuring the current strength was quite large - 3%, for technical measurements it was quite acceptable and satisfying to customers.

In addition to kiloammeters, I.K. Kikoin and his colleagues developed instruments for measuring power (wattmeters), energy meters and amperor (electric charge) meters, also necessary for electrolysis plants - all designed for currents of about 70 kA. It should be noted that not only was the idea of ​​the devices proposed and their design developed, but all these devices were manufactured in the workshops of the Institute of Metal Physics in Sverdlovsk, where I.K. Kikoin worked at that time, and supplied in the required quantities to electrolysis plants in the Urals and neighboring areas. This allows the Ural Aluminum Plant to begin producing products - the most important metal for the automotive industry.

Thus, work that began back in 1939 turned out to be in 1942 just the bones for the life of an important branch of the defense industry. On the day of the 25th anniversary of the Great October Socialist Revolution, it was announced that I. K. Kikoin, S. V. Gubar and V. S. Obukhov were awarded the State Prize. This was the first State Prize awarded to a physicist from the Urals.
