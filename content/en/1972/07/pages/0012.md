The emergence of γ quanta

Soon after the discovery of the radioactive decay of elements, E. Rutherford carried out his famous experiment to determine the nature of the new radiation. The magnetic field had no effect on one of the components of the rays emitted by the radium preparation - these were γ quanta.

Since then, the more useful emission of γ-quanta by nuclei has been the subject of a thorough study of physics.

Nuclei, like atoms, in addition to the ground state with minimal energy, can be in discrete excited energy states. The distance between the energy levels varies depending on the mass of the nucleus and the excitation energy from ten kE to several mee. The lifetime of the nucleus in excited states does not exceed $10^{-13} - 10^{-14}$ s. The transition of a nucleus from an excited state to a ground state is most often accompanied by the emission of γ quanta.

The energy of a γ-quantum is equal to the difference between the energies of the initial and final states. Sometimes the excitation energy of a nucleus can be converted into the kinetic energy of an electron, which as a result leaves the atom. This process, called internal conversion, most often occurs in the electron shell closest to the nucleus.
