But both sides were wrong because neither the quantity $m|v|$, where $|v|$ is the velocity modulus, nor the quantity $\frac{mv^2}{2}$ have the property of conservation during interactions. The quantity $mv$ actually has the conservation property. As for $\frac{mv^2}{2}$, in all cases when kinetic energy is converted into some other type of energy, for example, into potential interaction energy, into heat, into electric current energy, etc., it is not conserved at all. It, in essence, is not preserved in the process, the study of which led to the idea of ​​​​preserving the value $mv^2$ - in the process of control. If, for example, there are two standard balls of the same mass moving towards each other with the same speeds $v$, then the values ​​$mv^2$ before and after the collision are the same. But at the very moment of impact, the value $mv^2$ (more precisely, the kinetic energy $mv^2/2$) is zero! It is not $mv^2$ that is conserved, but what we now call the total energy.

It may seem to countries that a dispute that began in 1686 could continue for so long. Indeed, in the same 1686, Newton presented his famous work “Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy,” which outlined Newtonian mechanics. And from it it seemed that there was, in fact, nothing to argue about. But the fact is that Newtonian mechanics did not immediately gain universal recognition, especially outside England. Therefore, the disputing parties did not use either the concept of mass or the concept of force in the “Newtonian” sense. The word “force” means precisely the “force” of movement among those who argue, and not the reason for the acceleration of bodies. In addition, Newoton, of course, did not know about the existence of the law of conservation of energy (after all, the very concept of work and energy appeared only more than a hundred years later) and, in general, did not attach importance to conservation laws. And for a long time the word “force” was used simultaneously both in the Newotian sense (force is the cause of acceleration) and in the Leibniz sense, although what Leibniz calls “force” is actually energy. To some extent, such modern expressions as electromotive force (energy per unit of electric charge), horsepower (until recently - a unit of power), etc. are a relic of those distant times.

It is curious that when in 1847 the famous German scientist Hermann Helmholtz published a paper in which one of the most important laws of nature was formulated - the law of conservation of energy, the title of this article contained the words: “On the conservation of force”!
