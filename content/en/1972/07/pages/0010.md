In atoms more complex than hydrogen, electrons are grouped into shells, that is, groups of levels with similar energies and having the same principal quantum number $n$. Different shells are designated by the letters $K, L, M$, etc. On the $K$-shell close to the nucleus there are no more than 2 electrons, on the $L$-shell - 8, on the $M$-shell - 8, on the $N$-shell - 18, etc. The outer electrons bonding with the atom are weaker internal; This is explained by an increase in the distance to the nucleus and partial screening of the attraction of the nucleus from the internal electrons.

With increasing nuclear charge, the binding energy of $k$-electrons is quickly calculated (proportional to $Z^2$) and is 1.1 keV in a sodium atom, and 70 keV in a tungsten atom. Therefore, visible light quanta, whose energy ranges from 1.6 to 4.1 keV, are emitted by complex atoms during electron transitions between levels of the outer shell.

There is a widely known experiment in which the flame of a gas burner turns yellow when a pinch of table salt is thrown into it. Two very similar energy quanta emitted by sodium atoms in the yellow part of the spectrum are responsible for this effect (see Fig. 3).

The emission of light in gas tubes used for advertising is also due to transitions between certain levels of gas atoms excited by an electronic discharge. However, more often we have to deal with light sources that have a continuous (continuous) spectrum. Such light sources include the sun, incandescent lamps and fluorescent lamps (liminescent lighting). What is the reason for the transition from individual lines to continuous

spectrum? It turns out that only rarefied gases, in which the interaction between atoms is not VELKO, give linear spectra. If a burst of high temperature or high pressure gas atoms often experience collisions, then the emission lines become so dense that they form a continuous spectrum. (It is curious that although the spectrum of sunlight is continuous, the spectrum of the solar atmosphere, which manages to change during a total solar eclipse, consists of individual bright lines).
