Electromagnetic induction

L. G. Aspamazov

The solution to problems on electromagnetic induction is based on the use of Farad's law: if the magnetic flux, an arbitrary circuit, changes by values ​​$\Delta \Phi$ during the time $\Delta t$, then an electromotive force $E_{\text{инд}} = k \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}$ is induced in the circuit (k is the number of lentic coefficients, depending on the choice of system of units). In this form, we denote by $\Delta \Phi$ the abscultaneous magnitude of the change in magnetic flux, considering $E_{\text{инд}}$ to be always positive.

The direction of the current induced in the circuit is induced according to Lenz's rules: the current is directed so that its magnetic field counteracts the change in the magnetic flux.

The magnetic flux is equal to the product of the magnetic field induction component $B_1$, perpendicular to the plane of the circuit, and the area of ​​the circuit $S$ (Fig. 1): $\Phi = B_1 S = BS \cos \alpha$. Consequently, a change in magnetic flux can occur due to a change in the magnetic field induction, the area of ​​the circuit or its orientation.

When the conductor moves, the area of ​​the circuit increases by values ​​$\Delta S = l v \cdot \Delta t$. Change of flow $\Delta \Phi = B \cdot \Delta S = Bl v \cdot \Delta t$. That's why

It is useful to understand due to what energy heat is released at the resistance.

As you know, a force (Ampere’s law) acts on a conductor carrying current in a magnetic field. In our case (the magnetic field is perpendicular to the conductor) this force is equal to $Bl I$ and is directed opposite to the speed. According to the conditions of disassembly at a constant speed $3\Omega$, this means that a force $F$ is applied to it, balancing the braking force from the magnetic field. The work of force $F$ turns into heat, which is released at the resistance. The power of this force is equal to the power of heat loss:

$$N = F \cdot v = Bl I v = B^2 l^2 v^2 / R = W.$$

From the obtained formulas it follows that as the resistance $R$ increases, the current in the counter will decrease ($I = Bl v/R$). However, the voltage drop across the resistance, equal to the potential difference at the ends of the conductor, remains unchanged ($U = IR = Bl v$).

Problem 9. A conductor of length $l = 1$ m slides along horizontal rails in a vertical magnetic field with induction $B = 10^{-2}$ ml. The ends of the rails are closed to resistances $R_1 = 1$ om and $R_2 = 2$ om (Fig. 7). Determine the current flowing through the conductor if the speed of the conductor is $v = 10$ m/s. Neglect the resistance of the rails and conductor.

The conductor, rails and resistance form two circuits. E.d.c. the inductions induced in these circuits are identical in magnitude and equal to $Blv$ (see problem 5). However, they are directed in different directions. The area of ​​the circuit with resistance $R_1$ increases as the conductor moves. A current arises in the circuit, the magnetic field of which weakens the external field. The area of ​​the circuit with resistance $R_2$ decreases; the magnetic field of the current arising in it must add to the external field.

So through a moving conductor is equal to the sum of currents flowing in individual circuits: $I = I_1 + I_2$; $I_1 = E_{\text{инд}}/R_1 = Blv/R_1$; $I_2 = E_{\text{инд}}/R_2 = Blv/R_2$. Therefore $I = Blv(R_1 + R_2)/R_1 R_2 = 0,15$ a. The direction of current $I$ is shown in figure 7. If you change the direction of movement of the conductor, the directions of all currents will also change.
