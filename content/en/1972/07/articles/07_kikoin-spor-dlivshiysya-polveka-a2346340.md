In 1686, the German philosopher and natural scientist Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) published a short two-page article with a very long title, so long that we will not reproduce it in full here. But the title begins like this: “A brief proof of the remarkable error of Descartes and others regarding...”.

The article marked the beginning of a significant scientific dispute between Leibniz and his parties, on the one hand, and the successors of Descartes (Cartesians, as they are officially called) on the other. Renedecart himself (1596-1650) died 36 years before Leibniz’s article appeared. This dispute lasted more than 50 years.

What mistake did the important philosopher Leibnitz notice in the no less important philosopher Descartes? What was the long debate about?

What all moving bodies have in common, of course, is that they... move. But is it possible to say that some bodies have “more movement” and others have less, that some bodies move “more strongly” than others? By the time the Leibniz-Descartes dispute arose, scientists were already convinced that it was possible and necessary to talk about the “force” of movement, about the “quantity of motion.” It was not only clear which quantity expresses “force” or momentum. It would seem that speed is a sufficient characteristic of movement: the body with the greater speed moves more “strongly,” that is, with more movement! But is it correct to say that a small bullet traveling at, say, 700 m/s is moving “stronger” than a large projectile moving at only 300 m/s? Obviously, not only the speed of the body is important. It is also important whether the moving body is large or small.

The aforementioned dispute was about the quantity expressing the “force” of movement, the amount of movement.

The philosophical views of Leibniz and Descartes were in many ways opposite, but as physicists they agreed that the “force” of motion should be characterized by the effect that a moving body has on another body when it interacts with it (for example, when it moves). They also agreed that when, during the interaction of bodies, motion is transferred from one body to another, it (the motion) is conserved. This means that the amount of motion (or “force” of motion) lost by one of the bodies is gained by the other. This means that the “force” of motion is expressed of such a magnitude that is conserved during the movement of bodies and their interactions.

In 1644, Descartes in his book “Elements of Philosophy” proves that the product of the “magnitude of a body” (that is, its mass) and the absolute value of its speed has the property of conservation. This work expresses the “power” of body movement. Here Descartes made a very important mistake: the property of conservation is actually possessed by the product of the mass of a body by the velocity vector, and not by its absolute value. But Descartes did not consider it necessary to take into account the direction of movement, believing that “the movement of movement is not opposite.”

Leibnitz spoke out against the magnitude of the “force” of motion (“quantity of motion”) proposed by Descartes in his 1686 article, proving that the “force” of motion should be called the product of the “magnitude”

Descartes believed that a body that has a greater value of $mv$ has a greater “force” of motion or a greater amount of motion. And here's why: if we take two positive bodies with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ and subject them to the action of forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ for the same period of time $t$, then the ratio of forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ will be equal

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{m_1 a_1}{m_2 a_2}.$$

Let's multiply and divide the right side of this equation by $t$. Then we get

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{m_1 a_1 t}{m_2 a_2 t}.$$

But $a_1 t = v_1$, and $a_2 t = v_2$. That's why

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{m_1 v_1}{m_2 v_2}.$$

Since the ratio of forces is equal to the ratio of the magnitude $mv$, then $mv$ is the amount of motion (“silla” of motion).

Leibniz reasons as follows: if we take two bodies with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ and subject them to the action of forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ so that under the action of these forces both bodies travel the same distance $S$, then the ratio of the forces will be equal

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{m_1 a_1}{m_2 a_2}.$$

Multiplying and dividing the right side of this equality by $S$, we get

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{m_1 a_1 S}{m_2 a_2 S}.$$

But, as is known, $a_1 S = \frac{v_1^2}{2}$, and

$$a_2 S = \frac{v_2^2}{2}.$$

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} m_1 v_1^2}{\frac{1}{2} m_2 v_2^2} = \frac{m_1 v_1^2}{m_2 v_2^2}.$$

Since the ratio of force is equal to the ratio of magnitude $mv^2$, then $mv^2$ expresses the “force” of movement, or the amount of motion.

For the Cartesians, therefore, the “force” of motion is the magnitude $mv$, and for Leibniz and his side - the magnitude $mv^2$. In 1695, during a dispute, Leibniz proposed the name “living force” for the quantity $mv^2$ (more precisely, for $\frac{mv^2}{2}$), and it has not yet fallen out of use. In the language of modern mechanics, $\frac{mv^2}{2}$ is the kinetic energy of a body. A force applied to a body changes the kinetic energy of the system, and this change is equal to the work of the force, that is, equal to FS. The value $mv$ is the momentum of the body. It also changes under the action of a force applied to the body, and its change is equal to the impulse of the force, that is, equal to $Ft$.

Who was right in this dispute?

Strange as it may seem, Urgent.net should not be victorious in this. Both sides were right and wrong. They are right because both quantities - $mv$ and $mv^2$ are important characteristics of movement. The value of the quantity $mv$ determines the time during which the initially suspended body under the influence of a given force changes speed from zero to $v$. The value $mv^2$ (more precisely, the value $\frac{mv^2}{2}$) determines the distance that the body must travel under the influence of a given force while its speed changes from zero to $v$.
