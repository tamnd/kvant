How much does a gram of light cost?

Just recently, the specialized theory of relativity was the lot of a narrow circle of specialists. Fifteen years ago, only physicists studied it specifically. Gradually it began to penetrate the programs of technical schools. And now the basics of Einstein’s theory are taught in elective classes for high school students. Even more children read popular literature on this issue, which has appeared in abundance over the past few years.

But if you want to truly study the theory of relativity, just reading even very good books is not enough. The criterion for whether you are aware of everything, whether you really understand, can only be a small independent study - solving problems.

We briefly discussed here two books that help you learn how to solve problems in the theory of relativity. One of them sets out the theory of relativity itself, very clearly and server-like, but at the same time accessible to a strong high school student. We are talking about the book “Physics of Space-Time”*, published in the second edition in 1971, by E. Taylor and J. Wheeler. The authors of this book are famous American physicists and outstanding teachers. The second book is “Elementary Problems”

The formulations of problems in the book by E. Taylor and J. Wheeler are sometimes unexpected, but always interesting. The challenges and paradoxes of the theory of relativity are often funny and not at all simple. “When Einstein was a child,” write Taylor and Wheeler, “he puzzled over this riddle: the path runner looks at himself in the mirror, which he holds in front of him in his outstretched hand; If he runs almost at the speed of light, will he be able to see himself in the mirror? Unteachable rules for adding velocities, the dependence of time on the reporting system, the constancy of the speed of light in all inertial (that is, moving uniformly and rectilinearly from each other) reporting systems - isn’t all this amazing? The question that is in the title of this decision also turns out to be not meaningless.

Sokolovsky, this problem is formulated as follows: “How much does one gram of light cost if the electricity tariff is 4 kopecks per kilowatt-hour and the efficiency of an electric light source is 10%?” How much do you think? The number in the answer is startling - it turns out, 10 million rubles. Check it out if you don't believe me *).
