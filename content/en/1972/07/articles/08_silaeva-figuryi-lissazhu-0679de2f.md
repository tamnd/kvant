In «Kvant» No. 1 for 1972, we already talked about experiments with one of the simplest physical devices - a magnet *). In this article we will discuss a few more experiments that we can conduct at home.

Simple simple oscillations of a body are oscillations in which the deviation $x$ of the body from the equilibrium position changes according to the law

$$x = a \sin(\omega t + \varphi),$$

where $a$ is the amplitude, $\omega$ is the frequency, $\varphi$ is the initial phase of oscillations.

Such vibrations are called harmonic. Harmonic vibrations are performed by a mathematical pendulum, a weight on a spring, a direction in an electrical circuit.

In this article we will consider the case when a body participates simultaneously in two harmonic oscillations. If both oscillations occur along one straight line, then the equation of motion of the body will be represented by the sum of the equation of two motions:

$$x = A_1 \sin(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1) + A_2 \sin(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2).$$

It is not difficult to construct a graph of the displacement of a body from its equilibrium position as a function of time. To do this, you need to add the ordinates of the curves corresponding to the first and second movements. figure 1 shows an example of the addition of two harmonic oscillations (black sinusoids). The red line corresponds to the result of the broken swing. It is no longer harmonic.

More complex trajectories are obtained by adding oscillations in two mutually redistributing directions. An example of such an oscillation is the movement of a body, depicted in figure 2. In this case, the type of trajectory depends on the correspondence of the frequencies, amplitudes and phases of mutually redistributive oscillations. These trajectories are called Lissajous figures, named after the French physicist Lissajous, who first described them in 1863. The setup used by Lissajous

The trajectory of a body in the case when it simultaneously participates in two mutually perpendicular oscillations is described by a control system

$$x = A_1 \sin (\omega_1 t + \varphi_1),$$
$$y = A_2 \sin (\omega_2 t + \varphi_2),$$

where $x$ and $y$ are the projections of body displacement on the $X$ and $Y$ axes.

Let us assume for simplicity that $\varphi_1 = \varphi_2 = 0$ and $\omega_1 = \omega_2 = \omega$. Then

$$x = A_1 \sin \omega t,$$
$$y = A_2 \sin \omega t.$$

This means that $y = \frac{A_2}{A_1} x$; therefore, correspondence (2) describes a straight line segment. The angle of inclination $\alpha$ to axis $X$ is given by the equation

$$tg \alpha = \frac{A_2}{A_1}.$$

Let now $\varphi_1 = \varphi_1' + \frac{3\pi}{2}$. Then

$$x = A_1 \cos (\omega_1 t + \varphi_1'),$$
$$y = A_2 \sin (\omega_2 t + \varphi_2).$$

Let us first analyze the simplest case, when $A_1 = A_2$, $\varphi_1' = \varphi_2 = 0$ and $\omega_1 = \omega_2 = \omega$, that is

$$x = A \cos \omega t,$$
$$y = A \sin \omega t.$$

A point with coordinates $x$ and $y$ defined by this equation describes a circle of radius $A$. Indeed, $x^2 + y^2 = A^2 \cos^2 = \omega t + A^2 \sin^2 \omega t = A^2$. And this means that the trajectory of movement is a circle.

Let now $A_1 \neq A_2$. Let us construct the motion trajectory for the case $A_1 = 1$, $A_2 = 2$. At the moment of maximum deviation $x = A_1 = 1$, that is $\cos \omega t = 1$, $\omega t = 0$ and therefore $y = 2 \sin \omega t = 0$. Similarly, at $x = 0$ $y = 2$, at $x = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ $y = \sqrt{2}$ and so on:

By constructing a graph using these coordinates, we obtain an ellipse, the greater of which is equal to $A_2$,

Let now $\omega_1 = 2\omega$, $\omega_2 = \omega$, $\varphi'_1 = 0$ and $\varphi'_2 = 0$. then the system of equation (3) takes the form

$$x = A_1 \cos 2\omega t,$$
$$y = A_2 \sin \omega t.$$

Let us transform the equation for $x$ as follows:

$$x = A_1 \left( \cos^2 \omega t - \sin^2 \omega t \right) =$$
$$= A_1 \left( 1 - 2 \sin^2 \omega t \right) = A_1 \left( 1 - 2 \frac{y^2}{A_2^2} \right).$$

This curve is part of a parabola with its axis along the $X$ axis and its vertex at $x = A_1$ (Fig. 5). Thus, we have an open curve.

Let us now consider the influence of frequencies on the shape of the trajectory, and take the amplitudes of the transverse and longitudinal oscillations described by the system of equation (3) to be the same.

In what case do we get unclosed figures? Can general patterns be found? Let us consider the equations in the form
$$x = A_1 \cos p\omega t,$$
$$y = A_2 \sin q\omega t.$$

First of all, we note that at the point where the curve turns back along the same trajectory, the velocities of the body along the $X$ and $Y$ axes simultaneously become zero. After all, it is in this case that the body, marveling along the curve, stops, and then begins to marvel back. If $x = A_1 \cos p\omega t$, then
$$v_x = \frac{A_1 \cos p\omega t_2 - A_1 \cos p\omega t_1}{t_2 - t_1} =$$
$$-2A_1 \sin \frac{p\omega t_2 + p\omega t_1}{2} \sin \frac{p\omega t_2 - p\omega t_1}{2} = \frac{t_2 - t_1}{t_2 - t_1}.$$

When $t_2 \approx t_1 = t$ (the difference $t_2 - t_1$ is small), $\sin \frac{p\omega t_2 - p\omega t_1}{2} \approx \frac{p\omega t_2 - p\omega t_1}{2}$.

As a result
$$v_x = -A_1 p\omega \sin p\omega t.$$
Similarly for $v_y$ we get
$$v_y = A_2 q\omega \cos q\omega t.$$

Let's see when the velocities $v_x$ and $v_y$ go to zero.
$$v_x = 0, \text{ если } p\omega t = k\pi,$$
$$v_y = 0, \text{ если } q\omega t = \frac{\pi}{2} + m\pi.$$

From these conditions it is clear that the Lissajous figure is not closed in

Lissajous patterns can be observed on the oscilloscope screen. On a vertical scan, one harmonic oscillation is applied, on a horizontal scan, another. Their sum can take various forms. To do this, it is enough to change the frequency of the alternating voltage on the plates of the oscilloscope.

Each of you can make your own very simple device for observing and photographing Lissajous figures. Take an ordinary metal ruler and bend it so that the plane of one half of the ruler is different from the plane of its second half (see Fig. 9).

Place one end of the ruler in a vice. If you now swing the free end of the ruler, it will describe intricate figures in the air. These will be Lissajous figures.

The movement of the free end of the ruler consists of independent vibrations of the two parts of the ruler. However - from the vice to the bend and the second - from the bend to the end. The oscillations of each part of the variable plane of the ruler on this segment. Since the angle of inflection of the ruler is $\frac{\pi}{2}$, the oscillations are mutually alternating. The type of trajectory of the end of the ruler depends on the length and width of the ruler and on where to bend it.

You can use the same ruler to get different shapes. To change the correspondence between vertical and horizontal vibrations, it is enough to clamp the ruler in a vice in different places.

Since the frequency of oscillations depends on the length of the ruler, then by changing the correspondence between the lengths of the parts of the ruler, you will change the correspondence between the frequencies of mutually alternating oscillations of the end of the ruler. In this case, you will get different trajectories of the end of the ruler.

To form receiving figures, you need to attach a small flashlight light bulb to the free end. The light bulb is connected to the battery with wires stretched along the ruler (Fig. 9). By placing our complex magnet in a dark room, we can create oscillations in the ruler. The exposure time should be long enough. You can determine it by testing several experiments with different exposures.
