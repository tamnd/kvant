The angle p is called the parallel of the Sun. Obviously, p is the angle at which the equatorial radius of the Earth is visible from the center of the Sun. Its value for the average distance to the Sun is 8.8″. Considering R = 6400 km, we get

$$CO = 6400 \cdot \cos 8,8'' \approx 150\ 000\ 000\ \text{km}.$$

This is the approximation of the average distance to the Sun. This is precisely the average value, since the earth's orbit is an ellipse and the Sun is not at its center, but at the focus; therefore, the distance to the Sun during the year changes by approximately 1.7% = 2,550,000 km in either direction.

We present these explanations only to show the principle of determining the average distance between the centers of the Earth and the Sun. This distance is called the astronomical unit of length or a.e. for short. The astronomical unit is the basic measure for determining all distances in the solar and stellar systems.

The difficulty in making the measurements described in practice is determining the direction of AC and BC. Indeed, in relation to what can these directions be found? If stars were visible in the sky around the Sun during the day, they would have that distant background in relation to which these directions can be determined. But this is not the case, and besides, the center of the Sun is not marked with anything, so it is impossible to sight it indefinitely. That is why it is more convenient to determine the distance to a planet that is visible at night against the background of stars. (There are planets that are much closer to Earth than the Sun, which makes measurements easier.)

Initially, Mars was chosen for this purpose, observing which in 1672 for the first time for a more or less correct value of the parallel of Mars; and knowing it, as we have already mentioned, it was possible to deduce the parallel of the Sun.
