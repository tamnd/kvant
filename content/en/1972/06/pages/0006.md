In the first and last quarters, the disk of the Moon is visible exactly half illuminated, and the line separating the light side from the dark side is represented by the diameter of the lunar disk (Fig. 1). The angle between the directions Moon - Sun and Moon - Earth (angle ABC) is right, as can be seen from figure 1. If you change at this time the angle between the directions Earth - Moon and Earth - Sun (angle BAC), then you can find the ratio of the distances Earth - Sun and Earth - Moon (that is, the ratio $\frac{AC}{AB}$). During the time of Aristarchus of Samos, the value $87^\circ$ was obtained for the angle BAC. Therefore, $\frac{AC}{AB} = \sec 87^\circ \approx 19$. So the Sun was 19 times farther than the Moon. We now know that this result is greatly erroneous and that the Sun is almost 400 times farther than the Moon, since the angle BAC differs from the right angle by only 9', and sec $89^\circ 51' = 382$. Measurements cannot be made with sufficient accuracy, especially since due to the unevenness of the surface of the Moon it is impossible to detect the moment when exactly half of its disk is illuminated.

Only with the development of goniometric instruments and the use of optical tubes could it be possible to try to measure the distances to the nearest planets, and then, using Kepler’s third law, to calculate the distance to the Sun.

For this purpose, a simple geometric calculation method is used, which is widely used by surveyors and topographers when determining the distance to an inaccessible or distant object. Let's imagine that we are at point A on the river bank and we need to find the distance to point C, located on the other side of the river (Fig. 2). We select point B on “our” shore, the distance to which AB is easy to change, and in triangle ACB we change the angles CAB and CBA. Now, using the known side AB of the triangle, called the basis, and two attached angles, you can calculate the distance AC. This is all simple geometry, but to increase the determination of the distance to point C, it is necessary that the notch angle ACB is not too acute, that is, that the basis AB is not very small compared to the side AC and BC.

When applied to the Sun, this method is extremely difficult, since the largest basis that can be chosen on Earth - the diameter of the globe - is almost hopelessly small compared to the distance to the Sun. The notch angle in this case is only $17,6''$.

To understand the purpose of determining the distance to the Sun, imagine two observers on Earth (Fig. 3). However, at point A, from which the Sun is visible at the zenith (in the same direction as from the center of the Earth O), and another at the equator at point B; for him the Sun is on the horizon.
