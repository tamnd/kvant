⟦folio 68⟧

Those readers «Kvant» who have younger brothers or sisters will easily be convinced by looking at their textbooks that such pseudo-definitions have long ceased to be stuffed into first-graders. True, M.E. Podtyagin provides his “definition” with the following: “There are no rules for adding single-digit numbers,” but, unlike the tabological “definition,” it is simply wrong! (We all remember the “addition table” - although we usually don’t use this term - even before the multiplication table - and it is this that allows us to add any multi-digit numbers.) And how do you like the “rule”:

“Subtraction of natural numbers is possible only under the condition $c \geq b$”
(t. I, p. 13), not accompanied by any erroneous meanings of the designations $c$ and $b$. (True, his errors will not save him: anyway, the non-structured inequality would have to be replaced with a strict one, since the natural series in the book begins not from zero, but from one...) The following “rule” is also mysterious:

“To divide a part by a number, it is sufficient to divide by the product of the divisor and the quotient”
(t. I, p. 21) - it’s easy to remember, but we wouldn’t recommend answering this way on the exam to anyone... The author defines the combinatory property of adding numbers (vol. I, p. 12) based on the concept of the sum of several numbers, completely forgetting that this concept itself is introduced on the basis of the combinatory law for addition! Likewise, the chapter on equations (Vol. I, p. 137) begins with the definition:

“Two numbers or two expressions are considered equal if the difference between them is zero.”
— thus, the author defines the concept of “right” through the concept of “right”. On page 22, volume I, the author declares the tanistic “property of moving a row of divisions”, without even trying (in our opinion, however, quite reasonable!) to dictate what it (“property”) could consist of... And here’s another small rebus, again from the beginning of the first volume (p. 43): “$\frac{b}{2 \cdot 3}$ is divisible by 6 and by 2, but not by 12” - even if we ignore that The funny circumstance that in accordance with this paragraph the letter $b$ never appears again, the meaning of this amazing statement could only be understood by the participants of KBH!

On page 99 of the same first volume we can read about the ancient Greek panirus of Ahmes - how can we not remember the “ancient Greeks!” In general, the author is keenly interested in the history of mathematics: on page 3, volume II, he bothers to cite the “exact” (in any case, it is in quotation marks) account from the hitherto undiscovered historian of science “History of Geometry” by Eudemus of Rhodes!

And again “definitions”, “definitions”:

"$i = \sqrt{-1}$ is called the minimum unit"
(t. I, p. 458) - the drive is a deep “definition”, the author names 5 sources from which it was borrowed, including the Great Soviet Encyclopedia; “a decemmetric locus can be called an infinite set of points that have the same decemmetric property” (vol. II, p. 28 - the only information that can be extracted from this “definition” is that the locus of points cannot be a finite set of points), or a completely classic example of a pseudo-anch spell (vol. II, p. 111), where the area of a polygon is “defined” as an “all-wall number” that defines (?) the size of the part of the plane enclosed by this polygon - one might think that the word “size” is more understandable than “area”.

“Axims” like “A bounded straight line can be continued interruptively (?) along a straight line” (vol. II, p. 7) are more anecdotal, as are the author’s reflections on the existence of an axiomatic method, which boils down to a trivial method like “As we will see (?) below, ... not all axims are self-evident and can be verified experimentally” (vol. II, p. 6) - one is tempted to ask the author “to check on experience" at least one, the most "self-evident" axiom!

All the noted defects would be intolerable in a general textbook, but the author, as we understand, claims to create a manual of the “highest” type. The trusted reader finds himself in a funny position when he decides to prepare for university with the help of M.E. Podtyagin! So if you are unlucky enough to buy these two amazing books, then don't be stubborn and waste your money: throw these books away immediately!

Isn't this advice a bit hasty? No way! After all, the “guide” is literally books and contains the most basic mistakes. A few examples. On page 280, volume I, the case $y_1y_2 = 0$ is omitted, which is why the conclusion about the number of different roots of the bicaquadratic equation turns out to be incorrect. On page 60, volume I, the condition of irreducibility is not stipulated by the fact that these segments “are legs of unequal triangles” (a typical case of an incomprehensible difference between the direct and inverse theorem!) On page 223, volume II, the argument φ of the complex number $a + bi$ is determined by the formula $\varphi = \arctg \frac{b}{a}$ (incorrect!).
