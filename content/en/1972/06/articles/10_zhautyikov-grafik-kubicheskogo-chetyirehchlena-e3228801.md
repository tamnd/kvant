CUBIC QUADTER

In “Kvanit” No. 11 for 1971, some formulas were discussed that make it possible to express the roots of a cubic equation through its coefficients. However, in many problems it is useful to be able to imagine the graph of a “cubic equation”. This is what is discussed in the article published below.

Let's set our task to learn how to graph a function
$$y = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d \ (a \neq 0),$$ (1)
that is, a polynomial of degree 3. Of course, you can try to build a curve (1) from points. But this is too long and requires tedious calculations. We will talk about a simpler method that allows you to easily imagine in general terms the behavior of the curve described by equation (1) in each specific case.

We will distort the functions from the graph
$$y = x^3,$$ (2)
called a cubic parabola. This curve is constructed by points (Fig. 1); its appearance must be remembered. When studying the graph of a cubic polynomial (1), it plays a role similar to the role of the parabola $y = x^2$ in the theory of a quadratic trinomial.

A cubic parabola has the following basic properties:
1) it is symmetrical about the origin and passes through the third and first quarters (in other words, function (2) is odd);
2) at the origin, the cubic parabola intersects the x-axis and simultaneously touches it.
It is easy to tell further what the graph of a function looks like
$$y = ax^3,$$ (3)

Finally, consider the graph of the function
$$y = ax^3 + mx.$$

It is easy to construct by adding two graphs - the curve (3) already familiar to us and the straight line $y = mx$ passing through the origin of coordinates: for each value of the abscissa $x$ you need to add the corresponding ordinates of the curve (3) and straight line $y = mx$.

It is clear that the form of the graph of function (4) depends on the symbols of the numbers $a$ and $m$. Namely, if $a > 0$ and $m < 0$, then curve (4) has the form shown in figure 3 $a$, and if $a > 0$ and $m > 0$, then the form shown in figure 3 $b$. The graph of function (4) in the case of $a < 0$ and $m > 0$ (Fig. 3 $\theta$) is obtained by a mirror image relative to the ordinate axis of the curve shown in figure 3 $a$, and in the case of $a < 0$ and $m < 0$ (Fig. 3 $\epsilon$) - mirror image of the curve on figure 3 $b$; however, at figure 3 $\epsilon$ we notified $a$, and the parabola became steeper, but retained the general appearance.

The main properties of curve (4) are as follows:

1) it intersects (at the origin) the line $y = mx$, simultaneously touching it;
2) if the numbers $a$ and $m$ are of the same sign, then this curve intersects the abscissa axis only at the origin; if the numbers $a$ and $m$ are of different signs, then this curve crosses the x-axis three times (at points $x = 0$ and $x = \pm \sqrt{-m/a}$).

So, we learned how to draw the graph of function (4). Now we will show that for any function of type (1) it is always possible to find such (real) numbers $m, k, h$ that the graph of function (1) is obtained by moving curve (4) by $h$ to the left and by $k$ down.

In fact, if we move curve (4) by $h$ to the left and by $k$ down, we get a curve described by the equation
$$y + k = a(x + h)^3 + m(x + h),$$

if $h > 0$, or $|h|$ to the right, if $h < 0$, and $k$ whizz, if $k > 0$, or $|k|$ up, if $k < 0$, where the numbers $h$ and $k$ are determined by formulas (5).

Example. Construct a graph of the function $y = x^3 - 4x^2 - 4x + 16$.

Using formulas (5) we find:

$$m = -9\frac{1}{3}, \quad h = -1\frac{1}{3},$$

$$k = -5\frac{25}{27}.$$

Therefore, we need to plot the function

$$y = x^3 - \frac{28}{3}x$$
