FROM METER TO PARSEK

A. A. Mikhailov

Everyone knows what a meter is - a meter of length, introduced when the metric system of measures was established by the Paris Academy of Sciences in 1791 during the French Revolution.

The diversity of meter that had previously reigned in different countries presented a great inconvenience, especially when dealing with travel and international trade. In this regard, French scientists proposed a basic measure of length borrowed not from the size of the human body, but from inanimate nature, linking it with the size of the Earth as a planet, in the expectation that the size of the Earth is something unchangeable. After all, this is precisely what is required of any measure. Therefore, it was decided to take as the main measure of length one ten-millionth part of the distance from the pole to the equator, or more precisely, four parik meridians. This value was chosen due to the fact that part of this meridian was changed at the beginning of the 18th century. This change, made by French scientists, should identify - the length of one degree of the meridian turned out to be not the same in different parts of the Earth, but a trace of increasing from north to south. In this case, the Earth could not be an exact sphere, as equals thought, but should have been slightly elongated in the direction of the poles, that is, along the axis of rotation.

However, Newton's theoretical messages demanded the opposite - the continuity of the Earth at the poles and distance to the equator. This must be a consequence of the Earth's rotation around its axis: points on the equator that are further from the rotation axis and have a greater linear speed tend to move further away from the rotation axis than points that are close to the poles. Newton showed that the Earth should always take the shape of an ellipson formed by the rotation of the ellipse around a minor axis. The major axis of the ellipse is the diameter of the Earth's equator.

That’s when a dispute arose between scientists who were based on the results of the French measurement of the meridian, and theorists who shared Newton’s opinion.

To resolve the controversial issue, the French Academy of Sciences launched two expeditions in the thirties of the 18th century to measure the length of the meridian degree - one to the equator in Peru and the other under the Arctic Circle in Lapland (on the border of what is now Finland with Sweden).

The results of measurements carried out by these expeditions showed beyond doubt that the degree length of the meridian at the equator is noticeably shorter than at the Arctic Circle. Thus, Newton was right: the Earth has the shape of an ellipsoid.

According to later refined measurements, the arc length of the 1° meridian is as follows:

at the pole - 111,696 m,
at the equator - 110,576 m.

As for the size of the Earth, then, according to one of the newest definitions of the Soviet geodesy F.N. Krasovsky,

equator radius $a = 6378245$ m,
polar radius $b = 6356863$ m,

compression $\frac{a-b}{a} = \frac{1}{298,3}$.

Only with the development of goniometric instruments and the use of optical tubes could it be possible to try to measure the distances to the nearest planets, and then, using Kepler’s third law, to calculate the distance to the Sun.

For this purpose, a simple geometric calculation method is used, which is widely used by surveyors and topographers when determining the distance to an inaccessible or distant object. Let's imagine that we are at point A on the river bank and we need to find the distance to point C, located on the other side of the river (Fig. 2). We select point B on “our” shore, the distance to which AB is easy to change, and in triangle ACB we change the angles CAB and CBA. Now, using the known side AB of the triangle, called the basis, and two attached angles, you can calculate the distance AC. This is all simple geometry, but to increase the determination of the distance to point C, it is necessary that the notch angle ACB is not too acute, that is, that the basis AB is not very small compared to the side AC and BC.

When applied to the Sun, this method is extremely difficult, since the largest basis that can be chosen on Earth - the diameter of the globe - is almost hopelessly small compared to the distance to the Sun. The notch angle in this case is only $17,6''$.

To understand the purpose of determining the distance to the Sun, imagine two observers on Earth (Fig. 3). However, at point A, from which the Sun is visible at the zenith (in the same direction as from the center of the Earth O), and another at the equator at point B; for him the Sun is on the horizon. Let us assume that each observer somehow determined the direction to the Sun - corresponding to AC

We present these explanations only to show the principle of determining the average distance between the centers of the Earth and the Sun. This distance is called the astronomical unit of length or a.e. for short. The astronomical unit is the basic measure for determining all distances in the solar and stellar systems.

The difficulty in making the measurements described in practice is determining the direction of AC and BC. Indeed, in relation to what can these directions be found? If stars were visible in the sky around the Sun during the day, they would have that distant background in relation to which these directions can be determined. But this is not the case, and besides, the center of the Sun is not marked with anything, so it is impossible to sight it indefinitely. That is why it is more convenient to determine the distance to a planet that is visible at night against the background of stars. (There are planets that are much closer to Earth than the Sun, which makes measurements easier.)

Initially, Mars was chosen for this purpose, observing which in 1672 for the first time for a more or less correct value of the parallel of Mars; and knowing it, as we have already mentioned, it was possible to deduce the parallel of the Sun.
