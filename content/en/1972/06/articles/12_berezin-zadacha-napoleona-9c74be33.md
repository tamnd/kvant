NAPOLEON'S PROBLEM

The French Emperor Napoleon had a hobby (for more information about this, see, for example, “Entertaining Geometry” by Ya. I. Perelman) - composing geometric problems. Some of Napoleon's problems are simple and can be solved elegantly.

On the first page of the cover (above) figure is given to the conditions of a geometric problem, which, according to a note in the French magazine “Mathesis” (1938), was compiled by Napoleon.

Each side of an arbitrary triangle (it is colored green) is divided by points into three equal segments. On the middle segments, equilateral triangles are constructed externally (they are colored red). Then the vertices of the triangles that do not lie on the sides of the green triangle form a triangle whose sides are colored blue. We need to prove that the last triangle is equilateral.

To prove this, we will carry out an additional construction: we will connect the adjacent vertices of the triangles with segments. We will get triangles surrounded by orange. Each of the angles offset by figure 1 by arcs is equal to 120°. In addition, we replace that the triangles, which are made up of two orange and one red part, are equilateral, and that the sum of the “external” angles at the vertices of the three triangles, the bases of which are the blue segments, is equal to 360°. Then we cut out two triangles from the drawing plane and hinge around points E and C, as shown in figure 2.

The result is two triangles with common side EC, shown in figure 3. These triangles are equal in sides. The sums of pairs of angles highlighted by arcs at vertices E and C are 120° each. Consequently, each of the angles belonging to the common base of these triangles accounts for 60°. A triangle with blue sides is indeed equilateral.
