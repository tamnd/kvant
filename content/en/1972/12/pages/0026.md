6. Movement of a body in a viscous fluid

Let's consider the movement of a boat in water taking into account the force of viscous friction. Let the boat with the engine running move uniformly in the water (this means that the force of the thruster is balanced with the force of viscous friction). At time $t = 0$ the engine will turn off; you need to find the law of change in the speed of the boat.

It is obvious that after turning off the engine, only the braking force of viscous friction acts on the boat, which is proportional to the speed of the boat and is directed in the direction opposite to the movement: $F_{tp} = -kv$. Substituting the expression for the force into Newton's second law $F = ma$, we obtain $ma = -kv$ or

$$\Delta v \approx -\frac{k}{m} v \cdot \Delta t.$$

Therefore, the speed of the boat changes according to the law

$$v = v_0 e^{-\frac{k}{m} t}.$$

7. Weakening of the intensity of change when passing through an absorbing medium

A parallel beam of rays (or particles), passing through a layer of matter, decreases its intensity (Fig. 8). If the layer thickness is sufficiently small, then the beam intensity is proportional to the layer thickness: $\Delta I \approx k_1 \cdot \Delta l$. On the other hand, the number of absorbed quanta (or scattered particles) is proportional to the beam intensity: $\Delta I = -k_2 I$.

Coefficients $k_1$ and $k_2$ depend on the properties of the absorbing medium. Combining both formulas, we get

$$\Delta I \approx -k I \cdot \Delta l.$$

Hence, the intensity of a parallel beam when passing through an absorbing medium kills according to the law

$$I = I_0 e^{-kl}$$

(Lambert-Booger-Beer law).

8. Work of an ideal gas during the manufacturing process

When depicting the process, the work done by an ideal gas can be calculated as the product of pressure and volume change: $A = P(V_2 - V_1)$. In the case of a manufacturing process, this mold cannot be crawled, or the pressure does not remain constant. But with a small change in volume, we can neglect the change in pressure and calculate the work as

$$\Delta A \approx P(V_2 - V_1) = P \cdot \Delta V.$$

Expressing pressure using the Mendeleev-Clapeyron formula, we get

$$\Delta A \approx \frac{m}{\mu} RT \frac{\Delta V}{V}.$$

This allows us to find the dependence of $V$ on $A$:

$$\Delta V \approx \frac{\mu}{mRT} V \cdot \Delta A,$$

whence it follows that

$$V = V_0 e^{\frac{\mu}{mRT} A}.$$
