To hold the piston at a height $h > h_0$, a force $F = p_0 S$ must be applied to it. The dependence of the force $F$ applied to the piston on the height of its lifting $h$ is shown graphically in figure 5.

The height of the water column in the pipe $h_0 = p_0 / \rho g$ can obviously serve to change the atmospheric pressure $p_0$. However, mercury is usually used in barometers, and normal atmospheric pressure $p_0 = 1 \, \kappa \Gamma / c m^2$ then corresponds to a significantly lower height of the mercury column $h_{pt} = p_0 / \rho_{pt} g = 0,76 \, m$ (mercury density $\rho_{pt} = 1,36 \cdot 10^4 \, \kappa / m^3$).

An example of another hydrostatic device that is widely used in practice is communication vessels. The law of communication between vessels is known: if the pressure above the liquid in the vessels is the same, then the liquid levels in them are equal. It is not difficult to prove this law for the case of cylindrical vessels (Fig. 6).

Since the liquid in the connecting tube is in equilibrium, the pressure on it on both sides should be equal. Therefore, the liquid levels in the vessels are equal.

In the general case, to prove the law of communication between vessels, you can use the principle of branching, which is often used in hydrostatics. The essence of this principle is as follows: if the liquid is removed, the balance of the remaining part of the liquid will not be disturbed. Thus, in cylindrical communication vessels, we can mentally isolate a part of the liquid that would fill the communication vessels of any tortuous shape (Fig. 6), and imagine that the rest of the liquid is removed. Then the pressure of the part of the liquid we have isolated will not be disturbed and, therefore, the liquid levels in the tortuous vessels will be the same as in cylindrical vessels, that is, the same.

The law of communication of vessels is valid only for a homogeneous liquid. If liquids of different densities are poured into vessels, then the levels in the vessels may be different.
