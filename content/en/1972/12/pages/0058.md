The pressure on the core at the level $h_0$ of contact between water and mercury (Fig. 7) in both elbows should be the same (the law of communicating vessels for a homogeneous liquid). Therefore, $\rho_B g l=\rho_{pT} g(l-\Delta h)$, where the level difference $h_2-h_1$ is denoted by $\Delta h$. From here $\Delta h=(\rho_{pT}-\rho_B) l/\rho_{pT} \approx 0,09$ m.

The pressure in an elbow containing only mercury changes with height $h$ according to the law $p_1=\rho_{pT} g(h_1-h)$. This formula is also valid in the curved part of the tube. (Imagine that the curved elbow communicates with a straight cylindrical vessel, which also contains mercury; then the pressures at the same height in both vessels should be equal.)

In the other bend, in the region $h_0<h<h_2$, where we find only water, the pressure is $p_2=\rho_B g(h_2-h)$. Below level $h_0$ the pressure dependence on height is given by the same formula as in the first bend: $p_1=\rho_B g l+\rho_{pT} g(h_0-h)=\rho_{pT} g(h_1-h)$.

As is known, a buoyant force acts on a body immersed in a liquid. This force is the equilibrium force of fluid pressure on the body. Let us find, for example, the buoyant force acting on a cube with edge $a$, completely immersed in a liquid of density $\rho$. The force of pressure from the liquid on the upper face of the cube is equal to $F_1=\rho g h^2$, where $h$ is the distance from this face to the surface of the liquid (for simplicity, we assume that the plane of the upper face of the cube is parallel to the surface of the liquid). A force $F_2=\rho g(h+a) a^2$ acts on the bottom face of the cube. The pressure force on the side faces of the cube is balanced

each other. The equal force of pressure, that is, the buoyant force, is equal to $F=F_2-F_1=\rho g a^3$ and is directed vertically upward. We received Archimedes' law: the buoyant force is equal to the force of gravity acting on the fluid displaced by the body.

In general, Archimedes' law can be proven using a principle tree. We will subsequently replace the immersed fluid body (Fig. 8). It is obvious that this liquid is in equilibrium. Consequently, the force of gravity $P$ acting on it is balanced by the force of pressure from the surrounding fluid. If we now imagine that the part we have selected has responded, then the balance of the remaining part will not be disturbed, and therefore the pressure on the responding liquid does not change. The equal force of these forces $F$ will still be equal to the force of gravity $P$.

During the proof, we assumed that the body was completely immersed in liquid. However, similar reasoning can be easily verified in the case when only part of the body is in the liquid (do this yourself). And we again find that the buoyant force is equal to the force of gravity acting on the fluid displaced by the body: $F=\rho g V$, where $\rho$ is the density of the liquid, $V$ is the volume of the part of the body immersed in the fluid, $g$ is the acceleration of gravity.
