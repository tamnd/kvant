4. The phenomenon of self-niduction

Consider a circuit (Fig. 5) consisting of a source of e. d.s. $(E)$ and series-connected inductors $(L)$ and ohmic resistances $(R)$. Let the circuit be closed until time $t=0$ and current $I_0$ flow through it. At $t=0$, we short-circuit the circuit with the key $K$ and consider the change with time of the current $I(t)$ in the circuit $L$, $R$. According to Ohm's law, for the circuit under consideration, consisting of inductance and Ohm resistance, we have $IR \approx -L \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$ (here $-L \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$ is the self-induction emf). Hence,

$$\Delta I \approx -\frac{R}{L} I \cdot \Delta t,$$

where

$$I(t) = I_0 e^{-\frac{R}{L} t}.$$

For a circuit consisting of a coil $L$, a resistance $R$, and an e.g. source. d.s. $(E)$ (Fig. 6), Ohm's law will take the form $IR \approx E - L \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$. Let at moment $t=0$ key $K$ be closed

5. Cooling down of the body

According to Newton's law, with a small temperature difference between the body and the environment, the change in the energy of the body over time $\Delta t$ is determined by the formula

$$\Delta Q \approx -k (T - T_c) \cdot \Delta t,$$

where $\Delta Q$ is the amount of emitted energy (tell) during time $\Delta t$, $T$ is the body temperature, $T_c$ is the ambient temperature, the coefficient $k$ depends on the surface and nature of the body. For simplicity we put $T_c=0$, then $\Delta Q \approx -k T \cdot \Delta t$. On the other hand, the change in the amount of heat is equal to $\Delta Q = C \cdot \Delta T$ (here $C$ is the heat capacity of the body, $\Delta T$ is the change in its temperature).
