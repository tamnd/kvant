For many centuries, geometers have tried to prove the fifth postulate as a theorem. But each of them implicitly introduced some “obvious” property:

... The geometric locus of points on a plane that are equidistant from a straight line and lie on one side of it is a straight line, believed Posidonius (Rome, 1st century BC).

... Two approaching straight lines necessarily intersect,” said Omar Khayyam (Central Asia, 11th century).

... For any figure you can build a similar one,” John Valis was convinced (England, 17th century).

Alexis-Claude Clairaut (France, 18th century) built a theory of parallel lines based on the existence of a rectangle; Farkas Boyan (Hungary, late 18th - early 19th centuries) proved the fifth postulate, based on the fact that a circle can be drawn through any three points that do not lie on the same straight line.

But all these extensions turned out to be equivalent to Euclid’s fifth postulate or even a stronger postulate: through a point outside a certain line one can draw in their plane one and only one line that does not intersect the given one. And the last statement is equivalent to

Back in 1823, Lobachevsky prepared for publication the first geometry course he compiled. Trying to break away from Euclid, he constructed in the first half of the book “absolute geometry” - independent of the postulate of parallels. He was convinced that geometry, in which all Euclidean axions are true, except for the parallel axions, is not contradictory. The most important role in this conclusion was played by Lobachevsky’s critical attitude to the conviction that our geometric knowledge is unique: after all, the uniqueness of the geometric system would follow from this conviction.

A student of the great Euler, academician Nikolai Ivanovich Fuss, did not understand the revolutionary system of Lobachevsky, and gave a sharply negative review of the work. The academician was especially outraged by ... the introduction of the meter as a unit of measurement of length and the division of a circle into 400 degrees, not 360.

“This division was invented during the French Revolution,” Fuss writes in the decision, “but radiance was not accepted anywhere due to further inconveniences.”

This review is an erroneous introduction: Lobachevsky’s genius turned out to be inaccessible not only to Fuss, but also to many talented minds of that time, and the meter firmly won the rights of citizenship.
