It is impossible to outline the contents of Lipson's book, just as it is impossible to retell a volume of O'Henry's short stories. Therefore, we will limit ourselves to mentioning only a few noteworthy experiments or episodes reported by Lipson.

The small chapter “Atmosphere” talks about the “weighing of air” by Galileo, the meaning of the “Magdeburg hemispheres” of Guernica, the work of Toricelli, Vivanini, Pascal on changes in atmospheric pressure, and Dalton’s discovery of the law of partial pressures, which gave quantitative confirmation of the atomic-molecular theory. By the way, Dalton subsequently told various people about how he came up with the idea of ​​the structure of molecules. He himself did not know just how it happened. This is often accidental in science, no less often than in positivity. Try to find out from the pose how this or that line, this or that image was born!

Next comes the chapter “Tellota”, in which the reader, along with the names well known to him - Rumfoord, Davy, Faragent, Celsius, Joule - also met the name of Black, whose works determined the research of Rumfoord and Davy, but, unfortunately, were fully published only after the death of the scientist. But he was the first to work on the specific volumetric capacity and, by the way, discovered that the specific volumetric capacity of routine, the question of waiting, is almost 30 times less than that of water.

The author devotes a lot of space to the critical and careful measurements of the specific capacity of a wide variety of substances, carried out by the French scientist Dulong and Petit. This tedious, thankless process of collecting precise empirical data, which Rutherford later called “stamp collecting,” is extremely important in science. In this case, scientists discovered a law that was the first generalization in solid state physics and anecdotal evidence that tellote is a form of kinetic energy. The chapter ends with a description of the botanist Brown's discovery of the movement that bears his name. In 1905, Einstein predicted this phenomenon, unaware that it had already been discovered almost 80 years earlier.

The chapter dealing with gases naturally follows on from the previous two. It is related to the works of Boyle, Mariotte,

Gay-Lussac, Charles, Kelvin, Avogadro, Lavouzier, Boltzmann, well known from the school course, but presented from a non-educational perspective. Here the interaction between the hidden (“So physics, like a caterpillar, pulls itself forward,” the author figuratively replaces) and heralded connection between the results of experimental research and the creation of a generalizing theory is especially clearly manifested. After all, it was the universality of the specific properties of gases that was solved by the hypothesis about the atomic structure of matter, which turned this hypothesis into an indestructible theory.

“I didn’t really want to write about sound in this book,” - this is how the author begins the next chapter, about the act. Why? First of all, for the reason that not a single discovery in this area led to the emergence of any fundamental general physical ideas or to the displacement of existing ideas. And yet Lipson found it necessary to describe several practical experiments, managing to highlight a number of fundamental issues and consider interesting details. For example, Guernike, in order to find out whether sound propagated in water, turned to the help of fish, thereby anticipating the experiments of I. P. Pavlov in studying the conditions of reflexes in animals.

The consideration of sound vibrations helps the author move on to the description of optical processes. The struggle between two hypotheses is clearly shown here - Newton, who argued that light is a flow of particles, corpuscles, and Huygen, who defended the wave theory of light. Paying tribute to Newton, Lipson at the same time notes that excessive defying the authority of this brilliant scientist slowed down the development of experimentation for more than a century. The question of whether the speed of light is finite or infinite is discussed in great detail in this chapter; the experiments of Roemer, Fizeau, and Fku on its measurement are described. Such attention to the wave problem is understandable, but the speed of light is one of the few fundamental worlds of the designer, which also underlies the theory of relativity, as discussed in the next chapter.

This chapter is the only one in the book specifically devoted to the description of equipment, namely, optical instruments, since they, as the author rightly replaces, “allowed not only to obtain information about light, but also to make a contribution to the development of almost all other areas of physics.” And we can add - not only physics, but also biology, medicine, chemistry, astronomy, technology, everyday life. After all, common glasses are nothing more than a simple optical device; a microscope that allows Leeuwenhoek to penetrate the world of microorganisms - an optical device; a hypothetical body-covered device that receives light from distant worlds is also an optical device.
