HYDROSTATICS

L. G. Aspamazov

The liquid exerts pressure on the walls of the vessel in which it is located, or on any other surface in contact with it. Pressure is a scalar quantity. It varies by the absolute value of the force acting on the part of the liquid per unit surface area:

$$p = F/S$$

(Fig. 1). The pressure at different points on the surface may be different. Therefore, we must keep the area S quite small.

According to Pascal's law, fluid pressure does not depend on the orientation of the surface. No matter how the surface is located in a given place of the liquid, the pressure on it will be the same.

The pressure force is always perpendicular to the surface. Under normal conditions, it is directed as if the liquid were trying to expand.

Problem 1. In a vessel shaped like a cube with edge a, fill the burbot to the top with liquid of density p. Determine the pressure forces of the liquid on the bottom and walls of the vessel.

The pressure of the liquid at the bottom of the vessel is equal to the weight of a column of liquid of height a with a base area equal to unity (Fig. 2): $p_1 = \rho g a$, where g is the acceleration of free fall (for simplicity, here and in other problems where this is not specifically stated, it is assumed that there is no atmospheric pressure). Pressure force on the bottom of the vessel

$$F_1 = p_1 S = \rho g a^3.$$

The pressure on the side face of the cube will depend on the distance to the surface of the liquid. At depth h the pressure is $p = \rho g h$. Since pressure changes with depth according to a linear law, to determine the pressure force we must average pressure

$$p_{cp} = \frac{\rho g a + 0}{2} = \frac{\rho g a}{2}$$

multiply by the area of ​​the side face:

$$F_2 = \frac{\rho g a^3}{2}.$$

As is known, a buoyant force acts on a body immersed in a liquid. This force is the equilibrium force of fluid pressure on the body. Let us find, for example, the buoyant force acting on a cube with edge $a$, completely immersed in a liquid of density $\rho$. The force of pressure from the liquid on the upper face of the cube is equal to $F_1=\rho g h^2$, where $h$ is the distance from this face to the surface of the liquid (for simplicity, we assume that the plane of the upper face of the cube is parallel to the surface of the liquid). A force $F_2=\rho g(h+a) a^2$ acts on the bottom face of the cube. The pressure force on the side faces of the cube is balanced

each other. The equal force of pressure, that is, the buoyant force, is equal to $F=F_2-F_1=\rho g a^3$ and is directed vertically upward. We received Archimedes' law: the buoyant force is equal to the force of gravity acting on the fluid displaced by the body.

In general, Archimedes' law can be proven using a principle tree. We will subsequently replace the immersed fluid body (Fig. 8). It is obvious that this liquid is in equilibrium. Consequently, the force of gravity $P$ acting on it is balanced by the force of pressure from the surrounding fluid. If we now imagine that the part we have selected has responded, then the balance of the remaining part will not be disturbed, and therefore the pressure on the responding liquid does not change. The equal force of these forces $F$ will still be equal to the force of gravity $P$.

During the proof, we assumed that the body was completely immersed in liquid. However, similar reasoning can be easily verified in the case when only part of the body is in the liquid (do this yourself). And we again find that the buoyant force is equal to the force of gravity acting on the fluid displaced by the body: $F=\rho g V$, where $\rho$ is the density of the liquid, $V$ is the volume of the part of the body immersed in the fluid, $g$ is the acceleration of gravity.

Problem 7. A small cube of density $\rho_0 = 7,8 \cdot 10^3 \text{ kg/m}^3$ floats on the boundary of mercury with water (Fig. 11). Determine what part of its volume is in mercury. Density of mercury $\rho_1 = 1,36 \cdot 10^4 \text{ kg/m}^3$, density of water $\rho_2 = 10^3 \text{ kg/m}^3$.

From this formula it is clear that the buoyant force, as one would expect, is equal to the sum of the gravitational forces displacing the liquid. Problem 8. A vessel with a liquid of density $\rho$ is accelerated a. Determine the pressure of the liquid on the lubbine h and the force of pressure on the bottom of the vessel. The height of the water level in the vessel is H, the area of ​​the bottom of the vessel is S.

Let us select a column of liquid of height h with base area s (Fig. 12). It is acted upon by the forces of gravity $P = \rho g h$ and the pressure force ps directed upward. Equivalent to these using the acceleration of the column. According to Newton's II law: ma = $\rho g h$ -.ps, where m = $\rho h$ is the mass of the column. For pressure p at depth h, this gives p = $\rho(g - a)h$. The pressure force on the bottom of the vessel F = $\rho(g - a)HS$ will be less, the greater the acceleration of the vessel a. At a = g (free fall), the pressure force of the liquid becomes zero - a compound of weightlessness occurs. When a > g, the liquid will fall freely with acceleration g, and the vessel will fall with greater acceleration, and water will flow out of the vessel.

does the body float up if the vessel is called upward with acceleration? Determine the pressure force of the body on the bottom of the vessel if the acceleration of the vessel is a, the density of the liquid is $\rho_0$, the density of the body is $\rho$, and its volume is V.

The body lying at the bottom of the vessel is acted upon by the forces of gravity $P$, the reaction force $N$ and the buoyant force $F$ (Fig. 13). If the vessel dies, then the sum of these forces equals zero. When a vessel moves with acceleration a upward according to Newton’s II law, we have: $ma = N + F - mg$, where $m$ is the mass of the body.

Let us define the buoyant force $F$. Similar to solving the previous problem, it is easy to obtain that with the accelerated upward movement of a vessel, the pressure at a depth $h$ is given by the formula: $p = \rho(g + a)h$, that is, the pressure is $(g + a)/g$ times greater than in a stationary vessel. The compliance will be greater and the buoyancy force will be:

$$F = m_0 g \frac{g + a}{g} = m_0 (g + a),$$

where $m_0 = \rho_0 V$ is the mass of water displaced by the body. Substituting this expression into formula II of Newton's law, for the reaction force of the day $N$ we obtain: $N = (m - m_0)(g + a)$.

It is easy to see that in a vessel moving with upward acceleration, the reaction force of the day is always greater than in a stationary one. Therefore, the body not only does not float up, but, on the contrary, is pressed harder to the bottom.

Problem 10. A container with liquid moves horizontally with acceleration a. Determine the shape of the surface of the liquid in the vessel.

Let us select a horizontal column of liquid with length $l$ and cross-sectional area $s$ (Fig. 14). According to Newton’s II law $ma = (p_1 - p_2)s$, where $m = \rho ls$ is the mass of the column, $p_1$ and $p_2$ are the pressures on it from the left and right. Pressure at depth $h$ is determined by the general formula: $p = \rho gh$ (there is no vertical acceleration). Substituting the expressions for $m$ and $p$ into equation II of Newton's law, we obtain $al = (h_1 - h_2)g$, or $\frac{h_1 - h_2}{l} = \frac{a}{g}$. But $\Delta h = h_1 - h_2$ is the difference in height of a point on the surface of the liquid. We find that the surface of the liquid is a plane inclined to the horizon at an angle of $\alpha$, moreover $tg\alpha = a/g$.

Note that the fluid pressure at a given height is not the same here. A line of equal pressure is parallel to the surface of the liquid. If you enter the distance $h'$ from a point to the surface of the liquid, then the pressure at this point $p = \rho gh = \rho gh'/\cos \alpha = \rho h' \sqrt{g^2 + a^2}$. Therefore, we can say that the accelerated motion of the vessel is equivalent to replacing the acceleration of free pressure $g$ with values ​​$g' = g - a$ (Fig. 14). This statement applies equally to the previous two tasks.
