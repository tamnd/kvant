50 FUN PROBLEMS

On New Year's Eve, 1972, the Nauka publishing house presented all mathematics lovers with a wonderful gift - a translation of the book by the famous American mathematician F. Mosteller "Fifty Interesting Probabilistic Problems with Solutions" (circulation 200,000 copies, price 26 kopecks). In fact, the book contains 57 problems, 50 of which are solved and 7 of which are discussed.

What is probability theory seems to be of interest to many of our readers. Of course, you can take a textbook*) and read it. However, you cannot study theories without solving problems. On the other hand, one can learn theories by solving problems; In textbooks, entire sections of theory are presented in the form of problems. In the preface, F. Mosteller writes:

“...I owe most of my mathematical education to solving various problems. Over the years, it has become increasingly difficult for me to separate server activities from solving seemingly “toy” problems. Very often, elementary tasks turned out to be extremely difficult when solving server problems.”
The problems in F. Mosteller’s book will mostly resemble “Problem Book «Kvant». And, as in «Kvant», the solutions to each problem are a short note. The most important tasks

*) Nariter, F. Mosteller, R. Rourke, J. Thomas. “Probability”, “Mir”, 1969, or F. Feller. “Introduction to probability theory and its applications”, vol. 1, IL, 1952; vol. 2, “World”, 1967.

different, their solutions cover many sections of the theory, and sometimes go beyond its limits. For example, problem 56

Two urns

Two urns contain the same number of balls, some black and some white each. From these, $n$ ($n \geq 3$) balls are extracted and returned. Find the number $n$ and the contents of both urns if the probability that all white balls are drawn from the first urn is equal to the probability that either all white or black balls are drawn from the second.

If you understand it, you will notice that this is a probabilistic formulation of Fermat's Theorem (see «Kvant» No. 8, 1972); This problem is only discussed in the book. There are not tasks at all, but psychological tests:

Quo Vadis *)

Two strangers, having agreed on how to get to know each other, must meet on a certain day and time in New York, a city that they both do not know. However, they were too lazy to decide on a meeting place. Where should they go if they do try to meet?**)

However, most problems relate to probability theory. Here are some of them.

*) Where are you going? (lat.).
**) To solve the problem you need, of course, familiarity with the most frequently visited places in New York.

Frivolous jury member

In a three-person jury, two members independently make a correct decision with probability $p$, and the third one throws a coin to make a decision (the final decision is made by a majority vote). A jury of one makes a fair decision with probability $p$. Which of these juries is more likely to make a fair decision?

Three-way duel

A, B and C converge for a three-way duel. It is known that for A the probability of hitting the target is 0.3, for C it is 0.5, and B shoots without missing. Duzlyats can shoot in any position of their choice. A shoots first, then B, then C, etc. in cyclic order (the wounded one is eliminated from the duel), until only one person remains intact. What should strategy A be?

As you can see, the tasks are very interesting. If you can't solve them, don't despair. The solutions given in the book are no less interesting than the problems themselves.

A. N. Vilenkin, L. G. Limanov
