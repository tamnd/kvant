With Hooke's Law to the New Hebrides

A. A. Dozorov

Residents of one of the islands of the New Hebrides archipelago quite effectively cancel various celebrations *). The young man climbs onto a tower specially built for this purpose, calls the lyana to his feet, secures the ends of the lyana on the tower, and then rushes down to the ritual dance of the islanders. The Liangs absorb the blow, and the young man lands safely.

The height from which jumps are made ranges from 15 to 30 meters. It would seem that the higher the tower and the longer the platform, the greater the load on the jumper’s legs, so there must be a maximum height from which one can jump. However, at first glance this very reasoning turns out to be completely wrong. Hooke's law will help us find the correct answer.

Let the length of the thread in the free state be $l$. Under the influence of some force, the thread grows to length $l+x$. The value $x$ is called the absolute deformation of the thread, and the values ​​$\frac{x}{l} = \varepsilon$ are called the relative deformation. The deformation is called elastic if, after removing the force, the length of the thread becomes the same. For small elastic deformations ($x \ll l$), the magnitude of the absolute deformation is usually proportional to the force. The direction of the elastic force, which tends to return the thread to its original state, and the direction of deformation are opposite. All this is described by Hooke's law:

$$F = -kx. \tag{1}$$

The proportional coefficient $k$ is called the stiffness coefficient of the tensile body (thread, spring).

The larger the cross-section of the thread, the more force must be applied to cause the same elongation of the thread. In other words, the stiffness coefficient is a function of the cross section.
