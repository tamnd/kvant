9. Tsiolkovsky's task

Let us consider the motion of a spacecraft located at a great distance from the gravitational masses. It is obvious that if the engine does not work, then the ship will either end or move uniformly and in a straight line; we will assume that he is finished, $v_0 = 0$. Due to conservation laws, after turning on the engine, the amount of motion of the “ship-exhaust gases” system remains unchanged, since no external forces act on this system.

Let us denote by $m$, $v$ the mass of the ship with fuel and its speed at the moment of time $t$, $u$ - the speed of the emitted exhaust gas relative to the ship (a constant value). Then $mv = (m - \Delta m) \times (v + \Delta v) - \Delta m (u - v)$, whence $\Delta m = -\frac{\Delta v}{u - \Delta v} m$. Let's find the dependence of $m$ on $v$. For small $\Delta v$, taking into account that $u$ is a constant, $u \neq 0$, we have $u - \Delta v \approx u$, therefore

$$\Delta m \approx -\frac{1}{u} m \cdot \Delta v,$$

and, therefore,

$$m = m_0 e^{-\frac{v}{u}}.$$

Taking Prologarithm to base $e$ and simplifying, we get

$$v = u \ln \frac{m_0}{m}.$$

It follows that the speed of the ship is entirely determined by its initial speed, the relative speed of part of the exhaust gas and the amount of fuel consumed and does not depend on the law of fuel consumption.
