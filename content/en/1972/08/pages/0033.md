Currently, designers and engineers usually manage to calculate and create telesops, the optics of which make it possible to achieve resolution limits according to formula (2), but large telesops in practice cannot provide such resolutions - the turbulence of the atmosphere interferes. The state of the atmosphere is considered good if a resolution of $1''$ can be achieved using a large telescope. And only in certain short-term intervals, when the state of the atmosphere is exceptionally good, star pairs with angular distances between the components up to $0,4''$ are resolved.

If we compare the latest data with the theoretical resolution of a six-meter telescope ($\psi_0 = 0,02''$), it is clearly visible how much the atmosphere, even in good condition, spoils images: without it, when examining celestial bodies with noticeable angular dimensions, it would be possible to distinguish 20-50 times smaller details. If we take into account that the true angular sizes of individual stars are larger than the resolution angle of a 6-meter telescope (for example, the angular diameter of Mira Ceti is 0.056'', Betelgeuse - 0.047'', Antares - 0.040''), then in the absence of an atmosphere one could hope to finally see discs of stars, empty they would only be two to three times larger than the diffraction disks. But still, these would be true disks of stars (albeit with a superimposed diffraction pattern), and not just products of the wave nature of starlight passing through the telescope opening.

In the absence of an atmosphere, it becomes possible to increase the penetrating power of telescopes and, in addition, to carry out observations in ultraviolet and infrared rays with their help. Therefore, actronomers dream of taking telescopes beyond the earth’s atmosphere.

According to modern ideas, the future success of actronomy is largely related to the creation of actronomy observatories on artificial Earth satellites and on the atmosphereless Moon.
