To clarify the magnitude scale in the last century, it was assumed that stars of 6th magnitude compared to stars of 1st magnitude (a difference of 5 magnitudes) provide 100 times less illumination. Thus, the magnitude scale was based on the number $\sqrt[5]{100} = 2,512$. This means that a 1st magnitude star sends us 2.512 times more luminous flux than a 2nd magnitude star, and $2,512^2 = 6,31$ times more light than a 3rd magnitude star, etc.

Consequently, the ratio of illumination $E_1$ and $E_2$ from two stars, one of which has a magnitude of $m_1$ and the other of $m_2$, is determined by the following formula:

$$\frac{E_1}{E_2} = 2,512^{m_2-m_1}.$$

The human eye, with prolonged adaptation on especially dark nights and with good transparency of the atmosphere, is capable of seeing stars of the 8th magnitude. This is the visual noise threshold of the average human eye in physical units. It has been established by everyday life that when a 1st magnitude star is at the zenith on the surface at $1 m^2$, perpendicular to the rays, the luminous flux of $0,85 \cdot 10^{-6}$ lumens ($\lambda m$) falls.

It is not difficult to calculate that an eye with a pupil diameter of 6 mm receives a luminous flux from a star of 8th magnitude

$$F = \frac{3,14 \cdot 0,003^2 \cdot 0,85 \cdot 10^{-6}}{2,512^7} \approx 3,8 \cdot 10^{-14} (\lambda m).$$

Let us now estimate how many quanta entering the pupil in 1 second correspond to this threshold flow. We will assume that the stream consists of rays with a wavelength $\lambda = 5130$ Å ($v = 5,85 \cdot 10^{14} e\mu$), in relation to which the eye feels maximum when observing very faint stars. It has been established by everyday life that a luminous flux of 1 lm generated by such radiation corresponds to an energy flux of 0.00058 VM.

Consequently, a flow of energy enters the pupil of an eye with a diameter of 6 mm from a star of the 8th magnitude

$$W = 3,8 \cdot 10^{-14} \cdot 0,00058 \approx 2,2 \cdot 10^{-17} (\lambda m).$$

One radiation quantum with frequency $v = 5,85 \cdot 10^{14} e\mu$ carries energy

$$\varepsilon = hv = 6,62 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 5,85 \cdot 10^{14} = 3,87 \cdot 10^{-19} (\partial x).$$

Therefore, in one second from a star of 8th magnitude,

$$N = \frac{2,2 \cdot 10^{-17}}{3,87 \cdot 10^{-19}} \approx 60 (\text{кваантов}).$$

Let us immediately note that the results obtained should be viewed only as estimates, since the light from stars is not monochromatic - it does not consist of quanta of one part, and the diameters of the pupils and the sensitivity of the eyes of people are not the same.

The use of binoculars and telescopes significantly expands a person's vision capabilities. With their help, many faint stars become visible. Moreover, it turns out that some of these “faint” stars are actually systems consisting of two, three, or even a large number of stars (such systems are usually called “multiple stars”). When observing with a telescope, the larger the diameter of the object, the greater the luminous flux intercepted by the lens. The size of the object's diameter determines the optical power or penetrating power of the telescope. These terms mean the magnitudes of the extremely faint stars that can be observed with this telescope.
