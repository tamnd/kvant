The path of rays in a kaleidoscope

Directly in front of you you will see the figurine that was placed near the other kaleidoscope. Nearby you will see her again, only mirrored. This is understandable, because there is a mirror next to him - so a mirror reflection appeared. The role of a mirror is played by a strip of glass.

By the way, figure 3 explains what a mirror stripe is. It has two reflective surfaces: the front, polished, and the back, covered with amalgam. To verify this, place a sharpened pencil against the mirror. You will see two or even three reflections. The fact is that the beam falling on the glass is heard. One part of it is reflected from the front surface and gives the first reflected ray, and the second part enters the glass. This part is reflected in the rear surface (almost entirely in the mirror case and slightly in the glass case) and goes to the front surface. Through it, part of the ray comes out and gives a second reflected ray, and the other part of the word is reflected (inside the mirror) and begins a new journey, ending with the appearance of a third reflected ray. These rays weaken as their number increases, so that the third reflected ray is no longer noticeable.

If you put a mirror in a kaleidoscope, a second reflected beam will appear, which will also cause distortion so that nothing can be made out, especially with multiple reflections in mirrors. After all, the first and second rays are mixed relative to each other and give a shift in the image. When reflected
