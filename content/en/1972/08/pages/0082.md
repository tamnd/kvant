To “Problems on keravenities”
(cm. cmp. 16, 25)

1. The problem allows for an interesting geometric solution based on the Pythagorean theorem. At figure a solution for $n = 3$ is given.

Fig. 3.

The length of the broken line drawn in red is the left side of the inequality, the length of the diagonal is the right side. The equal sign occurs only when the following conditions are met:

$$x_1 = x_3 = \dots = x_{2n-1}; \quad x_2 = x_4 = \dots = x_{2n} \text{ и } x_1 + x_2 = 1.$$

In this case, the broken line merges with the diagonal of the square.

2. It is enough to set the inequality sign

$$\sqrt[3]{10} - 2\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{8}.$$

To do this, use the identity

$$(\sqrt[3]{10}-2)(\sqrt[3]{100}+2\sqrt[3]{10}+4) = (3-\sqrt{8})(6+2\sqrt{8}) = 2.$$

3. First of all, we will show that

$$x = \sqrt{2+V_2+\dots+V_2} < 2.$$

($n-1$ radical)

Indeed, by replacing the last of the radicals $\sqrt{2}$ by 2, we increase $x$, that is

$$x < \sqrt{2+V_2+\dots+V_2} = \dots = \sqrt{2+2} = 2.$$

Let's rewrite the original inequality as follows:

$$4(2-\sqrt{2+x}) > 2-x \text{ и далее}$$
$$(2-\sqrt{2+x})^2 > 0.$$

4. Note. Estimate amount

$$\frac{1}{5}\sqrt{5x+15} + \dots + \frac{1}{5}\sqrt{5x+15} +$$
$$\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{8-2x} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{8-2x}.$$

5. Note. Determine which is greater: $3-\sqrt[3]{18}$ or $\sqrt[3]{2}-1$.

(Answer: $\sqrt[3]{2}+\sqrt[3]{18}<4$.)

6. Note. Use the inequality $\sqrt{2}>1$.

To problem M116

The ratio of perimeters $P'/P$ for each $n>3$ can take any value from the interval $(1/2, 1)$. We will not prove this strictly, but will limit ourselves to describing examples when this ratio approaches $1/2$ and 1. If the polygon $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ “degenerates into a segment” so that two neighboring vertices - say $A_1$ and $A_2$ - approach one end of the segment, and the rest - to the other, then $P'/P$ approaches 1; if it "degenerates into a segment" such that $A_1$ and $A_2$ serve as the ends of this segment, and all other vertices approach the middle of the segment, then $P'/P$ approaches $1/2$.

The area ratio $S'/S$ for each $n>4$ can be as close as possible to $1/2$: it is enough to imagine that the polygon “degenerates into a triangle” so that $A_1$ and $A_2$ serve as two vertices of the triangle, and all other vertices approach the third vertex of the triangle. For each $n>5$ the ratio is easily brought closer to 1, causing the polygon to degenerate into a triangle so that $A_1$ and $A_2$ approach one vertex, $A_3$ and $A_4$ to another, and the remaining vertices (at least two of them!) to a third. But for $n=5$ it was not possible to get close to 1. And indeed, as you can prove, the ratio $S'/S$ for the fifth square does not exceed $3/4$.

To problem M120

Let the operation * be defined in a set of three elements 0, 1, 2 as follows: $1*2=1$, and $a*b=0$ for all other pairs $a, b$. Then $a*(b*c)=0$ for any three elements $a, b$ and $c$, but $(1*2)*2=1$.
