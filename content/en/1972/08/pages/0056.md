This can be checked. Using a needle, pierce a piece of stanyl foil with a series of holes of successively decreasing diameters. This should be followed as carefully as possible. The holes should be close to round; to do this, carefully turn the needle when piercing holes in the foil.

Sit in front of the window and look at the sky (you can look at any sufficiently strongly and evenly lit surface, for example, a whitewashed wall, a sheet of white paper). Close to the pupil of eye D (Fig. 4), place a piece of foil (screen E) with one of the holes you pierced. At arm's length in front of your eye, hold some small object R (a needle, a metal mesh with a mesh size of about 0.5×0.5 mm, rare fabric) and bring this object closer to the eye.

Starting from a distance between the object and the eye of less than 250 mm and up to a distance equal to the minimum focus distance of the aperture, a very sharp enlarged image of the object will be visible. If you move the subject even closer to your eye, the image becomes blurry. However, by placing a hole of varying diameter in front of your eye, you will again receive a sharp, even more enlarged image of the object (Fig. 5).

Try this experiment with all the holes you made.

"Hole" spotting scope

figure 6 shows the optical design of the “Hole” telescope, in which the lens is a round hole pierced in a sheet of foil, and the eyepiece is a magnifying glass with a focal length of approximately 2-5 cm. The aperture of the “lens” of such a telescope is small, so only very bright objects can be viewed through it, for example, the filament of an electric incandescent lamp.

Fig. 6.

Once you make a hole-in-one spotting scope, you will find that it can be used both as a microscope and as a telephone! In order for the “hole” spotting scope to work as a microscope, it is enough to bring the object closer to its “lens”. If the object is far from the “lens”, then the spotting scope works like a telephone (Fig. 7).

Real and minimum foci of a hole

Having made sure that the hole acts as a converging lens, let's check whether it is true that it also acts as a diverging lens.

A hole cut in the opaque screen E1 creates a real inverted R1 and a minimal direct R2 image of the object R (Fig. 8). Images of these images using lens L can be obtained on a white screen E2.

Fig. 7. Image of an electric lamp filament obtained using a “hole” telescope:
a) viewing the lamp up to the “lens” of 2 m,
b) viewing from the filament of the same lamp to the 60 mm “lens”.
