⟦folio 26⟧

An article by V. E. Belonuchkin and S. M. Kozel “Optical telephone” was published in the April issue of our magazine. It described the structure of the telephone, the principle of its operation, and spoke about the main optical characteristics of this device. The article published below talks about the possibilities of optical phones, which celestial bodies can be observed with modern phones and which cannot be observed with their help. To better understand this article, the reader should first be familiar with the article “Optical Telephone”.

The explosion of astronomical discoveries in recent decades is largely due to the modern telephone and technological overlay. Where are the limits of what is possible for modern optical phones? Which celestial bodies can be observed to the limit on the largest modern phones and which ones are inaccessible to them?

Before answering the questions posed, we will find out the limiting capabilities of the naked human eye when observing celestial bodies and then consider to what extent modern optical telephones extend these capabilities.

From the wide spectrum of electromagnetic waves that are emitted by celestial bodies, the human eye perceives a relatively narrow frequency band - from red to phylottic rays: $v_{кр} = 4,0 \cdot 10^{14} \text{ eU}, v_{\phi} = 7,7 \cdot 10^{14} \text{ eU}$, which corresponds to wavelengths $\lambda_{кр} = 7600 \text{ Å}, \lambda_{\phi} = 3900 \text{ Å}$ (this range of wavelengths is called the visible part of the spectrum of electromagnetic waves).

All other types of electromagnetic radiation from celestial bodies: radio waves of various ranges, infrared, ultraviolet, RENTEN and gamma rays are not perceived by the human eye.

With integrated moving light, the human eye is exposed to radiation with wavelength $\lambda = 5550 \text{ Å}$ (green region of the spectrum). In very low light conditions, including when observing faint celestial bodies, the maximum sensitivity of the eye shifts towards shorter wavelengths ($\lambda = 5130 \text{ Å}$).

Determining the boundaries within the possible celestial bodies with the naked eye, astronomers use a magnitude scale.

Even in ancient times, Hipparchus divided all visible stars into 6 magnitudes (of course, these were stars visible to the naked eye). He classified the strongest stars as stars of the 1st magnitude, and the weakest ones as stars of the 6th magnitude.
