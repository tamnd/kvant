If we neglect the friction forces, then the force developed by the large piston will always be equal to the rate with which the small piston pushes out with high pressure: $F = P_B \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$.

Let us equate these expressions $P_H \frac{\pi D^2}{4} = P_B \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$. From here we obtain the dependence of the high pressure value on the low pressure value: $P_B = \frac{D^2}{d^2} P_H = k P_H$. Here $k$ is the installation multiplication coefficient. The required pressure is usually obtained in one stroke of the piston, that is, as a result of one roll. If it is necessary to increase the pressure in a large vessel, then the multipliers are controlled by exhaust and discharge valves and the piston is moved several times, as in a large pump. Multipliers are “low-speed” machines, their productivity (amount of liquid rolled per minute) is low.

Compressors are used to create high pressures in large volumes. A compressor can be thought of as a pump whose piston is moved by a powerful electric motor through a crank mechanism. The general view of the compressor is shown in figure 4. In the Soviet Union, unique compressors were created that produce ten liters of liquid per hour at a pressure of 10-16 thousand atmospheres. Their distinctive feature is a large number of piston strokes per minute and a high degree of slope. In one stroke of the piston, the pressure in the cylinder rises from 30 to 16,000 atmospheres.

When gases roll down, their volume initially decreases approximately in proportion to pressure. At high pressures, the density of gases becomes close to the density of liquid ($\rho \approx 1 \text{ e/cm}^3$), their skimability decreases and large deviations from the Boyle-Marutte law are observed.
