This is indicated. Let us outline a way to solve this problem for $n=20$. In this case $P[n] = P[20] = 26$.

Let's divide 20 chess players into two groups: the first group has 16 people, the second - four. In the first group, we determine in the standard way (see §1 and §2 of Chapter III) the 1st prize-winner $A$ and the 2nd prize-winner $B$. This will take $15 + 3 = 18$ batches. In this case, $B$ will win no more than four people (see §3).

In the second group we determine the strongest $C$ according to the Olympic system (3 games). In this case, $C$ will win against two.

The next (22nd) game will be played between $B$ and $C$. If $B$ wins, then it is clear that $A$ is the champion, $B$ is the second prize-winner, and the five losers $B$ are vying for third place. For the remaining 4 games, you can find a third prize winner among them.

Let, on the contrary, $C$ win. Then the following situation will arise (see Fig. 1): chess players $A, B, C, D, E$ and $F$ (arrows point from winners to winners) compete for recognition of the place. Let's play the 23rd game between $D$ and $E$; let $E$ win in this game (the case when $D$ wins is simpler and is considered similarly).

The 24th game is played between $A$ and $E$. If $E$ wins, then $C$ is the champion, $E$ is the second prize-winner, and $A, D$ and $F$ are competing for third place. For the remaining 2 parts, we will find a third prize winner among them.

If $A$ wins the 24th game, then (see Fig. 2) the first two places are claimed by $A$ and $C$, and the third by $B$ and $E$. After playing two games (between $A$ and $C$ and between $B$ and $E$), we will determine the 1st, 2nd and 3rd winners. So, $I(20) = 26 = P(20)$.

The rules just given differ sharply from the standard rules in Chapter III. According to standard rules, chess players who lose at least one game do not participate in the next games until a champion is determined. Only by refusing this, we managed to claim the 1st, 2nd and 3rd prize-winners among 20 chess players in 26 (not 27) games.

2. Prove that the 1st and 2nd prize-winners among $n$ chess players can certainly be determined in $l[n] + n - 2$ games and may not be determined in fewer games.

3. Let us want to determine among $n$ chess players $k$ the strongest (1st, 2nd, ..., $k$ winners). Prove that a) this can certainly be done in $(k-1)l[n] + n-k$ games;
b) if

$$R < l[n(n-1)] \dots (n-k+2)] + n-k,$$

then the results of the games may turn out to be such that this cannot be done in $R$ games.
