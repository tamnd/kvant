⟦folio 34⟧

Let us first consider the case when $f=0$, that is, the only force acting on the particles is the friction force. From everyday experience it is known that the speed of such a particle will decrease all the time and eventually the particle will stop. The first part of this statement also follows from Newtonian mechanics. In fact, since the force (in this case the friction force) is directed against the speed, the acceleration is also directed in the same direction, and this means that the speed of the particle decreases.

How fast does this process proceed and how long do you need to wait until the particle stops? Analysis of the motion of a particle under the influence of friction leads to a paradoxical (at first glance) result: the particle will not stop at all.

True, over a time equal to $\frac{M}{\alpha} \ln 2$, its speed will decrease by half compared to the previous value.

What does "stop" mean?

In fact, no contradiction between theory and experiment is necessary so that the discrepancy between them is less than the error of the experiment. In this case, this condition is not met, since the speed predicted by the theory kills indefinitely over time and, starting from a certain moment, will stop less than the experimental error. If the state of rest is recorded not visually (by eye), but rather using fairly accurate instruments, then in the case of a microsoptic body one can detect the well-known Brunian motion. This, indeed, will be a refutation of the theory, but such a refutation is not surprising, since from the very beginning we took into account only the average effect of environmental particles - for example, gas molecules. If it is true that the picture based on this assumption does not reflect the impact of individual molecules on particles, which all together (averaged) are, as we know, the cause of friction.

Therefore, the question is: “How long will it take to stop?” - placed incorrectly. It should be replaced by the question: “When will the speed of the particle become practically indistinguishable from zero?”

The concept of “speed practically indistinguishable from zero” needs, of course, further decoding. Depending on the purpose for which the body needs to be stopped (for example, a boat at the pier so that a rope can be thrown onto the bollards), the practically zero speed can be one hundredth, a thousandth or, say, a millionth of the initial initial speed. To determine this, we assume that the speed practically indistinguishable from zero is one thousandth of the first initial speed. We have already said that during time $\frac{M}{\alpha} \ln 2$ the particle speed decreases by half. Let's call the quantity $\tau = \frac{M}{\alpha} \ln 2$ time constant. (This quantity is called the free particle time, if we are talking not about a macroscopic body, but about an atomic particle... There is nothing to be done; often the same quantity is called differently in different areas of physics.) In a time span of 10t, the speed will decrease by more than $2^{10}=1024$ times. Thus, the stopping time is equal to the decelerated time constant. If we consider the particle to have stopped only when its initial speed decreases a million times, then the stopping time will only double, that is, it will become equal to 20 tons.
