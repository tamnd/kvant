Satisfies compliance with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
Polyag $\frac{a}{c} = x$ and $\frac{b}{c} = y$, we obtain that $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ ($x$ and $y$ are rational numbers).
Hence $y^2 = (1 - x)(1 + x)$ or $\frac{y}{1 + x} = \frac{1 - x}{y}$. We denote the general (rational) value of the last two correspondences by $t = \frac{u}{v}$. Then $y = t(1 + x)$ and $(1 - x) = ty$, whence $tx - y = -t$, $x + ty = 1$.

From these equalities it follows in turn that
$$x = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}, \quad y = \frac{2t}{1 + t^2},$$
or, substituting $\frac{a}{c}$ for $x$, $\frac{b}{c}$ for $y$ and $\frac{u}{v}$ for $t$, we get
$$\frac{a}{c} = \frac{v^2 - u^2}{u^2 + v^2}, \quad \frac{b}{c} = \frac{2uv}{u^2 + v^2}.$$

Or: $a = (v^2 - u^2)r$, $b = 2uvr$, $c = (u^2 + v^2)r$, where $r$ is some rational proportionality coefficient. If we further assume that $a, b, c$ are coprime, then it easily turns out that they satisfy the correspondences $a = v^2 - u^2$, $b = 2uv$, $c = u^2 + v^2$, where $u$ and $v$ are positive integers that are coprime and not simultaneously odd. (These formulas, known to the mathematicians of Ancient India, are often called “induction formulas.”) And it is already quite easy to show (follow this yourself) that the numbers $a, b$ and $c$ satisfy the correspondence $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) proved Fermat's Last Theorem for the case $n = 3, 4, 5, 7$.

The largest contribution to the solution of Fermat's problems is associated with the name of the German mathematician Ernst Kummer (1810-1893).

It can be shown (with the help of elements of reasoning) that to prove Fermat’s theorem it is enough to consider equations of the form $a^p + b^p = c^p$, where $p$ is a prime number, and $a, b, c$ are integers, but not necessarily positive ones.

Kummer proved that the equation $a^p + b^p = c^p$ does not admit a solution in integer (nonzero) numbers for all prime exponents $p$ that satisfy some “regularity” condition, in particular, for all $3 < p < 100$. It is still not known whether the set of all regular numbers is infinite, but the set of irregular numbers is assumed to be infinite, that is, numbers for which Kummer’s proof does not work.

However, the main significance of Kummer's work on Fermat's Last Theorem is that Kummer "in passing" developed a completely new and important branch of number theory - the so-called theoretical factorization, which indicates that the agreement of Simon Flag forced such devils to thoroughly probe Kummer's work.

In very recent years, a geometric approach to the problem associated with Fermat's Last Theorem has developed. The following messages are the starting points here. If the equation $x^p + y^p = z^p$ has an integer solution, and $z \neq 0$, then the equation $\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^p + \left(\frac{y}{z}\right)^p = 1$ has a solution in rational numbers, and vice versa. Equation $x^p + y^p = 1$ can be spoken of as an equation of some curve on the plane $xOy$ (similar to how equation $x + y = 1$ defines a straight line, equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ defines a circle, etc.), and its solution in rational numbers can be spoken of as a point on a curve with rational coordinates (in short: rational point). It should be immediately noted that there are rational points on the curve $x^p + y^p = 1$; a simple example is the point $(0, 1)$; this corresponds to obvious solutions of Fermat's equation.
