An article by V. E. Belonuchkin and S. M. Kozel “Optical telephone” was published in the April issue of our magazine. It described the structure of the telephone, the principle of its operation, and spoke about the main optical characteristics of this device. The article published below talks about the possibilities of optical phones, which celestial bodies can be observed with modern phones and which cannot be observed with their help. To better understand this article, the reader should first be familiar with the article “Optical Telephone”.

The explosion of astronomical discoveries in recent decades is largely due to the modern telephone and technological overlay. Where are the limits of what is possible for modern optical phones? Which celestial bodies can be observed to the limit on the largest modern phones and which ones are inaccessible to them?

Before answering the questions posed, we will find out the limiting capabilities of the naked human eye when observing celestial bodies and then consider to what extent modern optical telephones extend these capabilities.

From the wide spectrum of electromagnetic waves that are emitted by celestial bodies, the human eye perceives a relatively narrow frequency band - from red to phylottic rays: $v_{кр} = 4,0 \cdot 10^{14} \text{ eU}, v_{\phi} = 7,7 \cdot 10^{14} \text{ eU}$, which corresponds to wavelengths $\lambda_{кр} = 7600 \text{ Å}, \lambda_{\phi} = 3900 \text{ Å}$ (this range of wavelengths is called the visible part of the spectrum of electromagnetic waves).

All other types of electromagnetic radiation from celestial bodies: radio waves of various ranges, infrared, ultraviolet, RENTEN and gamma rays are not perceived by the human eye.

With integrated moving light, the human eye is exposed to radiation with wavelength $\lambda = 5550 \text{ Å}$ (green region of the spectrum). In very low light conditions, including when observing faint celestial bodies, the maximum sensitivity of the eye shifts towards shorter wavelengths ($\lambda = 5130 \text{ Å}$).

Determining the boundaries within the possible celestial bodies with the naked eye, astronomers use a magnitude scale.

Even in ancient times, Hipparchus divided all visible stars into 6 magnitudes (of course, these were stars visible to the naked eye). He classified the strongest stars as stars of the 1st magnitude, and the weakest ones as stars of the 6th magnitude.

To clarify the magnitude scale in the last century, it was assumed that stars of 6th magnitude compared to stars of 1st magnitude (a difference of 5 magnitudes) provide 100 times less illumination. Thus, the magnitude scale was based on the number $\sqrt[5]{100} = 2,512$. This means that a 1st magnitude star sends us 2.512 times more luminous flux than a 2nd magnitude star, and $2,512^2 = 6,31$ times more light than a 3rd magnitude star, etc.

Consequently, the ratio of illumination $E_1$ and $E_2$ from two stars, one of which has a magnitude of $m_1$ and the other of $m_2$, is determined by the following formula:

$$\frac{E_1}{E_2} = 2,512^{m_2-m_1}.$$

The human eye, with prolonged adaptation on especially dark nights and with good transparency of the atmosphere, is capable of seeing stars of the 8th magnitude. This is the visual noise threshold of the average human eye in physical units. It has been established by everyday life that when a 1st magnitude star is at the zenith on the surface at $1 m^2$, perpendicular to the rays, the luminous flux of $0,85 \cdot 10^{-6}$ lumens ($\lambda m$) falls.

It is not difficult to calculate that an eye with a pupil diameter of 6 mm receives a luminous flux from a star of 8th magnitude

$$F = \frac{3,14 \cdot 0,003^2 \cdot 0,85 \cdot 10^{-6}}{2,512^7} \approx 3,8 \cdot 10^{-14} (\lambda m).$$

Let us now estimate how many quanta entering the pupil in 1 second correspond to this threshold flow. We will assume that the stream consists of rays with a wavelength $\lambda = 5130$ Å ($v = 5,85 \cdot 10^{14} e\mu$), in relation to which the eye feels maximum when observing very faint stars. It has been established by everyday life that a luminous flux of 1 lm generated by such radiation corresponds to an energy flux of 0.00058 VM.

Consequently, a flow of energy enters the pupil of an eye with a diameter of 6 mm from a star of the 8th magnitude

$$W = 3,8 \cdot 10^{-14} \cdot 0,00058 \approx 2,2 \cdot 10^{-17} (\lambda m).$$

One radiation quantum with frequency $v = 5,85 \cdot 10^{14} e\mu$ carries energy

$$\varepsilon = hv = 6,62 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 5,85 \cdot 10^{14} = 3,87 \cdot 10^{-19} (\partial x).$$

Therefore, in one second from a star of 8th magnitude,

$$N = \frac{2,2 \cdot 10^{-17}}{3,87 \cdot 10^{-19}} \approx 60 (\text{кваантов}).$$

Let us immediately note that the results obtained should be viewed only as estimates, since the light from stars is not monochromatic - it does not consist of quanta of one part, and the diameters of the pupils and the sensitivity of the eyes of people are not the same.

The use of binoculars and telescopes significantly expands a person's vision capabilities. With their help, many faint stars become visible. Moreover, it turns out that some of these “faint” stars are actually systems consisting of two, three, or even a large number of stars (such systems are usually called “multiple stars”). When observing with a telescope, the larger the diameter of the object, the greater the luminous flux intercepted by the lens. The size of the object's diameter determines the optical power or penetrating power of the telescope. These terms mean the magnitudes of the extremely faint stars that can be observed with this telescope.

If we do not take into account the loss of light in the optical and some other subtleties, then the optical power of the telescope when observing stars can be calculated at the nearest time, it is only necessary to determine

The angular dimensions of the diffraction patterns are generally small; To see them, it is necessary to use eyepieces that provide such magnifications that the angular dimensions of the details of the observed picture would be greater than the angle of resolution of the human eye.

Thus, a telescope with an ideal lens with a diameter of 6 cm allows one to obtain a diffraction pattern with an angular size of the radius of the central disk $\alpha = 14:6 = 2,3''$. In order for the eye to see this radius at an angle of $3' = 180''$, it is necessary to use an eyepiece that gives a magnification of $180'':2,3'' \approx 80$ times. With increasing magnification of the telescope, the visible angular dimensions of the diffraction images of stars increase, but at the same time the intensity of the diffraction patterns themselves decreases, and in faint stars the rings only disappear - there is not enough light energy to damage the retina over an increased area.

Currently, designers and engineers usually manage to calculate and create telesops, the optics of which make it possible to achieve resolution limits according to formula (2), but large telesops in practice cannot provide such resolutions - the turbulence of the atmosphere interferes. The state of the atmosphere is considered good if a resolution of $1''$ can be achieved using a large telescope. And only in certain short-term intervals, when the state of the atmosphere is exceptionally good, star pairs with angular distances between the components up to $0,4''$ are resolved.

If we compare the latest data with the theoretical resolution of a six-meter telescope ($\psi_0 = 0,02''$), it is clearly visible how much the atmosphere, even in good condition, spoils images: without it, when examining celestial bodies with noticeable angular dimensions, it would be possible to distinguish 20-50 times smaller details. If we take into account that the true angular sizes of individual stars are larger than the resolution angle of a 6-meter telescope (for example, the angular diameter of Mira Ceti is 0.056'', Betelgeuse - 0.047'', Antares - 0.040''), then in the absence of an atmosphere one could hope to finally see discs of stars, empty they would only be two to three times larger than the diffraction disks. But still, these would be true disks of stars (albeit with a superimposed diffraction pattern), and not just products of the wave nature of starlight passing through the telescope opening.

In the absence of an atmosphere, it becomes possible to increase the penetrating power of telescopes and, in addition, to carry out observations in ultraviolet and infrared rays with their help. Therefore, actronomers dream of taking telescopes beyond the earth’s atmosphere.

According to modern ideas, the future success of actronomy is largely related to the creation of actronomy observatories on artificial Earth satellites and on the atmosphereless Moon.
