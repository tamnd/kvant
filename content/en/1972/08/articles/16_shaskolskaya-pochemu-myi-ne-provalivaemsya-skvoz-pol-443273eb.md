Why do materials resist external loads? I will buy that mixtures occur in them. There are no and cannot be absolutely rigid materials in nature; all materials have flexibility to one degree or another. In many bodies the mixtures are very small. “If I step on a common building brick,” says Gordon, “then its height will decrease by approximately (1/20,000) cm. And any two neighboring atoms in the brick will become closer to each other by (1/500,000) Å, that is, by 2·10⁻¹⁴ cm.” Should such small mixtures be taken into account? It turns out they are not so small! “The strong ropes that extend across Forth Bay in Scotland,” Gordon gives an example, “are always elastically growing by about 0.1%, but with a total length of about 3 km, this stretch is already 3 meters! These small, invisible mixtures sometimes lead to the fact that a steel place can suddenly collapse, taking with it tens or hundreds of human lives, and an ocean steam code suddenly and instantly breaks in half. In the first 60 years of the 20th century, 27 ocean-going steamships were lost when they unexpectedly broke in half on the open sea.”

Gordon talks about stress concentration in materials and structures. The concentration of stress in a small area near a hole, a cut, a defect in the material, a step or a scratch on the surface. Local, local stress can interrupt the average voltage many times over. Soviet academician A.F. Ioffe showed that if the surface is moistened, thus removing the influence of scratches and unevenness on it, then a fragile rock salt crystal can be made so flexible that it can be tied according to the hands and even tied in a knot. Every surface unevenness, cut, every hole creates a concentration of stress. This concentration can be fatal to a solid structure, place, ship, building.

The concentration of stress in the material leads to the formation of cracks. When a glazier rejects glass, he does not bother to cut through the entire thickness of the sheet, he makes only a shallow cut on the surface, after which the glass breaks along such a scratch. An inexperienced person tries to cut a scratch deeper, an experienced craftsman guides a sharp diamond, cutting through the glass with a thin, still visible line: he knows from experience that the weakening effect of a scratch does not depend at all on its depth, but only on its shape, on the sharpness of its edge. The degree of stress concentration near a crack depends on the geometry of the cracks and their shape. If a crack that has formed due to one or another defect in the material does not spread, then the material will not fail brittlely. However, like plasticine, it can flow and be destroyed by pressure. Having analyzed in detail stress concentrations, the mechanism of crack formation and the mechanism of plastic flow, Gordon concludes: there are always two mechanisms that compete for the right to destroy a material - plate flow and brittle cracking. The material is inferior to one or the other of them. If it starts before it cracks, it is ductile. If it cracks before it begins to leak, then we are dealing with a brittle material. The potential for destruction is inherent in all materials. How is a material created with the optimal combination of both possibilities? It turns out that nature took care of this.
