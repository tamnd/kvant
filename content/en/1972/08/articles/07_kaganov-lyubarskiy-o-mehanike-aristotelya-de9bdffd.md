About Aristotle's mechanics

M. I. Kaganov, L. Ya. Lyubarsky

The process of cognition of nature is not limited to the calm accumulation of facts and the formation on their basis of absolutely accurate statements - the laws of nature. In the words of Albert Einstein, “there is a dramatic struggle between the old and the new.”*).

One should not, however, think that new theories simply cancel (overthrow) the old ones. As a rule, the new theory shows the old “its place.” Statements that previously seemed absolute are always valid and, as analysis from the standpoint of the new theory shows, are valid only within certain limits, limited by certain conditions.

In the area where it is valid, the old theory is still good. Otherwise, why would it be created?

By comparing Aristotle's mechanics with Newton's mechanics, we will show under what conditions Aristotle's mechanics is the correct theory. This example will help you understand how the transition from a new theory to an old one occurs.

Galileo and Aristotle

One of the outstanding examples of bold scientific criticism and at the same time a classic example of a “thought experiment” is Galileo’s misunderstanding about a falling stone. This alienation is directed against Aristotle’s test about the proportionality of force and speed and consists in the following.

If you take two stones, one lighter, the other heavier, and throw them from a base, then, according to Aristotle, the heavier stone will fall

faster than a light one, since a greater force acts on it. Well, there seems to be nothing absurd here. Each of us knows that a metal coin falls faster than a feather.

However, Galileo suggests: let's connect these stones with each other. What will happen? Again it's clear. A heavier stone will accelerate a lighter one, and that in turn will slow down its companion. So two stones connected together will fall slightly faster than a free light stone, but slower than a heavy stone. All this follows from Aristotle’s thesis and at the same time... contradicts this testis. In fact, we have the right to consider two stones connected together as one stone with a weight equal to the sum of the weights of the light and heavy stones. Since the weight of a double stone is greater than that of a heavy stone, it (according to Aristotle) ​​will fall faster than a heavy stone. So, following Aristotle, we came to two mutually exclusive conclusions. Therefore Aristotle's testis is incorrect.

It seems that a living Aristotle would have been able to object to Galil. But Galileo fought not against the living Aristotle, but against the dead, ossified dogma into which the church transformed Aristotle’s teaching. And Galil managed to defeat this dogma.

Let us first consider the case when $f=0$, that is, the only force acting on the particles is the friction force. From everyday experience it is known that the speed of such a particle will decrease all the time and eventually the particle will stop. The first part of this statement also follows from Newtonian mechanics. In fact, since the force (in this case the friction force) is directed against the speed, the acceleration is also directed in the same direction, and this means that the speed of the particle decreases.

How fast does this process proceed and how long do you need to wait until the particle stops? Analysis of the motion of a particle under the influence of friction leads to a paradoxical (at first glance) result: the particle will not stop at all.

True, over a time equal to $\frac{M}{\alpha} \ln 2$, its speed will decrease by half compared to the previous value.

What does "stop" mean?

In fact, no contradiction between theory and experiment is necessary so that the discrepancy between them is less than the error of the experiment. In this case, this condition is not met, since the speed predicted by the theory kills indefinitely over time and, starting from a certain moment, will stop less than the experimental error. If the state of rest is recorded not visually (by eye), but rather using fairly accurate instruments, then in the case of a microsoptic body one can detect the well-known Brunian motion. This, indeed, will be a refutation of the theory, but such a refutation is not surprising, since from the very beginning we took into account only the average effect of environmental particles - for example, gas molecules. Let us now turn to the very important question of the correspondence between the classical theory and the theory that generalized it.

Everyone (physicists, mathematicians, and even physiologists) faces very difficult problems when studying this figurative question. It is natural to expect that the “simpler” and more attractive the compared theories are, the easier it is to discern the connections that are characteristic of them. Therefore, replacing likewise, consider the simplest pair of theories: Aristotelian mechanics - Newtonian mechanics.

Let us consider several functional physical concepts within the framework of these two theories.

System Status

The concept of system state does not follow the path of system description. So, for example, if we say that the system consists of two balls of radius $r$ and mass $M$, connected to each other by a clamp, the natural length of which is $L$, and the rigidity is $k$, then from such a description of the system it is impossible to draw a conclusion about the position and speed of each of the balls, about the distance between them, the hardening of the balls, in general, about all those quantities that change over time time. Such quantities (we will call them variable parameters) can be much more complex in nature. So, if the system is the atmosphere of the Earth, then the variable parameters include temperature as a function of height and geographic coordinates, and wind speed as a function of these same variables, and many other parameters.

Let us choose some set $x_i$ ($i=1,2,...$) of variable parameters so as to satisfy the following two conditions:

1. Any set of values ​​of parameters $x_i$ can be realized, that is, the system can be brought into a state in which each of the parameters $x_i$ takes on a value prescription.

2. Knowing the values ​​of all parameters $x_i$ at the initial moment $t=t_0$, one can calculate their values ​​at any subsequent moment in time.

Let's consider an example - an electron in a constant electromagnetic field. In order to describe this system in the mechanics of Newton and Einstein, it is necessary, in addition to describing the electromagnetic field, to indicate the mass and charge of the electron; in Aristotelian mechanics, one must specify its mobility.

Let's try to set the state of the electron by specifying its arbitrary coordinates, speed and acceleration. It is immediately clear that within the framework of Newtonian mechanics, the totality of these quantities is not suitable for specifying a state. In fact, the position and speed of the electron determine the force acting on it from the electromagnetic field. According to Newtonian mechanics, force determines the acceleration of the electron. Therefore, acceleration cannot be arbitrary and must be excluded from the set of parameters given to the electron system. In Aristotelian mechanics, speed should also be excluded from this set, since within the framework of this theory, force uniquely determines speed. It can be proven mathematically that, knowing the position and speed of the electron at the initial moment of time, one can calculate the same values ​​at any subsequent moment in time, relying on Newton’s second law. In the same way, Aristotle's law allows you to find the coordinates of a point at any time if they are known at the initial moment.

So, in Newtonian mechanics, the state of the system is specified by indicating all points of the system and their velocities.

In Aristotelian mechanics, the state of a system is known if the positions of all its points are known.

Conservation laws

It is well known that conservation laws apply in Newtonian mechanics. They are most simply formulated for a closed system. This is the name of a system of bodies that interact with each other and do not interact with any other bodies. Frankly speaking, closed systems do not exist in nature. The gravitational and electromagnetic fields, the flows of cosmic particles do not allow any system of bodies to “close in on itself.” There are, however, almost closed systems. The forces acting on the bodies entering them from foreign bodies are very small, and such systems behave almost indistinguishably from closed systems. Therefore, it seems important to become familiar with the basic properties of closed systems.

It turns out that the seven magnitude energy of the system, the three components of its momentum and the three components of its angular momentum do not change over time (“conserved”) if the system is closed.

It is easy to find the conservation law in Aristotle's mechanics. For this purpose, let us consider some “closed” system of bodies. The word closed implies that from the point of view of Newtonian mechanics, not a single system of bodies in Aristotle’s mechanics is closed - which body interacts with the environment is responsible for the friction force.

Let $r_1(t), r_2(t), \ldots, r_n(t)$ be the radius vectors of the bodies forming a “closed” system. Let's build a vector

We invite the reader to independently prove the following statements: 1) the main vector of the “closed” system is preserved - does not change over time; 2) if external forces act on bodies, then the end of the main vector of the system (the body is no longer closed) moves as a body with mobility
