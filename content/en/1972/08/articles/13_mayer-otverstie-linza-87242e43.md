Diffraction of light
on the round answer

Observing the diffraction of light on a round hole cut into an opaque screen, one can notice a number of interesting patterns*). If the distance from the hole to the observation plane is small, then the diffraction pattern is a family of concentric light and dark wheels, in the center of which there can be either a light or a dark spot. We will increase the distance between the response and the observation plane. The general character of the picture is preserved, but light and dark spots successively change in the center. All these patterns can be explained using the method of constructing Fresnel zones.

We will assume that the exact light source $S$ is at infinity, so that a plane light wave parallel to the screen plane (Fig. 2) arrives at the screen $\partial_1$ with a circular answer. We need to estimate the illumination of the central point $A$ of the diffraction pattern obtained on the screen $\partial_2$.

Let us divide the response area into such collateral zones that the distances $AB_1, AB_2, \dots, AB_k$ satisfy the conditions:

$$AB_1 - AO = AB_2 - AB_1 = \dots = AB_k - AB_{k-1} = \frac{\lambda}{2},$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the incident light.

The oscillations caused at point A by waves coming from two adjacent zones are mutually compensated.

It is clear that if the hole opens an even number of Fresnel zones, then at point A the amplitude of the resulting oscillation will be minimal, which means that the screen illumination $\Delta_2$ at this point will also be minimal. The maximum illumination of point A will be if the hole opens an odd number of zones. That is why, when moving away from the plane from the screen responsible, dark and light spots in the center of the diffraction pattern successively replace each other.

Optical image

Pierce one of the walls of the opaque box with a small piece of good stretched pyrolysis paper (or, better yet, frosted glass). You have a camera obscura - the simplest device that produces images of objects, a device in which the image is created by a person in charge.

But a hole cannot give a different distribution of illumination on the observed plane other than that resulting from the diffraction of light. Which of these distributions can be considered the image of a luminous point with a large base? Of course, the one that most closely resembles the luminous point itself, that is, one in which the central light spot has the most illumination, and the light rings surrounding it have the most illumination.

Such a distribution of illumination will be on a plane infinitely distant from the answer. Indeed, at an infinitely distant point lying on the response axis, all waves coming from a light source (also lying at infinity) arrive in phase, and their amplitudes at this point are summed up as a result of interference. At all other points of lightening, waves from the light source arrive with a certain difference in their path, so that the light intensity in them is less than it could be if the waves arrived at them in phase. Since the intensity at the central point of the diffraction pattern has become less, then, according to the law of conservation of energy, the intensity of light in the circles surrounding it has increased. This means that the image of the exact light source has deteriorated.

So, the image of a luminous point created by the answer is a diffraction pattern, in the center of which there is a bright spot, and the difference in the path of the waves arriving at the center of this spot is zero.

But this is an ideal case. Practical waves in the center of the image always arrive with some path difference. What should be the maximum value of this path difference so that the image can be considered not too different from the ideal one? We can immediately say that the maximum path difference should not exceed half the wavelength, because in this case the hole opens no more than one Fresnel zone, and the light intensity in the center of the diffraction pattern is much greater than in the surrounding area.

We will call the optical center O of a round hole its center, and the main optical axis X is the straight line, passing through the optical center of the hole perpendicular to the plane of the opaque screen E (Fig. 3).

Let us denote the diameter of the hole by D, and the maximum difference in the wave path by δ. For any point x lying on the main optical axis, according to the Pythagorean theorem, we can write:

$$\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2 = (x + \delta)^2 - x^2,$$

$$\frac{D^2}{4} = 2\delta x + \delta^2.$$

The value of δ² is small compared to the other quantities included in this formula; it can be neglected. Then

$$x = \frac{D^2}{8\delta}.$$

According to the Rayleigh criterion, the maximum path difference δ should not exceed four waves, that is, 0 ≤ δ ≤ $\frac{\lambda}{4}$. Substituting these values ​​of δ into formula (1), we obtain

$$\frac{D^2}{2\lambda} \leq x < \infty.$$

This can be checked. Using a needle, pierce a piece of stanyl foil with a series of holes of successively decreasing diameters. This should be followed as carefully as possible. The holes should be close to round; to do this, carefully turn the needle when piercing holes in the foil.

Sit in front of the window and look at the sky (you can look at any sufficiently strongly and evenly lit surface, for example, a whitewashed wall, a sheet of white paper). Close to the pupil of eye D (Fig. 4), place a piece of foil (screen E) with one of the holes you pierced. At arm's length in front of your eye, hold some small object R (a needle, a metal mesh with a mesh size of about 0.5×0.5 mm, rare fabric) and bring this object closer to the eye.

Starting from a distance between the object and the eye of less than 250 mm and up to a distance equal to the minimum focus distance of the aperture, a very sharp enlarged image of the object will be visible. If you move the subject even closer to your eye, the image becomes blurry. However, by placing a hole of varying diameter in front of your eye, you will again receive a sharp, even more enlarged image of the object (Fig. 5).

Try this experiment with all the holes you made.
