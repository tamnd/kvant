MATHEMATICAL CIRCLE

KALEIDOSCOPES

A. N. Vilenkin

Probably, in childhood, each of you took apart at least one kaleidoscope, and you know how it works. However, let us remind you that when you took it apart, pieces of colored glass fell out of it, and inside there were three strips of mirror (or glass), located at an angle of 60° to each other, so that a regular triangle was formed.

Why the kaleidoscope is designed this way and how else it can be made, we will tell you in this article. The tasks included in the text (their results are used in calculations) will allow you not only to passively read the article, but also to independently “have a hand” in building a theory. However, all problems in the article begin with the words “Prove that...”, so during the first reading they can be replaced with the words “It is proven that...”, and then come back and take on the proof. At the end of the issue there are instructions for all tasks.

The path of rays in a kaleidoscope

Directly in front of you you will see the figurine that was placed near the other kaleidoscope. Nearby you will see her again, only mirrored. This is understandable, because there is a mirror next to him - so a mirror reflection appeared. The role of a mirror is played by a strip of glass.

By the way, figure 3 explains what a mirror stripe is. It has two reflective surfaces: the front, polished, and the back, covered with amalgam. To verify this, place a sharpened pencil against the mirror. You will see two or even three reflections. The fact is that the beam falling on the glass is heard. One part of it is reflected from the front surface and gives the first reflected ray, and the second part enters the glass. This part is reflected in the rear surface (almost entirely in the mirror case and slightly in the glass case) and goes to the front surface. Through it, part of the ray comes out and gives a second reflected ray, and the other part of the word is reflected (inside the mirror) and begins a new journey, ending with the appearance of a third reflected ray. These rays weaken as their number increases, so that the third reflected ray is no longer noticeable.

If you put a mirror in a kaleidoscope, a second reflected beam will appear, which will also cause distortion so that nothing can be made out, especially with multiple reflections in mirrors. After all, the first and second rays are mixed relative to each other and give a shift in the image. When reflected

Could it happen that the mathematical kaleidoscope is incorrect, that is, there is a point X and two sequences of reflections giving different images in X, but one of these images is visible from any point, and the second (or all the others) is never visible, that is, the physical kaleidoscope will be correct?

It turns out that this is not the case. It is absolutely clear that if the same image arrives at point X via geometric (reflections) via different routes, then it will be visible from any point. But at the same time, if from any observation point $T$ the same image is visible at point X, then exactly one image is obtained geometrically at point X. We will now proceed to prove the last statement and find all correct kaleidoscopes.

Correct kaleidoscopes

First, let's look at the simplest kaleidoscopes. True, their sections are “infinite” (unbounded) polygons, but this should not confuse you.

Task 6. Prove that if a kaleidoscon is mathematically correct, then the elements at its vertices are its functional set and what elements at its vertices all these elements have the form $\frac{180^\circ}{n}$ (n is a natural number correspondence; for different vertices these $n$ can be different).

The difference between tasks 5 and 6 is not great: in task 5 the mirrors are unlimited, but in task 6 they have finite sizes. Both problems are solved by mail in the same way. But Problem 6 gives a necessary condition for the mathematical correctness of a kaleidoscon: the angles at the vertices of its functional polygon must have the specified form.

Once we have one condition that every mathematically regular kaleidoscope must satisfy, we can try to find all kaleidoscopes that satisfy this condition. In any case, among them there will be all the original kaleidoscopes, which is a significant essence of our concepts.

Task 7. Find all kaleidoscopes that satisfy the necessary condition of correctness (task 6). This means the following: we need to find for what number of sides of the functional polygon and what lines at its vertices all these lines have the form $\frac{180^\circ}{n}$. Checking that all receiving kaleidoscopes are mathematically correct, that is, that the necessary conditions for the mathematical correctness of a kaleidoscope, formed in problem 6, is sufficient.
