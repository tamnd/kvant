MAXWELL'S PROBLEM

The outstanding English theoretical physicist James Clerk Maxwell (1831-1879) was interested in elementary geometry. Here is one of the problems he presented in an article on structural mechanics, which was published in The London, Edinburgh and Dublin Philosophical Magazine and Journal of Science (1864).

Inside the triangle, a point (Fig. 1) is arbitrarily taken and connected to the vertices. Prove that if you build a triangle with sides parallel to these segments, and through its vertices lines are drawn parallel to the sides of the given triangle (Fig. 2), then they will intersect at one point, as shown in figure.

Let's give a proof. At figure 3, a circle is described around each of the triangles that make up the data. The sides of a triangle with vertices at the centers of these circles are parallel to the same segments. The new triangle is similar to the triangle on figure 2, and the segments drawn through its vertices are also inclined to its sides, as the same colored segments on figure 2. Since these segments intersect at the center of the circumcircle of this triangle, the segments on figure 2 must also intersect at one point. The proof is complete.

Now it is not difficult to find a solution to the following problem of the remarkable German geometer Jacob Steiner (1796-1863): if from the vertices $A_1, B_1$ and $C_1$ of the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ the redistributors of the corresponding naimm segments $B_2C_2, C_2A_2$ and $A_2B_2$ of the triangle $A_2B_2C_2$ are omitted and these redistributions intersect in one point, then the redistributions from the vertices $A_2, B_2$ and $C_2$, respectively, to the sides $B_1C_1, C_1A_1$ and $A_1B_1$ intersect at one point (prove this).

And another question: what mechanical consequence can Maxwell deduce from the statement he proved?

V. N. Berezin.
