Place a paraffin barer in the bath at an angle of 45° and watch how the waves are reflected (Fig. 4). In particular, we can verify that the angle $\Theta_i$ between the direction of wave propagation and the line perpendicular to the barer is equal to the angle $\Theta_r$ between the direction of propagation of the reflected waves and the same perpendicular.

Set the barer at a different angle than 45° and change the wavelength and amplitude. It is easy to verify that, for any location of the barer, the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. This is the law of reflection for waves of any type.

Replace the paraffin barer with a glass plate 15 cm wide and 30 cm long. Install it so that the top surface is 1 cm from the bottom of the bath. Fill the bath with water so that the distance from the glass to the surface of the water is 2-3 mm, and create a series of plane waves. See how the waves change as they cross the edge of the glass and hit shallow water. Due to the change in speed, the waves will spread in a new direction over the glass, just like a line of soldiers moving from a land location onto a dirt road (Fig. 5). In this mode, the waves are deflected from the first direction due to refraction, a phenomenon observed in waves of all types when they travel at an angle from one medium to another, in which they propagate at a different speed. Waves on water are distinguished by the fact that they change the speed of propagation as the depth of the medium changes. Approximately, we can assume that the ratio of wave propagation velocities in shallow and deep water is proportional to the ratio of water depth.
