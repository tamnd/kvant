All body materials are composed of atoms of the 88 elements found on Earth. Most of these elements are a mixture of atoms of several types, differing from each other in mass. Such atoms of the same element, differing only in mass, are called isotopes. All 88 natural elements have about 400 natural isotopes, with an average of about 4.5 isotopes per element.

A significant number of isotopes were obtained in laboratories and artificially. More than 1,200 such isotopes are known today. They are created in nuclear reactions carried out by humans. Among them are not only isotopes of elements that exist in nature, but also isotopes of synthetic elements obtained in neutron fluxes from nuclear reactors and in beams of accelerated particles from modern accelerators.

Artificial elements have long found important practical applications. Thus, element No. 94, plutonium, is one of the foundations of modern nuclear energy, and it is produced in tons. However, the most interesting thing in isotope production is the synthesis and study of new atomic nuclei that lie beyond the lower edge of the periodic table.

Most often, new elements are obtained by synthesis (fusion) of two stable atomic nuclei.

In order for two nuclei to form a single nuclear substance, they must touch each other with their surfaces. This is not easy to achieve. All atoms of the nucleus carry a positive charge, and when they come close, they repel each other with great force. This force is determined by Coulomb's law and is equal to

Consequently, in order for a fusion reaction to occur, leading to the formation of a new nucleus, the energy of accelerated particles (projectile nuclei) must be greater than $B \left( 1 + \frac{m}{M} \right)$.

A more accurate calculation according to the laws of quantum mechanics, however, leads to an unexpected result. It turns out that even if $E < B \left( 1 + \frac{m}{M} \right)$, the nuclei can sometimes merge into a single drop. But the likelihood of such a “sub-barrier” merger, as it is called, is very small.

Now we just have to determine the work $B$, and then the desired value of energy $E$ will be known.

In a power plant field, the work done when moving a unit charge from point 1 to point 2 is equal to the potential difference of the electric field at these points $\varphi_2 - \varphi_1$. The field potential of a uniformly charged ball outside it is determined by the formula $\varphi = \frac{q}{R}$, where $q$ is the charge of the ball, and $R$ is the distance to the center of the ball.

Thus, the work $P$ when moving the charge $q_2$ from point 1 to point 2 is equal to $q_2 \left( \frac{q_1}{R_2} - \frac{q_1}{R_1} \right)$. In our case, $R_1 = \infty$ and $\frac{1}{R_1} = 0$, $R_2 = r_1 + r_2$. Therefore, using formula (2), we can write

$$B = \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_1 + r_2} = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{1,5 \cdot 10^{-13} \left( \sqrt{A_1} + \sqrt{A_2} \right)}.$$

Energy $B$ is called potential barer or simply barer. For light nuclei, the “height” of the barer is several million electrons ($Mэθ$), and during the fusion of heavy nuclei the barer reaches hundreds of millions of electrons. If we put the numerical value of the electron charge into formula (7) and express the energy $B$ in millions of electrons, then we get a simple correspondence for the height of the potential barrier:

$$B = \frac{0,96 Z_1 Z_2}{A_1^{1/3} + A_2^{1/3}}.$$

If the mass of the minsheni nucleus is significantly greater than the mass of the projectile nucleus, so that we can consider $1 + \frac{m}{M} \approx 1$, then the kinetic energy of a light accelerated particle required to carry out a nuclear reaction will be equal to the potential barer $B$ [see. formula (6)]. This is the physical meaning of this quantity.

After the projectile core reaches the minsheni core, special nuclear attractive forces begin to tell, the nature of which has not yet been sufficiently studied. These forces act over very short distances. When the nuclei touch the surfaces, nuclear forces more than compensate for electrostatic spalling, and atomic nuclei merge into a single nuclear “drop” - a compound nucleus:

$$\frac{22}{10}Ne + \frac{238}{92}U \rightarrow \frac{260}{102}X.$$

The formation of a compound nucleus is the first stage of the process of synthesis of a new atomic nucleus. A compound nucleus cannot yet be called atomic. It is unstable and its lifetime is too short for the formation of an atom: electrons simply do not have time to take their places in the orbits.

According to Einstein's correspondence, energy $E$ is related to mass: $E = mc^2$, where $c$ is the speed of light. Therefore, to increase mass by amounts of $\Delta M = M_{\text{соct}} - (M + m)$, energy $E^* = \Delta Mc^2$ is required. Some fraction of the kinetic energy of the accelerated particle is converted into the kinetic energy of the compound nucleus $M_{\text{соct}} \frac{V^2}{2} \approx (M + m) \frac{V^2}{2}$. Thus, the translational motion of the compound nucleus and the energy absorbed during fusion account for

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(M + m) V^2}{2} + \Delta Mc^2.$$

When processing compound nuclei as a result of the fusion of such accelerated particles as carbon $^{12}_{6}$C, oxygen $^{16}_{8}$O, heon $^{22}_{10}$Ne, argon $^{40}_{18}$Ar with heavy nuclei, the value $\varepsilon$ is significantly less than the kinetic energy $\frac{mv_0^2}{2}$ required to overcome the Coulomb barrier $B$. Thus,

$$\frac{mv_0^2}{2} - \varepsilon = E - \frac{(M + m) V^2}{2} - \Delta Mc^2 = B - \Delta Mc^2 = B + Q = E^*.$$

The value $Q = -\Delta Mc^2$, equal to the energy spent on processing a compound nucleus, is called the processing energy of this nucleus. The excess kinetic energy $E^*$ remaining in the compound nucleus is called the excitation energy of the compound nucleus.

The energy $E^*$ during the fusion of complex nuclei with heavy target nuclei reaches significant values. Thus, the value of $B$peak or the confluence of $^{22}_{10}$Ne with $^{238}_{92}$U [see formula (9)] is equal to 98.1 MeV. At the same time $Q = -56,1$ Mev.

And therefore $E^* = B + Q = 42$ Mev. Such energy greatly heats up the compound nucleus, it becomes unstable, begins to oscillate and, as a rule, is divided in a very short time into nuclei with atomic numbers close to 50.

The lifetime of a compound nucleus (more precisely, the average lifetime) lies in the interval $10^{-14} - 10^{-18}$ sec, depending on the excitation energy of the compound nucleus. According to everyday ideas, it is a very small value. But it is quite large compared to the “nuclear” unit of time. This unit is the time elapsed between two successive collisions of a nucleon with other nucleon nuclei. This time can be estimated from the following messages. To remain inside the nucleus, a nucleon must interact with its neighbors at distances of the order of $10^{-13}$ cm. The speed of a nucleon in the nucleus is approximately equal to $10^9$ cm/sec. Thus, for the nuclear unit of time we get $10^{-22}$ sec. Therefore, during the lifetime of a compound nucleus, say $10^{-18}$ sec, each nucleon will experience about $10^4$ collisions. In this case, the compound nucleus “forgets” about the method of its processing, and the probability of its decay in one way or another is determined only by its excitation energy.

The most energetic neutrons leave the nuclear drop, and therefore it cools strongly and becomes a stable atomic nucleus of a new element.
