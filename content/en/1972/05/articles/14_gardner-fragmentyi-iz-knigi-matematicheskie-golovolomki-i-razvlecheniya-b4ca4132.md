These days, it’s rare that anyone can come up with a math game that is both new and interesting. This is exactly how original and exciting the game of hex is, which first appeared fifteen years ago at the Niels Bohr Institute for Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen. Hex may well become one of the most popular and best-researched mathematical games of our century.

Hex is played on a diamond-shaped board made up of hexagons (Fig. 3). The number of hexagons can vary, but usually they prefer to play on a board with an identical hexagon along its side. The two opposite sides of a rhombus are called “black”, the other two are called “white”. The hexagons located in the corners of the rhombus belong to both sides. One player plays with black chips, the other with white. Players take turns placing a piece on any hexagon that is not already occupied by another piece. The goal of "black" is to build a target of black chips between the two "black" sides. "White" seeks to build a goal of white chips between the "white" sides.

The target can bend and turn at any angle. Chips are placed until one of the players reaches his goal. At figure 3, for example, you can see how “black” won. The game never ends in a draw, because one of the participants can lock the other only by hitting his goal. Although the rules of hex are very simple, it nevertheless turns out to be a surprisingly subtle mathematical game.

Hex was invented by the Danish Piet Hein. As a young man, Hayne spent several years at the Institute for Theoretical Physics and then, after making some technical images, began working in industry. This continued until 1940, until the German invasion of Denmark. During the occupation, Hayne went into hiding twice - he was the chairman of the anti-national democratic union, which was dissolved with the arrival of the Naists.

The idea for the hex game came to Hayne while he was thinking about the famous topological problem of four colors. Soon the rules of the game were published in the Politiken newspaper and the hex became incredibly popular in Denmark under the name “Polygon”.

Readers who want to play hex should prepare sheets of paper with boards drawn on them in advance. Moves can be marked with crosses and circles. If you prefer to move the pieces on a "real" board, you can draw a large board on a piece of thick cardboard or build it out of hexagonal ceramic tiles. If the tiles are large enough, then you can play with cleared checkers.

To understand all the intricacies of the hex game, it is better to use a playing field consisting of a small number of hexagons. On a 2x2 board (four hexagons), the one who makes the first move always wins. On a 3x3 board it is easy to win if the first move is made to the center of the board (Fig. 4). “Black” can think about developments in ways by occupying any of the two hexagons,

On a 4x4 board everything is much more complicated. The player who starts the game wins for sure only if he immediately occupies one of the four space cells (Fig. 5). Having made the first move to any other cell, he will certainly lose. By starting the game on squares 2 or 3, the first player will win on the fifth move; starting from cells 1 or 4 - on the sixth.

For a 5x5 board, it can also be proven that if the first player immediately occupies the central square, then he can win on the seventh move. For large documents, the analysis becomes too complex. A standard 11x11 board contains an astronomical number of conditions, and a complete analysis of the game of hex on such a board is beyond the limits of man of all furnaces.

Game theorists consider hex a particularly interesting game. Indeed, when playing on a standard board, it is not known what tactics need to be followed in order to be sure of winning. However, by proof by contradiction it can be shown quite elegantly that there is always a winning strategy for the first player on a board of any size.

One of the oldest topological puzzles known to any schoolchild is to draw a continuous line that intersects once each of the 16 links of the closed network of straight line segments depicted on figure 6. The curve drawn on this figure cannot serve as a solution to the puzzle because it does not intersect one link of the network.

When constructing a solution, you cannot use any tricks - drawing a curve through the vertices of the network, along its links, folding a sheet of paper, etc.

It is not difficult to prove that this puzzle has no solution on the plane. Two questions arise: can it be solved on a sphere? Is there a solution on the surface of the torus?
