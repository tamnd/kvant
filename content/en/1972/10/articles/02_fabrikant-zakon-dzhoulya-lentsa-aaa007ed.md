JOULE-LENZ LAW

V. A. Fabrikant

Heating of a conductor when an electric current passes is one of the most associated effects. However, a more careful analysis of the mechanism of the appearance of the Joule body shows that not everything is so simple here.

The article discusses the phenomenon associated with the Joule-Lenz effect. In the plasma of an electric discharge in gases, two temperatures can exist in the same volume, which is well specified hundreds of times. Such plasma is a mixture of two gases with sharply different temperatures. These temperatures cannot become equal due to the fact that the electric field heats only one of the gases and there is a reliable “fur coat” between the gases.

The engineer who calculates linear long-distance power transmission and the engineer who created a powerful electric furnace have diametrically opposite points of view on the Joule body that is released in the conductor when current passes. For the first, this has a harmful effect, forcing you to increase the voltage and dream of a superconducting cable; for the second, it is a useful effect that allows one to solve difficult technical problems. However, they agree that the effect this spring is important.

The law according to which a conductor is heated by electric current was first experimentally established in 1841-42. by the English physicist James Joule and, independently of him, somewhat later (in 1842-43), but with much greater accuracy, by the St. Petersburg academician Emilius Christanovich Lenz.

Having begun to study the release of a body during the passage of current, Joule became interested in a more general problem - the transformation of energy. In particular, he took up the transformation of work into tello, using electric current as an intermediate link *).

For his first experiments, Joule made a fairly simple device, consisting of a glass jar filled with water, in which a glass tube with a conductor wound around it and a thermometer for measuring the temperature of the water was lowered. The source of electrical power is a battery of galvanic cells. The increase in water temperature over a certain period of time during the passage of current was measured.

Having established the basic laws of heating a conductor during the passage of current, Joule came to the conclusion that this effect would arise due to thermal conductivity. According to his hypothesis, the body released in a galvanic battery, a consequence of thermal conductivity, will spread along the conductor and heat it.

*) Along the way, Joule came to the conclusion that electric motors will not be able to compete with steam engines due to the high cost of generating electric current! The conclusion for the middle of the 19th century is quite reasonable.

To test this hypothesis, Joule built a more complex device for the city. Instead of a galvanic battery, this device used an induction generator with a stater in the form of a powerful electromagnet to generate current, between the poles of which a rotor rotated in a state of water. The heating of water was measured with the rotor winding closed and open. The difference in these heatings, very high, was caused by the thermal effect of the current induced in the rotor winding. Joule was convinced of the fallacy of his hypothesis about the role of thermal conductivity and, when SEL to the correct source of the phenomenon, heating of conductors *).

Establishing the exact law of heat release during the passage of electric current was far from a simple experimental task for a physicist in the first half of the 19th century. E. H. Lenz approached the solution of this problem with great care as a first-class experimenter. He began by creating new measuring instruments and calibrating them, which required a lot of work.

In particular, to measure current, he built a so-called multitipator - a device in which the measured current passes through a stationary winding consisting of several turns of wire. A magnetic needle is suspended in the center of the winding. The rotation of the arrow is associated with the amount of current passing through the winding. Before multipictors, devices with a winding consisting of one turn were used. It is clear that the sensitivity of multitypes was much higher.

An unpleasant property of multitypes was that the magnetic needle near the equilibrium position

Joule's experiences with this device played a large role in substantiating the law of conservation of energy. Therefore, the energy wave unit deservedly bears his name.

The role of conservation laws

In the seemingly smooth derivation of the Joule-Lenz formula, there is a place that is not very clear. Why does an electron, when colliding, give off energy to the atom that is exactly equal to the additional energy acquired in the electric field?

Let's look at this question more closely.

When solving any physical problem, one must remember the structural laws of conservation. According to the laws of conservation of momentum and energy, an electron during a collision must give up a certain share of its kinetic energy to the atomic wave. It is not clear why this fraction of the total kinetic energy of the electron should be exactly equal to the additional energy of the electron acquired in the electric field.

We will assume that electrons and atoms collide like elastic balls.

Let's calculate what fraction of the total kinetic energy the electron should give to the atom at the central point (Fig. 2, a).

Let the electron move at speed $v$ before the collision, and let the atom end. Let us denote the speed of the electron after the collision as $v_1$, and the speed of the atom as $V_1$. Then, according to the law of conservation of momentum:

$$mv = mv_1 + MV_1,$$

Since $M \gg m$, $m$ can be neglected as a denominator compared to $M$. Then:

$$\frac{MV_1^2}{2} = \frac{4m}{M} \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

With non-central impacts (Fig. 2, b), the atom will receive a smaller share of the initial electron energy. When an electron flies tangent to the “surface”.atoma (Fig. 2, s), the transferred energy will be zero. The average energy fraction is defined as half the maximum fraction and zero, in other words, it is equal to half the maximum fraction. So, the average energy received by atoms from electrons at each collision is equal to

$$\frac{MV^2}{2} = \frac{2m}{M} \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

(10)

Since $m/M$ is of the order of $10^{-3}$ - $10^{-5}$, the electron can give only an insignificant part of its impact to the atom. This creates a kind of “fur coat” that is associated with the exchange of energy between electrons and atoms.

From the reversibility of elastic collisions in time, it follows that an atom striking an electron can give the electron only the same small part of its initial kinetic energy. It is not difficult to verify this by simple calculation.

Another way to calculate the joule of a body

In a conductor, not only free electrons move, but also atoms, performing a chaotic oscillation around their equilibrium positions.

It is necessary to consider the collision between two groups of moving particles.

In some collisions, electrons separate part of their energy from atoms; in others, on the contrary, atoms separate part of their energy from electrons. Thanks to the action of the electric field, the electrons are constantly “warmed up”, receiving additional energy. As a result, the electron gas has a slightly higher temperature $T_e$ than the temperature of the atoms $T_a$. On average, electrons release more energy from atoms than they receive from them.

As has already been shown, with each collision the electron can separate $2\frac{m}{M}$ of its kinetic energy. The same applies to atoms.

Let us use the usual expression for the average particle energy in terms of temperature. Then

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2} kT_e,$$

$$\frac{MV^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2} kT_a.$$

The energy flow that determines the heating of atoms is equal to the difference between two energy flows - the flow flowing from electrons to atoms, and the flow from atoms to electrons. It is clear that this resultant energy flow must be proportional to the number of collided electrons with atoms $\frac{N}{\tau}$, the energy transfer coefficient $2\frac{m}{M}$ and the difference in the average energies of electrons and atoms $\frac{3}{2} k(T_e - T_a)$.

Rigorous calculation shows that there is no need to enter any numerical coefficient into the final expression for the resulting energy flow, that is

$$W = \frac{N}{\tau} \frac{2m}{M} \frac{3}{2} k(T_e - T_a).$$

In formula (11) $W$ is Joule heat, but here it is expressed differently than in formula (3).

In the absence of an external electric field, there exists between electrons and atoms. The electron temperature is equal to the temperature of the atoms.

At thermal equilibrium ($T_e = T_a$), the resulting energy flux should be zero. The action of an electric field on electrons causes a violation of this thermal equilibrium.

By equating the right-hand sides of equalities (3) and (11) (we can do this, since they characterize the same quantity):

$$\frac{1}{2} \frac{e^2}{m} N\tau E^2 = \frac{N}{\tau} \frac{2m}{M} \frac{3}{2} k(T_e - T_a).$$

From here, for the difference between the electron and atomic temperatures, we obtain:

$$T_e - T_a = \frac{1}{6} \frac{M}{m} \frac{e^2\tau^2}{km} E^2.$$

Thus, as a result of the action of the electric field, a certain temperature difference is established. We see that this difference is proportional to the mass ratio $M/m$.

This circumstance compensates for the inverse mass ratio in formula (10) and makes the original derivation of the Joule-Lenz formula, in which $M/m$ does not appear at all, a law.

It should be noted that in the first moments of time after the application of an electric field to the conductor, equality (12) is not satisfied. The energy received by electrons from the electric field some time exceeds the energy given by electrons to atoms. As a result, $T_e$ grows extremely quickly, which in turn leads to an increase in the energy given off by electrons to atoms. This will continue, very much so, until the energy received by electrons from the field and the energy given to them by atoms are equal. After this, electrons in collisions with atoms will give them energy,

equal to the additional kinetic energy acquired in the electric field during the time t between two collisions. The electron temperature stabilizes to the level determined by equality (13).

It produces approximately the same effect when heating a house in winter. All the heat coming from the heating into the rooms goes through the walls of the house to the street. Otherwise, the temperature in the room would rise all the time. Heating is necessary to maintain the temperature difference between rooms and the street. The lower the thermal conductivity of the walls, the slower the heat leaves the room, the easier it is to maintain the temperature difference.

The electron gas and atoms in the conductor represent, as it were, two bodies with different temperatures, but moved in the same volume, and one of the “tells” is heated by an electric field. Tello, as always, moves from a hotter body (electrons) to a colder one (atoms).

The conductivity of the “wall” separating these two “tells” is determined by the coefficient preceding the temperature difference in formula (11).

Let us estimate the difference between the electronic and atomic temperatures for a metal conductor.

Time t is of the order of $10^{-14}$ s, $E$ is of the order of $10^{-3}$ b/cm. For a temperature difference of $T_e - T_a$ we obtain values ​​of the order of $10^{-9}$ °C!

Such an insignificant temperature difference is enough to provide the required resulting energy flow, despite the flat energy transfer coefficient at each collision between electrons and atoms. This is explained by the colossal number of collisions between electrons and atoms: $\frac{N}{\tau}$ of the order of $10^{36}$ s$^{-1}$.

In semiconductors and especially in gas plasmas, things may be completely different.

Nervous Plasma

Strictly speaking, the classical representation electrons we used do not apply to metallic conductors. The behavior of electrons in such conductors is of a purely quantum nature$^*$). Classical concepts are valid under the conditions of the passage of electric current through gases (gas plasma), but in gases completely different relationships can take place, and the Ohm and Joule-Lenz laws often lose their validity.

When an electric current passes through a rarefied gas, due to the large length of the free electron decay, the decay time t can be several orders of magnitude longer than in metals. $E$ also increases by several orders of magnitude.

As a result, the electronic temperature can reach ten thousand degrees while the temperature of the gas itself (atoms) is several hundred degrees (for example, in fluorescent or advertising lamps). The result is a mixture of two gases with temperatures differing ten or even hundreds of times. There can be no talk of any thermal equilibrium under such conditions. Such a plasma is called nonequilibrium.

It is clear that the smallness of the ratio $m/M$ favors the establishment of a larger temperature difference between the electrons and atoms of the nonequilibrium plasma.

Under conditions of a low-pressure electric gas discharge, Ohm's law is violated, since m and $N$ depends on $E$. Due to the higher energy of electrons, not only elastic, but also inelastic collisions of electrons with atoms can occur. In inelastic collisions, the kinetic energy of electrons is converted into the internal energy of atoms.

Finally, in some high-current discharge devices created for the purpose of obtaining controlled thermonuclear reactions, the inverse temperature ratio takes place (Fig. 3). The temperature of atoms (more precisely, ions) turns out to be higher than the electron temperature. The resulting energy flow flows in the opposite direction - from atoms to electrons. Under these conditions, electrons do not heat, but cool the atoms.
