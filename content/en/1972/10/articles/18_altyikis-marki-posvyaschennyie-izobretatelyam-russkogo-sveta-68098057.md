⟦folio 77⟧

BRANDS,
DEDICATED TO INVENTORS
"RUSSIAN LIGHT"

This year marks the 125th anniversary of the birth of two remarkable Russian electrical engineers and inventors P. N. Yablochkov (1847-1894) and A. N. Lodygin (1847-1923).

Back in 1823, the Russian physicist Vladimir Vasilyevich Petrov first described the “light phenomenon” he discovered - an electric arc between two coals, giving “a very bright light and flame”, from which “a dark peace can be quite clearly illuminated.”

However, at that time, the Petrov arc did not receive practical use for lighting, since its normal combustion required continuous monitoring of the coals. Someone had to control this light all the time, bringing the coals closer together as they burned.

For the first time in 1875, Pavel Nikolaevich Yablochkov created an “electric candle” suitable for practical use, whose portrait you see on a stamp issued in 1951 in the series “Scientists of our Motherland.”

To the right of the portrait is a “candle”.

These are two vertically placed coals separated by a clay lining capable of firing at the same speed as the coals. The result is a source of electric light that does not require special maintenance or regulation. The upper ends of the coals were connected by a thin ignition plate. When the current was turned on, the plate accumulated and burned, lighting the candle. The candle provided a dewy and steady light for almost two hours.

Parisian and London newspapers and magazines enthusiastically wrote: “Russian light” brightly illuminates the warm Tatras of Tattle, the Louvre museums, the Opera Square in Paris, the port in Le Havre, the Thames embankment in London, and the capital’s churches. In Paris alone, a thousand “Russian candles” flowed from the streets of 70 thousand gas jets.

In St. Petersburg, “Yablochkov candles” were used for lighting only in 1879. However, they were already doomed, since another famous Russian electrical engineer, Alexander Nikolaevich Lodygin, whose portrait you see on a stamp from the same series, created an electric storage lamp that was more convenient than a “candle”. This world's first storage lamp is depicted on the stamp to the right of the portrait.

In A. N. Lodygin’s lamp, an electric current is accumulated by a thin carbon rod placed under a glass cover. The lamp life was 30-40 minutes. After applying a vacuum and other improvements, the lamp life was increased to 700-1000 hours.

In the 1890s, Lodygin created an accumulation lamp with metal filaments, in particular tungsten. These lamps turned out to be much more economical than a lamp with carbon fibers.

P. N. Yablochkov and A. N. Lodygin also wrote a number of works on electrical engineering, but their main merit is that they were the first in the world to implement the practical use of electricity for lighting.

A. V. Altykis
