QUANTUM LABORATORY

BOOMERANG

F. Hess

Apparently, it is difficult to find a person who would not be surprised by the trajectory described by the boomerang. And, of course, many would really like to make a boomerang and figure out why it comes back. But the boomerang is not just a toy. A heavy boomerang is quite dangerous. Therefore, we invite our readers to make a boomerang from lightweight materials, for example, cardboard.

BELOW we publish an abbreviated version of the article published in the November 1968 issue of Scientific American magazine. The article was translated and prepared for publication by V. I. Bakhmin.

Imagine that a piece of wood that you threw flies in a circular path, then returns and calmly lies at your feet. Absurd! However, this is exactly how a boomerang behaves, of course, if you make it correctly and throw it correctly.

The boomerang was first made by the native inhabitants of Australia. The reader will probably be a little disappointed to learn that most Australian boomerangs do not return. Boomerangs can be roughly divided into two types: military and returning. Boomerangs of the first type are used as weapons for war or hunting. A good military boomerang flies very far, but does not return. Returning boomerangs are used almost exclusively for gaming.

In fact, understanding all the existing types of boomerangs is not as easy as it might seem. There are many types of native weapons in Australia. Different tribes have different boomerang shapes (Fig. 1).

It is not easy to determine from its appearance whether a given boomerang is returning. As a rule, the boomerang returns less massive, and the angle between its shoulders is more acute. In general, they range in length from 25 to 75 centimeters, width from three to five centimeters and thickness from half to one and a half centimeters. The angle between the arms of the boomerang can vary from 80 to 140 degrees. Everything reaches 300 grams.
