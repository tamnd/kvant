The role of conservation laws

In the seemingly smooth derivation of the Joule-Lenz formula, there is a place that is not very clear. Why does an electron, when colliding, give off energy to the atom that is exactly equal to the additional energy acquired in the electric field?

Let's look at this question more closely.

When solving any physical problem, one must remember the structural laws of conservation. According to the laws of conservation of momentum and energy, an electron during a collision must give up a certain share of its kinetic energy to the atomic wave. It is not clear why this fraction of the total kinetic energy of the electron should be exactly equal to the additional energy of the electron acquired in the electric field.

We will assume that electrons and atoms collide like elastic balls.

Let's calculate what fraction of the total kinetic energy the electron should give to the atom at the central point (Fig. 2, a).

Let the electron move at speed $v$ before the collision, and let the atom end. Let us denote the speed of the electron after the collision as $v_1$, and the speed of the atom as $V_1$. Then, according to the law of conservation of momentum:

$$mv = mv_1 + MV_1,$$

and according to the law of conservation of energy:

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{mv_1^2}{2} + \frac{MV_1^2}{2}.$$

From these two equations we get:

$$\frac{MV_1^2}{2} = \frac{4Mm}{(m+M)^2} \cdot \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

(8)

Since $M \gg m$, $m$ can be neglected as a denominator compared to $M$. Then:

$$\frac{MV_1^2}{2} = \frac{4m}{M} \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

(9)

With non-central impacts (Fig. 2, b), the atom will receive a smaller share of the initial electron energy. When an electron flies tangent to the “surface”.atoma (Fig. 2, s), the transferred energy will be zero. The average energy fraction is defined as half the maximum fraction and zero, in other words, it is equal to half the maximum fraction. So, the average energy received by atoms from electrons at each collision is equal to

$$\frac{MV^2}{2} = \frac{2m}{M} \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

(10)

Since $m/M$ is of the order of $10^{-3}$ - $10^{-5}$, the electron can give only an insignificant part of its impact to the atom. This creates a kind of “fur coat” that is associated with the exchange of energy between electrons and atoms.

From the reversibility of elastic collisions in time, it follows that an atom striking an electron can give the electron only the same small part of its initial kinetic energy. It is not difficult to verify this by simple calculation.
