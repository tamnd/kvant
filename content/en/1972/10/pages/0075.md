When answering, the jury gives the floor to the answerer and gives the opponent the right to turn the answerer anywhere in order to ask him a question or ask him to repeat an unclear place. Neither the decision, nor the jury, nor the rest of the team (and especially the cheerleaders) have the right to insert the answerer and opponent into the dialogue or ask questions. Only if the discussion between the respondent and the opponent drags on and goes sideways, the jury has the right to turn it around and pass the word on the decision. The reviewer usually does not ask questions to the answerer or the opponent, but only comments on the progress of the decision and the opposition. Only after the decision has been delivered, the jury members have the right to ask questions to the respondent.

Each task, regardless of its difficulty, is worth 12 points, which are distributed between the answerer, opponent and solution depending on the content of their performances. If some challenge was detected, then penalty points are allocated from the same 12 points. For a certain challenge, team A each from team B and C receives from 2 to 6 points. Team A had to carry out this type of punishment after several fights ended with negative scores.

Very often, one team gets all 12 points: either team B tells a simple problem, so A and C have nothing to add, or A calls B on an obviously difficult problem, B refuses, and then neither B nor C has anything significant to say about the solution, which they are not yet prepared to comment on.

The problem of incorrect calls causes a lot of trouble for the jury. Team A, forced to respond if B refuses, can either tell an obviously incorrect but “plausible” solution, or an incomplete, partial, or illegal solution. Since it can be difficult to distinguish a well-disguised conscious error in a decision from a “real” one, it is generally accepted that any call in which Team A is found to have a significant error in the decision or no decision at all is considered incorrect. The amount of the fine depends on who replaced the error: the respondent himself, the opponent, the decision or the jury. A solution is considered correct if none of the jury members manages to detect an error in it before the score for this problem is discovered; if the error is found after the account is discovered, the account will still remain the same. However, if the jury is unsure of the decision, but cannot immediately indicate the error, then the score is not explained until a specially designated jury member is convinced of the presence or absence of an error.
