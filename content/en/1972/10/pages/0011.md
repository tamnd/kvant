Indexes

The use of peniform roots is based on the following fact. Let $g$ be a periform root modulo $p$; $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{p-2}$ are the remainders from dividing the numbers 1, $g, g^2, \dots, g^{p-2}$ by $p$. Then all $a_j$ are distinct and all are nonzero: therefore $a_0, \dots, a_{p-2}$ are all non-zero elements of $Z_p$. As a result, every non-zero element $b \in Z_p$ corresponds to a unique $k \in Z_{p-1}$, for which $g^k \equiv b$ (mod $p$); $k$ is called the index $b$ relative to $g$ and is denoted by $ind_g b$ (Table 5). It is obvious that $ind_g ab = ind_g a + ind_g b$; $ind_g \frac{a}{b} = ind_g a - ind_g b$, where the following two operations are performed in $Z_p$, and on the right in $Z_{p-1}$. For this reason, indices in $p$-arithmetic play a role similar to logarithms; with their help, multiplication and division have properties like addition and multiplication. Textbooks on number theory provide detailed tables of indices, and, as in logarithmic tables, both tables for determining indices by elements $Z_p$ and tables for determining elements by indices are given.

We will limit ourselves to one example of using indexes. Consider the comparison $ax^l \equiv b$ (mod $p$), $(a, p) = 1$. The question of its solvability can be investigated directly, but if we go to the indices, we get a comparison of the first degree with respect to $y = ind_g x$: $ly = c$ (mod $p-1$), $c = ind_g b - ind_g a$. Since there is a one-to-one correspondence between nonzero remainders and indices, the same correspondence exists between the nonzero solutions of the first comparison and the solutions of the second.
