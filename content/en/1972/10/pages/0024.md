MOUNTAIN HEIGHT
AND FUNDAMENTAL
PHYSICAL CONSTANTS

This excerpt is taken from a lecture by the famous theoretical physicist W. Weisskopf, which he gave at the European Center for Nuclear Research (CERN) in 1969. The lecture was called “Modern physics in elementary presentation.” It was published in full in the journal “Advances in Physical Sciences,” volume 103, no. 1, 1971.

Translation by V. I. Rydnik.

We know that there are both high and low mountains on Earth. But why is the highest mountain (Chomolungma) only about 10 km high? Why not, say, fifty times more?

During the processes of mountain building and weathering, mountains are destroyed and many of them “lose” their height. What does it mean, however, that there is an upper “limit” to their height of the order of 10 km = 10⁶ cm? It turns out that this value is determined by the very physical existence of the solid substance of rocks. It is also related to such quantities as the acceleration of gravity and the number of protons and neutrons in the Earth's matter, which is approximately 3·10⁵¹.

So why can't mountains grow as high as they want? If a mountain turns out to be too high, it begins to “sink” into the earth’s crust below it, since the substances that make up the earth’s crust - granite, quartz, silicon dioxide - are not strong enough to “hold” a mountain whose height is greater than the defining limit. The weight of such a mountain turns out to be so large that it will disrupt the direction of communication between

with the atoms of rocks, as a result of which the rocks will melt, become liquid and be able to spread to the sides. This causes the mountain to sink. As the mountain sinks into the earth's crust, part of the potential energy of the mountain is released in the earth's gravitational field. This excess turns into the energy of “burning” the rock.

Let the mountain's initial height be h, and the mountain drop to a height x (see figure 1; the solid and dashed lines show the initial and final positions of the mountain). Let us take the mass of the mountain to be M.

As already mentioned, the decrease in the potential energy of a mountain in the earth’s gravitational field is equal to the energy of “burning” the mass of the mountain contained in the volume of height x, i.e.

$$Mgx = E_{0ж} \cdot nxS,$$

or

$$Mg = E_{0ж} \cdot nS,$$

where n is the number of molecules per unit volume of the mountain, S is the area of ​​the base of the mountain, $E_{0ж}$ is the energy of “burning” (i.e., latent heat of fusion) per one molecule, $\dot{g}$ is the acceleration of gravity.
