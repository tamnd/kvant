Now we move on to an important question: how will the boomerang move under the influence of all these forces and moments (and gravity, of course)?

As mentioned earlier, the average moment $M_1$ causes gyroscopic precession of the boomerang. Let's take a closer look at the gyroscope. If the gyroscope is rotated around its axis at a speed $\omega$ and a moment $M$ acts on it, directed along an axis perpendicular to the rotation axis, then the gyroscope begins to prepress, and the precession axis is perpendicular to both the rotation axis and the moment axis $M$ (Fig. 9). The angular velocity of precession is denoted by $\Omega$. There is a very strong lift force between $\omega$, $\Omega$, $M$ and the gyro moment of inertia $I^*$. Therefore, if you want to boom-

$$\Omega = M/I\omega.$$

For a boomerang, the moment $M$ is proportional to $\omega V$, so the precession speed $\Omega$ must be proportional

$$\frac{\omega V}{I\omega} = \frac{V}{I}.$$

Consequently, the speed of precession does not depend on the speed of rotation of the boomerang $\omega$.

An even stranger conclusion can be drawn. The rate of precession is proportional to $V/I$; the proportionality coefficient depends on the shape of the boomerang. Therefore, we can write

$$\Omega = CV,$$

where $C$ is the characteristic parameter of the boomerang. Now let the speed of the boomerang be doubled.

Consequently, the position of its plane will also change twice as fast. This shows, among other things, that the boomerang will fly along the same curve.

Thus, the diameter of the boomerang’s orbit, roughly speaking, does not depend on either the speed of rotation of the boomerang or its translational speed. This means that the length of the boomerang path is more or less constant.
