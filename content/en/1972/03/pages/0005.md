To the 200th anniversary of his death
Isaac Newton
A. Eystein

March 1972 marks the 245th anniversary of the death of the great English physicist and mathematician Isaac Newton. In this regard, we post a short article by another great physicist, Albert Einstein, published in 1927. The article is given with slight abbreviations. The full text of the article was published in Russian in the fourth volume of the collected scientific works of Einstein (Nauka publishing house, 1967).

It is not difficult to characterize Newton’s scientific activities, which forever ensured his special place in the history of human spiritual development. Newton was the first to try to formulate elementary laws that determine the time course of a wide class of processes in nature with a high degree of completeness and accuracy. His law of motion, together with the law of gravity, governs the movement of all celestial bodies, occurring under the influence of forces of mutual acceptance. Thus, Newton fulfilled the dreams of the ancient material philosophers - Democritus and Epicurus, who believed that there should be a fundamental interconnection of all physical phenomena without exception. After these successes, was there any doubt left that the development of all material phenomena in general produces the necessary regularity, which could be compared with the winding of a clock. In addition, it became obvious that the processes of thinking must be inextricably linked with the material processes occurring in the brain, and therefore the idea became inevitable that the thinking and desires of humans and animals should be based on the same strictly causal laws. Thus, Newton expressed his worldview as a whole.

Now we know that gravity is not the only force operating in nature. Gravity cannot explain the cohesion forces in bodies, electrical forces, or light. However, Newton's theory of motion, apparently, can serve as a completely sufficient foundation for understanding any physical process, if we assume that between particles of matter, in addition to gravitational forces, forces of a completely different kind also act. This expansion of the theory of motion was begun by Newton himself, who applied it, for example, to the theory of light.

Thus, Newton laid the foundations of that set of laws of nature that allows the laws of all phenomena. Newton believed that this could be achieved by reducing any processes to the movements of particles interacting with each other. This program lasted until the second half of the 18th century and proved its fruitfulness in the field of physics.
