It would seem that here the polyknei will no longer be able to surround the crosses, and especially if the second one puts only one polykne on each cross. The last statement is contained in the vast majority of letters.

What if the second one tries to organize a defense far from the first cross? Will he be able to at least put the crosses up? It turns out yes! And even if the second one answers each cross with only one answer. This strategy of the second (we will also need it for the final solution of the problem) is called “reflecting a love attack.”

So, we are trying to keep the crosses moving upward. Look at figure 5. It would seem impossible not to miss the crosses above the horizontal line, because after six moves the crosses can end up on any of the 13 squares marked with dots, and the second one can only place 6 crosses during this time. Now let's look at figure 6. The game has begun, and with each move the crosses threaten fewer and fewer fields behind the horizontal line.

After the second move there will be one square, after the third - nine, after four - seven. But the second one manages to place the polikki in even moves so that on the threatened fields they stand across the field. (Try to prove on your own that with any game of the first, the second can do this.)

Now, after the fifth move of the crosses, one of the two situations shown in figure 7 will result. In case a) the second one can place polykki in the two remaining unfilled cells in any order, and in case b) he first puts the polyk in the middle cell, and then, depending on the move of the first, to the left or to the right, and the path is blocked for the crosses.

So, the “love attack” has been repulsed! the second one may not miss the crosses

above the sixth horizontal line (from the one where the first cross is).
