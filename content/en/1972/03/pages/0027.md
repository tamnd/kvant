And so on, each time the crosses are placed on the free fields adjacent to the one where the crosses already stand, and the shelves are placed on any free fields. The beginner is asked to put as many crosses as possible, and the second one is asked to do this.

In this version, our readers solved a lot of the problem. They showed that a beginner can put no more than 7 crosses. figure 1 shows the regimental strategy on the first move that achieves this result. Indeed, after this the beginner can put a cross on one of the five fields. It is not difficult to see that only by placing it on the south-eastern field, the first can hope to put seven crosses in a fair game, because with the response of the regiments shown in figure 2, a, he can only hope to put crosses on the fields marked with dots, and of the two fields marked with red dots, he will be able to put a cross on no more than one. How the second one should place the shelves is clear from figure 2, 6.

If the beginner puts a second cross on another field, then after the second move, one of the situations shown in figure 3 occurs, where there are only 4 fields (they are also marked with dots) on which the beginner can put a cross if the second one plays correctly. It consists in cutting off all the advancement fields from the cross point placed together, similar to how it was done in the first case (Fig. 2, 6).

Let us now try the conditions of the regiment problem. Let's allow the second one to place not 3 tiles each, but only 2. It is interesting that if from the very beginning he tries to place shelves on the cells adjacent to the crosses, then the second one will not be able to delay the movement of the crosses. Look at figure 4. Here the shelves cannot place crosses moving up (red signs - first move, black - second, green - third).

This is clear, since with each move the regiments cover only two of the three fields leading up.
