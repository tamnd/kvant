But one cannot judge the importance of any mathematical research by individual particular problems. Topology can serve as an example of this. Topology is sometimes called “rubber geometry”—the geometry of figures; for example, topology can serve this purpose. In some cases this is true - it actually considers the properties of such figures. But its significance is due to the fact that there is no constant length on an elastic sheet. There cannot be any theorems similar to the Pythagorean theorem. Is it possible to draw conclusions like: “this curve is continuous, and that one is divided into two separate parts.” Continuity is the main property studied by topology. It talks about properties that are preserved under continuous transformations. Since there are so many things that can be transformed in this way, topology covers a wide range of topics. It increasingly attracts both pure mathematicians and engineers; it can be used to prove a number of surprising results. It combines the highest degree of generalization with maximum simplicity.

Everything we talked about above has the goal of expanding the scope of questions that are subject to mathematics. Research, discovery of patterns, explanation of the meaning of each pattern, depiction of new patterns based on the model of already known ones—all these types of activities expand the scope of mathematics. From a practical point of view it becomes extremely difficult to keep track of all the results obtained, and it cannot be said that the accumulation of unrelated theorems represents a welcome solution. Being both business people and artists, mathematicians feel the need to collect all these disparate results.

It is not surprising that the entire history of mathematics consists of alternating processes of “expansion” and “contraction.” For example, some problem attracts the attention of mathematicians, hundreds of articles are written, each of which covers only one side of the truth. The question is growing. Then some genius, relying on all the data, the collection with such difficulty, declares: “Everything that we know will become almost very similar if you look at it from this point of view.” After this, there is no longer any need for anyone, except historians of mathematics, to study hundreds of individual articles. Disparate conclusions are united into one simple doctrine, important facts are separated from the chaff, and the direct path to the desired conclusion is open to all. The amount of information that needs to be studied has been reduced. But this is not the end. After a new method has become worthy, new questions arise for which it is not sufficient to solve, and the search for answers begins again, articles are published again, and the process of “extension” begins again.

If it were possible to lead knowledge to two general laws, the mathematician would not be a Udoble. He would not rest until he had proved that both of these laws were based on the same principle. But even then he would be happy; on the contrary, he would become unhappy, since he would have nothing to do. But the prospect of such a disruption is completely unreal. Life is such that solving one problem always creates a new problem; otherwise life was unbearable. There is and always will be material to study and difficulties to overcome.

And it will always be like this. And if it turned out that all that is essential in mathematics is considering only phenomena with properties A, B and C, mathematicians would immediately ask: “What will happen if some object has only one of them?” And they would plunge back into work.
