On June 1, 1796, a note with the following content appeared in the newspaper Jenen-ser Intelligenzbbatt.

Every novice geometer knows that it is possible with geometric tools (that is, with a compass and a ruler) to construct various regular polygons, namely, a triangle, a pentagon, a decagon, and those that are obtained from each of them by successively doubling the number of its sides. This was known in the time of Euclid, and since then it seems that the conviction has been widespread that the field of elementary geometry does not extend further: at least I do not know of a successful attempt to extend it in this direction...

Moreover, the discovery that, in addition to these regular polygons, many others can be geometrically constructed, for example, a seventeen-sided polygon, seems worthy of attention to me.

The note is signed:
K. F. Gauss from Braunschweig, student of mathematics in Göttingen.

About construction with compasses and ruler

A segment of unit length is assumed to be given. Then, using a compass and a ruler, you can construct new segments, the lengths of which are obtained from the lengths of existing segments using the following operations: addition (Fig. 1a), subtraction (Fig. 1b), multiplication (Fig. 1b), division (Fig. 12) and square root extraction (Fig. 1d).

Having carried out these operations sequentially, using a compass and a ruler, you can construct any segment, the length of which is expressed through a single number of addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and square root operations. Such numbers are called quadratic irrational. It can be proven that no other segments can be constructed using a compass and ruler - but this is a topic for a special discussion.
