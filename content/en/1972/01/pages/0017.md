Thus, the question of artificial cooling methods comes down to the question of what to expand and how to expand.

What to expand?

It is clear that solids are unsuitable for expansion - they cannot change their volume in any noticeable way. For the same reasons, liquids are also unsuitable. Although the increase in the volume of liquid during their evaporation is used to cool them, especially low temperatures cannot be obtained in this way, because, as it cools, the liquid eventually solidifies. So the lowest temperature that can be obtained when a liquid evaporates is the temperature at which it solidifies. And for substances that are liquid at room temperature, the solidification temperatures are not so low.

It is best to use gases. Gases are capable of unlimited expansion, and if they are also previously at high pressure, then their mass is quite large and therefore the heat capacity of this gas is also quite large. Gases are also characterized by the greatest disorder in the movement of particles. And since our task is to reduce disorder, it is clear that the best ones are to start with a substance in which the disorder is great, so that there is something to reduce!

So, the most suitable substance for our task is gas. On the other hand, the final product, that is, the finished refrigerant, is best in the form of a liquid: a liquid always creates good thermal contact with the body immersed in it. Therefore, the technical part (but not exclusively) of the liquefaction properties of a particular gas.

As you know, any gas can be converted into a liquid. But for each gas there is a certain temperature, called the criterion $T_h$, above which it cannot be formed into a liquid state. In order for gas to be liquefied, its temperature must be below $T_h$. Then to burn it you only need to rock it. And to what extent it depends on how much its temperature is below $T_h$: the lower it is, the less pressure is needed for combustion. You can cool a gas so much that it becomes liquid at a pressure of 1 atmosphere.

To obtain moderate cold, gases with high $T_h$ values ​​are used. Often used, for example, ammonia, which has $T_h = 132,4^\circ\text{C}$. Recently, everyone has been using so-called freons - gases obtained from hydrocarbons by mixing hydrogen with fluorine, chlorine or bromine. In home refrigerators, in particular, freon-12 ($\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2$), which has $T_h = 112,04^\circ\text{C}$, is used.

The lowest temperatures for the production of gases, the critical temperatures of which are much lower than room temperature. These include: oxygen, which has $T_h = -118,4^\circ\text{C}$, nitrogen with $T_h = -146,9^\circ\text{C}$, hydrogen with $T_h = -239,9^\circ\text{C}$, and finally helium, which has the lowest critical temperature in nature. He has $T_h = -267,91^\circ\text{C}$.

Burning these gases is not so easy. To do this, they need to be very cool.
