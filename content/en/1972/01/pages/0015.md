Order and disorder in matter

Let us remember that the temperature of a body is determined by the energies of the random thermal motion of its atoms and molecules. To lower the temperature means to reduce the disorder that reigns among the molecules.

What is disorder? If every item in your apartment is always in a certain place assigned to it and it is always easy to find, then you maintain order in your apartment. If things are classified anywhere, then they cannot be found. Your apartment is a mess.

The larger the size of your apartment, the more time it will take you to find this or that thing; if things are evenly scattered, the more disorder there is in your apartment. Imagine that things in the room can move on their own, changing their position. Then it will become even more difficult to find them, and the more difficult it is, the higher the speed of their movement.

This everyday example helps us understand how to determine what order and disorder are in the molecular world. The degree of disorder is determined by the probability of finding molecules (or another particle) in a certain state, for example, at a certain point in space.

If there is a given number of particles uniformly distributed in a given volume, then the probability of detecting a particle at a given point will be greater, the slower the particles move, the slower they change their position in a given volume. But the speeds of molecules are determined by body temperature. Therefore, the higher the temperature of a body, the greater the degree of disorder among the molecules of which it consists. It is also clear from the above that the greater the volume provided by the particles, the greater the degree of molecular disorder.

Thus, the degree of disorder depends on two factors - the temperature of the body and its volume. An increase in temperature at a given volume, just like an increase in volume at a constant temperature, leads to an increase in the degree of disorder among the molecules.

It turns out that all the processes that occur in nature, in a closed system left to itself (that is, in a system consisting of particles interacting with each other, but not with other bodies), take place in such a way that the degree of disorder increases. And when this degree of disorder reaches its highest value, all processes stop by themselves. A state occurs called the state of equilibrium.

Imagine balls of two colors arranged in a certain order. It took quite a few pages to arrange the balls in the correct sequence. But a slight push is enough for the painstakingly created order to be disrupted and the balls to be randomly mixed. And it is impossible to restore the original order by any means. The probability of such an event means nothing.

And here is an example from the atomic-molecular world. By throwing a piece of sugar into water, we can easily obtain a solution in which sugar molecules are distributed throughout the entire volume of the vessel. But no matter how much we covet, the solvent will not separate into water and sugar by itself. In order to obtain sugar and water separately, you need to expend some energy by heating the solution and evaporating the water.
