⟦folio 52⟧

REVIEWS, BIBLIOGRAPHY

This issue publishes excerpts from a recently published book by the famous English teacher and popularizer of mathematics Walter Warwick Sawyer *]. It is clear that it is extremely difficult at a sufficiently high and at the same time accessible level for a wide audience to show a panorama of important sections of modern mathematics without embellishment, without vagueness and distortion.

W. W. Sawyer is one of those authors who succeeds in this. It contains fascinating and so significant content about some of the features of the creative activity of professional mathematicians.

We hope that reading this book will give you satisfaction and give you a glimpse into the world of mathematics and mathematicians. To understand what you read, “the most vague memories of school mathematics are enough.”
The publication was prepared by V. N. Berezin and M. L. Smolyansky.

Almost all mathematical discoveries are based on a very simple idea. Textbooks often hide this fact. They usually contain cumbersome conclusions and thereby create the impression that mathematicians are people who spend their entire lives behind desks and translating tons of paper. This is nonsense. Many mathematicians work very successfully in the bath, in bed, on trains or while riding a bicycle (preferably in light traffic). Mathematical calculations are made before or after opening. The discovery itself will arise from the basic ideas.

Few people realize how vast the scope of modern mathematics is. It would probably be easier to master all existing languages ​​than all the mathematical knowledge currently known. It seems to me that all languages ​​could be learned in one lifetime; but mathematics, of course, does not have everything.

*) W. W. Sawyer, Propositions to Mathematics, M., “Enlightenment”, 1972.
