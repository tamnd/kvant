⟦folio 2⟧

S. G. Gindikin

At school, in geometry lessons, we consider the problem of constructing regular polygons using a compass and a ruler. It is easy to construct a regular square quadrilateral. It is quite easy to construct a regular triangle and an almost equally regular hexagon (its side is equal to the radius of the circle circumscribed around it). A trickier thing is to build a regular pentagon.

Having learned how to construct the indicated polygons, it is easy to move on to constructing regular octagons, 10-gons, 12-gons, 16-gons, and 20-gons. It is somewhat more difficult to construct a regular 15-gon.

All these problems were solved back in ancient Greece. However, despite all the successful efforts of the creative Greek geometers, none of them succeeded in constructing either a regular heptagon or a regular pentagon; It was not possible to construct a regular p-gon for any prime p other than 3 and 5. For two thousand years nothing could be achieved in solving this problem. In 1796, Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855) proved the possibility of constructing a regular 17-gon using a compass and ruler.

Over the next few years, Gauss completely solved the problem of constructing regular n-gons with compasses and a ruler, finding out, in particular, that such a construction is impossible for n = 7, 9, 11, 13.

This article talks about constructing a regular 17-sided triangle. The main difficulty, the reader's expectations, is not related to the fact that he will need some new information (all of which does not go beyond the scope of a school textbook), but to the fact that Gaus's construction uses a number of deep ideas. Those readers who take the trouble to understand them will be returned with one of the most astonishing discoveries in the history of mathematics.
