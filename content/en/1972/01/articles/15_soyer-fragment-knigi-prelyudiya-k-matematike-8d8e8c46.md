The following trivial problem can serve as an illustration. “One glass contains ten spoons of water, the other contains ten spoons of wine. A spoonful of water from the first glass is poured into the second. The mixture is vigorously shaken, then a spoonful of the resulting mixture is poured into the first glass. The question is, what will be more: wine in the first glass or water in the second?
Obviously, in order to answer this question, we can continue with the following calculation. After a spoonful of water was poured into the second glass, there were 10 spoons of wine and 1 spoon of water, that is, a total of 11 spoons of liquid. Thus, one spoon of the mixture contains $\frac{10}{11}$ spoons of wine and $\frac{1}{11}$ spoons of water. We pour one spoon of the mixture into the first glass: it now contains $\frac{9}{11}$ water, and $\frac{10}{11}$ spoons of wine, in the second glass there will be $\frac{10}{11}$ spoons of water and $\frac{9}{11}$ spoons of wine. So, we see that the amount of wine in the first glass is exactly equal to the amount of water in the second glass.
This result may seem random, but if you change the conditions of the problem, you will find that you will always get the same quantities. If given $x$ spoons of water and $x$ spoons of wine, then the amount of wine that gets into the first glass will invariably be equal to the amount of water that gets into the second. Next, start with the first quantities of liquid in the glasses, for example, with $x$ spoons of water and $y$ spoons of wine, and then continue all the procedures as in the original problem, then in the end there will be as much wine in the first glass as there is water in the second.

This is a good example of mathematical tastelessness. In a clear and well-mathematical proof, the result does not appear unexpectedly in the last line, its logical inevitability should be visible throughout the entire proof.

Everything we talked about above has the goal of expanding the scope of questions that are subject to mathematics. It is not surprising that the entire history of mathematics consists of alternating processes of “expansion” and “contraction.” For example, some problem attracts the attention of mathematicians, hundreds of articles are written, each of which covers only one side of the truth. The question is growing. Then some genius, relying on all the data, the collection with such difficulty, declares: “Everything that we know will become almost very similar if you look at it from this point of view.” After this, there is no longer any need for anyone, except historians of mathematics, to study hundreds of individual articles. The amount of information that needs to be studied has been reduced. But this is not the end.
