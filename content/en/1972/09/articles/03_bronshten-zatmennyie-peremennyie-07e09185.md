In 1669, the Italian mathematician and astronomer G. Montanari discovered that the star Algol (β Perseus) periodically changes its brightness. (It is possible that the variability of the brightness of this star was known to the ancient Arabs, who called it posetomu Ras Al-Ghul - “the head of the devil.” This Arabic name later turned into Algol.) We know little about Montanari’s works, since many of his works have not reached us. It is only known that he discovered the variability of the brightness not only of Algol, but of those around this star. But we don’t know which ones exactly: Montanari’s catalog was lost.

More than a hundred years later, the eighteen-year-old deaf-mute young man John Goodrike took up the observations of Algol in English amateur astronomy. From his observations for 1782-1783. he found that Algol, which usually shines as a 2nd magnitude star, weakens to magnitude 3-4 approximately every three days.

In astronomy, it is customary to express the brilliance of stars in stellar magnitudes - units proportional to the logarithm of the illumination created by the star on a plane perpendicular to its rays. The brightness of a star depends only on its luminous intensity and distance from the Earth, and does not depend on whether we observe the star with a simple eye or through a telescope, since the illumination of the eye and the telescope lens by the light of the star is naturally the same. In astronomy, brilliance is usually denoted by the letter L (luminosity), and magnitude by the letter m.

A difference of 1 magnitude corresponds to a brightness ratio of 2.512 times *). The difference between two stellar magnitudes m₁ and m₂ is equal to

$$m_2 - m_1 = 2.5 \log \frac{L_1}{L_2},$$

where L₁ and L₂ are the brightness of stars.

Observing Algol, Goodrike noticed that the decrease in its brightness to a minimum and the return increase to the “normal” value last for 4.5 hours, after which the star maintains a constant brightness for 2 days and 12 hours, and then Libra repeats the cycle again. Goodrike's scales accurately determined the period of change in the star's brightness - 2 days 20 hours 49 minutes, which is only 3 minutes different from the modern value. But the most important condition is that Goodreik correctly observes the phenomenon he observed: he suggested that here there is an eclipse of one star (bright) by another (dark), revolving around the first. Algol-type stars are called eclipsing variables.

Goodreik's hypothesis was confirmed a hundred years later.

where $T$ is the absolute temperature of the body, $b$ is a constant value. Having determined from the observed spectrum of the star $\lambda_{\max}$ and value $b$, we can find the absolute temperature of the luminous body $T$. We have already said that in Algol’s spectrum a linear shift was discovered, either to the violet or to the red end of the spectrum with a period $T$ equal to the period $P$ of the star’s brightness change. This means that the star is either approaching us or moving away from us, forming in an orbit around the center of mass of the system with a period of $T = P$.

According to the Doppler principle, the speed of closest approach or retreat of the main star $\Delta v$, the wavelength of the spectrum lines $\lambda$ and its displacement $\Delta \lambda$ are related by the relation

$$\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{\Delta v}{c},$$

where $c$ is the speed of light. Having determined from the observed spectrum of the star the change in wavelength when the ruler is shifted, we can find using formula (7) the speed of movement of the main star in its orbit. For Algol it was found that $\Delta v \approx 46\ \text{km/c}$. This value is equal to the orbital speed $v$.

Now, knowing the period $T = P \approx 68\ u$ 52 min, we can determine the radius of the star:

$$R_1 = \frac{T \cdot v}{2\pi} \approx 1,86\ (\text{мин. }\ \text{км}).$$

The radius of Algol's orbit is almost 5 times larger than the radius of the Moon's orbit.

If in the spectrum of a star spectral lines are visible not only brightly, but also of a faint component (satellite star), then the radius of the satellite’s orbit $R_2$ can be found in the same exact way. $R_2 = 8,54\ \text{мин. }\ \text{км}$ was found for Algol's satellite (Algol B). Thus, the radius of the relative orbit, that is, the orbit of the satellite of the relative main star, is $R = R_1 + R_2 = 10,4\ \text{мин. }\ \text{км}$.

Knowing the radius of the relative orbit and the period of revolution, we can use Kepler’s third law to determine the sum of the masses of both stars. In its generalized form (derived by Newton), Kepler's third law looks like this:

$$\frac{T_1^2 (M_1 + M_2)}{T_2^2 (M_3 + M_4)} = \frac{a_1^3}{a_2^3}.$$

Here $T_1$, $T_2$ are the periods of revolution around the masses in the first and second systems of bodies, $a_1$, $a_2$ are the semi-major axes of the orbits, $M_1, M_2$ are the masses of the bodies of the first system, $M_3$, $M_4$ are the masses of the bodies of the second system. Taking the first system as the Algol system, the second as the Sun-Earth system, as a unit of mass, as is nice in the physics of stars, we will take the mass of the Sun (i.e., let’s put $M_3=1$), and neglect the mass of the Earth ($M_4=0$); We replace the semimajor axes with the radii of circular orbits. Then we get:

$$M_1 + M_2 = \left(\frac{R}{R'}\right)^3 \cdot \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^2.$$

Substituting $T_1=2,87$ days, $T_2=365,24$ days, $R'=149,6\ \text{мин. }\ \text{км}$ and the value $R = 10,4\ \text{мин. }\ \text{км}$ found above, we obtain $M_1 + M_2 = 5,4$, that is, the sum of the masses of both stars of the Algol system is 5.4 times the mass of the Sun.

To determine the mass of each star separately, remember that the distances of two bodies from the center of their masses are inversely proportional to their masses. Means,

$$\frac{M_1}{M_2} = \frac{R_2}{R_1}.$$

Analysis of the light curve allows not only to find the radii of the orbits of stars, but also to determine the elements of the orbit: its inclination to the line of sight, eccentricity, position of the orbital plane in space. A method for determining the orbital elements of an eclipsing variable star from its light curve was developed in 1912 by the American astronomer G. ResSELOM *). Here are the basics of this scale witty method.

Let's assume that we are observing an eclipse of a star (Fig. 3, b). As long as the main star is completely covered, the brightness of the system will not change. In this case, the main minimum on the light curve (Fig. 3, a) will be flat. In the secondary minimum there will be a ring-shaped eclipse (Fig. 3, b; a small star in front) and it will also be flat. Let us denote the brightness of the star at any moment of the eclipse, until it is total (or not choked), by $l$, the brightness at the primary minimum by $l'$, the brightness at the secondary minimum by $l''$. We take the brightness of a star outside of an eclipse as unity. Then the brightness loss will be equal to the correspondence of $1-l, 1-l'$ and $1-l''$.

The position of the satellite in orbit relative to the direction to the Earth at each moment of time $t$ is determined by the phase angle $\theta$ (see Fig. 4), which is equal to

$$\theta = \frac{360^\circ}{P} (t - t_0),$$

where $t_0$ is the moment of time corresponding to the middle of the main minimum on the light curve (Fig. 3, a), $P$ is the satellite’s orbital period, equal to the period of the brightness change. At moment $t=t_0$ the phase angle is $\theta=0$. To simplify the calculations, we will set $t_0=0$, that is, we will start the time from the middle of the main minimum.
