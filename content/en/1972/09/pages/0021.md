The answer to the last question can be obtained as follows: an integer modulo 6 is comparable to one of the remainders 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5; its fourth power is comparable, respectively, to the fourth power of the remainder, that is, to one of the numbers 0, 1, 16, 81, 256 and 625. Since $16 \equiv 4, 81 \equiv 3, 256 \equiv 4, 625 \equiv 1$ (mod 6), the fourth powers of integers occur only in the classes $x \equiv 0, x \equiv 1, x \equiv 3, x \equiv 4$ (mod 6).

Let's look at one more question. It is still unknown whether the set of pairs of twin prime numbers is finite or infinite, that is, prime numbers of the form

$$p, p + 2.$$

Examples of “twins” are 3 and 5, 5 and 7, 11 and 13. But with triples of prime numbers of the form

$$p, p + 2, p + 4$$

the situation is much simpler. Modulo 3 for $p$ we have three possibilities:

a) $p \equiv 0, 6) p \equiv 1, \text{ в) } p \equiv 2$ (mod 3).

The first of these classes contains a single prime number $p = 3$, which corresponds to the prime numbers $p + 2 = 5$ and $p + 4 = 7$. So the first possibility results in three (3, 5, 7). With $p \equiv 1$ (mod 3) $p + 2 \equiv 3 \equiv 0$ (mod 3), opening the possibility results in a three (3, 5, 7). With $p \equiv 2$ (mod 3) $p + 4 \equiv 6 \equiv 0$ (mod 3), opening $p + 4 = 3$ or $p = -1$, which is also not suitable. So, by comparing the prime, it turns out that there is a single triplet of “twins”: (3, 5, 7).

Task 1. Most

$$r(2^{25} + 1,641).$$

Solution. $2^{15} = 32768 \equiv 77$ (mod 641), opening $2^{30} \equiv 77^2 = 5929 \equiv 160$ (mod 641) and $2^{32} \equiv 160 \cdot 4 = 640$ (mod 641), meaning $2^{32} + 1 \equiv 641 \equiv 0$ (mod 641), meaning $r(2^{25} + 1,641) = 0$ or $2^{25} + 1 \div 641$*.

Problem 2. The last digit of the natural number $33^{22} + 22^{11}$.

Solution. You need to find $r(33^{22} + 22^{11}, 10)$.

Since $33 \equiv 3$ (mod 10), then $33^2 \equiv 9 \equiv -1$ (mod 10) and $33^{22} \equiv (-1)^{11} \equiv -1$ (mod 10). From $22 \equiv 2$ (mod 10) follows $22^5 \equiv 32 \equiv 2$ (mod 10), $22^{10} \equiv 4$ (mod 10) and $22^{11} \equiv 4 \cdot 22 \equiv 8$ (mod 10). Adding up the resulting comparisons, we have

$$33^{22} + 22^{11} \equiv 7 \text{ (mod 10)}.$$

Thus, this number turns out to be 7.

Problem 3. Prove that there are no natural numbers $x$, $y$ and $z$ satisfying the equation

$$2^x + 7^y = 19^z.$$

Solution. We use model 3. Due to $19 \equiv 1$ (mod 3) we have $19^z \equiv 1$ (mod 3). On the other hand, $2^x + 7^y \equiv (-1)^x + 1$ (mod 3), opening $2^x + 7^y \equiv 2$ (mod 3) or $2^x + 7^y \equiv 0$ (mod 3) (depending on the parity of $x$). Since $2^x + 7^y \neq 19^z$ (mod 3), then $2^x + 7^y \neq 19^z$.

We emphasize that when solving this problem we must use model 3. If we took model 4, then we would not have a contradiction. The question of choosing a suitable model when solving a problem is complex. If you are interested in it, look at the article by M. I. Bashmakov “Do you like fiddling with integers”, «Kvant», No. 3, 1971.
