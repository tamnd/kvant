In 1669, the Italian mathematician and astronomer G. Montanari discovered that the star Algol (β Perseus) periodically changes its brightness. (It is possible that the variability of the brightness of this star was known to the ancient Arabs, who called it posetomu Ras Al-Ghul - “the head of the devil.” This Arabic name later turned into Algol.) We know little about Montanari’s works, since many of his works have not reached us. It is only known that he discovered the variability of the brightness not only of Algol, but of those around this star. But we don’t know which ones exactly: Montanari’s catalog was lost.

More than a hundred years later, the eighteen-year-old deaf-mute young man John Goodrike took up the observations of Algol in English amateur astronomy. From his observations for 1782-1783. he found that Algol, which usually shines as a 2nd magnitude star, weakens to magnitude 3-4 approximately every three days.

In astronomy, it is customary to express the brilliance of stars in stellar magnitudes - units proportional to the logarithm of the illumination created by the star on a plane perpendicular to its rays. The brightness of a star depends only on its luminous intensity and distance from the Earth, and does not depend on whether we observe the star with a simple eye or through a telescope, since the illumination of the eye and the telescope lens by the light of the star is naturally the same. In astronomy, brilliance is usually denoted by the letter L (luminosity), and magnitude by the letter m.

A difference of 1 magnitude corresponds to a brightness ratio of 2.512 times *). The difference between two stellar magnitudes m₁ and m₂ is equal to

$$m_2 - m_1 = 2.5 \log \frac{L_1}{L_2},$$

where L₁ and L₂ are the brightness of stars.

Observing Algol, Goodrike noticed that the decrease in its brightness to a minimum and the return increase to the “normal” value last for 4.5 hours, after which the star maintains a constant brightness for 2 days and 12 hours, and then Libra repeats the cycle again. Goodrike's scales accurately determined the period of change in the star's brightness - 2 days 20 hours 49 minutes, which is only 3 minutes different from the modern value. But the most important condition is that Goodreik correctly observes the phenomenon he observed: he suggested that here there is an eclipse of one star (bright) by another (dark), revolving around the first. Algol-type stars are called eclipsing variables.

Goodreik's hypothesis was confirmed a hundred years later.
