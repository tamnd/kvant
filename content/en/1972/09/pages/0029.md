From the light curve we can determine for each moment in time the formetric phase of the eclipse, equal to the ratio of the loss of brightness at a given moment in time to the loss of brightness in the main minimum:

$$\alpha = \frac{1 - l}{1 - l'}.$$

FROM the definition of $\alpha$ it is clear that $\alpha$ is a function of the phase angle $\theta$. It is from the known from the observed dependence $\alpha(\theta)$ that we will determine the values ​​$r_1, r_2, i$.

It is easy to see that the formetric phase $\alpha$ uniquely depends on three quantities: on the distance $d$ between the centers of the disks of the circumferential stars ($AB$ by Fig. 5) and on their radii $r_1, r_2$ (or on one of them, $r_1$, and their ratio $k$). Numerically, it is equal to the ratio of the planes of the double segment $CDEF$ to the area of ​​the disk of the smaller star, that is, to the area of ​​the circle with center at $A$ (Fig. 5). How to find the distance between centers $d$?

If the orbital plane passed through the line of sight ($i=90^\circ$), then it would be $d = a \cdot \sin \theta$. But since the orbit has an inclination of $i \neq 90^\circ$, the magnitude of $d$ will be a function of two angles:

$i$ and $\theta$, of which we know only the second ($\theta$). Although the formula expressing the connection of $d$ with $i$ and $\theta$ is simple, we will not derive it here in order to avoid cumbersome calculations, but will write it in naive form:

$$d = f_1(i, \theta).$$

Moreover, according to the above,

$$d = f_2(\alpha, k, r_1).$$

Thus, we seem to have only two equations with four unknowns $d, i, k, r_1$. Eliminating $d$, we get one equation with a third unknown $i, k, r_1$.

But we have at our disposal not just one point on the light curve, but many. By choosing three points, we get a system of three equations with the third unknown, which can be solved and obtain the values ​​$i, r_1, k$ (and therefore $r_2 = kr_1$).

In the Russell method, two points are selected as fixed (they correspond to $\alpha=0,6$ and $\alpha=0,9$), and the third point is “slip”, that is, many points on the light curve are selected in turn as the third point. From the resulting 10-20 solutions for the unknowns, the average values ​​are taken. Thus, the entire light curve seems to be involved in the solution.

Knowing the radius of the circumferential stars in fractions of the orbital radius $R$ (we assumed $R=1$), we can find the abscyltic radii, and from them the volumes. By dividing the masses of stars by their volumes, we find the average densities of stars.

If the eclipse is not total, but partial, the solution will be more difficult, but Russell's method allows you to achieve success in this case as well.

For the example we have chosen - the Algol system - $r_1=2,0$ ml. $km, r_2=2,0$ ml. $km$. Knowing the sizes and masses, we can easily find the average densities of the circumferential stars $\rho_1=0,1 \ e/cm^3, \rho_2=0,06 \ e/cm^3$.
