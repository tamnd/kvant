It can be seen that both curves intersect at $\lambda = \lambda_* \approx 12$ cm. Thus, at very short wavelengths ($\lambda \ll \lambda_*$), their speed is determined by surface tension, and at long wavelengths ($\lambda \gg \lambda_*$), by the acceleration of gravity. In the intermediate region ($\lambda \approx \lambda_*$), the speed of surface waves is determined by both factors (gravity and surface tension). The expression for wave speed becomes more complex ($u_\Sigma = \sqrt{u^2 + u_\sigma^2}$). The dependence of $u_\Sigma$ on $\sqrt{\lambda}$ is shown on figure by a 4-dash line.

In physics, it often happens that it is much easier to study certain limiting cases (for us these are $\lambda \rightarrow 0$ and $\lambda \rightarrow \infty$), and the “intermediate region” requires more conditions. Fortunately, for us this region does not exist, because, looking at Roerich’s painting, we can reliably say that circular waves have a length clearly greater than $\lambda_*$, and, therefore, their speed is described by expression (2). To find it, you just need to “measure” $\lambda$. We will carry out the calculations below, but for now we will take one more step.

Let us remember that when a water strider beetle runs quickly through water or a bullet flies in the air at the speed of sound, characteristic “whiskers” are always formed, which are pressed closer to the moving body, the greater its speed $V$ compared to the speed $u$ of the propagation of disturbances caused by the body. On figure 5 three characteristic cases are given: if $V < u$, the waves overtake the body and only the test is skimmed in the direction of movement; if $V = u$, then the wave crests at one point (in the direction of movement) are directed towards each other; if $V > u$, then the body overtakes the waves, and a “whisker” is formed. (The photo on the second cover illustrates the latter case.)
