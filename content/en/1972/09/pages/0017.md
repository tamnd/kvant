The author continued this with his comrades (a total of 30 people were interviewed) and received the graphs shown in Figures 6 and 7. Interestingly, most of the respondents named the values ​​$h$ and $\lambda$, multiples of 5 cm, some - multiples of 2-2.5 cm, but no less (no one named, for example, $h = 27$, 1357891439 cm). This is the same as if you changed the linear distances with a minimum division value of 2-2.5 cm.

Using these graphs, we can find the average values ​​of $<h>$ and $<\lambda>$ and consider them fairly accurate.

The procedure for determining averages is simple. Each value $h_i$ must be multiplied by the number of people $n_i$ who named it, take the sum over all $i$ and divide by the total number of respondents. As a result we get

$$\langle h \rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{30} h_i n_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{30} n_i} = \frac{1}{30} \sum_{i=1}^{30} h_i n_i = \frac{1}{30} (10 + 2 \cdot 12 + 5 \cdot 15 + 7 \cdot 20 + 4 \cdot 25 + 6 \cdot 30 + 2 \cdot 40 + 1 \cdot 50 + 1 \cdot 60 + 1 \cdot 70) = \frac{789}{30} \approx 26 \text{ см} \approx 0,26 \text{ м.}$$

Likewise

$$\langle \lambda \rangle = \frac{1}{30} (2 \cdot 10 + 3 \cdot 12 + 4 \cdot 15 + 1 \cdot 18 + 8 \cdot 20 + 2 \cdot 25 + 2 \cdot 30 + 1 \cdot 40 + 3 \cdot 50 + 1 \cdot 70 + 1 \cdot 80 + 1 \cdot 90 + 1 \cdot 120 + 1 \cdot 150) = 32,5 \text{ см} = 0,325 \text{ м.}$$

These averages are marked in Figures 6 and 7 with red arrows.

The accuracy of our survey “measurements” can be roughly estimated by the width of the smooth dotted curve taken at half its height (red lines in Figures 6 and 7). For physical experiments, of course, a whole theory of errors has been developed, in general, based on what we have done, only in the experiment are the instruments “interrogated”.

What about the angle $\alpha$? All those interviewed agreed that it lies somewhere in the range of $90^\circ < 2\alpha < 180^\circ$ (the ship in the picture is quite typical). Thus,

$$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \sin 45^\circ < \sin \alpha < \sin 90^\circ = 1.$$

Now we have everything to make estimates of the ship's speed and surface waves.

**Results**

Substituting the values ​​$h$, $\lambda$ and $\sin \alpha$ from (5), (6), (7) into formulas (1) and (2), we obtain

$$V \geq \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 9,8 [m/c^2] \cdot 0,26 [m]}{1 \div \sqrt{2}/2}} = \sqrt{5,1 \div 7,25 [m^2/c^2]} = 2,25 \div 2,7 [m/c],$$

$$u = \sqrt{\frac{9,8 [m/c^2] \cdot 0,34 [m]}{2\pi}} \approx 0,7 [m/c].$$

Now we are surprised to notice that correspondences (8) and (9) are incompatible with inequality (4). Indeed, judging by the height of the bow wave, the ship floats at a speed $V$ no different than 2.25 $m/c$, and if we focus on the circular surface of the waves, which are almost concentric in the picture, the speed of the ship should be much less than the speed of these waves $u = 0,7 \text{ m/c}$, which is already less than $V$.
