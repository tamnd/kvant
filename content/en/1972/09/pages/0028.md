Analysis of the light curve allows not only to find the radii of the orbits of stars, but also to determine the elements of the orbit: its inclination to the line of sight, eccentricity, position of the orbital plane in space. A method for determining the orbital elements of an eclipsing variable star from its light curve was developed in 1912 by the American astronomer G. ResSELOM *). Here are the basics of this scale witty method.

Let's assume that we are observing an eclipse of a star (Fig. 3, b). As long as the main star is completely covered, the brightness of the system will not change. In this case, the main minimum on the light curve (Fig. 3, a) will be flat. In the secondary minimum there will be a ring-shaped eclipse (Fig. 3, b; a small star in front) and it will also be flat. Let us denote the brightness of the star at any moment of the eclipse, until it is total (or not choked), by $l$, the brightness at the primary minimum by $l'$, the brightness at the secondary minimum by $l''$. We take the brightness of a star outside of an eclipse as unity. Then the brightness loss will be equal to the correspondence of $1-l, 1-l'$ and $1-l''$.

The position of the satellite in orbit relative to the direction to the Earth at each moment of time $t$ is determined by the phase angle $\theta$ (see Fig. 4), which is equal to

$$\theta = \frac{360^\circ}{P} (t - t_0),$$

where $t_0$ is the moment of time corresponding to the middle of the main minimum on the light curve (Fig. 3, a), $P$ is the satellite’s orbital period, equal to the period of the brightness change. At moment $t=t_0$ the phase angle is $\theta=0$. To simplify the calculations, we will set $t_0=0$, that is, we will start the time from the middle of the main minimum.
