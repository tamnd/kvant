This means that the satellite in the Algol system is not completely dark, but emits a little light, and when it is partially obscured by the main star, the brightness of the system weakens somewhat. Since the geometric picture of bieclipses is completely symmetrical relative to the center of the main star, regardless of the angle of inclination of the orbit to the line of sight, it is obvious that the occluded area of ​​the satellite’s disk is exactly equal to the area of ​​the main star’s disk that is obscured in the main minimum (see Fig. 2, b). But then, from the correspondence of the amplitudes of brightness loss in the main and secondary minimum ($\Delta m_1$ and $\Delta m_2$) and the corresponding brightness values ​​($l_1$ and $l_2$), one can find the relation of the brightness surface of both stars ($B_1$, $B_2$). For simplicity, let us take the brightness of the system outside the eclipse to be unity ($L_0=1$); then from (1) we will have:
$$\Delta m_1 = -2,5 \log l_1; \Delta m_2 = -2,5 \log l_2; \log l_1 = -0,4 \Delta m_1; \log l_2 = -0,4 \Delta m_2;$$
$$l_1 = 10^{-0,4 \Delta m_1}; l_2 = 10^{-0,4 \Delta m_2}.$$
The brightness losses in the main and secondary minima will be equal to $(1-l_2)$ and $(1-l_2)$, respectively (we put $L_0=1$). But since the areas of the disks of both stars obscured during eclipses are equal, the surface brightness of the obscured stars is related to the loss of brightness in the corresponding minima:
$$\frac{B_1}{B_2} = \frac{1-l_1}{1-l_2} = \frac{1-10^{-0,4 \Delta m_1}}{1-10^{-0,4 \Delta m_2}}.$$

Substituting $\Delta m_1 = 1,27$ and $\Delta m_2 = 0,06$, we find for Algol $B_1/B_2 = 12,7$, that is, the surface of the main star is almost 13 times brighter than the surface of the satellite.

Knowing the ratio of the brightness of the main star and the satellite star, you can find the ratio of the temperatures of both stars. It was established experimentally that radiation per unit surface area of ​​luminous bodies is proportional to the fourth power of their temperature *). In our case, the radiation with unit surface is the brightness surface of stars. Therefore we can write:
$$B_1 = \sigma T_1^4, \quad B_2 = \sigma T_2^4,$$
(5)

where $B_1$, $B_2$ and $T_1$, $T_2$ are the rage surface and absolute temperatures of the main star and the companion star, $\sigma$ is a constant. From correspondence (5) it follows that
$$\frac{T_1}{T_2} = \sqrt[4]{\frac{B_1}{B_2}}.$$

For the Algol experiment
$$T_1: T_2 = \sqrt[4]{12,7} \approx 1,89.$$
The temperature of the main star can be determined by studying its spectrum, or more precisely, the distribution of rage along the spectrum.
