Wave surfaces

Wave surfaces are a rather complex object of study, but we can learn a lot about them based on very simple messages about the dimensions of the physical quantities that describe this phenomenon. To do this, you must first of all clearly imagine the principle of the phenomenon. A wave is a propagating vibration. Why can the surface of a surface, brought out of its equilibrium position by something, vibrate? Any oscillation is the result of the “game” of two factors: force, which returns it to the equilibrium position, and inertia, which forces this equilibrium position to slip through.

If a “hump” is formed on the surface of the liquid, then the liquid particles can be returned to the equilibrium position, for example, by a layer of gravity $F_g$ (see Fig. 3a), proportional to the acceleration of earth’s gravity $g$. Falling down, due to inertia, the “hump” appears below the equilibrium position; next to it, another “hump” will flow out, etc., as a result of which a wave will run, characterized by a certain speed $u$ and length $\lambda$ (the distance between the “humps”). In the case we are considering, the measure of the inertia of oscillating water is its density.

So, the movement of a wave along the surface of a liquid is described by the following quantities (we will immediately write their dimensions):

$$u \left[ \frac{m}{c} \right], \lambda \left[ m \right], g \left[ \frac{m}{c^2} \right], \rho \left[ \frac{\kappa_2}{m^3} \right].$$

How to connect them with each other? For example, how can we find the dependence of the wave velocity $u$ that induces us on other waves $\lambda$, $g$, $\rho$? This is where the dimensions already written out will come to our aid.

We see that the dimension $u$ contains a second in the denominator. Of the three other volyns, the dimension of time is included only in $g$ (there, the denominator of the stops is $c^2$). From here we quickly write $u \sim \sqrt{g}$, and, obviously, $g$ cannot be touched anymore: we received from it the required second in the denominator of the dimension $u$.
