So,

$$u \left[ \frac{m}{c} \right] \sim \sqrt{g \left[ \frac{m}{c^2} \right]} \cdot \lambda [m].$$

Note that we only got proportional, not equality, since $\sqrt{g\lambda}$ can be preceded by any factor $k$ that has no dimension, that is, $u = k \sqrt{g\lambda}$. It could be $k = 1/2$, or maybe $k = 1000000$. Here our theory is powerless, but it allowed us to find out the main thing - the physics of the phenomenon. The exact solution to the problem gives the value $k = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, that is

$$u = \sqrt{\frac{g\lambda}{2\pi}}.$$

(2)

Where is $\rho [\kappa e/m^3]$ here? It is not there, but we simply have nowhere to detail the kilograms included in the dimension $\rho$, there is nothing to reduce them so that in the final result the value $u$ changes only in $m/c$. This is a physical concept: after all, both the weight of the “hump”, which accelerates it downwards, and the mass of the “hump”, which determines its inertia, are proportional to $\rho$ - this is why the density has dropped out of formula (2). (A similar situation occurs in the case of a mathematical matrix, whose period $T = 2\pi \sqrt{l/g}$ for the same reason does not depend on the mass suspended on a thread of length $l$).

If the waves become very small, “ripples” (we will evaluate below what very small means), then another force strives to return the “hump” to a position of equality - the surface tension force associated with the surface tension coefficient $\sigma [\kappa e/m]$. This is similar to how if we lifted a stretched horizontal rubber band upward with our finger: then, due to the tension of the band on the finger, a force $F_\sigma \sim \sigma$ (Fig. 36) would begin to act downwards. In this case, the propagation of waves will depend on the magnitude of $\sigma [\kappa e/m]$, $\lambda [m]$, $u_\sigma [m/c]$, $\rho [\kappa e/m^3]$. (We denote the speed of surface tension waves by $u_\sigma$ to distinguish it from $u$ from formula (2).)

Teleru, we have enough experience to use dimensions and obtain an expression for wave speed (remember that $1 \kappa = 1 \kappa e \cdot m/c^2$):

$$u_\sigma \left[ \frac{m}{c} \right] \sim \sqrt{\frac{\sigma [\kappa e/c^2]}{\rho [\kappa e/m^3]} \cdot \lambda [m]}.$$

If we want to change the sign proportionally to an equal sign, we need to take into account the missing dimensionless factor. According to the exact theory, it is equal to $\sqrt{2\pi}$. So,

$$u_\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi \sigma}{\lambda \rho}}.$$

(3)

Isn’t it amazing that we got the main thing almost “for free”, without knowing any theory: the nature of the dependence between quantities! It is important to remember here that, according to the famous Italian physicist Enrico Fermi, “in physics... there is no place for confused thoughts... those who truly understand the nature of this or that phenomenon must obtain the basic laws from the messages of dimension”*).

For “icy” water

$$\sigma \approx 80 \frac{\partial h/cm}{\partial t} = 8 \cdot 10^{-2} \kappa e/m,$$

$$\rho = 1 \kappa/cm^3 = 10^3 \kappa e/m^3$$

Taking this into account, we plotted the dependence of the wave surface speed, determined by expression (2) and (3), on the value of $\sqrt{\lambda}$ (Fig. 4).
