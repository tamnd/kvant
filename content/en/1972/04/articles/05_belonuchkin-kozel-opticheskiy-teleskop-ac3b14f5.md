TELESCOPE

V. E. Belonuchkin
S. M. Kozel

The telescope is rightfully considered one of the greatest inventions of all time. Centuries have passed since the first primitive instrument discovered the Galileo of Jupiter's moons, bringing with it enormous progress in all fields of science. The significance of the telescope as a tool for understanding the world has not changed. It reveals to us the vastness of the Universe that is inaccessible to observation with the naked eye.

Before the invention of photography astrophotometric telescopes predawnacularly exclusively for visual observations. And the telescope still remains, first and foremost, a visual instrument. The telescope's design is based on simple physical principles, which we will discuss in this article.

The visual telescope performs two main functions. First, it creates a clear image of a distant object on the retina, greatly magnified compared to that created by the naked eye, and this allows one to discern details that are inaccessible to the naked eye. Secondly, the telescope creates the light of a celestial body and transmits it to our eye in a narrow beam.

Meanwhile, in books on optics you can find the following statements:
“Even in the most powerful telescope, stars appear to us as luminous points” and “Images of objects observed in a telescope cannot be brighter than when observed with the naked eye.”

What is a telescope used for, what can and cannot be seen with a telescope? To answer these questions, you need to understand the basic structure of the telescope.

Let's assume that we use the Kepler tube to examine the surface of the Moon. How does the picture we observe differ from what we saw with the naked eye? First of all, of course, size. If some two points of the lunar surface are visible to the naked eye at an angle $\alpha$, then when observed in a telescope the eye sees their images at a different, larger angle $\beta$ (Fig. 2) $^*$). The angular magnification given by the telescope is characterized by the ratio $\gamma = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$. The distance between the images of the considered points of the lunar surface on the retina in the first case is equal to $l' = \alpha f$, and in the second case $l' = \beta f$ ($f$ is the focal length of the eye lens); it changes $\gamma = \frac{l'}{l'} = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$ times.

Consequently, the angular magnification of the telescope is also a measure of the increase in the linear dimensions of the image on the retina. If the linear dimensions of the image on the retina increase by $\gamma$ times, then the image area increases by $\gamma^2 = \frac{\beta^2}{\alpha^2}$ times.

$^*$) For simplicity, we will assume that the angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are small enough, so that $\sin \alpha \approx \alpha$ and $\sin \beta \approx \beta$.

Let us now determine what the angular magnification of the telescope is. To do this, consider figure 3. A parallel beam of rays enters the lens $L_1$ at an angle $\alpha$ to the optical axis. The beam of rays exits the eyepiece $L_2$ and is deflected by an angle $\beta$ (the dotted line shows the side optical axis of the eyepiece). From triangles $ABO_1$ and $ABO_2$ we obtain:

$$\alpha \approx tg \alpha = \frac{AB}{AO_1} = \frac{AB}{f_1};$$

$$\beta \approx tg \beta = \frac{AB}{AO_2} = \frac{AB}{f_2}$$

($f_1$ and $f_2$ are the focal lengths of the object and eyepiece, respectively). Hence,

$$\gamma = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} = \frac{f_1}{f_2},$$

that is, the magnification given by the telescope is equal to the ratio of the focal lengths of the object and the eyepiece.

Now we know how to make a telescope with high magnification: for this you need long-focus lenses and short-focus eyepieces. But we should not forget that the telescope and the eye being observed constitute a single optical system. Therefore, when selecting the optical parameters of lenses and eyepieces, it is necessary to take into account the parameters of the eyes as an optical device. Let's look at this in more detail. Brightness is estimated by the observable by the illumination of the image on the retina. Let us consider a parallel beam of rays from some small element of the beam surface lying on the optical axis of the telecope. The diameter of the beam of rays emerging from the eyepiece is obviously equal to $d = \frac{D}{\gamma}$, where $D$ is the diameter of the object (that is, the diameter of the beam of rays passing into the lens).

Consider the case $d < d_{3p}$. In this case, the entire light flux entering the lens must reach the reticle (we do not take into account here the inevitable losses of light in the optical system, which amount to 20-30% even in good instruments). The illuminance of the retinal image is proportional to the luminous flux penetrating the pupil (hence proportional to $D^2$) and inversely proportional to the area of ​​the image (i.e. inversely proportional to the square of the telecoppa magnification $\gamma^2 = \frac{f_1^2}{f_2^2}$).

Thus, for $d < d_{3p}$ we have $E \sim D^2 \left( \frac{f_2}{f_1} \right)^2$.

In the case of $d > d_{3p}$ only part of the light will penetrate into the pupil

$$D' = \frac{f_1}{f_2} d_{3p} = \gamma d_{3p}.$$

Hence,

$$E' \sim (D')^2 \left( \frac{f_2}{f_1} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{f_1}{f_2} \right)^2 d_{3p}^2 \times \left( \frac{f_2}{f_1} \right)^2 = d_{3p}^2,$$

that is

$$E' \sim d_{3p}^2.$$

The illumination of the image of an extended object on the retina in the case of the naked eye $E_0$ is obviously proportional to $d_{3p}^2$. Thus, provided that $d > d_{3p}$, the illumination of the image on the retina is the same as in the case of the naked eye. At $d < d_{3p}$, the illumination of the image on the retina of the eye armed with a telescope is less than $E_0$.

So, you need to choose an eyepiece. It is clear that any eyepiece for which the diameter $d$ of the emerging beam turns out to be greater than $d_{3p}$ should be immediately rejected. As can be seen from figure 4, with such an eyepiece we do not use the possibility of a large objective, and it would be entirely possible to get by with a smaller objective. From the calculations carried out above, it follows that with $d < d_{3p}$ we lose in image brightness. Thus, there are good reasons to choose an eyepiece such that $d = d_{3p}$. As you can see, the telescope must be highly adapted to the human eye.

The telescope magnification at $d = d_{3p}$ is called normal:

$$\gamma_{\text{норм}} = \frac{f_1}{f_2} = \frac{D}{d_{3p}}.$$

When choosing the magnification of the astronomical lens, take into account that the diameter of the pupil depends on the observation conditions: during daytime observations, a lot of light enters the eye and immediately narrows ($d_{3p} \approx 3$ mm), during night observations the pupil expands ($d_{3p} \approx 6$ mm). The value $\gamma_{\text{норм}}$ should be selected accordingly. Typically, astronomers set the magnification somewhat higher $\gamma_{\text{норм}}$. In this case, the image on the retina is of higher quality, since distortions can be largely detected.

Now we will form the last and very important question. We have seen that at $d < d_{3p}$ (at $\gamma > \gamma_{horm}$) the result is a loss in the rage of the image. But suppose that we examine an object with a significant brightness using a telescope, and the loss in rage at $d < d_{3p}$ is insignificant (for example, when observing the surface of the Sun, it is still necessary to weaken the sunlight many times so as not to damage the eyes.) By choosing a magnification $\gamma > \gamma_{horm}$, we will get a large image on the retina. Will we see any new details of the object?

This is essentially a question about image quality, and can be framed differently: Is the image exactly like the object itself? It is clear that if the optical system introduces aberrations, then such a similarity does not exist. But let us assume that the optical system is ideal and the observation is carried out in an ideal calm atmosphere. It turns out that even in this case the image is not exactly like the observed object. There are images that are caused by an irreducible cause - diffraction of light waves.

Diffraction phenomena occur when waves pass near objects. These phenomena occur in the same way for waves of any physical nature. The easiest way to perform diffraction experiments is with waves on the surface of the wave. For this purpose, you can use the installation described in the article “Waves in a shallow dish” (Kvant No. 1, 1971). We will talk about diffraction of light waves. If you place an object with a small hole in the path of the waves, you can observe a characteristic diffraction phenomenon - the divergence of the wave beam. Figure 5 illustrates the phenomenon. In diffraction theory, it shows that the divergence angle of the wave beam is approximately equal to $\varphi \approx \frac{\lambda}{D}$, where $\lambda$ is the length of the incident wave, $D$ is the size of the hole. If a screen is placed at a sufficiently large distance $L$ behind the hole, you can see a characteristic diffraction pattern on it, consisting of a number of alternating light and dark areas (see Fig. 5). The radius of the central light spot, which accounts for a significant portion of the energy of the wave beam (about 85%), is approximately equal to $L \cdot \frac{\varphi}{2} = \frac{\lambda}{D} \cdot L$.

Thus, due to diffraction, the waves enter the geometric shadow regions. We can say that a diffracted wave beam is a collection of waves whose propagation directions lie in the range from $-\frac{\varphi}{2}$ to $+\frac{\varphi}{2}$. If a lens with focal length $F$ is placed in the path of such a beam, then the diffraction pattern in the focal plane of the lens is similar to the theme, as shown in figure 6. The radius of the central light spot is $R \approx \frac{\varphi}{2} \cdot F \approx \frac{\lambda}{D} \cdot F$.

Diffraction leads to the fact that the beams do not converge behind the lens at one point (focus), as is obtained according to the rules of geometric optics, but form an illuminated area (see Fig. 6; the scale of the diffraction pattern of high magnification). The last step left is to remove the diaphragm with the hole. Then the role of such a diaphragm will be played by the lens frame, and in the formula described above that determines the size of the central spot, $D$ must be understood as the diameter of the lens itself.
