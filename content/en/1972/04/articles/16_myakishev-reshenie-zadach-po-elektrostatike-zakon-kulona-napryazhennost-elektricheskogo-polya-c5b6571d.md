Solving problems in electrostatics
(Coulomb's Law. Electric field strength)

G. Ya. Myakishev

Electrostatics problems are often offered in entrance exams. Moreover, they are very different in their solution methods.

Problems in electrostatics (unlike, for example, problems in mechanics and molecular physics) can cause additional difficulties associated with the use of different systems of units. Along with the SI system, the absolute system of units continues to be used in electrostatics. The different systems are that the basic formulas used to solve problems have coefficients, excellent cession quantities and dimensions. Moreover, it is in the absolute electrostatic system SGSE that the coefficients of the electrostatic formulas have the simplest form. Therefore, it is more convenient to solve electrostatic problems in this system.

Coulomb's law

The first group of problems is related to the non-presidential application of Coulomb's law.

In a homogeneous medium with dielectric constant $\varepsilon$, two exact bodies with charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ at a distance $R$ interact with a force

$$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{\varepsilon R^2}.$$

(1)

In the SGSE system $k=1$, and in the SI system $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$, where $\varepsilon_0 = \frac{1}{4\pi \cdot 10^9} = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \frac{\phi}{M}$ is the electrical constant.

The SI unit of charge, the coulomb, contains $3 \cdot 10^9$ SGSE units of charge.

Problem 2. A thin wire spike of radius $R$ carries an electric charge $q$. In the center of the ring there is a charge of the same name as $q$, $Q$, and $Q \gg q$. Determine the tensile force of the stab.

Since $Q \gg q$, the interaction between individual elements of the ring can be neglected. Let us select a small ring element of length $R\alpha$ (Fig. 3). The sides of the charge $Q$ act on it by a force $F = \frac{Q\Delta q}{R^2}$, where $\Delta q = \frac{q\alpha}{2\pi}$.

The tension force of the ring $T$ is balanced by $F$. From the equilibrium condition, taking into account that $\alpha$ is small, we have $F = 2T \sin \frac{\alpha}{2} = T\alpha$.

The required tension force is $T = \frac{Qq}{2\pi R^2}$.

The problem was solved in the SGSE system. In the SI system

$$T = \frac{Qq}{8\pi^2\varepsilon_0 R^2}.$$

Electric field voltage

Interaction between stationary charged bodies is carried out through an electric field. Its quantitative characteristic is the voltage vector $E(x, y, z)$, which is the ratio of the forces acting on the exact charge to the magnitude of this charge. The force acting on the charge $q$ is equal to $*$)

$$F = qE. \tag{2}$$

When solving problems of calculating the electric field voltage, it is first necessary to determine the field voltage of the exact charge. It is equal

$$E = \frac{q}{\varepsilon R^2} \text{ в системе } \text{СГСЭ}, \tag{3}$$

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon R^2} \text{ в системе } \text{СИ}.$$

If there is a set of exact charges, then the field voltage is equal to the geometric sum of the voltages created by the individual charges.

To calculate the field voltage of a charged body of finite dimensions, it must be mentally divided into individual elements and the desired voltage must be calculated as the geometric sum of the tensions created by all the elements. But only in the case of simple symmetry in the distribution of charges on the body the problem of determining the voltage is solved

Task 5. In a strong uniform electric field, directed $E$ on one plum line at points 1 and 2, located at a distance $l_0$ from each other, finding a proton and an electron (Fig. 4). The initial speed of the driver is zero. What will be the distance between the particles after a time $t$ after the start of motion?

Let us choose the origin at point 2, where the electron is located, and take the direction of the electric field as the positive direction. Neglecting the interaction of particles with each other (strong field), we can consider the motion of the electron and proton to be equally accelerated. Then, according to the well-known kinematic formula, the coordinate of the proton at the moment of time $t$ will be equal to

$$x_1 = l_0 + \frac{e}{m_\pi} E \frac{\tau^2}{2},$$

where $e$ is the charge, $m_\pi$ is the proton mass. Electron coordinate

$$x_2 = -\frac{e}{m_\pi} E \frac{\tau^2}{2},$$

where $m_\pi$ is the electron mass. Required distance

$$l = x_1 - x_2 = l_0 + \frac{eE\tau^2}{2} \frac{m_\pi + m_\pi}{m_\pi m_\pi} \approx \approx l_0 + \frac{eE\tau^2}{2m_\pi},$$

since $m_\pi \gg m_\pi$.

Conductors in an electric field

When solving a large number of problems, you need to know how conductors behave in an electrostatic field. It can be shown that the direction of the field inside the conductor is zero, and on its surface it is directed perpendicular to the conductor. The charge inside the conductor is also zero; it is all concentrated on the surface of the conductor.

The surface charge is most dense at the sharp points, where the curvature of the surface is maximum. A visual confirmation of this can be obtained by solving the following problem.
