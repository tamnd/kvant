If we compare two telephones with lens diameters $D_1$ and $D_2$ ($D_1 > D_2$), then the larger telephone gives a gain in luminous flux equal to the ratio $\frac{D_1^2}{D_2^2}$; at the same time, it gives a gain in resolution by $\frac{D_1}{D_2}$ times. Of course, such a gain can be realized in the case of high quality lenses and under fairly good atmospheric conditions.

We often think of a telephone as a combination of high-quality optical elements, while forgetting that an astronomical telephone is a highly complex piece of engineering that combines high optical precision with perfect mechanical design. And yet, the telephone device is based on simple physical principles. The discussion of these principles was the focus of this article.

Exercises

1. Is it possible to see the Soviet Lunar Rover ($H \approx 1\text{ m}$) on the surface of the Moon using the world's largest telephone ($D=5\text{ m}$)? What is the size of the details of the beam surface that can be studied using such a telephone?

2. In the school physics circle, a Kepler telescope was built, the objective of which is a lens with a focal length $F=50\text{ cm}$ and a diameter $D=75\text{ mm}$. What is the focal length of the eyepiece if, when observed with a Kepler tube, the Moon appears 8 times less bright than when observed with the naked eye? Light loss in the tube is 50%. The diameter of the hole is taken to be 3 mm. What eyepiece should be used in this case?

3. An observer views a distant object using a Kepler tube. A lens with focal lengths $F_1=30\text{ cm}$ and $F_2=5\text{ cm}$ is used as the object and eyepiece of the tube. The observer sees a clear image of the object if the distance between the lens and the eyepiece is not less than $L_1=33\text{ cm}$ and not more than $L_2=34,5\text{ cm}$. At varying distances, does this observable lens see objects with the naked eye?

MEASUREMENT
GRAVITY
ON THE MOON
BY USING
TV

An American college teacher, Hooper, watched the astronauts landing on the moon on television at home. Suddenly he noticed that some heavy object was hanging from one of the compartments of the ship, swinging on something like a rope. A simple but beautiful idea came to his mind. Looking at his watch, he determined that the period of oscillation he was monitoring was about 5 seconds.

The length of the pendant could be estimated by comparing it with the height of the astronaut standing next to him; it turned out to be equal to approximately 1 m. Hence, use the matrix formula

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}},$$

it was also possible to estimate the increased gravity on the surface of the Moon

$$g = \frac{4\pi^2 l}{T^2} = \frac{4 \cdot 3,142 \cdot 100}{5^2} \approx 160 \left( \frac{cm}{sec^2} \right).$$

This value is almost exactly equal to 1/6 of the acceleration due to gravity on Earth. So, without leaving the house, Hooper performed a real “cosmic” experiment.

D. Borodin
