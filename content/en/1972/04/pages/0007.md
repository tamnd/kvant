Then the question posed in the conditions can be formulated as follows: what is the smallest number of rooks that can be placed on the board $n \times n \times n$ so that they cover the entire board (that is, all squares without exception)?

Exercise 1. Solve a similar problem for a “flat” chessboard $n \times n$. (Answer: $n$. Note that if $n \times n$ has fewer rooks on the board than $n$, it will find a rank and file that are free of rooks).

Initial rough estimates

Let us denote by $A_n$ the smallest number of rooks that can send the entire board $n \times n \times n$.

Since on the board $n \times n \times n$ each rook has $3n-2$ cells, and there are $n^3$ cells in total, then in order to send the entire board, you need to place at least $\frac{n^3}{3n-2}$ rooks. In other words,

$$A_n \geq \frac{n^3}{3n-2}.$$

It follows that

$$A_2 \geq 2, A_3 \geq 4, A_4 \geq 7, A_5 \geq 10.$$

It’s easy to come up with an example of a placement of $n^2$ rooks that span the entire board (they can be placed in one horizontal “layer”).

This gives this rough estimate:

$$A_n \leq n^2.$$

Exercise 2. Prove that

$$A_n \leq \frac{3n^2}{4},$$

using the following message: if you place $n_1$ rooks along edge $n_1$ of board $n_1 \times n_2 \times n_3$, then after that it remains to solve the problem for board $n_1 \times (n_2-1) \times (n_3-1)$.
