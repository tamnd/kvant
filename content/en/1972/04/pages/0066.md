Electric field voltage

Interaction between stationary charged bodies is carried out through an electric field. Its quantitative characteristic is the voltage vector $E(x, y, z)$, which is the ratio of the forces acting on the exact charge to the magnitude of this charge. The force acting on the charge $q$ is equal to $*$)

$$F = qE. \tag{2}$$

When solving problems of calculating the electric field voltage, it is first necessary to determine the field voltage of the exact charge. It is equal

$$E = \frac{q}{\varepsilon R^2} \text{ в системе } \text{СГСЭ}, \tag{3}$$

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon R^2} \text{ в системе } \text{СИ}.$$

If there is a set of exact charges, then the field voltage is equal to the geometric sum of the voltages created by the individual charges.

To calculate the field voltage of a charged body of finite dimensions, it must be mentally divided into individual elements and the desired voltage must be calculated as the geometric sum of the tensions created by all the elements. But only in the case of simple symmetry in the distribution of charges on the body the problem of determining the voltage is solved

*) Formula (2) is always simple, but the physical understanding of the force $F$ contains points that should be emphasized. The electric field acts on charged bodies, imparting acceleration to them or compensating for other forces if the bodies were not supplied. But the charges themselves do not act on the field with any force; they just create it. The electric field is not an object subject to Newtonian mechanics, and, accordingly, force (2) is not a body force in the sense of Newtonian mechanics. In the equation of motion (Newton's second law) it plays the same role as ordinary mechanical forces: gravity, elasticity, friction. But Newton's third law does not apply to it at all.
