Now we will form the last and very important question. We have seen that at $d < d_{3p}$ (at $\gamma > \gamma_{horm}$) the result is a loss in the rage of the image. But suppose that we examine an object with a significant brightness using a telescope, and the loss in rage at $d < d_{3p}$ is insignificant (for example, when observing the surface of the Sun, it is still necessary to weaken the sunlight many times so as not to damage the eyes.) By choosing a magnification $\gamma > \gamma_{horm}$, we will get a large image on the retina. Will we see any new details of the object?

This is essentially a question about image quality, and can be framed differently: Is the image exactly like the object itself? It is clear that if the optical system introduces aberrations, then such a similarity does not exist. But let us assume that the optical system is ideal and the observation is carried out in an ideal calm atmosphere. It turns out that even in this case the image is not exactly like the observed object. There are images that are caused by an irreducible cause - diffraction of light waves.

Diffraction phenomena occur when waves pass near objects. These phenomena occur in the same way for waves of any physical nature. The easiest way to perform diffraction experiments is with waves on the surface of the wave. For this purpose, you can use the installation described in the article “Waves in a shallow dish” (Kvant No. 1, 1971). We will talk about diffraction of light waves. If you place an object with a small hole in the path of the waves, you can observe a characteristic diffraction phenomenon - the divergence of the wave beam. Figure 5 illustrates the phenomenon. In diffraction theory, it shows that the divergence angle of the wave beam is approximately equal to $\varphi \approx \frac{\lambda}{D}$, where $\lambda$ is the length of the incident wave, $D$ is the size of the hole. If a screen is placed at a sufficiently large distance $L$ behind the hole, you can see a characteristic diffraction pattern on it, consisting of a number of alternating light and dark areas (see Fig. 5). The radius of the central light spot, which accounts for a significant portion of the energy of the wave beam (about 85%), is approximately equal to $L \cdot \frac{\varphi}{2} = \frac{\lambda}{D} \cdot L$.

Thus, due to diffraction, the waves enter the geometric shadow regions. We can say that a diffracted wave beam is a collection of waves whose propagation directions lie in the range from $-\frac{\varphi}{2}$ to $+\frac{\varphi}{2}$. If a lens with focal length $F$ is placed in the path of such a beam, then the diffraction pattern in the focal plane of the lens is similar to the theme, as shown in figure 6. The radius of the central light spot is $R \approx \frac{\varphi}{2} \cdot F \approx \frac{\lambda}{D} \cdot F$.

Diffraction leads to the fact that the beams do not converge behind the lens at one point (focus), as is obtained according to the rules of geometric optics, but form an illuminated area (see Fig. 6; the scale of the diffraction pattern of high magnification). The last step left is to remove the diaphragm with the hole. Then the role of such a diaphragm will be played by the lens frame, and in the formula described above that determines the size of the central spot, $D$ must be understood as the diameter of the lens itself.
