say a few words about the eye itself before turning to the phone.

In this case, we will talk about the replacement ability of the eye to rearrange (accommodate) when observing objects located at different distances. It is known that the eye has a significant area of ​​accommodation. A normal person has prostration over distances from 10 cm to infinity. In cases where the eye is equipped with a telescope (or other visual device), the image of the object created in the device must, of course, be located in the area of ​​accommodation. When considering the path of rays in optical instruments, it is assumed that the eye is accommodated to infinity. This means that rays from each point in the image of an object must hit the eye with a parallel ray. In this case, the path of rays in optical instruments turns out to be the simplest (the so-called telescopic path of rays).

Phones are different. Depending on what optical systems are used as an object and an eyepiece, several types of telescopes are distinguished. The lens of a telescope can be a lens (or a system of lenses) (Fig. 1, a, b). Such telescopes are called refractors. If a mirror system is used as an object (for example, such as in Fig. 1, c), the telescope is called a reflector.

We will not discuss the advantages and disadvantages of various optical systems here. To answer the questions that we have posed to ourselves, it is enough to consider one of the optical schemes, for example, the simplest telescope - the Kepler tube (Fig. 1, a).

The objective of the Kepler tube is a linear-focus collecting lens ($L_1$).
