A detailed proof of the correctness of the above method is presented to the reader. Let us note three cases that need to be considered, and a way to graphically find the complexes (!) roots of the original equation.

Case 1. The radius of the circle is greater than the ordinama of the center.

The circle intersects the $Ox$ axis at points $B(x_1, 0)$ and $C(x_2, 0)$ (Fig. 1).

Case 2. The radius of the circle is equal to the ordiname of the center, the circle touches the $Ox$ axis at the point $B(x_1, 0)$ (Fig. 2).

In this case, the equation has equal real roots, the abscissa of the tangent point is

$$x_{1,2} = -\frac{b}{2a}.$$

Case 3. The radius of the circle is less than the ordina of the center, the circle does not have common lobes with the axis $Ox$ (Fig. 3).

In this case, the equation has complexes and conjugate roots:

$$x_{1,2} = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm \frac{\sqrt{4ac-b^2}}{2a} i.$$

Fig. 3.

The real part of a complex root is expressed by the segment $OB = -\frac{b}{2a}$ – the abscissa of the center. The abscissa value of the coefficient of the minimum part is expressed by the tangent segment $BC$.

Using the above method, you can easily check the study of the roots of a quadratic equation for the sign and geometric proof of the Vieta formula. However, in this case it is impossible to use Vieta’s formulas when justifying the method, therefore, in conclusion, we indicate the algebraic interpretation of the proposed graphical solution to the quadratic equation.

Instead of the equation

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$

Let us consider the system of equations equivalent to it

$$\begin{cases} ax^2 + bx + c = ay^2 - (c + a)y = 0, \\ y = 0. \end{cases}$$

Obviously, if a quadratic equation has a root $x_1$, then the system has a solution $x=x_1$, $y=0$, and vice versa. The first equation of the system defines the circle (check!), and the second defines the $Ox$ axis. At the intersection we obtain points of the form $(x_i, 0)$, where $x_i$ are the roots of the quadratic equation.
