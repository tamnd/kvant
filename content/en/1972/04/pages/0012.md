OPTIC
TELESCOPE

V. E. Belonuchkin
S. M. Kozel

The telescope is rightfully considered one of the greatest inventions of all time. Centuries have passed since the first primitive instrument discovered the Galileo of Jupiter's moons, bringing with it enormous progress in all fields of science. The significance of the telescope as a tool for understanding the world has not changed. It reveals to us the vastness of the Universe that is inaccessible to observation with the naked eye.

Before the invention of photography astrophotometric telescopes predawnacularly exclusively for visual observations. And the telescope still remains, first and foremost, a visual instrument. The telescope's design is based on simple physical principles, which we will discuss in this article.

The visual telescope performs two main functions. First, it creates a clear image of a distant object on the retina, greatly magnified compared to that created by the naked eye, and this allows one to discern details that are inaccessible to the naked eye. Secondly, the telescope creates the light of a celestial body and transmits it to our eye in a narrow beam.

Meanwhile, in books on optics you can find the following statements:
“Even in the most powerful telescope, stars appear to us as luminous points” and “Images of objects observed in a telescope cannot be brighter than when observed with the naked eye.”

What is a telescope used for, what can and cannot be seen with a telescope? To answer these questions, you need to understand the basic structure of the telescope.
