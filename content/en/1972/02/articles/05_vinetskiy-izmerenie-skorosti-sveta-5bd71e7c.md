Even 300 years BC, Greek scientists already knew that light travels in a straight line, has the property of being reflected from the eye surfaces, and that the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. Ancient knowledge is also about the refraction of light. Claudius Ptolemy, the great astronomer and mathematician of antiquity, compiled tables of his measurements of the angles of incidence and refraction. But there was no consensus on the issue of the speed of light in ancient science. Most scientists of that time, including Aristotle, believed that the speed of light was infinitely high - after all, it could not be measured even approximately by any of the instruments and methods that existed at that time. However, 900 years ago, the Arab scientist and physician Avicenna proposed that the speed of light, although very high, is finite. His contemporary Algazen also agreed with him. But, of course, none of them could support their opinion with facts or experiments.

One way or another, all generations over the centuries have passed or interest in the nature of light, as evidenced by the myths, legends, and traditions that have reached us. This inexhaustible interest in natural phenomena was at first the only motive that prompted scientists to accept the experimental measurement of the speed of light. The increasingly exciting idea of ​​the “luminiferous ether”, the question of the corpuscular or wave nature of light, and then the relative theory required an accurate knowledge of the speed of light. In our time, the precise determination of this quantity has purely practical significance. It is necessary to know it when calculating the energy of nuclear reactions, and for measuring distances between space objects in connection with the expansion of space research, and in many other equally important problems.

Using this method, Foucault obtained the value $c = 298000$ km/sec for the speed of light in air.

In accordance with Arago's original plan, Foucault used his installation to change the speed of light in water. To do this, he slightly changed the property by equipping it with two symmetrically located spherical mirrors and placing water between one of them and the rotating mirror. This allows him to compare the speed of light in water and air and find that the speed of light in water is $3/4$ of its value in air. This result was the final blow to Newton's corpuscular theory of light.

The methods of physics and Foucault made it possible to change the speed of light with an accuracy of $\pm 500$ km/sec.

In 1873, Alfred Cornu, who repeated the experiment of physics, changed the speed of light with an accuracy of $\pm 200$ km/sec.

In 1877, the American physicist Albert Michelson, the conditions of light with an accuracy of $\pm 50$ km/sec.

But Michelson was not satisfied with the result. From 1877 until the end of his life, he repeatedly returned to changing the speed of light, continuously improving his installation. The value $c = 299796 \pm 4$ km/sec obtained by Michels was considered for a long time to be the most accurate.

In the problem of changing the speed of light, Michelson's experiments completed a stage that can be called classical. But even before the end of Michelson’s work, new experiments were carried out, already on a new technical and ideological basis, provided by the rapid development of physics. These experiments continue to the present day; their goal is to establish the most accurate value for the speed of light.
