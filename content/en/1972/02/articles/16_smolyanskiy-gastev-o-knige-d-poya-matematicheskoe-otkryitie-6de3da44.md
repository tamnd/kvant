About Polya's book "Mathematical Discovery"

Readers in our country have known the author of this book for a long time. Back in 1937-1938. Together with G. Szege, he published the two-volume “Problems and Theorems from Analysis”, in 1957 - “Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning”, in 1961 - “How to Solve a Problem”.
In the preface to the last book, D. Poya wrote: “A major scientific discovery provides a solution to a major problem, but in solving any problem there are grains of discovery.” And further: “The author sometimes puts himself in the position of a student, sometimes in the position of a student... the author argues from the point of view of a person who is neither a teacher nor a student, but is simply interested in solving the problem facing him.” This “third person” is, of course, the author himself, a famous scientist and mathematician. As for his “part-time” performance of the other two mentioned roles, it seems to be not just a successful literary and pedagogical find. Such “part-time work” is the most important component of György Pólya’s creative credo, which he professes with rare constancy.
In his book “Mathematical Discovery,” Pólya continues and develops the thoughts expressed in earlier books (although none of them requires prior knowledge of the others - this is where the author’s pedagogical skill comes into play). True to his principles, Polya doesn't so much talk about solving problems as he shows how it's done. And, of course, he invites you to follow him. And then on a separate path. “Problem solving is a practical art, like swimming, skating, or playing the piano; You can learn it only by good imitation and constant practice. And in this book you will not find a magic key that opens all the doors, it will not teach you how to solve all the problems, but it will give you many good role models and opportunities for receiving and opportunities for receiving and opportunities for receiving.”
But remember: if you want to learn how to swim, then boldly enter the water, and if you want to learn how to solve problems, then solve them! The book is literally written with interesting problems and no less interesting approaches to solving them. It would be tempting, needless to say, to try to give in the solution at least some idea of ​​these problems. But such an attempt would immediately spoil the book and thereby distort the idea of ​​the entire book as a whole. After the session, the content of a novel, even a very long one, can be retold in five, well, ten pages. What about a collection of thousands of small poems? Bring the entire ten best? Two tens? What if everything is fine? And who can guarantee the objectivity of the choice?
For now, we will limit ourselves to reprinting, without any changes, without any abbreviations and without any comments, one paragraph from Polya’s book. We do not claim that this is the best paragraph. Or the most typical one, perhaps. We just really like him. And he really wants the reader to pursue him. And if he himself later finds something in the book that he likes better, it doesn’t matter. In any case, the last thing Polia himself would like to do is take advantage of his unanimous and uniform tastes. There is nothing good in this.
Understanding full well that a simple enumeration of the names of Polya’s chapter can familiarize the reader even more than its intuitive title, we nevertheless (and perhaps precisely for this reason) will not deny ourselves this satisfaction: “The method of two geometric places”, “Descartes’ method”, “Recursion”, “Super retail”, “About problems”, “Expanding the scope of the method”, “Geometric representation of the solution process”, “Plan and program”, “Problems inside problems”, “Generation of an idea”, “Mental work”, “Discipline of the mind”, “Laws of discovery”, “On learning, teaching and learning to teach”, “Conjecture and the scientific method”.
Well, isn’t everything clear here? Never mind, this means it’s time for you to accept it as a “Mathematical Discovery.” (Don't forget to stock up on paper and pencils!) And if you don't have this book yet, read at least the next postcard from it first.

A. If a circle of the same radius passes through one point, then the circle passing through the other three points of their intersection also has the same radius.

This is the theorem that we need to prove. The statement of the theorem is short and clear, but it seems to lack details. Having made a drawing (Fig. 1) and introduced suitable notations, we arrive at the following, more detailed version of the problem:
