⟦folio 42⟧

Tasks

Solutions to problems from the Kwan Problem Book can be sent no later than a month and a half after the corresponding issue of the journal is published. On the envelope, after the journal address, indicate the solutions to which problems you are sending (for example, “Kwan’s Problem Book” M126, F128). At the beginning of the letter, indicate your last name, first name, patronymic, six-digit postal code and address (as well as the class and school where you are a teacher). The solution to each problem must be written on a separate sheet(s); Please include your last name at the end of each decision. A star mark for a task of increased difficulty.

M126. A polygon circumscribed around a circle of radius $r$ is somehow cut into triangles. Prove that the sum of the radii of the inscribed circles of these triangles is greater than $r$.

I. D. Novikov

M127. For each natural number $n$ we denote by $s(n)$ the sum of its digits (in decimal notation). Let's call a natural number $m$ special if it cannot be represented as $m=n+s(n)$, where $n$ is some natural number. (For example, the number 117 is not special because $117=108+s(108)=108+9$, but the number 121, as you can easily see, is special.) Is it true that there are only a finite number of special numbers?

M128. Find the ratio of the sides of a triangle, one of whose medians is divided by the incircle into three equal parts.

M129. a) 12 $n$ milk was poured into a bucket. How, using only jugs 5 and 7 $n$, to divide the milk into two equal parts?

6) * Solve the general problem: for which $a$ and $b$ can we divide $(a+b)n$ milk in half and use only the jugs in $a$ $n$, $b$ $n$ and $(a+b)n$ ?*.

V. V. Ushchakov

M130. What is the maximum number of points that can be placed?
a) on a plane;
b) * in space, so that none of the triangles with vertices at these points are obtuse?

G. A. Galperin

F138. An astronaut with a mass of 100 kg is outside a spacecraft whose mass is 5 $m$, on a halyard 64 $m$ long. Find the tension of the halyard if the ship is located between the astronaut and the Earth on the line connecting their centers of gravity.

When calculating, assume that the ship is moving in a circular orbit, the height of the crust $h$ from the Earth's surface is negligible compared to the radius of the Earth ($R=6400$ km).

*) In one transfusion from one vessel to another, you can pour out everything that is there, or fill the second vessel to the top.
