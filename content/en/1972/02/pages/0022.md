⟦folio 20⟧

Even 300 years BC, Greek scientists already knew that light travels in a straight line, has the property of being reflected from the eye surfaces, and that the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. Ancient knowledge is also about the refraction of light. Claudius Ptolemy, the great astronomer and mathematician of antiquity, compiled tables of his measurements of the angles of incidence and refraction. But there was no consensus on the issue of the speed of light in ancient science. Most scientists of that time, including Aristotle, believed that the speed of light was infinitely high - after all, it could not be measured even approximately by any of the instruments and methods that existed at that time. However, 900 years ago, the Arab scientist and physician Avicenna proposed that the speed of light, although very high, is finite. His contemporary Algazen also agreed with him. But, of course, none of them could support their opinion with facts or experiments.

One way or another, all generations over the centuries have passed or interest in the nature of light, as evidenced by the myths, legends, and traditions that have reached us. This inexhaustible interest in natural phenomena was at first the only motive that prompted scientists to accept the experimental measurement of the speed of light. However, from the largest experimental physicists

tator Albert Michelson, who devoted almost his entire life to measuring the speed of light, when asked by correspondents: “Why is this needed?” replied: “This is terribly interesting!” But even before Michelson’s work, determining the speed of light and its dependence on various conditions acquired the character of not only satisfying the curiosity of scientists. The increasingly exciting idea of ​​the “luminiferous ether”, the question of the corpuscular or wave nature of light, and then the relative theory required an accurate knowledge of the speed of light. In our time, the precise determination of this quantity has purely practical significance. It is necessary to know it when calculating the energy of nuclear reactions, and for measuring distances between space objects in connection with the expansion of space research, and in many other equally important problems.
