When warming occurs, the temperature of these objects turns out to be lower than the temperature of the surrounding air, and water vapor in the air condenses on them. If there is little vapor in the air, then red fluff flakes are obtained. When the air humidity is high, cold objects are covered with a crust of ice. Water simply condenses on cold objects and then freezes.

Particularly interesting are the patterns that cover the windows of carts, buses and trams in winter. In a sudden position, the temperature of the windows becomes lower than the air temperature in the room. Vapor molecules in the humid air in the room settle on them, forming red patterns. It is also important that the air in the room is not very humid. Otherwise, the steam first condenses on the glass and then freezes, forming a layer of ice. Patterns do not appear on the window if the window is open. In this case, the air temperature in the room near the glass decreases, becoming the same as the temperature of the glass itself. In the ice patterns that grow on the glass, you can see the large shapes that snow crystals can take.

You can watch the growth of snow crystals on your windows at home. However, unfortunately, patterns on windows are short-lived, and it is difficult to change the conditions of their growth. But you can “grow” patterns that are very similar to ice ones, using not water, but dissolving hyposulfite (it is sold in stores in the photo departments) or Carlsbad cols (you can buy it at the pharmacy). These patterns should not differ from ice ones and, by knighting them, you can change the growth conditions - the rate of heat supply or removal, the solution concentration (a change in the solution concentration corresponds to a change in air humidity), etc. The growth of crystals forming the patterns can be monitored using a microcoppa.

You can get the pattern like this. First, prepare a saturated mixture in six batches from which you will work. Wipe the glass with this brush and place it under the Fan. After about 5 minutes, the water will evaporate and a pattern will appear on the glass. The hardest thing here is to wet the glass well. The fact is that water generally does not wet the surface of the glass and does not spread over it, but forms droplets. Then, together with the patterns, you will get spots in which just dried sediment remains.

If you do not blow the glass or apply a lot of abrasive on it, then together with the pattern you will get pieces of crystals, they will grow “from below” (from the surface of the glass) and will appear above the glass. We want the crystals to grow small and onto all glass surfaces at once. In order for the solution to wet the glass, the surface of the glass must be thoroughly cleaned with gasoline or alcohol (you can also use cologne). It’s even better to use cols alcohol solution rather than water. Of course, you may not get the pattern right the first time - don’t despair. Remember that any physical experiment may require several attempts.

The artificial patterns you receive can be shaped. Two methods of formation are convenient.

The first is basic: patterns are photographed on film and then printed on photographic paper.

The second one is not entirely ordinary. The patterns are grown on a narrow plane of glass. This strip is placed in a photo enlarger along with the film, and is printed directly onto the paper. The crystals that form the patterns are non-transparent and look black when transmitted by light (on the print after development - white). Places where there are no crystals allow light to pass through and appear black on the print. This is how the pattern placed on the cover of our magazine was obtained. It was grown from the Carlsbad cols.
