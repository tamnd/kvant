⟦folio 60⟧

In applied exams at institutes, there are often problems in which one has to decide about some location of a sphere (or several spheres) relative to parameters. Solutions to such problems are based essentially on the following three facts:

1) if a plane (or straight line) touches a sphere, then consider from the center of the sphere to this plane (or straight line) equal to the radius of the sphere;
2) if a sphere touches two intersecting planes, then the center of the sphere lies in the bisector plane of a two-dimensional area formed by the empirical planes;
3) if the parameter is described around a sphere, then

$$V = \frac{1}{3} rS,$$

(1)

food $r$ is the radius of the sphere, $V$ and $S$ are the volume and total surface parameters.

These facts are known to every student from a school mathematics course. But in mathematics it is not enough just to know the formulations of the theorems - it is important to understand these theorems and be able to apply them to solve problems.

As a rule, the problems proposed in entrance exams are formulated simply, and most applicants quickly find some way to a solution. But often the chosen method turns out to be very complex and cumbersome, requiring long calculations. Thus, it is very difficult to obtain the complete final result. For each problem, you need to try to find the shortest and most “elegant” solution possible.

Sometimes, during an oral exam, the applicant is not asked to solve a problem, but only to indicate the path to solution, thereby checking how well the applicant is oriented in this section of the theory.

In this article we will look at problems in which the use of several geometric messages helps to find a simple and beautiful solution.

Task 1 (MIPT, 1966). Inside a regular metrahedron with an edge of the aitripent, there are four equal spheres so that each sphere touches three other spheres and three faces of the metrahedron. The name of these spheres.

Solution. Let $ABCD$ be a given tetrahedron, $O$ the center of a sphere within it, $r$ the radius of this sphere; $M, N, K, L$ are the data centers of the sphere, their radii will be denoted by $x$.

The tetrahedron $MNKL$ is correct, since each of its edges is equal to $2x$. The faces of the tetrahedron $MNKL$ are parallel to the faces of the tetrahedron $ABCD$ (prove it!), and the point $O$ is removed from each face of the tetrahedron $MNKL$ by a distance $r-x$. Therefore, point $O$ is the center of the sphere inscribed in the tetrahedron
