The last statement draws our attention to figure 3. This figure is somewhat remarkable; it reminds us of something familiar. (What exactly?)

Fig. 3.

Of course, for any of the quadrilaterals depicted in figure 3, for example, the OLAM quadrilateral, all four sides are equal to each other, that is, these quadrilaterals are rhommes.

The rhombus is a familiar figure to us; by mentally highlighting it on our figure, we can “see” the entire figure better. (What does this figure remind you of as a whole?)

Opposite sides of a rhombus are parallel. Based on this circumstance, it is possible to divide the 9 segments from which the figure depicted in figure 3 is composed into three groups,

each of which includes only segments parallel to each other; for example, into one of such segment groups AL, MO and BK. (What can this figure remind us of now?)

We must not forget the goal we are striving for. Let us assume that the conclusion of our theorem is true. By plotting on figure the center E of the circle e and its three radii, which end up at points A, B and C (Fig. 4), we (presumably) obtain new rhombuses, new parallel segments. (What does the whole figure remind us of now?)

Well, of course, figure 4 represents the projection

Fig. 4.

12 edges of a paralleliped arranged in such a way that all these projections have the same length.

Figure 3 is the projection of an “opaque paralleliped”: we see only 3 of its faces, 7 vertices and 9 edges, while 3 faces, 1 vertex and 3 edges on figure are not visible. This figure is part of figure 4, but such a part that defines the entire figure that interests us. If the paralleliped and the direction of projection are worked out in such a way that the projections of the nine edges shown in figure 3 are equal to r (i.e., what they should be according to the conditions of the problem), then the projections of the three remaining edges should also be equal to r. Three segments of length r emanate from the projection E of the eighth, invisible vertex, and this projection itself is the center of a circle passing through points A, B and C, the radius of which is equal to r.

Our theorem is proven, and it is proven with the help of an unexpected ingenious idea, which consists in the fact that we consider a flat figure as a projection of a spatial figure.

(This proof uses stereometric concepts. It seems to me that the problem here is not great, especially since it can be easily corrected. In fact, since we now know that the position of the center E can be characterized by the weight of the prime, the lengths of the segments EA, EB and EC can be reduced to distance without resorting to any stereometry. However, we will not insist on this point of view here.)
