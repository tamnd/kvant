Genius of the 18th century

A. Ya. Yakovlev

In May 1727, on a warm spring day, a twenty-year-old young man was walking along the Nevskaya Prospektiva. He has just arrived from Basel, Switzerland, where he received his first degree.

Back in 1721, the famous Johann Bernoulli, a professor at the University of Basel, noticed the unusual abilities of a fourteen-year-old student. For a number of years, young Leonhard Euler spent every Saturday at Bernoulli's house. Then he became friends with the sons of his teacher - Nikolai and Daniil, later professors at the St. Petersburg Academy.

On the recommendation of the Bernoulli brothers, young master of arts Leonard Euler also received an invitation to St. Petersburg.

After just a few months, the newcomer spoke the “incomprehensible” Russian language quite fluently. Soon his first works appeared in academic publications: works on mathematics, mechanics, and physics. From the next year, not a single volume of the Academy’s works was published without several works by the young scientist. And at the age of 26, Leonhard Euler, the future “common teacher of all mathematicians of the second half of the 18th century,” became an academician.

Over the course of 14 years, Euler wrote more than 75 major works. He gave lectures to students of the Academic University, took exams, compiled an excellent “Manual of Carithmetic”, dealt with the design of mechanical saws and fire pumps, worked in the Commission of Weights and Measures, in the Geographical Department, where he supervised the creation of a general map of Russia.

L. Euler (1707—1783)

Euler was a lecturing calculator. He quickly and accurately completed cumbersome calculations. Without limiting himself to the purely mathematical side of the matter, he occupied himself deeply with potatoes and drew quite a few maps himself. “Russian geography, through my work and Mr. Heinsus’s, has been brought into much better condition,” Euler later wrote. “Apart from France, there is almost no land that has a better map.”

In the early forties, the situation at the St. Petersburg Academy changed dramatically. The leadership of Akademiei was captured by Biron, Schumacher and other unnatural figures. In 1741, Euler accepted the present invitation of the Russian King Frederick II and moved to Berlin. At first he was a member of the Board of the Academy and the director of the mathematics class (department), and from 1759 he actually headed the Berlin Academy of Sciences.

In Berlin, Euler continued his work in the fields of mathematics and mechanics. However, Euler wrote most of his theoretical works

All this time, Euler continued to remain an honorary member of the Russian Academy of Sciences and received a large pension for those times (200 rubles per year). Leonhard Euler edited the mathematical sections of Russian journals, acquired books and instructions for St. Petersburg; In his apartment for years he had students - adjuncts from St. Petersburg. Written works were sent to him for feedback. In 1747, Schumacher sent Euler two works by M.V. Lomonosov, hoping to receive a negative response and remove Lomonosov from scientific activities. But Euler's response ruined Schumacher's plans.

“All his works,” wrote Euler, “are not only good, but also excellent, or he will explain physical and chemical materials... with such thoroughness that I am completely confident in the validity of his explanation.”

Since June 1766, Euler was again in St. Petersburg. By this time his vision had deteriorated sharply. Back in the thirties, while engaged in painstaking calculations and drawing charts, Euler lost sight in his left eye; Now the right eye could see almost nothing. The world's leading scientists were alarmed to learn about the deterioration of his vision. However, Euler himself treated the loss of vision with great skill. Possessing a phenomenal memory, he learned to write the universe with chalk on a blackboard and at the same time gave oral interpretations. Despite complete blindness, Euler showed an amazing ability to work, dictating entire books to academicians N. I. Fuss, M. E. Golovin (nephew of M. V. Lomonosov), his son Johann Albrecht and numerous students. At the same time, he did not stop administrative and organizational work at the Academy.

On September 18, 1783, Euler began to feel ill.

He only managed to say “I’m dying” and lost consciousness. A few hours later he died from a cerebral hemorrhage.

In the figurative expression of the French scientist Condorcet, “Euler stopped living and calculating.” He was buried at the Smolensk cemetery in St. Petersburg. The inscription on the monument read: “To Leonard Euler - St. Petersburg Academy.” In 1956, Euler's city was moved to the Leningrad nekropol.

Euler lived a long, productive life. Russia became his second homeland. Five of his children, 38 children, grew up here. The descendants of the great scientist still live in our country.

Leonhard Euler was elected academician in the countries of the world. He left important works on various branches of mathematics, mechanics, physics and a number of applied sciences; many of them are still included in the courses of computing educational institutions to this day. The two-volume “Marine Science” played a significant role in the development of shipbuilding and navigation in the 18th century. The “Lunyi Theory of Motion” and the tables based on it have been used by sailors from all over the world for more than a hundred years. The three-volume Dioptrics and other works on physics helped create high-quality telescopes and microscopes. It is difficult to even list the branches of science and technology in which the great scientist worked. But first and foremost he was a mathematician. His works on mechanics, physics, and astronomy, as a rule, represent a mathematical formulation and mathematical solution of the problems under consideration.

At the beginning of our year, the Swiss Society of Natural Scientists decided to publish Euler's complete works. Over 850 major scientific works comprised 72 large volumes.

Just to understand Euler’s scientific legacy requires an entire human life...

On the “map” of modern mathematics, the name of Euler will appear at every step. In mathematical analysis, when interpreting irrational functions, Euler's substitutions are used, reducing the problem to a simpler one: the interpretation of rational functions. However, among the simplest approximate methods for solving differential equations is Euler's broken line method. In higher sections of analysis, the so-called Euler integrals (B-function and G-function) play an important role. In number theory, Euler's comparison theorem and Euler's function are known - the number of natural numbers that are less than a given number and coprime to it. In mechanics, when describing the motion of bodies, Euler's equations are used, which determine the relative position of various coordinate systems. In hydrodynamics, the Euler number is considered - the ratio of the pressure difference at two characteristic points of a fluid flow. One could name ten more functions, formulas, and theorems associated with the name of Euler.

In conclusion, we propose to solve the following problem, proposed by L. Euler. Let us find the midpoints of the altitude segments of the triangle from the orthocenter to each of the vertices (lobes K, M, L on figure) of the Euler point. Prove that the midpoints of the sides of any triangle, the bases of its altitudes and the Euler points lie on the same circle. This circle is called the circle of nine points, or Euler's circle. Its radius is equal to half the radius of the circle circumscribed about the same triangle. The line connecting the orthocenter of the triangle with the center of the circumscribed circle is called Euler's straight line. The center of the Euler circle lies on this line exactly midway between the orthocenter and the center of the circumcircle.
