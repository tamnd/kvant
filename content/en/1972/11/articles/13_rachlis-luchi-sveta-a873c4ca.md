RAYS OF LIGHT X. Rachlis

Recently, the Mir publishing house published a small popular science book by X. Rachlis, “Physics in the Bath.” We place here one of the chapters of this book. Its translation was carried out by P.I. Nudelman.

At the end of this issue of the magazine, in the “Reviews, Bibliography” section, you can read a short story about this book.

Fill the bath with a little water so that its depth reaches about 10 centimeters. Let's turn off the tap and wait until all the waves subside, and then look down. There we will see an image of our face, almost the same as in a real mirror, only a little foggier. Let's make it brighter by illuminating the face with a flashlight.

Now let’s try to carefully pinch the water with our finger. You see, the image was distorted - at first it trembled, swayed, and then gradually calmed down and became motionless again. Now it has already taken its original form. Let's boil the water harder. Now the image has completely disappeared.

Why does the water surface require a mirror image? And why is it not as absent as in an ordinary mirror? Why does the image become brighter if you hold a flashlight to your face? Why does it disappear when the surface of the water becomes rough?

A good way to clarify all these questions is to start by shining a flashlight on the surface of the water, having first turned off the bathroom light. It is best to put a cardboard tube about 30 centimeters long on the front of the flashlight. If any of you are unable to obtain a ready-made tube, you can make your own by rolling up several sheets of paper and securing the end with adhesive tape.

In such a tube, a flashlight produces an almost non-diverging beam of light, as shown in figure 1. Let’s direct this beam down, on the surface of the water, and see what happens to it.

To begin with, we will hold the flashlight at an angle. Is light reflected from the surface of the water? There are several ways to check this. One of them is to try to find a light spot on a wall of water. Another beam of reflections can even be seen, especially if there are dust particles in the air. Look, it expands, it becomes blurry, it distorts, it trembles and sways.

Many rays (very narrow beams) of light sent by a flashlight strike the water at almost the same angle. If the surface of the water is calm and level, then it will reflect all the rays of light at the same angles, that is, all the rays are reflected accordingly and the beam of light will remain collected as it was. That's why, when the surface of the eye, a small bright spot forms on the wall.

When we “disturbed” the water, ripples formed on its surface. And although the reflected rays still depart from the surface at the same angle at which the incident rays arrived at each section of the water, now each section of the surface changes its position all the time (Fig. 3). Because of this, the angles of each individual beam of light towards the water are constantly changing, and the beam is reflected each time in a different place on the wall. The beam of light jumps, expands - it becomes smaller and larger, depending on how disturbed the surface of the water is.

So, only the eyes of the surface can give a concentrated reflection. On a smooth surface, reflections diverge in all directions.

Why does a mirror image form on the surface? Let's say someone looks into the water, trying to see the reflection of a small object A (Fig. 4). In figure the eye B of the looking person is shown greatly enlarged - this allows the best to understand what is happening with the light. Suppose that rays of sunlight fall on object A and are reflected by it.

As a rule, objects do not have such eyes as the surfaces of calm water or mirrors. Generally speaking, if there are irregularities on the surface of an object that are only 1/25,000 of a centimeter, this is already

Notice how rays B and Γ diverge as they slant toward the water. After being reflected from the surface, each at a different angle, they continue to diverge until they hit the eye. The eye cannot know where the rays actually come from. It seems to him that they are coming out of point F, located below the surface of the water. In other words, the eye sees a likeness, or image, of an object under the surface of the water.

In most cases, objects are larger than object A, shown in figure 4. But this does not change the fair reasoning. Each point of a large object gives its own image. In the eye, the image of all these points combines into one image, which looks exactly the same as the object itself.

Of course, to create such an image, you need a very smart reflective surface that allows the rays to be reflected in such a way that after reflection they are positioned relative to each other in the same way as before reflection. This is how it works on the ocular surface, and an uneven surface distorts the image or reflects the rays at random.

Any ocular surface reflects light just like the ocular surface of water. Most mirrors are made of very eye-catching glass, coated on the back with a thin layer of a good reflective medium (or other metal). A well-polished table or polished floor also gives a mirror reflection, as does window glass. Can serve as a mirror

Now let's try to answer the question: why is the image in water not as clear as in a mirror? This is due to several reasons. Let's take the flashlight again and illuminate the bathtub filled with water. Is all the light reflected? No, part of it, and a larger part, passes through the water and illuminates the bottom of the bath.

The proportion of light energy reflected from water is small—about 10 percent. As for the mirror, the opposite picture is observed there. A layer of metal located on the back of the mirror reflects most of the light falling on it—about 95 percent. This is why the images we see in an ordinary mirror are much brighter than the images on the surface of the water.

The second reason why the image in the water appears darker to us is that when we look into the bath, we are blocking the light with our heads. The light from a lamp hanging from the ceiling or sunlight pouring in through a window can only fall on the face in a roundabout way - it first comes from the walls, from the floor or from the walls of the bathtub. Needless to say, since the face is illuminated, the image turns out dim. When we hang a flashlight on our face, part of the light is reflected directly from the face, then goes into the water, and as a result the image becomes brighter.

Curved straight stick

What happens to light that is not reflected from the surface of the bath water? To answer this question, we continue with the following experiment. Let's pour a bath, as usual for bathing, and lower a straight stick into the water, tilting it slightly. Now, if we look at the stick, it no longer appears straight to us: the part of it that is in the water seems to be bent upward (Fig. 5).

Well, what if you take the stick out of the water? Then the broken part will again be completely straight, and the part immersed in water still seems to be curved upward.

Now let's take the stick completely out of the water - it is perfectly straight, just as it was. The bend has completely straightened.

It is probably clear to you that the stick does not bend at all when it is immersed in water and does not straighten when we take it out of the water. If anyone doubts this, let him send the “bent” part of the stick that is in the water. To the touch it is completely straight. There is clearly some kind of optical illusion taking place here, due to which we see something that is not really there. A mirror image is also a deception of this kind. Objects that seem to us to be behind the mirror are actually located in a different place.

Let's explore this phenomenon deeper. To do this, we will use a flashlight with a tube, which we made earlier when we were talking about mirrors.

Please note that point D is located above point B, which is why the stick seems to be bent upward. In addition, the stick looks shorter than it actually is. These two rules apply to all objects immersed in water. If an object is only partially submerged in water, its underwater part always appears shorter. Moreover, all objects in the water appear to be closer to the surface than they actually are.

Have any of you ever shot fish with an underwater gun? Anyone who has had to, probably knows how difficult it is. The fact is that the fish is not at all where we see it. If you aim directly at the fish, the arrow will certainly go higher. Therefore, you need to aim a little higher, making corrections for artificial light rays.

There is only one case where a very narrow parallel beam of light will not bend - if it goes vertically, that is, straight down. In this case, the light enters the water at a right angle and goes down without a bend.
