ABOUT THE STRUCTURE OF ICE

W.G.Bragg

The article by the famous English physicist William Bragg “On the structure of ice” appeared in 1938. In 1939, this article, translated by N. A. Shishakov, was published in the journal “Success of Physical Sciences”. Below we present a shortened version of this article.

The publication was prepared by I. Sh. Slyubodetsky.

In recent years, studies of the crystalline nature of ice and snow have revealed some facts that provide new explanations for old problems. However, one of them is the movement of ice and snow on the mountains, which has always been a subject of surprise. To determine the nature of the majestic movements of the Alpine glaciers, attempts were made at systematic measurements in the middle of the 19th century. It turned out that the ice flows, adapting to the curves and slopes in the valleys, like water. It was then established that the middles of glaciers move faster than the edges. At that time, I encountered great difficulties in explaining this curious plate-like structure of ice, which is at the same time a very brittle substance, but now this problem can be considered solved.

It turns out that there is an amazing connection between the majestic pictures of the movement of glaciers and the smallest details of the structure of water molecules and ice crystals.

The subject of previous attention is such an amazing regularity in the structure of snowflakes, which is especially striking when observing them in countries of the world. However, only at the end of the century this fact was subjected to systematic observation, which was then the subject of classification. At the same time, the perfect state between all six rays in any snowflake and at the same time the huge difference in the structure of various snowflakes seemed extremely interesting and puzzling. The first attempt at a physical explanation for the so-called laminarity of ice was proposed by Tyndall and Huxley in 1857. The basis of the explanation

It was based on the remarkable phenomenon of freezing of pieces of ice brought into contact, observed by Faraday. Such freezing occurs with the weakest contact when the pieces are in water (Fig. 3). Faraday said that water molecules pass from a liquid to a solid much more easily when they are surrounded by a significant already solidified mass. A thin layer of water near the places where both pieces come into contact hardens and holds them together, even if it could not freeze on its own, being away from the mass of ice. According to this view, freezing depends not only on temperature, but also on environmental conditions. Likewise, water vapor inside a crack, that is, between two surfaces, will condense and turn into ice much more easily than in an open place, where contact occurs with only one surface of the ice.

Ice under tension will crack. Tyndall and Huxley proposed that the cracks were "healed" by the deposition of vapor or liquid emanating from the surrounding parts of the ice. Therefore, the ice mass can adapt to the shape of its bed and the glacier as a whole, like a flow.

James Thomson in 1860 proposed a different explanation for this phenomenon. Having recognized the fact that Faraday observed, Thomson will nevertheless respond to his previous explanation; he attributed the bonding of pieces of ice to each other to the pressure exerted by the pieces

Indeed, if water expands when it freezes, then any pressure, insofar as it is affected by this expansion, must also result in freezing, and this is equivalent to moving beyond the freezing point. Therefore, at 0° ice under pressure should melt. This is actually observed experimentally. If any sharp object, for example, the tip of a knife, is placed on a piece of ice, then the sharpness penetrates deeper not because the ice is cut or crumbles, but because the ice melts under pressure. According to Thomson, when two pieces of ice are pressed together, melting should occur at the point of contact; the resulting water flows out and freezes somewhere else, since it is no longer under pressure.

Tyndall demonstrated the effect of freezing ice pieces in the following way. The ice was broken into small pieces, which were then compressed into a special shape. The result was a meltable mass of ice. In this way, by exposing ice powder to a pressure of several hundred kilograms per 1 cm², ice cups and other objects of various shapes can be produced.

The explanation for the phenomenon of glacier flow seems to be now becoming very obvious. According to Thomson and Tindalio, the change in shape of a moving glacier occurs as a result of the melting of ice in places of high pressure and the subsequent solidification of the release of melting water.

Of course, Thomson's statement that ice melts under pressure is absolutely correct. However, we cannot agree with his assumption that the Faraday freezing of floating pieces of ice is based on the same principle. Thomson argued that even with the simple contact of the pieces, some pressure will arise due to capillary action *). This pressure is what causes the Faraday effect. To show the unreasonableness of Thomson's idea, Faraday in 1860 set up his experiment under conditions where there was no capitular action. He made several disks out of ice, giving them a flat-convex shape, and let them float through the water with the convex side up. Under such conditions, the pieces could touch each other only under water (Fig. 4). With weak contact with each other, they froze together so firmly that to set all the masses in motion on the water, it was enough to act on only one point. Wooden disks, when in contact both along the waterline and under water and, therefore, in the presence of capitular action on each other, did not show any signs of fastening.

Despite this, both Faraday's and Thomson's principles failed to provide a satisfactory explanation for the movement of glaciers. For example, they did not explain the process of ice freezing at those low temperatures at which the world's glaciers are essentially frozen. However, Faraday himself pointed out that two pieces of ice do not freeze under pressure if they are cooled below zero.

The freezing of pieces of ice with each other and the melting of ice under pressure are consequences of the structural features of ice, which in turn are a consequence of the special nature of the bonds that hold the atoms and molecules of ice together. Recent years have seen significant advances in our knowledge of atomic bonds. For the present purpose it is sufficient to note that in some cases a single atom

an atom attaches other atoms to itself only at certain points on its surface. It would be more correct to express this idea by saying that the points that atoms connect with each other are distributed over the surface of the atoms according to a certain rule. In particular, a carbon atom binds four atoms to itself at points that lie on the surface at the four vertices of a regular tetrahedron. In other cases (for example, in the case of liquid routine), there is no such regular location of the connection point. Indeed, the internal properties of a liquid are determined by the absence of such guiding bonds that create the structure, or by the masking of their other bonds by stronger bonds.

One can imagine such extreme cases. In one of them, only forces of the first kind exist. This means that atoms have mutual attraction only when they are adjusted to each other by the contact points on their surface. A collection of such atoms could be either a solid body or a group of atoms not connected by mutual attraction. In the latter case, if there is sufficient thermal energy, a group of atoms should be able to form a gas. It may, however, be considered that with such obstructions a random coupling could take place. If the heat supply decreases, then this combination of atoms becomes more frequent and more permanent, which is why hardening begins in certain places. Each group of atoms thus united becomes a crystal, unless, of course, the process proceeds so rapidly as to render the formation of a true crystalline arrangement impossible. Atoms or molecules must strive to occupy places ready for them on already formed crystals. This is how crystals grow

At the other extreme, one can imagine atoms or molecules that have no directional attraction. If the thermal vibrations are not strong enough to keep the atoms or molecules isolated from each other, they will come together and form a liquid, the thickness of which will depend on how easily individual molecules can move among other molecules. With further cooling, the viscosity can increase continuously or in sudden jumps, to the point where this collection of molecules can be called a solid.

Natural substances lie between these two extremes, ice seeming to be closer to the first than other substances. Indeed, its crystals form an indefinite form of vapor. On the other hand, molecules of water vapor are continuously pulled away from the crystals by the action of the body or for some other reason and mix with the molecules of the surrounding medium. The ice “sublimates.” The ease or difficulty of condensation and separation of molecules must be determined by some correspondence between vapor density, temperature and surface conditions. In cracks, the chances of condensation are higher than in open areas, since in cracks, starting from their edges, the molecules are under double action. The detachment of molecules is more likely to occur from a convex surface. This effect is completely analogous to the effective dependence of vapor pressure on the shape of the liquid with which the vapor comes into contact, as was pointed out by Faraday. Inside the crack, the equilibrium between sublimation and precipitation must be different than outside, the latter acting at the expense of the former. In other words, the vapor pressure will decrease, that is, the freezing point temperature will increase.

At normal pressures, water is most dense at 4°C. As the temperature rises further down to the freezing point, the density becomes less and less. It is obvious that some of the molecules form sets that occupy larger spaces than molecules taken individually, since mechanical forces force atoms to take a certain location in space. At the same time, there is no need at all to consider these sets to be permanent: in all likelihood, they constantly arise, decay, and arise again.

Let us now consider the following drinks. The two main facts that require commitment are: the infinite variety of hexagonal shapes and the symmetry of each snowflake. The hexagonal shape of snowflakes is very much related to the hexagonal shape of ice crystals, but it has always been very difficult to explain why the six rays of each snowflake are so similar to each other and at the same time so different from the rays of any other snowflake. A satisfactory commitment can be given in the following way.

The whole question can be considered in the light of the same effective mechanical and precipitation processes as in the case of freezing of pieces of ice. When such a hexagonal structure is expressed from a certain core in an atmosphere of water vapor or in a liquid containing dissolved water, then the character of the posta must depend on the conditions under which this growth occurs. If there is a lot of steam in the surrounding space or the temperature is low, then growth proceeds quickly and the rays of snowflakes tend to penetrate the numerous surrounding water molecules. Growth occurs at the ends of the rays and on auxiliary branches, which form an angle of 60° with the former. If a snowflake is transferred to a space with low density

steam or with a higher temperature, then a figurative process occurs. Molecules, which are much lighter, come off from the ends of the branches than from the central regions of the snowflake, and are not deposited on it at the same speed. Thanks to this, the snowflake becomes tired in the central part, and its branches are shortened and even rounded. Since snowflakes are small in size, often no more than a few millimeters in diameter, the conditions around each of them can be considered the same at all points. If one ray lengthens, the other rays also lengthen. With all this, falling to the Earth, snowflakes can encounter a wide variety of local conditions, according to which they can take on many different forms, while the rays of each individual snowflake remain completely similar.

According to this obligation, the greater diversity of snowflake shapes is based on the existence of two opposing processes: the rapid growth of branches leading to their feathery form, and compaction in the centers of this feathery structure. In the first process growth predominates over sublimation to a greater extent than in the second, so that they differ in degree rather than in quality. The shape of each snowflake tells us about the changes in atmospheric conditions that it had to endure.

Tyndall performed an excellent experiment, with the help of which he was able to demonstrate the structure of ice. This experience is called "receiving ice flowers." A beam of light from an arc lamp is passed through a piece of ice. At various points in this piece where there are impurities or irregularities, the body is retained and causes melting. The resulting voids in this case have a hexagonal shape, since the destruction of the structure occurs in the direction exactly opposite to growth. These figures represent, so to speak, negative snowflakes. With the help of suitable equipment these figures can be projected onto the screen. If a covet with water is placed on the path of the rays from the lantern to the ice, then the heat rays are absorbed, and the growth of such voids in the crystal stops.
