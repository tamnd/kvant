LABORATORY
IN THE BATHROOM

Physics exists. In any natural phenomenon you can always find something that has already been or is being studied by physicists. Whatever a person does - sit down, lie down, get up, walk - in all this there is a reason for physical analysis. We constantly come across various physical phenomena in our daily lives and therefore we never think about these phenomena. They are too accustomed and lively. But if we were more attentive and observant, life would surround us with many interesting problems.

Many laws of physics can be studied in the school physics classroom. There, special devices will come to your aid. Well, what about at home? How many physical phenomena can be studied at home?

The answer to this question is given in the book “Physics in the Bath” by Hugh Rachlis*). Its author convincingly shows that in a common bathroom you can study a wide variety of physical phenomena - sound, light, tell, mechanical, magnetic.

In this issue of our magazine, in the “Laboratory «Kvant»” section, we publish one of the chapters of Rachlis’ book. After reading it, you will be convinced that the experiences described in it are accessible to everyone.

The experiments described by Rachlis are very simple. They go to the very basics

school physics course. However, as the famous proverb says, it is better to see once than to hear it once. And of course, it’s better to do it yourself at least once than to see others do it. The author rightly writes:

“Seeing for yourself is always very important and for several reasons.

Firstly, this is exactly how scientists act. When one scientist reports some of his observations, others try to repeat what he did and verify his observations themselves. If only the author himself can do this, then something is impossible here. A phenomenon is only accepted as reliable when many scientists observe it.

Secondly, when testing an idea on your own, along the way you can also notice phenomena that are not mentioned in the book. These unexpected observations can lead to new discoveries and provide new information. When always repeating one or another experiment described in a book, you need to be very careful. And then it is possible that you yourself will be honored with new discoveries.”

Rachlis’s book is the first step to independent research. It shows how many interesting problems lie in the most everyday events. The author himself spoke about this in a short introduction to his book.

“We are surrounded by many objects, to which we do not pay the slightest attention until something happens to him.”

or extraordinary. If, for example, an ashtray that was standing peacefully on the table suddenly flies into the air by itself - oh, then, of course, there will be a server reason for reflection. How could this serve? Why? Is it possible to make the ashtray rise and fall according to our wishes?

But in order to teach research, it is not at all necessary to wait for such amazing processes. Let's say that one fine day the same ashtray on our table will quietly kill us. And at that time (we hear) a truck drives along the street. This can also lead to a very interesting study - the vibrations that the truck causes, and the conditions under which the ashtray responds to these vibrations.

The situation is exactly the same with the bathroom. We fill it with water. We enter it. Let's sit down. The water is rising. She sways and splashes. Waves run across its damage. The water becomes dirty. Various objects float or sink in it. Soap bubbles form. Light is useful on the walls of the bath and on the surface of the water. Then we open the drain and the bathroom empties. This can create a whirlpool. All these events that are easy for everyone

In fact, the conversation begins with the phenomenon that everyone constantly sees in the bathroom, and then, with logical inevitability, goes beyond it. The author takes us to the circus and tells us about the secret of the “mathematical abilities” of wooden dogs. He explains the reasons why the body of a violin is different from the body of a cello, and briefly talks about the residual magnetism of rocks. The magnetic properties of the bathtub turn out to be an excellent reason for a nonsense about terrestrial magnetism.

At the end of each chapter, several problems are given, as well as independent research that can be done on this topic.

This book is valuable because the reader does not need to know anything in order to understand everything in it. It will be read with enthusiasm by a schoolchild who has just started studying physics, and by an adult who either has not studied this subject before or has forgotten it.

It is possible that to some of our readers the experiments presented in Rachlis’ book seem too simple. After all, their basis is well known from the school physics course, where there are experiments and the latter. But they are just described, some of them were demonstrated to you by the teacher, and you did a lot of them yourself in class and in the school physics classroom. Here you are given the opportunity to slowly sit and think for yourself.

After reading this book, you will not regret the time spent on it. And when you close the last page, you see that a kitchen, a yard, a river, a forest, a country road can become the same occupying physics laboratory.
