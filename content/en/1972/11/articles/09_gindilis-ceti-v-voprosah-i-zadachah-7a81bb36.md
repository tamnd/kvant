Nowadays a lot is said and written about the problem of communication with external civilizations. Experts briefly call it CETI, after the first letters of the English words Communication with Extraterrestrial Intelligence, which translated into Russian means “connection with external intelligence.” They squeak not only in science fiction works, but also in Serbez, a wave of Serbez scientific works. Scientific seminars, meetings, and conferences have been devoted to this topic more than once. Recently in Armenia, at the Bioprakan Astrophysical General School, the First International Conference on this problem was held. Many outstanding Soviet and foreign scientists took part in its work - specialists in the field of astrophysics, physics, biochemistry, biology, history, philosophy, sociology, cybernetics, information theory and communications. For several days they discussed the possibility of detecting external civilizations, the problem of establishing contact with external intelligence *). Indeed, is it possible to establish contact with intelligent people of other worlds, separated from us by gigantic distances, which are easier to write down using numbers than to imagine the power of imagination?

How to do this?

Let's arm ourselves with a pencil and paper and think about this problem together.

What is the maximum possible speed?

You know this, of course; it is equal to the speed of light in vacuum—300,000 km/s. At this speed, to reach the nearest star, it would take

But the package can reach speeds arbitrarily close to the speed of light, so getting to a nearby star will actually take about four years.

How long will it take to fly to the center of our Galaxy? To the Andromeda nebula?

Well, it's quite simple. The distance to the center of the Galaxy is approximately 34,000 light years, therefore, if you fly at the speed of light, it will take 34 thousand years. Really simple, but disappointing: again too long. The situation for each is hopeless - after all, we are flying at the maximum possible speed. Where is the way out? However, stop! There is an inaccuracy in our reasoning. We have not taught the pace of time on Earth and in our package. For an observer who remained on Earth and follows our flight with his watch, the actual time will pass 34,000 years. But on a moving ship, according to the theory of relative time, time flows differently. The closer the speed is to the speed of light, the slower time flows on it. This means that if we get close enough to the speed of light, we will be able to fly anywhere and almost never get together.

Let the ship move with a constant speed $v$. You have been given a task: to fly to the Andromeda nebula in 10 years. What should the ship's speed be? What is its ratio to the speed of light?

The distance to the Andromeda nebula is approximately two million light years. To fly this distance in 10 years, the relativistic reduction of time, that is, the ratio of the proper time $t$ of the spacecraft to the time $t$, changed by the clock of the earthly observer, must be equal to

$$\frac{\tau}{t} = \frac{10}{2000000} \frac{v}{c}.$$

But at a constant speed of the ship this value

$$\frac{\tau}{t} = \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2}.$$

From here

$$\frac{v}{c} \approx 1 - 1.25 \cdot 10^{-11} = 0.999999999875c,$$

So, if the ship moves at a speed of 0.999 999 999 987 5 $c$, it can fly to the Andromeda nebula in 10 years.

But in order to achieve such speed, the ship must first be accelerated using some kind of movement. Consequently, it is also necessary to take into account the time it takes to boost the ship. The efficiency of the packages is higher, the closer the flow rate of the working fluid (“fuel”) is to the final speed of the packages. Therefore, the most effective means of achieving near-light speeds is the use of photon packets, which operate through the angulation reaction of matter and antimatter. The end product of this reaction is γ radiation. So, the speed of outflow of the working fluid is equal to the speed of light.

So far we have only been interested in flight time. Let's think now about the required power. Let the ship move in the optimal manner described above with constant acceleration to the halfway point and subsequent deceleration for the remaining section. On the way back, everything is repeated in the same order (Fig. 2). Let the acceleration of the ship be $g$, the final mass 10 tons, the further flight 1,000 light years (very close compared to the Andromeda nebula!). What is the initial mass of the ship? What is the energy equivalent of this mass? What is the driving power?

Since the flight range is 1,000 light years, the length of the acceleration section is 500 light years. Polling $x=500$, from formula (3) we find $\mu=10^3$. Consequently, if $M_0$ is the initial mass of the package, then at the end of the acceleration section its mass is $M_1=10^{-3} M_0$. Since the length of the deceleration section is also 500 light years, and the acceleration (deceleration) is still $g$, then the mass of the package at the end of this section is $M_2=10^{-3} M_1=10^{-6} M_0$. On the way back, after braking, the mass of the package will be equal to $M=10^{-6} M_2=10^{-12} M_0$. This is the useful mass of the ship, equal to 10 tons. From here we get $M_0=10^{13}$ tons, which is equal to the mass of the Martian satellite Phobos. The energy equivalent of this mass is $E=M_0 c^2 \approx 10^{40} \text{эр}$. This is $10^{13}$ times the current annual energy consumption around the globe.

The difficulties we encountered in the field are understandable. In an effort to reach our “brothers in mind,” we are trying to accelerate a heavy body (a ship with astronauts) to near light speed. But in nature there are particles without rest mass (photons, neutrinos), which do not need to be accelerated, or can only exist by moving at the speed of light. Should we use their services? In high life, we constantly do this when the opportunity arises, watching TV or talking on the phone. Electromagnetic waves will faithfully transmit information to our subscribers. So let's try to use it to communicate with other civilizations.

Let's try to take a different path. We will emit electromagnetic waves in all directions in space, using them to transmit information to the definition. From an energy point of view, this is the most unfavorable type of connection (when energy is dissipated in all directions). But any subscriber, no matter in what direction he is, can receive our signals, so we certainly won’t miss.

Let's calculate the range at which signals can be received from our transmitter. The energy flux passing through a unit surface at a distance $R$ from the transmitter is equal to

$$\frac{P}{4\pi R^2} \text{ бт/m}^2$$

where $P$ is the transmitter power.
If $A$ is the effective area of ​​the receiving antenna, then the receiving signal power will be equal to

$$P_c = \frac{PA}{4\pi R^2} \text{ бт}$$

Let's put $R=10^5$ light years, which is equal to the diameter of our Galaxy, and let $A=10^4$ m$^2$ (similar radio telecommunicators already exist on Earth). Then the signal power will be $2.4 \cdot 10^{-14}$ bt. Such a signal can be fully detected using highly sensitive receiving equipment. This means that no matter where our subscriber is within the Galaxy, he can register the signals we send. In this case, the transmitter can operate continuously for a whole year before we exhaust the available energy reserve ($10^{40}$ er$^2$). During this time, it will be able to transmit $10^{14}-10^{15}$ bits of information. For comparison, one page of printed text contains approximately $10^4$ bits of information. Therefore, we could transmit a text of $10^{10}-10^{11}$ pages. This is a whole library - tens of millions of volumes with 1,000 pages in each volume.

So now decide what is better: fly to somewhere I don’t know where, or send your radio receiver to everyone who can hear it.
