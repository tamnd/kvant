Let us now see what technical difficulties must be overcome so that the spacecraft can reach the fourth cosmic speed and reach the Sun.

Let us assume that the solar probe is launched from a circular orbit of an artificial satellite, which is located at an altitude of 200 km above the earth's surface. Considering that the speed of the satellite is 7.8 km/s, we will find the initial speed of launch from orbit: 31.8—7.8=24 km/s.

Let us use the well-known formula that gives the ratio of the initial mass $M$ of the package system to the final mass (payload) $m_n$:

$$\frac{M}{m_n} = e^{v/c} \left( \frac{s-1}{s-e^{v/nc}} \right)^n,$$

where $e$ is the base of natural logarithms, $v$ is the speed that must be imparted to the payload, $c$ is the speed of the source of gases from the rocket's tube, $n$ is the number of stages, $s$ is a design parameter indicating the ratio of the mass of the stage filled with fuel to the mass of the empty stage. The values ​​of $s$ and $c$ are assumed to be the same for all stages.

Let us assume that the design parameter has an extremely good value $s=20$, and let it move, accelerating the space probe to a speed of 24 km/s, using liquid hydrogen as fuel and liquid oxygen as oxidizer. Such fuel provides a source speed $c$ of the order of 4 km/s.

For $n=1$ and $n=2$ in our case it turns out that $s<e^{v/nc}$. This means that single-stage and even two-stage rockets cannot achieve the required speed at $c=4$ km/s. For $n=3$ we get $M/m_n=1380$, and for $n=5$ the relation is $M/m_n=906,6$.

Consequently, with the current chemical fuel (i.e. at $c=4$ km/s; the value $c$ for chemical fuels in the future cannot exceed 5 km/s) for every kilogram of payload delivered to the Sun, there should be 1380 kcal of mass starting from orbit in the case of a three-stage option and 906.6 kcal in the case of five steps. Increasing the number of steps even to 10 initially reduces the mass ratio $M/m_n$ to only 627.9.

If we assume that the mass of the space probe is 500 kcal, then at $n=5$ we get $M=453,3$ m. To look at a five-stage rocket system of such a mass in low-Earth orbit, it would be necessary to launch from Earth five rockets of the same class as the American lunar system “Saturn-5”. From an energy point of view, after a small probe to the Sun it is no easier than to carry out several lunar expeditions!

The matter would have been accomplished in detail if not chemical, but nuclear engines had been turned on in near-Earth orbit. Even if we assume a moderate value for such engines $c=8$ km/s, then at $n=2$ it will be relatively $M/m_n=30,11$. This means that a two-stage nuclear rocket launching from a circular orbit could have a mass of 15 m with the previous package load of $m_n=0,5$ m. In this case, there is no need for installation in orbit: launching a satellite of such a mass has long been possible.

However, you can get to the Sun using conventional chemical engines, if you take advantage of the gravitational fields of Jupiter or Venus. Jupiter can throw a space probe towards the Sun, just as the Moon throws its station towards the Earth, and Venus can bring a proplegation closer to the Sun in the vicinity of the trajectory of the space zone.
