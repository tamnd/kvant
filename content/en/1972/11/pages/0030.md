A sphere of this radius contains about 10 million stars. But the packet can only reach one of them. How to choose it? Isn't the risk of making a mistake too great?

Let's try to take a different path. We will emit electromagnetic waves in all directions in space, using them to transmit information to the definition. From an energy point of view, this is the most unfavorable type of connection (when energy is dissipated in all directions). But any subscriber, no matter in what direction he is, can receive our signals, so we certainly won’t miss.

Let's calculate the range at which signals can be received from our transmitter. The energy flux passing through a unit surface at a distance $R$ from the transmitter is equal to

$$\frac{P}{4\pi R^2} \text{ бт/m}^2$$

where $P$ is the transmitter power.
If $A$ is the effective area of ​​the receiving antenna, then the receiving signal power will be equal to

$$P_c = \frac{PA}{4\pi R^2} \text{ бт}$$

Let's put $R=10^5$ light years, which is equal to the diameter of our Galaxy, and let $A=10^4$ m$^2$ (similar radio telecommunicators already exist on Earth). Then the signal power will be $2.4 \cdot 10^{-14}$ bt. Such a signal can be fully detected using highly sensitive receiving equipment. This means that no matter where our subscriber is within the Galaxy, he can register the signals we send. In this case, the transmitter can operate continuously for a whole year before we exhaust the available energy reserve ($10^{40}$ er$^2$). During this time, it will be able to transmit $10^{14}-10^{15}$ bits of information. For comparison, one page of printed text contains approximately $10^4$ bits of information. Therefore, we could transmit a text of $10^{10}-10^{11}$ pages. This is a whole library - tens of millions of volumes with 1,000 pages in each volume.

So now decide what is better: fly to somewhere I don’t know where, or send your radio receiver to everyone who can hear it.

UNKNOWN SUPER HEAVY ELEMENT?

Until recently, it was believed that the xenon discovered in meteorites is formed only as a result of the fission of pulotonium 244. In 1969, it was proposed that xenon could also arise from the fission of a still unknown LET SUPERHEAVY Element, which had already decayed by the time the meteorite fell.

What do scientists now know about this still elusive hypothetical element? Its heat of evaporation, according to Professor E. Anders and J. Larimer (USA), is $(54 \pm 3)$ kcal/mol, and its boiling point $(2500 \pm 400)^\circ\text{K}$. The most suitable “candidates” for this element would be elements numbered 111 (eka-gold) and 115 (eka-bismuth) in the periodic table. It is followed in terms of probability by elements 113 (eka-thalium), 114 (eka-lead), 112 (eka-mercury) and 116 (eka-polony). Elements 105 (eka-tantalum) to 110 (eka-platinum) are not volatile enough and should very much be excluded from this list.
