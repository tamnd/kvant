Indeed, if water expands when it freezes, then any pressure, insofar as it is affected by this expansion, must also result in freezing, and this is equivalent to moving beyond the freezing point. Therefore, at 0° ice under pressure should melt. This is actually observed experimentally. If any sharp object, for example, the tip of a knife, is placed on a piece of ice, then the sharpness penetrates deeper not because the ice is cut or crumbles, but because the ice melts under pressure. According to Thomson, when two pieces of ice are pressed together, melting should occur at the point of contact; the resulting water flows out and freezes somewhere else, since it is no longer under pressure.

Tyndall demonstrated the effect of freezing ice pieces in the following way. The ice was broken into small pieces, which were then compressed into a special shape. The result was a meltable mass of ice. In this way, by exposing ice powder to a pressure of several hundred kilograms per 1 cm², ice cups and other objects of various shapes can be produced.

The explanation for the phenomenon of glacier flow seems to be now becoming very obvious. According to Thomson and Tindalio, the change in shape of a moving glacier occurs as a result of the melting of ice in places of high pressure and the subsequent solidification of the release of melting water.

Of course, Thomson's statement that ice melts under pressure is absolutely correct. However, we cannot agree with his assumption that the Faraday freezing of floating pieces of ice is based on the same principle. Thomson argued that even with the simple contact of the pieces, some pressure will arise due to capillary action *). This pressure is what causes the Faraday effect. To show the unreasonableness of Thomson's idea, Faraday in 1860 set up his experiment under conditions where there was no capitular action. He made several disks out of ice, giving them a flat-convex shape, and let them float through the water with the convex side up. Under such conditions, the pieces could touch each other only under water (Fig. 4). With weak contact with each other, they froze together so firmly that to set all the masses in motion on the water, it was enough to act on only one point. Wooden disks, when in contact both along the waterline and under water and, therefore, in the presence of capitular action on each other, did not show any signs of fastening.

Despite this, both Faraday's and Thomson's principles failed to provide a satisfactory explanation for the movement of glaciers. For example, they did not explain the process of ice freezing at those low temperatures at which the world's glaciers are essentially frozen. However, Faraday himself pointed out that two pieces of ice do not freeze under pressure if they are cooled below zero.
