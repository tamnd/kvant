Some of you may perhaps be confused by the fact that the stick in the water appears to be bent upward, and the light beam appears to be bent downward. If you haven't noticed this, note again the difference between figure 5 and 6.

This contradiction is apparent, and it is easy to explain if you understand in more detail what happens to the light coming from the water. figure 7 shows the straight stick AB as it really is. In the chapter it is observed that, located above the water, the light reflected from the end of stick B enters. But, passing from water to air, the ray of light coming from B does not go in a straight line, but bends at point G. and, obviously, follows the path of the BGV, since it enters the eye is observed.

The observed cannot know where the light beam actually comes from. The beam enters the eye in the direction of GV, which means that it is observed that the light comes in a straight line, which passes slightly to the side of G, that is, from somewhere from point D. Please note that point D is located above point B, which is why the stick seems to be bent upward. In addition, the stick looks shorter than it actually is. These two rules apply to all objects immersed in water. If an object is only partially submerged in water, its underwater part always appears shorter. Moreover, all objects in the water appear to be closer to the surface than they actually are.

Have any of you ever shot fish with an underwater gun? Anyone who has had to, probably knows how difficult it is. The fact is that the fish is not at all where we see it. If you aim directly at the fish, the arrow will certainly go higher. Therefore, you need to aim a little higher, making corrections for artificial light rays.

There is only one case where a very narrow parallel beam of light will not bend - if it goes vertically, that is, straight down. In this case, the light enters the water at a right angle and goes down without a bend.
