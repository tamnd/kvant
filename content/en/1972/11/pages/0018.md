At the other extreme, one can imagine atoms or molecules that have no directional attraction. If the thermal vibrations are not strong enough to keep the atoms or molecules isolated from each other, they will come together and form a liquid, the thickness of which will depend on how easily individual molecules can move among other molecules. With further cooling, the viscosity can increase continuously or in sudden jumps, to the point where this collection of molecules can be called a solid.

Natural substances lie between these two extremes, ice seeming to be closer to the first than other substances. Indeed, its crystals form an indefinite form of vapor. On the other hand, molecules of water vapor are continuously pulled away from the crystals by the action of the body or for some other reason and mix with the molecules of the surrounding medium. The ice “sublimates.” The ease or difficulty of condensation and separation of molecules must be determined by some correspondence between vapor density, temperature and surface conditions. In cracks, the chances of condensation are higher than in open areas, since in cracks, starting from their edges, the molecules are under double action. The detachment of molecules is more likely to occur from a convex surface. This effect is completely analogous to the effective dependence of vapor pressure on the shape of the liquid with which the vapor comes into contact, as was pointed out by Faraday. Inside the crack, the equilibrium between sublimation and precipitation must be different than outside, the latter acting at the expense of the former. In other words, the vapor pressure will decrease, that is, the freezing point temperature will increase.

At normal pressures, water is most dense at 4°C. As the temperature rises further down to the freezing point, the density becomes less and less. It is obvious that some of the molecules form sets that occupy larger spaces than molecules taken individually, since mechanical forces force atoms to take a certain location in space. At the same time, there is no need at all to consider these sets to be permanent: in all likelihood, they constantly arise, decay, and arise again.
