Molecules, which are much lighter, come off from the ends of the branches than from the central regions of the snowflake, and are not deposited on it at the same speed. Thanks to this, the snowflake becomes tired in the central part, and its branches are shortened and even rounded. Since snowflakes are small in size, often no more than a few millimeters in diameter, the conditions around each of them can be considered the same at all points. If one ray lengthens, the other rays also lengthen. With all this, falling to the Earth, snowflakes can encounter a wide variety of local conditions, according to which they can take on many different forms, while the rays of each individual snowflake remain completely similar.

According to this obligation, the greater diversity of snowflake shapes is based on the existence of two opposing processes: the rapid growth of branches leading to their feathery form, and compaction in the centers of this feathery structure. In the first process growth predominates over sublimation to a greater extent than in the second, so that they differ in degree rather than in quality. The shape of each snowflake tells us about the changes in atmospheric conditions that it had to endure.

Tyndall performed an excellent experiment, with the help of which he was able to demonstrate the structure of ice. This experience is called "receiving ice flowers." A beam of light from an arc lamp is passed through a piece of ice. At various points in this piece where there are impurities or irregularities, the body is retained and causes melting. The resulting voids in this case have a hexagonal shape, since the destruction of the structure occurs in the direction exactly opposite to growth. These figures represent, so to speak, negative snowflakes. With the help of suitable equipment these figures can be projected onto the screen. If a covet with water is placed on the path of the rays from the lantern to the ice, then the heat rays are absorbed, and the growth of such voids in the crystal stops.
