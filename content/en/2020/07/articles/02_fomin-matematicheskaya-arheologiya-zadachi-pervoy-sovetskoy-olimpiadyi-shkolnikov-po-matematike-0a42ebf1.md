Mathematical "archaeology":

Objectives of the first Soviet Olympiad for schoolchildren in mathematics

D.FOMIN

FOR MANY YEARS, everyone who was ever interested in the problems of the Leningrad Mathematical Olympiads (LMO) was well aware that versions of the first LMOs disappeared during the war and blockade or were simply not preserved. And in the first post-war years, this sad fate did not bypass the northern capital - perhaps only the problems of the Olympiads of the second half of the 1950s were found in more or less complete versions (and even then not for every year and not for every class). On the one hand, this is understandable. It is likely that in those years these newfangled competitions were not perceived as something worthy of special attention. Moreover, various revolutionary (in the full sense of the word) initiatives in education fell on the heads of teachers and schoolchildren literally every day. Often, not even a quarter or two would pass before everything was cancelled, new ministers, directors and other leading enthusiasts arrived, who invented and introduced completely different methods of teaching, innovative ideas for identifying young talents, raising socialist science to an unprecedented level, and so on. It is possible that many professors who organized the Olympiad did not consider it necessary to store

When, in the late 1980s, I tried to find the problems of the Olympiads of those distant pre-war years, the general consensus was “it’s hopeless.” And the results of my excavations in the State Public Library (now the Russian National Library), in documents and reports of the Palace of Pioneers and in more than thirty personal archives led me to the same sad conclusion. It is theoretically possible that this is still not a complete set of all options. I personally find it hard to believe that the jury prepared a separate option for each of the 48 participants, especially since their number was not known in advance. The idea of ​​a multi-choice Olympiad was to eliminate the possibility of cheating - even then, at the very first Olympiad, attention was already paid to this, which, however, is not surprising. However, eight options should have been enough - Alexandrova recalls that the participants were seated in several classrooms in the main building of the university. Apparently, the organizers wanted to ensure that in each room there were schoolchildren with different options for conditions - then such an organization of the oral round looks quite reasonable. Of course, there is a non-zero probability that the number of options could be more, say ten or twelve. In such a case, however, it would be difficult to explain why the publication did not contain a complete set of problems. As for the problem of coloring a cube, it cannot be completely ruled out that it was included in the list of training tasks sent to schools, based on the solution of which schools formed their “teams” for the second round (this school stage was quite deservedly called the first round of LMO-1934). Such an assumption could theoretically explain the recollection of one of the participants that this problem was the very first problem of the Olympiad. However, I honestly admit that this hypothesis does not seem plausible to me. In article [2] it is mentioned that the list of problems of the first round consisted of “90 problems from various sections of algebra, geometry and trigonometry”; by the way, many of them are also listed in the appendix to this article under the heading “Exercises for students”; another list of training tasks is given in the previous issue (1934, No. 3) of the same magazine “Mathematics and Physics in Secondary School”; There is not a single non-standard task on these lists.
