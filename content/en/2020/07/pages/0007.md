What happens to cosmic rays as they fly through the Universe After leaving their “accelerator” - the active nucleus of the galaxy, cosmic rays then fly out of their galaxy. Those particles that reach the Earth cover distances of tens and hundreds of millions of light years. These are huge distances. Photons travel through them over tens and hundreds of millions of years, and they fly at the highest speed in nature. What happens to cosmic rays as they fly through the Universe? Galaxies are distributed sparsely in the Universe, at large distances from each other, and their sizes are very small on a universal scale. Therefore, the entire or almost entire path of cosmic rays runs through intergalactic space. The eye, not armed with instruments, perceives intergalactic space as absolutely empty and dark. But that's not true. Photons of different energies fly in intergalactic space. Some of them are emitted by stars in galaxies. Others remain from the time of the birth of the Universe, and these photons are called relict. (There are gas and dust molecules and magnetic fields in intergalactic space, but we won’t talk about them.) Cosmic particles interact with photons, and this leads to two effects. The first effect is a change in the original shape of the spectrum of cosmic rays, namely: in the spectrum of the Earth, compared to the original spectrum, there is not enough particles of the highest energies, about $10^{20}$ eV and higher.
