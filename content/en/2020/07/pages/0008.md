And cosmic rays of lower energies practically do not interact with relict photons, since their energy is not enough to produce other particles. Having spent part of the energy in the Universe, ultra-high energy particles move into the ranks of cosmic rays with lower energies. As a result, their energy spectrum changes compared to the original spectrum. This means that to answer the question of where particles are accelerated near a supermassive black hole, it is not enough to compare the measured spectrum of cosmic rays with the theoretical initial spectra. But the passage of cosmic rays through intergalactic space leads to another effect. It is as follows. Elementary particles, which are born in the interactions of particles with relict photons, do not live long and give rise to electrons, positrons and quanta. They, in turn, interact with photons, generating more and more electrons, positrons and quanta. The number of particles grows like an avalanche, and a giant cascade of particles appears in space, which continue to interact with intergalactic photons. The particles in the cascade move so far apart that the distance between them exceeds the size of the solar system. (Born elementary particles also give rise to neutrinos, but they fly through the Universe without interacting.) The birth of intergalactic cascades is the second effect that appears as a result of the interactions of cosmic rays with photons in space. For our research, cascade quanta are of greatest interest. In addition to them, in intergalactic space there are photons of various energies, and all these photons and quanta make up

extragalactic background. It is measured by instruments placed on board satellites. The bulk of the extragalactic background is the radiation of individual sources that are so far from us or so weak that they are not visible to instruments. It is possible that other processes also contribute to the extragalactic background. For example, dark matter particles, when decaying, also give rise to quanta. By studying the extragalactic background, it is possible to determine its components and the proportion of cascade radiation in it. It turns out that the proportion of cascade radiation depends on in which zone of the supermassive black hole the cosmic rays were accelerated. And knowing both the cascade radiation and the spectrum of cosmic rays on Earth, it is possible to find out where and how the particles were accelerated. (Recall that in each zone they are accelerated in a different way.) Now physicists are studying ultra-high energy particles on Earth and in space. The cosmic ray spectrum is measured on Earth, and the extragalactic background is measured in space. By analyzing data from these measurements, physicists study processes in the vicinity of supermassive black holes.

Conclusion Cosmic rays were discovered over a hundred years ago, in the 1910s. Many years have been spent understanding the interactions of cosmic rays with the earth’s atmosphere, understanding that particles of different energies have different origins: low-energy cosmic rays are emitted by the Sun, while more energetic ones are born in our Galaxy. Scientists suggest that the sources of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays are active galactic nuclei. At their centers there are supermassive black holes, in the vicinity of which the particles acquire enormous energy. The study of these cosmic ray sources requires the creation of new instruments for measurements in space. It requires an understanding of what processes are possible.
