Applications

Magnetic field of the accretion disk Interstellar gas is permeated by a magnetic field; it is present in all stars. Almost all matter in stars and the interstellar medium is ionized and consists primarily of charged particles. As a result, in space the magnetic field is frozen into the medium: the magnetic field line is, as it were, attached to those charged particles that were on it at the initial moment, and when the particles move, they drag the line along with them. Gas falling onto a black hole and forming an accretion disk is carried along with it by a magnetic field, so the disk becomes magnetized. And if the gas density increases, i.e. As the gas compresses, the field lines become denser and the magnetic field intensifies. Therefore, the field of the accretion disk can be much larger than the field penetrating the interstellar gas and stellar matter.

The disk rotates, and its magnetic field induces electric fields both in the disk itself and in the magnetosphere of the black hole. They accelerate charged particles. Under certain conditions, an induced electric field can accelerate particles to ultra-high energies. Another mechanism for accelerating particles in space is associated with inhomogeneities of the magnetic field, and the electric field does not participate in it. The possibility of such particle acceleration was first understood and considered by the Italian physicist Fermi, which is why this mechanism is called the Fermi mechanism. Near supermassive black holes, the Fermi mechanism operates in a jet that escapes from the accretion disk. Particles in a jet are accelerated this way. The jet is formed from the material of the accretion disk. In a jet, plasma clots are ejected from the disk through two funnel-shaped channels along its rotation axis. The thickness of the accretion disks around supermassive black holes varies, depending on the characteristics of the black hole. Depending on the thickness of the disk, the clumps fly through it for months and years. All this time, the clumps interact with the sides of the channel (with the matter of the disk) and with the radiation of the disk (after all, the accretion disk glows brightly in different ranges, it is filled with thermal, optical, radio and other photons). As a result of interaction with matter and radiation, waves of different types are excited on the surface of the bunch, including shock waves. Let's explain what it is. A section of the surface of the clot experiences an impact and receives a push. Under the influence of a push, a section of plasma begins to move along the clot, setting in motion the plasma layers lying in front. The boundary between the plasma moving along the bunch and the one stationary relative to the bunch is sharp. It's called the shock front
