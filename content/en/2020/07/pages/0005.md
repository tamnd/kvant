Beyond the Milky Way. Active galactic nuclei

The Milky Way is just one of the many galaxies in our Universe. Galaxies differ in appearance, size, intensity of star formation, and the ratio of old and young stars. Astronomers classify them according to these characteristics. But galaxies also differ in the characteristics of their central part (this is also an important feature for the classification of galaxies). The central part differs from other regions of the galaxy in its increased concentration of stars and interstellar matter - gas and dust. This part of the galaxy is compact. It is called the core of the galaxy. The reason for the condensation of matter in the galactic core is gravity. However, not all galaxies have nuclei. Small galaxies do not have them, since there is not enough gravity there to condense matter in the center. In most galaxies, the bulk of the energy is emitted by the stars, and the radiation from the galactic core is also the radiation from the stars that make it up. Such galactic nuclei are called stationary or inactive. But there are a small number of galaxies in which a huge stream of energy bursts from the core, as if there were a hundred million or even a hundred billion Suns shining at the center of the galaxy. The radiation from the core is variable - it decreases sharply and then increases sharply, and over a short period of time (hours, months, years). In this case, the energy of the nucleus is emitted in various ranges - radio, x-ray, ultraviolet, infrared and gamma range. Portions of plasma are ejected from the core, and clouds of gas move quickly near it. These processes cannot be explained by the high density of stars and interstellar matter. Such nuclei are called active or non-stationary. There are few galaxies with active nuclei, approximately 1% of the total number of galaxies. They are divided into several types

We will not touch upon the classification of active nuclei, since it is not important for our study. And its essence is as follows. Since a huge amount of energy is released in active galactic nuclei, processes may occur there in which cosmic rays acquire ultra-high energies. Having flown out from the region where the particles were accelerated, cosmic rays leave the boundaries of their galaxy and, having covered enormous distances in space, reach the Earth. Is it possible to study active nuclei by registering these cosmic rays on Earth, even though the particles have traveled a giant path in space? In this study, it is important to answer the following questions: where does the colossal energy come from in active galactic nuclei; how particles accelerate there; what happens to particles when they fly through intergalactic space.

What is the source of energy for the active galactic nucleus?

Currently, the dominant hypothesis is that the activity of the nucleus is caused by a supermassive black hole at the center of the galaxy. A black hole is an object with such strong gravity that not even light can escape from it. How can this be? In order to overcome the gravitational force of a celestial body, the bodies must have a second escape velocity. For black holes, the second escape velocity is greater than the speed of light. But in nature, nothing moves faster than light, so nothing comes out of black holes. Since not even light comes out of them, black holes are dark and invisible to the observer.
