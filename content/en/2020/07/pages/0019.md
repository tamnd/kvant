In 1941, Leningrad University was evacuated to Elabuga, and then to Saratov; many professors, graduate students and students went to the front, their records and archives disappeared - either in besieged Leningrad, or during numerous moves. When, in the late 1980s, I tried to find the problems of the Olympiads of those distant pre-war years, the general consensus was “it’s hopeless.” And the results of my excavations in the State Public Library (now the Russian National Library), in documents and reports of the Palace of Pioneers and in more than thirty personal archives led me to the same sad conclusion. In the first edition of the collection of Leningrad Mathematical Olympiads ([6]), I wrote something like this: it seems extremely unlikely to find problems from the pre-war Olympiads, there is no real chance of this. Here it is necessary to mention that in 1984, on the occasion of the fiftieth anniversary of the first Olympiad, an interesting article by N.M. Matveev and S.E. Rukshin was published in the journal “Mathematics at School” [5], in which the authors, in particular, quoted the memoirs $С.В.Валландера^{1}$, and then informed us that “history has preserved the first problem of the first Olympiad.” However, the article lacked precise attribution for this statement. Here is the condition of the problem given there: Problem about a cube. How many different ways are there to color the faces of a cube with six colors? (Two coloring methods are called different if they cannot be combined by rotating the cube.) When I read this text shortly after its publication, my first reaction was - what a pleasant surprise! After admiring the problem, I thought a little, because I had the feeling that I had already seen it somewhere. And in fact, upon opening the collection [3], I immediately discovered this problem... in the version of the first Moscow Mathematical Olympiad in 1935. Could Muscovites use the problem from the Leningrad Olympiad the previous year? Needless to say, in our time this would be absolutely impossible. But then, in the era of the very first mathematical competitions, at a time when the speed of dissemination of highly specialized technical information was extremely low, such questions, of course, were not taken so seriously. It was difficult to say anything for sure, although it seemed to me that this (i.e., reusing an already published problem) was still unlikely - even if we assume that the Muscovites liked this problem so much that they could not do without it, then it would be quite enough to slightly modify its condition; it would be quite easy to do this. Therefore, I had a hypothesis - however, not based on anything - that over the years, there was a kind of involuntary “overlay” of the MMO-1935 variant on the memories of LMO-1934.
