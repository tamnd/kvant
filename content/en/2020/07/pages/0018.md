MATHEMATICAL WORLD

Mathematical "archaeology":

Objectives of the first Soviet Olympiad for schoolchildren in mathematics

D.FOMIN

FOR MANY YEARS, everyone who was ever interested in the problems of the Leningrad Mathematical Olympiads (LMO) was well aware that versions of the first LMOs disappeared during the war and blockade or were simply not preserved. And in the first post-war years, this sad fate did not bypass the northern capital - perhaps only the problems of the Olympiads of the second half of the 1950s were found in more or less complete versions (and even then not for every year and not for every class). On the one hand, this is understandable. It is likely that in those years these newfangled competitions were not perceived as something worthy of special attention. Moreover, various revolutionary (in the full sense of the word) initiatives in education fell on the heads of teachers and schoolchildren literally every day. Often, not even a quarter or two would pass before everything was cancelled, new ministers, directors and other leading enthusiasts arrived, who invented and introduced completely different methods of teaching, innovative ideas for identifying young talents, raising socialist science to an unprecedented level, and so on. On the other hand, it should be noted that in those years, both in Leningrad and Moscow, there were enthusiasts who even then intuitively understood how important it was to preserve for history these first Olympiad problems, the materials of the first circles. They carefully preserved these texts in their archives, and then published the first articles and collections of problems dedicated to mathematical competitions. Among the Muscovites here, first of all, it is worth mentioning Rostislav Nikolaevich Bonchkovsky (1905–1942) and Joasaf Ivanovich Chistyakov (1870–1942). As for Leningrad, it is known, for example, that the leaders of the circles at the Palace of Pioneers, Mikhail Lvovich Verzhbinsky (1909–1962) and Mark Konstantinovich Gavurin (1911–1992), carefully collected sets of Olympiad problems and materials for classes with schoolchildren (see [7]). But then they went to the front, and now, probably, no one knows what happened to their archives. The daughter of M.L. Verzhbinsky wrote in her memoirs about her father that during the blockade winters, due to a lack of firewood, the unique Chippendale furniture stored in their apartment was chopped up for kindling.
