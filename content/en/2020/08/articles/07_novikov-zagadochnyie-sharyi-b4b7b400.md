Mystery balls

S. NOVIKOV

IN THE LIFE OF EVERY PERSON sometimes he has to deal with a closed (often unfamiliar) object, the internal structure of which can only be guessed at. Such objects become a kind of “black boxes”, when only input and output signals (or some of their values) are available to an external observer, and what is happening inside is unknown. About the behavior of a black box you can

judge only by observing the reactions of output quantities to changes in input ones. This makes it possible to ultimately reveal the “secret” of such an object. You may have already encountered black boxes - for example, in electrical problems. In our case, the black boxes are ten identical-looking plastic balls with a diameter of 75 mm, the internal contents of which are unknown. First, we will describe the behavior of the balls in different experimental situations. Try to express your thoughts explaining the reasons why the ball (or balls) behaves in a certain way in the described situation. And only then read our explanations.

Explanations of experiments

Fig. 3

Experiment 1. The answer “lies on the surface”: the mass of one ball is greater than the mass of the other. A lighter ball will roll down the inclined plane faster; in our case, it is empty. A massive ball has a greater moment of inertia than an empty one, and it needs significantly more energy to spin. To demonstrate this experiment, we placed a piece of foam rubber with a large number of massive lead pellet balls embedded in it inside the second ball. Experiment 2. There may be a “loose” substance inside the ball. As the ball rolls, the substance slowly moves from a higher position to a lower one. This slows down the movement of the ball down the inclined plane. In our version, small (no more than 1 mm in diameter) lead pellets were used for this.

Experiment 3. A massive cube-shaped piece of lead was placed inside our ball. When the ball rolled down an inclined plane, the cube turned over. This gave the ball a spasmodic movement. Experiment 4. Both balls demonstrate a stable equilibrium position. The stability of a body that has a fulcrum occurs when its center of gravity occupies the lowest possible position. The fifth ball illustrates the behavior of the children's toy tumbler. Inside the ball, a massive lead weight is attached to its body with glue. In a state of rest (stable equilibrium), the center of mass of the ball is located very low, not far from the fulcrum. In addition, the center of mass point and the fulcrum point lie on the same vertical (plumb and axial) line. When the ball is tilted, its center of gravity rises and moves laterally in relation to the fulcrum and the vertical line. The moment of gravity relative to the fulcrum tends to return the ball to its original state. Inside the sixth ball, just below its equatorial plane, there is a massive lead disk (“donut” with a rectangular cross-section), which is rigidly attached to the body. The difference from the fifth ball is that its center of mass is high from the fulcrum. When the ball is tilted, the gravity arm relative to the fulcrum turns out to be much larger than in the case of the tumbler. Therefore, the moment of gravity relative to the fulcrum will be greater than in the previous case. As a result, the amplitude of the ball’s oscillations increases, which means the damping time of the oscillations also increases. Experiment 5. When the ball is rotated at a certain angle, the magnet slides along its inner surface, taking a new position, and the ball continues to remain motionless. Experiment 7. Inside the ball there is a miniature electric motor, which is rigidly attached to the body. The axis of rotation (shaft) of the electric motor coincides with the direction of the diametrical vertical line of the ball. A small massive disk is mounted on the motor shaft. When the electric motor is connected to a current source, the shaft and massive disk begin to rotate in a certain direction. At the same time, the body of the ball turns in the other direction. All this is a consequence of the manifestation of the law of conservation of angular momentum. If the total moment of all external forces acting on a body is zero, then the angular momentum of this body remains constant. If some part of a stationary body acquires a certain angular momentum that has a certain sign (angular momentum is a vector quantity), then some other part of it will certainly acquire an angular momentum of the opposite sign.

The law of conservation of angular momentum is taken into account when designing helicopters. Thus, helicopters designed by M.L. Mil have two propellers. The first rotor rotates in a certain direction in a horizontal plane above the helicopter cabin. And to prevent the helicopter body from rotating in the other direction, a tail rotor is placed behind it, which, while working, prevents the body from rotating. Helicopters manufactured according to the designs of N.I. Kamov have two rotor screws located on the same axis above the cabin, but rotating in opposite directions.

Experiment 8. Inside the ball there is a miniature electric motor, which is rigidly attached to the body of the ball. A disk is mounted on the electric motor shaft, and in such a way that its axis is shifted relative to the axis of the shaft (they say that the disk is mounted with eccentricity). As a result, when the electric motor operates, mechanical vibrations occur, which are called vibrations. These vibrations make the brush move along with the ball.

The oscillatory movement of eccentrics is used in the operation of vibrating conveyors designed for transporting finely dispersed (up to tens of microns), granular and lump (up to 1000 mm or more) materials. They are often used to move ore in the mining industry.
