Water filling pump and elementary physics

S. PARNOVSKY

PUMP IN THE KITCHEN? THIS IS RARE unless it is something plumbing or part of a water purification filter. But you can still remember kitchen appliances that use a pump. In its simplest form, it is designed for pouring ordinary drinking water, delivered in large bottles (Fig.1). A couple of presses on the lid, which serves as the handle of a hand pump, and water is already pouring into the placed container. The physics behind this is very simple: a pump drives air into a plastic bottle, creating excess air pressure in the upper part, above the water. This pressure is transferred to the water, and after it exceeds a certain value, the water will begin to Fig. 1. Water bottle flow from the tube. The pump for pouring it $принципе^{ния}$ also operates a manual pump in a thermopot - a device for heating water and keeping it hot (a kind of hybrid of a thermos and a kettle; Fig.2). The water temperature does not affect the operation of the pump in any way, so you can safely limit yourself to the simplest case of pouring water at room temperature. What is the minimum required excess air pressure? If you are familiar with Pascal's formula for pressure inside a liquid, then the answer p U gh comes instantly.
