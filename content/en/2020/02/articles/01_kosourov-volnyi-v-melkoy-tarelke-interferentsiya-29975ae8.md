Waves in a shallow dish (interference)

A.KOSOUROV

When throwing pebbles into the water, observe the circles they form. Kozma Prutkov

THERE IS NO PHENOMENA MORE UNIVERSAL IN NATURE than the propagation of waves. Waves on the surface of water, sound, light, radio, transfer of deformation from one part of a solid body to another - all these are wave processes. Quantum mechanics shows that the movement of microparticles is governed by wave laws. All these waves have different physical natures, different propagation speeds, different frequencies and wavelengths. But regardless of this, the movement of any waves has a lot in common. Having studied the laws of propagation of waves of one nature, most of them can be transferred practically without changes to waves of another type.

Published in «Kvant» No. 1, 1971.

In practice, it is most convenient to study waves on the surface of the water. What is a wave? Throw a stone into the pond. On the calm horizontal surface of the pond, scattering circles will appear. Points on the water surface that the wave has reached begin to oscillate relative to their equilibrium position. This position corresponds to a horizontal surface. The farther a point is from the place where the stone fell, the more late it “learns” about the fall of the stone. Disturbance spreads at a certain speed. The points to which the disturbance reached simultaneously are in the same stage of oscillatory motion, i.e. in the same phase. The speed of movement of this phase is the speed of the wave. It is measured in the direction normal to the wave front. For waves of any nature, it is always possible to indicate physical objects that experience disturbance under the influence of the wave, i.e. deviation from its equilibrium values. For sound, these are periodic increases and decreases in pressure. For radio waves and light, rapid changes in electric (and magnetic) field strength. The best way to observe waves is by looking at the shadow on the bottom of the plate in direct sunlight or the light of a bright lamp. Each wave, acting like a cylindrical lens, produces a light stripe on the bottom of the plate that follows the shape of the wave front. However, waves traveling at a speed of about 10 cm s merge for the gaze fixed on a stationary plate. They are visible only near the source, where their amplitude is large. To see them across the entire surface of the water, you need to quickly turn your head. In the same way, with rapid head movements, you can see the spokes of a rolling wheel. It is very impressive to observe waves on the frosted glass of a camera, especially a large-format one. By holding the camera in your hands and gently rocking it, it is easy to ensure that the waves are visible over the entire surface. At the same time, it seems that they are running very slowly. You can look at the reflection of the surface of the water in the mirror. Slight movements of the mirror also make waves visible across the entire surface. But it is most convenient to observe waves under stroboscopic lighting. If you illuminate the installation with short flashes of light with a frequency equal to the frequency of the wave source, then from one flash to the next the waves will move one wavelength and the wave pattern of the waves will appear motionless. This kind of lighting is extremely easy to achieve. It is enough to turn on a small light bulb parallel to the windings of the bell magnet. From a distance of 0.5–1 m, it will illuminate the installation well and evenly, and the motionless shadow pattern of the waves will be clearly visible. For photography, it is better to use direct sunlight. Now attach a bent wire “fork” with pieces of cork at the ends to the bell hammer. The distance between the ends of the fork is 2–3 cm. If the ends are lowered into the water at the same time, you will get two sources of waves that oscillate not only synchronously, or in time, but also in phase, i.e. waves from both sources will occur at the same time. The picture will look something like this. Areas of large amplitudes fan out from the wave sources, separated by areas of “silence.”

The central region of large amplitudes is located perpendicular to the line connecting the sources. Both regions of silence and regions of high amplitudes pass between the sources. Studying the interference pattern with a ruler will show that the distance between the two maxima on the line connecting the sources is equal to half the distance between the two humps, i.e. half the wavelength. If you change the distance between the sources, the number of large amplitude bands will also change. The greater the distance between the sources, the more “feathers” there are in our fan. But the distance between the maxima on the line connecting the sources is always equal to half the wavelength. Therefore, the total number of bands with maximum amplitude will be twice as large as the number of wavelengths that fit at the distance between the sources. It follows that if the distance between the sources is less than half the wavelength, then there will be no interference at all. Such sources act as one, producing one system of circular waves. This can be seen by gradually decreasing the distance between the sources. Please also note that, if we continue the wave front passing through the hump in some region of large amplitudes, we will encounter a depression in the neighboring region. In other words: when passing through the zero region, the phase of the wave changes to half a full oscillation. Now imagine that we do not have two hammers, but two light sources emitting light waves, and we place a screen perpendicular to the surface of the water. We will see illuminated areas corresponding to the intersection of the screen and areas with large amplitude, and dark, unlit areas. In other words, we will see dark and light interference fringes. We’ll talk about how to carry out interference experiments in optics some other time, but now let’s try to explain what we saw. Now it is completely clear why the distance between the maxima on the line connecting the sources is equal to half the wavelength. Indeed, the waves arrive at the midpoint of this line in the same phase and reinforce each other. If you move from this point by half a wavelength, then the path of one wave will increase by half the wavelength, and the other will decrease by the same amount. The path difference between the waves at this point (the difference in the paths traveled by the waves from the sources to the point) will be equal to one wavelength, and the waves will again mutually intensify. This will happen every half wavelength. The maximum corresponding to zero path difference is called zero maximum or zero order interference. Maxima with a path difference of one wavelength are maxima of the first order of interference, etc. The maximum order of interference is determined by the integer closest to 2d O, where d is the distance between the sources and O is the wavelength.
