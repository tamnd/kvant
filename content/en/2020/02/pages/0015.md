Can the same string be subject to two different sizes? Our definition does not exclude this possibility. For example, a line composed of only monosyllabic words would obey any size.

Exercise 2. a) Prove that a line subject to both iambic and trochee consists of monosyllabic words. b) Prove that a line of 9 syllables, subject to two different three-syllable meters, contains at least 6 words.

In practice, this means that it is impossible to combine different two-syllable meters, as well as different three-syllable meters. It would seem that it would be easier to combine a two-syllable meter with a three-syllable one, although this still poses serious restrictions on the length of words.

Exercise 3. Prove that if a line obeys both a trochee and a dactyl, then the 4th syllable is part of either a monosyllabic word or a word consisting of at least 4 syllables.

Nabokov gives an example of a line subordinate to iambic and amphibrach: Mysterious and unearthly. But a line is not a poem yet. Moreover, the strong syllables of bisyllabic and trisyllabic meters combined in one poem would be completely different, and that poem (if it existed) could be read in two completely different ways. We can be sure that you will not find such a poem in any poetry collection. Another thing is the combination of two-syllable and four-syllable meters, and the reason is that 4 is divisible by 2. For example, what does it take for a line that is subject to iambic to also obey, say, 4.2 -meter? It is necessary that in a word containing more than one even syllable, the stress falls on a syllable whose number is not divisible by 4. Eliminate the last “not”, and you get the condition that iambic is subordinated to 4.4 meter. Four-syllable sizes. We can now turn to consider them. For each four-syllable meter, we will assign an associated two-syllable meter: for (4,1) and (4,3) meter, by definition, this is considered to be a trochee, for (4,2) and (4,4) meter, it is iambic. Further, if a line obeys a four-syllable meter, we will speak of its meter as a non-independent four-syllable if it also obeys a conjugate two-syllable meter, and as an independent one otherwise. Let's start with an independent four-syllable meter. I must admit that I do not know of any convincing examples of Russian poetry subordinated to an independent four-syllable meter. Probably there are none - one can come to this conclusion by reading the article by poet L.E. Lyapina “Russian Peons” in the already cited collection “Russian Versification”. (Peonies are the scientific name for four-syllable meters; and five-syllable meters are scientifically called pentons.) Why is this so? In search of an answer, let's try to construct a line subordinated to an independent four-syllable meter: I wrote forty articles for the magazine «Kvant».
