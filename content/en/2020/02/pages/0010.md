The rotation of the Earth leads to oblateness at the poles; its equatorial radius is 20 kilometers greater than the polar one. This reason and the heterogeneity of the earth's matter lead to the inequality of the total moment of gravitational forces from the objects of the Solar System to zero. It causes the precession of the earth's axis. The so-called “celestial poles”, projections of the earth’s axis onto the celestial sphere, move along it, describing circles with an angular opening twice in $23,5\infty$. In the heliocentric system, where the Sun is motionless, this corresponds to the angle between the Earth's axis of rotation and the plane of the Earth's orbit. It was already noted above that this is the reason for the change of seasons. The axis of rotation slowly but surely turns, the angular speed of this rotation corresponds to a rotation period of 26,000 years. And the seasons should move just as slowly along the annual calendar—by about a month every 2,200 years. And accordingly, for each area on Earth (its geographic latitude), the culminating daily height of the Sun above the horizon and the average annual value of this height will change, i.e. the climate will change. It is possible that this is the reason for the periodic appearance and disappearance of the so-called little ice ages in temperate latitudes (another possible reason is long-term changes in solar activity). It is quite obvious that the precession of the earth's axis should be somehow reflected in calendar systems, primarily in those associated with the starry sky, for example, in the astrological calendar. This is a small correction and was never taken into account until the modern Gregorian calendar. This calendar uses the so-called true or tropical year, the duration of which is defined as the time interval between two successive passages of the center of the Sun's disk through the vernal equinox. Thus, the influence of the precession of the earth's axis is already taken into account automatically. And the problem of developing solar calendars is coordinating the length of the tropical year with the length of the day. The duration of the tropical year in the modern era is 365.2422 days. And this “addition” of 0.2422 should be close to some rational number - the ratio of integers. The article by V.G. Surdin “Leap calendar” in the collection “Mathematical Component” (M.: Mathematical Etudes Foundation) discusses the general principle of constructing calendars based on a mathematical apparatus called “continued fractions”. The rational additions calculated using this apparatus are (in days) 1 4, 7 29, 8 33, 31 128... This means that to 365 days in a calendar year 1 day should be added once every 4 years, 7 days - once every 29 years, 8 days - once every 33 years, 31 days - once every 128 years, etc. The average annual error will be less and less - 11.25 minutes for the fraction 1 4, 70 seconds for the fraction 7 29, 20 seconds for the fraction 8 33 and slightly less than 1 second for the fraction 31 128. The reader immediately notices that the option with 1 4 is the Julian calendar with its “leap” addition on February 29, 1 once every 4 years. What is the current Gregorian calendar from this mathematical (arithmetic) point of view? The answer to this question is in the second part of the article.
