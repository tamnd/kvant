If a word contains at least one strong syllable of (n,k)-size, then its stressed syllable is also a strong syllable of (n,k)-size. In other words: if the stressed syllable of a word is not strong, then that word contains no strong syllables at all.

For example, the iambic discussed above is 2.2 meter. A poem is in (n,k)-meter if each line is in (n,k)-meter. When reading a poem, strong syllables are emphasized with more or less distinct stress. But let us emphasize once again: one should not confuse, much less equate, strong and stressed syllables. A strong syllable does not have to be stressed (the first syllable of the word zalenog from the second line of Eugene Onegin), but a stressed syllable does not have to be strong (the word is not in the same line). The rule placed in the frame precisely indicates when a strong syllable must be stressed, i.e. when the syllable with a certain number in a line actually gets stressed when read correctly. In more detail, (n, k)-meter is called n-complex time signature with kth strong beat.

Exercise 1. a) Prove that if in a line of (n,k)-meter a strong syllable belongs to a word containing no more than n syllables, then this syllable is stressed. b) If a line contains no words containing more than n syllables, then the condition of subordination of this line to (n,k)-meter is equivalent to the fact that all strong syllables are stressed.
