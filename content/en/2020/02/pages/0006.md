Just as on the surface of the Earth two angles on a spherical surface (latitude and longitude) are used as coordinates, on the inner surface of the sphere of the starry sky, angular spherical coordinates can also be used. There are special measuring instruments for this. But such devices had yet to be created - the most ancient of them were invented in Babylon and Ancient Greece in the fifth and fourth centuries BC. But calendars already existed.

Constellations became an analogue of angular coordinates for ancient stargazers. Even in ancient times, it was noticed that the night sky can be divided into sections, each of which contains a group of stars forming a schematic figure. These groups (constellations) began to be given various names, most of which were the names of gods and mythological heroes, as well as animals. The names of the constellations were the first coordinates on the celestial sphere. The ancient Greeks distinguished 88 such “coordinates”. Over the life of several generations, stellar figure looked unchanged - the stars seemed as if attached to the celestial sphere, which as a whole revolved around the earthly observer, describing a full circle per day.

If you are patient and sit motionless on a moonless night, looking at the sky, then it is not difficult to determine that in a couple of hours the celestial sphere with the stars rotates through an angle of approximately $30\infty$. This bewitching panorama was wonderfully expressed by our great poet:

The night is frosty, the whole sky is clear, The heavenly lights are a wondrous choir Flowing so quietly, so harmoniously.

Of course, we know that in reality the “wonderful choir” does not flow anywhere. It is not the stellar sphere that rotates around the stationary Earth, but, on the contrary, the Earth rotates around its axis. But for thousands of years, almost all people, looking at the sky, thought differently. There were only a few dissenters; they were forced to remain silent. The first who put forward the heliocentric system of the world and made its substantiation was the outstanding Greek mathematician, astronomer and philosopher Aristarchus of Samos (third century BC). But the curses of the priests fell upon him. It took almost two thousand years for the geocentric system to give way to the heliocentric system through the works of Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo and many other astronomers. And even then not right away. The famous “but still she spins!” the great Galileo most likely said it mentally, leaving the Inquisition court with a promise not to speak like that out loud again. That is why initially all calendars were based on the geocentric system of the world, in which the Earth is motionless. And even today she is good not only as a poetic image. The geocentric system is convenient for creating calendars. It is on this basis that spherical astronomy is built, which introduces a certain imaginary sphere at a huge distance from the Earth, much greater than the distance from the Earth to the Sun. Indeed, even the star closest to the Sun is 270,000 times further away from us.
