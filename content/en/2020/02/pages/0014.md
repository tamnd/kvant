A wolfhound is rushing at my neck, But I am not a wolf by blood. Better stuff me, like a hat, into the sleeve of the Hot fur coat of the Siberian steppes... (Mandelshtam) Authors of poems written in three-syllable meter have to put up with its monotony, which, however, gives the poems some solemnity (“Bacchic Song” by Pushkin), and in other cases makes the poem related to the soulful Russian song (remember When I served as a coachman at the post office and Slavnoe the sea, sacred Baikal, and the Tramp is approaching Baikal, and even Upwards you, comrades, are all in place, and even you fell victim in the fatal struggle!). But is there a way to overcome the stringy monotony of the three-syllable meter? There is such a method, and it is very old: you do not need to strictly follow the three-syllable meter, but systematically violate its rhythm. An example of such a modified trisyllabic meter is the hexameter, perhaps the most ancient of poetic meters. An example of a hexameter can be Pushkin's lyceum epigram: Tredyakovsky's grandson Clit writes songs with a hexameter... The strong syllables of a hexameter are the 1st, 4th, 7th, 9th, 12th and 15th syllables; thus the hexameter is a mixture of dactyl and anapest. Here is an interesting example from Lermontov:

They loved each other so long and tenderly, With deep melancholy and insanely rebellious passion! But, like enemies, they avoided recognition and meeting, And their short speeches were empty and cold. This is a dactyl, but without stress on the 1st syllable. Agree that the size of this poem cannot be called monotonous or boring. A similar size is found in Feta:

Exhausted by life, by the treachery of hope, When I yield my soul to them in battle, Both day and night I close my eyelids And somehow strangely sometimes I see clearly.

In later poets, a modified trisyllabic meter is found very often in one way or another. I will limit myself to one example from Gumilyov: A girl dropped her ring into a well, a night well. He stretches his light fingers to the cold spring water. Give me back the ring, the well, It contains a red Ceylon ruby...

In this poem, even lines are written in amphibrachium, and odd lines are written in modified amphibrachium: the stress is transferred from the 2nd syllable to the 3rd. Non-classical sizes. In our definition of (n,k)-size, n can be any natural number (although it is natural to take it not too large, say, so that the line has at least two strong syllables). What about (n,k)-size with n z 2 and 3? Let's start with n = 1. Obviously, any text is subject to 1,1 -meter: all syllables are strong. Reading such poems means chanting (shai-bu-shai-bu!). Poems of this kind exist, for example: He doesn’t eat, he doesn’t drink, his little son (Nekrasov), Ko-siko-sa-po-ka-ro-sa (Tvardovsky). But they are rare.
