The best way to observe waves is by looking at the shadow on the bottom of the plate in direct sunlight or the light of a bright lamp. Each wave, acting like a cylindrical lens, produces a light stripe on the bottom of the plate that follows the shape of the wave front. However, waves traveling at a speed of about 10 cm s merge for the gaze fixed on a stationary plate. They are visible only near the source, where their amplitude is large. To see them across the entire surface of the water, you need to quickly turn your head. In the same way, with rapid head movements, you can see the spokes of a rolling wheel. It is very impressive to observe waves on the frosted glass of a camera, especially a large-format one. By holding the camera in your hands and gently rocking it, it is easy to ensure that the waves are visible over the entire surface. At the same time, it seems that they are running very slowly. You can look at the reflection of the surface of the water in the mirror. Slight movements of the mirror also make waves visible across the entire surface. But it is most convenient to observe waves under stroboscopic lighting. If you illuminate the installation with short flashes of light with a frequency equal to the frequency of the wave source, then from one flash to the next the waves will move one wavelength and the wave pattern of the waves will appear motionless. This kind of lighting is extremely easy to achieve. It is enough to turn on a small light bulb parallel to the windings of the bell magnet. From a distance of 0.5–1 m, it will illuminate the installation well and evenly, and the motionless shadow pattern of the waves will be clearly visible. For photography, it is better to use direct sunlight. Now attach a bent wire “fork” with pieces of cork at the ends to the bell hammer. The distance between the ends of the fork is 2–3 cm. If the ends are lowered into the water at the same time, you will get two sources of waves that oscillate not only synchronously, or in time, but also in phase, i.e. waves from both sources will occur at the same time. The picture will look something like this. Areas of large amplitudes fan out from the wave sources, separated by areas of “silence.”

The central region of large amplitudes is located perpendicular to the line connecting the sources. Both regions of silence and regions of high amplitudes pass between the sources. Studying the interference pattern with a ruler will show that the distance between the two maxima on the line connecting the sources is equal to half the distance between the two humps, i.e. half the wavelength. If you change the distance between the sources, the number of large amplitude bands will also change. The greater the distance between the sources, the more “feathers” there are in our fan. But the distance between the maxima on the line connecting the sources is always equal to half the wavelength. Therefore, the total number of bands with maximum amplitude will be twice as large as the number of wavelengths that fit at the distance between the sources. It follows that if the distance between the sources is less than half the wavelength, then there will be no interference at all. Such sources act as one, producing one system of circular waves. This can be seen by gradually decreasing the distance between the sources. Please also note that, if we continue the wave front passing through the hump in some region of large amplitudes, we will encounter a depression in the neighboring region. In other words: when passing through the zero region, the phase of the wave changes to half a full oscillation. Now imagine that we do not have two hammers, but two light sources emitting light waves, and we place a screen perpendicular to the surface of the water. We will see illuminated areas corresponding to the intersection of the screen and areas with large amplitude, and dark, unlit areas. In other words, we will see dark and light interference fringes. We’ll talk about how to carry out interference experiments in optics some other time, but now let’s try to explain what we saw.
