Fig. 2. Addition of oscillations during the interference of waves from two point sources. Red lines are humps, blue lines are depressions. Thickened arcs show the resulting waves

which came out of the sources at the same time. From figure it is clear that waves with the same numbers simultaneously arrive on a line equidistant from the sources. This is understandable, since the same paths traveled to the points of this direct wave. Based on the principle of superposition, we can conclude that on this line both the heights of the humps and the depths of the depressions will double. To the right and left of this line are the points at which the humps of one wave system coincide with the troughs of another. While waves from one source will cause an upward deflection at these points, waves from another at the same moment will cause a downward deflection at these points. The total deviation at these points will be close to zero. Let's connect all such points with a continuous black line. If you follow the numbering of the humps and troughs, it is easy to conclude that to all points on the right line, the left waves travel a path half the wavelength longer than the right ones. For the left line, the right waves lag behind by half the wavelength. To the right and left of the zero lines are the points of intersection of the first hump with the second,

"QUANTA"

⟦column⟧

the second with the third, etc. It is easy to understand that these are also areas of maximums. By connecting these points, we get a line, up to the points of which one wave system lags behind the other by one wavelength. Continuing the analysis of the drawing, you can find all the zero lines and all the maximum lines. The lines we got are called hyperbolas by mathematicians. (A hyperbola is the locus of points, the difference in distances from which to two points - foci - is constant.) Now it is completely clear why the distance between the maxima on the line connecting the sources is equal to half the wavelength. Indeed, the waves arrive at the midpoint of this line in the same phase and reinforce each other. If you move from this point by half a wavelength, then the path of one wave will increase by half the wavelength, and the other will decrease by the same amount. The path difference between the waves at this point (the difference in the paths traveled by the waves from the sources to the point) will be equal to one wavelength, and the waves will again mutually intensify. This will happen every half wavelength. The maximum corresponding to zero path difference is called zero maximum or zero order interference. Maxima with a path difference of one wavelength are maxima of the first order of interference, etc. The maximum order of interference is determined by the integer closest to 2d O, where d is the distance between the sources and O is the wavelength. Now try to analyze it yourself using a drawing, and maybe check experimentally how the picture will change if one of the sources emits waves delayed by half the period or some other part of it. What if the shift between oscillations changes randomly? To experimentally implement waves with a phase shift, it is enough to make the ends of the fork of different lengths.
