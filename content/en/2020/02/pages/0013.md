Which size is better? If you ask a friend: “Have you subscribed to the magazine «Kvant»?”, then most likely you will emphasize the words “subscribed” and «Kvant», leaving the words “you”, “on” and “magazine” unstressed. Total, 2 stresses on 9 syllables. I think this is the norm for colloquial speech: one stress per 4-5 syllables. If you are answering a lesson or reading a speech from a piece of paper, there will be more stress (this phenomenon is described by the word “mumbling”). When you read poetry “with expression,” there will be even more stress: about a third of all syllables will be stressed. This circumstance prompted some critics of the second half of the last century to conclude that the most suitable meters for Russian versification are three-syllable meters. Indeed, three-syllable meters occupy a significant place in the work of poets of this time, primarily Nekrasov and his circle. But something else is also true. Pushkin almost never used trisyllabic meters: 80% of his poems were written in iambic, 15% in trochaic, and very few in trisyllabic meters, and these were mostly parodies, epigrams, etc. (To be fair, it must be said that among the few poems by Pushkin written in three-syllable meters, there are very well-known ones; besides those mentioned above, one can name Ring out, bacchanalian melodies..., I look like crazy at a black shawl... and some others. By the way, among three-syllable meters, Pushkin gave a clear preference to amphibrachy; I don’t know how to explain this.) Many poems by Lermontov and almost all of Tyutchev's poems. Preference for two-syllable meters (and among them the iambic) was given by poets contemporary to Nekrasov, such as Fet and Maikov, not to mention later poets. Let's try to figure out what is the reason for this.

Two-syllable versus three-syllable meter. If we read a poem written in iambic or trochee and emphasize all the strong syllables, we will get a counting rhyme. For example: You ’ walked a month from the tumbn, $Вы^{’}$ nul nuzhik and ’ з karmbna... To bring the number of stresses to the norm about which we agreed above (one stress per 3 syllables), we must skip - or muffle - the stress on parts of strong syllables. This gives two-syllable verses many wonderful qualities. The first of these is diversity. Depending on which strong syllables are emphasized, a line of poetry can sound completely different. Compare, for example: Under an embankment, in an unmown ditch... (Block)

Will you have to bury me... (Shakespeare translated by Marshak)

It's hard to believe that the sizes of these lines are the same, but they are! Moreover, the same poem, written in iambic or trochee, can sound rhythmically completely different from different performers. The second quality is closeness to spoken language. The ability to not drum all strong syllables with equal force allows you to muffle the rhythm of the verse, thereby bringing it closer to prose of the highest quality. Personally, I cannot imagine how a “novel in verse” could be written in trisyllabic meter. Finally, thirdly, the selection of a part of strong syllables in a two-syllable size can itself obey some arithmetic rule, creating an additional rhythm. For example, if in a poem subject to a two-syllable meter, all even strong syllables are muted and the odd ones are emphasized (or vice versa), then the poem will become a four-syllable meter!
