PROBLEM WORKER

There are deformations, but an absolutely rigid body does not deform. Another example: the inductance of conductors (or - which is the same thing - the energy of the magnetic field that arises around a conductor when current is passed through it) is often neglected. However, in the well-known problem about a capacitor, which after charging is short-circuited with a conductor, one cannot simultaneously neglect the resistance of the conductor and its inductance - if the resistance is very small, then the current turns out to be very large and even with low inductance the energy of the magnetic field turns out to be significant. A simple analysis shows that inductance can indeed be neglected only if the condition

$L^{≪} R^{2} C$.

Very often, when using a “bad” model, the ends do not meet – energy disappears (or appears) somewhere, there are not enough equations to solve the problem, etc. This should always be a concern! Let's look at a useful example: a homogeneous beam is suspended on two threads. It is required to find the tension forces of the threads. This problem can be solved very simply: we will assume that the threads are inextensible, and we will draw up equilibrium equations, i.e. Let us write down the conditions for the equality of forces and moments of forces, and the unknown quantities will immediately be determined. Let's now add another thread. Troubles immediately appear - there are not enough equations. What's the matter? The reason here is quite serious - the model is not suitable. In the case of two threads, it was suitable - small stretches of the threads were insignificant, they only led to the fact that the beam hung slightly crookedly, and the forces practically did not change. If there are three threads, the situation is completely different. If we loosen one thread a little, the entire load will immediately fall on the other two. This means that small stretches are significant and the “inextensible threads” model is not suitable. (After all, you can neglect some factor

The situation can be easily corrected - you need to set the rigidities of the threads (can be the same if their lengths are the same) and, from geometric considerations, find the connection between the elongations of the threads, and therefore between the tension forces. This will be the missing equation. Systems of this kind are called “statically indeterminate”, meaning the need to add equations characterizing the physical properties of bodies (for example, rigidity) to the equations of forces and moments - the equations of statics. Statically indeterminate problems are not that rare. For example, the problem of the distribution of pressure forces on a brick lying on an inclined plane. It is similar to the previous one, only there are not three supports (threads), but a lot. The slightest unevenness on the base of the brick radically changes the result - the brick cannot be considered an absolutely solid body. Particularly tricky problems are those where many bodies interact and there is friction. One example: the problem about sand poured into the back of a car (see problem F656). The pressure on the wall of the body depends on how we poured the sand, and on how exactly the car is moving (let us remind those who have not ridden in the back of a truck for a long time that even when moving at a constant speed, the body shakes violently). Due to continuous pushes in different directions, the dry sand is constantly mixed, while the frictional forces between the sand grains constantly change direction. On average, the effect of friction forces is compensated, and the sand resembles a liquid poured into the body. (Something similar happens when, wanting to observe the pattern of magnetic field lines, we sprinkle iron filings on a sheet of paper and shake it to reduce friction.)
