About multiplication

A. SAVIN

It depends on who you are, but the MULTIPLICATION TABLES were difficult for me. Of course, “twice two is four” and “five five is twenty five” were easy to remember, but “seven eight” or “nine six” just couldn’t fit into my head. I calculated “difficult” works approximately like this: “five eight is forty, and two times eight is sixteen, which means a total of fifty-six.” But the teacher demanded fluent answers, and the table had to be “drilled” by heart. This is familiar not only to me - it’s not without reason that for hundreds of years, schoolchildren from different countries, each in their own language, called it the “multiplication dot.” (By the way, among my students - physics and technology students - there are those who know it very unsteadily.) In the end, I learned the table. Subsequently, it turned out that it was not the teacher who needed it, but myself: with its help, it turned out to be possible to multiply any numbers, and quite quickly. So I lived for many years in full confidence that without a multiplication table it was impossible to quickly multiply numbers. This confidence was reinforced by what I learned about methods of multiplication in India, China, and Europe during the Renaissance... And then one day I came across the “Russian peasant method of multiplication,” which was common in Russia two centuries ago, and was amazed to discover that Russian peasants knew how to multiply numbers without the help of a multiplication table! It was enough for them to be able to multiply and divide by two, as well as add numbers. This is how they did it. Let's write one of the numbers on the left, the second on the right on one line.
