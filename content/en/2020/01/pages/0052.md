How many different colors are there in the world? Where do different colors even come from? These and many other questions have long occupied inquisitive people. The great Newton made a great contribution to the creation of the science of color. He observed the decomposition of sunlight (white) light as it passed through a glass prism into the so-called spectrum (from the Latin spectrum). The resulting colors exactly match the colors of the rainbow. The prism allowed Newton to obtain rainbows in “laboratory” conditions. Here they are, the colors of the rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. Look again at the title of the article. We used a humorous phrase to help remember the sequence of colors in the solar spectrum

Surely some of you will remember another mnemonic rule - the phrase “every hunter wants to know where the pheasant sits.” It was thanks to Newton's experiments at the end of the 17th century that it was established that white light is a mixture of different colors. (In particular, Newton showed that if white light, decomposed into a spectrum, is mixed, then white light will again be obtained.) It took a long time until scientists realized that light is oscillations of electric and magnetic fields associated with each other propagating in space, in other words, electromagnetic waves. The most common example of this kind of waves is radio waves. It has been proven that the nature of light and radio waves is the same, they differ only in the frequency of vibration. The frequency of radio waves is thousands of times lower than that of light. Each color corresponds to a definition of vibration frequency. If we use an analogy with musical instruments, then red corresponds to “bass”, and purple corresponds to high notes. The speed of propagation of electromagnetic waves is c = 300,000 km s.
