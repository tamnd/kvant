Particles or waves?

Degree of freedom

From the very beginning, mechanics encountered problems that could be divided into two completely different classes. The motion of material points and rigid bodies was described by Newton's equations. From these equations it was possible to determine the trajectories of motion of bodies, for example the planets of the Solar System, and describe how movement occurs along the trajectories. But there were other objects. The movement of water in channels, the propagation of sound in the air, the bending of an iron beam - all these problems belonged to the mechanics of continuous media, and they were dealt with by hydrodynamics, aerodynamics, elasticity theory and other branches of mechanics. A continuous medium and a system of material points were represented as completely different physical objects. If, while solving a problem about the flow of water, we mentally isolated a small volume of liquid, then this volume was not connected in any way with the molecules of the liquid (they generally learned about molecules many years after the equations of hydrodynamics were written). Waves in water or air (such as those called sound) and planets moving around the sun seemed to have little in common. Everything was clear, but in optics the question remained unresolved: what is light? A flow of tiny particles, as Newton, a supporter of the corpuscular theory, thought, or are these waves in some medium, the world ether, as Huygens, the creator of wave optics, thought? The popularity of each theory varied at different times, but no one could find a decisive argument in favor of one of them: light in some phenomena behaved like a stream of corpuscles, in others - like waves. Now we know well that there is no contradiction in this - it became possible to believe in this only thanks to quantum theory. In the last century, the contradiction seemed insoluble. The difference between a system of material particles and a continuous medium appears very clearly if you look at the number of coordinates that define the state of the system. The position of each point in space is specified by three numbers - three coordinates. They say that a material point has three degrees of freedom. If a system includes N material points, then it is said to have 3N degrees of freedom. The same reasoning can be carried out for speeds. The speed of one point is described by three numbers - three components of the speed vector. The velocities of N points require 3N numbers for their description. How many numbers must be given to describe the state of the sea surface? Strictly speaking, for each point on the surface you need to specify three numbers - the vector of water velocity at a given point; therefore, there will be an infinite number of numbers. The surface of the sea appears to us as a system with an infinitely large number of degrees of freedom. Even the fact that water consists of molecules, and therefore the number of degrees of freedom can be determined by counting the molecules, does not make the task easier: there are so many molecules that practically the number of degrees of freedom remains infinitely large. In reality, we are not interested in the movement of each molecule. When waves move across the sea, for example from a moving ship, we can describe the pattern of wave distribution using relatively few numbers. We can set the amplitude and phase of each wave; Although there are many waves, there are still fewer waves than molecules. Moreover, the pattern basically repeats itself over time: the waves are more or less the same. Many molecules move in each wave, the movement is collective in nature, and we can talk about collective degrees of freedom on the surface of the sea, in contrast to individual degrees.
