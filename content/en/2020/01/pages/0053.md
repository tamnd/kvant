It is thousands of times faster than the speed of sound, so that a radio listener sitting near a radio at home in Vladivostok hears the singer’s voice at a concert broadcast from Moscow earlier than a listener sitting in the back row of the concert hall. The given value for the speed of light (c = 3 ◊ $10^{5}$ km s) refers to vacuum. When light propagates through a transparent substance, the speed is lower. Of particular interest is the fact that when light of different colors passes through matter, it travels at different speeds. This phenomenon is called dispersion, and it is thanks to dispersion that the prism decomposes white light into a colored spectrum. So, from white light you can get all the colors of the rainbow. What do the different colors in the pictures in this magazine mean? As soon as you take a magazine with you into a dark room, all the colors will disappear altogether, the pictures will become just gray - they themselves do not “glow”, no matter what color they are. To see them in different colors, they need to be illuminated. In this case, the light falling on the page is partially absorbed, partially scattered, and we see precisely the scattered light rays. It is very important that different substances (due to the structural features of their molecules) have selectivity to color - they absorb and scatter light differently from different parts of the spectrum. Ocher appears yellow to us because almost all rays except yellow are absorbed from the white light falling on it. Or remember about... a tomato. At different stages of maturation, it preferentially scatters green rays first, then red ones. This shows changes in the molecular structure of the tomato substance. By the way, it is no coincidence that in chemistry color is an important characteristic of a substance. The time has come to leave earthly worries and... soar. However, why then is the night sky not blue even with a full moon? Or why are different parts of the sky colored blue differently - some more brightly, others less brightly? And if we turn to the sunset? An attentive physicist who observed the sunset noted that the western part of the sky first takes on a yellow or orange tint; then, as the sun turns fiery red, the glow of the sky changes from yellow-orange to bright green; finally, up to approximately a height of $25\infty$ above the horizon, the sky turns pink. Since the cause of all the listed color collisions is the scattering of sunlight in the atmosphere, let us dwell on this process in more detail.
