The explanation of the peculiarities of the color of the sky belongs to the English physicist W. Rayleigh. In general terms, the essence of the matter is as follows. The color of the sky is determined by the dependence of light scattering on the frequency of the latter. An electromagnetic wave coming from the sun “swings” the electrons that make up the air molecules. The blue part of the spectrum has the strongest effect on electrons. Thus, from the incoming light wave, the electrons of air molecules “take away” the vibrations of the blue part of the spectrum. But, having entered into a forced oscillatory motion, the electrons give back the energy taken from the wave. However, they give off this energy in all directions, and not just in the direction of the fall of the initial wave from the sun. This process is the scattering of light. Essential for explaining the scattering of light in the atmosphere is the uneven distribution of air molecules in the atmosphere - constant fluctuations in air density. If the molecules were evenly distributed in space, the scattering would be completely different - the sky would simply be black. So, air molecules scatter the blue part of the spectrum most strongly, and therefore the parts of the sky that we see in diffused light appear blue to us.$^{1}$ The farther away the corresponding parts of the sky are from the sun, the less bright they will seem to us. The reason is that the longer the path of the sun's rays travels in the atmosphere, the smaller the proportion in them is of the blue component. Now, you probably understand why they say “red sun”? To the observer, the radiation is precisely the red-yellow part of the spectrum. It is obvious that smoke, dust and other small particles contained in the air significantly affect the scattering of light in the atmosphere. It is known that after large volcanic eruptions, sunrises and sunsets sometimes play with amazing colors - the sun and moon can even turn blue. Let us give a description of the consequences of the catastrophic eruption of the Krakatau volcano in 1883, which is given in his book “Entertaining Geology” by the wonderful Russian scientist V.A. Obruchev: “Fine ash darkened the sun in Japan and in other places at a distance of over 3000 kilometers. This ash, which floated for a long time in the atmosphere, caused the bluish color of the sun and moon in Africa and the Pacific Islands and the wonderful red dawns at the end of 1883 and at the beginning of 1884, observed throughout the earth.” The blue color of the sun and moon in this case is caused by the scattering of light on atmospheric aerosols with a size of 0.4–0.9 microns, i.e. size comparable to the wavelength of visible light. Atmospheric aerosols of the indicated sizes are formed by deposition of smoke, ash, etc. on solid particles. during volcanic eruptions and large forest fires. It is precisely because of their relatively large size that they scatter light from the red part of the spectrum more than from the blue part. This scattering is associated with the name of the German scientist Mie. When observing the sun and moon through such an aerosol, they appear bluish - the red component of white light is scattered, and only blue light reaches us.
