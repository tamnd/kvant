How many different colors are there in the world? Where do different colors even come from? These and many other questions have long occupied inquisitive people. The great Newton made a great contribution to the creation of the science of color. He observed the decomposition of sunlight (white) light as it passed through a glass prism into the so-called spectrum (from the Latin spectrum). The resulting colors exactly match the colors of the rainbow. The prism allowed Newton to obtain rainbows in “laboratory” conditions. Here they are, the colors of the rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. Look again at the title of the article. Surely some of you will remember another mnemonic rule - the phrase “every hunter wants to know where the pheasant sits.” It was thanks to Newton's experiments at the end of the 17th century that it was established that white light is a mixture of different colors. (In particular, Newton showed that if white light, decomposed into a spectrum, is mixed, then white light will again be obtained.) It took a long time until scientists realized that light is oscillations of electric and magnetic fields associated with each other propagating in space, in other words, electromagnetic waves. The most common example of this kind of waves is radio waves. It has been proven that the nature of light and radio waves is the same, they differ only in the frequency of vibration. The frequency of radio waves is thousands of times lower than that of light. Each color corresponds to a definition of vibration frequency. If we use an analogy with musical instruments, then red corresponds to “bass”, and purple corresponds to high notes. The speed of propagation of electromagnetic waves is c = 300,000 km s. The given value for the speed of light (c = 3 ◊ $10^{5}$ km s) refers to vacuum. When light propagates through a transparent substance, the speed is lower. Of particular interest is the fact that when light of different colors passes through matter, it travels at different speeds. This phenomenon is called dispersion, and it is thanks to dispersion that the prism decomposes white light into a colored spectrum. So, from white light you can get all the colors of the rainbow. What do the different colors in the pictures in this magazine mean? As soon as you take a magazine with you into a dark room, all the colors will disappear altogether, the pictures will become just gray - they themselves do not “glow”, no matter what color they are. To see them in different colors, they need to be illuminated. In this case, the light falling on the page is partially absorbed, partially scattered, and we see precisely the scattered light rays. It is very important that different substances (due to the structural features of their molecules) have selectivity to color - they absorb and scatter light differently from different parts of the spectrum. Ocher appears yellow to us because almost all rays except yellow are absorbed from the white light falling on it. Or remember about... a tomato. At different stages of maturation, it preferentially scatters green rays first, then red ones. This shows changes in the molecular structure of the tomato substance. The explanation of the peculiarities of the color of the sky belongs to the English physicist W. Rayleigh. In general terms, the essence of the matter is as follows. The color of the sky is determined by the dependence of light scattering on the frequency of the latter. An electromagnetic wave coming from the sun “swings” the electrons that make up the air molecules. The blue part of the spectrum has the strongest effect on electrons. Thus, from the incoming light wave, the electrons of air molecules “take away” the vibrations of the blue part of the spectrum. But, having entered into a forced oscillatory motion, the electrons give back the energy taken from the wave. However, they give off this energy in all directions, and not just in the direction of the fall of the initial wave from the sun. This process is the scattering of light. Essential for explaining the scattering of light in the atmosphere is the uneven distribution of air molecules in the atmosphere - constant fluctuations in air density. If the molecules were evenly distributed in space, the scattering would be completely different - the sky would simply be black. So, air molecules scatter the blue part of the spectrum most strongly, and therefore the parts of the sky that we see in diffused light appear blue to us.$^{1}$ The farther away the corresponding parts of the sky are from the sun, the less bright they will seem to us. The reason is that the longer the path of the sun's rays travels in the atmosphere, the smaller the proportion in them is of the blue component. Now, you probably understand why they say “red sun”? Direct sunlight on the way to us “loses” the blue component, and the predominant share of the radiation coming to us

It is obvious that smoke, dust and other small particles contained in the air significantly affect the scattering of light in the atmosphere. It is known that after large volcanic eruptions, sunrises and sunsets sometimes play with amazing colors - the sun and moon can even turn blue. Let us give a description of the consequences of the catastrophic eruption of the Krakatau volcano in 1883, which is given in his book “Entertaining Geology” by the wonderful Russian scientist V.A. Obruchev: “Fine ash darkened the sun in Japan and in other places at a distance of over 3000 kilometers. This ash, which floated for a long time in the atmosphere, caused the bluish color of the sun and moon in Africa and the Pacific Islands and the wonderful red dawns at the end of 1883 and at the beginning of 1884, observed throughout the earth.” The blue color of the sun and moon in this case is caused by the scattering of light on atmospheric aerosols with a size of 0.4–0.9 microns, i.e. size comparable to the wavelength of visible light. Atmospheric aerosols of the indicated sizes are formed by deposition of smoke, ash, etc. on solid particles. during volcanic eruptions and large forest fires. It is precisely because of their relatively large size that they scatter light from the red part of the spectrum more than from the blue part. This scattering is associated with the name of the German scientist Mie. When observing the sun and moon through such an aerosol, they appear bluish - the red component of white light is scattered, and only blue light reaches us.
