And although no one doubted the positive answer to this question, direct experimental testing of conservation laws would still be very useful. Such a test was carried out in 1925 by Arthur Compton, who studied how light quanta are scattered when they fall on a resting electron. Compton discovered, firstly, that the electron experiences recoil, i.e. the electron receives an impulse from the quantum, and secondly, that the quantum loses energy and its frequency decreases. Qualitatively, the picture was similar to a particle collision. But Compton showed more - that the energy and momentum of the electron and quantum at the end of the collision are exactly the same as those obtained from the equations describing the laws

How a tree saves you from the rain

...it seems like it's going to rain, it seems like it's going to rain... A. Milne

Let's ask ourselves an extremely simple question: how does a tree protect from rain? Of course, we are not talking about a palm tree whose leaves can cover a herd of elephants. But you can hide from the rain under a spruce or a birch tree. What happens then? Do not rush to answer, which suggests itself - that the drops linger on the leaves. After all, more than two or three drops will not stay on the sheet. Do not think that drops evaporate from the leaves during rain - the humidity is so great when it rains that even washed clothes do not dry. How do small leaves, and especially spruce or pine needles, save you from the rain? Or did we deceive ourselves by hiding under a tree when “... bad weather broke out: they say-

Published in «Kvant» No. 7, 1991 and «Kvant» No. 4, 1992.

The light was sparkling, and the rain was pouring down like buckets” (G.H. Andersen)? As everyday experience shows, birch or spruce - and no worse than a palm tree - really saves you from the rain. And here's how. A drop falling on a leaf (or needle) slides down, as if on a cable car, to the part of the leaf farthest from the trunk - the tip. A drop falls from the tip and... falls onto the next sheet located below. In rainy weather in the forest you can hear “...many different shades of the sound of raindrops falling from leaf to leaf” (Marcel Aimé), and see how “...the stupefied moisture ran from cup to cup like a thunderstorm” (B. Pasternak). Rolling off the leaf (needles), the drop moves away from the trunk by three to four centimeters. And if it encounters ten or twenty leaves (needles) on the way, it will be thrown back a considerable distance. It was as if it just rolled across the roof. That's it. E. Gurovich
