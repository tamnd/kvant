About multiplication

A. SAVIN

It depends on who you are, but the MULTIPLICATION TABLES were difficult for me. Of course, “twice two is four” and “five five is twenty five” were easy to remember, but “seven eight” or “nine six” just couldn’t fit into my head. I calculated “difficult” works approximately like this: “five eight is forty, and two times eight is sixteen, which means a total of fifty-six.” But the teacher demanded fluent answers, and the table had to be “drilled” by heart. This is familiar not only to me - it’s not without reason that for hundreds of years, schoolchildren from different countries, each in their own language, called it the “multiplication dot.” (By the way, among my students - physics and technology students - there are those who know it very unsteadily.) In the end, I learned the table. Subsequently, it turned out that it was not the teacher who needed it, but myself: with its help, it turned out to be possible to multiply any numbers, and quite quickly. So I lived for many years in full confidence that without a multiplication table it was impossible to quickly multiply numbers. This confidence was reinforced by what I learned about methods of multiplication in India, China, and Europe during the Renaissance... And then one day I came across the “Russian peasant method of multiplication,” which was common in Russia two centuries ago, and was amazed to discover that Russian peasants knew how to multiply numbers without the help of a multiplication table! It was enough for them to be able to multiply and divide by two, as well as add numbers. This is how they did it. Let's write one of the numbers on the left, the second on the right on one line. So, for the numbers allocated to figure 7, we have: 42 = ( 25 + 48 + 63 + 32 ) 4.) For long Over the millennium of development of mathematics, many ways of multiplying numbers have been invented. The Italian mathematician of the late 15th and early 16th centuries, Luca Pacioli, in his treatise on arithmetic, gives 8 different methods of multiplication. Here are two of them, in my opinion, the most interesting. In the first, which is called the “small castle” (Fig.8), the digits of the upper number, starting with the highest, are multiplied in turn by the lower number and written in a column with the required number of zeros added, and then the results are added up. The advantage of this method over the usual one is that the digits of the most significant digits are determined from the very beginning, and this can be important for rough calculations. The second method has an equally romantic name: “jealousy” (or “lattice multiplication”). A lattice is drawn into which the results of intermediate calculations are then entered (in fact, the required numbers from the multiplication table).
