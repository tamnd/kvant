How do physicists work?

Experimenters and theorists. There are two types of physicists - experimentalists and theorists, and these two professions are almost never combined in one person. Physicists-experiment-

thors explore the relationships between physical quantities, or, to put it more solemnly, discover the laws of nature, using experimental setups, i.e. making measurements of physical quantities using instruments. To do this, you need to deeply understand the phenomena being studied in order to know what and how to measure. Theoretical physicists study nature using only paper and pencil, i.e. deducing new relationships between observed quantities, relying on the laws of nature previously found experimentally and theoretically. The reason for the separation of these two professions is not only that each of them requires its own special knowledge: knowledge of measurement methods in one case and mastery of mathematical apparatus in the other. The main reason is that these professions correspond to different characters of thinking and different forms of intuition. Intuition, i.e. the ability to subconsciously find the right path plays a vital role, especially in the first stages of work. Unfortunately, the gift of intuition does not arise immediately, but as a result of hard work, as a reward for solving many scientific problems. Since theoretical physics deals with more abstract concepts than experimental physics, a theoretical physicist requires a more abstract form of intuition, sometimes close to the intuition of a mathematician. In the last century, when physics was not yet so specialized, many physicists combined both professions. Thus, Maxwell, who theoretically obtained equations that describe all electromagnetic phenomena, was also involved in experiments. Hertz, who discovered electromagnetic waves experimentally, was also a good theoretician. And yet, in each case, it is possible to indicate which of the professions is the main one: for Maxwell it is theoretical physics, and for Hertz it is experimental physics. In the 20th century, one of the most remarkable examples of a “universal” physicist - both theoretician and experimentalist - was

Enrico Fermi. Along with many other works, Fermi created the theory of radioactive decay and, together with the physicists of his group, discovered experimentally the artificial radioactivity of elements arising from bombardment by neutrons. Another example of an outstanding theoretician closely associated with experiment was Academician G.I. Budker, who combined theoretical physics with remarkable engineering ideas. He led the theoretical development and practical implementation of an accelerator using colliding beams of charged particles in the Novosibirsk Academgorodok. However, these are rare exceptions, and a young person interested in physics must decide for himself which of the two professions he chooses. In what follows we will talk about the work of theoretical physicists. Physics and mathematics. So, the task of a theoretical physicist is to obtain relationships between observable quantities using mathematical calculations. Doesn't this mean that theoretical physics is something like applied mathematics? This point of view is completely wrong. Both in the nature of the problems and in the methods of approaching problems, mathematics and theoretical physics are categorically different. In mathematics, mathematical rigor plays a vital role, i.e. the logical impeccability of all conclusions arising from the accepted axioms, and the study of all logically possible relationships. The task of physics is to recreate the most accurate picture of the world without strict “rules of the game”, using all known experimental and theoretical facts, as well as guesses based on intuition, which will later be tested experimentally. Thus, a mathematician explores all logically possible types of geometries, while a physicist finds out what geometric relationships are realized in our world.
