SCHOOL AT KVANT

About one poem by A.S. Pushkin

A.KIKOIN

AMONG THE POETIC TREASURES LEFT BY our great national poet A.S. Pushkin, there is a poem directly related to physics, or more precisely, to mechanics. This poem is called "Movement". It is small in volume - it has only 8 lines, but is very rich in content. In the first four lines we read:

There is no movement, said the bearded sage. The other fell silent and began to walk in front of him. He could not have objected more strongly; Everyone praised the intricate answer.

Here the poet talks about the legendary dispute between two ancient Greek philosophers. “The Bearded Sage” is Zeno of Elea; his opponent in the dispute is Diogenes of Sinope. The first argued that movement was impossible; the second began to silently “walk in front of him,” as if clearly demonstrating the absurdity of such a statement. And then A.S. Pushkin writes:

But, gentlemen, this funny incident Another example brings to mind: After all, every day the sun walks before us. However, stubborn Galileo is right.

With these words, the poet shows that Diogenes’ proof is not at all as flawless as it seemed to those who praised the “intricate answer.” Indeed, the sun does every day what Diogenes did when marching before Zeno: it “walks before us.” In reality, as “stubborn Galileo” argued after Copernicus, the sun is at rest, but the earth moves (rotates around its axis). Diogenes could not give proof of this, so he “kept silent.” Now the incorrectness of Zeno's statement can be proven without resorting to Diogenes' method. Let's try to do this. First of all, let’s try to imagine how Zeno came to the obviously absurd conclusion that “there is no movement.” Zeno apparently reasoned as follows. A body, moving along a certain trajectory, can be caught somewhere at any moment. We can assume that in this place and at this moment the body is at rest, i.e. that its speed is zero. Consequently, motion is only a name given to a multitude of states of rest that follow one after another. In each such state of rest, the displacement of the body is naturally equal to zero. Adding up this continuous set of zeros, Zeno, of course, ended up with zero: “there is no movement”! So, Zeno’s mistake lies precisely in the fact that he considered the speed of the body at each point of the trajectory to be equal to zero. In fact, at every moment of time a moving body has a speed - the so-called instantaneous speed v. And since it has speed, then for any arbitrarily short period of time 't the body makes a small displacement $'^{s} v '^{t}$. The number of such small movements, in the limit – infinitely small, for the entire time of movement t is infinitely large. But the sum of an infinitely large number of infinitesimal terms is not equal to zero, but to a well-defined value: s = vt (if the speed v is the same at all points).
