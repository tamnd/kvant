Conversation with Andrei Nikolaevich Kolmogorov

We are in an old wooden house in the village of Komarovka near Moscow, where Andrei Nikolaevich usually spends the end of the week. Bright, modestly furnished room. In one of the corners there is an old but high-quality record player with special shelves for records. The walls are lined with shelves of books. In the middle of the room there is a large table with many books, reprints of articles, manuscripts, art albums. Andrei Nikolaevich sits by the window at a small desk. Next to the typewriter and neatly folded, scribbled sheets of paper there is a tape recorder on which our conversation is recorded. We bring to your attention a transcript of this conversation. – Andrey Nikolaevich, we often hear about the increasing specialization of science. At the same time, it is known that you were involved in such widely separated areas of mathematics as probability theory and algebraic topology, mathematical logic and the theory of dynamical systems. What do you think is the future of science – universality or specialization? – Mathematics is great. One person cannot study all its ramifications. In this sense, specialization is inevitable. But at the same time, mathematics is a unified science. More and more new connections arise between its sections, sometimes in the most unexpected ways. Some sections serve as tools for other sections. Therefore, confining mathematicians within too narrow limits must be disastrous for our science. The situation is made easier by the fact that work in the field of mathematics is, in principle, collective. There must be a number of mathematicians who understand the interconnections between the most diverse areas of mathematics. You also need, at least in general terms, to understand the connections between your special field of research with related fields, to understand that, in essence, scientific work in mathematics is a collective work. – What can you say about the relationship and connections between applied and pure mathematics? – First of all, it should be noted that the very distinction between applied and pure mathematics is extremely arbitrary. Questions that seem to belong to pure mathematics and have no applications very often unexpectedly turn out to be important for various applications. On the other hand, when studying applied mathematics, a scientist almost inevitably comes across related questions that are solved by the same methods, attracting him with their logical beauty, but, strictly speaking, no longer receiving direct applications. Probably, in the practical work of a mathematician it is necessary to show due breadth. There is no doubt that mathematicians must, it is their duty, to deal with all those questions that are urgently imposed by questions of practice. If related questions, even if they do not have immediate applications, are attractive, if only because of the beauty and naturalness of the problems that arise, then, of course, they also need to be addressed.

– Norbert Wiener writes in his autobiographical book that he stopped doing functional analysis when he felt that “Kolmogorov was stepping on my heels.” How do you feel about competition in mathematics? – Wiener’s statement is not entirely clear to me. I haven't done much in functional analysis. My most interesting work on functional analysis is called “The Wiener Spiral and Some Other Interesting Curves in Hilbert Space.” As for competition, competition can be friendly, then it differs little from cooperation. A close collaboration, when two mathematicians simultaneously and in parallel think about the same problem, can sometimes be very productive. But at the same time, it sometimes happens that the participation of one of the employees turns out to be practically unnecessary and then it is reasonable for him to step aside without offense. – Has mathematics always been your main hobby? When did you finally choose mathematics as a profession? – No, as often happens, the paths of my development were more tortuous. From early childhood it was known that I could count well and that I was interested in mathematical problems of an arithmetic nature; I also became acquainted with the principles of algebra relatively early. But all this applies to a very early age. Somewhat later, in middle school, completely different hobbies prevailed - in particular, history. The return to mathematics occurred in the very last years of high school. When I graduated from high school, I hesitated for a long time in choosing a future path. In my first student years, in addition to mathematics, I studied most seriously in the seminar on ancient Russian history of Professor S.V. Bakhrushin. I didn’t give up the idea of ​​a technical career, for some reason I was fascinated by metallurgy, and, in parallel with the university, I entered the metallurgical department of the Mendeleev Institute of Chemical Technology and studied there for some time. The final choice of mathematics as a profession, in fact, occurred when I began to receive my first independent

scientific results, i.e. from eighteen to nineteen years old. – When do you usually show aptitude for mathematics? Always, like you, at an early age? – I taught quite a lot in high school. I have the impression that interest in mathematics in middle school, at the age of twelve or thirteen, often turns out to be temporary and disappears completely by high school. This happens especially often in girls. In my opinion, it is worth working with those schoolchildren who are passionate about mathematics at the age of 13–14–15. With skillful cultivation, their abilities gradually develop and, as a rule, are no longer lost. There are, of course, many exceptions. Of course, a serious interest in mathematics may appear later. – Which mathematicians of the older generation had the greatest influence on you? – During my student years, I was a student of Nikolai Nikolaevich Luzin. Besides him, Vyacheslav Vasilievich Stepanov, Alexander Yakovlevich Khinchin, Pavel Sergeevich Alexandrov and other mathematicians of their generation had a great influence on me. – What would you like to say about your students and which of them would you like to mention? – I was lucky to have talented students. Many of them, having started working with me in some area, then moved on to a new topic and, completely independently of me, received wonderful results. It would be difficult to single out the most worthy of mention. I will say only as a joke that at present one of my students controls the earth's atmosphere, and another controls the oceans. – Andrey Nikolaevich, what is your daily routine? – Naturally, during my rather long life, the daily routine at different periods was different. I will, perhaps, only describe the daily routine that Pavel Sergeevich Alexandrov and I established for ourselves for those 3-4 days a week that we spent outside the city, near Moscow, in the village of Komarovka. The day started at 7 o'clock in the morning. The first hour was devoted to gymnastics and jogging. At 8 o'clock we had breakfast and got to work.

Tell us why. – Your remark about many mathematicians who are interested in serious music seems correct to me. If you go to a concert hall, especially the Small Hall of the Moscow Conservatory, you will see a disproportionate number of mathematicians there. Apparently, there are some deep connections between mathematical creativity and a real interest in music. But it seems to me quite difficult to find out and explain these connections. I will note, however, that my friend Pavel Sergeevich Aleksandrov said that for him every direction of mathematical thought, topic for creative reflection was associated with one or another specific piece of music. Among my favorite composers I would name, first of all, Mozart, Schumann, and, of course, the greatest musicians - Bach, Beethoven. – Linguists and literary scholars have paid attention to your publications on poetry. What can you say about this less common combination: mathematics and poetry? – I would like to divide this question into two, since my passion for poetry has the same involuntary, spontaneous nature.

I note, however, that if all our readers began to write independent scientific works, then scientific journals would not withstand such an onslaught. Therefore, I will also express a more modest wish - that your school passion for mathematics will be useful to you in later life. In «Kvant» we are trying to show you how diverse the applications of mathematical science are.
