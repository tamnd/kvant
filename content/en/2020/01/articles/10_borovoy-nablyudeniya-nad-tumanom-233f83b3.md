To imagine how much the content of water vapor in the air changes with temperature changes, we present the following figures: the mass of saturated water vapor in $1 м^{3}$ air at 30 q C is 30 g; when air is cooled to 10 q C, the mass of saturated steam decreases to 10 g $м^{3}$; This means that in every cubic meter of air, 20 g of water should be released in the form of drops of fog or dew. You can determine the size of the droplets that make up the fog as follows. Hold a piece of glass smeared with Vaseline in the fog, and then examine the remaining drops of moisture on it under a microscope. A microscope is necessary because even the largest drops reach sizes of only tens of microns, their diameter is almost a hundred times less than the thickness of a match. Fogs usually do not last long. Small drops merge into larger ones and fall down - the fog falls like rain onto the ground. But it may also happen that in the warm air flow droplets of fog evaporate and the fog dissipates. All this can be observed in a simple experiment that requires a coffee pot, a frying pan and a small piece of rubber tubing. We insert the tube tightly into the spout of the coffee pot, pour water into it and place it on the stove. When the water boils, a white stream of water mist will begin to escape from the tube. Let's bring (using tweezers) the end of the tube to a cold frying pan standing on the adjacent burner. The fog will rise above it and partially settle in drops on the cold surface of the metal. But if you heat the pan, the fog will disappear.
