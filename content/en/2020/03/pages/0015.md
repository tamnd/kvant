what complex numbers are.$^{5}$ To begin with, we can look at them as formal sums of the form z = x + iy, where x, y are real numbers, and i is some new object called the imaginary unit and satisfies the equality $i^{2} = - 1$. For such sums, one can define the operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication and division so that a very harmonious and beautiful theory is obtained. For example, it turns out that any polynomial of degree $n \ge 1$ has exactly n roots in the set of complex numbers (including multiplicity). It is convenient to represent complex numbers as points on a plane, correlating the number z = x + iy with a point with coordinates ( x; y ). In this case, the quantities x and y are called, respectively, the real and imaginary parts of the complex number z, and the distance from the origin to such a point, equal to $x^{2} + y^{2}$, is called its modulus. The horizontal axis along which the real parts of complex numbers are plotted is called the real axis, the vertical axis is called the imaginary axis, and the plane itself is called the complex plane. The point $( x ; - y )$, symmetrical to the point (x; y) relative to the real axis, corresponds to the number $x - iy$, which is called conjugate to the number z and is denoted by z. Note that the real numbers we are familiar with feel great in the new world: they can be thought of as complex numbers with a zero imaginary part. Let us note, finally, that many functions that are well known to us, such as polynomial, exponential, sine, cosine, logarithm and others, can also be defined for a complex argument. The study of their properties is the subject of the theory of functions of a complex variable, which has many applications in mathematics and physics. Riemann zeta function and its zeros

So, Riemann indicated a way in which the value of $\zeta ( s )$ can be determined at any point $s = \sigma + it$ of the complex plane, with the possible exception of s = 1, so that the resulting function is given by series (4) for all real s > 1 and at the same time for all s retains a number of important properties inherent in (4). It turned out that the zeta function has infinitely many zeros, forming two sets. The first consists of “trivial” or real zeros at points of the form $s = - 2, - 4, - 6,$ ... All real zeros have multiplicity 1, and the behavior of the zeta function in the neighborhood of each of them is well known. The main mystery of the Riemann zeta function lies in the zeros of the second set. All of them lie in a narrow vertical strip of the form $0 \le \sigma \le 1$ and are located symmetrically with respect to the real axis and the vertical line $\sigma = 0,5$ (Fig.4).
