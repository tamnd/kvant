Introduction of a new style How was the introduction of the Gregorian calendar? In Catholic countries, the reform of 1582 was adopted almost immediately (due to the threat of excommunication in case of disobedience). But in Protestant countries it caused a storm of protests and fierce controversy even among scientists. German, Dutch and Swiss Protestants showed themselves to be especially zealous in this, who believed that “it is better to separate from the Sun than to get together with the Pope.” At the same time, the then most famous German astronomer, Johannes Kepler, although he was a Protestant, spoke out for reform. But they did not listen to him, and the implementation of calendar reform in Protestant countries lasted for several decades. England resisted the longest, which, in particular, still causes uncertainty about the birthday of the great Newton. The Orthodox Church (Constantinople, Antioch, Greek, Bulgarian, Russian, etc.) refused to accept the Gregorian system and some new rules for determining the day of Easter, so the days of Orthodox Easter and Catholic Easter may differ by a week. According to the Gregorian calendar, the earliest celebration of Easter is April 2, and the latest is May 8. To determine the day of Easter, even before the calendar reform, a system was developed in which the 19-year cycle of calendar repetition of the lunar phases also played a large role. Here is an algorithm for determining the day of Easter (it can be easily found on the Internet). Christianity came to Russia from Byzantium at the end of the 9th century. At that time the Christian Church was united. When Christianity split into two confessions in the 11th century, Rus' remained faithful to the Byzantine confession, which was called orthodox (faithful only to the decisions of the first seven ecumenical councils). Now in Russia this denomination of Christianity is usually called the Orthodox Church. Russia remained faithful to antiquity even after the state end of Byzantium in 1453. Therefore, the transition to the Gregorian calendar did not immediately take place in Russia, especially since the Catholic denomination (church) of Christianity, led by the popes, was hostile to the Russian state. Until 1917, the church in Russia was not separated from the state, but one of the first decrees of the new government was a decree on the separation of church and state. Bearing in mind the organization of the world proletarian revolution, the transition to the European Gregorian calendar also became necessary. Already in November 1917, this issue was raised for discussion by the Council of People's Commissars, which adopted on January 24, 1918 the “Decree on the introduction of the Western European calendar in the Russian Republic.” The decree stated: “In order to establish in Russia the same calculation of time as almost all cultural peoples, the Council of People's Commissars decides to introduce a new calendar into civil use after the month of January of this year. To do this, the first day after January 31 of this year should be considered not February 1, but February 14, the second day should be February 15, etc.” Note that the new calendar was not called “Gregorian” in the decree, so as not to raise the question that it was introduced by the Pope. Let us conclude this topic with the words of the American astronomer G. Moyer: “The Gregorian calendar represented a very satisfactory compromise between the necessary accuracy and extremely desirable simplicity.”
