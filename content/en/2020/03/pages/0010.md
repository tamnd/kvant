Gregory XIII from it: “It was our concern not only to restore the equinox to its anciently appointed place, from which since the Council of Nicaea it had retreated for approximately ten days, and to return the full moon to its place, but also to establish a method and rule by which it would be achieved so that in the future the equinox and the full moon would never move from their places... And therefore we prescribe and command regarding the month of October of the current 1582, so that ten days from the third day before the Nones (October 5) to the eve of the Ides (October 14) inclusive were withdrawn.” In addition, the 19-year cycle of changes in lunar phases was put in order so that Easter could be calculated in advance. At the same time, the transition to the modern counting of days from the first to the last day of the month began to occur. The new calendar system was called Gregorian, or new style.

But already in the 18th century the difference was already 11 days, since 1700 was a leap year in the Julian calendar, but in the new calendar it was not a leap year (17 is not divisible by 4 without a remainder). For the same reason, in the 19th century the difference between the old style and the new was 12 days, and in the 20th century - 13 days. In our 21st century, the difference is still 13 days, since 2000 was a leap year in both calendars, but in the 22nd century the difference will increase to 14 days. The Gregorian calendar is noticeably more accurate than the Julian calendar. Its average annual error is only 30 seconds. If according to the Julian calendar a shift of the vernal equinox by 1 day occurs in 128 years, then according to the Gregorian calendar such a shift will occur in 2800 years!
