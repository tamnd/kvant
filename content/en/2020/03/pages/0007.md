For example, after February 23, an additional month of 22 or 23 days was inserted, and after it expired, the February count of days until the March calendars continued again. This month was called marcedonia from the Latin “reckoning”, since all monetary payments for the past year were made in this month. The Nones and Ides in Marcedonia were like a short month, and the Kalends were completely absent. This order lasted for many hundreds of years. But at the beginning of the second century BC, the Roman priests who controlled the calendar began to manipulate the duration and timing of the insertion of this extra month. In the Roman Republic, the entire range of administrative positions - consuls (the highest position), quaestors, censors, etc. – had an elected term of exactly one year. And since these positions brought a certain income and other benefits in life, extending their terms was a profitable business. By manipulating the calendar, the priests could increase these deadlines in favor of one or another official, probably not selfishly. There could also be economic reasons for changing the time of insertion into the calendar of the month of reckoning. The population of the republic was informed about the specific upcoming calendar by the priests at the end of February. About this confusing ancient Roman calendar, Voltaire said many years later: “The Roman generals always won, but they never knew on what day it happened.”

Julian calendar It was established in 46 BC by decree of the Roman dictator and high priest, commander and statesman Gaius Julius Caesar (100–44 BC). Julius Caesar reformed the calendar, primarily relying on his rights as the high priest. He took the Egyptian (Alexandrian) solar calendar as a basis. Gaius Julius Caesar corresponded to a solar year of 365.25 days. The extra day every four years was not February 29, as we are used to, but an intercalary day between February 24 and 25, or according to the Roman calendar - between the sixth and fifth day before March 1. It received the official name “twice sixth before the Kalends of March” - bis sectum Kal.Mart. This very bis sectum turned into the word leap year for us, and the corresponding years subsequently began to be called leap years. The beginning of the year was moved by Caesar from March 1 to January 1. That's actually the whole reform. Its clarity and simplicity so delighted the Romans, exhausted by their calendar, that in gratitude (including for military services), the Roman Senate renamed the month of Quintilis to Julius (Caesar was born in this month). A year later, on the Ides of March 44 BC, Caesar was killed by conspirators led by Brutus. A power struggle began between the commanders Antony and Octavian. The priests took advantage of the confusion in power and for some time continued to “command” the calendar at their discretion, changing the order of leap years and inserting an extra day.
