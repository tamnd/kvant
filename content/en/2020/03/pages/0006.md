These were the ancient Georgian and ancient Armenian calendars. Nowadays, the Alexandrian calendar is used only by Copts - direct descendants of the ancient Egyptians who adopted Christianity in 284. Copts are a significant part of the modern Egyptian population (about 10%). In Egypt and its capital they live compactly, forming enclaves within the country, preserving the language and their ancient customs as they were in the third century AD. Interestingly, the Alexandrian-type solar calendar existed in France during the French Revolution, while France was a republic (1789–1799), and during the brief period of the Paris Commune (March 18–May 28, 1871). The names of the months of this calendar fully reflected seasonal changes in the weather and in agricultural work, for example: Brumaire - the month of fog, Thermidor - the month of heat, Germinal - the month of sowing, wheat germination, Vendémière - the month of grape harvest. Each month was divided into 3 decades, the days of which had names consisting of Latin words: primidi - the first day, duodi - the second day, etc. A very slender and attractive calendar system! The extra days had romantic names - the holiday of Genius, the holiday of Feat, etc. Once every 4 years, one extra day was dedicated to sports games and competitions. It turns out that 100 years before the advent of the modern Olympic movement in France, they remembered the Olympic Games of Ancient Greece, which took place every 4 years. It is no coincidence that the initiator of the organization of modern Olympiads was the Frenchman, Pierre de Coubertin.

Calendar of Ancient Rome

⟦column⟧

The calendar of the Roman Republic (509–27 BC) was solar, but extremely confusing. The Romans were very superstitious and did not like even numbers. Seven months had 29 days, four months had 31 days, and February had 28 days. This month was named after Februus, the Etruscan god of the underworld and the Roman god of purification. This month was a memorial week. Other months were named either after the gods (Janus, Mars, Maia, Juno) or by numbers, starting with the fifth (quintilis, sextilis, september, october, november, december). The exception is Aprilis (opening, warmed by the Sun). Quintilis (July) was the fifth month, since the year began in March. The names of days in the Roman calendar were very complicated. There were no weekly cycles. Each month had three special days. All the first days of the months were called kalends, hence the word “calendar”. The seventh day in the long months (31 days each) and the fifth in the remaining months were called nones. And the 15th in the long months and the 13th in the rest were called the Ides. The days before these dates were eves (hence our Russian “on the eve”). And the remaining days were called in a very strange way - inclusive counting. For example, August 4 (a short month in which the nones fell on the 5th) was called the eve of the August nones, August 11 was the third day before the Ides of August (falling on August 13), and August 23 was the eighth day before the September calendars. It is interesting that there were no second days before the nons, ids and kalends; they were called eves. Well, the first days (inclusive) were these very Nones, Ides and Kalends. The annual count of days of the ancient Roman calendar gives 355 days. The 10.25 days missing before the solar year required the inclusion of additional days in the calendar. And this event was confusing to the extreme.
