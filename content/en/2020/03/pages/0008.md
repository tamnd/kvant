The Julian calendar of the Senate granted lifelong "empires" (emergency rights that were previously given to the commander for a short period of military action). This meant the actual transformation of the republic into an empire. The Senate awarded Octavian the title of emperor and the name Augustus (“multiplying”). Augustus made the Julian calendar the state calendar, mandatory throughout the vast territory of the Roman Empire from January 1, 4 AD. The month of septiles was renamed August and the alternation of long and short months was corrected - it became as it is now. The Julian calendar existed throughout Europe (and Byzantium) until the end of the 17th century. And now only the Orthodox (Orthodox) Christian Church lives on it.

The need to change the Julian calendar So why was it necessary to replace the Julian calendar? The reason for this is purely arithmetic. The Julian calendar is based on the fact that the period of the solar cycle, the so-called calendar year, is 365.25 days. According to the generally accepted Julian calendar, it seemed that the small difference between these periods was insignificant and did not interfere with the calendar. As is easy to determine, it leads to a shift of the calendar by one day in 128 years. When the power of the Roman Empire gradually disappeared and then, in the “dark centuries” of the early Middle Ages, few people were interested in this shift. But in the 16th century, during the era of the “autumn of the Middle Ages,” which is more often called the Renaissance, human life and social consciousness changed so much that many public figures and scientists began to express concern about the inaccuracy of the calendar. In the Christian European world, the documentary beginning is considered to be the fourth century AD, when by decree of the Roman Emperor Constantine, Christianity became the state religion. Over the 12 centuries that have passed since then, the Julian calendar has shifted by more than 9 days. One of the causes of concern was the shift of the vernal equinox from March 21 to March 12. And this day was associated with the beginning of many agricultural works, and the preparation time for them was significantly reduced. According to the calendar, spring came earlier and earlier. But there was another reason for concern. It had a religious justification. In the Christian communities of the Roman Empire, by the beginning of the 4th century, the custom had been established to celebrate the legendary date of the resurrection of Christ as the brightest holiday. The events associated with the execution of Christ took place in Jerusalem, the capital of the Roman province of Judea, on days that were an important Jewish holiday called “Passover.” Since the 12th century BC, this holiday has been celebrated in the Jewish religion to commemorate the successful exodus of the Jews from Egypt, where they were considered an inferior race. At the beginning of our era (as, indeed, now) the lunar-solar calendar continued to operate in Judea, according to which the spring month of Nisan moves relative to the natural calendar, for example, relative to the day of the spring equivalence.
