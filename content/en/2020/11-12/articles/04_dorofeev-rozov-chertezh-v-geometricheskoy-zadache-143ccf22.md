Drawing in a geometric problem

G.DOROFEEV, N.ROZOV

Applicants look at the ROLE OF DRAWINGS IN SOLVING a geometric problem differently. Some people think that the drawing is not needed at all, and they carry it out in a deliberately careless manner. Others, on the contrary, consider the drawing itself to be a sufficient argument in reasoning and do not even find it necessary to substantiate in any way what is “visible from the drawing.” Both of these extreme views are wrong. Of course, no drawing, even the most accurate one, can replace a logical proof, but is only an illustration of reasoning. Any geometric fact that we “saw” in the drawing must be strictly substantiated - only then can we assert that this fact actually takes place and is not obtained from the correct (or, what is much more dangerous, incorrect) figure. At the same time, a visual drawing is a good assistant in solving a problem: it can suggest the idea of ​​necessary reasoning and calculations, give rise to the idea of ​​using a certain theorem, or come up with a successful additional construction. It is not for nothing that mathematicians often resort to geometric illustrations to make the ideas of a proof more understandable. However, only a drawing that correctly reflects the essential geometric features of the configuration discussed in the condition can help solve the problem. That is why the drawing should be treated very carefully. Often applicants limit themselves to the first more or less successfully completed figure, without being interested in how accurately the completed drawing meets the conditions of the task.

Meanwhile, in many problems, it is in principle impossible to carry out a complete solution from one drawing, since the condition of the problem allows the existence of geometrically different configurations. In addition, such attachment to one “random” drawing leads to another problem: in the course of solving the problem, a contradiction may be discovered between the resulting results and the original drawing, which usually confuses those entering. However, with a correct understanding of the role of the drawing, there is nothing wrong with this - you should simply abandon the original image and make a new drawing that corresponds to the geometric information that has appeared (of course, provided that the reasoning and calculations carried out are correct). When constructing a drawing, it can be useful not to make an approximate sketch, which gives only a general idea of ​​the geometric configuration, but to strive to consistently construct the drawing, based on the given tasks and general geometric facts.
