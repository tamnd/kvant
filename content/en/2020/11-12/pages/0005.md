In the USSR, even before the Second World War, the Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute (LPTI), and a group of employees led by A.P. Alexandrov, began protecting ships from magnetic mines. In fact, from the very beginning they chose the third direction: instead of solving the problem of complete compensation of the magnetic field, they set the task of significantly reducing the ship’s magnetic field. To what level? The average value of the induction of the Earth's magnetic field over its entire surface is about 5 $10^{5}$ tesla, at the magnetic poles it is 6.6 $10^{5}$ tesla, and at the magnetic equator 3.4 $10^{5}$ tesla. The natural fluctuation of the Earth's magnetic field reaches a level of 0.1–0.2 $10^{5}$ T. The sensitivity of the mine should not be higher than this value, otherwise it will explode without any ships. But this means that when compensating for the ship’s magnetic field, a demagnetization accuracy of 0.1 $10^{5}$ T is quite sufficient for protection against magnetic mines. Before compensation began, a map of the magnetic field created by the ship was measured. Two schemes for incomplete compensation have been proposed: stationary and non-stationary. According to the first scheme (stationary), several windings were placed on the ship, through which direct current was passed. In this case, the magnetic field from the windings was approximately equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the magnetic field of the ship. According to the second scheme (non-stationary), the ship was dynamically demagnetized. The windings through which alternating current was passed were placed either on the ship itself or next to the ship (on a special ship) and were removed after demagnetization. During dynamic demagnetization, the amplitude of the magnetic field gradually decreased from the saturation field of the ferromagnet to zero. This complete demagnetization effect was temporary, and over time the magnetization reappeared. But by repeating the demagnetization procedure every few months, it was possible to maintain the ship’s magnetic field at a low level. In this way it was possible to reduce the ship's magnetic field by more than ten times. This saved many ships and submarines. The created “LPTI system” was implemented on about a hundred ships and actually protected them from German magnetic mines. An interesting feature of this example of a duel between a “virus” and a “mask” concerns the characters involved in this confrontation. Along with A.P. Aleksandrov, I.V. Kurchatov was actively involved in the demagnetization of ships (“mask”) in the USSR. And the development and improvement of magnetic mines (“virus”) in Germany is one of the leading physicists W. Gerlach. Subsequently, Kurchatov became the head of the Soviet atomic program, and Gerlach headed the German “Uranium Project”. So their duel continued not only in the mine, but also in the atomic direction. It would seem that the need to demagnetize ships to protect against magnetic mines is very old, almost forgotten
