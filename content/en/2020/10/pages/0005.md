Diamond is a rare crystal, large samples of which can only be created by nature at extreme temperatures and pressures. And yet, chemically, diamond is the simplest modification of carbon (Fig.2): in it, each atom has four neighbors, with which it forms very strong chemical bonds, called covalent. The tendency of a carbon atom to have four neighbors is the result of fairly simple laws of chemistry. Indeed, the carbon atom, the sixth element of the periodic table, has six electrons. In a simplified model, electrons are distributed around the nucleus into shells. The two electrons closest to the nucleus form a “closed shell”: they are chemically inert. The remaining four electrons are in the second shell, more distant from the nucleus. The “octet rule” (or, for the lightest elements, the “duet rule”), coined by ancient chemists, is a law that modern scientists explain using quantum mechanics. To comply with it, atoms are willing to gain or lose electrons, forming ions, or share electrons with other atoms, creating covalent bonds. In diamond, each of the four outer shell electrons combines with an outer shell electron of another atom to form four C–C bonds, and thus the octet rule is satisfied. So, each atom in a diamond has four neighbors. The resulting structure is incredibly strong, making diamond a very hard substance. As we have already mentioned, diamond is a rare modification of carbon. By a quirk of chemistry, carbon atoms are more likely to form a two-dimensional structure in which each one bonds with only three others. This is a two-dimensional crystal - graphene (Fig.3). The remaining unused fourth electron of the outer shell (one per atom) is ready to participate in the formation of a weak bond that connects one layer of graphene to another, another to a third, etc. This packing results in the formation of graphite, the most common form of crystalline carbon (Fig.4).
