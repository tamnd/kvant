In our example, the spectrum of the matrix consists of the numbers 5, 1, 1; Please note that not only negative numbers appear in it, but also multiples, i.e. Several (linearly independent!) eigenvectors can correspond to the same eigenvalue. To conclude our brief excursion into linear algebra, let’s say, as we have already promised, a few words about the theory of dimension. Vectors $X^{1}$, … $, X^{M}$ of dimension N are said to be linearly independent if there is no nontrivial (i.e. one in which at least one of $D_{i}$ is different from 0) linear combination $D_{1} X^{1}$ … $D_{M} X^{M}$ equal to the zero vector. The number of linearly independent vectors M in such a system does not exceed the dimension N, and in the case of M N the system of linearly independent vectors is called a basis. Bases can also be characterized in opposite (or, as mathematicians often say, dual) terms. Namely, a system of $X^{1}$, … $, X^{N}$ N-dimensional vectors is a basis if and only if any other N-dimensional vector Y can be expressed as a linear combination $D_{1} X^{1}$ … $D_{N} X^{N}$.
