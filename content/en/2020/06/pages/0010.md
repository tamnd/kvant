5. Andreev's theorem

In Lobachevsky space, the equal sign in (6) is replaced by the “less than” sign. It is not difficult to find all Coxeter polyhedra in Lobachevsky space, which are triangular pyramids. However, in this case they constitute only an insignificant part of all Coxeter polyhedra. Just as on the Lobachevsky plane there are Coxeter polygons with an arbitrarily large (in fact, any) number of sides, in Lobachevsky space there are Coxeter polyhedra with an arbitrarily large number of faces. But unlike polygons, they can have a very complex combinatorial structure. Therefore, it is not easy to obtain a complete description of them. To the extent that this seems possible at all, this was done by E.M. Andreev in 1970. He proved a general theorem that applies not only to Coxeter polyhedra, but also to all convex polyhedra whose dihedral angles do not exceed S 2. Such polyhedra are called acute-angled (although they can also have right dihedral angles). As we proved in Section 4 (the proof does not change in the case of Lobachevsky space), every acute-angled polyhedron is simple. Andreev's theorem provides necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence in Lobachevsky space of an acute-angled polyhedron of a given combinatorial structure, different from a triangular pyramid, with given dihedral angles. These conditions are as follows:

⟦column⟧

1) if any three faces converge at one vertex, then the sum of the angles between them is greater than S (the necessity of this condition was proved in Section 4); 2) if any three faces are pairwise adjacent, but do not converge at one vertex, then the sum of the angles between them is less than S; 3) if any four faces are adjacent “in a circle” (like the lateral faces of a quadrangular prism), then not all angles between them are equal to S 2; 4) if the polyhedron is a triangular prism, then not all angles formed by the bases with the lateral faces are equal to S 2. Andreev’s theorem is in some sense similar to the famous theorem of A.D. Aleksandrov on the existence of a Euclidean polyhedron with a given development. However, there is no (and cannot be) its exact analogue in Euclidean geometry. This is one of the specific theorems of Lobachevsky geometry, similar to the criterion for the equality of triangles at three angles. Using Andreev’s theorem, it is not difficult, for example, to prove the existence in Lobachevsky space of “rectangular” polyhedra (that is, polyhedra all of whose dihedral angles are right) with an arbitrarily large number of faces. (The reader can easily do this on his own.) Thus, we get many different rectangular kaleidoscopes in Lobachevsky space. Without being able to discuss this in more detail, we will only note that since the end of the last century, kaleidoscopes in Lobachevsky space have been applied to the arithmetic of quadratic forms, and in the last 15 years they have been used in three-dimensional topology.
