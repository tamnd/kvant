KALEIDOSCOPES

Kaleidoscopes in Lobachevsky space have been the subject of intensive research since 1965 in connection with certain problems in group theory. Their complete description in any dimension is still far from complete. There is an amazing result (belonging to the author of the article) that at n t 30 there are no kaleidoscopes at all in the n-dimensional Lobachevsky space. Examples of such kaleidoscopes are known only for n d 8. In addition to the already mentioned applications of kaleidoscopes in geometry (regular polyhedra), the theory of functions of a complex variable and group theory, there are no less important applications in number theory, the theory of Lie algebras, algebraic geometry and other branches of mathematics. It should be said, however, that the term “kaleidoscope” is not used in serious mathematical literature. Instead they say "a discrete group generated by reflections." In the article we do not have the opportunity to talk about applications of kaleidoscopes (with the exception of the connection between spherical kaleidoscopes and regular polyhedra). However, the study of kaleidoscopes in themselves constitutes a bright page of geometry. More complete information about kaleidoscopes can be found in the review article [2].

If the angle D is an integral part of S, as, for example, at figure 1,b, then this does not happen. Since the images of each point do not extend beyond the plane perpendicular to the common axis of our mirrors (it is precisely this plane that is depicted in figure 1), the phenomenon we discovered is essentially two-dimensional. We can talk about the mirror reflection of plane figures relative to straight lines, and figure 1 demonstrates that images obtained by multiple reflections relative to the sides of an angle do not overlap if and only if the value D of this angle is an integer part of S. More precisely, if D S k, where k t 2 is a natural number, then the entire plane turns out to be divided into 2k equal angles with a common vertex, in each of which one image of the interior of the original angle is obtained. In half of these corners the images are mirrored, in the other half, which includes the original corner itself, they are not reversed. Let us now imagine a convex polygon formed by mirrors. In what case will the images obtained by multiple reflections of the interior of a polygon relative to its sides not overlap? From the previous it follows that for this it is necessary that all the angles of the polygon be integral parts of S. It can be shown that this condition is also sufficient. If it is fulfilled, then the entire plane turns out to be divided into polygons equal to the original one, in such a way that any two polygons that have a common side are symmetrical with respect to this side. In each of the polygons of this partition, one image of the interior of the original polygon is obtained. figure 2 shows the partition of the plane obtained from an equilateral triangle. It is this division that we see when looking through an ordinary kaleidoscope.
