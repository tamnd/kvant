This modification, pelletron, produces up to 0.5 mA. Historically it was used for physical experiments, for example to power linear accelerators, now - probably only for educational purposes. The generator contains a dielectric ring strip stretched between two rollers and positioned vertically - something like a vertical escalator. At the lower end there is a moderately high voltage source that ionizes the air. The ions stick to the tape and are carried upward by it. At the top, the charge is removed from the tape and transferred to a large diameter conducting ball.

Question 7. How is the maximum voltage of the Van de Graaff generator limited, how is energy accumulated in it, and what happens to the ions delivered to the top by the tape?

There are several other devices in which high voltage is created by the work of moving charges. Here are three examples, two of which you are likely familiar with. The first is the Kelvin dropper. Try to guess how it works by looking at figure 7. The device is primitive - two metal cans below, two metal rings above, two wires and a pipe with a fork from which it drips.

It turns out that the fundamental point here is random charge asymmetry. For example, one can was moved across the table, the other was not, and the first one became electrified. What happens next? For example, the left one was charged with a minus. Then the right ring is also charged with a minus, the right end of the pipe with a plus, the drops from it and the right jar with a plus, and the further, the more. But the question is: what is the limit of tension? Close relatives of this charming device in terms of operating principle are the electrophore machine and its predecessor the Goltz machine. Note that when discussing the operation of electrophore machines, disputes often begin as to whether electrification by friction is important for their operation. From sources on the Internet we can conclude that electrification by friction will make the process of charge accumulation faster. And in older designs it was actually used. In later designs, designed not to achieve the highest stresses, but intended to demonstrate the effect, they began to do without this. Electrification by friction was seriously studied by M.I. Kornfeld (publications in the journal “Solid State Physics”), and a popular presentation is in the journal «Kvant» (No. 6, 1985).

Finally, we note that living organisms have sources of electricity, for example, some fish. People don’t seem to use such sources, but fish do. They use them for location and as weapons. And there is another source of electricity, and we ourselves live inside this source - this is the atmosphere. In this case, we can point out some of the processes that make such a battery work. Charges are transferred in the atmosphere by aerosols - water droplets, ice crystals. And they are forced to move by air currents and gravity.
