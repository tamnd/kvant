2. The resistor begins to melt (10 points) When current is passed through a wire-wound resistor, it heats up, and its temperature varies nonlinearly depending on the flowing current. At low currents, which for typical resistors are on the order of 10 mA, the temperature of the resistor T is practically indistinguishable from the ambient temperature $T_{0}$. When the current increases to a certain limiting value $I_{max}$, the resistor very quickly (almost instantly) heats up to a very high temperature and melts (burns out). Let the resistance of a certain resistor at room temperature and a current of 1 mA be equal to 10 Ohms. It is known that the value of the limiting current for this resistor is $I_{max} = 1,35$ A. Find the resistance of the resistor and the voltage across it at a current $0,8I_{max}$. We can assume that the resistance of the resistor depends linearly on temperature: $R ( T ) = R_{0} ( 1 + \alpha ( T - T_{0} ) )$, where $R_{0}$ is the resistance of the resistor at temperature $T_{0}, \alpha$ is an unknown coefficient. The heat transfer power is proportional to the temperature difference between the resistor and the environment. Changing the resistance of the resistor does not affect the amount of current in the circuit. P. Kryukov
