PROBLEM "QUANTUM"

Problems in mathematics and physics

We have been running this section from issue to issue since the founding of the magazine. The problems published in it are non-standard, but their solution does not require knowledge beyond the scope of the school curriculum. The most difficult tasks are marked with an asterisk. After formulating the task, we usually indicate who proposed it to us. Of course, not all of these problems are published for the first time. Solutions to problems in mathematics and physics from this issue should be sent to the email addresses: math@kvant.ras.ru and phys@kvant.ras.ru, respectively, or to the postal address: 119296 Moscow, Leninsky Prospekt, 64-A, «Kvant». Send the conditions of each original problem proposed for publication, along with your solution to this problem, to the same addresses. Problem M2598 – M2601 were offered at the spring round of the XLI Tournament of Cities. Problem F2605–F2608 were offered at the final round of the Engineering Olympiad for schoolchildren in the 2019/20 academic year. The author of the problems is S. Muravyov.

Problem M2598–M2601, F2605–F2608

M2598. Can a tetrahedron be cut by two different planes into two squares: one with a side of no more than 1, and the other with a side of no less than 100? M. Evdokimov

M2599. 2N guests came to Ivan’s birthday party. Ivan has N black and N white cylinders. He wants to organize a ball: put top hats on the guests and line them up in round dances (one or several) so that in each round dance there are at least two people and people in top hats of the same color do not stand next to each other in the round dance. Prove that Ivan can throw a ball exactly (2N)! in various ways. (Cylinders of the same color are indistinguishable; all guests are distinguishable.) G. Pogudin

M2600. Given a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD. Circles with diameters AB and CD intersect at two points X 1 and Y 1. Circles with diameters BC and AD intersect at two points X 2 and Y 2. Circles with diameters AC and BD intersect at two points $X_{3}$ and $Y_{3}$. Prove that the lines $X 1 Y 1, X 2 Y 2, X_{3} Y_{3}$ intersect at one point. M. Didin

M2601. Gleb thought of natural numbers N and a, where a < N. He wrote the number a on the board. Then Gleb began to perform the following operation: divide N with a remainder by the last number written on the board and write the resulting remainder from the division also on the board. When the number 0 appeared on the board, he stopped. Could Gleb initially choose N and a such that the sum of the numbers written on the board would be greater than 100N? I. Mitrofanov

M2587. In each cell of a strip of length 100 there is a chip. For 1 ruble you can swap the places of any two adjacent chips, and you can also swap for free any two chips between which there are a) exactly three chips; b) exactly four chips. For what is the least amount of rubles you can rearrange the chips in reverse order?

Answer: a) for 50 rubles; b) for 61 rubles. Let's number the cells in order from 1 to 100, and the current number of the chip will be the number of the cell on which it stands. a) Evaluation. Let the chips be rearranged in reverse order. Then each chip had to change the parity of its number. But note that a free operation does not change the parity of the chip numbers, but a paid operation changes it for two chips. Therefore, you will need to do at least 100 2 = 50 operations. Example. Let us first note that the free operation does not change the remainder of the chip number when divided by 4 and, on the contrary, you can freely rearrange 25 chips in any order, the numbers of which give the same remainder when divided by 4. We will perform 49 exchanges: 2–3, 4–5, 6–7, 8–9, …, 98–99. Then, using free operations, we move chip 1 to cell 5, and chip 100 to cell 4, after which we swap them for 1 ruble. Now each chip occupies the cell with the number of the desired remainder when divided by 4, in order to rearrange them in the reverse order to the original one using free operations. b) At this point, the free operation does not change the remainder of the cell number when divided by 5. On the contrary, you can freely rearrange in any order 20 chips, the numbers of which give the same remainder when divided by 5. The following model is convenient for the solution: we arrange the chips in 5 piles of 20 chips each, in pile number 0 - chips with a remainder of 0 when divided by 5, in pile number 1 - chips with a remainder 1 when divided by 5
