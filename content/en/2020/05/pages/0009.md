Since E. Wigner and H.B. Huntington proposed in 1935 that at sufficiently high pressures molecular hydrogen should become atomic metallic, and in 1968 Neil Ashcroft predicted that solid metallic hydrogen at extremely high pressures should become superconducting at approximately room temperature, Attempts to test these predictions experimentally continue. Although a group of Harvard scientists in 2017 announced the observation of hydrogen metallization at 495 GPa, and a group of French scientists in 2019 - at 425 GPa, there is no indisputable experimental confirmation of the metallization and superconductivity of hydrogen at room temperature, and modern theoretical calculations predict increasingly higher transition pressures. An alternative route to room-temperature superconductivity is based on the idea of ​​“saturating” chemical compounds with hydrogen atoms. According to the theory, during chemical synthesis under pressure in hydride systems (i.e., in compounds containing hydrogen), it is possible to achieve such small distances between hydrogen atoms that would facilitate the dissociation of $H_{2}$ molecules and the formation of a metal “sublattice” of hydrogen atoms in the hydride structure. Recently published results from diamond anvil experiments have shown the ability to synthesize hydrogen-rich metal hydrides, which at very high pressures are said to become superconducting at temperatures up to 260 K (13 q C), almost as if in the freezer compartment of a refrigerator. This particular 2019 record is for high-pressure lanthanum hydride $( LaH_{10} ),$; the previous one was set by sulfur trihydride $( H_{3} S ),$, which showed superconductivity under high pressure at temperatures up to 203 K (70 q C). An important direction of modern scientific research is the creation of materials with high energy density, capable of rapid energy release under certain conditions under the influence of external influences. Among these materials, some of the most promising are polynitrides—nitrogen compounds in which several nitrogen atoms are linked by simple single bonds. The energy stored in them is estimated to be several times greater than that released by the most effective explosives currently known. The work of our group in Bayreuth showed the fundamental possibility of synthesizing polynitrides by direct chemical reaction of nitrogen with metals at high pressures and temperatures and preserving them under normal conditions. If we could produce them in significant quantities, it would revolutionize propulsion technology, such as the aerospace industry. However, so far they are synthesized only in diamond anvils. Despite the still distant prospects for application, this stage of work is extremely necessary to understand the processes of formation, structure and composition of new materials in detail, which in the future can help in solving issues of their potential industrial synthesis. Polynitrides are also interesting due to their unique and unpredictable chemistry and crystallography.
