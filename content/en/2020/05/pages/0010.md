Cavities and unfilled spaces “collapse” under pressure, which means one might intuitively assume that porous crystalline structures cannot form during chemical synthesis at high pressures. Imagine our surprise when, while studying the chemical reactions between nitrogen and transition metals (Hf, W, Os) at high pressures and temperatures, we discovered that at a gigantic pressure of 1,000,000 atmospheres (which corresponds to pressure at a depth of 2,500 km inside the Earth) they form compounds with a porous structure (Fig.7). We called them “myths,” but not because it is difficult to believe in their existence under such pressures, but because they are metal-inorganic frameworks (MIF). Formed by metal and nitrogen atoms, these three-dimensional frameworks are permeated with channels populated by nitrogen molecules $N_{2}$. Thus, for rhenium nitride $ReN_{2}$, initially synthesized at very high pressures, we were able not only to significantly reduce the synthesis pressure (which naturally increased the amount of the resulting substance), but also to preserve and study it under room conditions. As a result, we discovered in this new material a unique combination of unusual physical properties - exceptionally low compressibility, superhardness and metallic conductivity. These properties were previously considered incompatible. The above examples clearly show how powerful a thermodynamic variable pressure is, which allows you to significantly change the structure, chemical bond and physical properties of a substance. The higher the pressure, the more complex the electronic interactions, the more diverse the materials that can be synthesized, and the more unusual their properties. The limit of the capabilities of a classical diamond anvil cell is pressures of the order of 300–400 GPa, corresponding to the pressures at the center of the Earth.
