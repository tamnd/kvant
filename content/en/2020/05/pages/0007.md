It’s true, but it’s a fact that a significant contribution to the development of high-pressure physics was made by... resolution 181 (II) of the United Nations General Assembly on Palestine in 1947 and smugglers. Jewelry smuggling has always existed, but particularly large quantities of diamonds found their way into America following Britain's decision to withdraw troops from Palestine in 1948. Before leaving the danger zone, many converted their wealth into diamonds and tried to smuggle them in. Millions of dollars worth of gemstones were seized by US customs in the 1950s, but the government refused to release them into the carefully regulated commercial market. Instead, they were turned over to scientists at the government's National Bureau of Standards. Thus, researchers Charles Weir, Alvin Van Valkenburg, and engineer Elmer Bunting who worked with them gained access to a virtually unlimited supply of diamonds. It was they who came up with the idea of ​​a high-pressure cell with opposing diamond anvils, which not only created pressure, but also made it possible to optically observe the compressed sample through transparent diamond “windows.” It would seem that the idea was on the surface. By that time, a device with opposing conical anvils (Fig.1,в), made of a hard material, tungsten carbide, allowed its inventor Percy W. Bridgman, winner of the 1946 Nobel Prize in Physics, to study materials under pressures of 100 thousand atmospheres. Replace the carbide anvils with diamond ones and you're done! However, no. The path to this design was not direct. Initially, the prototype for creating a transparent miniature high-pressure cell was a device with a piston cylinder. The material chosen was a magnificent gemstone – the purest emerald-cut diamond crystal of 7.5 carats (1 carat = 0.2 g) (Fig.6)

In such a cell, the diamond played only the passive role of a strong and transparent cylindrical chamber in which a miniature piston made of rigid wire moved. Diamond is the hardest material known and it took four full months of drilling to make the tiny hole, 0.4mm in diameter and 6.3mm long. The result was the world's most unusual high-pressure chamber. Alas, not for long. Expanded from the inside by the high pressure created by the metal piston, the diamond simply shattered into pieces. The problem was not only the insufficient strength of the piston - the creators of the “drilled cell” did not take into account that diamond, being extremely strong in compression, cannot withstand high tensile loads. It took a lot of time and... diamonds until, after crushing a fair amount of them, the researchers agreed on one elegant solution.
