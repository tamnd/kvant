But by allowing us to consider infinite “stalks of bamboo” (on which, nevertheless, the game is always completed in a finite time), we can also obtain positions that can be associated with any real numbers. Moreover, it turns out that “within game theory” it is possible to select games that can be declared as “numbers”. And it is natural to define operations on them - addition, multiplication, and so on. However, among such numbers there will be not only all real numbers, but also many others. Look at the three right pictures on figure 9. The second of these three games, the infinite blue “bamboo stalk”, will correspond to the “infinite number” - the ordinal Z. And the infinite stalk, in which the blue edge is only the lower one, will correspond to its inverse “number” 1 Z. And all these are the surreal numbers invented by Conway - which I, however, did not define here. You can read about this in the book by Pierre Dehornoy [3]; and the article [4] in «Kvant» already in 1979 was devoted to surreal numbers (without any connection with game theory). To complete the story about games, we can mention two books by Conway - “On Numbers and Games” and “Winning Ways for your Mathematical Plays”, co-authored with Berlekamp and Guy. Both became bestsellers immediately after their release.

Conway and Shipman [5], where various proofs of the irrationality of the root of 2 (and other square roots) are collected. This is a really interesting read: how the same result can be obtained by completely different methods. Of course, it also mentions the classical proof, in which it turns out that both the numerator and the denominator must be even, but not only that. So, Tannenbaum's proof appears there. Namely, if 2 were rational, then we could take the smallest pair of integers p, q such that the area of ​​a square with side p is equal to the sum of the areas of two squares with side q. But placing two such squares inside a square with side p (Fig.10, left), we see that in this case the area covered twice (which is a square with side 2q p ) is equal to the uncovered area (which is two squares with side p q each). And we get a contradiction with the minimality of the pair p, q.
