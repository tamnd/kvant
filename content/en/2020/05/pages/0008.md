The discovery of diamondiferous bedrock (kimberlites) in South Africa in 1869 allowed scientists to evaluate the thermodynamic conditions for the formation of diamonds in the Earth's interior and understand, although not immediately, the decisive role of pressure for their synthesis. The first “man-made” diamonds were obtained on December 16, 1954 by a team of the American company General Electric in the Belt-apparatus (from the English belt) (Fig.1,g). And in 1959, after the publication of the “recipe” in the journal Nature, anyone with a press to create pressures of 55 thousand atmospheres and temperatures of 1500 q C could become a diamond producer. But that's another story. What is interesting for our story is that scientists first relied on the Earth to understand the origin of diamonds, and today they rely on diamonds to understand the origin and structure of the Earth itself. Modern ideas about the phase composition of the mantle and core of the Earth are based on the results of experiments in diamond anvils. Only they make it possible to create the necessary static pressures and temperatures in the laboratory (up to 4 million atm. and above 5000 q C, as in the Earth’s core) and study what happens to materials under such extreme conditions. Samples in cells are heated by lasers, and the physical and chemical changes that occur in the materials are studied using X-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, Mössbauer and infrared spectroscopy, as well as other modern methods in laboratories and synchrotron radiation sources. The range of research, of course, is not limited to problems of earth sciences and planetary science. What other questions concern scientists currently? a very wide range of fundamental and applied problems in physics, chemistry and materials science. Let us give just a few examples concerning three particularly interesting and important modern areas - testing theories in solid state physics, synthesizing new materials, and studying the properties of solids under conditions of extreme pressures and temperatures. This is important not only for deepening our understanding of the nature of things, but also for finding opportunities for targeted synthesis of materials with specified unique properties. The search for alternative energy sources and new technologies that make it possible to use energy as efficiently as possible and with minimal losses is the focus of modern materials science. Superconductivity, i.e. The ability of some materials to conduct electric current without any resistance is one of the most exciting physical phenomena in solid state physics since its first experimental discovery in 1911. At atmospheric pressure, the record for maximum critical temperature is held by cuprates, which exhibit superconductivity at temperatures up to 138 K (135 q C). However, such temperatures are still too low and the use of superconductors at technologically significant temperatures close to room temperature is still not possible. Although some researchers question whether superconductivity at room temperature is even achievable, it has been repeatedly discovered at temperatures that were previously unexpected or thought impossible (as is the case with high-temperature superconductors operating at temperatures above liquid nitrogen's 77 K). The discovery of such a superconductor would be of enormous technological significance. Among other things, it would help solve the world's energy problems, create even faster computers, new storage devices and ultra-sensitive sensors.
