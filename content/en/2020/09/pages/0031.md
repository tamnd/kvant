SCHOOL AT KVANT

Equal size from Proizvolov

A. BLINKOV

VYACHESLAV VIKTOROVICH PROZVOLOV was an outstanding composer of mathematical problems for schoolchildren. Many of them were included in his wonderful book “Growth Challenges,” last published in 2003, but not all. Now a new, expanded edition of this book is being prepared at ICSME. Most of V.V. Proizvolov’s problems are distinguished by memorable facts in conditions, the ability to present a seemingly well-known idea in a new way, brevity and elegance of solutions. Being a real mathematician, having “caught” an idea, he tried to develop it, so his problems are easy to combine into series, from which he can create circle classes for schoolchildren, which is what many teachers do. One of the series of Proizvolov’s problems has already been presented in the article “Angle in a square” («Kvant» No. 4, 2014). And for this article, problems related to areas have been selected. In them you can trace several of Vyacheslav Viktorovich’s favorite ideas and techniques. We emphasize that many of the proposed problems can be solved in different ways, but we will discuss specifically the author’s solutions, which do not require cumbersome calculations and at the same time, as a rule, contain deep ideas. Let's start with a relatively simple problem, the idea of ​​solving which is known to almost every schoolchild, but it is not so easy to identify. Problem 1. Parallelograms ABCD and ВEFG with a common vertex B are located so that point C lies on the segment EF, and point G on the segment AD. Prove that these parallelograms are equal in area. Solution. The area of ​​each parallelogram is equal to twice the area of ​​triangle BCG, since this triangle has

Here’s a similar idea, but it’s much more difficult to see and call $реали-^{Рис.} 1$. Problem 2. Point $B_{1}$ is given on side AC of triangle ABC. On sides AB and BC, construct points $C_{1}$ and $A_{1}$ respectively so that the areas of triangles $AB_{1} C_{1}, BC_{1} A_{1}$ and $CA_{1} B_{1}$ are equal. Solution. The equivalence (equality of areas) of the indicated triangles is equivalent to the equivalence of parallelograms, for which the sides of the triangle $A_{1} B_{1} C_{1}$ are diagonals (Fig.2).

It follows that it is necessary to construct a broken line $B_{1} KC_{1} MA_{1} N$, the successive links of which are respectively parallel to AB, AC, BC, AB and AC. Then the area of ​​the parallelogram $AC_{1} KB_{1}$ is equal to the area of ​​the parallelogram $CKC_{1} M$ (they have a common side CK and the altitudes to it are equal), and the area $CKC_{1} M$, in turn, is equal to the area of ​​the parallelogram $BC_{1} MA_{1}$ (they have a common side $C_{1} M$ and the altitudes to it are equal). From the fact that $AC_{1} KB_{1}$ and $BC_{1} MA_{1}$ are equal in size, it follows that the areas of triangles $AB_{1} C_{1}$ and $BC_{1} A_{1}$ are equal. To prove that the triangle $CA_{1} B_{1}$ has the same area, it is enough to carry out a similar reasoning. But first we need to prove that $CB_{1} NA_{1}$ is a parallelogram. This is not difficult, since $NA_{1} B_{1} C$ by construction, and the equality of these sides follows from the fact that $AB_{1} C_{1} K MC$. In the next problem there is another case of standard equal-area (and you need to see it first!). But above all, it illustrates another idea that attracted Proizvolov - the use of unification and intersection of figures.
