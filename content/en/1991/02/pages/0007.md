Act 4. Continuous dedication and pinnacle of glory

It is clear that the radius of the drop is non-negative and does not increase with time. This means that with an increase in speed and a decrease in $r(y)$, the second term on the right side of equation (3) can be equal in absolute value to the first, and at this point along the path the rate of permutations changes: $\frac{dv}{dy} = 0$. This point is canceled by the letter $B$ by figure 3. You can now determine the value of the coefficient

center of resistance $C_a$, which remained unknown when determining the aerodynamic force using the method of dimensional theory:

$$0 = g - \frac{3}{4} C_a \frac{\rho}{\rho_0} \frac{v^2}{r_B} \Rightarrow C_a = \frac{4}{3} g \frac{\rho_0}{\rho} \frac{r_B}{v^2}.$$

Substituting the values $\rho_0 = 800$ kg/m$^3$, $\rho \approx 1$ kg/m$^3$, $g \approx 10$ m/s$^2$, and taking the values of the speed and radius of the drop by figure $3 - r_B \approx 0,9$ mm = $9 \cdot 10^{-4}$ m, $v_B \approx 5$ m/s, we obtain $C_a \approx 0,2$.

What's next, beyond point $B$? And then the speed of the drop should fall, and it is clear why. After all, the textile force is proportional to the mass of the drop, i.e., the cube of its radius ($\sim r^3$), and the aerodynamic force is proportional to the cross-sectional area of ​​the drop, i.e., the square of the radius ($\sim r^2$), so that as the radius decreases, the textile force kills faster than the aerodynamic force. Thus, beyond point $B$ the drop slows down and its speed decreases.

Now it's time to discuss how the radius of the drop changes. First of all, the reason for its decrease is completely clear: the heat from the flow around the drop affects the evaporation of its upper layers. Let's write down this thought.

Let us estimate the thermal energy supplied to the droplet. The flow of air mass flowing around a drop is of the order of $\rho v \pi r^2$ (kg/s). This is the mass of air that every minute enters into heat exchange with the drop. Each mass of matter carries energy, consisting of the kinetic energy of moving molecules and the potential energy of their interaction. If we consider the surrounding influence on an ideal gas, then its specific energy is equal to $c_p T$ (for an ideal gas, the interaction energy of its molecules is zero; the index $p$ for heat capacity indicates constant pressure). For a gaseous mass that has just evaporated from a drop of temperature $T'$, the specific energy is $c_p T'$; in the condensed state (in a liquid drop) it is equal to $c_p T' - L(T')$, where $L(T')$ is the specific heat of evaporation, which includes the specific potential energy of interaction of molecules.
