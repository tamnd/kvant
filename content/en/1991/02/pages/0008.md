Thus, during time $dt$, thermal energy of order $\rho v \pi r^2 c_p (T - T') dt$ arrives at the droplet. This energy goes to the evaporation of mass $dm$, which can be written in the form

$$-L dm = C_Q \rho v \pi r^2 c_p (T - T') dt. \tag{4}$$

Here the story is the same as with the momentum flow (aerodynamic force $F_a$): we introduced a dimensionless coefficient $C_Q$, the exact value of which we do not know and in which more subtle (but less significant for us) details of the process are “hidden”.

Substituting equalities $dt = dy/v$, $dm = 4 \pi r^2 \rho_0 dr$ into equation (4), we obtain

$$\frac{dr}{dy} = -\frac{C_Q}{4} \frac{\rho}{\rho_0} \frac{c_p (T - T')}{L}. \tag{5}$$

Thus, the “rate” of change in the radius of a drop with distance is constant. This is what the straight line $r(y)$ on figure 3 speaks about. By measuring its slope, from (5) one can find the dimensionless coefficient $C_Q$. Actually, this is why this experiment is needed - to clarify our theoretical messages; otherwise we would have managed without him! The solid curves $r(y)$ and $v(y)$ on figure 3 were precisely obtained from the condition that the experiments coincided with the calculations.

Act 5. Life at a crawl

However, the above equations describe the dynamics and heat and mass transfer of a drop, although over most of its trajectory, but not over all. Starting from a certain point (let’s denote it $D$), the laws of life of a drop change. A further description of its life could also be carried out in quite detail, even more accurately than before, but this would require the introduction of difficult-to-understand coefficients of thermal conductivity $\mu$ and thermal conductivity $\lambda$. Or it would be possible to bypass this section altogether in silence, citing the fact that there should also be dark periods in the lives of the heroes. Therefore, as a compromise, we will make only a sketch of the main features of this period.

The most significant thing is that after point $D$ the drop becomes thick and light, that the value $m \frac{dv}{dt}$ on the left side of equation (1) ceases to play a noticeable role, and the gravity force $mg$ is balanced by the resistance force of the impact. But the latter also becomes different. It is already proportional to the first power of the radius and speed and another quantity called the coefficient of gravity; this new force $F_\mu = 6 \pi \mu r v$ is called the Stokes force, and the movement of the drop is called creeping because it resembles the slow settling of a pellet in thick honey.

So, after the point $D$

$$0 = -6 \pi \mu r v + mg,$$

$$v = \frac{2}{9} \frac{\rho_0 g}{\mu} r^2. \tag{6}$$
