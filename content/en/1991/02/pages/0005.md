The evaporation of a water drop under room conditions is small. And in humid air, a drop does not evaporate at all and can even grow due to condensation (remember how long drops of rain or dew hang on trees and grass, and you can specifically observe a drop of water on a saucer). Let us also pay attention to the fact that air is a “foreign” gas for a drop of water, but for a nitrogen drop it is “almost our own” (after all, the main component of air is nitrogen).

What can you learn about the life of a nitrogen drop from its “portrait”? Firstly, the “portrait” allows you to change the size of the drop (average radius $r$) and, therefore, its mass $m = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \rho_0$ ($\rho_0 = 800 \text{ кг}/\text{м}^3$ is the density of liquid nitrogen) and its surface $s = 4\pi r^2$. Secondly, since the time $\Delta t$ between shots is known, from the dwgy $\Delta y$ of successive images of the same drop (Fig. 2.6) one can easily find its speed $v = \Delta y/\Delta t$. And by changing the height of the droplet, one can experimentally find the change in the radius and speed of the droplet during the falling process depending on $y: r(y)$ and $v(y)$.

The measurement results are shown on figure 3 in the form of circles. The scatter of data characteristic of any experiment is visible (for speed $v$ the experimental error is calculated and shown in the form of horizontal segments). Measurements showed that the droplet size kills monotonically, and the speed first increases and then, after passing through the maximum value, decreases.

Now it’s a matter of theoretical understanding of the results obtained, which makes it possible to describe the characteristic periods of the life of a parashte drop.
