Act 1. Misconception

It would seem that nothing could be simpler than making a nitrogen drop: drink liquid nitrogen into a tin jar with a small hole - the drops will fall one after another, like water from a leaky container. We tried it, but it didn’t work out: nitrogen poured out of the hole in solid shavings, sliding, like butter, along the layer of its own steam formed at the walls of the jar. After all, these walls, the initial temperature of which was close to room temperature ($T \approx 300$ K), were perceived by liquid nitrogen ($T' = 77$ K) as a red-hot furnace. One could wait for a “coat” of condensed water vapor and carbon dioxide contained in the air to grow on the outer surface of the jar, but during this time a lot of nitrogen would flow away; and so as not to leak, probably

one could come up with some kind of valve; but it would be possible... But we equipped the corresponding vertical tubes about a centimeter long (Fig. 1) with a porous substance that quickly took the temperature of liquid nitrogen and passed liquid nitrogen at a rate of approximately two drops per second.

These drops with an initial diameter of about two millimeters, falling freely in the air, sang through the mismo of a vertically located film cassette and, illuminated by flashes of a point light source, left a shadow pattern on the film. The duration of the flashes was very short, about one millionth of a second, and therefore the picture turned out to be quite clear - during such a short time of sipping, the drop and the pattern of flow around it changed very little.

At figure 2, a one of the many obtained pictures of a nitrogen drop is shown, at figure 2.6 - the result of its processing in color conditions on a machine that can “colorize” black and white photographs at the request of the experimenter. In these pictures, the trail behind the drop is clearly visible; it is formed due to the sharp difference in the densities of the surrounding air and the cold mass of nitrogen gas evaporated from the surface of the drop (a similar trace can be seen “in the light” in a glass of water behind a piece of dissolving sugar dropped on the edge of a spoon).
