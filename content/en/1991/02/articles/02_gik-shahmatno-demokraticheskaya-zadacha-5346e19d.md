CHESS DEMOCRATIC PROBLEM

In our time of democracy, when decisions are made by a large number of votes, I. Akulich from Minsk came up with scales as a pressing problem.
What is the maximum number of pieces that can be placed on the board so that each of them fits:
a) at least half of the rest;
b) no less than most of the others;
b) no less than a qualified major player (i.e. 2/3) of the rest?
Here is the solution to the problem given by I. Akulich.
Since pieces placed in the place of a rook, bishop, king or pawn keep the same squares under attack and some more, it is enough to look for solutions that use only pieces and knights (the squares they cover do not coincide).
Let us estimate from above the required number of figures. Suppose they are already arranged and one of the conditions a) - b) is met. Consider the rightmost figure of the lowest horizontal line on which there are figures. If these are figures, then he can keep no more than four figures under obstrelial fire (to the left, above, to the left-top and to the right-top of himself). If this is a knight, then it also pleases no more than four pieces (there are no pieces below the horizontal line in question). So, the average figure of the desired arrangement has at least one, which holds no more than four figures under fire. Then for case a) the number of remaining figures is no more than 4, for case 6) - no more than 3, for case b) - no more than 2. Therefore, the maximum number of figures in each case does not exceed:
a) 1+4+4=9;
b) 1+4+3=8;
b) 1+4+2=7.
In cases a) and b) the maxims are achieved. Corresponding arrangement for a)

The situation is more complicated with case c). Try to prove for yourself that it is impossible to arrange either seven or six figures in such a way as to satisfy the conditions of the problem. And you can arrange five figures.

From letters from readers

Imagine that each side has a king and pieces on the board. Everyone is visionary, but such a situation is possible (it was invented by V. Sidorov) in which neither the white nor the black pieces, despite the general expanse of the board, can check with impunity.

On what square should White's pieces be placed so that they are not able to force mat in 1 move (the position should not be mated)?

The answer is unexpected. The only such square on the board is b8! After 0...K:b8 there is no mate in one move, there is no mate at all.

In a “serial” problem (only white moves), a certain set of pieces must be positioned so that the series of moves ending in checkmate is as long as possible. In «Kvant» No. 5 for 1988, map positions were examined and 22 records were given. Soon one of them was beaten by seventh grader A. Popov.

In this position mat is given in 8 moves along with:
1. Rb1 2. Rl:b4 3-7. Npb3—c4—d5—e6—f7 8. Rh4×.

Many readers tried to break the record, but all the other positions submitted turned out to be defective: mat is given using various series of moves, which is prohibited in a serial problem.

E. Gik
