Upcoming tasks in space

Doctor of Technical Sciences
K. FEOKTISTOV

At present, there is still no established consensus on the most important directions for the development of human activity in space. A wide variety of programs are offered.

For example, the program, which is associated with the name of the American astronaut Sally Ride, envisages as its main goals for the next 50 years the research and creation of bases on the Moon, astronauts and planets, the creation of means of travel in the solar system, and the creation of space settlements.

Soviet scientists working in the field of fundamental sciences consider it necessary in the next decade to concentrate efforts on the study of near-Earth space, the Earth's magnetosphere, on studies of solar terrestrial communications, the Sun, the solar corona, Mars, and on astrophysical research using automatic spacecraft.

There are also more practical proposals, which are called the development of satellite communication and television systems, systems of meteorological satellites, the creation of satellite systems for environmental monitoring and research of the Earth's natural resources, the creation of economically feasible and efficient production in orbit.

So there are all sorts of different opinions. And, probably, it would be wrong to contrast practical devices with natural

The most pressing problems facing people today include

- environmentally, natural resources,
- political instability in the world, disunity of peoples, division of states into opposing blocs, distrust between them, etc.,
- increasing overpopulation of the Earth.

In the study of the Universe, as it seems to me today, the most interesting tasks include

— research of the solar system,
— research of stars, galaxies, celestial objects on the screens of the Universe using astrophysical instruments,
— research into the possibilities of flights to the stars.

With this approach, the following main directions of space work could be proposed.

Activity
in the interests of satisfying
basic human needs

We are talking about work of an applied nature that could bring specific benefits to people and, if possible, be economically viable. Three groups of work can be distinguished here.

- Group A: already defined, almost traditional, work such as economic control of the land surface, seas and oceans using spacecraft, satellite meteorological service, satellite navigation for ships, aircraft, ground transport, satellite systems for receiving signals from those in distress, communications and television.

— Group B: work related to the deployment of economically profitable production in orbit, with the transfer into orbit of necessary, but dangerous production on the surface of the Earth. To organize this work, it will be necessary to expand ground-based and, most importantly, space experiments to search for reliable and efficient technological processes in orbit.

This group of works also includes Japanese work on the emergence and feasibility of creating solar orbital power plants to supply the Earth with cheap and economically clean electricity.

This also includes work at orbital stations to determine the area of ​​the most effective activity of people directly in orbit, since until now this area has not been found and it remains unclear where, when performing what kind of work, it is more efficient for a person to be automatically employed.

— Group B: work in the interests of maintaining peace on Earth, maintaining stability and strengthening trust between states, preventing aggression. This refers to the creation of an international, open to public, satellite system for observing and monitoring the earth's surface, airspace, and underwater conditions. It is proposed to create systems of satellites that would give everyone the opportunity to see what is happening on Earth, day and night, to monitor the movements of military equipment and military equipment, the construction of suspicious (possibly military) objects, and to monitor the implementation of international agreements. Now practically only the Soviet Union and the United States of America have satellite reconnaissance systems, which are not yet year-round and do not provide a sufficiently high-quality picture at dusk and at night.

Exploration and exploration of the solar system

This area of ​​work includes
- exploration of the Sun,
— research of astroids,
- exploration of the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, etc.,
— research into the possibility and feasibility of space settlements.

It is unlikely that these studies will bring us fundamentally new information, but it would be simply unreasonable not to consider what is under our noses. Research can be carried out using automatic spacecraft. And only in the case of complete failure with the delivery of probe and atmosphere from Mars and, at the same time, when information appears indicating the possibility of detecting living organisms on Mars, should we always consider organizing a rafted expedition to Mars. (It is understood that the sample and atmosphere will be delivered to Earth to study them in order to determine whether there are living organisms in them, and if they are, to find out their genetic code or their reproductive mechanism and thereby obtain information in favor of one or another hypothesis about the origin of life: the “spontaneous generation” or “seeding” hypothesis.

Exploring the Universe

These are the most interesting, one might say, intriguing areas of research. They can provide us with the most valuable and necessary information. These works include
— research using space radiotelexes launched into near-solar and near-Earth orbits, working together in the radionferometry mode,
— study of the surrounding world using modern orbital astrophysical instruments (such as the Hubble Space Telescope) in various spectral ranges,
— research using optical telescopes with a spaced base, which can be located, for example, on the Moon,
— research into the problems of flight to the stars.

All of these are expensive undertakings, but if many countries combine the efforts and distribute the work wisely, they can be accomplished. In any case, the costs of their implementation will be significantly less than the military expenditures of states.

Technical means

To implement such a work program, it is necessary to improve existing space technology and create completely new ones, conducting theoretical and experimental research.

You can already imagine today what technical means will be needed for this.

1. Low-orbit systems of unified satellites for economic control, natural resource research, meteorological observations and ground-based automated information processing centers with an automated system for delivering results to subscribers.

2. A system of platforms in geostationary orbit for global communications, television, environmental monitoring, natural resource exploration, and meteorological observations.

Satellites in geostationary orbit are stationary relative to the Earth's surface. You cannot have a large number of satellites in this orbit, otherwise they will begin to interfere with each other’s work. Therefore, it will be necessary to create multifunctional platforms in the future.

5. Orbital stations.

Apparently it is advisable to create orbital stations of different types: laboratory stations of the Salyut and Mir type, such as the American Freedom station currently being developed, orbital stations of the cloud type.

The latter type of station seems to me the most promising. The idea of ​​a cloud station is that the individual parts of the station - its models - are not rigidly connected to each other, but “float” their uses from each other at distances of, say, 10-100 kilometers (modern space technology makes it possible to ensure the stability of such a “cloud” without significant fuel consumption). Moreover, each station model operates under optimal conditions for it: on the technological module there is no interference (microoverloads) from the astronauts (for example, when they are engaged in physical control) and from the operation of the attitude control engines; telescope models are oriented without interference with the required accuracy and in the desired direction; a gas station with dangerous reserves of fuel components can be removed at a sufficient distance from the base residential model.

6. Orbital factories for the production of supernumerary materials, biologicals and other production processes that will be cost-effective or practical in orbit.

7. Automatic spacecraft of the international satellite system for observing and monitoring the surface of land, waters, airspace and underwater conditions with a system for providing information to subscribers.

An international surveillance system could have three subsystems:

— 12-16 satellites with optical-technical apparatus for daytime observation,

— 12–16 satellites with radars for all-weather and round-the-clock surveillance,

— 3-6 satellites with an apparatus for observing in the infrared range.

Modern optical-technical space equipment already makes it possible to view objects with dimensions of the order of a meter from orbit and transmit the image through relay satellites to subscribers.

So-called side-scan radars placed in orbit, with sufficient power, will allow for round-the-clock and year-round monitoring of the Earth's surface, impacts, and even monitoring the movements of submarines. In principle, using orbital radars it would be possible to distinguish objects several meters in size.

With the help of such a system of satellites, it would be possible to obtain a rate of update of information about what is happening on the Earth's surface of the order of 30-60 minutes.

8. Systems of radio telexes launched into near-Earth and near-solar orbits and operating in a single radiointerfermetric scheme.

The resolution of an optical telescope (the angular size of an extremely different object) is determined by the ratio of the wavelength at which the observation is made to the diameter of the telescope. For a telescope with a diameter of about 3 meters, placed outside the atmosphere, in principle it is possible to obtain a resolution of the order of hundredths of an arcsecond. For a system of radiotelexes placed in orbit and working together, the resolution will be determined by the ratio of the wavelength at which radio greening is received to the distance between the radiotelexes.
