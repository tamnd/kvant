Starting with four lectures on physics, I would like, before moving on to a systematic presentation of the subject, to remain today on one general question and share with you some messages about the position that physics occupies and, in my opinion, should occupy in a number of sciences, the study of which you are going to devote the next few years.

I would like to give you material on the basis of which you could see for yourself that an engineer always needs physics, throughout his activity, and that it cannot be looked at as a subject that is needed - and is it even necessary? - pass once, and then you can score, since anyway, what an engineer needs to know from physics is repeated once again when passing special subjects.

When I say that an engineer needs physics, I mean not only that he must be familiar with those individual phenomena and laws that he directly encounters in his practical activities. Such a statement would be self-evident. That a civil engineer, when calculating the strength of a structure, must be familiar with the basic laws of control, that an electrical engineer in designing, say, a lighting network must know Ohm's law relating current strength, resistance and electromotive force of batteries, etc. - this, of course, does not need proof. No, when I say that an engineer needs physics, I mean that he needs a broad knowledge of this subject in its entirety; I argue that he needs knowledge of physics in itself as a whole discipline with its specification methodology, and not just depending on current applications. I affirm, finally, that for this it is not enough for an engineer to know only the experimental part of it, but that he must be thoroughly familiar with the theory.

But in order to show that this is really the case, we must first of all set out to clarify the structure of physics as a science and see in what correspondence the theory is with the experimental material that physics operates on.

Of course, we cannot resolve here the question of the existence of physical science in its entirety. This question, related to the theory of knowledge and extremely interesting for both the physicist and the physiologist, is much more complex than it might seem at first glance. It always occupies a place in the history of physical thought. But for our purpose, we don’t need to go too deep into it. It will be enough for us to occupy only one side of it, although, it is true, here too we will have to start somewhat from afar.

There is no doubt that the only means by which we obtain our information about the world around us is our senses. But single sensory uplifts are too temporary and unstable to serve as material for further processing. And so a person identifies and records in memory those common features of individual ascensions that are repeated and which are practically important for him. This process, which takes place completely involuntarily, leads to the formation of what in logic is called concepts.

Education is the first step on the path to understanding nature. They are the basis on which the future is built. But the primary concepts formed in this way have, it seems to me, the following property. They defy strict definition. We all know the concept of “light”. But we cannot explain in words, define one another being of this concept. To learn it, you need to have eyes, you need to see how everything around us is illuminated when the sun rises and how it plunges back into darkness when it sets. If it were more meaningful, you can learn the same thing by turning on and off

light bulb. But one thing is certain: words and definitions are powerless here. Try to explain to Slena from birth what light is.

So, the initial physical concept cannot be taught with just words. This is why, let me emphasize this here, neither a textbook nor a teacher is sufficient to teach physics. The student must do at least a little experimental work himself. He must, at least superficially, hear for himself, perceive for himself the phenomena that he is told about.

We got a little sidetracked. Let us return to the first steps along the path of knowledge of nature, to the concepts vaguely imposed on us by nature. As a person mutilated his stock of concepts imposed by experience (experiential knowledge), all present there was a need for their systematization, without which there is no way to understand the endless abundance of phenomena surrounding us. In this systematization, our ability to form another kind of concepts, concepts more definite than those discussed above, and less dependent on our feelings, is of great service to us. First of all, this includes the concept of number and those concepts that mathematics operates on. In the area of ​​these concepts, in other words, in the area of ​​mathematical thinking, we feel incomparably more confident than when operating with material vaguely supplied to us by our senses. With the help of mathematical concepts, one can definitely formulate premises and just as definitely and easily draw conclusions and conclusions from them. And so, knowing this power behind him, a person begins - first instinctively, and then, with the development of science, consciously - to adapt mathematical concepts and especially the concept of number to raw experimental material, to physical concepts.

In this process of transition from qualitative relationships to quantitative conclusions, the most important stage of scientific thought. Both the concept of measurement and the process of measuring physical quantities are based on it. We can safely say that any area of ​​physical phenomena generally becomes scientific only from the moment we learn to see measurements in it. So, for example, until there was a precise concept of temperature and they did not know how to measure it, the science of heat almost or even did not exist at all.

To use mathematics in the sense described, we are now trying to systems in the phenomena around us and to make it easier for ourselves to understand them by looking for such mathematical formulas in the general sense of the word, not always narrowly algebraic, that would cover as much as possible the number of individual facts or the general aspect of various phenomena. If such a formula is found, then we say that we have found a physical law. Let's take an example. The law of refraction of light when passing from one transparent medium to another states: the incident and refracted ray lies in the same plane, and the ratio between the angle of incidence and the angle of refraction is constant. For example, for water and air this ratio is 1.33. What is this law?

This is a formula covering countless individual levels of refraction. It saves us from the need to do the experiment in each individual case, fill out or fill in the tables for each individual case with the angle of incidence and the corresponding angle of refraction of the beam. Knowing the law of refraction, you are sure that at any moment when it comes to you, with the help of simple calculations you will be able to solve any question that presents itself in this direction.

In order to better understand this enormous material, people tried to find such pictures, such points of view that would allow them to combine individual laws into one whole. This is how a physical theory, or rather theories, was created.

Theory, therefore, stands in the same relation to individual laws as laws stand to individual phenomena. The systematizing role of theory, of course, does not exhaust all its essence, but still, as you can see, it is of enormous importance in the systematization of our knowledge. And here mathematics is a huge help. The relationships between the various aspects of physics and their development that have just been considered can be traced to any physical theory. It is very easy to do this, for example, on optics.

Optics, by the way, is one of the most ancient scientifically developed branches of physics. The study of optical phenomena already in ancient times led to the establishment of certain laws. The law of rectilinear propagation of light and the law of reflection from mirrors have been known for a long time. Later, in the 17th century, the law of supply was discovered. Depending on which group of phenomena was supported by the study and from what points of view they were approached, various fragmentary laws were established. The phenomena of light diffraction, interference, etc. were discovered. But as long as there were no promises, a unifying point of view, it was extremely difficult to understand the totality of optical phenomena. Moreover, individual laws seemed to be in conflict with each other. The bending of light did not correspond, for example, with the rectilinear propagation of the beam.

This was so until the success of a number of brilliant physicists, of whom Huygens and Fresnel should be named first of all, managed to find the picture that we now call the wave theory of counting and which allows us to combine all optics into one harmonious whole. And everything that turned out to be complex and contradictory became simple and clear.

And take Newton's theory of the world. Combining with genius courage phenomena that seem disparate in the eyes of our senses, such as the fall of a stone and the movement of heavenly bodies, it is graceful precisely because of its simplicity. In one simple formula it contains all the dynamics of the entire universe.

I will limit myself to these examples. maybe there are some things in them that are unclear to you. It's nothing. An understanding of the factual side will come as you study physics, but I think that for you, at least in general terms, the relationship between its experimental and theoretical sides has now been clarified.

These two sides, as you have seen, are closely related; together they represent one whole. In achieving our ultimate goal - understanding nature - theory can help by systematizing our experience and giving us the opportunity to use the material. Theory, and therefore the tool that, as we have seen, it uses - mathematics, is not ballast and something artificially attached to the science of nature. No, it is that tool without which we would not be able to master the surrounding nasir, both in a practical sense and in the sense of satisfying mental needs.

I think, don’t consider it a paradox, that one cannot demand knowledge of only experimental physics, but not at all because it is too little, but because it is too difficult. A more or less complete knowledge of experimental physics is impossible without the help of human theory.

The presented view of the systematizing role of theory is a very beautiful comparative one made by Poincaré.

experimental data, individual phenomena - these are the volumes of which the library consists. Theory is the catalog of our library. Just as without a catalogue, a library, especially a large one, is only a collection of books, very valuable books that cannot actually be used, in the same way, physics without theory is not a science, but only a rather poorly defined conglomerate of individual facts, which there is no way to understand.

And now it will not be difficult for us to answer the question that we asked ourselves at the beginning. Does an engineer need physics as a whole? Isn’t knowledge of individual, non-presidential facts necessary for his practical work enough for him? The answer, it seems to me, is clear.

In order for a product to work—let me speak in the language of Poincaré’s comparison—it is not enough for an engineer to read and know a few books from a huge library of knowledge. He should be familiar with, or at least be able to understand, the catalog of the entire library.

History technically knows many examples of mysterious failures, repeated failures and sometimes having indirect consequences. And very often it turns out that the mystery is not due to the presence of actual new, hitherto generally unknown factors, but to the lack of a broad physical horizon among those who dealt with these issues. And when brothers with truly broad physical knowledge were behind the solution, the riddle was not only clarified and a way was found to prevent failure, but often new paths were opened for further progress.

Let me conclude with one example that, it seems to me, illustrates the importance of a broad physical horizon in resolving technical issues.

I mean the question of optical instruments and, in particular, the question of microsop... Microsop became especially interested in the middle of the last century, after its use in biology opened up completely new paths in the study of life phenomena. But after the first successes, it was discovered that the microsops that existed at that time were not good and were not strong. Schon researchers felt that if it were possible to build microsops with high magnification, it would at the same time be possible to penetrate even further into existing life.

And also the prospect has always attracted people with a special force. And imagination did not remain before the construction of microsops, magnifying by tens, hundreds of thousands and millions of times, they expected miracles from their use. Researchers expected that with their help it would be possible to penetrate into the most intimate details of the structure of living matter.

It is clear that with such a situation, specialist designers of optical devices set about successfully developing the microsop. And they considered it fundamentally possible to achieve any increase. The whole issue seemed to point to overcoming technical difficulties.

The fact is that at that time all calculations concerning optical devices were carried out exclusively with the help of so-called geometric optics. The calculations were based on the microsop theory, which operates with light rays as straight lines... And from the point of view of geometric optics, there really is no fundamental limit for the possibility of increasing the microsop.

However, it soon became clear that the work aimed at conditioning the microsop did not give the results that seemed to be expected. There was a contradiction between what seemed achievable and what was achieved that could not be explained.
