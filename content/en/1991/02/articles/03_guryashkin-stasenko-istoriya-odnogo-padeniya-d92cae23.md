Act 1. Misconception

It would seem that nothing could be simpler than making a nitrogen drop: drink liquid nitrogen into a tin jar with a small hole - the drops will fall one after another, like water from a leaky container. We tried it, but it didn’t work out: nitrogen poured out of the hole in solid shavings, sliding, like butter, along the layer of its own steam formed at the walls of the jar. After all, these walls, the initial temperature of which was close to room temperature ($T \approx 300$ K), were perceived by liquid nitrogen ($T' = 77$ K) as a red-hot furnace. One could wait for a “coat” of condensed water vapor and carbon dioxide contained in the air to grow on the outer surface of the jar, but during this time a lot of nitrogen would flow away; and so as not to leak, probably

one could come up with some kind of valve; but it would be possible... But we equipped the corresponding vertical tubes about a centimeter long (Fig. 1) with a porous substance that quickly took the temperature of liquid nitrogen and passed liquid nitrogen at a rate of approximately two drops per second.

These drops with an initial diameter of about two millimeters, falling freely in the air, sang through the mismo of a vertically located film cassette and, illuminated by flashes of a point light source, left a shadow pattern on the film. The duration of the flashes was very short, about one millionth of a second, and therefore the picture turned out to be quite clear - during such a short time of sipping, the drop and the pattern of flow around it changed very little.

At figure 2, a one of the many obtained pictures of a nitrogen drop is shown, at figure 2.6 - the result of its processing in color conditions on a machine that can “colorize” black and white photographs at the request of the experimenter. In these pictures, the trail behind the drop is clearly visible; it is formed due to the sharp difference in the densities of the surrounding air and the cold mass of nitrogen gas evaporated from the surface of the drop (a similar trace can be seen “in the light” in a glass of water behind a piece of dissolving sugar dropped on the edge of a spoon).

The evaporation of a water drop under room conditions is small. What can you learn about the life of a nitrogen drop from its “portrait”? Firstly, the “portrait” allows you to change the size of the drop (average radius $r$) and, therefore, its mass $m = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \rho_0$ ($\rho_0 = 800 \text{ кг}/\text{м}^3$ is the density of liquid nitrogen) and its surface $s = 4\pi r^2$. The measurement results are shown on figure 3 in the form of circles. The scatter of data characteristic of any experiment is visible (for speed $v$ the experimental error is calculated and shown in the form of horizontal segments). Measurements showed that the droplet size kills monotonically, and the speed first increases and then, after passing through the maximum value, decreases.

Now it’s a matter of theoretical understanding of the results obtained, which makes it possible to describe the characteristic periods of the life of a parashte drop.

Act 3. Inevitable braking

As the drop accelerates, the air resistance becomes lower and lower. How to describe this power?

How many times have they told the world that the force of air resistance moves

body moving relative to it (aerodynamic force) is proportional to the density of the air, the square of the size of the body (or its cross-sectional area) and the square of the speed:

$$F_a = -C_a\rho r^2 v^2.$$

This statement can be obtained, for example, from dimension messages. Here the minus sign takes into account that the force is directed in the direction opposite to the movement, and the dimensionless coefficient $C_a$, generally speaking, depends on the speed, but this dependence is weak and can be obtained from experiments or other more complex theoretical reports.

You can also use reasoning. The flow of air mass inside a cylinder with a cross section equal to the cross section $\pi r^2$ of a spherical drop is equal to $\rho v \pi r^2 (\kappa r/c)$; but a unit of mass carries momentum (specific impulse) $mv/m = v$, so the total flux of momentum in that cylinder is $\rho v \pi r^2 v = \rho v^2 \pi r^2(H)$. Of course, the line of gas current when flowing around a drop is somehow bent - so some kind of factor $C_a$ appears, taking into account everything that is not taken into account in these considerations.

So, taking into account this aerodynamic force, the equation of motion of a drop (Newton’s second law) can be written as

$$m \frac{dv}{dt} = mg - \rho \pi r^2 v^2 C_a.$$

And if we divide both parts by the mass of the drop $m = 4/3 \pi r^3 \rho_0$, we get

$$\frac{dv}{dt} = g - \frac{3}{4} C_a \frac{\rho}{\rho_0} \frac{v^2}{r},$$

i.e., the acceleration of the drop consists of two parts: the acceleration of the gravitational force $g$ and the negative acceleration (braking) associated with the aerodynamic force.

There is another way to write the last equation. Recall that the integral of time $dt$ can be obtained by dividing the distance traveled $dy$ by the speed of movement $v: dt = dy/v$. Means,

$$\frac{dv}{dt} = v \frac{dv}{dy} = \frac{d}{dy} \left( \frac{v^2}{2} \right),$$

Act 4. Continuous dedication and pinnacle of glory

It is clear that the radius of the drop is non-negative and does not increase with time. This means that with an increase in speed and a decrease in $r(y)$, the second term on the right side of equation (3) can be equal in absolute value to the first, and at this point along the path the rate of permutations changes: $\frac{dv}{dy} = 0$. This point is canceled by the letter $B$ by figure 3. You can now determine the value of the coefficient

center of resistance $C_a$, which remained unknown when determining the aerodynamic force using the method of dimensional theory:

$$0 = g - \frac{3}{4} C_a \frac{\rho}{\rho_0} \frac{v^2}{r_B} \Rightarrow C_a = \frac{4}{3} g \frac{\rho_0}{\rho} \frac{r_B}{v^2}.$$

Substituting the values $\rho_0 = 800$ kg/m$^3$, $\rho \approx 1$ kg/m$^3$, $g \approx 10$ m/s$^2$, and taking the values of the speed and radius of the drop by figure $3 - r_B \approx 0,9$ mm = $9 \cdot 10^{-4}$ m, $v_B \approx 5$ m/s, we obtain $C_a \approx 0,2$.

What's next, beyond point $B$? And then the speed of the drop should fall, and it is clear why. After all, the textile force is proportional to the mass of the drop, i.e., the cube of its radius ($\sim r^3$), and the aerodynamic force is proportional to the cross-sectional area of ​​the drop, i.e., the square of the radius ($\sim r^2$), so that as the radius decreases, the textile force kills faster than the aerodynamic force. Thus, beyond point $B$ the drop slows down and its speed decreases.

Now it's time to discuss how the radius of the drop changes. First of all, the reason for its decrease is completely clear: the heat from the flow around the drop affects the evaporation of its upper layers. Let's write down this thought.

Let us estimate the thermal energy supplied to the droplet. The flow of air mass flowing around a drop is of the order of $\rho v \pi r^2$ (kg/s). This is the mass of air that every minute enters into heat exchange with the drop. Each mass of matter carries energy, consisting of the kinetic energy of moving molecules and the potential energy of their interaction. If we consider the surrounding influence on an ideal gas, then its specific energy is equal to $c_p T$ (for an ideal gas, the interaction energy of its molecules is zero; the index $p$ for heat capacity indicates constant pressure). For a gaseous mass that has just evaporated from a drop of temperature $T'$, the specific energy is $c_p T'$; in the condensed state (in a liquid drop) it is equal to $c_p T' - L(T')$, where $L(T')$ is the specific heat of evaporation, which includes the specific potential energy of interaction of molecules.

figure 4 shows a qualitative view of the dependence of the specific “solid content” on temperature for gaseous nitrogen (straight line $c_p T$ for an ideal gas) and for liquid nitrogen (lower curve). They can (if necessary) be constructed quantitatively using tabular data. We have enough indications of figure values ​​$c_p$ and $L$, which are useful for numerical estimates.

Thus, during time $dt$, thermal energy of order $\rho v \pi r^2 c_p (T - T') dt$ arrives at the droplet. This energy goes to the evaporation of mass $dm$, which can be written in the form

$$-L dm = C_Q \rho v \pi r^2 c_p (T - T') dt. \tag{4}$$

Here the story is the same as with the momentum flow (aerodynamic force $F_a$): we introduced a dimensionless coefficient $C_Q$, the exact value of which we do not know and in which more subtle (but less significant for us) details of the process are “hidden”.

Substituting equalities $dt = dy/v$, $dm = 4 \pi r^2 \rho_0 dr$ into equation (4), we obtain

$$\frac{dr}{dy} = -\frac{C_Q}{4} \frac{\rho}{\rho_0} \frac{c_p (T - T')}{L}. \tag{5}$$

Thus, the “rate” of change in the radius of a drop with distance is constant. This is what the straight line $r(y)$ on figure 3 speaks about. By measuring its slope, from (5) one can find the dimensionless coefficient $C_Q$. Actually, this is why this experiment is needed - to clarify our theoretical messages; otherwise we would have managed without him! The solid curves $r(y)$ and $v(y)$ on figure 3 were precisely obtained from the condition that the experiments coincided with the calculations.

Act 5. Life at a crawl

However, the above equations describe the dynamics and heat and mass transfer of a drop, although over most of its trajectory, but not over all. Starting from a certain point (let’s denote it $D$), the laws of life of a drop change. A further description of its life could also be carried out in quite detail, even more accurately than before, but this would require the introduction of difficult-to-understand coefficients of thermal conductivity $\mu$ and thermal conductivity $\lambda$. Or it would be possible to bypass this section altogether in silence, citing the fact that there should also be dark periods in the lives of the heroes. Therefore, as a compromise, we will make only a sketch of the main features of this period.

The most significant thing is that after point $D$ the drop becomes thick and light, that the value $m \frac{dv}{dt}$ on the left side of equation (1) ceases to play a noticeable role, and the gravity force $mg$ is balanced by the resistance force of the impact. But the latter also becomes different. It is already proportional to the first power of the radius and speed and another quantity called the coefficient of gravity; this new force $F_\mu = 6 \pi \mu r v$ is called the Stokes force, and the movement of the drop is called creeping because it resembles the slow settling of a pellet in thick honey.

So, after the point $D$

$$0 = -6 \pi \mu r v + mg,$$

where

$$v = \frac{2}{9} \frac{\rho_0 g}{\mu} r^2. \tag{6}$$

It’s worth stopping and thinking again here. Indeed, judging by figure 3, the speed of the drop continues to decrease, so that $\frac{dv}{dy} \neq 0$ and $\frac{dv}{dt} = v \frac{dv}{dy} \neq 0$; and the mass is not yet zero, and we wrote “zero” together $m \frac{dv}{dt}$. What does it mean?

Let us remember that, having swung, you can throw a pebble far, but poplar fluff with the same arm swing will fly away very close: the fluff will immediately slow down, its speed will become zero (if the air is perfectly calm). The physicist will say that the relaxation time, or “acclimation”

After point $D$, the heat supply to the drop will be determined in the main process of thermal conductivity. In this case, during the time $dt$ the drop will receive warm energy of the order of $4\pi r^2 \lambda \frac{T-T'}{r} dt$, and due to this heat the mass $dm$ will evaporate, i.e.

$$-L \frac{dm}{dt} \sim 4\pi r^2 \lambda \frac{T-T'}{r},$$

$$r_D^2 - r^2 \sim t - t_D.$$

This law, according to which the surface area of ​​an evaporated drop kills in proportion to time, was established by the Russian scientist B.I. Sreznevsky (1883) on the basis of the theory of dimensions and numerous experiments with stationary drops of various substances (see also problem F1221 in «Kvant» No. 7 for 1990). Comparing the resulting equation with (6), we see that the thermal velocity of a drop kills linearly over time, which means that the coordinate of the drop is quadratic, as with uniformly slow motion, and $v \sim \sqrt{y_L - y}$, where $y_L$ is the point of complete evaporation (if the movement continued further according to this law). But then $r \sim \sqrt[4]{y_L - y}$.

Stop, the thoughtful reader will say, but the speed of the drop at the initial moment should be equal to zero, and for some reason we didn’t notice this in the second act? And they did the right thing - we will answer, since at the moment of separation from the dropper the speed of the drop is no longer zero, but the size of a table, that the mode of creeping movement in the air is not observed from the very first moment of its birth. In addition, there remains a subtle question about the influence of the proximity of the dropper itself (after all, in our delusions we always think of all bodies as remote “to infinity” from an isolated drop).

Act 6. The last microsecond

And then there comes a moment in the life of a drop when its size becomes equal, and then less than the length of the free space of air molecules (between contacts with each other) $l \approx 10^{-7}$ m. This means that the drop is no longer in a continuous, but a rarefied environment, and the laws of life of a drop change. How long does a drop have to live?

Now approximately $1/6n \langle u \rangle$ molecules fall per unit surface per unit time. Air ($n - \text{плотность молекул}, \langle u \rangle = \sqrt{8RT/\pi M}$ - their average thermal speed). Each molecule brings energy of the order of $3/2kT$ (more precisely $5/2kT$, if we take into account that the airiness is from diatomic molecules; but this is not important for estimating the order of magnitude). Thus, energy comes to the entire spherical surface of the drop per unit time

$$4\pi r^2 \frac{1}{6} \rho \langle u \rangle \frac{5RT}{2M}.$$

Equating this energy to the order of magnitude

$$-L \frac{dm}{dt} = -L 4\pi r^2 \rho_0 \frac{dr}{dt},$$

we obtain an approximation of correspondence

$$\frac{dr}{dt} \approx -\frac{5}{2} \frac{RT \rho \langle u \rangle}{L\rho_0 6M}.$$

Epilogue,
or What they kept silent about in Tragedini and who needs it

Well, of course, we haven’t discussed everything. For example, as already mentioned, one could consider the so-called processes of molecular transfer of mass (diffusion), momentum (viscosity) and energy (conductivity). This would require knowledge of the corresponding coefficients and would make it possible to describe the section of droplet motion from point $D$ to point $S$, beyond which the free-molecular flow regime begins. Further, the influence of oxygen present in the air, a 《alien》 gas from the point of view of the substance of the nitrogen drop, has not been discussed; one can expect that this oxygen, like any substance, will tend to diffuse to where it does not exist, that is, inside the drop; in any case, it somehow interferes with the evaporating nitrogen molecules. It is also not taken into account that the shape of the drop is not necessarily spherical; it fluctuates during movement (this can be seen from figure 2, $\theta$). Further, we believe that the temperature of the drop throughout its entire life remains equal to the temperature of saturated vapor at atmospheric pressure above a flat surface of liquid nitrogen, but this may not be entirely true.

And finally, who needs this drop with its tragedy?

The behavior of individual drops and entire clouds of them needs to be known in many cases. For example, if you need to make visible (visualize) the flow pattern around a model of a future aircraft, add droplets of liquid nitrogen to the flow and place them in the right place - as a result of the scattering of light on the “fog” consisting of these droplets, as well as some of the water and carbon dioxide condensed in their cold traces, all the dependencies of the air around the model will become visible. And in the so-called cryogenic wind tunnels, liquid nitrogen is specially injected into the flow to cool it, and it is important to know how quickly nitrogen droplets with different initial sizes evaporate. And in many processes of chemical technology, purification of industrial gases and in other cases, it is necessary to know the initial behavior of an individual drop (of any substance) with a changing mass.

It is useful for us that this little tragedy of a nitrogen drop allows us to discuss some processes and show how experiment and theoretical reasoning complement each other.
