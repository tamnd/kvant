J. WALKER

As common as it is, you most likely haven't noticed all of its amazing features. One cannot be ignored in practice, while others make it possible to carry out very dangerous tricks, which were once shown by daredevils at carnivals.

Let's start with observation. Heat a pan of tap water with a flame or an electric heat source. When water is heated, air molecules dissolved in it are released from the solution and collected in tiny bubbles in cracks at the bottom of the pan. (These areas are small enough that surface tension prevents water from pouring into them when filling the pan.) Over time, each bubble inflates and its buoyancy increases. Eventually the bubble breaks away from the cracks and floats to the surface of the water. Since the crack is still filled with air, another bubble begins to form there. The formation of air bubbles is a sign that the water is heating up, but it is far from boiling.

Water exposed to the atmosphere boils at a temperature sometimes called the normal boiling point $t_k$. For example, $t_k = 100^\circ\text{C}$ at an air pressure of 1 atm. Since the water at the bottom of the pan does not come into contact with the atmosphere, it remains a liquid even if it is heated above $t_k$ by several degrees. When heated and overheated, it is constantly washed away with the rest of the water by convection (hot water rises, and colder water will mix it up).

As the temperature of the pan increases further, the bottom layer of water will begin to evaporate and the water molecules will collect into small bubbles of steam in the dry cracks. This boiling phase is marked by staccato sounds, buzzing and sometimes buzzing. The water almost sings about how wrong it is to heat up. Every time a bubble of steam rises into colder water, it suddenly disappears because the steam inside it condenses. With each such disappearance, a sound wave is created - the buzzing sound that you hear. When the temperature of the entire body of water rises, the bubbles will not be able to disappear until they break away from the cracks and travel part of the way to the surface of the water (Fig. 1).
