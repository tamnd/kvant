Of course, this “resect” is known to everyone who studied Ampere’s law in a school physics textbook. Our task is to introduce you to the useful properties of the Ampere force acting on a curved section of a conductor with current in the simplest magnetic field - a uniform one.

Let the conductor be a spatial (in some cases flat) broken line consisting of $N$ straight segments ($\Delta l_1, \Delta l_2, \dots, \Delta l_N$). Let us first sum up the forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ acting on the conductor segments ($\Delta l_1$ and $\Delta l_2$).

Let us assume that the vector $\vec{B}$ has redistributed the planes of the selected segments (Fig. 1). The force $\vec{F}_1$ is redistributed to the vector ($\Delta l_1$), and its direction is determined by the right of the left hand. Similarly, the force $\vec{F}_2$ redistributed to the vector ($\Delta l_2$). The modules of these forces are equal respectively

$$F_1 = BI(\Delta l)_1 \text{ и } F_2 = BI(\Delta l)_2.$$

Let us add the forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ and the segments ($\Delta l_1$ and $\Delta l_2$) in pairs. Since $\angle LMN = \angle ADC$ are angles with mutually redefined sides, triangles $LMN$ and $ADC$ are similar, and the vector $\vec{F}_{12} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2$ is redefined to the vector ($\Delta l_{12}$), and $F_{12} = BI(\Delta l_{12})$. Consequently, the Ampere force acting on the section of the conductor $ADC$, which represents two sides of the triangle, is equal to the Ampere force acting on the straight section of the conductor ($\Delta l_{12}$ (the third side $AC$ of the same triangle), if both conductors have the same text points.

Let us now consider another, also simple case, when the vector $\vec{B}$ lies in the plane of the conductor segments ($\Delta l_1$ and $\Delta l_2$ (Fig. 2). Let us direct the coordinate axis $X$ along the vector $\vec{B}$, and the axis $Y$ - redistributed to it. Forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ are directed away from us over the cultural plane figure - in accordance with the rule of the left hand. The total force $\vec{F}_{12}$ is directed in the same way, and its model is equal.

$$F_{12} = F_1 + F_2 = BI(\Delta l)_1 \sin \alpha_1 + BI(\Delta l)_2 \sin \alpha_2 = BI(\Delta l)_{12y} = BI(\Delta l)_{12z} \sin \alpha.$$

Here we took advantage of the fact that the sum of the projections onto the $Y$ axis of the vectors ($\Delta l_1$ and $\Delta l_2$ is equal to the projection of the total vector ($\Delta l_{12}$).

Thus, in the general cases we have considered, when calculating the Ampere force, two adjacent straight segments of a conductor can be replaced by one, the beginning of which is at the beginning of the first segment, and the end at the end of the second.
