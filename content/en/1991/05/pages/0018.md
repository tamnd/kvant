At the beginning of the twentieth century, mechanical calculating machines appeared. Their appearance accelerated the search for new perfect numbers.

The tenth was found in 1911, it turned out to have 54 digits:

$$618 970 019 642 690 137 449 562 111 \times 2^{88}$$.

The eleventh, with 65 digits, was discovered in 1914:

$$162 259 276 829 213 363 391 578 010 288 127 \times 2^{106}$$.

The twelfth was also found in 1914, it already consists of 77 digits:

$$2^{126} \cdot (2^{127} - 1).$$

In 1932, mathematician Lemaire decided to find the thirteenth perfect number. To do this, he decided to determine whether the last number of the form $2^p - 1$ that Mersenne considered prime, namely the number $2^{257} - 1$, was prime.

He had to work for a whole year, using the calculating instruments known at that time, but as a result he became convinced that this number was composite, and the twelfth perfect number remained the most perfect until 1952.

The thirteenth perfect number was found by an electronic adding machine. On January 30, 1952, the American mathematician Robinson at the University of California used an electronic adding machine to study the prime numbers $2^p - 1$. Robinson decided to first determine the number $2^{257} - 1$. He invited Lemaire to be present at this verification, who spent a year twenty years ago on this calculation. Lemaire received great satisfaction when he saw that the machine achieved the same result, completing his year's work in eighteen seconds. In order for a new perfect number to be found, it was therefore necessary to find a new prime number. The machine continued searching for prime numbers. She checked 42 numbers in two hours, the smallest of which had more than 80 digits! All these numbers turned out to be composite.
