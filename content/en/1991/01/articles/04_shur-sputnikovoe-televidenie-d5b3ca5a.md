SATELLITE TELEVISION

Candidate of Technical Sciences
A. SHUR

Everyone knows about terrestrial television. In terrestrial television, the antennas of our home televisions are pointed at the local broadcast television (TB) station. But probably not everyone knows that satellite television has already appeared, where antennas are pointed directly at an artificial Earth satellite. The satellite is equipped with a receiver that receives the TV signal from a ground-based transmitting station, and a transmitter that emits it towards the Earth.
Satellite television covers the entire globe. It is of great interest because it provides the opportunity to accept

mother of TB programs of any country in the world. Terrestrial TV is convenient and more accessible for local programming. Already at the present time in the USA and Western Europe, millions of television viewers use one or another television system and can receive 15-20 TB programs.

Signal frequency
The principle of television, as is known, is the sequential transmission of electrical signals corresponding to the brightness of the elements (dots) of the image. With amplitude modulation, the power of the useful signal

changes with the brightness of the element. Typically the image signal bandwidth is 6.25 MHz. Sound also requires a frequency band of up to several hundred kilohertz. As a result, one frequency channel (one TB program) occupies a frequency band of 8 MHz. With the frequency model, the signal power remains unchanged. The frequency of the signal changes in proportion to the intensity of the image element (and the volume of the sound). This type of model has some advantages, but one channel occupies a frequency band 3-4 times wider than with the amplitude model.

Transmission of signals with such a wide frequency band is only possible in the high frequency range. In the range of metro and diameter waves for television, frequency bands from 50 to 230 MHz (wavelength $\lambda=6-13$ m) and from 470 to 1000 MHz ($\lambda=0,63-0,3$ m) are allocated. The development of television has now led to the fact that almost all of these frequencies have already been used, and even many times. The World Administrative Radio Conference (WARC) has allocated another frequency band from 11.7 to 12.5 GHz specifically for satellite television. This band belongs to cantidad television waves ($\lambda \approx 2,5$ cm). The band contains 60 channels with a width of 27 MHz.

Radio waves of the cantidad range, as well as radio waves of other TB ranges, propagate in free space in a straight line, like a beam of light. In the line-of-sight zone (i.e., in the absence of obstacles between the receiver and transmitter), the loss of signal energy is minimal. When the beam is “covered” by a building, trees or the Earth’s outlet, the practical reception is terminated. At radio waves shorter than about 5 cm, signal losses in marine sediments begin to show, especially in the river. In satellite systems for indication reception, waiting for two to three hours can greatly degrade or even “break” the image.

Service areas

The presence of line of sight determines the service area of ​​the transmitting TV station, or more precisely, the territory where the station provides reliable reception. Above the earth's surface, the line-of-sight distance, in general, depends on the height of the antennas and the terrain bluff. Above-level spherical earth surface, when the height of the transmitter antenna $H$ is much greater than the height of the approximate antenna, line-of-sight distance (see Fig. 2)

$$D \approx \sqrt{2RH} \approx 3,6 \cdot 10^3 \sqrt{H} \text{ (м)}$$

($R$ is the radius of the Earth).

The station's service area is limited to the visible horizon. At powerful TV stations, the antenna height usually does not exceed 300 m, so the radius of service areas does not exceed 70 km. To cover the entire country with television, a community network of numerous high-power and low-power (with a zone radius of several kilometers) stations was built. However, there are still settlements where, due to their isolation or the complexity of the terrain, there is no terrestrial television.

Signal strength

To estimate the signal power from the satellite, let us imagine that a disk passes through the center of the service area, perpendicular to the direction of wave arrival. The diameter of the disk $D$ will be considered approximately equal to the diameter of the service area. The antenana, as we have already said, radiates only 60% of the transmitter power $P$ in the area of ​​the radiation pattern. Therefore, per unit area of ​​the disk the power drops $p = 0,6P / (\pi D^2/4)$, and the receiving antenna with an aperture diameter $d$ loses power $p(\pi d^2/4)$. But you also need to take into account that the antenna extracts only 60% of the power incident on it. As a result, in the center of the zone the signal power will decrease in accordance with the radiation pattern of the transmitting antenna.

On the satellite, the power supply for the equipment comes from solar panels. Solar panels usually do not allow transmitter power to exceed 200 W. To illustrate the formula, assume $P = 100$ W, $D = 700$ km and $d = 1$ m. The calculation gives the signal power in the center of the zone $P_c \approx 0,7 \cdot 10^{-10}$ W, at the edge - $0,35 \times 10^{-10}$ W. Is it a lot or a little? This question can be answered only when the sensitivity of the receiver is known.

Receiver sensitivity

This characteristic of the receiver determines its ability to provide normal reception with minimal signal power. The sensitivity is higher, the smaller the signal value it is estimated. It can be shown that by increasing the gain, the sensitivity of the receiver can be improved indefinitely and made capable of receiving increasingly weaker signals. However, the sensitivity limit is set by noise. If the signal strength is insufficient, noise in the form of chaotic wandering lines and dots is visible on the TV screen. The image appears as if behind a veil of invoice. Normal reception of the present is only possible when the signal exceeds the noise to a certain extent.

The distortion of noise is twofold: one noise arises in the receiver itself, the other is received by the antenna from the surrounding space. In the receiver, noise arises from the chaotic thermal movement of electrons.

The set-top box is necessary to re-equip a signal from the centimeter range to the detmmeter range, to increase the sensitivity of the TV and to convert a frequency-modulated signal into an amplitude-modulated one. Satellites designed for individual TV reception serve “small” zones in order to obtain an acceptable signal level and, accordingly, a moderately priced receiving device with an antenna with a diameter of about 1 m. Five such zones will be created on the territory of the Soviet Union, from the fifth position in orbit. The program for the zones will be formed with cdw in time relative to Moscow.

For collective reception, large-diameter antennas and a highly sensitive amplifier are used. Thanks to the ability to receive weak signals, satellites can serve large areas. For example, the Soviet Ekran-M system transmits Central Television programs to a network of terrestrial transmitting stations in areas of Siberia and the Far East. The Moscow Global system ensures reception of Soviet television in the postal areas of all countries of the world. The antenna operating on centimeter waves has a diameter of 4 m!

Reception from Western satellites on the territory of the Soviet Union is difficult. This is not only due to low signal levels. These satellites use a different TV standard and some signals are encoded to allow free viewing. An interesting press report: by agreement with foreign companies, collective reception of Western TV satellites will be organized in Moscow via the existing cable network.
