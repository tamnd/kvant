The condition that a circle with center $C$ and radius $r$ intersects the sides of the square at 8 points is equivalent to the fact that the distance from $C$ to all vertices is greater than $r$, and to all sides is less than $r$.

It is clear that the desired set has the same 4 axes of symmetry as the square. Therefore, it is enough to find all the points of this set in one of the 8 angles into which these axes divide the plane. The easiest way to do this is using the coordinate method.

Let our square be given by the conditions $0 \leq x \leq 1$, $0 \leq y \leq 1$, and the center $C$ of a circle of radius $r$ have coordinates $(x; y)$ and lie in the triangle $0 \leq y \leq x \leq 1/2$

M1237. Let the point $O$ inside the triangle $ABC$ be such that $\overrightarrow{OK} + \overrightarrow{OM} + \overrightarrow{ON} = \overrightarrow{0}$, where $K, M, N$ are the redistribution bases dropped from $O$ to the sides $AB, BC, CA$ of the triangle. Prove the inequality

$$\frac{OK + OM + ON}{AB + BC + CA} \leq \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}.$$

Due to the condition on the point $O$, the segments $OK, OM, ON$ can be moved in parallel so that a triangle is formed (see figure). After rotation by $90^\circ$, the sides of this triangle will become parallel to the sides of triangle $ABC$, hence these triangles are similar. We denote the similarity coefficient by $k$: $k = OK/AB = OM/BC = ON/CA$. Then the left-hand side of the inequality is proved to be equal to $k$. On the other hand, representing the area $S$ of triangle $ABC$ as the sum of the areas of triangles $AOB, BOC$ and $COA$, we obtain

$$2S = a \cdot OK + b \cdot OM + c \cdot ON = k(a^2 + b^2 + c^2),$$

where $a, b, c$ is the length of side $\triangle ABC$. Thus, the problem reduces to proving the inequality

$$S \leq \frac{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}{4\sqrt{3}}.$$

Let us present one of the proofs of this fairly well-known inequality, using Heron’s formula and the inequality between the arithmetic mean and the geometric mean of three numbers (the letter $p$, as a general one, denotes the semiperimeter):

$$S = \sqrt{p(p - a)(p - b)(p - c)} \leq \leq \sqrt{p((p - a + p - b + p - c)/3)^3} = p^2/3\sqrt{3} \leq \leq (a^2 + b^2 + c^2)/4\sqrt{3}$$

M1238. The set of natural numbers is divided into two parts. One of them does not contain three-line arithmetic procedures. Is there necessarily an infinite arithmetic procedure in the other?

Answer: not necessarily. An arithmetic procedure from natural numbers is uniquely defined by the pair $(a, d)$, where $a$ is its first term, $d$ is the difference. Therefore, all such procedures can be confused. (The $(a, d)$ pairs are divided into groups with a constant sum $a + d$, and then numbered within each group, say, in ascending order $a$. This numbering method gives the following sequence of pairs: $(1,1); (1,2); (2,1); (1,3); (2,2); (3,1); (1,4), ...)$ Let $A_i, i = 1, 2, ...$ be the $i$th process in our numbering. Starting with any number $p_1$ from $A_1$, we will sequentially choose the numbers $p_2$ from $A_2$, $p_3$ from $A_3$, etc. so that $p_{i+1}$ is greater than $2p_i$. Then the set $P = \{p_1, p_2, ...\}$ does not contain three-Lenin arithmetic procedures, since $p_k - p_j > p_j - p_i$ for any $k > j > i$. At the same time, the set of natural numbers not included in $P$ does not contain a single infinite process, since at least one element of each such process is included in $P$.

F1248. There is a piece of chalk on a horizontal board. The chalkboard is given a horizontal speed $V_0$ and the board is also chalked after a time $t$ after the first point. The coefficient of friction between the chalk and the board is $\mu$. Find the length of the chalk mark on the board and the total displacement of the chalk relative to the board.

If in time $t$ the chalk manages to stop, then the length of its mark on the board is equal to

$$l_1 = \frac{V_0^2}{2\mu g},$$

and the total displacement relative to the board is

$$s_1 = 0.$$

If the time $t$ is less than the time $t = V_0/(\mu g)$ the chalk stops relative to the board, then by the time the board stops the speed of the chalk is equal to

$$v = \mu g\tau,$$

path length and offset are equal

$$l'_2 = s'_2 = V_0\tau - \frac{\mu g\tau^2}{2}.$$

After the board stops, the chalk will go in the opposite direction and cover the path

$$l''_2 = s''_2 = \frac{v^2}{2\mu g} = \frac{\mu g\tau^2}{2}.$$

Therefore, the total length of the chalk trace in this case is equal to

$$l_2 = l'_2 + l''_2 = V_0\tau,$$

and the total displacement is

$$s_2 = s'_2 - s''_2 = V_0\tau - \mu g\tau^2.$$

F1251. In the circuit shown in figure 1, after setting the currents, the keys are instantly transferred from position 1 to position 2. Assuming the coils are ideal, determine the amount of heat that will be released on the resistor $R$ after the inflection. Source emf $E$, internal resistance $r$.

Fig. 1.

After setting the currents, before throwing the key through the coils $L_1$ and $L_2$, the current flows

$$I = \mathcal{E} / r.$$

Since the coils are ideal (the ohmic resistance of their windings is zero), the voltage drop across the resistor $R$ and, therefore, the current through the resistor are zero. The instantaneous transfer of the key from position 1 to position 2 means that during the transfer the current in the coils practically does not have time to change.

After transferring the key, the current electrical circuit has the form shown in figure 2. The initial values ​​of the currents are equal to:

$$I_1(0) = I_2(0) = I = \mathcal{E} / r,$$

$$I_3(0) = 0.$$

From Ohm’s law for a closed loop $abcd$ (for an arbitrary moment of time $t$) we have

$$L_1 \frac{dI_1}{dt} = 0 \Rightarrow I_1 = \text{const.}$$

Since $I_1 = I_2 + I_3$, we get

$$\frac{dI_1}{dt} = \frac{dI_2}{dt} + \frac{dI_3}{dt} = 0.$$

Due to the presence of resistor $R$, the current $I_2$ will decrease, i.e., fade. In this case, the decrease in current $I_2$ will be numerically equal to the increase in current $I_3$ (since $dI_2 = -dI_3$). When current $I_2$ drops to zero, current $I_3$ will be equal to the initial value of current $I_2$ ($I_2(0) = \mathcal{E} / r$).

In the new established mode, currents flow in the circuit

$$I_1(\infty) = I_3(\infty) = \mathcal{E} / r, I_2(\infty) = 0.$$

F1252. A vertical spoke is moved in front of the display from left to right at a speed of $v_0 = 1$ m/s, while the inclined “shadow” of the spoke is not visible on the illuminated screen. Why does this happen? What is the angle of inclination of the “shadow” to the vertical? The electron beam will draw a full frame in a time of $t = 0,02$ s, take the number of lines in the frame equal to $n = 512$. Will the angle be the same if you move the knitting needle in front of a TV screen?

Under the influence of electrons hitting the screen, a fluorescent glows, covering the inner surface of the front part of the kinesack. This glow goes out almost instantly, but the eye (more precisely, the brain) retains the image for a fraction of a second. During this time, the electron beam manages to compress the weight of the screen, and the “shadow” on the screen is made up of points that are shielded from the eyes at different times.

Let the spoke cover some point on a given line. Then on the next line the spoke will obscure the point, mixed by a distance (horizontally) $l = v_0 t$, where $t$ is the time it takes the ray to travel through one line (in fact, this time is a little longer - after all, taking into account the mixing, the ray will travel a little more than the line). Reception frame size is equal to $20 \times 15$ cm. The beam passes through one line in a time of $0,02 \text{ с}/512 = 39$ μs. During this time, the knitting needle will shrink by 39 microns. The distance between the lines is $0,15 \text{ м}/512 = 0,29$ mm. Thus, the angle of the “shadow” with vertical is approximately equal to

$$\alpha = \arctg \frac{0,039}{0,29} \approx 7,7^\circ.$$

It can be seen that the mixing is small compared to the length of the string, so our approximation turns out to be reasonable.

The case with the telephone is slightly different from the case with the raster one. The fact is that in television they use the so-called cross-beam scanning, in which for $0,02$ s the beam will draw only odd lines of the frame, and then in the next $0,02$ s even lines will be drawn - between the odd ones. This is done in order to transmit 25 full frames per second (as in cinema), but draw them twice as often - and there is no “blinking” noticeable to the eye. This means that when calculating the angle, the number of lines in $0,02$ s should be taken equal to $512/2$.
