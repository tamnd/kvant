CAPDIDACI ABOUT LIGHT
AND COLOR

Captain of Pedagogical Sciences
S. TIKHOMIROV

In the new issue of the magazine for last year, we offered our readers several problems on the topic “Light Phenomena,” taken from fiction («Kvant», 1990, No. 11, p. 34). Today, as promised, we are returning to these tasks. Their discussion allows you to take a broader look at physical phenomena familiar to you from textbooks.

Let's start with the smooth spread of light and the formation of shadows. Why do the shadows lengthen in the evening? The reason for this is that the height of the Sun above the horizon changes during the day - it rises before noon, and decreases in the evening. Because of this, the length of the shadows changes, but “in the reverse order.” The writer I. Sokolov-Mikitov observed the sunrise in the tundra, when, when the sun's rays appeared, the shadows from the feet “stretched almost a kilometer.” (We hope that you were able to easily explain why in F. Iskander’s story the boy who determined the time by the shadow was late.)

In an episode from N. Gogol's story, you, of course, recognized the phenomenon of light passing through small branches. At the same time, on the screen (on the screen) it turned out to turn the image of the objects located in front of the answer. Based on this phenomenon, a device called a camera obscura, or “hole” camera, operates. It was invented in the middle of the 16th century and at one time was widely used for accurate natural sketches.

Pozt A. Prasolov wrote about the visible convergence of railway rails in the distance. This is a phenomenon, an illusion of vision. How can its occurrence be explained? The fact is that our visual perception does not always correctly reflect the true sizes of objects and the distances between them. For example, the further an object is from the observer, the smaller it appears. This is due to the fact that each size of an object depends on the angle from which it is viewed. This is clearly shown by figure 1. Observe, the eye whose eye is depicted on figure will evaluate the length of the same vertical stick differently depending on the distance to it, i.e. depending on its angular size. In the extreme left position it seems larger to him.

Fig. 1.

(And to us, reader, this stick seems larger than the others. What’s the matter? The point is in the thin lines on figure, which “set” the angular dimensions for us too. Here we should talk about the role that our brain plays in the perception of external images, in the “translation” of these images into our visual assessments. But this is a separate and rather complex topic that we will not touch on today)

Now it is not difficult to understand why the rails seem to converge in the distance: as you move away from the observer, the angular size of the distance between the rails (the angular size of the sleeper) decreases. The phenomenon of visible convergence of parallel lines in the distance is called perspective. Perspective perception of space was developed during the long evolution of vision in humans.

The writer V. Korolenko noted that during a solar eclipse, when only a narrow luminous crescent remained of the daylight, the shadows on the ground became “pale and unclear.” Why? The fact is that in low light the contrast color of the eye deteriorates, i.e. the eye is less able to distinguish the boundaries between areas with different illumination.

Let us also discuss what forms the shadows of various objects take during a partial eclipse of the Sun. You've probably noticed that on a sunny day small objects, regardless of their shape (butterfly, bird, ball), have the shape of a circle. This is a shadow image of the Sun. In an excerpt from G. Haggard’s novel “King Solomon’s Mines,” you, of course, recognized the picture of a lunar eclipse. The writer noticed that during an eclipse the Moon takes on a copper-red hue. This phenomenon can be explained from a physics point of view. The moon, as we know, shines with reflected sunlight; An eclipse occurs when the Earth “blocks” the Moon from the Sun. But, as careful research shows, even during an eclipse, the Moon is illuminated by the Sun: due to refraction in the lower layers of the Earth’s atmosphere, the sun’s rays are bent, as if “bending” the Earth and hitting the Moon, so that a clear geometric shadow of the Earth on the Moon is not obtained. Physicists say that blue colors are dissipated to a greater extent in the atmosphere. (This phenomenon, by the way, is explained by the head of color.) In the transmitted light, yellow-red colors will remain. The more exposed the rays of light are, the more they lose their blue colors and the more intense the red. (Remember how red the Sun and Moon are at the horizon.) And during an eclipse of the Moon, light rays pass through the earth’s atmosphere twice: to the Moon and back to us on Earth.

day. Many small sparks dry up, go out and light up again. How to explain this praised by F. Totchev?
There is always excitement, ripples, and small whirlpools on the surface of the water.
The water surface can be represented as a collection of wavy and convex mirrors. Concave surfaces focus the sun's rays, so many bright sparkles appear on the water (Fig. 4). The waves on the surface mix all the time, so the sparks dry up and go out.
The world that Ichthyander, the “Amphibian Man,” saw from under the water is difficult to imagine. Physics explains such types by the phenomenon of total internal reflection.
Remember: when a ray of light passes from an optically denser medium to a less dense one, it is refracted and deviates more from the vertical. At figure 5 you can follow the path of rays passing from water to air. When the angle of incidence is approximately 48.5°, the beam will not come out of the water at all. This is the limiting angle for water. For all angles of incidence greater than the limiting one, the beam will not come out from under the water, but will be reflected from its surface, as from a mirror. The beam is said to undergo total internal reflection. The correspondence and the limiting angle are called the angle of total internal reflection.
How is the water surface seen from the depths? It appears in the shape of a cone or funnel, with you at the top. The cones are inclined to each other at an angle equal to two extreme angles (97°). The entire surface world will be contained within the inner cone, albeit in a distorted form. Beyond the edges of the cone lies a shiny surface of water, in which, like in a mirror, the underwater world is reflected.
Now let’s figure out how a fisherman appears from the water when he enters the water.
The fisherman's feet are visible directly in the water. But besides this, there is also a reflection of the legs from the mirror surface of the water (Fig. 6). The upper part of the fisherman's body is visible through the water-air boundary. Here the underwater ray of vision is observed to be refracted. And what does he see? The fisherman's upper body sees in the air without legs! This is the image that appears on the retina of the eye.
Now let's look at why the edges of the cone are surrounded by multi-colored borders. White sunlight is a “mixture” of rays of different colors. Each rod of rays has its own refractive index, and therefore its own limiting angle. That's why the edges of the cone have a rainbow color.
