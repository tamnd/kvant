A good elastic rope or wire does not have sharp corners. Therefore, we will study only *smooth* curves (without any kinks) drawn on the earth's surface. Such curves are allowed to have any self-intersection point.

There are also too many smooth curves. It is impossible to count them all. The only way to understand our question is to agree to consider curves that are “not very much” different from each other to be the same.

Types of curves

A curve is a popular mathematical object that has many interesting characteristics: curvature, length, number of self-intersection points, inflection points, etc. All of them are worth studying. (Some of them are described in S. Tabachnikov’s article “On Plane Curves” in “Kvanit” No. 11, 1988.) Which ones are important for us? Maybe the length? But there are still too many curves of the same length. Consider curves that have the same curvature to be identical? Then there will be more different curves than functions - a lot... In order not to guess anymore, we will talk about all the characteristics of the curve at once.

We will understand the expression “the curves will not differ very much from each other” literally and consider the same curves that differ by “small movement”. Now we will have to consider any two curves the same, which can be deformed (strapped) into each other so that they remain smooth all the time (Fig. 1). After all, deformation can also be broken down into a series of “small movements.” We will call such curves curves-

We have discarded all visible differences between the curves. It is natural to assume that with such a naive agreement, all curves are of the same type. For non-closed curves this is true. Let's imagine a rope lying on the ground, starting to straighten at one end. Such a rope will smoothly unfold into a straight line (Fig. 2). So, it is interesting to consider only closed curves.

Now everything is ready to form a rigorous mathematical question:

How many different types of closed curves are there on earth?

This question has many variations and additions, leading us to a very popular area of ​​modern mathematics. This will be decided ahead, but for now let’s consider the Earth flat.

There are infinitely many different types of closed curves on the plane.

The surface of the Earth is a sphere. How many curves are there on it? A sphere is a plane with one point (Fig. 4). Figure 4 is called stereographic projection. Let's make a stereographic projection from a point not lying on the curve. Then this curve will fall on the plane. Does this mean there are as many types of curves on a sphere as there are on a plane? Yes, we are not far from those who truly believe the Earth is flat. Here is the correct answer.

There are exactly two different types of closed curves on a sphere.

We start the proof with the picture (Fig. 5). As you can see, the number of revolutions is no longer maintained. This is what distinguishes curves on a sphere from curves on a plane. Convex

Among all smooth curves on the plane, there is a class of curves that do not have inflection points. We will call such curves convex. A small neighborhood of any point on a convex curve lies on one side of its tangent and can intersect with any line at most two points (Fig. 7).

The concept of convexity is easily and naturally generalized to the sphere. We will call large neighborhoods (equators) “straight lines” on the sphere. A curve on a sphere is called convex at a given point if the neighborhood of the curve at this point lies on one side of the tangent equator (Fig. 8).

Convex curves occur in nature when a charged part rushes across magnetic field lines. According to the rules of the gimlet, it will twist in the same direction all the time (Fig. 9).

Curious mathematicians, of course, might have missed the question:

Theorem. There are exactly three different types of closed convex curves on the sphere (Fig.10).

This remarkable result was published by the American mathematician J. Little*) in 1970 in the journal on differential geometry. It remains a mystery how this interesting geometric question could have escaped the attention of the classics back in the last century.

Curves and Equations

In conclusion, let's talk about the connection between curves and differential equations. Recall that the curve lies on the sphere, and the sphere is in three-dimensional space. As before, let a point move along the curve and cover the entire curve in some time. Since at each moment of time the radius vector $\vec{r}(t)$ drawn from the center is perpendicular to the sphere, and the velocity vector $\vec{r}(t)$ touches the curve, and therefore the sphere. These vectors cannot be collinear. If the curve is convex, then the acceleration vector $\vec{r}(t)$ does not lie in the plane, it is spanned by the vectors $\vec{r}(t), \vec{r}(t)$, since the projection of acceleration onto the tangent plane to the sphere is directed towards the “concavity” of the curve and is not collinear $\vec{r}(t)$ (Fig. 14). Therefore, the radius vector, velocity and acceleration are three independent vectors, i.e. any vector can be expanded into them.

Consider the vector $\vec{r}(t)$, i.e., a vector whose coordinates are the product of the coordinates $\vec{r}(t)$: if $\vec{r}(t) = (r_1(t); r_2(t); r_3(t))$, then $\vec{r}(t) = (\vec{r}_1(t); \vec{r}_2(t); \vec{r}_3(t))$.

Let us expand the vector $\vec{r}(t)$ by $\vec{r}(t), \dot{\vec{r}}(t), \ddot{\vec{r}}(t)$ (of course, the expansion coefficients depend on time):

$$\vec{r}(t) = a(t)\vec{r}(t) + b(t)\dot{\vec{r}}(t) + c(t)\vec{r}(t).$$

But then what coordinate $r_i(t)$ ($i=1, 2, 3$) is a function satisfying the differential equation

$$\ddot{r}_i(t) = a(t)\ddot{r}_i(t) + b(t)\dot{r}_i(t) + c(t)r_i(t).$$

So, what convex curve on the sphere have we matched with the equation. Perhaps some readers will find similarities between this construction and the reasoning of the Middle Age thinker Denogardus (see the article “On the Great Denogardus Number and Hooke’s Law,” published in No. 8, 1989) about the movement of celestial bodies.

Another important note: a closed curve corresponds to an equation in which all coefficients and all solutions are periodic functions.

One final note.

We have seen that the curve $a$) on figure 9 is from the curves $b$) and $c$). How does this affect the properties of the corresponding equation?

Let the point pass along the curve again. Any equation equation is the coordinate of that point as a function of time (i.e., the PROJECTION of the point onto a straight line emanating from the center). The distance the equator is perpendicular to a given axis. The intersections of the equator with the curve are the points at which the coordinate becomes zero. There are no more than two. Therefore, equations for a period (i.e., when $t$ changes from 0 to $T$) cannot have more than two zeros. We have obtained a very important class of equations, which mathematicians call in the elegant word neo-oscellular equations.
