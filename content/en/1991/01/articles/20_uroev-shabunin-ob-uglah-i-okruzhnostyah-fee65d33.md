About angles
and circles

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. LEVEL,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
M. SHABUNIN

...Or a triangle in a semicircle field,
But not a straightforward one to draw.
Dante. Divine Comedy. Paradise.

The triangle that Dante writes about
cannot be built (Fig. 1). This fact
the author of the immortal pose leads
along with other scientific truths, which, apparently, were considered generally known among educated contemporaries. Here it is appropriate to recall the years of life of the great Dante Aligera - 1265-1321, and also to note that Dante was not interested in mathematics.
He was inspired and went down in history as a poet and philosopher, the creator of the Alliance literary language.

Our article will discuss both triangles written “in the field of a semicircle” and other configurations that are necessary to study.

Theorem 1 (about the inscribed angle).
An inscribed angle is equal to half the central angle subtended by the same arc.

Often uses an equivalent formulation of Theorem 1:

Let α be the central angle subtended by the arc AnB of the given circle (Fig. 2). Then from the points belonging to the arc AnB, the chord AB is visible at an angle α/2.

Note that, by virtue of this theorem, the angle subtended by the diameter is a right angle, therefore a triangle inscribed “in the field of a semicircle” is figuratively rectangular. This is convenient to use, for example, in the following problem.

Problem 1. A circle $C_1$ is constructed on the segment AB as a diameter, and BD is its chord. Circle $C_2$ touches $C_1$ at point A and line segment BD at point K. Prove that AK is the bisserrisis of angle DAB (Fig. 3).

Solution. The center O of circle $C_2$ lies on the diameter AB of circle $C_1$. The OK radius drawn to the point of contact has been redistributed by BD. Angle ADB is right because it rests on the diameter, so segments AD and OK are parallel, and angles DAK and AKO are equal, lying crosswise. In an isosceles triangle AOK, angles AKO and KAO are equal, therefore AK bisects angle DAB.

However, more often one comes to use a simple corollary of Theorem 1:

Angles inscribed in a circle and resting on the same arc or on equal arcs are equal.

The general principle of the approach to solving problems in which circles are involved is this: look for all kinds of inscribed angles, for which purpose draw various chords. The more equal inscribed angles we find, the clearer the path leading to the solution will be visible.

Problem 2. In a quadrilateral ABCD inscribed in a circle, through vertices A, B and the intersection point P of the diagonal, draw a circle intersecting side BC at point E. Prove that if AB=AD, then CD=CE (Fig. 4).

Solution. Let's draw the chord AE and find equal inscribed angles. Such angles in one circle will be DAC and DBC, and in the other - PAE and PBE. Therefore, $\angle DAC = \angle CAE$. In addition, DCA and ACB based on equal angles are equal. This implies the equality of triangles DCA and ECA and, accordingly, their sides DC and EC.

Exercise 1. Prove the following theorem about chords. Let the chords AB and CD of a given circle intersect at a point M. Then $AM \cdot MB = CM \cdot MD$. (This product, independent of the choice of chord, is called the degree of the point M with respect to the given circle.)

A formula for the size of an angle with a vertex outside or inside a circle is often useful.

Right 2. a) Let an angle with vertex $A$ lying outside the circle cut arcs $BmC$ and $DnE$ (Fig. 5) on it. Prove that in this case the angle is equal to half the difference between the central angles subtended by these arcs, i.e. $\angle DAE = \frac{1}{2}(\angle DOE - \angle BOC)$.

6) Formulate and prove a similar statement on your own for an angle with a vertex inside a circle.

Theorem 1 is proven in a school course. We will need a significantly more general theorem.

Theorem 2. The locus of points from which a given segment $AB$ is visible at some angle $\alpha$ consists of two arcs of circles symmetrical to the arcs (Fig. 6).

Proof. The fact that from any point lying on one of the arcs, the segment $AB$ is visible at an angle $\alpha$,

$$\angle APB = 180^\circ - \left( \frac{1}{2} \angle CAB + \frac{1}{2} \angle CBA \right) = 180^\circ - \frac{1}{2} (180^\circ - \angle ACB) = 90^\circ + \frac{1}{2} \angle ACB.$$

The magnitude of the angle $ACB$ does not change (according to Theorem 1), therefore the magnitude of the angle $APB$ is also constant. In this case, according to Theorem 2, the point $P$ completely runs through the arcs of the circle passing through the points $A$ and $P$. Afterwards it will be established that the center of this circle is the point $Q$ - the middle of the arc $AB$.

Theorem 2 allows us to find out whether any set of points belongs to the same circle.

From Theorem 2, in particular, an important corollary follows:

The vertex of a right triangle lies on a circle built on its hypotenuse as its diameter.

Right 4. Diameters $AB$ and $CD$ of a circle with radius $R$ intersect at an angle $\alpha$. From an arbitrary point $M$ of the circle, perediculars $MP$ and $MQ$ are dropped onto these diameters. Prove that the length of the segment $PQ$ does not depend on the height of the point $M$, and find $PQ$.
