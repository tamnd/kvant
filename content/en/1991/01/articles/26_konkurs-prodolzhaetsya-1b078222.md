In the October issue last year, our magazine announced the beginning of the Soviet-American competition for young lovers of astronautics and astronautics.

The initiators of the competition were the magazine «Kvant» (USSR) and “Quantum” (USA-USSR), the All-Russian Youth Aerospace Society (VAKO) “Soyuz” and the International Educational Network (USA).

The winners of the competition will take part in Soviet-American schools in the summer of New Year. One of the schools will be held in the USA, the other (in the same place) in the USSR (in the pioneer camp “Orlyonok” on the Black Sea).

A few words about type I.

We have received hundreds of letters with solutions to assignments. And although not all participants coped with them, the editors hope that time was not wasted on the right.

Let’s make a reservation right away - both in the first type and in subsequent ones, the main thing for us is not the scrupulousness of the calculations performed (although this is also highly valued), but the ability to grasp the essence of the problem, make the necessary assumptions and then carry out the calculations.

When solving the first problem, some of the participants thought that the probe initially flies with a root equal to the root of free fall on Mars. Correct answer: the probe will move 12.5 kilometers away.

In the second problem, some of the competition participants (obviously, primarily from among regular readers «Kvant») noticed that beacons can be located on a circular arc with a radius of 15 m. We congratulate those who paid attention to this and carried out research on these sluai. Well, for the case when the beacons are located at the same perpendicular to the middle of the walls of the base (as most readers understand), the answer is this: the volume of the lunar base is 4.5 · 10⁴ m³.

To answer the third problem, it was not at all necessary to read A. Clark’s novel. We were interested in getting to know your Odissen proposals. Some participants did not limit themselves to forecasts, but even sent entire stories.

In this issue we offer you type II tasks. The deadline for responding decisions is no later than March 15, 1991. Our address: 103006, Moscow K-6, st. Gorky, 32/1, magazine «Kvant» (Competition “KQ-91”).

Attention! We ask you to indicate in your letter not only the school number and class, but also the degree of English language proficiency, since when selecting a school that will take place in the USA, the knowledge of the English language (to the extent that allows you to listen to lectures and participate in classes) is mandatory. We remind you that, unfortunately, senior schoolchildren will not be able to attend the precinct at school.

So,

Type II tasks

Problem 1. The space station moves around the Earth in an equatorial circular orbit, the altitude of which is $H = 3000$ km. Estimate how long the “night” lasts on board the station, i.e. the station is in the shadow of the Earth.

Problem 2. From observations it is known that under terrestrial conditions a watermelon does not split if it is dropped from a height of $h \leq 30$ cm (counting from its lowest point). From what height can you drop watermelons on the moon in such a situation? The acceleration of gravity on the Moon is 6 times less than on Earth.

Task 3. To study the upper layers of the atmosphere of Venus in the Veta project, a balloon probe was used.

Determine the volume of the probe if the pressure at the heights under study is $p = 50000$ Pa, and the temperature is $t = 10^\circ\text{C}$. We can assume that the atmosphere of Venus consists entirely of two-issed carbon $\text{CO}_2$, and the probe is filled with He gel. Take the mass of the probe together with the apparatus equal to $m = 20$ kg.
