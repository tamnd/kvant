Flushing away the big question
Curious mathematicians, of course, might have missed the question:
How many different types of closed convex curves on a sphere are there?
Indeed, for mathematicians it turned out to be much more important than the question of curves without the convexity condition. Interest in them and its various generalizations has unexpectedly jumped over the past two years...

Fig. 10.

The convexity requirement is very significant and changes the answer completely. What is surprising, however, is not this, but the fact that this answer was received only twenty years ago.

Theorem. There are exactly three different types of closed convex curves on the sphere (Fig.10).

This remarkable result was published by the American mathematician J. Little*) in 1970 in the journal on differential geometry. It remains a mystery how this interesting geometric question could have escaped the attention of the classics back in the last century.

Let's compare figure 10 and 6. At first glance, an additional curve b) has been added to curves a) and b). We will see that in reality curve a) has “split off” from other curves with an odd number of revolutions into an important independent class.

*) His last name is spelled Little (in English - small).

Curve a) cannot be drawn into a curve with self-intersection points; any equator intersects it no more than twice.

Before we get serious about the proof, let's talk a little about the motivations. Why is it useful to study convex curves? Firstly, the question we are considering is one of those rare geometric questions that, thanks to their simplicity and clarity, turn mathematics into a beautiful science, and this embodiment is sufficient for it to be interesting in itself. Second, a curve is the simplest geometric object and is related to many other important mathematical concepts. Therefore, any reasonable question about curves must have some application. This is precisely the situation with the question of the convexity of curves on a sphere. We'll talk about this a little more later.
