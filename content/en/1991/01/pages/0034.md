What can be said about the shadow of bodies that are rather large? They will also change during the eclipse. Any extended object can be represented as a sum of small bodies. The shadow of each small body is crescent-shaped, and the shadow of a large body will consist of many sickles.

In an excerpt from G. Haggard’s novel “King Solomon’s Mines,” you, of course, recognized the picture of a lunar eclipse. The writer noticed that during an eclipse the Moon takes on a copper-red hue. This phenomenon can be explained from a physics point of view. The moon, as we know, shines with reflected sunlight; An eclipse occurs when the Earth “blocks” the Moon from the Sun. But, as careful research shows, even during an eclipse, the Moon is illuminated by the Sun: due to refraction in the lower layers of the Earth’s atmosphere, the sun’s rays are bent, as if “bending” the Earth and hitting the Moon, so that a clear geometric shadow of the Earth on the Moon is not obtained. However, in the earthly air the blue colors are “lost” from the white rays of the sun - like

Physicists say that blue colors are dissipated to a greater extent in the atmosphere. (This phenomenon, by the way, is explained by the head of color.) In the transmitted light, yellow-red colors will remain. The more exposed the rays of light are, the more they lose their blue colors and the more intense the red. (Remember how red the Sun and Moon are at the horizon.) And during an eclipse of the Moon, light rays pass through the earth’s atmosphere twice: to the Moon and back to us on Earth.
