In 1932, the composition of atomic nuclei was established: the nucleus of an atom consists of positively charged particles - protons and electrically neutral particles - neutrons, the mass of which is almost equal to the mass of protons. Consequently, the atomic number of an element (formerly the atomic number) is the number of protons in its nucleus and, accordingly, the number of electrons in the electron shell of its atom.

By this time, 92 cells in the periodic table were already filled - from hydrogen to uranium, although 4 cells were still empty. Four elements could not be detected either in the earth's crust, or in water, or in the atmosphere. True, the generosity of nature was reflected in the fact that many cells of the table turned out to be “escaped” not for one atom, but for many - for isotopes of the same chemical element, so that there were many more different atoms than 88 and even 92. But couldn’t chemical elements exist outside the periodic table - to the left of the beginning or to the right of its end?

To the left of the beginning of the table. Back in 1920, E. Rutherford suggested that there might be an element with number zero. And when the neutron was discovered in 1932, it became clear that it was the one suitable for the role of the “inhabitant” of the zero cell of the table. This is indeed a chemical element with zero atomic number, although even in the most modern tables it is not customary to draw a zero cell with a neutron in it.

It seemed that merging the table “to the left” was over - there seemed to be nowhere to go to the left of zero.

To the right of the end of the table. After the discovery of the neutron and the creation of the first neutron sources, they began to be used for irradiation of various

substances and studying the origins of nuclear reactions.

It turned out, for example, that very often reactions like this occur:

$$\text{Na} + \frac{1}{2} \text{n} \rightarrow \text{Na} + \gamma.$$

This is the so-called radiation capture of a neutron - a neutron is “captured” by a sodium nucleus with the emission (radius) of a gamma quantum. But the isotope $$\text{Na}$$ is radioactive and with a period of $T \approx 14$ hours decays with the emission of a $\beta$-particle (electron $-_{0}e$):

$$\text{Na} \rightarrow \text{Na} + \beta.$$ (*)

Thus, the end result of neutron irradiation is that sodium is converted into its neighbor on the right - magnesium.

Similar reactions are observed for many elements. I call this E. Fermi’s happy idea - to set up an experiment with neutron irradiation
