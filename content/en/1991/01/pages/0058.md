A formula for the size of an angle with a vertex outside or inside a circle is often useful.

Right 2. a) Let an angle with vertex $A$ lying outside the circle cut arcs $BmC$ and $DnE$ (Fig. 5) on it. Prove that in this case the angle is equal to half the difference between the central angles subtended by these arcs, i.e. $\angle DAE = \frac{1}{2}(\angle DOE - \angle BOC)$.

6) Formulate and prove a similar statement on your own for an angle with a vertex inside a circle.

Theorem 1 is proven in a school course. We will need a significantly more general theorem.

Theorem 2. The locus of points from which a given segment $AB$ is visible at some angle $\alpha$ consists of two arcs of circles symmetrical to the arcs (Fig. 6).

Proof. The fact that from any point lying on one of the arcs, the segment $AB$ is visible at an angle $\alpha$,

$$\angle APB = 180^\circ - \left( \frac{1}{2} \angle CAB + \frac{1}{2} \angle CBA \right) = 180^\circ - \frac{1}{2} (180^\circ - \angle ACB) = 90^\circ + \frac{1}{2} \angle ACB.$$

The magnitude of the angle $ACB$ does not change (according to Theorem 1), therefore the magnitude of the angle $APB$ is also constant. In this case, according to Theorem 2, the point $P$ completely runs through the arcs of the circle passing through the points $A$ and $P$. Afterwards it will be established that the center of this circle is the point $Q$ - the middle of the arc $AB$.

Theorem 2 allows us to find out whether any set of points belongs to the same circle.

From Theorem 2, in particular, an important corollary follows:

The vertex of a right triangle lies on a circle built on its hypotenuse as its diameter.

Right 4. Diameters $AB$ and $CD$ of a circle with radius $R$ intersect at an angle $\alpha$. From an arbitrary point $M$ of the circle, perediculars $MP$ and $MQ$ are dropped onto these diameters. Prove that the length of the segment $PQ$ does not depend on the height of the point $M$, and find $PQ$.
