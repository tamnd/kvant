A convex non-self-intersecting curve on a sphere lies on a hemisphere, that is, entirely on one side of a certain screen.

This simple statement was proven back in the 20s. First of all, let's show its meaning. The statement means that the curve $a$) lies on some hemisphere. Such a curve is no different from a curve on a plane. Indeed, there is a simple way to convert a curve on a hemisphere into a flat one - to project it from the center of the sphere onto a plane (Fig. 12). In this case, in contrast to stereographic projection, diameters (more precisely, half-diameters belonging to the hemisphere) turn into straight lines on the plane. Therefore, a convex curve on a hemisphere transforms into a convex curve on a plane. Curve $a$) on figure 10 for any deformation is forced to remain on the hemisphere (it does not matter that the hemisphere can change; in this case, the point of application of the plane on which the projection occurs will “roll” over the sphere). A curve on a plane cannot transform into a curve with a larger number of revolutions. Therefore, the curve $a$) cannot transform into the curve $\theta$).

To prove the statement, we draw a tangent equator to any point on the curve. A convex curve without self-intersections cannot intersect this equator at another point (and, therefore, lies in relation to it on one of the hemispheres). In fact, if we allow such an intersection, then there is a point on the curve at which the curve is convex in the opposite direction (Fig. 13). This means that the curve also has a couple of bends.
