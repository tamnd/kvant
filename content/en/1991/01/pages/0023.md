M1238. The set of natural numbers is divided into two parts. One of them does not contain three-line arithmetic procedures. Is there necessarily an infinite arithmetic procedure in the other?

Answer: not necessarily. An arithmetic procedure from natural numbers is uniquely defined by the pair $(a, d)$, where $a$ is its first term, $d$ is the difference. Therefore, all such procedures can be confused. (The $(a, d)$ pairs are divided into groups with a constant sum $a + d$, and then numbered within each group, say, in ascending order $a$. This numbering method gives the following sequence of pairs: $(1,1); (1,2); (2,1); (1,3); (2,2); (3,1); (1,4), ...)$ Let $A_i, i = 1, 2, ...$ be the $i$th process in our numbering. Starting with any number $p_1$ from $A_1$, we will sequentially choose the numbers $p_2$ from $A_2$, $p_3$ from $A_3$, etc. so that $p_{i+1}$ is greater than $2p_i$. Then the set $P = \{p_1, p_2, ...\}$ does not contain three-Lenin arithmetic procedures, since $p_k - p_j > p_j - p_i$ for any $k > j > i$. At the same time, the set of natural numbers not included in $P$ does not contain a single infinite process, since at least one element of each such process is included in $P$.
