And yet she believes...

The surface of the Earth is a sphere. How many curves are there on it? A sphere is a plane with one point (Fig. 4). Figure 4 is called stereographic projection. Let's make a stereographic projection from a point not lying on the curve. Then this curve will fall on the plane. Does this mean there are as many types of curves on a sphere as there are on a plane? Yes, we are not far from those who truly believe the Earth is flat. Here is the correct answer.

There are exactly two different types of closed curves on a sphere.

We start the proof with the picture (Fig. 5). As you can see, the number of revolutions is no longer maintained. This is what distinguishes curves on a sphere from curves on a plane. Having “turned around” the sphere, the curve has lost two turns. Now it’s easy to perform the same operation on a curve with any number of turns (you just need to add a few loops anywhere on the curves at figure 5). We found that any curve can be deformed into one of the curves on figure 6. Which one depends on the parity of the number of revolutions.

But how to prove that curves a) and b) are of different types not only on the plane, but also on the sphere? Indeed, strictly speaking, the number of revolutions in this case is not determined at all. The parity of the number of self-intersections, which is already familiar to us, helps out. For curve b) this number is odd, and for curve a) it is even (equal to zero).

Convex

Among all smooth curves on the plane, there is a class of curves that do not have inflection points. We will call such curves convex. A small neighborhood of any point on a convex curve lies on one side of its tangent and can intersect with any line at most two points (Fig. 7).

The concept of convexity is easily and naturally generalized to the sphere. We will call large neighborhoods (equators) “straight lines” on the sphere. A curve on a sphere is called convex at a given point if the neighborhood of the curve at this point lies on one side of the tangent equator (Fig. 8).

Convex curves occur in nature when a charged part rushes across magnetic field lines. According to the rules of the gimlet, it will twist in the same direction all the time (Fig. 9).
