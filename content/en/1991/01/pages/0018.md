Curves and Equations

In conclusion, let's talk about the connection between curves and differential equations. Recall that the curve lies on the sphere, and the sphere is in three-dimensional space. As before, let a point move along the curve and cover the entire curve in some time. Since at each moment of time the radius vector $\vec{r}(t)$ drawn from the center is perpendicular to the sphere, and the velocity vector $\vec{r}(t)$ touches the curve, and therefore the sphere. These vectors cannot be collinear. If the curve is convex, then the acceleration vector $\vec{r}(t)$ does not lie in the plane, it is spanned by the vectors $\vec{r}(t), \vec{r}(t)$, since the projection of acceleration onto the tangent plane to the sphere is directed towards the “concavity” of the curve and is not collinear $\vec{r}(t)$ (Fig. 14). Therefore, the radius vector, velocity and acceleration are three independent vectors, i.e. any vector can be expanded into them.

Consider the vector $\vec{r}(t)$, i.e., a vector whose coordinates are the product of the coordinates $\vec{r}(t)$: if $\vec{r}(t) = (r_1(t); r_2(t); r_3(t))$, then $\vec{r}(t) = (\vec{r}_1(t); \vec{r}_2(t); \vec{r}_3(t))$.

Let us expand the vector $\vec{r}(t)$ by $\vec{r}(t), \dot{\vec{r}}(t), \ddot{\vec{r}}(t)$ (of course, the expansion coefficients depend on time):

$$\vec{r}(t) = a(t)\vec{r}(t) + b(t)\dot{\vec{r}}(t) + c(t)\vec{r}(t).$$

But then what coordinate $r_i(t)$ ($i=1, 2, 3$) is a function satisfying the differential equation

$$\ddot{r}_i(t) = a(t)\ddot{r}_i(t) + b(t)\dot{r}_i(t) + c(t)r_i(t).$$

So, what convex curve on the sphere have we matched with the equation. Perhaps some readers will find similarities between this construction and the reasoning of the Middle Age thinker Denogardus (see the article “On the Great Denogardus Number and Hooke’s Law,” published in No. 8, 1989) about the movement of celestial bodies.

Another important note: a closed curve corresponds to an equation in which all coefficients and all solutions are periodic functions.
