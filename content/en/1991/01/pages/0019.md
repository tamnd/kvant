1) The equation $\ddot{r}(t) = -r(t)$ has a periodic solution $r_1(t) = 1, r_2(t) = \sin t, r_3(t) = \cos t$.

2) The equation $\ddot{r}(t) = -4\dot{r}(t)$ has a periodic solution $r_1(t) = 1, r_2(t) = \sin 2t, r_3(t) = \cos 2t$.

3) The equation $\ddot{r}(t) = 0$ has solutions $r_1(t) = 1, r_2(t) = t, r_3(t) = t^2$, of which only one is periodic. This equation does not suit us.

Equation 1) corresponds to the curve at figure 15, $a$ (the intersection of a sphere and a plane), i.e., to the curve $a$) at figure 9. A point on the curve moves uniformly at a constant speed and will move through the entire CIRCLE IN TIME $T = 2\pi$. Equation 2) is of the other type. At first glance, it follows the same curve. But during the time $T = 2\pi$ the point passes through the curve twice, that is, it makes two revolutions (see Fig. 15, 6). With a “small movement” this “twice GERET curve” goes into the curve $b$) at figure 5, 6.

We consider equations to be identical if they can be converted into each other by changing their coefficients, but in such a way that the solutions remain periodic.

How many different equations of order 3 with periodic solutions are there?

This question turns out to be a reformulation of the question about convex curves.

there are three on the sphere, and the answer to it is given by Little’s theorem: there are three of them. The first two equations are already indicated in examples 1) and 2).

Exercise 4. Find the third.

Note. Come up with an equation corresponding to a circle drawn three times.

Using the example of the connection between curves and equations, we illustrated only one of the applications of Little's theorem. The following exercises are for those who find this application interesting.

Exercise 5. How many different equations of order 2 with periodic solutions are there?

Answer. Infinite number.

Note. Think about curves on a plane.

One final note.

We have seen that the curve $a$) on figure 9 is from the curves $b$) and $c$). How does this affect the properties of the corresponding equation?

Let the point pass along the curve again. Any equation equation is the coordinate of that point as a function of time (i.e., the PROJECTION of the point onto a straight line emanating from the center). The distance the equator is perpendicular to a given axis. The intersections of the equator with the curve are the points at which the coordinate becomes zero. There are no more than two. Therefore, equations for a period (i.e., when $t$ changes from 0 to $T$) cannot have more than two zeros. We have obtained a very important class of equations, which mathematicians call in the elegant word neo-oscellular equations.

If we started this article with questions about equations of order 3, then probably many professional mathematicians would find it difficult to answer them and even indicate a way to solve them. As we can see, a toy question about curves can lead to the most server-side consequences.
