Finally, let's prove (3). Note that among $\Pi_n$ “polyline, arrangement” pairs, the first link of the polyline is necessarily horizontal (otherwise there will be no asterisks below the polyline in the first line). By removing the first column, as well as the row containing the asterisk it contains, we obtain one of the $\Pi_{n-1}$ “polyline, arrangement” pairs in the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ (Fig. 3) table. Conversely, by marking any of the $2n-1$ vertices with such a broken line and replacing it with a vertical link and a new line starting with an asterisk, we uniquely restore the pair “arrangement, broken line” in the table $n \times n$. This implies equality (3).

The second proof explains why the answer column is obtained in this problem, but requires familiarity with some concepts of probability theory, which we will not explain in detail here.

First, we describe the rule that assigns a pair of “polyline, arrangement” to an arbitrary set of $n$ points in the square $K = \{(x; y): 0 \leqslant x, y \leqslant 1\}$, all $2n$ of which coordinates are different.

Points are numbered from left to right. Then all $2n$ coordinates of the point are arranged in ascending order, the abscissa of the $i$th point is replaced by the symbol $X_i$, and the ordinate by $Y_i$. The result is a sequence of symbols $X_1, \dots, X_n$ and $Y_1, \dots, Y_n$, in which the symbols are in ascending order of indices. Now we will move along the resulting sequence from left to right and at the same time we will draw a broken line, starting from the point $A$, and put asterisks: if the next symbol of the sequence is $X_i$, then we take a step to the right (horizontal link of the broken line), if it is a symbol $Y_j$, then we take a step down (vertical link) and in the line that we intersect, we put an asterisk on $j$th place, i.e. at the intersection of column $X_j$ and row $Y_j$. (This entire procedure is illustrated on figure 4.) In order for this asterisk to be under the broken line, it is necessary and sufficient that the link $Y_j$ be to the right of the asterisk, i.e., so that by the time this link is carried out, the broken line has taken at least $j$ steps to the right $(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_j)$. In other words, in our sequence the letter $Y_j$ should appear to the right of $X_j$. This means that the ordinate of the $j$th point must be greater than its abscissa. If we want the stars to fall under the broken line, then the last condition must be satisfied for all points: at figure 5, where the ordinate axis is directed downward, all points must be under the x=y diagonal of the square $K$.

Now let’s assume that the points in the square $K$ are chosen randomly, i.e., according to strict terminology, each of their $2n$ coordinates is uniformly distributed on the segment [0; 1] and does not depend on other coordinates (the probability of coordinates matching is 0). Then all possible orders of coordinates on [0; 1] will be equally reliable. Consequently, all corresponding sequences of symbols $X_i$ and $Y_j$ are equally probable, i.e., all “broken, arrangement” pairs.
