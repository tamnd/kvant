This experience is described by many historians of science, who at the same time explicitly or implicitly confirm the validity of Franklin’s conclusion. Unfortunately, Addenbrooke's study (1922) went almost unnoticed, which showed the fallacy of Franklin's conclusion.

Addentbruk made a collapsible capacitor consisting of three cylinders: one glass and two metal, tightly adjacent to the glass from the inside and outside, respectively. The researcher charged such a capacitor, then carefully disassembled it and brought metal cylinders into contact with each other. If the cylinders were charged, then they naturally had to be discharged. Addentbruk reassembled the capacitor. As in Franklin's experiment, the capacitor turned out to be charged practically the same as originally. But Addentbuk was in no hurry to confirm Franklin's conclusion. He continued a similar experiment with a paraffin cylinder together with a glass one, and in this case the result was the opposite of Franklin’s: the restored capacitor was uncharged, and the charges, as it turned out, “sat” on the metal cylinder-plates (of course, until they were worn out).

AddentBruk concluded that the “Franklin effect” is due to the water film, which under normal conditions is always covered with glass. The fact is that the charges in a state of equilibrium are located on the surface of the conductor, the role of which is played by a film of water. When the conductor is removed (draining the water, for example), almost all of the conductor's charges will remain on this film. If the glass is dried in stacks and tested in a dry atmosphere, then the “Franklin effect” is not observed.

Of course, in the Franklin experiment there is always a “flow” of ions onto the glass, but this effect is insignificant.

The electret effect is also unimportant in this case*).

It should be noted that the water film on the rim of the vessel does not charge it due to the low mobility of ions (the discharge of the capacitor through the film occurs much more slowly than its charge).

There are many high school physics problems that involve thought experiments involving removing and replacing the dielectrics of a capacitor. In this case, it is tacitly assumed that there is no “Franklin effect”, i.e. only the capacitor plates are charged. As we see, in reality the situation is more complicated.

*) You can read about the electret effect in the article “Electrets are dielectric analogues of magnets”, published in issues 6 and 7 of our magazine this year. (Note ped.)
