The free compression of a cloud under the influence of its own gravity can be represented as the movement of all its parts along highly elongated elliptical orbits, for which Kepler’s laws are valid.

It can be seen that small-scale occurrences ($\lambda \ll (RT/G\rho\mu)^{1/2}$) are stable with respect to random collapse, and large-scale occurrences ($\lambda \gg (RT/G\rho\mu)^{1/2}$) are unstable: if they arise by chance, they will not be able to expand again.

Our reasoning allowed us to estimate the value of the critical size $\lambda_J$:

$$\lambda_J \sim \left( \frac{RT}{G\rho\mu} \right)^{1/2},$$

from which it is easy to obtain an estimate for the critical mass $M_J$:

$$M_J \sim \rho \lambda_J^3 \sim \left( \frac{RT}{G\mu} \right)^{3/2} \rho^{-1/2}.$$

The last two formulas are (up to numerical factors) the valued Jeans formulas.

In order to better understand the meaning of these results, we obtain them once again from the condition of gravity equilibrium of a gas cloud under the influence of pressure and gravity forces, which, by definition

$\lambda_J$ and $M_J$, occurs just at $\lambda = \lambda_J$ and $M = M_J$. The characteristic value of the gravitational acceleration in the region is equal in order of magnitude

$$g \sim G \frac{M}{\lambda^2} \sim G\rho\lambda.$$

The gas pressure must balance the force of gravity; it is equal to zero at the outer edge of the cloud and should reach the value

$$p \sim \rho g\lambda \sim G\rho^2\lambda^2$$

(remember the formula for the pressure of so much liquid $p = \rho gh$). Expressing $p$ from the equation of state:

$$p = \frac{\rho RT}{\mu},$$

we get the same value for $\lambda_J$ and $M_J \sim \rho \lambda_J^3$ as before. Using more mathematics, it is not difficult to obtain the exact values ​​of $\lambda_J$ and $M_J$, which we present here without proof:

$$\lambda_J = \left( \frac{2RT}{G\rho\mu} \right)^{1/2}, M_J = \left( \frac{RT}{2G\mu} \right)^{3/2} \rho^{-1/2}.$$

The theory of gravitational instability is based on these simple formulas. It follows from it that if in a gaseous medium, for some reason, gravitational densities of various gas scales arise, then those of them whose mass exceeds $M_J$ must uncontrollably skim and become denser. The obviously arisen densities of small gases in their number and density prevail over large-scale gases. Therefore, it is most likely that gravitational instability will first undergo occurrence with mass $M \approx M_J$.
