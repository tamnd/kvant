While the water evaporates...

Let's observe the evaporation of water in the vessel. Fill a small saucepan or glass with water and monitor its level throughout the day. This process is quite slow, and there is time to reflect and make some assessments. In particular, we will try to estimate the evaporation rate and compare it with our observations.

What is the mechanism of the evaporation process? Let us remember that in order to convert a certain amount of water into steam at a low temperature, it is necessary to transfer a certain amount of heat to the water - the so-called heat of vaporization. For example, at room temperature ($T = 290$ K), the heat of vaporization is 2.46 kJ for every gram of water evaporated. Since 1 g of water contains 1/18 $N_A$ molecules, where $N_A = 6,02 \cdot 10^{23}$ mol$^{-1}$ is Avogadro’s number, we find that to remove one molecule from a liquid it is necessary to expend $E_1 = 7,35 \times 10^{-20}$ J of energy. In atomic calculations, it is customary to express energy in electron volts (eV). Since 1 eV = $1,6 \cdot 10^{-19}$ J, then $E_1 = 0,46$ eV.

Where does the expended energy go? The answer is very simple - to overcome the force of attraction that acts from the liquid on the melting molecule. Each molecule interacts with the other molecules surrounding it. The interaction force at small distances ($r < r_0 \sim 10^{-8}$ cm) has the nature of repulsion, and at large distances ($r > r_0$) it has the character of attraction. In the thickness of a liquid, each molecule is surrounded by other similar molecules on all sides, and the average resulting force is zero. The molecule finds itself in a different position, trying to leave the surface of the water and fly into free space. It attracts molecules located on the surface, and this force is not compensated by anything. Therefore, in order to overcome attraction and finally leave the surface of the water, the molecule must have sufficiently high kinetic energy. Compare: the average kinetic energy of translational motion of water molecules, equal to ($3/2$) $kT$, where $k = 1,38 \cdot 10^{-23}$ J/K is Boltzmann’s constant, at $T = 290$ K is 0.038 eV, which is an order of magnitude less than the energy $E_1$ that must be imparted to a molecule to remove it from the liquid. This means that only those few molecules that, being near the surface, as a result of random attractions that are an order of magnitude greater than the average value, can leave the surface.
