It originates from Isaac Newton. 5 years after the great physicist published his law of gravity, his friend, the Reverend Richard Bentley, then head of Trinity College, Cambridge, asked in a letter to Newton whether the force of gravity might just be the cause of the formation of stars (a question worthy of considering Bentley as a co-author of the theory of gravitational instability). Newton, in a letter dated December 10, 1692, replied to him as follows:

“It seems to me that if all the matter of our Sun and planets and all the matter of the Universe were uniformly scattered in the depths of the heavens, and if each particle had an inherent gravitation towards all the others, and if, finally, the space in which this matter was scattered were finite, the matter outside this space, thanks to the indicated gravitation, would be attracted to all the matter inside and as a result would fall into the middle of all space and form there one huge spherical mass. However, if this substance were evenly distributed over infinite space, it could never unite into one mass, but part of it would be dispersed here, and another there, creating an infinite number of huge distances from each other throughout this infinite space. This is exactly how both the Sun and the fixed stars could have been formed, if we assume that the substance was luminous in nature.”
