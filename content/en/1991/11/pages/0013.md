England. Beginning of the 20th century. James Hopwood Jeans (1877-1946), a brilliant graduate of Cambridge University, for several years has been carrying out a series of outstanding works on advanced areas of theoretical physics: he publishes a monograph on the kinetic theory of gases, research on molecular physics and the theory of radiation. During these same years, under the influence of George Darwin (1845-1912), a professor of astronomy and physics at Cambridge University, the son of the famous biologist Charles Darwin, James Jeans carried out a series of works on theoretical astronomy. Among them was the fundamental work “The Stability of a Spherical Nebula,” published in 1902 in the “Communications of the Royal Society of London.”

This work on the behavior of gas condensations under the influence of their own gravitational force became the basis of the modern theory of gravitational instability, which explains the origin of practically all structural elements of the Universe - from galaxies and their clusters to stars, planets and their satellites. Astronomers now call the characteristic dimensions and masses of gravitational-stable gas seals Jeans and are marked with the index J (from James Jeans). For example, $M_J$ is the Jeans mass, $R_J$ is the Jeans radius.

In general, J. Darwin could be proud of the fact that he attracted the attention of young Jeans to astronomy: his ward did a lot for the development of cosmogony and stellar dynamics. But could J. Darwin, who held the position in 1899-1900, have guessed? post-resident of the Royal Astronomical Society in London, that a quarter of a century later J. Jeans would assume this honorary position and, in memory of his mentor, establish daily Darwin lectures? However, the connection between Darwin and Jeans turned out to be even deeper. Darwin, as you know, became famous for his study of tides and the figures of rotating liquid bodies. Jeans continued his research into the cosmogonic role of tidal phenomena and even developed the tidal theory of the origin of the solar system, which was very fashionable in the first half of the 20th century. In this theory, the birth of a planetary system is considered as a rare fact, caused by the close collision of another star near the Sun, which tore out a clot of melted matter from the Sun’s shell. And although in relation to the Solar system this hypothesis is now remembered only as a historical fact, the mechanism of tidal interaction undoubtedly plays a large role in the world of galaxies, star clusters, possibly during the formation of stars - at the so-called protostellar stage of their evolution.
