The second task is a “black box” with two outputs containing the source. It was necessary to find the EMF and internal resistance of the source using a voltmeter (its resistance was more comparable to the internal resistance of the source) and a potentiometer with a resistance of 1 kOhm. It was necessary to figure out how to make two (or better yet, three) known resistors from this potentiometer. One way is to set the potentiometer slider approximately in the middle and use a voltmeter to select a more precise position, turning on the “halves” one by one. Now we have a resistor with a resistance of 500 Ohms and another one with a resistance of 250 Ohms, if we connect the “halves” in parallel. Then it’s quite simple - you need to create several circuits and calculate the unknown EMF and internal resistance of the source (you could take more circuits than needed for the calculation and check if there is, say, a diode in the “black box”). In the first problem for the eleventh grade - optical - it was necessary to change the refractive index of glycerol and an unknown substance (pieces of irregularly shaped fused quartz). Equipment: a cylindrical lamp with parallel ends, a light bulb on a stand, a battery, water, glycerin, graph paper. The idea is clear - “drown” the pieces in a mixture of water and glycerin so that they are not visible. In this case, we can assume that the optical properties of the mixture are the same as those of the pieces, and the rest is clear.

In the second problem, it was necessary to find the temperature of the filament of an overlay light bulb at a given current. The temperature coefficient of tungsten was determined, and it was necessary to change the resistances at room temperature and at the operating point. The difficulty is that even a small current noticeably heats up the filament, so the filament had to extrapolate the characteristics of the filament in the region of low currents.
