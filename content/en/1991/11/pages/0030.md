MYSTERY
LEYDEN JAR

L. KRYZHANOVSKY

The English chemist, physicist and historian of science Joseph Priestley (1733-1804) called the Leiden experiment the most remarkable discovery in the field of electricity. This experiment, which was enhanced by the invention of the first capacitor, was a scientific sensation of the 18th century: everyone was fascinated by the long head spark and studied the “electrical resistance” when a Leyden jar was discharged through the body of the experimenter; The signs valued the ability of the Leyden jar to accumulate a large charge and store it for a long time.

In the Arkhangelskoye museum-estate near Moscow there is a painting by the artist Charles-Amédée Van Loo (1719-1795) “Electric Experience” (1777), figure with which you see here on the next page. What, in fact, is the experience, so faithfully depicted by the artist?

Before the invention of the Voltova stola (1799), only machines based on trenium electrification served as laboratory sources of electricity. Such a machine is depicted in the picture - a glass ball, which, when the third one rotates on a pad, generates a charge (previously the ball simply rubbed against the assistant’s hands). The girl depicted in the center of the painting stands on an insulating stand. The rod that the girl holds in her left hand almost touches the spinning ball. Sparks are visible between the hysterjem ball. The human body is generally a good conductor, so the other sterjem, which the girl holds in her right hand, also turns out to be charged.

The main participant in the experiment is a poor black boy. In his right hand he holds a vessel of water in which the just mentioned rod is immersed. The vessel is the Leyden jar in its original form (1745). In the Leyden jar depicted in the picture, the dielectric is glass, the inner electrode is water, and the outer electrode is the experimenter’s palm. The picture shows the moment of charging the capacitor. A moment will pass, the little black man will bring his free hand closer to the rod, a spark will leak between the rod and the hand - and the capacitor will discharge through the little black man, who will experience an electric shock.

One of the first studies of the Leyden jar was carried out by the American scientist, educator and politician Benjamin Franklin (1706-1790), who established, in particular, that charges of equal magnitude and opposite sign simultaneously accumulate in the Leyden jar.

Franklin wondered where the charges actually “sit” in the Leyden jar. To obtain an answer to this question, Franklin continued this experiment. He charged the Leyden jar, and then took the rod out of it and poured the “electrified” water into another vessel. The Leiden experiment with this vessel did not work, then, pouring new water into the first Leyden jar, Franklin discharged it through the body and experienced an electric shock of almost the same force as if he had not poured out the “electrified” water.
