Infrared image of the central part of a dense cloud in the constellation Orion. The bright points are fading protostars and very young stars surrounded by gaseous shells. The light, blurry spots are gas fragments that have not yet been sufficiently compacted.

It is natural to consider the radius of a gas fragment at the moment of the onset of its gravitational instability to be the values ​​$R_J=\lambda_J/4$. Based on the fact that the mass of the fragment should be equal to the mass of the Sun, and we accept $T\approx 20$ K, we obtain $n(\mathrm{H}_2)=10^4$ cm$^{-3}$ and $R_J=10^4$ a. e. Indeed, this is the original radius of the Orth cloud! Do we still need proof of the validity of Jeans' theory?

Of course, simple Jeans formulas do not take into account the multichemical processes occurring in the interstellar medium. These formulas are correct for an ideal homogeneous gas at rest, which cannot be found anywhere in nature. Real interstellar matter is in constant motion, the speed of which often exceeds the speed of sound; it experiences a strong influence of the magnetic field, attraction to the stars of the Galaxy and radiation pressure from the brightest of them. Therefore, many physicists after Jeans took up the task of clarifying and developing the theory of gravitational instability. Let us note among them E. M. Lifshtz, S. Chandraskar, Ya. B. Zeldovich and J. Silk. Now this theory has been developed in detail in the field: it takes into account the expansion and rotation of the gaseous medium, its interaction with the magnetic field and with other sources of gravity. It is important, however, that taking into account additional physical factors did not change the fundamental conclusions of the theory: the values ​​of the Jeans mass and Jeans radius are still used in most works on assessing the effects of gravitational instability.
