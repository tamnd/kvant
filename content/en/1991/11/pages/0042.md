Möbius strip
The Möbius strip is one of the "mathematical surprises". It's very easy to get it:
Glue rings from a paper strip, just before gluing, turn one end 180°. If the strip of paper was long, then such a turn could have happened by accident. It was said that a maid helped open her “Möbius strip” by once sewing the ends of a strip together incorrectly.

Be that as it may, in 1858, Leutsz professor August Ferdinand Mobius (1790-1868), a student of K. F. Gauss, astronomer and geometer, sent a work to the Paris Academy of Sciences that included information about this sheet. He waited for seven years to review his work and, without waiting, published its results. At the same time as Möbius, another student of K. F. Gauss, Johann Benedict Listing (1808-1882), a professor at the University of Göttigen, invented this leaf. He published his work three years earlier than Mobius, in 1862. What struck these two German professors? And the fact that the Möbius strip has only one side. We have led to the fact that every surface with which we deal (a sheet of paper, a bicycle tube or a volleyball tube) has two sides. It is not difficult to make sure that the Mobius strip is one-sided: start gradually painting it in some color, starting from any place, and upon completion of the work you will find that it is completely colored.

The second surprise awaits us at the moment when we try to cut the Möbius strip along its midline. The “normal” ring would then break up into two pieces, and the Möbius strip would turn into one twisted ring. To demonstrate this property many times, it is convenient to construct a Möbius strip from a zipper. Another surprising thing is that the resulting ring is already more fertile.

Surprise number three: the Möbius strip has only one boundary, and does not split into two parts, like a common ring. Note that the property of one-sidedness does not disappear at the surface if it is bent, stretched, compressed, but not glued or torn. The properties of geometric objects that do not change during such transformations are studied by the mathematical science of topology. (It is curious that this name was given to her by Johann Listing.)
