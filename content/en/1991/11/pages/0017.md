Unfortunately, Jeans slightly exaggerated his capabilities: he could not convincingly prove the working ability of his formulas, since at that time almost nothing was known about the physical properties and composition of the gas mechanism from which stars are formed. However, now we can do it.

Observations by astronomers have shown that the chemical composition of stars and the gas mechanism are highly stable: for every 1000 hydrogen atoms there are about 100 gel atoms and 2-3 atoms of other, heavier elements. In the dense, cold clouds in which stars form, hydrogen is in the form of molecules $H_2$ and its molar mass is $\mu (H_2) = 2$ g/mol. And taking into account all other chemical elements, the average molar mass of the gas mechanism is $\mu_{cp} = 2,3$ g/mol.

Astronomers prefer to express the density of the gas mechanism $\rho = (\mu_{cp}/N_A)n$ in terms of the concentration of molecular hydrogen $n(H_2)$. Taking into account the composition of the gas mechanism, we obtain $n = 1,1n(H_2)$. Substituting $\rho$, $n$ into the Jeans formulas, we rewrite them in a more modern form:

$$\lambda_J = 3,2 \cdot 10^4 a. e. \left( \frac{T}{10K} \frac{10^4 cM^{-3}}{n(H_2)} \right)^{1/2},$$

$$M_J = 0,3M_\odot \left( \frac{T}{10K} \right)^{3/2} \left( \frac{10^4 cM^{-3}}{n(H_2)} \right)^{1/2}$$

Here $M_\odot = 2 \cdot 10^{30}$ kg is the mass of the Sun, a. e. — an astronomical unit equal to the distance from the Earth to the Sun (1 a.e. = 1.5 · 10^{13} cm). In recent years, it has become known that typical sites for star formation are small-scale condensations in molecular cloud mechanisms, where the temperature is $T = 5—20$K and the hydrogen concentration is $n(H_2) \approx 10^4—10^6$ cm$^{-3}$. For these values ​​$M_J = (0,02—2)M_\odot$. Indeed, the masses of most stars are in this range. Jeans' theory is correct!

Another test for this theory can be the size of the cloud - the ancestor of stars. For the Solar System, this size is estimated from observation at $10^4$ a. e.: this is exactly the radius of the dense inner part of the Oort cloud, in which the bulk of cometary nuclei is concentrated. What does Jeans' theory predict in this regard?
