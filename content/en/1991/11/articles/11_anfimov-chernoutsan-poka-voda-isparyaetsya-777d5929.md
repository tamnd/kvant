While the water evaporates...

Let's observe the evaporation of water in the vessel. Fill a small saucepan or glass with water and monitor its level throughout the day. This process is quite slow, and there is time to reflect and make some assessments. In particular, we will try to estimate the evaporation rate and compare it with our observations.

What is the mechanism of the evaporation process? Let us remember that in order to convert a certain amount of water into steam at a low temperature, it is necessary to transfer a certain amount of heat to the water - the so-called heat of vaporization. For example, at room temperature ($T = 290$ K), the heat of vaporization is 2.46 kJ for every gram of water evaporated. Since 1 g of water contains 1/18 $N_A$ molecules, where $N_A = 6,02 \cdot 10^{23}$ mol$^{-1}$ is Avogadro’s number, we find that to remove one molecule from a liquid it is necessary to expend $E_1 = 7,35 \times 10^{-20}$ J of energy. In atomic calculations, it is customary to express energy in electron volts (eV). Since 1 eV = $1,6 \cdot 10^{-19}$ J, then $E_1 = 0,46$ eV.

Where does the expended energy go? The answer is very simple - to overcome the force of attraction that acts from the liquid on the melting molecule. Each molecule interacts with the other molecules surrounding it. The interaction force at small distances ($r < r_0 \sim 10^{-8}$ cm) has the nature of repulsion, and at large distances ($r > r_0$) it has the character of attraction. In the thickness of a liquid, each molecule is surrounded by other similar molecules on all sides, and the average resulting force is zero. The molecule finds itself in a different position, trying to leave the surface of the water and fly into free space. It attracts molecules located on the surface, and this force is not compensated by anything. Therefore, in order to overcome attraction and finally leave the surface of the water, the molecule must have sufficiently high kinetic energy. Compare: the average kinetic energy of translational motion of water molecules, equal to ($3/2$) $kT$, where $k = 1,38 \cdot 10^{-23}$ J/K is Boltzmann’s constant, at $T = 290$ K is 0.038 eV, which is an order of magnitude less than the energy $E_1$ that must be imparted to a molecule to remove it from the liquid. This means that only those few molecules that, being near the surface, as a result of random attractions that are an order of magnitude greater than the average value, can leave the surface.

Of course, the body of water absorbed is not directly transferred to those molecules that fly out from the surface. These molecules gain excess energy randomly from their surroundings. However, as a result of the fact that not just any, but only the most “energetic” molecules leave the liquid, each remaining molecule has a slightly lower energy rate. If the liquid does not “compensate” for this loss by absorbing a sufficient amount of heat from the environment, then its temperature will decrease. To prevent this from happening, a colic heat must be supplied to the liquid, just equal to its heat of vaporization.

Sometimes schoolchildren have the misconception that evaporated fast molecules have an abnormally high energy and therefore steam must be a hot liquid. This is, of course, not true. Only at the beginning of its “free” movement does the molecule have excess energy. Predating the force of attraction, it loses part of its excess energy, and the average energy of the melted molecules turns out to be equal to the average energy of steam of the same temperature.

This is all well and good, you say, but we are not yet one step ahead in estimating how quickly water should evaporate. Moreover, it became clear that in order to make any reasonable estimates it is necessary to have a clearer understanding of the “structure” of the liquid and the nature of the movement of its molecules. But we will still be honored to continue the necessary calculations. And here's how.

It turns out that it’s enough... to mentally place our glass of water in a closed jar. After some time, saturated water vapor will form in this jar, and the evaporation process will stop. More precisely (and in this case!), evaporation will occur in the same way as before, but the number of molecules evaporated from the liquid will be equal to the number of molecules entering the liquid from the vapor. In this case, they say that dynamic equilibrium has been established between the liquid and vapor. It becomes possible to estimate the intensity of evaporation indirectly, considering not the liquid itself, but its saturated vapor. The advantage is that liquid is a very complex object, and steam is quite simple. In any case, when making estimates, saturated steam can be considered with good accuracy as an ideal gas.

Let's make an assessment by making some simplified proposals. We will assume, in particular, that all molecules have the same speed $v$ and that they can move not in an arbitrary direction, but only in one of six directions specified by the coordinate axes (one of the axes is vertical). In one second, 1/6 of the molecules located in a cylinder with a height of $v$ (see figure) will fall on a section of the liquid surface with an area of ​​$S$:

$$\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{6} nvS,$$

where $n$ is the concentration of molecules (a more detailed calculation gives the set 1/4 together with 1/6). To estimate the speed $v$, we use the expression for the mean square speed $v_{cp} = \sqrt{3kT/m}$ (here $m$ is the mass of one molecule), the concentration of saturated vapor $n$ is expressed through its pressure $p_H$ using the ideal gas equation of state $p_H = nkT$. Then for the mass of water that “falls out” from saturated steam onto a plane $S$ of the surface in 1 s, we obtain

$$\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{6} mnv_{cp} S = \frac{1}{2} p_H S \left(\frac{M}{3RT}\right)^{1/2},$$

where M is the molar mass of water, $R$ is the universal gas constant. Substituting the numerical data ($p_H$ at 17 °C is 0.02 · 10⁵ Pa), we find that in 1 s, 0.16 g of water comes from saturated steam per 1 cm² of surface. If we assume that evaporation occurs at the same rate, then the water level in the vessel should drop by 1 cm every 6 seconds!

It is clear that this does not at all resemble the real picture. We have suffered a very honorable defeat, and now it is necessary to understand its principles. Discussion of some of them.

The first reason lies in the experiment itself. The real process of evaporation is significantly replaced due to the fact that the air in the room is not dry, but moist. Moreover, humidity itself of about 60-80% would lead to a replacement of the evaporation process by only 3-4 times. But if no air flow is created near the surface of the water (for example, by turning on the Ventilator), then the humidity indefinitely near the surface turns out to be quite close to 100% and the evaporation process is replaced many more times. Our estimate of the evaporation rate refers to an ideal servant, when the return flow of molecules to the surface is completely absent.

It turns out, however, that even in this ideal case we are mistaken by about 30 times, but this error is associated with the calculation itself. The fact is that only 3-4% of the molecules adjacent to the surface are captured by this surface (“attached”) and end up inside the liquid. The remaining molecules are reflected back.

Let's sum it up. If an absolutely dry air flow picked up all the emitted molecules, the surface of the water in the vessel would drop by 1 cm not in 6 seconds, but in 3 minutes. But still much faster than one might expect. Isn't it true?
