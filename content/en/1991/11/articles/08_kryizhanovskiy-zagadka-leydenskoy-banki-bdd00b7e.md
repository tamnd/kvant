MYSTERY
LEYDEN JAR

L. KRYZHANOVSKY

The English chemist, physicist and historian of science Joseph Priestley (1733-1804) called the Leiden experiment the most remarkable discovery in the field of electricity. This experiment, which was enhanced by the invention of the first capacitor, was a scientific sensation of the 18th century: everyone was fascinated by the long head spark and studied the “electrical resistance” when a Leyden jar was discharged through the body of the experimenter; The signs valued the ability of the Leyden jar to accumulate a large charge and store it for a long time.

In the Arkhangelskoye museum-estate near Moscow there is a painting by the artist Charles-Amédée Van Loo (1719-1795) “Electric Experience” (1777), figure with which you see here on the next page. What, in fact, is the experience, so faithfully depicted by the artist?

Before the invention of the Voltova stola (1799), only machines based on trenium electrification served as laboratory sources of electricity. Such a machine is depicted in the picture - a glass ball, which, when the third one rotates on a pad, generates a charge (previously the ball simply rubbed against the assistant’s hands). The girl depicted in the center of the painting stands on an insulating stand. The rod that the girl holds in her left hand almost touches the spinning ball. Sparks are visible between the hysterjem ball. The human body is generally a good conductor, so the other sterjem, which the girl holds in her right hand, also turns out to be charged.

The main participant in the experiment is a poor black boy. In his right hand he holds a vessel of water in which the just mentioned rod is immersed. The vessel is the Leyden jar in its original form (1745). In the Leyden jar depicted in the picture, the dielectric is glass, the inner electrode is water, and the outer electrode is the experimenter’s palm. The picture shows the moment of charging the capacitor. A moment will pass, the little black man will bring his free hand closer to the rod, a spark will leak between the rod and the hand - and the capacitor will discharge through the little black man, who will experience an electric shock.

One of the first studies of the Leyden jar was carried out by the American scientist, educator and politician Benjamin Franklin (1706-1790), who established, in particular, that charges of equal magnitude and opposite sign simultaneously accumulate in the Leyden jar.

Franklin wondered where the charges actually “sit” in the Leyden jar. To obtain an answer to this question, Franklin continued this experiment. He charged the Leyden jar, and then took the rod out of it and poured the “electrified” water into another vessel. The Leiden experiment with this vessel did not work, then, pouring new water into the first Leyden jar, Franklin discharged it through the body and experienced an electric shock of almost the same force as if he had not poured out the “electrified” water. in glass, and not in water, as he first assumed.

This experience is described by many historians of science, who at the same time explicitly or implicitly confirm the validity of Franklin’s conclusion. Unfortunately, Addenbrooke's study (1922) went almost unnoticed, which showed the fallacy of Franklin's conclusion.

Addentbruk made a collapsible capacitor consisting of three cylinders: one glass and two metal, tightly adjacent to the glass from the inside and outside, respectively. The researcher charged such a capacitor, then carefully disassembled it and brought metal cylinders into contact with each other. If the cylinders were charged, then they naturally had to be discharged. Addentbruk reassembled the capacitor. As in Franklin's experiment, the capacitor turned out to be charged practically the same as originally. But Addentbuk was in no hurry to confirm Franklin's conclusion. He continued a similar experiment with a paraffin cylinder together with a glass one, and in this case the result was the opposite of Franklin’s: the restored capacitor was uncharged, and the charges, as it turned out, “sat” on the metal cylinder-plates (of course, until they were worn out).

AddentBruk concluded that the “Franklin effect” is due to the water film, which under normal conditions is always covered with glass. The fact is that the charges in a state of equilibrium are located on the surface of the conductor, the role of which is played by a film of water. When the conductor is removed (draining the water, for example), almost all of the conductor's charges will remain on this film. If the glass is dried in stacks and tested in a dry atmosphere, then the “Franklin effect” is not observed.

Of course, in the Franklin experiment there is always a “flow” of ions onto the glass, but this effect is insignificant.

The electret effect is also unimportant in this case*).

It should be noted that the water film on the rim of the vessel does not charge it due to the low mobility of ions (the discharge of the capacitor through the film occurs much more slowly than its charge).

There are many high school physics problems that involve thought experiments involving removing and replacing the dielectrics of a capacitor. In this case, it is tacitly assumed that there is no “Franklin effect”, i.e. only the capacitor plates are charged. As we see, in reality the situation is more complicated.

*) You can read about the electret effect in the article “Electrets are dielectric analogues of magnets”, published in issues 6 and 7 of our magazine this year. (Note ped.)
