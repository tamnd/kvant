ON SURFACE AND IMPACT

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. PANOV

The theory of motion on a surface and the theory of impact are two perfectly complementary branches of mechanics. The study of motion on surfaces was carried out by such giants as Euler, Lagrange, Hamilton, Jacobi. The concepts developed by immi form the basis of modern mechanics, mathematics and physics. Experiments on the collision of balls were carried out by Huygens and Newton. It was these experiments that allowed Huygens to formulate the law of conservation of momentum*).

Here we discuss a number of specific experiments. Here is the first one, which will serve as a model for everything else. Place a small steel ball inside a glass bottle and, meeting it, make the ball move along the inner surface

bottles. Unfortunately, in this experiment it is difficult to closely monitor the movement of the ball, since it quickly stops. Frictional forces replace the movement of the ball, and the gravity force at the upper part of the trajectory lifts the slowly moving ball from the surface of the vessel. Let's perform a pure experiment, assuming that we are in conditions of weightlessness and considering

Movement on an absolutely smooth surface

An absolutely smooth surface is such a surface that, when moving,

no friction forces act on the body along it. Let's take a small ball and just launch it along the surface. Absolute smoothness means that the absolute velocity of the ball is constant and that there is no lateral drift of the ball along the surface, i.e., the acceleration vector is directed perpendicular to the surface. Let's give the following definition:

a curve on a surface is called geodesic if the acceleration vector of a point uniformly moving along this curve is always directed perpendicular to the surface.

Thus, a small ball moving along an absolutely smooth surface moves along a geodesic. This means that studying the motion of a ball is a property of studying geodesics on this surface.

Problem 1. Prove that on a sphere, circles of greatest radius are geodesics.

Note. Let the ball move in a certain direction. Let us draw a circle of sufficiently large radius tangent to this direction. What do you think, when the ball welds from this circle, where will it turn?

Geodesics are among the most important concepts in mechanics, mathematics and physics. Moreover, geodetic and related concepts form a common part of the foundation on which these three sciences rest.

Let us describe one more characteristic property of geodesics. Let us fix two points M and N on the surface. They can be connected by various curves lying on the surface. The average of the curves has a shortest path. It turns out that this shortest path is geodesic.

Thus, geodesics can be described as the shortest lines on a surface, or more precisely
a curve lying on a surface will be geodesic if any subtagonally small arc A of it is a shortest arc connecting points A and B.
Unfortunately, the movement of a real ball inside a real bottle occurs relatively along the geodesic. There are two simple approaches that allow you to get a clear idea of ​​the progress of geodesics.

Geodesics on a surface of rotation. Clairaut's theorem

Most of the surfaces on which we can make a ball move - the surface of a bottle, a can, a graphic, and so on - are all rotating surfaces. They have a rotation axis, and when rotated around it at any angle, they align with themselves. It is precisely geodesics on such surfaces that are discussed in the next theorem.

Clairaut's theorem. Geodesic distance on a rotation surface. Let point A gap this geodesic and r be the distance from point A to the axis of rotation of the surface, and α be the angle between the geodesic and the meridian at point A. Then the value r sin α is constant along the geodesic:

$$r \sin \alpha = c$$

Here is what can be learned from this theorem. Let point A move along the geodesic in the direction of decreasing r. Then from the correspondence r sin α=c it follows that as r decreases, the sine of the angle α increases. At the moment when the geodesic touches the parallel, i.e. α=π/2, from Clairaut’s theorem we find that r reaches the minimum possible value equal to c. After touching, the geodesic is reflected from the parallel, r begins to increase to the maximum, and then again drops to the same minimum value c. Thus, a point on a geodesic moves in a ring between parallels of the same radius, alternately touching them. This description gives a clear idea of ​​the behavior of geodesics and, thereby, of the movement of a small ball along an absolutely smooth surface of rotation.

Machine for moving along geodetic lines

The second approach to the study of geodesics is experimental. It will apply not only to the rotation surface, but to any surfaces in general. Take some adhesive tape, adhesive tape or insulation. The main thing is that the tape is narrow and does not stretch. Unfold this tape along the surface under study, starting from a certain point and in a certain direction. Make sure that the tape is laid on the surface without wrinkles. If the tape is quite narrow, then this is possible. Then the tape will lie along the geodesic. Thus, armed with a roll of adhesive tape, you can examine the behavior of surveyors on any surface. And it's actually a fun activity.

Along the cylinder

We have just emphasized the similarity between the movement of a ball on an absolutely rough rotating surface. Now let's give an example that shows the striking difference between these types of movement. We will consider the movement of the ball inside the cylinder and this time we will turn on the force of gravity, directing it along the axis of the cylinder. To visualize in this case the movement of a ball along an absolutely rough cylinder, take a sheet of tracing paper and draw a paraboly on it. Then wind this sheet onto the cylinder so that the parabolic shaft coincides with one of the straight lines of the cylinder. A parabola wound around a cylinder is the trajectory of the ball

inside an absolutely smooth cylinder. Save this sheet of tracing paper, we will need it later.

This all looks natural enough, but as soon as we move on to a completely rough cylinder, amazing things begin to happen. It turns out that gravity cannot pull the ball down - the ball will always move along a sinusoidal curve contained between two parallels of the cylinder. This, of course, is an unexpected result, and of course, it casts doubt on the real existence of surfaces that have the property of absolute roughness. But let's not rush to conclusions just yet.

Balloon and coin

Now we will get acquainted with another type of movement on the surface. We will get a balloon and a one-kopeck coin. Place a coin inside the balloon and inflate it. Shake the ball like shaking a filled bottle. At some point, the coin stands on its edge and, with a slight hiss and whistle, begins to roll inside the ball. The coin will roll for quite a long time (20-30 seconds) and will travel about a hundred meters. During this time, you can already understand its surface. First of all, attention is drawn to the fact that after several revolutions the movement of the coin stabilizes, and it begins to move along the parallel of the maximum radius - we believe that the balloon is a rotation surface.

If you suddenly change the position of the ball, then after several revolutions the coin still returns to the parallel of the maximum radius. This tendency to move along a parallel of maximum radius is called asymptotic orbital stability. By the way, a small table ball placed inside an air ball moves exactly like a coin. Only he slows down some faster.

Asymptotic stability is a completely new quality, not characteristic of movement either on an absolutely smooth or on an absolutely rough surface. In both cases, movement along the maximum parallel is one of the possible movements. However, consider the following problem.

Problem 5. Prove that motion along the maximum parallel does not have asymptotic orbital stability for either an absolutely smooth or an absolutely rough brauchen surface.

Note. Consider a movement going at a slight angle to a parallel of maximum radius, and consider that it will always pass in a strip between two fixed parallels.

There is another remarkable fact that can be discovered in this experiment. We will compress the air ball along its axis. With some compression, the asymptotic stability of movement along the maximum parallel is lost - the coin will move away from this parallel and no longer strives to return back. It can be assumed that loss of stability occurs at the moment when, during the compression process, the ball takes the shape of a sphere.

Problem 6. Let the coin move in a circle of large radius

inside a balloon shaped like a sphere. Prove that this motion does not have the property of asymptotic stability.

Note. Movement along this great circle is no better than movement along any other large circle. Including along a circle running at a slight angle to the original one.

So, we have before us a completely new type of movement with the necessary behavior of trajectories. To give its description, similar to the description of an absolutely smooth or absolutely rough movement, it is necessary to move from the language of trajectories to the language of interaction of a coin or a table ball with a rubber film.

Again from movement to impact, or from coin to laser

And in the experiment with a coin, an amazing state is observed between movement on the surface and impact. It has already been mentioned that replacing a coin with a small steel ball does not significantly change the nature of the movement. However, when the steel ball, after losing speed from the surface, it begins to bounce, colliding with the rubber film. And you can notice that the energy losses during these collisions are quite small - after the impact, the ball rises to almost the same height from which it fell. Of course, the impact of the ball, and even when you hold it in your hands, is not a rigid enough structure. To reduce energy loss during a collision, it must be replaced with a more rigid one. For this design, I chose a glass jar with a rubber film stretched over it. In fact, energy losses have decreased dramatically. Unfortunately, having repeatedly collided with the flat film, the ball quickly moved away from its center, bumped into the walls and stopped its movement. A way out of this situation was found. Since these experiments took place in the winter, a jar with a stretched film and a ball inside was placed on the battery, the impact did not expand and the film bent outward. The convex film turned out to have a focusing effect on the motion of the ball, and all collisions occurred near its center. However, it is not at all necessary to follow a correct experiment and make the ball move inside the jar. It turned out that it was even easier to put the jar on the table and throw the ball onto the film on top. This is how the experiment took on its final form.

So, stretch a piece of rubber film over a glass jar. Push the film so that some air comes out from under it and it walks inside. Secure the film on the jar with a rubber ring. Now release the ball from a small height in the center of the film. With the appropriate selection of cans, film and ball, collisions can last a very long time - more than a minute. Simple calculations (again, see “The Squeezing Ball...”) show that in this case the energy loss during each impact is less than one percent. These are very small losses. Sustainability
In an experiment with a ball falling on a film, a remarkable phenomenon is observed. If the initial height $h$ from which the ball falls is small, then all collisions occur near the center of the film. If the initial height $h$ is greater than a certain critical height $h_{\text{кр}}$, then the movement of the ball becomes unstable - after two or three collisions the balls jump out of the film. Using the model of absolutely elastic collisions, it can be calculated that the height $h_{\text{кр}}$ coincides with the height of the “optical focus” of the film surface. This is the height at which a beam of light rays directed along the axis of the film is focused after reflection.
The same problem of stability arises when analyzing the operation of our mechanical model of a laser resonator. We will assume that the reflecting balls are spherical surfaces with radii $R_1$ and $R_2$, $R_1 < R_2$. Here you need to find out for what distances $h$ between the reflecting surfaces the balls will move steadily along their common axis and for what distances $h$ after several collisions it will grow out of the space between the films. The answer is quite unexpected.

It turns out that there are two stability zones: if $0 \leq h < R_1$ or $R_2 < h < R_1 + R_2$. If $R_1 \leq h \leq R_2$ or $h \geq R_1 + R_2$, then the movement will be unstable.
