They immediately had to face extreme difficulties. King Louis XVI issued a decree in which local authorities were ordered to provide all possible support to the expedition. But royal power in France was on its last legs. Revolutionary sentiments among the people intensified every day. People appearing with a royal decree in their hands were looked at with suspicion. Already at the third station from Paris, Méchain, heading south, was detained by citizens who mistook him for a counter-revolutionary conspirator. Only with the help of the police did he manage to prove his innocence and save himself from death.

Mechain began his work in Spain, from the southernmost point of the measured arc - Barcelona. Mechain worked very successfully for nine months, and then almost died. While observing from a water pump, he fell, got caught in a transmission and received several dangerous bone fractures. Although he returned to work two months later, he did not recover from his injuries until his death.

Mechain's work was complicated by the war that soon began between France and Spain. The astronomer turned out to be a prisoner, and only in 1794 did he manage to get to his homeland.

Delambre, who measured the light part of the arc, also had to face great difficulties. He had to check the result of previous measurements made back in 1740. But many structures that were used fifty years earlier were destroyed. Delambre had to, often at the risk of his life, climb dilapidated bell towers that constantly threatened to fall. On some structures it was possible to work only in calm weather, and in case of wind it was necessary to descend immediately.

The use of signals, especially mail, aroused suspicion among the people. Although after the overthrow of the king, astronomers received recommended letters to local resource authorities from the National Assembly, this was still not enough. Bright lanterns lit up on high bases, the light of which could be seen ten kilometers away. The people believed that these signals were being given by foreign spies: after all, France was surrounded by enemy armies that would stop the revolution. Delambre and his landowners had to be targeted more than once; they were forced to read popular lectures to citizens to teach them the tasks of their expedition.

When winter began, new difficulties arose. The astronomer had to spend whole days on the narrow platforms of high bell towers, where the observers were not protected from the wind, dzhedra and snow. With hands stiff from the cold, they had to make precise measurements of angles. Fogs made the separated signals invisible. Delambre rightly tried to see one bell tower for six whole days; to make the observation, he had to return after a few months.

This is how astronomers worked, fighting the elements and the completely legitimate mistrust of the revolutionary people. And yet, all measurements were made with such precision as was only possible in the then state of science. It later turned out that the measurements of Mechain and Delambre should be a meter long, different from the true length of the forty-mile part of the meridian by 0.2 mm!

The work of Méchain and Delambre made it possible to define the basic unit - the meter. With the help of Greek and Latin prefixes (klu, hecto, deca, dets, etc.), large and small measures were formed. The table of measures was simplified to the extreme, since it was based on the number 10. Measures of weight, liquid, dry solids, monetary units - everything was set depending on the meter.

Thanks to its greatest advantage, the metric system of measures has spread throughout many European countries. In our Soviet Union it was legalized in 1926.
