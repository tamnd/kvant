“However,” the fastidious schoolboy will say, “the textbook already gives definitions of a convex polygon and a convex polyhedron.” It is a polygon (polyhedron) lying on one side of each line (plane) passing through its side (face). Are the “school” and “general” definitions equivalent, that is, are all polygons and polyhedra that are convex according to one of the definitions also convex according to the other?

Of course, these definitions are equivalent. The most meticulous students can carry out rigorous proof on their own.

Any student will immediately notice that the “general” definition is simpler and more universal than the “school” one: it refers to the figure itself, and the “school” one requires that the polygon must be on a plane (this is necessary for the concept “on one side of a straight line”). For example, for the face of a polygon, you first need to “draw” the plane containing it and only then can you determine whether the face is convex.

Convex shell

Further, the property is nonconvex; all is not lost for it: it can be complemented to a convex polyhedron. The latter is called its convex hull.

The method of obtaining a convex shell of a limited flat figure is very simple: you should grow a closed elastic band so that it covers the figure and release it. If the elastic band does not twist, then the part of the plane bounded by it is the convex shell of the figure.

For a spatial body, you should do the same: put this body inside a balloon and carefully pull the balloon, allowing the air to escape.

Here is the exact definition: the convex hull of a set $M$ is the minimal convex set containing $M$, i.e., a convex set containing $M$ and not containing other convex sets containing $M$. The convex hull of the set $M$ can theoretically be constructed by taking the intersection of all convex sets containing $M$. Indeed, this intersection is convex and, in addition, any convex set containing $M$ also contains the intersection.

As in many other cases, in order to avoid writing the phrase “convex hull of the set $M$”, the notation conv ($M$) (from the word convex - convex) was invented.
