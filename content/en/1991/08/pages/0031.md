F1284. A large vessel of mass m is filled with water through a small hole in the bottom of the vessel (see figure). In order to pump water of mass M into a vessel, work A had to be done. The hole is opened and the water begins to flow out. At the same time, raise the vessel so that the upper boundary of the water in it remains at the same height relative to the ground. How much work will have to be done before the container is empty?

If the hole in the bottom of the vessel is small, the rate at which the water level h moves into the vessel is also small, and water flows out of the hole at a speed of $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. Indeed, when empty, in a short time $\Delta t$ a small amount of water $\Delta V$ will flow out of the vessel. The states of water at the beginning and at the end of the interval $\Delta t$ are distinguished by the fact that the potential energy of mass $\rho \Delta V$, located at height h, turned into the kinetic energy of flowing water, also of mass $\rho \Delta V$:

$$\rho \Delta V gh = \rho \Delta V \frac{v^2}{2} \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{2gh}.$$

(As is known, a body falling from a height h moves at this speed.)

When we raise a vessel so that the upper boundary of the water in it remains at the same height relative to the ground, we move the vessel at a very low speed, so that the speed of water flowing out relative to the ground can be considered equal to the speed of its flowing out relative to the vessel.

Let us consider the entire process described in the problem conditions as a whole. At the very beginning, all the water is at the level of the hole in the bottom of the vessel. When water was pumped into the vessel, work A was done. Then the water returned to its previous level, as if it had been freely supplied from a height H. In this case, the water lost energy equal to MgH. This means that by raising water, we do work MgH. Plus, we also spent mgh energy to lift the vessel itself.

So, finally, we have to do the work

$$A' = (M + m)gh - A.$$
