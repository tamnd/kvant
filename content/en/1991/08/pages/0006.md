Since the signs of the charges of these particles are opposite, and they move towards each other, the Lorentz force deflects them, as shown in figure 3, 6, in the same direction. Colliding with electrons, electrons transfer their excess energy to them, thereby causing vibrations of the conductor lattice on a part of the incident wave. This, in fact, is what EMAP is all about. Elastic lattice vibrations can be detected by conventional active methods, for example, piezoelectric or magnetostrictive transducers. Non-contact recording of sound is also possible, since the incidence of a sound wave on the boundary of a conductor is accompanied by electromagnetic radiation - this effect is called reverse EMAT.

In order to imagine the scale of the described phenomenon, we present an expression for the efficiency of transformation, defined as the ratio of energy flows in the electromagnetic wave incident on the metal and the sound wave entering from its surface:

$$K = \frac{(S/c)(B^2/8\pi\varrho S^2)}{(1+\beta^2)}$$

In this formula, $c$ is the speed of light, $B$ is the induction of a constant magnetic field, $\varrho$ is the density of the metal, $S$ is the speed of sound in the metal. An important parameter for all EMAT mechanisms

$$\beta = 2\pi^2\delta^2\lambda^2$$

describes the spatial distribution of the exciting force, expressing it through the ratio of the depth of penetration of the electromagnetic wave into the conductor $\delta$ to the length of the elastic wave in it $\lambda$.

It can be seen that the efficiency of the transformation is determined, firstly, by the ratio of the speeds of sound and light - for all solids this value is $\sim 10^{-5}$, secondly, by the ratio of the energy stored in a constant magnetic field $B^2/8\pi$ to the value $\varrho S^2$, equal to the modulo elasticity of the substance $E$, and, finally, by the distribution of the exciting force. Let us immediately make a reservation that the parameter $\beta$ is small only in well-conducting media, for which the penetration depth of the electromagnetic wave is small compared to the length of the elastic wave. This circumstance distinguishes metals as the best objects for the implementation of various EMAT mechanisms. In good metals - aluminum, copper, silver - at room temperature in a magnetic field of $B=1$ T at a frequency of $f=1$ MHz, the EMAT efficiency is $K \sim 10^{-10}$. An equally small fraction of energy is transformed during the reverse transformation. Thus, if an experiment is carried out using an EMAT for both excitation and reception of sound, then the double conversion efficiency is $K^2 \sim 10^{-20}$.
