Soap film always strives to take such a shape that its surface is as small as possible.

Trying to achieve a lamp glass of a finite shape, one in which one end is narrower than the other. Moisten the entire inside of the glass with soapy water and let the water drain. Now lower the wide end of the glass into the water, holding it vertically. Carefully remove the glass from the solution. The soap film covered the hole. Now hold the glass horizontally and you will find that your film will move out of place and run towards the narrow end of the glass.

If you dip the glass into the solution over and over again, the films will run one after another, as if trying to catch up with each other.

Ball in a barrel
Make a ring of wire on the bottle and twist the ends of the wire to form a handle. Make another ring of the same diameter; moisten the rings with the soapy solution that we used for previous experiments. Then blow a bubble between these two rings (like we have on the left side of the picture). Carefully expanding the rings, stretch this ball into a cylinder; stop as soon as the surface of the cylinder begins to take a concave shape.

Now ask someone to moisten a tube in soapy solution and insert its end into the cylinder through the film, which is tightened with the upper ring. Let your friend very, very carefully blow out the second one inside the first bubble.

Our cylinder will gradually split apart and turn into a barrel.

When the second bubble approaches the walls of the “barrel”, you need to lightly separate it from the tube, then carefully pull the tube out of the “barrel”.

So our ball hung between the lid and the bottom. If we now expand the rings, our ball turns into an egg. And if you bring the rings closer together, the “barrel” will develop to the sides, the ball will touch the bottom, and both bubbles will end up in the same bubble.

Touch-me-not ball
I just told you how to blow a soap bubble - a ball in a cylinder bubble. When you look at this small ball, it seems to you that it is touching the film of the cylinder. But in reality this is not the case! The ball does not touch the cylinder! Here is an experience that will help us verify this. Ask a friend to hold the wire rings so that the cylindrical soap bubble takes a horizontal position. If the distance between the rings does not exceed three times their diameter, our cylinder will retain its shape in this position.
