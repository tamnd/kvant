Here a wide range of different cases is possible and more diverse, so I will limit myself to one example showing how from an essentially trivial direct statement a problem rich in geometric content is obtained. It is quite indo that the point of intersection of the altitudes (point $H$) of the acute triangle $ABC$ has the following property:

$$\angle HAB = \angle HCB, \quad \angle HBA = \angle HCA, \quad \angle HAC = \angle HBC.$$

In this regard, the question of a natural task arises:

Problem 4. Find the locus of points $M$ for which the equalities $\angle MAB = \angle MCB, \quad \angle MBA = \angle MCA$ hold, where $ABC$ is the given acute triangle.

Solution. It is clear that the desired location of points inside the triangle belongs to its point of intersection of heights. By the way, a rather non-trivial problem arises here: is there the only such point inside the triangle? We will prove its uniqueness. To do this, continue $AM, BM$ and $CM$ until they intersect with the sides of the triangle at points $A_1, B_1$ and $C_1$ (Fig. 3). Points $A, C, A_1$ and $C_1$ lie on the same circle. So, $\angle MA_1C_1 = \angle MCA = \angle MBC_1$ and $\angle MAC = \angle MC_1A_1$. Thus, $M, B, A_1$ and $C_1$ also lie on the same circle as $\angle MBA_1 = \angle MC_1A_1 = \angle MAC$. Now denoting the angles by $\alpha, \beta$ and $\varphi$, as in figure 3, we find that $\alpha + \beta + \varphi = \pi/2$, from which it follows that $AA_1, BB_1$ and $CC_1$ are the altitudes of our triangle. However, our geometric location is not limited to one point of intersection of heights. It also includes the arc $AB$ of the circle circumscribed around $ABC$, as well as the midpoints of the arcs $BC$ and $CA$ (prove this).
