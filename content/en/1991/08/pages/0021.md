You can construct a countless number of cones using the following properties of cones with a common vertex: a non-empty intersection of such cones is a cone, and a union is also a cone.

A schoolchild who has read this far knows that a convex cone is a cone that is a convex polygamy. For example, if a cone generates a convex polygamy, then it is also convex. Note that if a convex cone that does not contain oppositely directed rays has its vertex removed, then the remaining set will still be convex.

Illumination of space

It's time to finally illuminate the space. The “mathematical sum” of our problem is as follows: in space there are four convex cones $K_1, K_2, K_3, K_4$ with a common vertex $O$, none of which contains oppositely directed rays. In addition, any three of them have a common ray, and the intersection of all four is the point $O$. We need to prove that the union of $K$ cones $K_1, K_2, K_3, K_4$ is the entire space.

Exercise 3. Form and prove a similar result for illuminating a plane.

An attentive student will remember that, in fact, we have already taken the first step towards illuminating space: we noticed that it is enough to construct a tetrahedron $T$, the boundary of which is contained in $K$, so that the point $O$ lies inside $T$.

Now we will build such a tetrahedron. Let us choose four points $A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4$ in space as follows: $A_i$ is any point different from $O$ from the intersection of three cones $K_j$ with numbers $j$ not equal to $i$. Let $T$ be the convex hull of conv $(A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4)$. Now we just need to show that $T$ is a tetrahedron whose boundary is contained in $K$, and that $O$ lies inside $T$.

Three $T$ (see Fig. 7 for the case of a plane).

We hope that the student has not yet forgotten the aim of “divide and rule.” The time has come to apply it. So, by Radon's theorem, the set $\{O, A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4\}$ can be divided into two disjoint subsets $M$ and $N$ such that conv $(M)$ intersects conv $(N)$. For the definition, we will assume that the point $O$ is contained in $M$.

The division has occurred. It's time to rule. We denote by $I$ the set of indices $i$ such that $A_i$ is contained in $M$, and by $J$ the set of indices $j$ such that $A_j$ is contained in $N$ (note that $I$ can be empty, and $J$ - no). Then, due to the convexity of $K_j$, the set conv $(M)$ lies in $K_j$ for all $j$ from $J$, and conv $(N)$ lies in $K_i$ for all $i$ from $I$. Since any number 1, ..., 4 is contained in either $I$ or $J$, the intersection of conv $(M)$ and conv $(N)$ is contained in the intersection $K_1, K_2, K_3, K_4$, and therefore consists only of the point $O$. This means that $O$ is contained in conv $(M)$, and thus in $T$.

So $O$ lies in $T$. The next step is to kill two birds with one stone. If we prove that $O$ does not belong to the convex hull of any three points from the number $A_1, ..., A_4$, then, firstly, the points $A_1, ..., A_4$ do not lie in the same plane and, thus, $T$ is a tetrahedron, and secondly, $O$ lies strictly inside $T$.
