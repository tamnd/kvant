The study of conductors at low temperatures has shown that under the influence of a magnetic field many of their properties change non-monotonically, or, as they say, exhibit quantum oscillations. The description of the nature of this phenomenon goes beyond the scope of statics; for us, the only important thing is that quantum oscillations can only be observed in perfect crystals, completely devoid of mechanical stress.

The question also arose about the existence of quantum oscillations of the speed of sound in metals. This is where traditional contact techniques came into conflict with the requirements of the experiment. Thermal stresses in contact are dangerous even with small changes in temperature; they are even more great when the sample is cooled from room temperature (300 K) to gel temperature (4 K).

To use the experimental capabilities of the new approach, which is used, in particular, for studying quantum oscillations of the speed of sound, we will consider one of the options for conducting an experiment on the non-contact excitation of sound waves in metals.

The sample, shaped like a thin plate, is placed, as shown in figure 5, in an inductor, which creates an electromagnetic field on its surface. If you select the frequency of this field so that it is equal to the frequency of natural vibrations of the plate, the amplitude of the excited sound in it will increase many times over.

Such resonant excitation can be observed experimentally by changing the frequency dependence of electromagnetic energy absorption in a sample in the presence of a constant magnetic field. The position of the active resonance makes it possible to determine the speed of sound with high accuracy. If we now apply an inductive magnetic field and monitor the position of the active resonance in frequency, it turns out that the resonant part will successively increase and decrease, which corresponds to quantum oscillations of the speed of sound.

We have given only one example of a concrete implementation of the EMAT technique and only one example of an experimental study. However, low-temperature changes do not exhaust the possibilities of the new approach.

Progress in this area has been surprisingly rapid. Non-constructive excitation of sound is currently not only one of the areas of radio spectroscopy, but also one of the methods of non-destructive testing of materials, which has found application in various fields of industry.

The entire history of the development of EMAT research shows how fruitful is the interaction of ideas and methods developed in various physical disciplines.
