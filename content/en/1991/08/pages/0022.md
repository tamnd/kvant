Save cost

The generous schoolboy has probably already asked himself the question: “If we are talking about such a daunting task as illuminating the entire space, then is it worth saving on projectors? Is it possible, for example, to illuminate the entire space by having a path of convex light ends with a common vertex $O$, each four of which have a common ray?” The answer to this question about economy is given by the famous Helly theorem:

If a space is given $n$ convex sets, where $n > 4$, every four of which intersect, then all $n$ sets also have a common point.

Of course, five convex sets obtained by removing the vertex $O$ from each cone satisfy the conditions of Helly's theorem, and thus have a common point, say $A$. But then the ray oppositely directed with respect to the ray $OA$ is not contained in either end. The space could not be fully illuminated by five projectors. It's worth saving on projectors.

So, dear student, Helly’s theorem saved us from unnecessary and useless expenses. It helps out in many other cases too. And besides, it is a clear questioning of the principle of “divide and rule.” Now we will see it.

Helly's theorem is proven by induction on the number of the set $n$. Let $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ be convex sets satisfying the conditions of the theorem. If $n=4$, then all sets have a common point by condition. Let us assume that the theorem is true for $n=k$ and prove it for $n=k+1$.

Consider all sets $X_1, \dots, X_{k+1}$ except $X_i$. By condition, every 4 of them intersect, which means, by the induction hypothesis, these $k$ sets have a non-empty intersection. Let $A_i$ be any point from this intersection.

Sharing! By Radon's theorem, the sets $\{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_{k+1}\}$ can be partitioned into two disjoint subsets $M$ and $N$ also that conv ($M$) and conv ($N$) have a non-empty intersection.

Power! Let us denote by $I$ the set of indices $i$ such that $A_i$ is contained in $M$, and by $J$ the set of indices $j$ such that $A_j$ is contained in $N$. Recall that for $i$ not equal to $j$, the point $A_i$ is contained in $X_j$. Therefore, all points $A_i$ of $M$ are contained in each of the set $X_j$, where $j$ of $J$. Conversely, all points $A_j$ of $N$ are contained in each of the set $X_i$, where $i$ of $I$. Due to convexity, conv ($M$) lies in every $X_j$ for $j$ of $J$, and conv ($N$) lies in every $X_i$ for $i$ of $I$. Thus, the intersection of conv ($M$) and conv ($N$) is contained in all sets $X_1, \dots, X_{k+1}$, which proves the theorem.

Exercise 4. Form and prove Helly's theorem for the case of a plane. (The validity of Helly’s theorem for a plane can be verified by superimposing convex spots of light on the stage of a theater or arentserka.)
