Where do tasks come from?
I. SHARYGIN

After some thought, I decided to begin this article with two contradictory statements that should end it. Firstly, I would not want every student who reads this article, and also, I hope, to be found, immediately composing their own problems. And secondly, I invite everyone to take part in a competition for composing problems (primarily geometric ones). Send your works to «Kvant» addressed to the author of the article. The best creative achievements (subjective criteria) will be published and even awarded (although, of course, not as generously as in the Capital Show telegraph).

Now to the point. I want to share my rather extensive experience in drawing up various geometric tasks, reveal some secrets of my kitchen, the formation of aesthetic and even ethical principles. Let me start with the fact that it is convenient to divide the tasks into three groups: educational, competitive and olympiad. We can also talk about creative tasks, but this is more likely to be confirmed than a formal attribute; the characteristic “creative” no longer refers to the task itself, but to the process of solving it. However, it is worth identifying “problem” type tasks as a separate group.

There is a certain set of characteristic 克斯 techniques that are often used when composing certain types of problems.

I'll start with an example.

Problem 1. Prove that for an arbitrary triangle, the projected diameter of a neocircle, perpendicular to one side of this triangle, on the other side is equal to the triangle.

Solution. Behind this elegant complexity lies a broad fact accompanying the theorem of sines: $a=2R \sin A$ (Hereinafter, $a$, $b$, $c$ are the sides of the triangle, $A$, $B$, $C$ are its angles, $R$ is the radius of the circumscribed circle). The literary formulation of this problem only attracted us that even the seasoned leaders of “Zazdanika «Kvant»”, who included it in their problem book, could not resist its charms. This task is more educational than described. Its meaning is to show a known fact from a new and unexpected point of view.

A very common technique is what can be called “replacing a supporting figure.” I, as the leader of the “Tasks” section in the “Mathematics at School” magazine, always resort to it if I don’t have enough for this.

The following theorem has been widely known for a long time:

Theorem. If points C', A' and B' are taken arbitrarily on lines AB, BC and CA, respectively, different from the vertices of triangle ABC, then the circles passing through A, B', C'; A', B, C' and A', B', C have a common point.

(Sometimes this theorem is called Mikel's theorem, and the common point of intersection of the circles is denoted by M and is called Mikel's point.) The proof of this theorem is generally not difficult. The only difficulty, if you do not resort to oriented angles, is the need to try different cases of relative positions of points C', A' and B'. In the case depicted on figure 1, a, denoting by M the point of intersection of the circles A, B', C' and A', B, C', we can easily show that the points A', B', C, M lie on the same circle.

Problem 2. On side AB of a convex quadrilateral ABCD, a point E is taken, different from points A and B. Segments AC and DE intersect at point F. Prove that the circles circumscribed about triangles ABC, CDF and BDE have a common point.

Very beautiful and effective problems can arise when translating a geometric problem from a geometric language into an algebraic one. For example, you might be given a problem to construct a triangle using those heights (can you solve this problem?). The idea is that a triangle with sides a, b, c is similar to a triangle with sides $1/h_a$, $1/h_b$, $1/h_c$ according to the third criterion of similarity of triangles.

Here a wide range of different cases is possible and more diverse, so I will limit myself to one example showing how from an essentially trivial direct statement a problem rich in geometric content is obtained. It is quite indo that the point of intersection of the altitudes (point $H$) of the acute triangle $ABC$ has the following property:

$$\angle HAB = \angle HCB, \quad \angle HBA = \angle HCA, \quad \angle HAC = \angle HBC.$$

In this regard, the question of a natural task arises:

Problem 4. Find the locus of points $M$ for which the equalities $\angle MAB = \angle MCB, \quad \angle MBA = \angle MCA$ hold, where $ABC$ is the given acute triangle.

Solution. It is clear that the desired location of points inside the triangle belongs to its point of intersection of heights. By the way, a rather non-trivial problem arises here: is there the only such point inside the triangle? We will prove its uniqueness. To do this, continue $AM, BM$ and $CM$ until they intersect with the sides of the triangle at points $A_1, B_1$ and $C_1$ (Fig. 3). Points $A, C, A_1$ and $C_1$ lie on the same circle. So, $\angle MA_1C_1 = \angle MCA = \angle MBC_1$ and $\angle MAC = \angle MC_1A_1$. Thus, $M, B, A_1$ and $C_1$ also lie on the same circle as $\angle MBA_1 = \angle MC_1A_1 = \angle MAC$. Now denoting the angles by $\alpha, \beta$ and $\varphi$, as in figure 3, we find that $\alpha + \beta + \varphi = \pi/2$, from which it follows that $AA_1, BB_1$ and $CC_1$ are the altitudes of our triangle. However, our geometric location is not limited to one point of intersection of heights. It also includes the arc $AB$ of the circle circumscribed around $ABC$, as well as the midpoints of the arcs $BC$ and $CA$ (prove this).

Most often, in problems of this type, a certain geometric structure is “constructed,” the parts of which are taken to be certain figures and their elements.

An illustration here can be provided by the stereometric “monster” problems encountered in exams at such challenges as MIPT, Mekhanite and VMC MSU and some others. I won’t give examples of such tasks, especially since you don’t have to go after them. It is enough to recall the stereometric problems from the 1990 versions, published in the journal «Kvant», No. 1 and 2 for 1990 and 1991.

The condition of a geometric design almost certainly leads to a multi-year problem, turning it into a kind of “shelf” problem (many shelves and each with its own tasks) or a “matryoshka” problem (several tasks, one inside the other).

However, problems of this kind do not necessarily have to be based on a complex geometric structure. Here is a simple example made up of two (or three) “shelf” tasks:

Problem 5. Design a convex quadrilateral and divide it into four triangles. Prove that the product of the areas of two opposite triangles is equal to the product of the areas of the other two triangles.

Problem 6. Prove that of all the quadrilaterals inscribed in a given circle, the square has the most density.

Solve problems 5 and 6 using them and construct the next problem.

Problem 7. A quadrilateral ABCD is inscribed in a single circle; the diedynamico intersect at point M. Find the area of ​​this quadrilateral if it is known that the product of the areas of triangles ABM and CDM is equal to 1/4.

Solution. To solve it, it is enough to note (Fig. 4) that $S_1S_3 = S_2S_4 = 1/4$ (problem 5), and also that $S = S_1 + S_3 + S_2 + S_4 \geq 2\sqrt{S_1S_3} + 2\sqrt{S_2S_4} = 2$ (inequality about the arithmetic mean).

In addition (problem 6) $S_{ABCD} \leq 2$, since the area of ​​a square inscribed in a unit circle is 2. From all this it follows that $ABCD$ is a square and its area is 2.

Typically, this leads to the appearance of details in the conditions that have little or even no effect in the solution, which, of course, reduces the natural level of the problem (“Every gun must shoot!”). As an example, it may not be very convincing, since the analysis may be based on personal conjectures, all the same since the XXIV All-Olympics.

Many problems are designed by the authors according to the solution idea they like. True, quite often the “solvers” find other, sometimes simpler, solutions that are different from the author’s.

In connection with task 8, I would like to take one of “my” tasks as an example. I wanted to construct a problem in which the reasoning that three bisectors intersect at one point, or rather, that thirds pass through the point of intersection of two bisectors of the angles of a triangle (not necessarily internal ones, see figure 5), would be repeated twice, and the second stage would essentially rely on the first. Problem 9. In triangle $ABC$ angle $B$ is equal to $120^\circ$. On the side $AC$ the point $M$ is taken, and on the line $AB$ the point $K$ is taken so that $BM$ is the bisector of the angle $ABC$, and $CK$ is the bisector of the angle mixed with $ACB$. Line segment $MK$ intersects side $BC$ at point $P$. Prove that $\angle APM = 30^\circ$.

Solution. This is a two-step reasoning. For triangle $BMC$, the segments $BK$ and $CK$ are the bisectors of the external angles $B$ and $C$ (Fig. 6). Therefore, $MP$ is the bisector of angle $BMC$, and $P$ is the intersection point of the bisectors of the angles external to angles $B$ and $M$ of triangle $ABM$. This means that $AP$ is the bisector of angle $BAC$. Finally we get

$$\angle APM = \angle PMC - \angle PAM = \frac{1}{2} (\angle BMC - \angle BAM) = 30^\circ.$$

Problem 10. At the base of a quadrangular paramda lies a convex quadrilateral, two sides of which are equal to 6, and the remaining two are equal to 10, the height of the paramda is 7, the side faces are inclined to the plane of the base at an angle of 60°. Find the volume of the paramda.

Solution. According to the condition, the double angles at the base are equal to either 60° or 120° (but not necessarily only 60°. This is where the slight subtlety in the formulation lies!), and the vertex of the paramda is projected at a point equidistant from the side, or rather, from the lines forming the quadrilateral. From the latter it follows that a quadrilateral at the base cannot be a parallelogram. Its two adjacent sides are equal to 6, the other two, also adjacent, are equal to 10. But if the quadrilateral ABCD has $AB=BC(=10)$, $AD=DC(=6)$, then it has two points $O_1$ and $O_2$, equidistant from its sides (Fig. 7). It follows from the condition that the distances from the projection of the vertex of the paramda to its sides are equal to $7/\sqrt{3}$. If the vertex is projected to the point $O_1$, the center of the circle inscribed in ABCD, then the area of ​​ABCD must be equal to $16 \cdot \frac{7}{\sqrt{3}}$. But the area ABCD does not exceed 60 (it is equal to 60 if the angles $A$ and $C$ are right), but $16 \cdot \frac{7}{\sqrt{3}} > 60$. This means that the vertex is projected to the point $O_2$, the distances from which to the sides of the quadrilateral ABCD are equal to $\frac{7}{\sqrt{3}}$.

Now we can easily find the area ABCD, $(10-6) \frac{7}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{28}{\sqrt{3}}$, and then the volume of the paramda $\frac{64}{\sqrt{3}}$.

Special case

Many general theorems that provide us with a powerful tool for solving problems, such as Ceva's theorem in geometry or inequalities about averages in algebra, can also act as a tool for composing problems. For example, take Pascal's theorem: if $A, B, C, D, E$ and $F$ are six points located on a circle, then the three points at which the pairwise lines $AB$ and $DE$, $BC$ and $EF$, $FA$ and $CD$ intersect are located on the same line. Let's also consider the problem:

Problem 11. Let sides $AB$ and $CD$ of a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD intersect at point $M$, and sides $BC$ and $AD$ at point $K$. Prove that the tangents to the circle at points $B$ and $D$ intersect on the line $KM$.

You don’t have to have a lot of observability to notice that this problem is part, or rather a limiting case, of Pascal’s theorem.

Professional mathematicians, who are also involved in the organization of mathematical Olympiads, first draw beautiful and interesting problems from their scientific activities, transferring special cases of serious mathematical theorems to school soil, adapting to it certain lemmas, which in large numbers arose in the proof of almost any serious mathematical theorem. True, geometric examples are not too frequent; Serbian mathematics is still very far from school geometry. (This phrase should in no way be considered as a reproach to geometry.) Therefore, I will limit myself to one example, perhaps not very bright and characteristic.

There is such a theorem “about the zigzag”. Given two circles (possibly in space). It is known that there is a set of 2n points $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_{2n}$ such that the odd-numbered points are located on one circle, the even-numbered points on the other, and $A_1A_2 = A_2A_3 = \dots = A_{2n}A_1$. Then there are infinitely many such sets of 2n points, and any point of the first circle can be taken as the point $A$, and the distance between successive points will be the same as that of the original set of points.

I don't know an elementary proof of this theorem. However, its partial cases can be used as elementary problems. For example:

Problem 12. Two circles with radii $R$ and $r$ and distance between centers $a$ are given on the plane. Find the side of a rhombus whose two opposite vertices lie on one circle, and the remaining two on the other.

This problem is quite simple, so I will limit myself to just indicating the answer: $\sqrt{R^2 + r^2} - a^2$.
