NEW DIRECTION
IN RADIO SPECTROSCOPY
SOLIDS

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. VASILIEV

This article will discuss a new approach to the study of the mechanical properties of substances, mainly on the phenomenon of the primary conversion of electromagnetic waves into sound waves (and vice versa) on the surface of a conductor. Within the framework of this approach, purely electromagnetic experimental methods are successfully used to determine the hardness and density of metals, the speed and attenuation of sound waves propagating in them.

In the scientific literature, this phenomenon in all its varieties, as well as the associated change techniques, obtain the general name of Electro-Magnetic-Acoustic Transformation, or, in short, EMAT.

Electromagnetic excitation of sound in metals can be considered as one of the areas of radio spectroscopy - a branch of solid state physics that studies the interaction of an electromagnetic field with matter. Unlike, however, traditional areas of radio spectroscopy, which study the absorption of electromagnetic energy at fixed frequencies, EMAT is not bound by the conditions of resonant interaction and is generated at all frequencies and at all temperatures.

The main reason is that the direct conversion of sound and electromagnetic energy over time is not considered at all in the EMAT dependence. Indeed, since any macrosopper body, including metal, is electrically neutral, an electromagnetic wave, at first glance, cannot set matter in motion at all. In addition, it is known that an electromagnetic wave is almost completely reflected from the boundaries of the conductor (Fig. 1). (Remember that the reflective element of the mirror is simply a metal film deposited on the surface of the glass.) Finally, for a long time there was no special need to develop EMAT - the problem of sound generation was satisfactorily solved with the help of traditional contact transducers, and the development of technical experiments was primarily associated with their improvement.

The action of contact transducers is based on pieosis or magnetostrike, that is, on the ability of some crystals to change their size and shape under the influence of an electric or magnetic field. To generate sound in a solid, it was enough to glue the transducer crystal to the surface of the object being studied and apply an electromagnetic wave to this crystal.

Individual interaction

No matter how well a metal reflects an electromagnetic wave, there is always some, even a very small, part of the wave energy that penetrates its surface layer. This is precisely what all mechanisms of non-contact sound excitation are based on. The simplest of these mechanisms, called induction interaction, is comparator, when the substance under study is in a strong magnetic field. In the absence of a magnetic field, electrons and ions in the surface layer of the metal, under the influence of the field of an electromagnetic wave, move, as shown in figure 3, and in opposite directions, losing from time to time their selective kinetic energy during collisions. In this case, part of the energy of the electromagnetic wave penetrating into the conductor turns into a body, and no sound generation occurs. Colliding with electrons, electrons transfer their excess energy to them, thereby causing vibrations of the conductor lattice on a part of the incident wave. This, in fact, is what EMAP is all about. Elastic lattice vibrations can be detected by conventional active methods, for example, piezoelectric or magnetostrictive transducers. Non-contact recording of sound is also possible, since the incidence of a sound wave on the boundary of a conductor is accompanied by electromagnetic radiation - this effect is called reverse EMAT.

In order to imagine the scale of the described phenomenon, we present an expression for the efficiency of transformation, defined as the ratio of energy flows in the electromagnetic wave incident on the metal and the sound wave entering from its surface:

$$K = \frac{(S/c)(B^2/8\pi\varrho S^2)}{(1+\beta^2)}$$

In this formula, $c$ is the speed of light, $B$ is the induction of a constant magnetic field, $\varrho$ is the density of the metal, $S$ is the speed of sound in the metal. An important parameter for all EMAT mechanisms

$$\beta = 2\pi^2\delta^2\lambda^2$$

describes the spatial distribution of the exciting force, expressing it through the ratio of the depth of penetration of the electromagnetic wave into the conductor $\delta$ to the length of the elastic wave in it $\lambda$.

It can be seen that the efficiency of the transformation is determined, firstly, by the ratio of the speeds of sound and light - for all solids this value is $\sim 10^{-5}$, secondly, by the ratio of the energy stored in a constant magnetic field $B^2/8\pi$ to the value $\varrho S^2$, equal to the modulo elasticity of the substance $E$, and, finally, by the distribution of the exciting force. Let us immediately make a reservation that the parameter $\beta$ is small only in well-conducting media, for which the penetration depth of the electromagnetic wave is small compared to the length of the elastic wave. This circumstance distinguishes metals as the best objects for the implementation of various EMAT mechanisms. In good metals - aluminum, copper, silver - at room temperature in a magnetic field of $B=1$ T at a frequency of $f=1$ MHz, the EMAT efficiency is $K \sim 10^{-10}$. An equally small fraction of energy is transformed during the reverse transformation. Thus, if an experiment is carried out using an EMAT for both excitation and reception of sound, then the double conversion efficiency is $K^2 \sim 10^{-20}$.

Magnetoelastic interaction

An important place among metals is occupied by those that have magnetic properties. In them, as in ordinary metals, induction and deformation interactions operate. The matter is complicated, however, by the fact that in magnets, in addition to the reaction of electrons and ions to the electric field of an electromagnetic wave, it is also necessary to take into account the effect of the magnetic field on elementary atoms as well. As a result of the interaction of electrons, ions and elementary currents with an electromagnetic wave and with each other, quite a few different EMAT mechanisms are realized in magnets. These include, for example, sound generation mechanisms associated with the mixing of domain boundaries and rotation of the magnetization vector in domains. It also turned out that the transition of a metal from one magnetic state to another is accompanied by a gigantic (gain gain compared to inductive interaction!) return of EMAT efficiency. The qualitative effect of the giant transformation can be explained by the fact that the position of the elementary atoms of currents during the transition of metal from one device to another is not determined in any way, and therefore the electromagnetic wave is able to strongly swing these currents, and they, in turn, make them oscillate.

The study of conductors at low temperatures has shown that under the influence of a magnetic field many of their properties change non-monotonically, or, as they say, exhibit quantum oscillations. The description of the nature of this phenomenon goes beyond the scope of statics; for us, the only important thing is that quantum oscillations can only be observed in perfect crystals, completely devoid of mechanical stress.

The question also arose about the existence of quantum oscillations of the speed of sound in metals. This is where traditional contact techniques came into conflict with the requirements of the experiment. Thermal stresses in contact are dangerous even with small changes in temperature; they are even more great when the sample is cooled from room temperature (300 K) to gel temperature (4 K).

To use the experimental capabilities of the new approach, which is used, in particular, for studying quantum oscillations of the speed of sound, we will consider one of the options for conducting an experiment on the non-contact excitation of sound waves in metals.

The sample, shaped like a thin plate, is placed, as shown in figure 5, in an inductor, which creates an electromagnetic field on its surface. If you select the frequency of this field so that it is equal to the frequency of natural vibrations of the plate, the amplitude of the excited sound in it will increase many times over.

Such resonant excitation can be observed experimentally by changing the frequency dependence of electromagnetic energy absorption in a sample in the presence of a constant magnetic field. The position of the active resonance makes it possible to determine the speed of sound with high accuracy. If we now apply an inductive magnetic field and monitor the position of the active resonance in frequency, it turns out that the resonant part will successively increase and decrease, which corresponds to quantum oscillations of the speed of sound.

We have given only one example of a concrete implementation of the EMAT technique and only one example of an experimental study. However, low-temperature changes do not exhaust the possibilities of the new approach.

Progress in this area has been surprisingly rapid. Non-constructive excitation of sound is currently not only one of the areas of radio spectroscopy, but also one of the methods of non-destructive testing of materials, which has found application in various fields of industry.

The entire history of the development of EMAT research shows how fruitful is the interaction of ideas and methods developed in various physical disciplines.
