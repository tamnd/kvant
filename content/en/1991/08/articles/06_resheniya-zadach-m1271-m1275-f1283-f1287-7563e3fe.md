Let $AMNB$ be the desired trapezoid and $NP$ its height. Since a trapezoid is inscribed in a circle, its sides are equal. But the trapezoid is also described, therefore which of its sides is equal to the receipt of the bases, i.e., the segment $AP$. From the similarity of triangles $ANB$ and $NPB$ (Fig. 1) the equality follows

$$AP^2 = NB^2 = AB \cdot BP,$$

(*)

i.e., the point $P$ divides the segment $AB$, as they say, “in extreme and mean ratio.” The construction of such a point can be done as follows: we restore to $AB$ the perpendicular ULAR $BD = \frac{1}{2} AB$ (Fig. 2), put the segment $DE = DB$ on the segment $AD$, and the segment $AP = AE$ on $AB$. If $AB = 2$, then, as is easy to check, $AP = \sqrt{5} - 1$ and for the point $P$ the correspondence (*) is fulfilled.

Then you can, by reconstructing the perpendicular ULAR $PN$ at the point $P$ to the segment $AB$ to the intersection with the half-distance, construct the desired equal-sided trapezoid $AMNB$ and prove (using triangles) that its lateral side is equal to $AP$, i.e. you have the bases, therefore, it is described.

M1272. Prove that for any n positive numbers $a_1, a_2, ..., a_n$ whose sum is equal to 1, the inequality holds
$(1/a_1^2-1)(1/a_2^2-1)...(1/a_n^2-1) \geqslant (n^2-1)^n$.

If we refer two numbers u, v (0<u<v<1) so that their sum remains constant, then the product $uv=(u+v)^2/4-(u-v)^2/4$ increases and the product $(1/u^2-1)(1/v^2-1)=(1-u^2-v^2+u^2v^2)/u^2v^2=(1-u+v^2)/u^2v^2+2uv+1$ decreases. Of n numbers with sum 1, among which not all are equal to 1/n, there is a number u less than 1/n and a number v greater than 1/n (a similar message played a key role in the “shortest proof of Cauchy’s theorem”, see the article by Yu. P. Solovyov in «Kvant» No. 3 for this year). We will refer u and v, preserving the sum, until one of them becomes equal to 1/n; in this case, the product $P=(1/a_1^2-1)\times...\times(1/a_n^2-1)$ will decrease. If in the resulting set not all numbers are equal to 1/n, you can repeat this procedure with another pair of numbers and do this until all numbers are equal to 1/n; in this case P will become equal to $(n^2-1)^n$. Thus, the original product P was no less than this value.

M1274. Prove that the difference between the numbers

$$\frac{1}{2+\frac{1}{3+\frac{1}{...+\frac{1}{n-1}}}}$$

and $$\frac{1}{2+\frac{1}{3+\frac{1}{...+\frac{1}{n-1}+1}}}$$

modulo does not exceed $$\frac{1}{(n-1)!n!}$$

Let $a_k=k+\frac{1}{k+1+\frac{1}{...+\frac{1}{n-1}}}, b_k=k+\frac{1}{k+1+\frac{1}{...+\frac{1}{n-1}+1}}$

- the “tails” of two given continued fractions, starting with $k$, and $h_k$ - the abscimal value of the difference between them. Then

$$a_{k-1}-b_{k-1}=\frac{1}{a_k}-\frac{1}{b_k}=\frac{b_k-a_k}{a_k b_k},$$

so $h_{k-1}<h_k/k^2$ at $k=2, 3, ..., n-1$ (since $a_k>k$ and $b_k>k$); $h_{n-1}=1/n$. Hence the difference we are interested in is estimated as follows:

$$h_1<\frac{h_2}{2^2}<\frac{h_3}{2^23^2}<...<\frac{h_{n-1}}{2^23^2...(n-1)^2}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!n!}.$$

M1275*. The sequence of natural numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$, ... is such that for any $k \geqslant 1$
$$a_{k+2} = a_k a_{k+1} + 1.$$
Prove that for $k \geqslant 9$ the number $a_k - 22$ is.

Let $k > 8$. We will consider the remainders from dividing the numbers $a_i, i > k - 6$ by $m = a_{k-6}$. We use the notation $a \equiv b(\text{mod } m)$ to indicate that the numbers $a$ and $b$ give the same remainder when divided by $m$, and we use the fact that the remainder of the sum (and the product) is equal to the remainder of the sum (respectively the product) of the remainders of these numbers (see the article by A. Egorov in «Kvant» No. 6, 1991).

Since $a_{k-5} = a_{k-6} a_{k-7} + 1 \equiv 1(\text{mod } m)$
and $a_{k-4} = a_{k-5} a_{k-6} + 1 \equiv 1(\text{mod } m)$,
then $a_{k-3} = a_{k-4} a_{k-5} + 1 \equiv 2(\text{mod } m)$,
$a_{k-2} = a_{k-3} a_{k-4} + 1 \equiv 3(\text{mod } m)$,
$a_{k-1} = a_{k-2} a_{k-3} + 1 \equiv 7(\text{mod } m)$,
$a_k = a_{k-1} a_{k-2} + 1 = 22(\text{mod } m)$.

This implies that $a_k - 22$ is divisible by $a_k$. Of course, it is also possible to prove that (starting from some $k$) the numbers $a_k - b$ will also be composite, where $b$ is any other number in the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 7, 22, ..., determined by the same recurrence relation: each number is 1 greater than the product of the two previous ones.

F1283. A man with a heavy cart stands in front of a closed shed. He needs to get to the maazin, located at a distance of 300 m from the shlabum, as quickly as possible. The maximum force with which a person can act on the cart is 500 N, the maximum speed of the cart is 5 m/s, the mass of the loaded cart is 2000 kg. It is known that the gate will open in exactly 30 seconds. What is the minimum time for a person to transport a load to the maazin?

It is clear that by the time the barrier opens, the speed of the cart should be as high as possible. This means that in $t_0 = 30$ s the cart needs to be rolled back, darkened and accelerated forward.

Let the time for moving backwards be equal to $t$, and $t/2$ goes for acceleration with maximum acceleration and the same amount for braking. Then there is time $t_0 - t$ left to accelerate forward. Let's find what speed the cart will get in front of the opening slide:

$$a = \frac{F}{M} = \frac{500 \text{ H}}{2000 \text{ кг}} = 0,25 \text{ м/c}^2$$

$$\frac{a(t_0 - t)^2}{2} = \frac{a(t/2)^2}{2} + \frac{a(t/2)^2}{2} \Rightarrow t_0 - t = t_0(\sqrt{2} - 1),$$

$$v = a(t_0 - t) = at_0(\sqrt{2} - 1) \approx 3 \text{ м/c} < v_m = 5 \text{ м/c}.$$

So, the cart approaches the slab at a speed of $v$, then accelerates to a speed of $v_m$, then moves uniformly and, finally, slows down to a stop (in general, the conditions of the task do not directly say that the speed of the cart is reduced in front of the shops). Finally, the time it takes for the cart to travel to the store after opening the slag will be equal to

$$t_x = \frac{v_m - v}{a} + \frac{l - (v_m^2 - v^2)/(2a) - v_m^2/(2a)}{v_m} + \frac{v_m}{a} \approx 72 \text{ с.}$$

A. Korshkov, N. Korshkova

F1284. A large vessel of mass m is filled with water through a small hole in the bottom of the vessel (see figure). In order to pump water of mass M into a vessel, work A had to be done. The hole is opened and the water begins to flow out. At the same time, raise the vessel so that the upper boundary of the water in it remains at the same height relative to the ground. How much work will have to be done before the container is empty?

If the hole in the bottom of the vessel is small, the rate at which the water level h moves into the vessel is also small, and water flows out of the hole at a speed of $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. Indeed, when empty, in a short time $\Delta t$ a small amount of water $\Delta V$ will flow out of the vessel. The states of water at the beginning and at the end of the interval $\Delta t$ are distinguished by the fact that the potential energy of mass $\rho \Delta V$, located at height h, turned into the kinetic energy of flowing water, also of mass $\rho \Delta V$:

$$\rho \Delta V gh = \rho \Delta V \frac{v^2}{2} \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{2gh}.$$

(As is known, a body falling from a height h moves at this speed.)

When we raise a vessel so that the upper boundary of the water in it remains at the same height relative to the ground, we move the vessel at a very low speed, so that the speed of water flowing out relative to the ground can be considered equal to the speed of its flowing out relative to the vessel.

Let us consider the entire process described in the problem conditions as a whole. At the very beginning, all the water is at the level of the hole in the bottom of the vessel. When water was pumped into the vessel, work A was done. Then the water returned to its previous level, as if it had been freely supplied from a height H. In this case, the water lost energy equal to MgH. This means that by raising water, we do work MgH. Plus, we also spent mgh energy to lift the vessel itself.

So, finally, we have to do the work

$$A' = (M + m)gh - A.$$

F1286. On a non-conducting rod, depicted at right angles, two beads of equal mass $M$ are mounted, carrying charges of opposite signs $Q$ and - $Q$ (see figure). At the initial moment, the beads are motionless and are located at distances $d$ and $2d$ from the vertex of the angle. Let's let them go. Where will the “far” bead be at the moment when the “near” one reaches the top of the corner? Find the speeds of the beads at the moment when the distance between them is $d$.

Since the masses of the beads are the same, their accelerations - they are determined by the corresponding projections of the electrical interaction force - turn out to be proportional to the distances to the vertex of the corner. This means that both the speeds and movements of the beads are also proportional to these distances. Then the first question can be answered immediately - the beads will reach the top of the corner at the same time.

Now - the second question. Let at the moment when the distance between the beads is $d$, the speed of the first bead is $v_1$, and the second is $v_2$. Immediately replace that $v_2 = 2v_1$, and write the law of conservation of energy of a closed system:

$$-\frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \sqrt{d^2 + 4d^2}} = -\frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 d} + \frac{Mv_1^2}{2} + \frac{M(2v_1)^2}{2}.$$

From here we get

$$v_1 = Q \sqrt{\frac{1 - 1/\sqrt{5}}{10\pi\varepsilon_0 d M}}, \quad v_2 = 2Q \sqrt{\frac{1 - 1/\sqrt{5}}{10\pi\varepsilon_0 d M}}.$$

F1287. A closed coil of copper wire was placed not far from the included transformer. The current in it turned out to be out of phase by π/4 relative to the current in the transformer winding. How many times does the power dissipated in a coil change if it is made not of copper, but of nichrome, keeping all dimensions unchanged? Their rum has a resistivity 65 times higher than copper. The amplitude of the current in the transformer winding is considered the same in general cases.

The induction emf $E$ excited in the coil is the same in general cases. To calculate the current in a coil, you need to take into account both active and inductive resistance:

$$X_{обш} = \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2}.$$

In the first case, the phase shift is equal to π/4, therefore, the active and inductive resistance of the copper coil are equal to each other. In the second case, the active resistance is much greater than the inductive one (65 times). This means that the phase shift of the current in the low-chromium turn relative to the current in the transformer winding is practically equal to zero, and the total resistance of the turn is equal to its active resistance.

Now let's find the ratio of powers dissipated in turns:

$$\frac{P_2}{P_1} = \frac{E^2 / (65R)}{(E / \sqrt{R^2 + R^2})^2 R} = \frac{2}{65} = \frac{1}{32,5}.$$

A. Zilberman
