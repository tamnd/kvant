For holography, as is clear from the previous one, radiation fluxes are needed, which are ideally coherent (or close to them) light waves, otherwise the same interference patterns cannot be obtained. Only lasers can produce such radiation, and even then not all of them. Gas lasers have the highest degree of coherence, but the resulting ones are still mostly unsuitable for holographs. But gas lasers, unlike receiver lasers, operate only in continuous mode, so their modulation requires external modulators - this explains the appearance in the GZU circuit of optical gates 3 and 7, which are re-outputted according to the laser beam program.

The information entered into memory is placed on an optical transparency. In the simplest case, it is a photographic plastic with alternating dark and arbitrary areas: the random place corresponds to a logical “one”, the dark one corresponds to a logical “zero”. But, having copied one record into memory, it is necessary to replace it with another one using purely mechanical means - this is slow, inconvenient, and unmodern. Therefore, if you use a controlled optical transparency, in which the dimming or brightening of individual sections of the plate occurs on command from the electronic control unit.

The recording process is as follows. A page of information is “fitted” into the transparency - one or another set of dark and light spots, shutters 3 and 7 are opened for a while, and the page in the form of a hologram is transferred to the upper left corner of the photo plastic. A new page of information is displayed on the banner, and at the same time the deflector comes into play, the purpose of which is to change the direction of the laser beam (from the Latin deflectio - “deviate”). Shutters 3 and 7 “silk” again, and the second page “settles” on the photographic plastic next to the first. The process is repeated many times until the entire photographic plastic is filled - the total number of holograms can be hundreds and thousands. After testing, photoplastic material can be stored for as long as it is suitable. When reading information, the deflector, upon command from the programmer, “aims” at the desired hologram. Then shutter 3 closes (shutter 7 remains closed during reading, “locking” the object beam) - and the restored hologram (i.e., a previously recorded page of black-and-white cells) is displayed on a multi-element matrix photodetector containing a column of photosensitive elements equal to the number of dots in the page. Electrical pulses arising on each element are sequentially fed to the outputs of the devices - the reading of one hologram is completed.

In order for the GZU from laboratory samples (and this is the case today) to step towards perfect industrial products, a perfect elemental base is also necessary. Highly coherent high-power laser receivers; banners containing millions of rapidly reprogrammable cells; deflectors that quickly and seamlessly navigate in thousands of close, but still different directions; multi-element highly sensitive photodetectors with electronic self-scanning of signals - all this, in principle, already exists, all this is rapidly developing and will soon become the property of memory device developers. A pessiist, however, may notice that “just about” has stretched out for more than 30 years. But let's be optimistic about perfect graphic memory.
