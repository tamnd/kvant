Then you will have a vessel with water, at the bottom of which there is a cavity filled with black ink. (To achieve the perfect picture, we needed to repeat this operation more than a dozen times, but, of course, in order to “just look”, two or three attempts are enough.)

Now all that remains is to turn on the light bulbs and observe the movements that occur. The lamp should be placed at a distance of 3-5 cm from the surface of the water, and in order to avoid an explosion, make sure that accidental drops of water do not fall on it.

Let's see what's happening in our bank. When exposed to light, the ink heats up, becomes warmer than water, and tends to rise. This is the general principle of the so-called free thermal gravitational convection. The patterns of specific movements that have arisen and their characteristics will depend on many parameters, the main of which are the intensity

the intensity of the luminous flux, the depth of the liquid poured and the diameter of the cavity in which the ink is poured.

Let us consider the main types of movements observed in these experiments, assuming that the light flux is approximately constant. If the ink occupies a small area at the bottom, then the main movement is an intense fountain of ink (Fig. 1), which, when the liquid level in the vessel is low, reaches its surface. If the level of liquid in the vessel is high, then this single fountain becomes unstable, and at some distances from the bottom a thickening forms, from which a new curved fountain appears to emerge. This process is directed at the bottom. Falling into the liquid, the ink reaches a certain position at which the movement turns or slows down sharply. After some time, a new movement of the drop arises from this position to the next Stationary level. If you slightly increase the diameter of the ink cavity, then the single fountain will be unstable; raise the output with two or three jets (Fig. 2). With an average diameter of the cavity, a self-oscillatory mode occurs - a mushroom-shaped movement (Fig. 3), in which the jets that make up the stem of the “mushroom” either approach or separate from the center. If the area of ​​ink at the bottom is very large, then a chaotic rise of individual curved jets of ink, commonly called thermals, is observed (see figure on p. 44). This movement is typical for the regime of turbulent convection in a flat layer.

As is often the case, experiments that are simple at first glance hide many incomprehensible and unsolved problems.
