The photographic emulsion captures a pattern of interference between these two beams, which appears in the form of light and dark wavy lines and dots without any external signs of the image hidden in it. Nevertheless, this picture, despite the apparent chaos, contains a complete visual image of the subject. The degree of emulsion darkening is determined not only by the intensity of the object beam at this point (as in photography), but by the difference in the phases of the object and reference waves. If the waves arrive in phase, their amplitudes are added, if in antiphase, they are subtracted. The reference wave, not distorted by anything, carries within itself, as it were, a basis for comparison, the beginning of a report for the phase of the object wave; holodraffia is sometimes said to contain "built-in optics". Thus, the interference pattern contains encoded information about both the amplitude and the phase of the wavefront of the light scattered by an object, and this means a complete image of the object.

There was an instantly abandoned, “frozen” light wave reflected by the object. But how can one discern the object itself in this bizarre cipher? Now this is not difficult, you just need to use the principle of wave reversibility. If you illuminate the photographic plastic with the recorded head with a stream of light exactly identical to the reference one (close shutter 4; see figure 1), then the image that appears is indistinguishable in all three dimensions from the appearance of the real object. This process is called photo restoration (reading).

Holography, which makes it possible to record, save, and read a light wave with all its subtleties, naturally attracted the attention of those who thought about optical memory. Back in 1967, fifteen years before the advent of optical disk drives, the concept of holodraffic filling devices (HFDs) began to be developed. But limited technical means did not allow success to be achieved at that time. Today, when optoelectronics is on the rise, it’s a different matter.
