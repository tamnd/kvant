The third place can be any of the four remaining squares, the fourth can be any of the three, for a total of 12 possibilities. We can place the last two squares in two ways, but the resulting arrangements will differ by one transposition, therefore, with one arrangement of the last two squares we will get an arrangement with an even number of transpositions, and with the other - with an odd number. Therefore, the last two places are determined uniquely if all the previous ones are established. So, it turns out that a distance of only 12 arrangements is enough. We will now reduce this number further.

Let's write down these 12 arrangements, grouping them in pairs:

123456 123564 123645
126543 124653 125463
124365 125346 126354
125634 126435 124536

In each sequence, the arrangement differs by only one operation of reversing the last four digits. Therefore, if we learn to move the upper arrangement to its original position, then we can do the same with the lower one. In addition, the upper arrangement in the first pala is required; it no longer needs to be translated. There are 5 arrangements left, for example, the top ones in the remaining pairs. So, the solution has been received, but many questions remain. For example, is it possible to shorten the sequence of operations indicated by V. Khristinkov? From which positions does the transition to the initial one require the maximum number of operations? What is this number? How many different positions can one get in a given number of moves? How many of them cannot be obtained in fewer moves?

To answer these questions, of course, I would like to have a common implementation of the “Tashkent ruler” - “cube - MK”, invented by M. Krasilovsky. It turns out that those who have a programmable microcalculator also have a common implementation, since the required numbers can be implemented on the microcalculator indicator by the corresponding program. True, M. Krasilovsky’s program is designed for a ruler not of 6 numbers, but of 8 (Fig. 3), but it does not in any way prevent, for example, leaving the last two digits alone, and on the other hand, it is interesting to solve the problems posed here for this longer ruler. We are not presenting this 66-step program in hopes that someone will come up with a more cost-effective program. We wish you success in this matter. M. Krasilovsky also proposed two more puzzles “cube - MK-2” and “cube - MK-3”, in which other methods of turning over elements are implemented. Try it and you will create other types of rulers and programs for their implementation on a microcalculator.

In conclusion, we would like to remind you of the “Two-Dimensional Rubik’s Cube” puzzle, published in No. 8, 1991. The situation there is very similar to the one described here - the puzzle does not have a material question, but exists only on the computer display. In our case, it turned out that it was enough to have only a microcalculator.
