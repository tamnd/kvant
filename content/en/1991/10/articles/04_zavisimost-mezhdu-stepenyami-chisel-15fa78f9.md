Of course, the first thing that optical memory benefits from compared to magnetic memory is the recording density on the media. The locality of laser action provides a gain of 1-2 orders of magnitude, reaching a density of $10^8$ bits/cm²; in the future, using the same principles, it is possible to overcome the threshold of $10^9$ bits/cm².
Is it a lot or a little? Judge for yourself. Back in 1986, the entire Encyclopedia Britannica was recorded on an optical CD, and maximum recording density was unthinkable at that time. If used optically, the king of rock, Elvis Presley, the world record holder for the number of recorded songs (659!), would have needed not 90 giant discs, but only one CD.

It is very important that information can be entered into ODN not only in digital, but also in so-called analog form - graphics, figure, drawings, texts can be directly inscribed with a laser bow on the medium. Thanks to this, existing data banks can be converted into additional image banks—artificial memory will become richer and more diverse.

An increase in recording density, the relative simplicity of the device and the expertise of the ODN lead to a decrease in the “density of a bit of stored information” - an indicator that, according to most authorities, turns out to be certain when it comes to choosing between different storage devices. Many people are attracted to ODN by the durability and reliability of information storage - very often this becomes the decisive factor. Optical discs retain burnt marks for both 10 and 30 years, some companies are not afraid to call the round number 100 years, and the infinity of laser reading is a reliable guarantor of preserving the integrity of the recorded data. On magnetic media, everything is different: the domains are destroyed within a few years, and when reading, the head almost scrapes along the surface, sometimes causing damage to it.

True, optical memory is noticeably inferior to magnetic memory in terms of sampling time, which is generally natural: in a larger storage it is more difficult to find what you need than in a smaller one. And one more thing - sometimes it’s not easy for optical disks to break through the barrier of patterns developed for magnetic memory over decades. ODN will not satisfy these standards in everything; their own have not yet been created, and in industry, compliance with the standard is the same as showing a pass at the checkpoint.

While optical and magnetic memories are sorting out their relationships with each other, let’s see how both of them correspond to natural memories—the human brain. Although, what to look at, it’s already clear that these are, as the Yakovs say in Odessa, two big differences. And yet... Naturally, machine and biological memory are identical in functional purpose: filling (“recording”), storage, retrieval (“reading”). In the brain, mechanisms of operational and long-term memory can be distinguished. The first is based on active processes of excitation of nerve cells - this is how speed is achieved, but this requires continuous expenditure of bioenergy. Poznym, the capacity of such a memory is insignificant, and as soon as a person falls asleep, the RAM is erased - everything is like in a computer when the power is turned off. The mechanism of long-term filling is based on chemical transformations of cells (why not domain magnetization reversal?), so after recording, additional energy consumption is not needed.

The capacity of the brain is enormous - in computer language it is estimated at $10^{12}-10^{13}$ bits (this is several thousand densely filled optical disks). The recording process is as follows. A page of information is “fitted” into the transparency - one or another set of dark and light spots, shutters 3 and 7 are opened for a while, and the page in the form of a hologram is transferred to the upper left corner of the photo plastic. A new page of information is displayed on the banner, and at the same time the deflector comes into play, the purpose of which is to change the direction of the laser beam (from the Latin deflectio - “deviate”). Shutters 3 and 7 “silk” again, and the second page “settles” on the photographic plastic next to the first. The process is repeated many times until the entire photographic plastic is filled - the total number of holograms can be hundreds and thousands. After testing, photoplastic material can be stored for as long as it is suitable. When reading information, the deflector, upon command from the programmer, “aims” at the desired hologram. Then shutter 3 closes (shutter 7 remains closed during reading, “locking” the object beam) - and the restored hologram (i.e., a previously recorded page of black-and-white cells) is displayed on a multi-element matrix photodetector containing a column of photosensitive elements equal to the number of dots in the page. Electrical pulses arising on each element are sequentially fed to the outputs of the devices - the reading of one hologram is completed.

A trigger is an electronic device (electronic circuit) that has two states of rest, two states of stable equilibrium.

First, let's turn to the mechanical analysis - to the matrix in the form of a rigid rod with a weight at one end and a hinge at the other, like the matrix in an ancient (double) watch. However, our matnik should be installed on the table with the weight up and the hinge down, i.e., not at all like in a walking watch.

The matnik with a load at the top in the opposite position is unstable: it falls either to the left or to the right of the vertical (see figure) and lies on its side on the plane on which the hinge is fixed. Let us denote (for definition) the equilibrium position on the left by the number zero, and the equilibrium position on the right by one.

Let's return now to the trigger. The output terminals of this electronic device either have a certain voltage, for example, 1 volt, or there is no voltage, i.e. 0 volt. An external pulse arriving at the input of a trigger changes its state, i.e., it transfers the trigger from the stable state in which it is (for example, 0 at the output) to another (1 at the output). It is important that after the transient process, a stable trigger produces a voltage of either one volt or zero. In this sense, the behavior of the trigger is similar to the behavior of a mechanical pendulum mounted with the weight up.
