Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. BUVNOV

One of the reasons for climate change in certain regions of the globe is the thermal heating of the “dirty” atmosphere (some spectra of this problem were discussed in the article “Let's talk a little about the weather...”, published in «Kvant» in No. 11-12 for 1988). Due to severe contamination of combustion products that form as a result of natural or artificial disasters (volcanic eruptions, fires, emissions of dust and earth during strong explosions), the atmosphere becomes opaque to solar radiation, which significantly changes the circulation of air flows. Thus, fires in the oil wells of Kuewait, which broke out during the Gulf War, changed the country's climate for a long time, reducing the average temperature by 10 degrees.

Such “natural” experiments, offered by life itself, necessitate the study of similar phenomena in laboratory conditions. In a period of constant “transition from scarcity to abundance,” it is a sadistic temptation to propose experiments in the “era of stagnation” that use starch or sunflower oil. A visit to the “Svet” store, which had nothing but syrupy protruding 500-watt light bulbs, helped the author overcome this temptation. Let us recall the experiments on modeling the reaction of the Earth's atmosphere to global disasters, where high-power lamps were used. Experiment-

you were conducted by high school graduate P. Berlov. The equipment used is very simple and similar experiments can be replicated at home, all you need is a jar of water, black ink and a high wattage light bulb.

The first step is to create an “atmosphere” (or “ocean”) in which we will observe convective currents. Take a clear jar or aquarium, preferably larger (the shape doesn’t matter). Place a cavity at the bottom of the jar into which the ink will then be poured. If the bottom of the vessel is flat, then such a cavity can serve as a ring attached to the bottom with plasticine; in extreme cases, the edges of the cavity can be made of plasticine. The height of the cavity should not be more than 2-3 mm, since a higher height will lead to significant ink consumption. The success of the experiment largely depends on how to install the cavity. Even a slight tilt can cause ink to spread throughout the vessel.

Now fill the jar with water and let it sit for a while.

The next step is to fill the cavity at the bottom with ink. It is better to take black ink, which clearly shows convective movements and dissolves well in water. It is clear that you cannot first pour ink and then water, since the ink will immediately mix with water. It is best to pour ink at a temperature lower than the water temperature. In this case, the density of the ink is greater than the density of water and the ink spreads well along the bottom of the cavity. So first cool the ink, then draw it with a squeegee or rubber bulb through the contail tube, wipe off the remaining ink and, carefully bringing the tube to the bottom, slowly pour the ink into the cavity. Then you will have a vessel with water, at the bottom of which there is a cavity filled with black ink. (To achieve the perfect picture, we needed to repeat this operation more than a dozen times, but, of course, in order to “just look”, two or three attempts are enough.)

Now all that remains is to turn on the light bulbs and observe the movements that occur. The lamp should be placed at a distance of 3-5 cm from the surface of the water, and in order to avoid an explosion, make sure that accidental drops of water do not fall on it.

Let's see what's happening in our bank. When exposed to light, the ink heats up, becomes warmer than water, and tends to rise. This is the general principle of the so-called free thermal gravitational convection. The patterns of specific movements that have arisen and their characteristics will depend on many parameters, the main of which are the intensity

the intensity of the luminous flux, the depth of the liquid poured and the diameter of the cavity in which the ink is poured.

Let us consider the main types of movements observed in these experiments, assuming that the light flux is approximately constant. If the ink occupies a small area at the bottom, then the main movement is an intense fountain of ink (Fig. 1), which, when the liquid level in the vessel is low, reaches its surface. If the level of liquid in the vessel is high, then this single fountain becomes unstable, and at some distances from the bottom a thickening forms, from which a new curved fountain appears to emerge. This process is directed at the bottom. Falling into the liquid, the ink reaches a certain position at which the movement turns or slows down sharply. After some time, a new movement of the drop arises from this position to the next Stationary level. If you slightly increase the diameter of the ink cavity, then the single fountain will be unstable; raise the output with two or three jets (Fig. 2). With an average diameter of the cavity, a self-oscillatory mode occurs - a mushroom-shaped movement (Fig. 3), in which the jets that make up the stem of the “mushroom” either approach or separate from the center. If the area of ​​ink at the bottom is very large, then a chaotic rise of individual curved jets of ink, commonly called thermals, is observed (see figure on p. 44). This movement is typical for the regime of turbulent convection in a flat layer.

As is often the case, experiments that are simple at first glance hide many incomprehensible and unsolved problems.
