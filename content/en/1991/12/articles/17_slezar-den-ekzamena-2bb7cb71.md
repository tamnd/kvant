(fantasy story)

G. SLEZAR

In the Jordan family, I never spoke about the exam until the very day when their son Dickie turned 10 years old. It was on his birthday that Mrs. Jordan first mentioned the exam in the presence of her son, and her alarming tone angered the father of the family.

- Forget about it! - he said sharply to his wife. “Dicky will pass this test.”

They were sitting at breakfast, and the boy raised his head from the plate with curiosity. Dickie was a teenager with a wary look, straight blond hair and quick, nervous movements. He didn’t figure out what caused the unexpected disagreement, but he knew for sure that today was his birthday, and he first of all wanted harmony in the house. Somewhere there, in a small closet, there were bags of gifts wrapped in ribbons, just waiting to be unpacked, and in the fancy kitchen, something sweet was being prepared at that moment in an automatically controlled oven hanging on the wall. He wanted his birthday to be happy, and therefore the moisture in his mother’s eyes and his father’s gloomy gaze did not correspond to the mood of anxious anticipation with which he greeted the morning.

- What exam are you talking about? he asked.

The mother lowered her eyes.

- This is simply a test of mental abilities, which is designed correctly for children who have reached the age of twelve. You will have such a test next week. But there is no need to worry about her.

- Are you saying that this is like an exam at school?

“Something like this,” the father confirmed, getting up from the table. — By sending the best ones to read your comics, Dickie.

The boy slowly wandered to that part of the living room that, since infancy, had been considered “his” corner. He took a comic book from the rack that was lying on top, but apparently the colorful colorful pictures did not captivate him. Then he trudged to the window and began to peer sadly into the impenetrable curtain of fog.

— Why is it raining today? he asked. - Couldn't he go tomorrow?

The father, collapsed in a wreck, angrily defended the pages with the correctness of the newspaper.

“It’s coming, it means it’s necessary, that’s all.” After a good rain, grass grows.

- Why, father?

- Because it grows, that’s all. Dickie wrinkled his brow.

- By the way, why is the grass green?

“Nobody knows,” the father snapped, but immediately regretted his harshness.

After receiving it, they celebrated Dickie's birthday. The beaming mother handed colorful bundles to her son, and even the father put a smile on his face and patted his son on the head. Dickie kissed his mother and, with a serene expression, exchanged leadership with his father.

Then a cake with a genie candle was brought, and that was the end of the celebration.

An hour later, Dickie sat by the window and watched the rays of the sun unsuccessfully try to break through the clouds.

“Father,” he asked, “is he far from the sun?”

During breakfast, Dickie again noticed moisture in the eyes of the materials. He did not associate it with the exam ahead of him until his father suddenly spoke about this topic.
“Listen, Dickie,” he began, frowning too sternly, “you have one thing to do today.”
- I know, dad. Hope...
- In general, there is nothing to worry about. Thousands of children undergo this test every day. The government wants to know what your abilities are, Dickie. That's all.
“I get good grades at school,” Dickie said somehow uncertainly.
- Here it’s a different matter. This is a special test. You see, they give you a liquid to drink, and then you go to a room where a special machine is installed...
-What kind of liquid is this? - asked Dickie.
- Yes, that’s nonsense. Tastes like a peppermint patty. They want to be sure that you are telling the truth. It is not that the government doubts the creation of purity, but the intake of liquid guarantees such confidence.
Dickie's face showed confusion and fear. He looked at his mother, but she managed to put on her face something vaguely reminiscent of a smile.
“Everything will be fine,” she assured her son.
- Certainly! - picked up the father. - You're a nice boy, Dickie, you'll pass the test. And then we'll go home and dispatch this event. Fine?
“Yes,” Dickie agreed.

He entered the building of the Government Educational Service fifteen minutes before the appointed hour. Stepping on the marble slabs, they crossed a huge arch with columns, passed an arch and entered an automatic elevator, which took them to the fourth floor.

There, opposite room number four hundred and four, a young man in a uniform without insignia sat at a polished table. In his hands he had a notepad with a list of appointments for that hour; the young man checked the Jordans against the list with the letter "D" and then allowed them to enter.

Room number four hundred and four was reminiscent of a courthouse: it was dull, cold and official, with a row of metal tables moving around in it, a row of long benches. There were already several fathers and sons waiting in line; A black-haired woman with thin lips cut up the profiles.

Mr. Jordan filled out the form and returned it to Jensha. Then he said, turning to Dickie:

- Now we won’t have to wait long. When your name is called, go straight to the door at the end of the room.

About eleven minutes to the minute you call out the name Jordan.

“Good luck, son,” said the father, without looking at Dickie. “I’ll come get you when the check is over.”

Dickie walked to the door and checked the round knob. There was a pay station in the room where he found himself, so that the boy could barely distinguish the face of the official in a gray uniform who answered his greeting.

“Sit down,” the official said affectionately. He pointed to the high chair next to his desk. “Is your name Richard Jordan?”

- Yes, sir.
— Your classification number is 600-115. Drink this, Richard.

He took a plastic glass from the table and handed it to the boy. The liquid in the glass looked like skim milk and only tasted like the promised mint patty when separated. Dickie drank the contents before the day and returned the empty glass to the official.

The boy was overcome by an invincible saltiness; Meanwhile, the official was intently writing something on a piece of paper. Then he looked at his watch and
