I'll solve the problem.
Possible options

V. KORSUNSKY

As you know, among physical problems there are those that have more than one answer. It is easy to recognize them in their faces by the conditions in which phrases like “In what cases...?”, “Consider both options”, etc. are found. But sometimes a seemingly innocent task may contain a catch due to the ambiguity of the solution. Often, having learned the correct answer, a student, after some thought, discovers his mistake. What if there is no place to find out the answer? What if this is a test? Or, should the exam be taken at an institute where examiners are trained not to ask leading questions?

Let's try to learn to recognize in person those tasks where the “options” are disguised. Let's look at a few typical examples.

Problem 1. The body is located on an inclined plane with an angle of inclination to the horizon α. Friction coefficient between the body and the plane μ. Find the acceleration of the body.

Here, almost everyone can quite quickly (and figure 1 will help you with this) get the answer that teachers and applicants have long known by heart:

$$a = g(\sin \alpha - \mu \cos \alpha).$$

And yet... Let's check the answer with such, say, numbers: α=30°,

μ=0.8. It turns out that the body moves by itself... up! Where is the mistake?

Let's reread the conditions. It turns out that the body is located on an inclined plane, and does not slide along it. So it follows that a≠0 is open? After this hint, it is not difficult to clarify the solution: you must first find a correspondence between μ and α, at which the body begins to move. It's easy to see that it looks like this:

$$\mu \leq tg \alpha.$$

Then the correct answer will be:

$$a = g(\sin \alpha - \mu \cos \alpha),$$

$$a = 0,$$

if μ>tg α.

Morality. Be careful with the friction force. Remember that the correspondence F_{τp} = μN depends is true only for the case of sliding, but if the body ends up, this, generally speaking, is not the case. Therefore, any task involving friction forces, if its conditions do not contain direct indications of the nature of the body’s movement, requires preliminary—even a small—research. It's not difficult to do it at all. For example: “Assume that the body is moving...” and honestly test this assumption. It is practically impossible to make a mistake any further.

From the Mendleev-Clapeyron equation $pV = \frac{m}{M} RT$, where $V = l^3$, we obtain

$$p = \frac{m}{M} \frac{RT}{l^3} = 9 \cdot 10^6 \text{ Pa}.$$

And the verdict follows just as quickly: FALSE!

Can you guess where the error is? Well, of course, not all the water evaporates, but only enough so that saturated steam forms above the remaining water. And one more thing - a temperature of 373 K, equal to 100 °C, is the only temperature for which the pressure of saturated water vapor must be remembered by heart. At 373 K, water vapor cannot create a pressure greater than 10⁵ Pa. It is this pressure that is established in the vessel. Of course, only a small part of the water will evaporate.

Note that this problem can become multivariate if it is formulated in a general form, like the first one.

Thus, water vapor, massaging under an ideal gas, can also “substitute its legs.” That is why in all problems related to evaporation, condensation of water and other phase transitions, it is useful to start the solution with a help phrase: “Assume that the process has gone through completely” (which process is clear from the conditions of the problem).

Problem 3. A circle of radius R is drawn on a horizontal plane. In the center of the circle, a glass cone with a base radius stands vertically, resting its apex on its center

$R$, raster area 60° and refractive index n. The circle is viewed from a great distance along the axis of the cone. What is the apparent radius of the circle?

This problem, like some other problems in geometric optics, admits variable solutions. The reason is that the image of any point is created by an infinite number of rays, and therefore the nature of the image of an object of finite dimensions significantly depends on the relative position of the extreme rays.

The difference between this task and the previous ones is that there is a danger of “losing” one of two equally natural answers. To find them, we use the principle of ray generation.

Let's mentally send the beam vertically down at the very edge of the base of the cone (Fig. 2). Obviously, the ray, after refraction on the side surface, will deviate towards the center of the circle, so the apparent radius of the circle will be equal to $R' = R$, and even “with a margin” (points located further from the center than A do not provide an image). Everything would be fine, but we forgot about total internal reflection. Indeed, if $n > 2/\sqrt{3}$, then the outermost ray will not pass through the side surface. And all the other rays too.
