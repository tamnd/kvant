FTL SHADOW
AND EXPLODING QUASARS

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
M. FEINGOLD

Let's start by considering a purely everyday (and even somewhat boring) situation: the march is underway. Since the mathematical description of any situation is associated with idealization, our liquid is somewhat different from the real liquid: it consists of infinitesimal droplets that completely fill space and fall back at a constant speed $u$ on the horizontal plane $a$. This sad monotony is broken by a single sign - the water-impenetrable particle $S$ (“umbrella”), under which a continuous chain of small bubbles is formed in a continuous water captivity. We will also consider the dimensions of the umbrella, and thus the dimensions of the resulting bubbles, to be negligible. Since there is no water in the bubbles, we will call them dry holes. And let's call a continuous chain of dry holes stretching from the umbrella a dry thread.

Let the umbrella $S$ first rise motionless at some height above the plane $a$. Then the position of the dry thread $q$ is determined only by the direction of the rain - the thread will be parallel to the rain jets. In our case, it will be answered, and, therefore, directly under $S$ on the plane $a$ a rain-protected area $S'$ will appear. From a geometric point of view, $S'$ can be thought of as the projection of $S$ onto $a$. From an everyday point of view, $S'$ is the only refuge where a microsoppian traveler at $a$ could escape from the rain (Fig. 1).

Suppose now that the umbrella $S$

begins to move at a constant speed $\vec{v}$ at an angle $\theta$ to the lead line. This causes a corresponding movement of the dry thread $q$ and point $S'$. Therefore, our path, in order to stay dry, comes along with $S'$ (Fig. 2). At what speed should the track move?

The “obvious” answer suggests itself: since at $v=0$ the point $S'$ coincides with the projection of the umbrella onto $\alpha$, then at $v \neq 0$ it must move with a speed equal to the projection $\vec{v}$ onto $\alpha$, i.e. $v'=v \sin \theta$. Therefore, the speed of $v'$ cannot exceed $v$. Further, although $S'$ will lag behind the geometric projection $S$ (due to the finite speed of the "producing" jed), it will, of course, always move in the same direction. Finally, since the speed of the umbrella is less than the speed of light $c$, then $v'$ is always less than $c$.

All statements in this answer are incorrect. They do not take into account that if the velocity $\vec{v}$ has a vertical component equal to $v_{\parallel}=v \cos \theta$, the time integral between the formation of a bubble and its fall onto the plane changes over time (i.e., it is different for different bubbles), and this leads to a rather unexpected consequence. To clarify them, let us solve the problem strictly.

First of all, a clear definition of the speed $v'$ should be given. This can be done using the concept of dry holes introduced above. The holes continuously “sprinkle” from the umbrella and, together with the djed, fall at a speed of $u$. The distance is two positions of the umbrella $S$ at times $t_1$ and $t_2$. At each of these moments, a dry hole separates from $S$ and rushes vertically down. Let us denote the moment of the fall of the first of them onto the plane $\alpha$ by $t'_1$, and the second by $t'_2$. Let the distance between the points of incidence of these holes be $\Delta x'$. The speed of movement of the point $S'$ will be the value

$$v' = \frac{\Delta x'}{\Delta t'},$$

Where

$$\Delta t' = t'_2 - t'_1.$$

Let us find how the value $v'$ depends on $u, v$ and $\theta$ (Fig. 3). The distance between the umbrella positions at moments $t_1$ and $t_2$ is $\Delta l = v \Delta t = v(t_2 - t_1)$, so

$$\Delta x' = \Delta l \sin \theta = v \Delta t \sin \theta.$$

$$\Delta t' = \Delta t - \Delta t_{12} = \Delta t \left( 1 - \frac{v}{u} \cos \theta \right).$$

Formula (3) coincides with the well-known expression for the classical Doppler effect, according to which the period $\Delta t'$ between successive signals from the movement of its source changes by $v_{\parallel}/u$ of its own value $\Delta t$ in the case of a stationary source. Here $v_{\parallel} = v \cos \theta$ is the continuing (i.e., parallel to the visual ray) component of the source velocity, $u$ is the signal velocity. The role of the signal in our case is played by dry holes, the source of which is the particle $S$. Due to the obvious dependence of $\Delta t'$ on the continuous motion of the source, the corresponding effect is called the continuous Doppler effect.

Now substituting expressions (2) and (3) into formula (1), we find

$$v' = \frac{v \sin \theta}{1 - \frac{v}{u} \cos \theta}.$$

The same result can be obtained using the concept of a dry thread, substituting that $S'$ occurs when the thread intersects the plane $\alpha$. The thread is carried forward at a speed of $\vec{u}$, and the source from which it is “pulled” moves at a speed of $\vec{v}$. As a result, the thread, while falling, remains parallel to the vector $\vec{w} = \vec{u} - \vec{v}$, and the problem is reduced to finding the speed of the point of its intersection with the plane $\alpha$ (Fig. 4). This problem can be solved quite simply, and we invite the reader to make sure that its solution leads to formula (4).

Now let's see what this formula says.

Firstly, it is clear that if the denominator is small enough, then the value $v'$ can turn out to be much larger, and, in particular, exceed the speed of light. This conclusion does not contradict the law of nature, since it refers to an “object” that is not one physical body. Indeed, two different positions of the point $S'$ on the plane $\alpha$ are due to the incidence of two different dry holes on $\alpha$; and there are no restrictions in nature on the period of time between such events. So it may turn out that our traveler, in order to stay dry, needs to swear at the top of his lungs, even if the umbrella $S$ is still moving, and at $v' > c$ the traveler, no matter how frisky he is, will not stay at point $S'$ at all: superluminal speed cannot be achieved by a material body.

Secondly, if $1 - \frac{v}{u} \cos \theta < 0$, then point $S'$ will move against the geometric projection of the umbrella. For example, when moving the umbrella to the east, the traveler, in order to avoid the sniffle, has to run to the west!

Both of these results become completely clear if we use the idea of ​​a dry thread $q$ falling onto the plane $\alpha$ (Fig. 4). We already know that the resulting point of intersection of the thread with the plane can move as quickly as possible. In particular, when $v_{\parallel} = v \cos \theta = u$, the thread $q$ is parallel to the plane $\alpha$ and falls on it simultaneously with all the properties of the point ($\text{формула (3) при этом дает } \Delta t' = 0$). This means that point $S'$ projects a free trajectory on the plane $\alpha$ instantly, i.e. $v' \rightarrow \infty$ (Fig. 5). Reverse

In this case, from formula (3) it follows $\Delta t' < 0$, i.e., the one of the two holes that was emitted earlier than the other falls onto the plane $\alpha$ later. In the conditions of our task, modifications are acceptable. For example, you can replace rain with falling rays of light. Then in formula (4) the speed $u$ will be replaced by $c$, and the formula for $v'$ will take the form

$$v' = \frac{v \sin \theta}{1 - \frac{v}{c} \cos \theta}.$$ (5)

In this case, the dry hole will turn into a dark hole, and the dry straight line will turn into a dark straight line (the shadow of the umbrella). The denominator in expression (5) is always positive, since $v < c$, therefore, the shadow $S'$ (the ray projection of the umbrella) can only move in the same direction as its geometric projection. However, the speed of this movement can also be superluminal if the right side of expression (5) is greater than $c$, i.e. if

$$\frac{\beta \sin \theta}{1 - \beta \cos \theta} > 1,$$ (6)

It is not difficult to determine the range of values ​​of $v$ and $\theta$ for which inequality (6) holds. This area is shown in figure 7 (colored segment). If the direction and speed of the umbrella are such that the point $v$, $\theta$ is inside the segment, then the shadow of the umbrella moves at superluminal speed. The corresponding phenomenon can be called a superluminal shadow. As can be seen from figure 7, under the conditions of this problem such a shadow can appear when the umbrella moves at a speed exceeding $c/\sqrt{2}$.

Beam $q$ is the instantaneous position of all photons emitted by source $S$ vertically at different times. This beam illuminates the exact spot $S'$ on the plane $\alpha$. The spot $S'$ becomes the image of the corresponding position of the source $S$, which it occupies at the moment of emission of light that hits $S'$. At the same time, our problem turns from an empty mathematical trinket into a model for observing the hail astrophysical process.

Let's imagine that a distant space object explodes and pieces of hotter than luminous plasma fly out in all directions from the explosion. One of the stusks ($S$) is moving towards us with a speed of $v$ at an angle of $\theta$ to the line of sight drawn to the exploded object. Then the image of the tap on the plane $\alpha$, projected by the ray $q$, moves along the plane with a speed $v'$, described by formula (5). But, according to this formula, with values ​​of $v$ and $\theta$ satisfying inequality (6), the speed $v'$ will be greater than $c$. And if we judge the effectiveness only by its visible result, we will come to the wrong conclusion about the superluminal speed of the stusk itself. This “observational” effect was called the relativistic cannonball. For example, if a relativistic cannonball moves almost directly towards the observer, then the observed one appears to be moving in a plane perpendicular to the line of sight (called the picture plane), at superluminal speed.*)

You can give a simple example that clearly shows the reasons for this effectiveness. Let the relativistic plasma knock move towards the Earth at a speed equal to 99% of light, but not directly towards us, but slightly to the side, so that in 300 years it will move in a plane 25 light years from the explosion site. But at the same time, along the line of sight it will approach us by 300·0.99 ≈ 297 light years. Therefore, radiation from the new position of the stusk will come to us not 300 years after the first observation, but after 300-297 = 3 years. Consequently, to terrestrial observation it will seem that the knock has shifted in the picture plane by 25 light years in just 3 years, i.e., it moved at a speed of about eight light years! But this speed is really apparent, since its calculation uses the time interval $\Delta t'$ between the arrival of signals to observation, which (due to the proximity of the source) can be many times less than the time interval $\Delta t$ between the emission of these signals (longitudinal Doppler effective).

*) Of course, the described scheme gives a very forgiving (and not entirely adequate) picture of relativistic astrophysical observation. The real estate agent emits in all directions, which makes it possible to observe its various positions. Due to the extreme remoteness of the source, its image on photographic plates is swept away by fractions of a millimeter per year, even under the conditions considered here. The speed of the corresponding movement in the picture plane $a$, which should be represented as being in the area of ​​motion of the source itself, is calculated by mixing the image on the photographic plastic, taking into account the distance to the source and can be converted to the value $c$.
