It wasn't that long ago that the saloons of the American Midwest could be called a curious game. It starts like this. The bartender called a tipsy cowboy over to him, laid out seven one-dollar coins on the bar in two piles - three and four dollars - and invited the guy to win them: “If you bet five coins, you get twelve.” The cowboy, having counted out five coins, places them on the counter. This resulted in a third pile of coins. The game consisted of players taking turns taking coins from these piles. You are allowed to take any number of coins at a time, but only from one pile. The one who took the last coin took all the others.

The DATs immediately flocked to the riser, vying with each other to tell the cowboy which pile and how many coins, in their opinion, should be taken. You can create a similar picture with us when playing three thimbles. Only if the “thimbles” are Schlera and their trump card is sleight of hand, then in the game of “twelve dollars” the one who can count better wins.

Let’s imagine the thought process of a cowboy who had to make the first move: “I’ll take one whole pile. If there are two left, it will be easier to play. Which one should I take? I’ll take a small one so that there are still a lot of coins left.”

Here he takes a pile of three coins. There will be two piles left - 4 and 5 coins. In response, the bartender takes one coin from the larger pile. You get two identical piles of coins. The cowboy takes a coin from one pile, the bartender takes one from another. The cowboy takes two coins from one pile; the bartender also takes two coins from another pile. Each flavor has one coin left. The cowboy's only option is to take one coin. The bartender takes the other one - the last one - and pours all the remaining coins into the counter.

“Let's play again!” - getting excited, the cowboy demands, laying out another five dollars.

“At your service,” the bartender answers, again protruding from his seven dollars into two piles - 3 and 4 dollars.

“Take your time, think,” his inner voice tells the cowboy. “Okay,” the cowboy answers him, “last time I lost by taking a small pile. What if I take another one? No good - the bartender will again equalize the coins in the other two piles. Let me take one coin from the larger pile. No! The bartender will again arrange two equal piles, taking a small pile. What if you take two coins? Then the bartender will take the middle pile and again there will be two piles with an equal number of coins. Then I'll take some coins from it. Teler, he won’t be able to do his damn thing anymore!” With a confident movement, the cowboy takes three coins from a larger pile and looks at the bartender with a smile.

He takes three coins from the middle pile, and piles of 1, 2 and 3 coins remain on the riser. And then the cowboy begins to realize that he has lost, since no matter what he does, the bartender can always form two piles with an equal number of coins in response. Hanging his head, the cowboy returns to his place amid the laughter of those around him. And it is invisible to the guy that a thousand years ago the Chinese sages learned to play this game.

Who wins: the beginner or his partner? How should you play to win? It turns out that the one who goes first wins. To do this, he must take two coins from a small pile. Any other move loses. So, there are three educational piles - 1, 4 and 5 coins. Teler, no matter how the second player plays, starting with his second move, he can either form two piles with an equal number of coins, or form three piles of 1, 2 and 3 coins. And here the second player can already surrender.

So, we learned to play “him” - that’s what Professor Harvard called this game.

It turned out that it is very convenient to write numbers here not in the decimal number system, which we usually use, but in the double number system, which was so relied upon by the creator of the first computers for its simplicity.

Let us recall that if in the decimal system any natural number is represented as $N = a_n 10^n + a_{n-1} 10^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 10^1 + a_0$, where $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n$ are integers from 0 to 9, then in the double number system it is represented as $N = b_m 2^m + b_{m-1} 2^{m-1} + \dots + b_1 2^1 + b_0$. For example, $1991 = 1 \cdot 10^3 + 9 \cdot 10^2 + 9 \cdot 10^1 + 1 \cdot 10^0 = 1 \cdot 2^{10} + 1 \cdot 2^9 + 1 \cdot 2^8 + 1 \cdot 2^7 + 1 \cdot 2^6 + 0 \cdot 2^5 + 0 \cdot 2^4 + 0 \cdot 2^3 + 1 \cdot 2^2 + 1 \cdot 2^1 + 1 \times 2^5 = 111110001112$. Index 2 indicates that this number is written in the double number system.

Since it is quite difficult to have an unlimited number of one-zol coins, in the future we will operate with coins in stones together. Ch. Bouton discovered an amazing pattern: let’s write numbers under each other in a column, expressing the number of pebbles in each pile in the double number system. If each digit contains an even number of units (we will call such a set of numbers correct), then in this case, if the game is played correctly, the one who goes second will win. Otherwise, the beginner will win.

It is not at all difficult to verify the validity of this. Let's write several numbers in the double system one below the other, for example,

1010101

Let's try to add another number to them so that in each double digit the sum is even, i.e., the resulting system becomes correct. As is easy to see, this can be done in the only way: we put 1 in the digit of this number if the sum there was odd, and 0 if this sum was even. In this case, we should assign the number 1110000. Therefore, if we had a correct set of numbers, then when we replace one (exactly one!) number with another, the set will change to be correct. On the contrary, if a set of numbers is not correct, then it is not difficult to replace one of the numbers with a smaller one so that the resulting set is already correct.

It remains to note that a set of zeros is regular, and a set that is not regular contains at least one zero number.

Let's summarize. If the original set of numbers indicating the corresponding pile of stones is correct, then the player who takes the stones second will win, and if this set is not correct, then the beginner will win. On his first and every subsequent move, he takes such a number of stones from one pile that the correct set remains. Since the number of stones is always killing, and his partner always leaves him at least one stone, the beginner will win.
