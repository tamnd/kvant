I. YAGLOM

Key numbers

We denote the first nine natural numbers with special signs

1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9.

It would be inconvenient to do the same with all numbers encountered in practice. Further, our needs were limited to counting within thousands; it would be necessary to fill in a thousand special characters. Naturally, for a long time people began to choose one or another series of “key” numbers and only designate them with special signs. This is, for example, the Roman numbering system, based on key numbers

1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000.

These numbers in the Roman numeral system are denoted by letters

I (“and”), V (“ve”), X (“ix”), L (“el”),
C (“ce”), D (“de”), and M (“um”).

Such designations were often derived from figure, which once depicted the corresponding numerals (I - finger, V - five fingers, X - two fingers), and are often abbreviations of Latin words (centum - one hundred, demimille - five hundred, mille - thousand).

Since we need the Roman numbering system only as an example, we will consider it in an older, simplified version, when the number “four” was written in the form III, and not in the form IV. The number 3477 in this old Roman system is written as follows:

$$\text{MMMCCCCLXXVII} = 3 \cdot 1000 + 4 \cdot 100 + 50 + 2 \cdot 10 + 5 + 2.$$

The cashier does the same if he has banknotes in denominations of 100 rubles, 50 rubles, 25 rubles, 10 rubles, 5 rubles, 3 rubles and 1 ruble. For a cashier, numbers are key

100, 50, 25, 10, 5, 3 and 1.

Wanting to pay, say, 499 rubles, he first gives out as many hundred-dollar bills as possible so as not to give out too much:

$$499 = 4 \cdot 100 + 99$$

Then he issues as many 50 ruble bills as possible in order to issue no more than the remaining 99 rubles to be paid:

$$499 = 4 \cdot 100 + 1 \cdot 50 + 49,$$

etc. Sometimes he may end up with a remainder less than the next key number. This will be the case in our example after issuing two ten rubles:

$$499 = 4 \cdot 100 + 1 \cdot 50 + 1 \cdot 25 + 2 \cdot 10 + 4.$$
