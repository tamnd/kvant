There is one star in the sky that cannot be classified as a point source. This is the Sun. It has a completely noticeable angular size - $\approx 0,5^\circ$. Most of the light sources around us are also extended. An extended source can be considered as a collection of an infinite number of point sources. In this case, speaking about the Sun, for simplicity, let’s replace it with a “dumbbell” diameter, at the ends of which there are two luminous star points. The luminous flux on the Earth's surface from two stars is approximately twice as large as from one. If this dumbbell is turned along the line of sight, then one star will block the other, and the luminous flux will not increase. Thus, the luminous flux from the illuminated area depends on the orientation of this area relative to the line of sight, and we were even able to grasp the nature of this dependence: the more perpendicular to the line of sight the area is turned, the greater the luminous flux. In addition, the luminous flux is obviously proportional to the area of ​​the illuminated surface $S'$ and the solid angle at which the receiving area of ​​the illuminated surface is visible (Fig. 2):

$$\Delta \Phi = B \cos \theta \cdot S' \Delta \Omega.$$

The proportionality coefficient $B$ is another important formulaic quantity called bright

bone. For this case, the values ​​$I = BS' \cos \theta$

naturally called the power of light. Let us note right away that, although the understandable luminous intensities apply to both a point and an extended source, the understandable brightnesses do not apply to a point source: the latter has neither size nor orientation. Brightness is measured, as a rule, in special units - stilbes (sb). The brightness values ​​of some sources are given in the table:

| Light source | Brightness, Sat |
| :--- | :--- |
| full moon | 0,25 |
| candle flame | 0,5 |
| luminescent lamp | 0,6 |
| incandescent lamp | 600—1300 |
| The sun is at its zenith | $1,6 \cdot 10^5$ |
| discharge spark in gas | $\approx 10^7$ |

You have probably already noticed one oddity in this table: from experience we know that a luminescence lamp gives practically the same amount of light as an incandescent lamp, but its brightness is three orders of magnitude lower. However, there is no mistake here: a luminescence lamp is actually a very dim source, it just has a very large emitting area!

If the luminous surface consists of identical ideal point light sources, then it turns out that $B = \text{const}$ does not depend on the direction. Such sources are called Lambertian.
