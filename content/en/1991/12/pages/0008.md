Let's imagine that a distant space object explodes and pieces of hotter than luminous plasma fly out in all directions from the explosion. One of the stusks ($S$) is moving towards us with a speed of $v$ at an angle of $\theta$ to the line of sight drawn to the exploded object. Then the image of the tap on the plane $\alpha$, projected by the ray $q$, moves along the plane with a speed $v'$, described by formula (5). But, according to this formula, with values ​​of $v$ and $\theta$ satisfying inequality (6), the speed $v'$ will be greater than $c$. And if we judge the effectiveness only by its visible result, we will come to the wrong conclusion about the superluminal speed of the stusk itself. This “observational” effect was called the relativistic cannonball. For example, if a relativistic cannonball moves almost directly towards the observer, then the observed one appears to be moving in a plane perpendicular to the line of sight (called the picture plane), at superluminal speed.*)

You can give a simple example that clearly shows the reasons for this effectiveness. Let the relativistic plasma knock move towards the Earth at a speed equal to 99% of light, but not directly towards us, but slightly to the side, so that in 300 years it will move in a plane 25 light years from the explosion site. But at the same time, along the line of sight it will approach us by 300·0.99 ≈ 297 light years. Therefore, radiation from the new position of the stusk will come to us not 300 years after the first observation, but after 300-297 = 3 years. Consequently, to terrestrial observation it will seem that the knock has shifted in the picture plane by 25 light years in just 3 years, i.e., it moved at a speed of about eight light years! But this speed is really apparent, since its calculation uses the time interval $\Delta t'$ between the arrival of signals to observation, which (due to the proximity of the source) can be many times less than the time interval $\Delta t$ between the emission of these signals (longitudinal Doppler effective).
