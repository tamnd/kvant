V. KORSUNSKY

As you know, among physical problems there are those that have more than one answer. It is easy to recognize them in their faces by the conditions in which phrases like “In what cases...?”, “Consider both options”, etc. are found. But sometimes a seemingly innocent task may contain a catch due to the ambiguity of the solution. Often, having learned the correct answer, a student, after some thought, discovers his mistake. What if there is no place to find out the answer? What if this is a test? Or, should the exam be taken at an institute where examiners are trained not to ask leading questions?

Let's try to learn to recognize in person those tasks where the “options” are disguised. Let's look at a few typical examples.

Problem 1. The body is located on an inclined plane with an angle of inclination to the horizon α. Friction coefficient between the body and the plane μ. Find the acceleration of the body.

Here, almost everyone can quite quickly (and figure 1 will help you with this) get the answer that teachers and applicants have long known by heart:

$$a = g(\sin \alpha - \mu \cos \alpha).$$

And yet... Let's check the answer with such, say, numbers: α=30°,

μ=0.8. It turns out that the body moves by itself... up! Where is the mistake?

Let's reread the conditions. It turns out that the body is located on an inclined plane, and does not slide along it. So it follows that a≠0 is open? After this hint, it is not difficult to clarify the solution: you must first find a correspondence between μ and α, at which the body begins to move. It's easy to see that it looks like this:

$$\mu \leq tg \alpha.$$

Then the correct answer will be:

$$a = g(\sin \alpha - \mu \cos \alpha),$$

$$a = 0,$$

if μ>tg α.

Morality. Be careful with the friction force. Remember that the correspondence F_{τp} = μN depends is true only for the case of sliding, but if the body ends up, this, generally speaking, is not the case. Therefore, any task involving friction forces, if its conditions do not contain direct indications of the nature of the body’s movement, requires preliminary—even a small—research. It's not difficult to do it at all. For example: “Assume that the body is moving...” and honestly test this assumption. It is practically impossible to make a mistake any further.
