We have already discussed above the problems associated with implementing prototypes of exact sources. If it is very small (atom), then it can be located at a finite distance from the observed one. If it is large, then it comes to be carried away to infinity (star). In both cases, a minuscule amount of energy reaches the observable. In a new precise source - a laser - it was possible to combine the incompatible: a finite distance to the observed and high energy.

Here is the answer to the laser riddle. Formula (8) simply does not apply to it, since, as we have already said, the concept of rage does not apply to exact sources.

Let us now see what a laser is practically capable of. The general principle of diffraction states that the angle at which rays diffracted by some interference are deflected is of the order of magnitude $\alpha = \lambda/D$, where $D$ is the characteristic size of the interference, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength. This is approximately what the divergence of the laser beam will be, since it is due to diffraction. Roughly, we can assume that at a distance $L$ the diameter of the laser beam will be equal to

$$d = D + L \frac{\lambda}{D}. \tag{12}$$

Let's take a laser with a beam diameter of 1 cm and a wavelength of 1.06 microns. (For the determination, we are considering a solid-state laser with an aluminum-trieum garnet crystal as the active medium.) At a distance of 1 km, the diameter of the beam, according to (12), increases 10 times, but the malm weight remains: $d' \approx 10$ cm. Let us now substitute the same wood under this beam and estimate the laser power $W$ required to burn it. We have, according to (5) and (11),

$$E' = \frac{W}{\pi (d'/2)^2}; W = 8 \cdot 10^{-5} B_\odot. \tag{13}$$

Laser power is usually expressed in energy units, and the brightness of the Sun, given in our table, is given in light units. The connection between light and energy quantities is not only unambiguous, like, say, the connection between calories and joules: it depends on the spectrum of the source. In particular, for the Sun

$$B_\odot = 2 \cdot 10^7 \frac{B_T}{M^2 \cdot cp}.$$

Thus, from (13) we finally obtain

$$W = 1,6 \text{ кВт.}$$

This power is fulfilled. The same (and even much greater) powers are usually available with a light source (spotlights), but, as we have seen, they cannot be used as a burner.
