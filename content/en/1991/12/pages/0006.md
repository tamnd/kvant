$$\Delta t' = \Delta t - \Delta t_{12} = \Delta t \left( 1 - \frac{v}{u} \cos \theta \right).$$

Formula (3) coincides with the well-known expression for the classical Doppler effect, according to which the period $\Delta t'$ between successive signals from the movement of its source changes by $v_{\parallel}/u$ of its own value $\Delta t$ in the case of a stationary source. Here $v_{\parallel} = v \cos \theta$ is the continuing (i.e., parallel to the visual ray) component of the source velocity, $u$ is the signal velocity. The role of the signal in our case is played by dry holes, the source of which is the particle $S$. Due to the obvious dependence of $\Delta t'$ on the continuous motion of the source, the corresponding effect is called the continuous Doppler effect.

Now substituting expressions (2) and (3) into formula (1), we find

$$v' = \frac{v \sin \theta}{1 - \frac{v}{u} \cos \theta}.$$

The same result can be obtained using the concept of a dry thread, substituting that $S'$ occurs when the thread intersects the plane $\alpha$. The thread is carried forward at a speed of $\vec{u}$, and the source from which it is “pulled” moves at a speed of $\vec{v}$. As a result, the thread, while falling, remains parallel to the vector $\vec{w} = \vec{u} - \vec{v}$, and the problem is reduced to finding the speed of the point of its intersection with the plane $\alpha$ (Fig. 4). This problem can be solved quite simply, and we invite the reader to make sure that its solution leads to formula (4).

Now let's see what this formula says.

Firstly, it is clear that if the denominator is small enough, then the value $v'$ can turn out to be much larger, and, in particular, exceed the speed of light. This conclusion does not contradict the law of nature, since it refers to an “object” that is not one physical body. Indeed, two different positions of the point $S'$ on the plane $\alpha$ are due to the incidence of two different dry holes on $\alpha$; and there are no restrictions in nature on the period of time between such events. So it may turn out that our traveler, in order to stay dry, needs to swear at the top of his lungs, even if the umbrella $S$ is still moving, and at $v' > c$ the traveler, no matter how frisky he is, will not stay at point $S'$ at all: superluminal speed cannot be achieved by a material body.

Secondly, if $1 - \frac{v}{u} \cos \theta < 0$, then point $S'$ will move against the geometric projection of the umbrella. For example, when moving the umbrella to the east, the traveler, in order to avoid the sniffle, has to run to the west!

Both of these results become completely clear if we use the idea of ​​a dry thread $q$ falling onto the plane $\alpha$ (Fig. 4). We already know that the resulting point of intersection of the thread with the plane can move as quickly as possible. In particular, when $v_{\parallel} = v \cos \theta = u$, the thread $q$ is parallel to the plane $\alpha$ and falls on it simultaneously with all the properties of the point ($\text{формула (3) при этом дает } \Delta t' = 0$). This means that point $S'$ projects a free trajectory on the plane $\alpha$ instantly, i.e. $v' \rightarrow \infty$ (Fig. 5). Reverse
