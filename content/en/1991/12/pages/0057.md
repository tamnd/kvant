(fantasy story)

G. SLEZAR

In the Jordan family, I never spoke about the exam until the very day when their son Dickie turned 10 years old. It was on his birthday that Mrs. Jordan first mentioned the exam in the presence of her son, and her alarming tone angered the father of the family.

- Forget about it! - he said sharply to his wife. “Dicky will pass this test.”

They were sitting at breakfast, and the boy raised his head from the plate with curiosity. Dickie was a teenager with a wary look, straight blond hair and quick, nervous movements. He didn’t figure out what caused the unexpected disagreement, but he knew for sure that today was his birthday, and he first of all wanted harmony in the house. Somewhere there, in a small closet, there were bags of gifts wrapped in ribbons, just waiting to be unpacked, and in the fancy kitchen, something sweet was being prepared at that moment in an automatically controlled oven hanging on the wall. He wanted his birthday to be happy, and therefore the moisture in his mother’s eyes and his father’s gloomy gaze did not correspond to the mood of anxious anticipation with which he greeted the morning.

- What exam are you talking about? he asked.

The mother lowered her eyes.

- This is simply a test of mental abilities, which is designed correctly for children who have reached the age of twelve. You will have such a test next week. But there is no need to worry about her.

- Are you saying that this is like an exam at school?

“Something like this,” the father confirmed, getting up from the table. — By sending the best ones to read your comics, Dickie.

The boy slowly wandered to that part of the living room that, since infancy, had been considered “his” corner. He took a comic book from the rack that was lying on top, but apparently the colorful colorful pictures did not captivate him. Then he trudged to the window and began to peer sadly into the impenetrable curtain of fog.

— Why is it raining today? he asked. - Couldn't he go tomorrow?

The father, collapsed in a wreck, angrily defended the pages with the correctness of the newspaper.

“It’s coming, it means it’s necessary, that’s all.” After a good rain, grass grows.

- Why, father?

- Because it grows, that’s all. Dickie wrinkled his brow.

- By the way, why is the grass green?

“Nobody knows,” the father snapped, but immediately regretted his harshness.

After receiving it, they celebrated Dickie's birthday. The beaming mother handed colorful bundles to her son, and even the father put a smile on his face and patted his son on the head. Dickie kissed his mother and, with a serene expression, exchanged leadership with his father.

Then a cake with a genie candle was brought, and that was the end of the celebration.

An hour later, Dickie sat by the window and watched the rays of the sun unsuccessfully try to break through the clouds.

“Father,” he asked, “is he far from the sun?”
