Before taking measurements, think about the meaning of equation (4), why the jet decreases downwards and according to what law its radius decreases (see example 4).

Let's take measurements. And here is the result. In order for the pingong ball to sink halfway under the stream, for a stream of length $L = 60$ cm with an initial radius $r_0 = 0,8$ cm, the water consumption $Q$ should be approximately equal to $130 \text{ cm}^3/\text{с}$. This gives $C_x$ a value close to 0.5. If you measure the length of the stream $L$ in the experiment, then you will need to measure the flow of water in the stream so that the ball remains submerged to the same depth. However, no matter what the water flow rate, the value of $C_x$ remains practically the same. The results of our measurements $C_x$ at different water flow rates in the stream are shown in figure 5; the dotted curve is the calculated value of the diameter of the jet $D = 2r$, which it has, the magnitude of its contact with the ball. Check for yourself that for jets of different lengths, regardless of the values ​​of $r$ and $v$, approximately the same value of the hydrodynamic drag coefficient is obtained, namely $C_x = 0,5 \pm 0,1$.

Let's now try to comprehend the result obtained. When calculating the resistance force $F_x$ we used Newton's formula (2). Firstly, since this formula describes the experiment well ($C_x$ almost does not change), we can say that we tested it fairly (in the speed range $v \approx 1 - 10 \text{ m/c}$). Secondly, by measuring the value $C_x$, we found that, strangely enough, the ball “takes” only about 1/4 of the jet’s impulse, the rest “goes” into the water in which the ball floats: the hydrodynamic drag coefficient turns out to be about 1/2, and not 2, as would be the case in the case of an absolute inelastic “impact” of the jet on the ball! The latter experimental fact is not so obvious. At first glance, it seems that a narrow jet falling on the almost flat top of the ball ($r \ll R$) should transfer most of its momentum to it.
