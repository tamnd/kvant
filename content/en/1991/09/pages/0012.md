In each specific case, calculating the value of wave resistance is a complex task, which can usually be solved only by resorting to experimental conditions using empirical data. But it is relatively easy to estimate how high the wave resistance can be.

Consider, for example, the rectilinear and uniform movement of a motor boat on a deep lake. From the hull of the motorboat, two oblique waves diverge symmetrically along the course - bow and body, which, when added, give a characteristic picture of waves, well known to all fish and swimming enthusiasts. The angle $\alpha$, which is formed by the crests of the waves with the direction of movement of the boat, does not depend on its speed and is equal to about $20^\circ$. On each side of the boat's course line, 2-3 small ridges stand out. The amplitudes of the remaining waves are much smaller, so when making estimates you can completely ignore them - they make a small contribution to the overall result. The waves from the boat spread over hundreds of meters, fading slightly over time.

We will approximately consider the wave crest to be a triangle with height $H$, measuring this value from the equilibrium level of the lake. The length of the base of the triangle is equal to $\lambda/2$, where $\lambda$ is the length of the wave propagating from the boat. The resistance of the front of waves that are born per unit time is numerically equal to $v \cos \alpha$. The work spent on creating the crest is converted into potential energy of the wave.
