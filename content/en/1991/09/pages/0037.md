It is the positomy Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation $pV=NkT$ that remains valid at any temperature, as long as the gas is so rarefied that the interaction of its molecules can be neglected (pressure is associated exclusively with the translational motion of the molecules). But in the expression for gas energy $W_{\text{газа}}=Ni/2kT$ at high temperatures one must substitute one value $i$ (all degrees of freedom are involved), and at low temperatures - another (some degrees of freedom are “frozen”).

And finally, before moving on to other systems, let’s not step on the gas, let’s give one more correspondence - between the pressure of the gas and its energy. Comparing the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation ($pV=NkT$) and the expression for the energy of translational motion ($W_{\text{пост}}=3/2NkT$, here the number of translational degrees of freedom $i_{\text{пост}}=3$), it is easy to find

$$pV=\frac{2}{3}W_{\text{пост}}.$$

The fact that the energy of a gas is associated exclusively with the total number of its degrees of freedom, and the pressure, in turn, is expressed through energy, that is, again through the number of degrees of freedom (translational only), brings up a very important idea. Or maybe it is not necessary for the system to consist of a large number of non-interacting molecules? maybe everything is determined only by degrees of freedom, and these degrees of freedom are already connected with molecules (and then we have an ideal gas) or with something else - this, so to speak, is the second question?

To live this idea out, let’s consider a system that is very far in its external characteristics from ordinary gas. Imagine a closed cavity, the steps of which are maintained at a constant temperature $T$. Like any heated body, steppes emit internal electromagnetic waves. The cavity appears to be filled with electromagnetic material due to.

The idea that thermal radiation in equilibrium with the walls can be considered as an ideal gas, where different degrees of freedom correspond to vibrations of different frequencies, arose as soon as we began to study this object. This seemed all the more plausible since the pressure due to radiation is related to its energy in almost the same way as for a general ideal gas:

$$pV=\frac{1}{3}W.$$

However, the first attempts to realize the energy of such a “gas” led to discouraging results. The fact is that waves of any frequency, however high, can exist in a position. And if the energy $1/2kT$ is allocated to each such “degree of freedom”, then the total energy of all waves turns out to be infinitely large. It was this paradoxical situation with thermal radiation in a cavity (it was called the “ultraphilic catastrophe”) that forced Planck to put forward the quantum hypothesis discussed above. He suggested that the energy of vibration with frequency $v$ can only change in portions equal to $hv$ ($h$ is Planck’s constant). This means that all vibrations with frequencies for which $hv \gg kT$ turn out to be “frozen” and do not contribute to energy. Based on his hypothesis, Planck derived a formula for the frequency distribution of the energy of electromagnetic oscillations in a cavity and obtained excellent agreement with experimental data. The similarity between an ordinary ideal gas and a gas of electromagnetic waves turned out to be so great that special particles—photons—began to be associated with individual elementary waves with energies $hv$.
