Example 6. Newton's formula for calculating resistance forces is not always valid. This formula does not allow, for example, to correctly calculate the force acting on a spoon that is immersed in a jar of honey. She doesn’t describe how a small table ball sinks in a tall vessel with vegetable oil, and she can’t explain at all why the milky fog that mushroom pickers love and which causes so much trouble for drivers, stove-makers and car owners takes so long to fall.

In all of these cases, to determine the resistance forces, you need to know not only the shape and size

measures of the body, the relative speed of movement in the medium and its density, but also another characteristic of the medium, which is called the viscosity coefficient and is generally denoted by the letter $\eta$. This coefficient shows how much or little internal, or, as they say, viscous friction, is in a liquid or gas.

If viscous forces are decisive when a ball moves in a medium, then the ball experiences braking resistance, which can be calculated using the Stokes formula

$$F_x=6\pi\eta Rv,$$

and not according to Newton's formula (2).

But how do you know whether the medium is viscous and what formula should be used when determining the resistance forces in each specific case?

Let's consider a spherical drop of water freely falling in the air (or a hailstone ball, avoiding differences in the densities of water and ice). We will be interested in the steady-state speed of the drop falling, which can be changed and from the condition of dynamic equilibrium we can find how the LOB resistance depends on the speed, change of drop and parameters of the medium (its density and viscosity coefficient). We will assume the mass of the drop to be constant, i.e., we will consider the condensation of vapor and the evaporation of moisture from the drop.
