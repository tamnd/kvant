Formula (2) is used in practice, say, when estimating the force of wind pressure on a sail or building, the force of resistance acting on a moving object, be it an airplane, a bird, an automatic vehicle or a submarine. In aerodynamics, formula (2) is one of the main ones. However, the following must be kept in mind. Since the pressure in the flow depends on the flow speed, the resulting force of hydro- or aerodynamic drag depends on how the liquid or gas flows around the body: what is the speed of the medium near the surface, whether the flow is smooth or hovering, where the so-called raw material of the flow occurs at the boundary of the body, etc. It is the variety of types of flows of a continuous medium near the body (see, for example, Fig. 6) that often leads to difficulties in determining the value $F_x$ and $C_x$. For bodies such as a ball, a disk (transverse to the flow), a not very long cylinder, etc., the values ​​of $C_x$ in a wide range of speeds are usually of the order of 0.1-1. For a well-streamlined remote drop-shaped body with a rounded head, the drag coefficient can reach values ​​of 0.04–0.06.

The value of the coefficient of hydrodynamic resistance is several times smaller.
