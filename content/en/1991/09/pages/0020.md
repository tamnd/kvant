In this case, parallelograms are skewed squares, but for many other lattices this is not the case, so we will call these figures parallelograms.) What is the area of ​​such a parallelogram? Try some examples.

Studge scribbled in his notebook.

— At $p=17$ the parallelogram has an area of ​​17 square units. And at $p=41$ the area is 41 square units. I think that in general, for a prime number $p$, the area of ​​the parallelogram should be $p$ square units.

- This is indeed true, although I will not prove it now. But you might be wondering why I paid attention to the area of ​​a parallelogram.

- Yes, I thought about it.

“This is due to theories proved by Herman Minkowski, a Russian mathematician who taught in Germany. He invented the space (Minkowski space), which Einstein took advantage of in his theory of relative smallness, then the nodal points of the lattice should be close to each other. Therefore, some of them should turn out to be completely similar from the origin. He proved the theorem by strictly formulating his idea. Suppose we have a lattice formed by parallelograms, and suppose also that we draw a circle centered at the origin. Minkowski's theorem states that if the area of ​​a circle is at least four times the area of ​​a parallelogram, then there is at least one nodal point of the lattice, other than the origin, that lies inside the circle. We can use Minkowski's theorem to prove that a lattice point close to the origin solves the problem of representing the prime number $p$ as the sum of two squares. Let's take $p=17$ for example. The area of ​​the parallelogram is also 17. Take a circle of slightly larger radius than the square root of 17, say radius 5. Then the area of ​​the circle is $5^2\pi=25\pi=78,54$, which is greater than $4\times17=68$, so the conditions of Minkowski's theorem are met. So far so good?

- I'm watching every word.

— According to Minkowski's theorem, a lattice point that does not lie at the origin of coordinates is located inside the circle. Known for her $(x, y)$. Then the sum $x^2+y^2$ is less than or equal to the square of the radius of the circle. In other words, $x^2+y^2\leq25$. However, for lattice points, $x^2+y^2$ is a multiple of 17. This multiple is not zero, since this lattice point does not coincide with the origin. What zero multiples of 17 are less than or equal to 25?

“The number itself is 17,” said Studge. “No big ones.”

- That's it! Therefore, $x^2+y^2$ must be equal to exactly 17, and this is the solution to the problem. The same method of work in the general case, he boasted of two. Minkowski's visionary idea gave birth to a new mathematics called the geometry of numbers, according to the purpose of his book written in 1896. It uses geometry to study the theory of numbers. It would seem that these areas of the word are connected with each other!
