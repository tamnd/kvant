Example 7. There is one more - third - mechanism of braking of bodies, which is also often encountered in practice and which, under certain conditions, can become decisive.

Pour water into a bucket or bathtub and immerse the pencil vertically into it to a certain depth. Start stirring the pencil parallel to the surface of the water. If the speed of the pencil is low, then you won’t notice anything interesting. But if the pencil is stirred quickly, then a diverging group of waves will form behind it, which will carry a certain energy. The formation of waves consumes the power of movement (in this case, the hand), which provides resistance to the medium.

From our experience, we know that waves often appear behind a moving body, and they can be of various weights. The pattern of waves from a pencil or motorboat in deep water is different from the small capillary waves that can be observed in a bucket, glass or saucer. The waves from a motorboat in shallow water have a different appearance than in deep water. A supersonic aircraft creates a shock wave in a three-dimensional medium (air), rather than at an interface (water and air), like a pencil. In addition, unlike ordinary waves on water, a shock wave in a gas is accompanied by a fluid rolling of the medium and its heating.
