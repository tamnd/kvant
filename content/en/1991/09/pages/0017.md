Bob Scratchit also couldn't get enough of it in his less comfortable bed. He kept thinking about where he could find the true logical conclusion in time to give Jim a gift from Russia.

Stooge finally got his way, but after 29=4+25 seconds there was a terrible howl followed by a loud explosion. Was it Pastor Snows who threw his chamber pot at Widow Cleene's cat again? No, it looks like the noise was in his own bedroom. Studge felt the fear shaking off. A luminous body appeared in front of him.

“I,” the vision thundered, “two words from the chest.”

“Do whatever you want to me, you vile scoundrel, I’m too tired to resist.”

The spirit, speaking louder than the chestiest, placed the box on the table and commanded:

- Open it.

Inside, Studge found something similar to a television screen and a turntable, like a telephone. He took the device in his hands:

- What is this?

- Modulocop. It allows you to ignore what doesn’t interest you, what you don’t want to see.

— Like poor people, for example? I do this all the time anyway.

“But with the help of a modulocop you will be able to look at things in an unusual light, in isolation from conventional logic. In particular, if you use a turntable to set the device to a certain number, you will not see a single number that is a multiple of it. Set the device to “channel 4” and look at my fingers. How many fingers can you see?

- Two. Where did the other eight go?

— The modulocop first subtracts eight fingers, since 8 is the most multiple of 4, which is less than 10; then he shows the remaining two fingers. Mathematicians describe the operation of the device succinctly: “10 modulo 4 equals 2.”

Studge looked through the modulocopter at the 147 coins lying on his stack. Seeing only three, he screamed. Having removed the modulocop, he was relieved to find that all his coins were in place.

“Stop playing,” he strictly ordered the two. “Leave the device tuned to channel 4, take out your notebook and look at the table of prime numbers.” What do you see?

- Nothing but ones and threes, and two, which is an exception. Every prime number that is a sum of squares looks like 1; All prime numbers that are not sums of squares look like 3! But, of course, the conditions 4k+1 or 4k+3 work here - either 1 or 3, if we take modulo 4.

He thought for a moment.

“But I still don’t understand why the value of a prime number, taken modulo 4, is important.”

— Instead of simple numbers, look at squares.

Studge examined the table through the modulecop. Duty followed.

“All I see,” he said finally, “is that the equation 1=1+0 is repeated over and over again.”

- Yes. Why, do you understand?

— Because squares modulo 4 can only be zeros and ones?

- That's it. If you square an even number, you will get a multiple of an even number, which through a modulocop will look like 0. If you square an odd number, then any result - 1, 9, 25, 49 - will be 1 greater than a multiple of 4. Therefore, the sums of squares modulo 4 will look like 0+0=0, 0+1=1, or 1+1=2. CHERO is missing?

“Three,” said Studge.

- Right. The sum of two squares, taken modulo 4, can be equal to 0, 1 or 2, but not 3. Therefore, prime numbers (in fact, raise any numbers) of the form 4k+3 cannot in any way be the sum of two squares. Now do you see why module 4 is important? - with these two initial words
