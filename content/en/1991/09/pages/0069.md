Feeling how everything inside me was shaking, I walked next to Selina to the house. The clean room outside the window where the fireplace was lit was empty, but children's toys lay in disarray on the floor. Cubes with letters and a small wheelbarrow the color of peeled carrots. While I was watching, a boy came into the room and began to open up the cubes with his feet. He didn't notice us. A few seconds later, a young woman entered the room and picked up the boy in her arms, squeezing him cheerfully. She, as before, went to the window. I smiled shyly, but neither she nor the boy returned my smile.

Goosebumps ran across my skin. Are they both blind? I quietly backed away.

Selina screamed. I turned to her.

- Rug! - she said. - It will get wet.

Having crossed the yard under the jam, she grabbed the finished fishy blanket and ran back, straight to the door of the house. Something convulsively slipped into my subconscious.

- Selina! - I shouted. - Don't go in there!

But I'm late. She opened the wooden door, looked inside and remained with her palm pressed against the door. I walked up to her and took the blanket from her limply unclenched fingers.

Closing the door, I looked around the inside of the house. The clean room in which I had just seen the woman and child was filled with rickety furniture, littered with old newspapers, clothes, and dirty dishes. The room was damp and there was no one in it. The only thing I recognized was a small car - broken, with peeling paint.

I shut the door and ordered myself to kill what I saw. Some men keep their house in order even when they live alone. Others don't know how to do this.

Selina's face was as white as a sheet.

- I don’t understand... I don’t understand...
“Slow glass, but double-sided,” I said softly. “Light passes through it both into the house and out of the house.”
- You think?...
- Don't know. This doesn't concern us. Now pull yourself together. There comes Hagen with the glass. “The spasm of hatred that had shaken my insides suddenly disappeared.

Hagen entered the yard, holding a rectangular frame wrapped in oilcloth under his arm. I handed him the check, but he was looking at Selina. He, apparently, immediately realized that our insensitive fingers were fishing in his soul. Selina looked away. She suddenly became old and ugly and stubbornly stared at the horizon.

“Let me take the rug from you, Mr. Garlen,” Hagen finally said. “You were rightfully at a loss.”
- Nothing. Here's the check.
“Thank you.” He was still looking at Selina with a country expression of supplication. “Thank you for the purchase.”
“Thank you,” I answered with the same stereotypical phrase, with the same senseless superiority.

I took the heavy frame and led Selina to the three along which we had to go down to the highway. When we reached the first soaked jam and skolsky steps, Hagen called out to me:

- Mister Garlen!
I looked back reluctantly.

“I’m not guilty of anything,” he said in an even voice. “Their common truck was killed on the highway six years ago.” The driver was drunk. My son just turned seven. I have the right to save at least something.

I nodded silently and began to walk down the stairs, hugging my wife tightly, rejoicing in the feeling of her hand on my shoulder. Before turning, I looked behind the streams of djedzem and saw that Hagen, slouched, was sitting on the bench where we saw him when we entered the yard.

He was looking towards the house, but I could not discern whether anything was visible in the window.
