In a gas, the body affects the particles of the medium, changing their speed. According to Newton's third law, a force acts on a body from the environment in the opposite direction, which is called the resistance force. Let's consider the component of the drag force, which is directed along the line of motion (say, the $X$ axis) and which aviators call LOB resistance. From Galileo’s principle of relative motion it follows that to determine this force of uniform motion, it makes no difference whether the body is moving in a stationary medium, or whether the medium is moving towards it with the same speed, and the body is motionless. Let's start with a simple example.

Example 1. Let a disk, ball or cylinder with radius $R$ move with a speed $v$ along its axis in space, filling a set of stationary particles that do not interact with each other (Fig. 1; the mass of each particle is $m$, their concentration $n$). What are these particles? One can imagine, for example, a rarefied cold gas in which the movement of molecules is not taken into account. The one who imagines that the particles are snowflakes will also be right, and the problem describes the movement of a snowball (simply a snowball) flying in the air and colliding with snowflakes that float towards it upon collision, etc.

Per unit time, the body encounters $N=nvS$ particles on its path, where $S=\pi R^2$ is the area of ​​the transverse section

body readings. If the collisions of particles with a body are inelastic, then the drag force is equal to

$$F_x = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = mvN = 2S \frac{\rho v^2}{2},$$

where $\rho = mn$ is the density of the medium.

In a similar way, we can calculate the resistance force $F_x$ acting on a body with an arbitrary profile. You just need to know the speed, density and area of ​​force that a body experiences when moving in a permitted environment.

The situation is somewhat more complicated when the impact is elastic, that is, when a particle hits an arbitrary surface and bounces off it. But even in this case $F_x \sim v^2$, only the proportionality coefficient in formula (1), generally speaking, depends on the shape of the body.

How does a continuous medium differ from a particle independence system? After all, a continuous medium, be it a liquid or a dense gas, also consists of molecules or atoms.
