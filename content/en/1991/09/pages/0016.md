Which prime numbers are sums of squares?

-Who is this guy in black?
— The gentleman in the wig, Stooge, is the great mathematician Pierre Fermat, best known for his unproven “last” theorem, and one of the founders of number theory. He writes a letter to his hider, Marin Mersenne. If we returned to the present, we could read the letter in the original, it is dated December 25, 1640. The letter reports an amazing discovery.

- And what kind of discovery was this?
— It is known as Fermat's Christmas theorem. Some prime numbers can be represented as the sum of the squares of two numbers: for example,

$$5 = 1 + 4 = 1^2 + 2^2$$

or

$$13 = 4 + 9 = 2^2 + 3^2.$$

Other prime numbers cannot be represented in this form: for example, 3 or 11. Fermat found a way to distinguish between these prime numbers.

Studge took a tattered notebook from his pocket and began to count. He soon solved the problem for all numbers less than 100 (see table top left).

-Have you noticed a pattern? - asked two theorems of the past. Studge shook his head. “Okay, since today is your birthday, I’ll give you two tips.” First, you need to ignore the prime number 2, which is an exception. (This is the only even number among all prime numbers!). Secondly, we need to consider the remainder when divided by 4. Every odd prime number is greater than any multiple of 4, either by 1 or 3; that is, it can be represented either as 4k+1 or 4k+3. For example, 5=4×1+1 is represented in the form 4k+1.

Stooge added a new column to his table showing which form of representation belonged to which prime number, 4k+1 or 4k+3; Now the prime numbers already showed a pattern.

“Prime numbers represented as a sum of squares seem to be expressed in the form 4k + 1,” said Stooge with surprise. “Except 2, the numbers that you say are an exception.”
