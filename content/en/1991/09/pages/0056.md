a distant object is observed (for example, sitting on the Sun), the planet moves with a speed of $v_n$, and the vehicle moves with a speed of $v_a$; $\alpha$ is the angle between the directions of these speeds. From the point of view observed on the planet, the device is approaching it at a speed of $v_+$ and, obviously, will move away at the same speed, changing only its direction by an angle of $\beta$.
In these considerations we mean that the coordinate system associated with the planet is practically inertial: after all, the projet of the apparatus affects the motion of the planet fantastically weakly. From a distant point of view, it is observed that the vehicle moving away from the planet has a speed of $v_{++}$.
Using the parallelogram rule, we get
$$v_{++}^2 = v_a^2 + v_n^2 - 2v_a v_n \cos \alpha$$
And
$$v_{++}^2 = v_a^2 + v_n^2 - 2v + v_n \sin (\beta/2).$$
It is immediately clear that if the vehicle and the planet were moving towards each other ($\alpha \approx 180^\circ$), and as a result of the maneuver, the velocity vector of the vehicle changed its direction to the opposite direction ($\beta \approx 180^\circ$), then double the speed of the planet was added to the speed of the vehicle. This is the most advantageous case (Fig.3). But for the maneuver to be quite effective in terms of speed increment, it is desirable to have $\beta$ and $\alpha \geq 90^\circ$. Of course, the apparatus must at the same time pass over the surface of the planet and not crash into it, i.e., the minimum approach distance must exceed the radius of the planet. The laws of celestial mechanics indicate that in this case the maximum speed of the vehicle’s retreat relative to the planet after their approach will be equal to $v_{maxc} = 0,46v_{II}$, where $v_{II}$ is the separation speed (second cosmic velocity) at the surface of the planet. If a planet orbits a star, then $v_{maxc} \approx v_{opc} + 0,5v_{II}$, accordingly, where $v_{opc}$ is the orbital speed of the planet.
By the way, in order for the vehicle to leave the solar system after its merger with the planet, the condition $v_{maxc} \geq \sqrt{2}v_{opc}$ must be met, i.e., the vehicle’s speed must be greater than the speed of separation from the planet’s orbit. As can be seen from Table 1, not all planets of the Solar System can play the role of effective conditions: only giant planets, with a single perturbation maneuver of the device, can throw it out of the Solar System. The fourth column of the table shows what speed the device will have if it leaves our planetary system in this way.
