I consider the following problem to be one of my favorite geometric discoveries:

Problem 22. What is the maximum number of lines that can be proven through some point in space so that all pairwise angles between them are equal?

Answer: 6. At the same time, I understand perfectly well that this fact was probably known in ancient times, for example, to Archimedes.

It is not difficult to prove that there cannot be more than six such lines. Indeed, let $l_1$ and $l_2$ be two lines of our family passing through the point $O$. Then all remaining straight lines must belong to the intersection of two finite surfaces: the axis of one of them is the straight line $l_1$, and the straight line $l_2$ is one of the generators; for the second, on the contrary, $l_2$ is the axis, $l_1$ is the generator. Such two finite surfaces intersect along at most four straight lines.

An example of six straight lines that have the necessary property are the diagonals of the icosadron - a regular twenty-sided (twelve-pointed) triangle. If you have a hard time understanding icosadr, you can construct the required example as follows. Let's take six vectors: $(a, \pm b, 0)$; $(\pm b, 0, a)$; $(0, a, \pm b)$ as direction vectors of our straight lines. Since all vectors have the same length, the abscillitic values ​​of all pairwise scalar products must be equal. Assuming that $a \geq b > 0$, we arrive at the equality $a^2 - ab - b^2 = 0$. You can take $b = 1$, $a = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$.

In geometry, as probably in no other subject, the path from a learning task to an unsolved problem is short. In the end, the formulation of the questions is not so different: “find the smallest area of ​​a triangle containing the unit circle” and “find the smallest figure that can cover any flat figure of diameter 1.” But if the first is a simple school problem, then the second is Lebesgue’s still unsolved problem on the minimum “cover.” But in order to formulate such a meaningful problem, one must have good mathematical culture and taste. We should not forget the follow-up who claims that one friend can ask so many questions that a hundred wise men cannot answer.

Unlike other mathematical disciplines, an experiment in the literal, physical sense of the word arose in geometry. Many geometric discoveries of antiquity are the result of observation and experiment. It is very possible that the famous modern geometer Connelly, who built a deformable polyhedron (a polyhedron that can deform so that each of its faces remains unchanged), experimented a lot in the process of his work - glued together or otherwise created spatial models. The Connelly polyhedron solved one of the oldest mathematical problems, and the fact that the solution to this problem turned out to be quite elementary, and was published in full in the journal «Kvant» (in issue 9, 1978), considering the level of modern mathematics, seems fantastic. This is only possible in geometry and is extremely rare.

Here I want to give an example of another “small” discovery made experimentally. In elementary geometry there is the following problem: what is the largest number of unit squares that can be cut from a square with sides $4 + \alpha$, where $0 < \alpha < 1$? This is a problem for recording polyhedral geometers, in particular the famous Hungarian geometer Erdős. It served as a reason to give in the magazine “Matematika at School” the address of the experiment, although in a different sense, following the problem.
