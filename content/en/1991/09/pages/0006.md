Consequently, not only those liquid or gas objects that are exactly on the body’s path interact with a moving body, but also environmental objects adjacent to its trajectory that the body pushes away or carries along with it (Fig. 2).

How does $F_x$ depend on $v$ and on environmental parameters? It is clear that if the speed changes, then during steady motion the mass of the liquid $M$ will change approximately in proportion to $v$, with which the vettelo will meet and occur per unit time, i.e. $M \sim \rho v S$, where $S$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the body, $\rho$ is the density of the medium. And each element of this mass transmits an impulse to the body, also approximately proportional to $v$. Therefore, the resistance force depends on the speed of the body, first considered by Newoton, and has the form (compare with formula (1))

$$F_x \sim \rho v^2 S,$$

or in another post

$$F_x = C_x \frac{\rho v^2}{2} S,$$

(2)

where the quantity $\rho v^2/2$ is called dynamic pressure. (It has the dimension of pressure and is nothing more than the kinetic energy of a unit volume of a moving medium in which a stationary body is located.) The proportional coefficient $C_x$ in formula (2) depends primarily on the shape of the body and, generally speaking, on the flow regime and speed, if it varies within a fairly wide range. In aerodynamics, this quantity is called the drag coefficient; in hydrodynamics, it is called the drag coefficient or hydrodynamic drag coefficient.

Comparing figure 1 and 2, we note a significant difference between these two examples. The liquid flows around the body, and does not leave behind it a “shadow” of empty space, as when moving in a rarefied cold medium. Consequently, the continuous medium exerts pressure on the body not only from the front (i.e., on the bow, if we are talking about a ship), but also from other sides. From the rear side of the body (i.e., from the stern), the direction of the force acting on the body coincides with the direction of movement. Thus, the flow of liquid or dense gas around a body leads to a decrease in the resulting drag force $F_x$. Flows around the body, the liquid retains part of its impulse without transferring it to the body. This is a very important circumstance. We will become familiar with the decrease in the coefficient of hydrodynamic resistance due to flow around a body by checking the following experiment.

Example 3. Let us determine the hydrodynamic resistance experienced by a ball floating under a stream of liquid, and find the coefficient $C_x$ for the ball in the flow. This simple experiment does not require complex equipment. All we need is a bucket or tub, a measuring cup or jar of known volume, a ruler, a watch with a second hand, and a light ball, such as a plastic tennis ball or a small rubber ball.

Let's open the water tap. And there is already quite a lot of water in the container. If you put the ball on the water, then, as you would expect, it floats away from the scab. But as soon as you place the ball in the stream or push it close to it, the ball will immediately be captured by the stream and... remain in it at the place where the stream meets the surface of the water.
