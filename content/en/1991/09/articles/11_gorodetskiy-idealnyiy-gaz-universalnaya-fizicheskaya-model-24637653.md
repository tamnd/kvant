Ideal gas - a universal physical model

Behind the enormous diversity that physics studies, it is often quite difficult to discern any general approaches and universal models that arise with amazing stability in a variety of fields of knowledge. And along with the loss of the universal, the beauty and unity of the physical picture of the world disappears. Everything is drawn out by the gray captivity of boredom and the misunderstanding that stems from it.

Perhaps one of the most typical examples of what has been said is an ideal gas, when mentioned, one usually imagines simply a very rarefied gas. Meanwhile, this is not entirely true. An ideal gas is a universal physical model that is used to describe a wide variety of natural objects. To see this universality, we will try to translate the main results of the theory of a general ideal gas into a more general language.

The main feature of an ideal gas is the absence of interaction between molecules. Or, better said, the smallness of the energy of interaction of a molecule with its environment compared to its own energy.

What does a molecule's own energy consist of? First of all, the kinetic energy of its translational motion. In order to specify the kinetic energy of any body, and in particular a molecule, it is enough to specify three components of its speed (or momentum). When - because our space is three-dimensional. We can say it differently - in three-dimensional space, a molecule always has a third degree of freedom associated with the movement of its center of mass. These degrees of freedom are called translational. As we will see below, it is in the language of degrees of freedom that the universal model of an ideal gas is most clearly manifested.

In addition to translational motion, a molecule, if it has some kind of spatial structure, can take part in rotation around any of the three coordinate axes. This means that, for example, with the third translational, the molecule also has three rotational degrees of freedom. (The exception is molecules whose atoms are elongated in a rigid chain: they can rotate only around two axes, the first

pendicular to this chain. Therefore, such molecules have only two rotational degrees of freedom.)

But that's not all. A real molecule is not an absolutely rigid, monolithic formation: the atoms that make up the molecule can vibrate relative to each other. This means that, in addition to constructional and rotational degrees of freedom, there are also oscillatory degrees of freedom. By enumerating in this way all the movements in which a molecule or its constituent elements can participate, we can accurately indicate how many degrees of freedom it has.

Why is this necessary? And then, that there is an extremely important and very general theorem, which states that for each degree of freedom of any equilibrium system there is an average energy equal to $1/2kT$, where $T$ is the absolute temperature, $k$ is Boltzmann’s constant, indicating how many joules of energy correspond to one degree of temperature (a degree is also a unit of measurement of energy, only a systemic one). So, for example, the total energy of the three construction degrees of freedom is equal to $3/2kT$. But the energy of the constructional motion of a molecule is its kinetic energy. Hence it turns out that

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2}kT.$$

Sometimes they say that temperature is a measure of the kinetic energy of a molecule. It would be more accurate to say that temperature is a measure of energy per degree of freedom. Thus, if we know the number of degrees of freedom of one molecule (we denote this number by the letter $i$), it is not difficult to find the total energy of this molecule ($W_{\text{mol}}$) and, accordingly, the internal, internal energy of the entire gas containing $N$ molecules ($W_{\text{raza}}$):

$$W_{\text{mol}} = \frac{i}{2}kT, \quad W_{\text{raza}} = N \frac{i}{2}kT.$$

The given formulas look extremely simple. But this one is simply deceptive! The fact is that there is a significant deep difference between translational and all other degrees of freedom, discovered at the dawn of the 20th century by the Great German physicist Max Planck. The essence of its property is that, while the energy of the constructional movement can change continuously, the energy of the oscillatory and rotational movement changes only in portions - quantima.

When applied to molecules, the discreteness of energy becomes clear, in particular, that in order to excite the vibrations of the atoms that make up the molecule, or to bring the molecule as a whole into rotation, some finite energy is needed for the wave. The existence of such a threshold excitation energy $W_{\text{пор}}$ for some degree of freedom (collaborative or rotational) leads to the fact that this degree of freedom no longer requires $1/2kT$, but less energy. The point here is this. The distribution of energy across degrees of freedom occurs during collisions of gas molecules. In order of magnitude, the energy transferred during collisions is equal to $kT$ (the average kinetic energy of the constructional motion of molecules). If the temperature of the present gas is high, which is $kT \gg W_{\text{пор}}$, then the presence of a quantum excitation threshold is not felt, the excitation of a given degree of freedom occurs without interference and it accounts for the usual share of energy $1/2kT$. In the opposite case ($kT \ll W_{\text{пор}}$), this degree of freedom is practically never excited during collisions and accounts for a very small fraction of the normal energy rate. In other words, at high temperatures this degree of freedom should be taken into account along with the construction ones, but at low temperatures it is “frozen out”. We can say that the number of degrees of freedom involved depends on the temperature.

It was already noted above that the only movement whose energy can change continuously is the construction movement. And this means that there is no “freezing out” of the construction movement.

It is the positomy Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation $pV=NkT$ that remains valid at any temperature, as long as the gas is so rarefied that the interaction of its molecules can be neglected (pressure is associated exclusively with the translational motion of the molecules). But in the expression for gas energy $W_{\text{газа}}=Ni/2kT$ at high temperatures one must substitute one value $i$ (all degrees of freedom are involved), and at low temperatures - another (some degrees of freedom are “frozen”).

And finally, before moving on to other systems, let’s not step on the gas, let’s give one more correspondence - between the pressure of the gas and its energy. Comparing the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation ($pV=NkT$) and the expression for the energy of translational motion ($W_{\text{пост}}=3/2NkT$, here the number of translational degrees of freedom $i_{\text{пост}}=3$), it is easy to find

$$pV=\frac{2}{3}W_{\text{пост}}.$$

The fact that the energy of a gas is associated exclusively with the total number of its degrees of freedom, and the pressure, in turn, is expressed through energy, that is, again through the number of degrees of freedom (translational only), brings up a very important idea. Or maybe it is not necessary for the system to consist of a large number of non-interacting molecules? maybe everything is determined only by degrees of freedom, and these degrees of freedom are already connected with molecules (and then we have an ideal gas) or with something else - this, so to speak, is the second question?

To live this idea out, let’s consider a system that is very far in its external characteristics from ordinary gas. Imagine a closed cavity, the steps of which are maintained at a constant temperature $T$. Like any heated body, steppes emit internal electromagnetic waves. The cavity appears to be filled with electromagnetic material due to.

The idea that thermal radiation in equilibrium with the walls can be considered as an ideal gas, where different degrees of freedom correspond to vibrations of different frequencies, arose as soon as we began to study this object. This seemed all the more plausible since the pressure due to radiation is related to its energy in almost the same way as for a general ideal gas:

$$pV=\frac{1}{3}W.$$

However, the first attempts to realize the energy of such a “gas” led to discouraging results. The fact is that waves of any frequency, however high, can exist in a position. And if the energy $1/2kT$ is allocated to each such “degree of freedom”, then the total energy of all waves turns out to be infinitely large. It was this paradoxical situation with thermal radiation in a cavity (it was called the “ultraphilic catastrophe”) that forced Planck to put forward the quantum hypothesis discussed above. He suggested that the energy of vibration with frequency $v$ can only change in portions equal to $hv$ ($h$ is Planck’s constant). This means that all vibrations with frequencies for which $hv \gg kT$ turn out to be “frozen” and do not contribute to energy. Based on his hypothesis, Planck derived a formula for the frequency distribution of the energy of electromagnetic oscillations in a cavity and obtained excellent agreement with experimental data. The similarity between an ordinary ideal gas and a gas of electromagnetic waves turned out to be so great that special particles—photons—began to be associated with individual elementary waves with energies $hv$.

And this is nothing more than the reduction of various systems to an ideal gas. And in this sense, the ideal gas model itself is one of the very few fundamental physical models that have played and continue to play a prominent role in creating a physical picture of the world around us.
