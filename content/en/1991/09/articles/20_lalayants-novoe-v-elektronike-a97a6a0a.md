New in electronics

The “hottest” computer of the beginning of 1991 was the personal “notepad”, which receives and reproduces the guiding text in printed form. Its creation was announced at a press conference in St. Petersburg by the Californian company Go from Foster City. The new personal computer is the fruit of the company's secret thirty-year work on kidney research. The media is an achievement as a revolution in the production of personal computers.
Let us remind you that the first real computer was introduced into everyday office practice in Telar back in 1977. Six years later, the first portable personal computers appeared that you could take with you in your car, train, etc., while continuing to work on the road. A couple of years ago, the first pocket computers appeared with capabilities suitable for everyday practice. But still, progress in creating personal computers was more evolutionary than revolutionary. What stood in the way of the post-industrial computer revolution, oddly enough, was the keyboard. It turns out that tens of millions of people in a country like the USA, due to their psycho-physiological parameters, cannot learn to quickly work on the keyboard. This training is quite difficult for both children and the elderly. (The overwhelming majority of men in the city are worse at typing than women, who dominate typing.)
Thus, the computer market has maintained a niche community of ten million potential consumers for three decades. The electronics industry has tried to fill this gap in recent years. Before “Go”, “grids” (which is translated as “grids”), arbitrary subsidiary of Tandy, were thrown onto the fish. About 10 thousand of them were sold for about 2.4 thousand dollars. The main buyers of the Grid were people who wanted to write unambiguous prescriptions, receipts and fill out a lot of other documentation - pharmacists, salespeople, warehouse managers. The computer got its name from the thinnest network of conductors that react to the touch of an electronic pen. Something similar is produced in Japan by Sony and Canon.
However, the new Go computer represents, as R. Schafer, editor of the leading American electronics industry magazine, the Technology Computer Letter, puts it, “an exciting new opportunity in the development of the computer industry.” This is truly a completely new concept, a 2-kilogram personal computer the size of a large notepad or sketchbook. The upper surface of the “block” is a large liquid crystal display with a grid of conductors that respond to the touch of an electronic “style” (cardash). The latter can be connected to a computer by wires or even be completely autonomous.
The main advantage of Go personalization is that the computer does not require a keyboard and mouse. If you need to know some text on a computer, then you simply test it by hand on the screen with a pencil - a “stylus”. The computer stores this text in memory and will display it on the screen at any time. If you don’t like a word or expression in a document (draft contract, statement), then you can remove it from the text by lining it up in a circle. And the unnecessary sentence is simply crossed out.
The new computer is very convenient for editing work because it allows you to use the usual editor icons. Moreover: if you want to insert a phrase into a document printed on paper, then you need to reduce it in the space between the lines. With Go, everything is simpler: you draw a small cart in the margins, on which you “carry” what you need. The computer itself will expand the lines so that the new phrase falls into place.
“Go” understands not only handwriting, but also drawings. It is enough to depict any figure on the screen, and it will immediately reproduce it in a drawing-accurate form. All that remains is to make the corrections and clarifications you need, and the drawing, in standard form, is ready.
So far, Go has only been tested among products and other representatives of the service sector, but in two years the company promises to throw out pocket models for the fish. However, computers the size of a common blackboard will be issued for educational institutions, press and congress centers. It is expected that already in 1992, up to a quarter of a million Go computers will be sold for a total amount of about three billion dollars.
The new computer is a combination of the best that the electronics industry has accumulated from portable minicomputers, laser CD players and software needed to recognize figure and handwriting.

In addition to letters and lines, the computer also recognizes screen inputs and tick marks. For example, if a document does not fit on one page, then the “tick” at the end of it automatically “turns the page.” All this is provided by a “very smart” microprocessor and 4 MB of memory, which is about 4 times more memory than a typical personal computer.
A disk drive for CDs is mounted under the screen, as well as additional memory chips for permanent data storage. In the opposite models we tried to use a floppy drive, but they turned out to be too heavy and noisy. This forces the Go user to connect to a standard office computer or floppy drive to transfer programs and documents. However, the Go connection can also be done using a regular telephone line.
Over time, new computers will incorporate a cellular telephone circuit into their computer, allowing the user to receive and transmit electronic data anywhere and at any time, communicating conveniently via faxes and telephones.
Go was created by three young scientists, the main one being 38-year-old S. Kaplan, the son of a Mangetin lawyer. He graduated from the University of Pennsylvania, where he worked on artificial intelligence, and at one time headed the programming department at the famous Lotus company, where he developed the popular 1-2-3 program for the PC. His two deputies, the co-founder of the Go company, are 36 and 34 years old, respectively. They were not afraid to challenge such giants of the electronic world as IBM and Microsoft, the world famous Villa Gates. Microsoft (headquartered in Redmond, Washington) has had a well-proven DOS operating system for ten years. "Go" uses a fundamentally new operating system, which Kaplan calls "Pensionit", i.e. "tip of the pen."
The new system of special lekka is easy to understand and convenient for beginners. When the computer is turned on, the user sees on the display a set of “dossiers” or “folders” with a set of certain “documents”. It is enough to poke a stylus into one or another dossier, and an image of “papers” will immediately unfold in front of you. This can be compared to a questionnaire, in which you only need to enter information about yourself in capital letters, and after which the document is ready, all you have to do is send it by e-mail in the appropriate place.
Microsoft fell behind its competitor this time. His system of this kind, which V. Gates calls "Penundoz" - "Windows for the pen" - may appear on the market only by mid-1992. Gates believes that as a fishy relationship develops between the two competitors, his firm is sure to find a “troublemaker” from California. The young multi-millionaire is very aggressive, but experts believe that there is enough space in the market - vast and saturated - not only for them, but also for other manufacturers of similar computers. Kaplan's company decided to give the mighty IBM a chance, so Microsoft's hetemony on the fish program may really come to an end. In addition to IBM, Go's clients include such well-known companies as Tandy and NCR.
PC Letter magazine, through its editor S. Alsop, calls the unfolding "fever" one of those magical moments that show the American electronics industry at its best." The Japanese showed great interest in the message, considering the transition to Go-type computers to be a completely natural step that they should also take. Thus, a new round of powerful competition is expected, without which progress in such an area as computers is simply impossible. And this competitive struggle is creeping up on everyone.
The Americans have the potential of the Japanese in the field of chips. We are talking about a new success for Texas Instruments, which announced that it was the first to base its computers on “dynamic random access memory” (DRAM) chips with a capacity of 16 Mbit, which makes it possible to reduce more than one and a half thousand pages of typewritten text on one chip.
Japanese cyps, which absolutely DOMINATE fish, have a standard capacity of only 4 Mbits. Four Japanese firms announced, like the Americans, that they would also throw 16 Mbit chips at the fish in the first quarter of 1991, but they would begin large-scale production only in 1992. Thus, developments in this area have accelerated: previously, a 4-fold increase in memory capacity was achieved in three years, but now - in just two. Experts estimate the capacity of fish of similar types by 1993 at 30 billion pieces.
