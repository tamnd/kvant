Interstellar travel is helped by... stars

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. SURDIN

Introduction

Implementing interstellar travel is an extremely exciting task. Its solution would make it possible to study the stellar surface in detail, discover the planets of the system and, possibly, establish contact with representatives of the outer size. We will not discuss here all the possible ways to solve this problem: over the past decades, many proposals have been made to this effect -

from photonic and nuclear starships (for example, the English project Daedalus) to the use of a solar sail. In this article we will talk about only one relatively new idea, which, in principle, makes it possible to send a large number of small automatic Hondas to various stars in our Galaxy.

Perturbation maneuver

Trajectories of interplanetary flights are often laid near planets not only for their study, but also to use the gravity of the planet for additional acceleration of the spacecraft. A change in the flight path under the influence of the planet’s gravitational field is called a perturbation maneuver. It was he who was used several times during the trip.

The flyby of each intermediate planet is planned in such a way that its attraction accelerates the spacecraft and tells it the desired direction of movement.

The mechanics of this effect are easy to understand with a simple example. If a massive ball is rolling on the table, and a light one is rolling towards it, then upon collision the massive one will hardly change its speed, and the light one will rebound with increased speed (Fig. 1; try to solve this problem yourself, using the laws of conservation of kinetic energy and momentum). The same thing happens during a “gravitational collision” of a planet with a spacecraft flying towards it. The only difference is that the collision of created bodies occurs almost instantly at the moment of contact, and the gravitational collision takes a long time. But the laws of mechanics apply the same. Therefore, the result is the same: having passed the planet object, the spacecraft increases its speed. How long?

From the point of view observed on the planet, the device is approaching it at a speed of $v_+$ and, obviously, will move away at the same speed, changing only its direction by an angle of $\beta$.
In these considerations we mean that the coordinate system associated with the planet is practically inertial: after all, the projet of the apparatus affects the motion of the planet fantastically weakly. From a distant point of view, it is observed that the vehicle moving away from the planet has a speed of $v_{++}$.
Using the parallelogram rule, we get
$$v_{++}^2 = v_a^2 + v_n^2 - 2v_a v_n \cos \alpha$$
And
$$v_{++}^2 = v_a^2 + v_n^2 - 2v + v_n \sin (\beta/2).$$
It is immediately clear that if the vehicle and the planet were moving towards each other ($\alpha \approx 180^\circ$), and as a result of the maneuver, the velocity vector of the vehicle changed its direction to the opposite direction ($\beta \approx 180^\circ$), then double the speed of the planet was added to the speed of the vehicle. This is the most advantageous case (Fig.3). But for the maneuver to be quite effective in terms of speed increment, it is desirable to have $\beta$ and $\alpha \geq 90^\circ$. Of course, the apparatus must at the same time pass over the surface of the planet and not crash into it, i.e., the minimum approach distance must exceed the radius of the planet. The laws of celestial mechanics indicate that in this case the maximum speed of the vehicle’s retreat relative to the planet after their approach will be equal to $v_{maxc} = 0,46v_{II}$, where $v_{II}$ is the separation speed (second cosmic velocity) at the surface of the planet. If a planet orbits a star, then $v_{maxc} \approx v_{opc} + 0,5v_{II}$, accordingly, where $v_{opc}$ is the orbital speed of the planet.
By the way, in order for the vehicle to leave the solar system after its merger with the planet, the condition $v_{maxc} \geq \sqrt{2}v_{opc}$ must be met, i.e., the vehicle’s speed must be greater than the speed of separation from the planet’s orbit. So, we have found out that a special choice of trajectory, passing in the vicinity of the planets, allows the spacecraft to increase its speed and leave the Solar System without wasting fuel. Obviously, the necessary energy for this is drawn from the mechanical energy of the planets. Is it possible to use the same acceleration principle during the flight of the device “in the vastness of the Galaxy”? After all, stars also move in space, and, therefore, the perturbation maneuver of those performing them can impart additional speed to our international probe.

Astronomers know that the speed characteristics of stars range from 10-20 km/s for young objects to 250-300 km/s for the oldest of them. This means that each meeting with a star, with the appropriate selection of the merger trajectory, will add several tens, or even hundreds of kilometers per second to the device. The limit for effective perturbation maneuver, as in the case of planets, is the value $v_{\text{макс}}$, but for stars it is significantly higher than for planets (see Table 2). True, for normal stars in a wide range of their masses and size, the value of $v_{\text{макс}}$ remains approximately the same as for the Sun. It is relatively small ($\approx 300$ km/s), and therefore such stars are not of great interest to us. Particularly attractive for perturbation maneuvers are old compact stars - white dwarfs, neutron stars and, generally speaking, black holes, although we will not talk about the latter further due to their extremely little knowledge.

The luminosity of most compact stars is not great, and in this sense they do not pose a greater danger to the design of a mismo spacecraft. But there is one physical effect that can significantly limit the possibility of perturbation maneuver, especially affecting a thin star. We are talking about the tidal influence of the gravitational field of the star, which tends to impart to different parts of the apparatus a mutual acceleration $a = 2GM_*\Delta r / R^3$, where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $M_*$ is the mass of the star, $R$ is the distance from the apparatus to the center of the star, $\Delta r$ is the distance between parts of the apparatus. As can be seen from Table 2, tidal acceleration is quite high near the surface of a neutron star and can pose a danger to the design of the device. Now the industry produces some electronic-mechanical devices that have accelerations of up to $10^6$ m/s². Therefore, the interval we selected in Table 2 for the minimum distance between the probe star from the surface of the neutron star seems reasonable to the wave.

As we see, in the vicinity of the Sun, the acceleration time varies according to the years when using any stellar population. But if we are in the core of a globular cluster, then this time would be reduced to several thousand years, and in the core of the Galaxy - to only a few centuries. By the way, in the core of the Galaxy it makes sense to launch a probe from a planetary system at a speed of $v_0=300-400$ km/s. Then it will accelerate with the help of white dwarfs to a speed of about 5000 km/s in just 100 years, and with the help of neutron stars in three centuries it can accelerate to almost 100 thousand km/s (of course, if the design of the probe allows it to approach neutron stars).

In principle, more sophisticated options for gravitational acceleration between stellar probes are possible. For example, astrophysicists know double star systems consisting of a neutron star and a white dwarf. These compact stars are formed in orbit at speeds of more than 1000 km/s. Approaching one of the components of such a system can immediately add about 2000 km/s to the probe’s speed! Such systems should be found especially often in the cores of globular clusters. In general, we would like to note that the central parts of globular star clusters are extremely attractive places for civilizations taking their first steps towards the colonization of space.

Exploratory space exploration is likely to follow the same path.
Now it is difficult to imagine that projects of international ships like the English “Daedalus” will ever be implemented, for which it is necessary to build a rocket of almost a kilometer in length with a nuclear diaget. Such a maximum can only be assembled in Earth orbit; installation work will take at least fifteen years. A flight of a small crew would require almost all the nuclear fuel available on the planet. Only then can this rocket accelerate to a speed of several thousand kilometers per second and reach the nearest star in about a hundred years. A single aircraft will require a lot of resources and effort; its cost will be in trillions of dollars. It is unlikely that all this is realistic.

It seems to us that a more promising method for studying deep space is the creation of many similar, relatively efficient microprobes with a size of no more than 1 m and a mass of about 10-100 kg (but it is possible that significantly less), because technology in this area is developing rapidly. Only with this approach will the energy and material resources of science be available for a detailed study of the Galaxy. Having a small size, microprobes will be able to invade regions of relatively dense interstellar and interplanetary materials and approach compact and massive objects.

The strategy for exploring the Galaxy using microprobes should be the subject of a separate discussion. In this case, it is necessary to consider the possibility of optical communication as the most preferable over long distances, as well as the possibility of returning probes to the launch area. Costa, if similar probes launched from other planetary systems pass through the Solar System from time to time, then we have no way to detect them now. Probably, the majority of supposed “brothers in mind” find themselves in the same position in relation to our probes. Therefore, such a method of “microzonding” the Galaxy is the most dangerous and responsible in relation to humanity.
