Variation of conditions

The simplest example, which quite accurately illustrates this example, is given by the following series of problems: construct a triangle along a) trem sides; b) trem medians; c) trem heights; d) trem bisectors. This example shows how much the level of difficulty can change under similar conditions. In our four, the completely elementary first task is to complete a meaningful, although not very difficult, task. The third problem is already more complicated, and the fourth cannot be solved at all with the help of a compass and a ruler.

An interesting idea with which you can create a series of problems was proposed by Kiev mathematics teacher V. Kutsenok. Let's take some geometric correspondence, say, the equality $ah_a=bh_b$, and ask the question: what are the properties of the triangle for which the correspondence is satisfied, obtained from the one under consideration by replacing the heights with medians or bisectors? As a result, you can get the following task:

Problem 13. There are two points A and B on the bottom plane. Find the locus of points on the plane C such that

In general, when varying the conditions of a problem, interesting series can arise. I will give an example of two such series (without trying to complete them).

There is a well-known classical theorem stating that from the equality of the two internal bisectors of a triangle it follows that it is equilateral (the Steiner-Lemus theorem). In itself, the statement of this theorem is true and acceptable, if not for the difficulties that one encounters in its proof, in contrast to related trivial theorems concerning medians or heights. Already here you can see the bad rights of the bisectors. But it is fully manifested in the next task.

Generalization

The entire development of mathematics is a path of constructive generalizations. Of course, when using this technique to obtain new elementary problems, we, as a rule, do not pretend to do much, but nevertheless it is necessary to remember and understand its meaning.

Generalization can go in different directions. Sometimes it is possible to remove some restrictions in the problem under consideration and extend the corresponding statements to a wider set of objects. So, one day in an old mathematical magazine I came across the following problem:

Problem 16. In a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, side AD is the diameter of the circle, and the bisectors of angles B and C intersect at AD. Prove that the equality AB+CD=AD holds.

I did not like the solution to this problem given in the magazine. After some solutions, we managed to find another solution, which did not use in any way the fact that AD is the diameter of a circle. This condition is
only to them. As a result, a completely new

Problem 16'. Prove that if the bisectors of angles B and C of a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD intersect on side AD, then AD=AB+CD.

The essence of the many solutions mentioned (there are many others) is as follows. Let us take a point P on AD at which the bisectors of angles B and C intersect. Let us describe a circle near BCP and denote by M the second point of intersection of this circle with AD. Then, taking into account the equality of the corresponding angles of the inscribed quadrilaterals ABCD and BCPM, we prove the equilaterality of the triangles ABM (AB=AM) and CDM (CD=DM). Make these judgments yourself.

From this example it is also clear how useful it is when solving a problem not to limit one method, to consider various solutions, paying special attention to more geometric ones, since they reveal more deeply the internal properties of figures and allow one to separate the main from the secondary. In this regard, another, more recent example. It was proposed for the All-Russian Olympics in 1990 (see «Kvant», No. 11, 1990).

Problem 17'. Two loops emerge from the top of the node and are located inside the node. Some lines intersect the sides of the node at points D and E, and the rays at points K and M. Prove that the ratio $\frac{KM}{DE}$ will be smallest if $DK=ME$.

To prove this, let's consider some other line, and we can assume that it passes through E and intersects the other side and the rays at points $D_1$, $K_1$ and $M_1$ (Fig. 3). Let's put $DK=ME=a$, $KM=b$, $OD_1=\lambda OD$. Drawing a line parallel to DE through $D_1$, we will have:

$$D_1K_2=\lambda a, \quad K_2M_2=\lambda b,$$

$$\frac{D_1M_1}{M_1E}=\frac{\lambda(a+b)}{a},$$

$$D_1M_1=\frac{\lambda(a+b)}{\lambda(a+b)+a}\cdot D_1E,$$

$$D_1K_1=\frac{\lambda a}{\lambda a+b+a}\cdot D_1E,$$

$$K_1M_1=\left(\frac{\lambda(a+b)}{\lambda(a+b)+a}-\frac{\lambda a}{\lambda a+b+a}\right)D_1E.$$

The final task is to prove the simple algebraic inequality

$$\frac{\lambda(b^2+2ab)}{(\lambda(a+b)+a)(\lambda a+b)} \leq \frac{b}{2a+b},$$

which is converted to the form

$$(\lambda-1)^2a(a+b) \geq 0.$$

Problem 18. Two tangents AB and CD are drawn to two spheres, A and C on the surface of one sphere, B and D on the other. Prove that the projections of AC and BD onto a line passing through the centers of the sphere are equal.

The answer to this question is different. A counterperiod is a terrahedra whose two angles, corresponding to two intersecting edges, are obtuse.

The statement of this theorem obviously generalizes to an arbitrary convex equilateral polygon. Less obviously, it generalizes to equilateral polygons. Indeed, let $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ be an equilateral polygon (Fig. 4, $n=5$). Consider a regular $n$-gon $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ with sides parallel to and containing the original $n$-gon. But the distance to each original $n$-gon is less than the distance to the side parallel to it of the second regular $n$-gon by a constant amount. This means that the entire sum of distances from $M$ to side $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ differs from the sum of distances from $M$ to side $A_1'A_2' \dots A_n'$ by a constant value, that is, it is itself constant.

Problem 20. On the plane there are given $n$ different unit vectors, the sum of which is equal to zero. Let's consider

a convex $n$-gon whose sides are perpendicular to the corresponding vector. Then for all interior points of this $n$-gon the sum of the distances to its sides is the same.

Other ways of generalizing the original theorem about a regular triangle are also possible. For example, go out into space. By the way, the question arises: is Theorem-Problem 20, or rather, its analogue, true in space?

Discoveries and problems

The previous examples illustrated certain technical techniques. And yet, the main source of new tasks is curiosity, the desire to get to the bottom of the matter every time, the ability to see the familiar from an unexpected point of view. This is when the most interesting geometric problems and discovery problems appear. In my opinion, one of the most attractive Olympic problems of recent years belongs to this category of problems.

Problem 21. Is it possible to cut three regular tetrahedrons with a single edge from a single wooden cube?

This task is expected to take place at the All-Russian Olympics in 1989. What is interesting here is that in many Olympid collections the problem of cutting two tetrahedra from a single cube was discussed. But it turns out that you can cut out as many as three! (Take three pairwise crossing edges of a cube. Each of them will be an edge of one tetrahedron. The midpoints of the opposite edges of each tetrahedron coincide with the center of the cube. Now prove that these tetrahedrons no longer have common points.)

Of course, it is not necessarily a beautiful fact discovered personally in the MI, but it turns out to be open to the whole person. Moreover, many old geometric theorems are open to recognized geometric experts. Much, too much, of the thousand-year-old geometric culture is utter.

I consider the following problem to be one of my favorite geometric discoveries:

Problem 22. What is the maximum number of lines that can be proven through some point in space so that all pairwise angles between them are equal?

Answer: 6. At the same time, I understand perfectly well that this fact was probably known in ancient times, for example, to Archimedes.

It is not difficult to prove that there cannot be more than six such lines. Indeed, let $l_1$ and $l_2$ be two lines of our family passing through the point $O$. Then all remaining straight lines must belong to the intersection of two finite surfaces: the axis of one of them is the straight line $l_1$, and the straight line $l_2$ is one of the generators; for the second, on the contrary, $l_2$ is the axis, $l_1$ is the generator. Such two finite surfaces intersect along at most four straight lines.

An example of six straight lines that have the necessary property are the diagonals of the icosadron - a regular twenty-sided (twelve-pointed) triangle. If you have a hard time understanding icosadr, you can construct the required example as follows. Let's take six vectors: $(a, \pm b, 0)$; $(\pm b, 0, a)$; $(0, a, \pm b)$ as direction vectors of our straight lines. Since all vectors have the same length, the abscillitic values ​​of all pairwise scalar products must be equal. Assuming that $a \geq b > 0$, we arrive at the equality $a^2 - ab - b^2 = 0$. You can take $b = 1$, $a = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$.

In geometry, as probably in no other subject, the path from a learning task to an unsolved problem is short. In the end, the formulation of the questions is not so different: “find the smallest area of ​​a triangle containing the unit circle” and “find the smallest figure that can cover any flat figure of diameter 1.” But if the first is a simple school problem, then the second is Lebesgue’s still unsolved problem on the minimum “cover.” But in order to formulate such a meaningful problem, one must have good mathematical culture and taste. We should not forget the follow-up who claims that one friend can ask so many questions that a hundred wise men cannot answer.

Unlike other mathematical disciplines, an experiment in the literal, physical sense of the word arose in geometry. Many geometric discoveries of antiquity are the result of observation and experiment. It is very possible that the famous modern geometer Connelly, who built a deformable polyhedron (a polyhedron that can deform so that each of its faces remains unchanged), experimented a lot in the process of his work - glued together or otherwise created spatial models. The Connelly polyhedron solved one of the oldest mathematical problems, and the fact that the solution to this problem turned out to be quite elementary, and was published in full in the journal «Kvant» (in issue 9, 1978), considering the level of modern mathematics, seems fantastic. This is only possible in geometry and is extremely rare.
