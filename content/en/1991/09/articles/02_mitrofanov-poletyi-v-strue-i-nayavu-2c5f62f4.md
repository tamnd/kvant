(On the movement of bodies in life and gases)

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. MITROFANOV

What effect does the environment have on the movement of a body in it? Let's say, if you are playing in the yard or walking down the street, you hardly notice that the environment (air) is offering you any resistance. And a student who is late for class will suddenly begin to assert that the main reason for being late is the force of environmental resistance. But as soon as you stick your hand out the open window of a speeding train or get caught in a strong wind, the question of environmental resistance will no longer seem meaningless. Air, naturally so weightless and intangible, at high speeds suddenly begins to resemble an elastic wall or an irresistible force. These are the assessments that await the raft of a high-speed aircraft if it has to eject in flight. Not only aviators or astronauts, but also people of many others, including the most “earthly” professions, are well familiar with the property of a medium to provide resistance to a body moving in it.

One of the first scientific studies of the resistance of the medium belongs to Newoton.*) Since at that time too little data had been accumulated on the interaction of moving bodies with the medium, Newoton was forced to experiment with throwing bodies himself. In 1710 and 1719 in the Cathedral of St. Pavel in London, he conducted experiments with balls moving in water. From experiments, the scientist determined the coefficient in his own derivation

In a gas, the body affects the particles of the medium, changing their speed. According to Newton's third law, a force acts on a body from the environment in the opposite direction, which is called the resistance force. Let's consider the component of the drag force, which is directed along the line of motion (say, the $X$ axis) and which aviators call LOB resistance. From Galileo’s principle of relative motion it follows that to determine this force of uniform motion, it makes no difference whether the body is moving in a stationary medium, or whether the medium is moving towards it with the same speed, and the body is motionless. Let's start with a simple example.

Example 1. Let a disk, ball or cylinder with radius $R$ move with a speed $v$ along its axis in space, filling a set of stationary particles that do not interact with each other (Fig. 1; the mass of each particle is $m$, their concentration $n$). What are these particles? One can imagine, for example, a rarefied cold gas in which the movement of molecules is not taken into account. The one who imagines that the particles are snowflakes will also be right, and the problem describes the movement of a snowball (simply a snowball) flying in the air and colliding with snowflakes that float towards it upon collision, etc.

Per unit time, the body encounters $N=nvS$ particles on its path, where $S=\pi R^2$ is the area of ​​the transverse section

body readings. If the collisions of particles with a body are inelastic, then the drag force is equal to

$$F_x = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = mvN = 2S \frac{\rho v^2}{2},$$

where $\rho = mn$ is the density of the medium.

In a similar way, we can calculate the resistance force $F_x$ acting on a body with an arbitrary profile. You just need to know the speed, density and area of ​​force that a body experiences when moving in a permitted environment.

The situation is somewhat more complicated when the impact is elastic, that is, when a particle hits an arbitrary surface and bounces off it. But even in this case $F_x \sim v^2$, only the proportionality coefficient in formula (1), generally speaking, depends on the shape of the body.

Example 2. Until now, we have considered movements in a permitted environment. But what about life if bodies move at a constant speed in a liquid, for example in water or in a dense gas (air), i.e. in a table environment? How does a continuous medium differ from a particle independence system? After all, a continuous medium, be it a liquid or a dense gas, also consists of molecules or atoms. But if in a permitted environment the particles move independently of each other, experiencing only rare collisions, then in a table environment the particles act as a “collective”. Indeed, the distance between particles

How does $F_x$ depend on $v$ and on environmental parameters? It is clear that if the speed changes, then during steady motion the mass of the liquid $M$ will change approximately in proportion to $v$, with which the vettelo will meet and occur per unit time, i.e. $M \sim \rho v S$, where $S$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the body, $\rho$ is the density of the medium. And each element of this mass transmits an impulse to the body, also approximately proportional to $v$. Therefore, the resistance force depends on the speed of the body, first considered by Newoton, and has the form (compare with formula (1))

$$F_x \sim \rho v^2 S,$$

or in another post

$$F_x = C_x \frac{\rho v^2}{2} S,$$

(2)

where the quantity $\rho v^2/2$ is called dynamic pressure. (It has the dimension of pressure and is nothing more than the kinetic energy of a unit volume of a moving medium in which a stationary body is located.) The proportional coefficient $C_x$ in formula (2) depends primarily on the shape of the body and, generally speaking, on the flow regime and speed, if it varies within a fairly wide range. In aerodynamics, this quantity is called the drag coefficient; in hydrodynamics, it is called the drag coefficient or hydrodynamic drag coefficient.

Comparing figure 1 and 2, we note a significant difference between these two examples. The liquid flows around the body, and does not leave behind it a “shadow” of empty space, as when moving in a rarefied cold medium. Consequently, the continuous medium exerts pressure on the body not only from the front (i.e., on the bow, if we are talking about a ship), but also from other sides. From the rear side of the body (i.e., from the stern), the direction of the force acting on the body coincides with the direction of movement. Thus, the flow of liquid or dense gas around a body leads to a decrease in the resulting drag force $F_x$. Flows around the body, the liquid retains part of its impulse without transferring it to the body. This is a very important circumstance. We will become familiar with the decrease in the coefficient of hydrodynamic resistance due to flow around a body by checking the following experiment.

Example 3. Let us determine the hydrodynamic resistance experienced by a ball floating under a stream of liquid, and find the coefficient $C_x$ for the ball in the flow. This simple experiment does not require complex equipment. All we need is a bucket or tub, a measuring cup or jar of known volume, a ruler, a watch with a second hand, and a light ball, such as a plastic tennis ball or a small rubber ball.

Let's open the water tap. And there is already quite a lot of water in the container. If you put the ball on the water, then, as you would expect, it floats away from the scab. But as soon as you place the ball in the stream or push it close to it, the ball will immediately be captured by the stream and... remain in it at the place where the stream meets the surface of the water.

In an equilibrium position, the Archimedes force $F_A$ balances the gravitational force of the ball $mg$ and the resistance force $F_c$:

$$mg + F_c = F_A.$$

This equation is $m = \rho_m 4/3\pi R^3$, where $R$ is the radius of the ball, and $\rho_m$ is its average density. The radius of a qing pong ball is $R \approx 1,9$ cm, its mass is $m \approx 2,5$ g, i.e. $\rho_m \approx 0,09$ g/cm$^3$, which is approximately 11 times less than the density of water $\rho_b = 1$ g/cm$^3$ (the bottom of the ball floats, almost without sinking). Formula (2) is used in practice, say, when estimating the force of wind pressure on a sail or building, the force of resistance acting on a moving object, be it an airplane, a bird, an automatic vehicle or a submarine. In aerodynamics, formula (2) is one of the main ones. However, the following must be kept in mind. Since the pressure in the flow depends on the flow speed, the resulting force of hydro- or aerodynamic drag depends on how the liquid or gas flows around the body: what is the speed of the medium near the surface, whether the flow is smooth or hovering, where the so-called raw material of the flow occurs at the boundary of the body, etc. It is the variety of types of flows of a continuous medium near the body (see, for example, Fig. 6) that often leads to difficulties in determining the value $F_x$ and $C_x$. Example 6. Newton's formula for calculating resistance forces is not always valid. This formula does not allow, for example, to correctly calculate the force acting on a spoon that is immersed in a jar of honey. She doesn’t describe how a small table ball sinks in a tall vessel with vegetable oil, and she can’t explain at all why the milky fog that mushroom pickers love and which causes so much trouble for drivers, stove-makers and car owners takes so long to fall.

In all of these cases, to determine the resistance forces, you need to know not only the shape and size

measures of the body, the relative speed of movement in the medium and its density, but also another characteristic of the medium, which is called the viscosity coefficient and is generally denoted by the letter $\eta$. This coefficient shows how much or little internal, or, as they say, viscous friction, is in a liquid or gas.

If viscous forces are decisive when a ball moves in a medium, then the ball experiences braking resistance, which can be calculated using the Stokes formula

$$F_x=6\pi\eta Rv,$$

and not according to Newton's formula (2).

But how do you know whether the medium is viscous and what formula should be used when determining the resistance forces in each specific case?

Let's consider a spherical drop of water freely falling in the air (or a hailstone ball, avoiding differences in the densities of water and ice). We will be interested in the steady-state speed of the drop falling, which can be changed and from the condition of dynamic equilibrium we can find how the LOB resistance depends on the speed, change of drop and parameters of the medium (its density and viscosity coefficient). We will assume the mass of the drop to be constant, i.e., we will consider the condensation of vapor and the evaporation of moisture from the drop.

Example 7. There is one more - third - mechanism of braking of bodies, which is also often encountered in practice and which, under certain conditions, can become decisive.

Pour water into a bucket or bathtub and immerse the pencil vertically into it to a certain depth. Start stirring the pencil parallel to the surface of the water. If the speed of the pencil is low, then you won’t notice anything interesting. But if the pencil is stirred quickly, then a diverging group of waves will form behind it, which will carry a certain energy. The formation of waves consumes the power of movement (in this case, the hand), which provides resistance to the medium.

From our experience, we know that waves often appear behind a moving body, and they can be of various weights. The pattern of waves from a pencil or motorboat in deep water is different from the small capillary waves that can be observed in a bucket, glass or saucer. The waves from a motorboat in shallow water have a different appearance than in deep water. A supersonic aircraft creates a shock wave in a three-dimensional medium (air), rather than at an interface (water and air), like a pencil. In addition, unlike ordinary waves on water, a shock wave in a gas is accompanied by a fluid rolling of the medium and its heating.
