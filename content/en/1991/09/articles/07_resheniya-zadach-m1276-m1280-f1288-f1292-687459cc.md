M1276. For a given chord MN, the distance circles are triangles ABC, the bases of which are the diameters AB of this circle that do not intersect MN, and the sides AC and BC pass through the ends M and N of the chord MN. Prove that the altitudes of all such triangles ABC, omitted from vertices A and B, therefore the third altitudes pass through the point H of their intersection, and the points C, M, N and H lie on the same circle δ with the diameter CH. Let P be the center of this circle. Note that when the diameter AB moves, the value of the angle C of the triangle remains unchanged - it changes semi-differently between the arcs AB and MN, which are constant in size (see figure). Since the chord MN is motionless, the circle δ (along which point C and the point H, which is diametrically opposite to it, moves), and thus its center P also remains unchanged: the diameter CH - the section of the height we are interested in - simply rotates around the point P.

E. Kulyanin

M1277. Prove (for positive $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$) the inequality
$$\sqrt{\frac{a_1+a_2}{a_3}} + \sqrt{\frac{a_2+a_3}{a_4}} + \dots + \sqrt{\frac{a_{n-1}+a_n}{a_1}} + \sqrt{\frac{a_n+a_1}{a_2}} \geqslant n\sqrt{2}.$$

As follows from the inequality $x^2+y^2 \geqslant 2xy$ (the inequality between the arithmetic mean and the geometric mean of two numbers $x^2$ and $y^2$), the left-hand side of the inequality being proved is not less than
$$\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{4}{\sqrt{a_1a_2}} + \frac{4}{\sqrt{a_2a_3}} + \dots + \frac{4}{\sqrt{a_{n-1}a_n}} + \frac{4}{\sqrt{a_na_1}}\right).$$

Since the product $n$ of the roots in parentheses is equal to 1, according to Cauchy's theorem on the arithmetic mean and the geometric mean for $n$ numbers (this theorem was discussed in detail in the third issue of this year's magazine), their sum is not less than $n$. Hence the necessary inequality follows.

V. Senderov, L. Kurlyandchik

M1278. There are n real numbers $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ satisfying the conditions $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n = 0$ and $x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_n^2 = 1$. Prove that $x_i x_j \leq -1/n$ is $i, j$ for some.

Let us first consider the “extreme” case, when the numbers take only two different values: $k$ of them are equal to $a < 0$, and the remaining $(n-k)$ are equal to $b > 0$. Then $ka + (n-k)b = 0$, that is, $ka = -(n-k)b$ and $ka^2 + (n-k)b^2 = -(n-k)ab - kab = 1$, whence $ab = -1/n$. Let's try to reduce the general case to this by “developing” the variables. Note that if we replace the pair of numbers $u, v$ $(u < v)$ with $u - t, v + t$ $(t > 0)$, then the sum will not change, but the sum of squares will increase:

$$(u - t)^2 + (v + t)^2 = u^2 + v^2 + 2t(v - u) + t^2.$$

Let $a < 0$ be the smallest and $b > 0$ the largest of the given $n$ numbers $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$. If there are two numbers in this set other than $a$ and $b$, separate them without changing the sum so that one of them equals one of the extreme numbers $a$ or $b$. After several operations we obtain a set of $n$ numbers, in which all but one - let's call it $c$ - are equal to $a$ (let them be $k$) or $b$ ($m = n - k - 1$), the sum of which is 0, and the sum of squares is not less than 1:

$$ka + c + mb = 0,$$

$$ka^2 + c^2 + mb^2 = -(c + mb)a + c^2 - (ka + c)b \geq 1.$$

Since $a \leq c < b$, $ca - c + cb - ab = (c - a)(b - c) > 0$. Adding this inequality to the previous one, we obtain the required:

$$-(m + k + 1)ab = -nab > 1,$$

or $ab < -1/n$.

(From this proof it is clear that equality is achieved only in the “extreme” cases considered at the beginning.)

The problem also has a direct algebraic solution. For any $i, 1 \leq i \leq n$

$$(x_i - a)(x_i - b) \leq 0.$$

Summing these inequalities, we get

$$\sum_{i \leq 1}^{n} x_i^2 - (a + b) \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i + nab \leq 0$$

(*)

But $\sum_{i \leq 1}^{n} x_i^2 = 1$, $\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = 0$, so (*) turns into $nab \leq -1$.

N. Vasiliev, E. Stolov

M1280*. Prove that in the period of the decimal fraction 1/3$^{100}$ the following occurs:
a) at least 20 identical numbers in a row;
b) sequence of numbers 123456789.

We will prove a significantly stronger statement: any sequence of 46 digits will occur in the period of the decimal fraction 1/3$^{100}$.

Note that $3 \cdot 9^9 > 10^9$ (you can check this directly, or you can use $(1 + 1/k)^k < 3)$, so $3^{100} = 3^{95} \cdot 3^5 = 3^{5 \cdot 19} \times 243 > 10^{45} + 2 = 10^{47}$; knowing the logarithm of 3, it is easy to check that this estimate is quite accurate: our fraction 1/3$^{100}$ starts with exactly 47 zeros (which makes the formula answer to question a) trivial; however, it is relatively easy to show that in the period 20 and even all other numbers other than 0 will appear 45 times in a row).

Let us first prove by indication on $n$ that the length of the period of the decimal fraction 1/3$^n$ is equal to $3^{n-2}$ (at $n \geqslant 2$). For $n=2$ this is true: $1/9 = 0,11111...$ Let $1/3^n = A_n/(10^{3n-2} - 1)$, where $A_n$ is a natural number, not be made to 3 (this means that the length of the decimal fraction 1/3$^n$ is $3^{n-2}$). Then $10^{3n-2} - 1 = 3^n A_n$ and $10^{3n-1} - 1 = 10^{(3n-2)} - 1 = (10^{3n-2} - 1)(10^{2 \cdot 3n-2} + 10^{3n-2} + 1)$. The second bracket obviously leaves a remainder of 3 when divided by 9; let it be equal to $9B_n + 3$, then

$$10^{3n-1} - 1 = (10^{3n-2} - 1)(9B_n + 3) = 3^{n+1} A_n(3B_n + 1) = 3^{n+1} A_n$$

where $A_{n+1}$ is not divisible by 3, so $1/3^{n+1}$ has a period of length $3^{n-1}$. (Note that by indication it is also easy to verify that $A_n$ gives a remainder of 1 when divided by 3.) As a small absence, we will show, using this, why in our fraction 1/3$^{100}$ there will be groups of identical digits in a row. The fact is that the period $A_n$ with a sufficiently large $n$ begins with a series of zeros. When moving to $A_{n+2}$ - it can be obtained by dividing a group of nine $A_n$ by 9 with a column - at the beginning of the $(k+1)$-th $A_n$ we will get a series of numbers $k$ (where $k=0,1,...,8$). It is curious that if $n$ of the present is large, that the series has a length of more than 9, we can, by dividing the series ...11111... by 9, explicitly indicate the place where in the number $A_{n+4}$ the numbers 012345679 will appear in a row (this is the period 1/81), but due to the absence of eight, these messages are slightly insufficient to solve problem 6).

Let us now return to the general statement communicated. Let's put $M=3^{98}$ and mentally divide 1 by $9M=3^{100}$ with a column. In the process of division, we must obtain a periodic decimal fraction with a period of length $M$, so that in total we will encounter $M$ different remainders - until the next remainder again turns out to be equal to 1. The remainders are natural numbers less than 9$M$, and each of them gives a remainder 1 when divided by 9, i.e. has the form $9q+1$ ($q$ - integer); period the remainder is 1, and each subsequent one is obtained from the previous one by multiplying by $10=9+1$ - adding zero - and, perhaps, subtracting a multiple of 9$M$; all these procedures do not change the number “modulo 9”. F1288. In the middle of a large round room with a diameter of 20 m and a ceiling height of 3.2 m, there is a large safe in the form of a cube with an edge of 3 m. Using a toy catalult located on the floor, we want to throw a pebble onto the middle of the “roof” of the safe so that the pebble does not touch the ceiling. What is the minimum speed required for this? At what ceiling height in the room is this even possible?

It is more convenient to consider the opposite situation - throw a pebble from the point where we want to hit, and make sure that it does not touch the ceiling and hits the floor (and not the wall). Of course, you need to throw the pebble parallel to the edge, and not along the diagonal.

Let's consider a trajectory that touches the edge of the pebble and almost defines the ceiling, i.e. its upper point is located exactly in the middle. For this trajectory, we can calculate the projection of the speed of the pebble at the moment of throwing:

$$v_b = \sqrt{2g(H-a)} \approx 2 \text{ м/c}, \quad v_r = \frac{a}{4} \sqrt{\frac{g}{2(H-a)}} \approx 1,87 \text{ м/c},$$

where $H=3,2 \text{ м}, a=3 \text{ м}$.

Now it’s easy to find the minimum speed of a pebble when it falls on the floor:

$$v_{\min}^2 = v_r^2 + v_b^2 + 2ga = \frac{ga^2}{32(H-a)} + 2gH, \quad v_{\min} \approx 8,2 \text{ м/c}$$

If you reduce the height of the ceiling, then this speed increases and at certain heights the pebble will hit the edge of the floor. This will happen at ceiling height

$$H^* = a + \frac{a}{(2d/a)^2 - 1} \approx a + \frac{a}{151,1} \approx 3 \text{ м} + 2,0 \text{ см.}$$

Z. Rafailov

F1289. A piece of ice with mass $M$ was placed in a heat-insulated cylindrical vessel at a temperature $t_0=0^\circ\text{C}$ and it was firmly attached to the bottom. Then they filled this ice with water of the same mass $M$. The water completely covered the ice and reached level $H=20 \text{ см}$. Determine what the temperature of this water was if, after establishing the temperature of this water, it dropped by $b=$

The decrease in the total volume occupied by water and ice is due to the fact that when thermal equilibrium is established, part of the ice melts, and the resulting water occupies a smaller volume. From the analysis of the numerical data, it is clear that not all the ice melted - if all the ice were hidden, the water level would drop by $b^* = H(\rho_B - \rho_\pi)/(\rho_B + \rho_\pi) \approx 0,87 \text{ см}$. Therefore, the established temperature of the system is $t_{\text{КОН}}=0^\circ\text{C}$.

Let us denote the mass of melted ice by $m$ and write the heat balance equation:

$$cM(t_B - t_{\text{КОН}}) = \lambda m.$$

From here we get

$$t_B = \lambda m/(cM).$$

A uniform magnetic field of induction B is directed perendicular to the bus plane. The bridge moves at a constant speed v. Find the charge on capacitor $C_3$.

In a moving conductor, an induced emf $E = BLv$ arises, the polarity of which is easy to determine using Lenz’s rule. This leads to a redistribution of charges on the capacitors.

Let us use the equivalent circuit shown in figure 2 (we choose the signs of the charges arbitrarily), and we obtain the system of equation

Solving the system for charge $q_3$, we find

$$q_3 = \frac{E}{1/C_1 + C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3} = \frac{BLv}{1/C_1 + C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3}.$$
