About two methods of interaction

It is known that the motion of a body changes when other bodies act on it. The characteristic, or, as they say, measure of interaction is force. It is included in the equation of motion, expressing Newton's second law. The more intense the interaction of a tag, the greater its result - the acceleration of each of them.

But force is not the only measure of interaction. There are other, no less important characteristics. This is the potential energy of interaction. Force and potential energy seem to have little resemblance to one another. One difference between them immediately catches the eye: force is a vector quantity, potential energy is scalar. Nevertheless, between these two quantities, characterizing the same interaction, there should and do exist connections. This is almost what this article is about.

Gravitational interaction.
Let a body of mass $m$ move down a smooth inclined plane with an angle of inclination $\alpha$. Let's direct the coordinate axis $X$ along the plane, as shown in figure 1, and consider the movement of the body in the area between points with coordinates $x_1$ and $x_2$.

The body is affected by the forces of gravity and the reaction forces of the support, but the projection of the latter onto the $X$ axis is equal to zero, so it will not interest us. The projection of the force $\vec{F} = m\vec{g}$ onto the axis $X$ is equal to

$$F_x = -mg \sin \alpha.$$ (1)

The potential energy of the body (more precisely, the “body-Earth” system) at the initial point is equal to $mgh_1$, and at the final point it is equal to $mgh_2$, where $h_1$ and $h_2$ are heights, measured from some condition of the selected zero level. Thus, when changing

$$\Delta E_p = m g h_2 - m g h_1 = - m g (h_1 - h_2).$$
The coordinate $x$ of the body changes by the amounts
$$\Delta x = x_2 - x_1 = - (x_1 - x_2).$$
From figure 1
$$\frac{h_1 - h_2}{x_1 - x_2} = \sin \alpha.$$
Hence,
$$\Delta E_p = - m g (x_1 - x_2) \sin \alpha = m g \Delta x \sin \alpha.$$ (2)
Comparing expressions (2) and (1), we obtain
$$F_x = - \frac{\Delta E_p}{\Delta x}.$$
This means that the projection of the force vector $\vec{F}$ onto the axis $X$ is equal to the change in potential energy taken with the opposite sign per unit displacement along the axis $X$. An inclined plane is different from a flat one in that for each unit of path along it there is a greater change in potential energy than along a flat one. A round mountain slope is more difficult to climb because each step along it involves a greater change in potential energy than on a gentle slope.
The reader can easily see for himself that for the projection of the force vector onto the $Y$ axis, a similar formula is obtained: $F_y = - \frac{\Delta E_p}{\Delta y}$. And if, when the body moves, the coordinate $z$ also changes (the body moves not along a plane, but in space), then for the projection $F_z$ one can obtain a similar formula: $F_z = - \frac{\Delta E_p}{\Delta z}$.

Elastic interaction. Let us now consider the case when a body moves under the action of an elastic force, in the direction of a spring. The upper part of figure 2 shows an undeformed spring with a body attached to it. Let us direct the coordinate axis $X$ along the axis of the spring, and the origin of the connection coordinates with the right end of the undeformed spring.
Now let the spring grow so that the coordinate of its end becomes $x_1$. An elastic force equal to $-kx_1$ acts on the body. Let us assume that under the influence of this force the body moved to position $x_2$, where the elastic force became equal to $-kx_2$. But we will assume that the body is acted upon by a constant force equal to the average value $-k \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}$, so the projection of the force on the axis $X$ is equal to
$$F_x = - k \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}.$$ (3)
What is the potential energy? In the initial position it is equal to $k x_1^2/2$, and in the final position $-k x_2^2/2$. This means that the change in potential energy
$$\Delta F_p = \frac{k}{2} (x_2^2 - x_1^2) = - \frac{k}{2} (x_1 + x_2) (x_1 - x_2).$$ (4)
Comparing equalities (4) and (3), we find, as in the case of gravity,
$$F_x = \frac{\Delta E_p}{x_1 - x_2} = - \frac{\Delta E_p}{\Delta x}.$$
And here the change in potential energy per unit of displacement along the coordinate axis is equal to the projection of the force on this axis. “Strong” spring (with high stiffness $k$)

differs from “weak” (with low rigidity) in that there is a greater change in potential energy per unit length of movement. A “strong” spring is like a round slope, a “weak” spring is like a gentle slope.

New word - gradient. In the formulas $F_x = -\Delta E_p / \Delta x$, $F_y = -\Delta E_p / \Delta y$ and $F_z = -\Delta E_p / \Delta z$ it follows that the quantities $\Delta E_p / \Delta x$, $\Delta E_p / \Delta y$ and $\Delta E_p / \Delta z$ must also be projections of some vector, although the potential energy $E_p$ is a scalar quantity. What kind of vector is this?

Let's carry out the following purely mathematical operation. Let us mentally direct along the three coordinate axes $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ three vectors, modulo equal to units (they are called unit vectors); let's denote them $\vec{i}$, $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{k}$ (Fig. 3). By multiplying the projections of the vector $\vec{F}$ by the corresponding unit vectors, we obtain three vector quantities $-F_x \vec{i}$, $F \vec{j}$ and $F_z \vec{k}$, they are called components of the vector $\vec{F}$ along the coordinate axes. The vector $\vec{F}$ is expressed through its components as follows:

$$\vec{F} = F_x \vec{i} + F_y \vec{j} + F_z \vec{k}.$$

No can also be written like this:

$$\vec{F} = -\left( \frac{\Delta E_p}{\Delta x} \vec{i} + \frac{\Delta E_p}{\Delta y} \vec{j} + \frac{\Delta E_p}{\Delta z} \vec{k} \right).$$

The quantity in parentheses on the right side of the equation is a vector. It is called the potential energy gradient and is denoted by the symbol grad, so

$$\vec{F} = -\text{grad} E_p.$$ (5)

This is the connection between two measures of interaction - force and potential energy: force is equal to the potential energy gradient taken with the opposite sign.

How is this country vector directed? From formula (5) it is clear that it is directed towards increasing potential energy. If, for example, we are talking about gravity, then the potential energy increases as the body rises. The vector grad $E_p$ is directed there, vertically upward.

You can, of course, say it differently: the force of the project is directed towards a decrease in potential energy. In the general case, the potential energy gradient vector always indicates the direction of the most intense change in this quantity.

So... We have found a connection between two measures of interaction for the case when it manifests itself in the form of gravitation and elasticity. But there is also a mechanical interaction that manifests itself in the form of friction. For him, the only measure, the only characteristic is strength (friction force). There is no potential energy associated with this force because the friction force does not depend on the coordinates, while potential energy is a function of the coordinates.

Thus, in mechanics both measures of interaction are used - force and potential energy, but force is useful in all cases, but potential energy is not.

Let us note in conclusion that in quantum mechanics, which describes the phenomena of the microworld, the measure of interaction is exclusively potential energy. Forces are not included in the equation of quantum mechanics.
