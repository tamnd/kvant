The answer received may cause internal dissatisfaction, although there are no contradictions within the framework of the assumptions made. The situation will become clearer if we pay attention to the fact that when the engine exits a moving rocket, a redistribution of the chemical energy of the fuel and gravitational energy accumulated during the rise to a height of $H_0$ occurs between the kinetic energy of the rocket and the kinetic energy of the gases ejected by the engine, and during the crawl of the rocket.

Let's consider the energy balance for various methods of launching a rocket. At the initial moment, the total energy of the system is
$$E = (m + \Delta m)gH_0 + W,$$
where $W$ is the chemical energy of the fuel. With the “traditional” method of launching a rocket at an altitude $H_0$ after the engine, the energy is equal to
$$E = (m + \Delta m)gH_0 + \frac{\Delta m c'^2}{2} + \frac{mv'_2^2}{2}.$$

In this case, the mechanical energy of the rocket, which determines the height of its rise, is equal to
$$E - \frac{\Delta m c'^2}{2} - \Delta mgH_0.$$

When the engine is turned on at point $B$, the same energy $E$ is distributed in this way:
$$E = \frac{mv'_2^2}{2} - \frac{\Delta m c'^2}{2},$$

where $c$ is the velocity of the emitted gases relative to the stationary reporting system. The part of the energy used to gain altitude in this case will be equal to
$$E - \frac{\Delta m c'^2}{2}.$$

Since $c = c' - v_1$, then $c^2 = c'^2 - 2c' v_1 + v_1^2 = c'^2 - 2c' v_1 + 2gH_0$. It is clear that
$$\frac{\Delta m c^2}{2} < \frac{\Delta m c'^2}{2} + \Delta mgH_0,$$
i.e.
$$E - \frac{\Delta m c'^2}{2} - \Delta mgH_0 < E - \frac{\Delta m c^2}{2}.$$
