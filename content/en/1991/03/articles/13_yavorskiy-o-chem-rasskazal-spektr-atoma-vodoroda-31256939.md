What does the spectrum of the hydrogen atom say?

As is known, the simplest radiation is from isolated atoms, for example, atoms of monatomic gases or vapors of neoctor metals. Such spectra are a set of discrete spectral lines of different intensities, corresponding to different wavelengths. They are called line spectra.

When gases or vapors, whose molecules consist of several atoms, glow, positive spectra appear—collections of groups of spectral lines. Finally, radiation emitted by heated liquids and solids has a continuous spectrum that contains all possible wavelengths.

Before the advent of spectroscopic calculations, it was believed that calculations in astronomy had the most accuracy. However, it turned out that the accuracy of calculations in spectroscopy is not only not inferior, but in some cases even exceeds astronomical accuracy.

Balmer found that the spectra of other atoms, more complex than hydrogen, could also be described by formulas similar to the formula he discovered. In his opinion, finding the “master number” for the atoms of other elements would be a very difficult task. Fortunately for all atomic physics, and especially for spectroscopy, Balmer was wrong. The value $k$ was included in the spectral formulas for studying the atoms of all chemical elements (however, the formulas themselves differ from Balmer’s in a number of correction terms).

Rydberg constant. Full spectrum of hydrogen atom. In 1890, the Swedish spectrocopist physicist Rydberg wrote down Balmer's formula in “inverted” form” for the quantity $N=1/\lambda$. It is called the wave number and shows how many wavelengths in vacuum fit into a unit length. The wavenumber is easily related to the frequency of light $v$:

$$v = \frac{c}{\lambda} = cN,$$

that hesitation. True, from the general context it is clear what exactly is being discussed, but sometimes this introduces unnecessary confusion.

“Inverting” Balmer’s formula, we obtain for the wave number

$$N = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{k} \frac{n^2 - 4}{n^2} = \frac{4}{k} \left( \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right).$$

Let us denote the constant value $4/k$ by $R$ (the first letter in Rydberg’s last name). Then finally Balmer’s formula can be written in the form in which it is usually used:

$$N = R \left( \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right),$$ where $n=3,4,5,6,\dots$

Balmer's formula shows that as the number $n$ increases, the wave numbers of “neighboring” spectral lines have increasingly similar values ​​(the difference between them decreases)—the spectral lines merge. All spectral lines, the wave numbers of which are calculated using the Balmer formula, are required by the Balmer spectral series. The largest number of spectral lines of the Balmer series (37 lines) was found in the spectrum of the solar chromosphere and prominences (clouds of hot gases formed on the Sun and ejected from it). The Rydberg constant was measured with great accuracy on the Balmer series lines. It turned out to be equal to $R=109677,581 \text{ cm}^{-1}$.

The surprising coincidence of the results of measurements of the wavelengths of the lines of the spectrum of hydrogen in the visible region of the spectrum with calculations using the Balmer formula prompted research to study the spectrum of hydrogen in other regions. These Japanese were a success. In addition to the Balmer series, other series were discovered in the hydrogen atom spectrum, and all of them were described by spectral formulas similar to the Balmer formula.

Thus, in the far ultraviolet part of the spectrum - in the wavelength region of ~1200 Å and less - Lyman discovered a series of lines, now called the Lyman series:

$$N = R \left( \frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right),$$ where $n = 2, 3, 4, \dots$

In the infrared part of the spectrum, three series of spectral lines were discovered: in the wavelength range from 10,000 to 20,000 Å - the Paschen series, described by the formula

$$N = R \left( \frac{1}{3^2} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right),$$ where $n = 4, 5, 6, \dots$

In this formula, for each series of lines, the number $m$ has a constant value from 1 to 5: $m = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$, and within this series the number $n$ takes on a series of increasing numerical values, starting from $m + 1$.
