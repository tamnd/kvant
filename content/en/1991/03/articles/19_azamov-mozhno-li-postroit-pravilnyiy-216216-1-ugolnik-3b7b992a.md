Is it possible to construct a regular $2^{16}(2^{16}+1)$-gon?

A debate broke out on this topic when fans of “Quant” gathered around the table one day.
Mathematician-logician. Yes! Since $2^{16}+1$ is a Pierre prime number, then, as Karl proved, such a regular polygon can be constructed.
Mathematician-historian. It was built by the German professor Hermes. Doubling the number of sides is not difficult. The presentation occupies a substantial suitcase and is stored at the University of Göttingen. Anyone who wants can read it.
Mathematician-editor. But no one has solved his work yet! And it is unknown whether it is true or not!
Mathematician and methodologist. The possibility of construction is beyond doubt. But the question is posed somewhat differently - how to do it here. (Places a sheet of paper, a pencil, a ruler and a compass on the table.)
Mathematician-philosopher. I don’t understand what the problem is - it has been proven that the construction method exists; it is known that the proof is completely constructive. Therefore, it is possible!
Mathematician - economist. It’s possible - it’s not possible, we need to calculate (takes out a micro calculator). If it takes, say, a second to build each side, and the work is organized in three shifts, then... Wow - 136 years. No, you can't!
Mathematician - cyberneticist. Why a computer? Let's make a program... For

mi in the crystal lattice of the graph is 1.4 Å, then ... mmm... the circumference is $9,4 \cdot 10^9$ Å, ... In principle, you can arrange the required number of carbon atoms on the circle! ...Bot is only at an equal distance... mmm... fluctuations...
Experimental physicist. Why these calculations? (Takes a compass and draws a circle.) Please. Let one of you, without stepping up, prove to me that this is not a regular 4,295,032,832-gon!

A. Azamov
