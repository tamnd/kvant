COMPUTING
NO COMPUTING

Academician A. MIGDAL

If mathematics is the art of avoiding calculations, then theoretical physics is the art of calculating without mathematics.

The statement given as an epigraph arose as a result of disputes between physicists and mathematicians and needs clarification. Further, the simplest calculations in physics cannot be done without mathematics. However, at the first, most important stage of the work of a theoretical physicist, when the physical picture of the phenomenon is established, mathematics recedes into the background and plays only an auxiliary role. But before talking about the connection between physics and mathematics, you should know what theoretical physics is.

I. HOW DO PHYSICISTS WORK?

Experimenters and theorists

There are two types of physicists - experimenters and theorists, and these two professions are almost never combined in one person. Experimental physicists study the correspondence between physical quantities, or, more solemnly speaking, discover the laws of nature, use experimental setups, that is, produce changes in physical quantities using devices. To do this, you need to deeply understand the phenomena being studied (in order to know what and how to change). Theoretical physicists study nature using only paper and pencil, that is, inferential correspondences between observable quantities, relying on previously found experimental and theoretical laws of nature. The reason for the separation of these two professions is not only that each of them requires its own special knowledge: knowledge of measurement methods in one case and mastery of mathematical apparatus in the other. The main reason is that these professions correspond to different characteristics of thinking and different forms of intuition. Intuition, i.e. the ability to subconsciously find the right path, plays a crucial role, especially in the first stages of work. Unfortunately, the gift of intuition does not arise immediately, but as a result of hard work, as a reward for solving many scientific problems.

Since theoretical physics deals with more abstract concepts than experimental physics, theoretical physics requires a more abstract form of intuition, sometimes close to the intuition of mathematics. In the past century, when physics was not yet so specialized, many physicists combined both professions. Thus, Maxwell, who theoretically obtained equations that describe all electromagnetic phenomena, was also involved in experiments. Hertz, who discovered experimental electromagnetic waves, was also a good theoretician. And yet, in each it is powerfully indicated which of the professions is the main one: for Maxwell it is theoretical physics, and for Hertz it is experimental physics.

A mathematician, even if he deals with applied problems, that is, problems that do not come from mathematics, takes on solving only those problems that do not require additional unproven propositions. A physicist, as a rule, deals with problems in which there is insufficient initial data for a solution, and the art consists of guessing which missing relationships are implemented in nature. It is for these contracts that not mathematical, but physical intuition is required.

Confidence in physics is achieved by obtaining the same result from different initial sentences. At the same time, it comes to introducing unnecessary, logical, unnecessary axioms, each of which in itself is not absolutely reliable. The only condition is to be able to assess the degree of persuasiveness of a proposal and to clearly understand which proposals require further verification.

If any area of ​​physics reaches such a development that all its results can be deduced from a few strictly established axioms, then this area will cease to be part of the developing physical science and will be transferred to the section of applied mathematics or technology. This is what happened with classical mechanics.

Of course, it is very useful to analyze the structure of a physical theory, that is, to find out from which initial proposals certain results are obtained. However, the center of gravity in such an axiomatic approach is not in the generality and mathematical structure of the conclusions, but in the correct choice of initial proposals and the assessment of which of them are most reliably confirmed by experience.

And this requires the intuition of a physicist. In cases where this work is done by a mathematician, he must, at least temporarily, be done by a theoretical physicist. Otherwise, he risks finding himself, in the words of the useful sitetak Letz, in the position of an eximoca, who develops rules of behavior for the residents of the Congo during the heat.

So, mathematics and physics are sciences with different tasks and with different methods of approaching problems.

In mathematics, the reliability of results is achieved by logical consistency and analysis of all logical possible solutions. In physics, only those solutions are considered that can be implemented in nature, and reliability is achieved by repeatedly checking the proposals made. Mathematical rigidity in physics is impossible and unnecessary. It is no more necessary to achieve mathematical precision in physics than it is to demand that logging foremen speak in poetry at work. But at the same time, a theoretical physicist must be fluent in mathematics, that is, know and be able to use all those mathematical methods that may be useful in solving physical problems.

As another example, consider the problem of finding the external oscillation period of a small weight. First of all, let's find out what quantities can be included in the expression for the period. $$T = C \sqrt{m/k}.$$

The general property of all oscillators is the following: regardless of the specific device of the oscillatory system (i.e., oscillating around the equilibrium position) of the system, the energy of the system at any moment in time can be written in the form

$$E = \gamma q^2 / 2 + \beta q'^2 / 2,$$

The term $\Pi = \gamma q^2 / 2$ is the potential energy of the oscillator; the coefficient $\gamma$ is called the stiffness of the oscillator. The term $K = \beta q'^2 / 2$ is the kinetic energy of the oscillator; the coefficient $\beta$ is called the oscillator mass. (Remember how the energy of the simplest oscillator is written - an external weight - and you will understand why $\gamma$ is called stiffness, and $\beta$ is called the mass of the oscillator.)

No matter how specifically the oscillator is structured, its angular part of oscillations $\omega = 2\pi / T$ is expressed as follows through rigidity $\gamma$ and mass $\beta$:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\gamma / \beta}.$$

The introduction of a “generalized” oscillator, the coordinate of which is not necessarily the distance from the equilibrium position, but can have any meaning, as long as the energy has the same structure as $E$ in expression (*), allows us to draw conclusions about the increase in the oscillating system without considering the physical nature and origin of the processes in it.

For clarity, let’s give another example of an oscillator, which is not similar to all the examples given above. Suppose there is an inductor $L$ of good conductive wire, the ends of which are connected to a capacitor embone $C$, which has a charge $Q_0$. Electromagnetic oscillations occur in the circuit. If the coil is made of a superconductor (this requires low temperature), the oscillations will be practically undamped.

The energy of such a system consists of two terms: the energy of the magnetic field in the coil and the energy of the electric field in the capacitor. The energy of the electric field of the capacitor is proportional to the square of the charge $Q$, which is currently located on the capacitor plate: $\Pi = \frac{1}{C} Q^2 / 2$. The energy of the magnetic field of the coil is proportional to the square of the current $I$ currently flowing through the coil: $K = LI^2 / 2$. But the current strength is equal to the rate of change of the capacitor charge over time: $I = Q'$. Thus, the energy of the magnetic field of the coil is $K = LQ'^2 / 2$.

Therefore, the energy of the system at any moment of time is equal to

$$E = \frac{1}{C} Q^2 / 2 + LQ'^2 / 2.$$

According to quantum mechanics, the energy of an electron in an atom can only take discrete values.

Possible values ​​of electron energies in a hydrogen atom are given by the expression*

$$E_n = - \frac{me^4}{\hbar^2} \frac{1}{2n^2}.$$

The differences in the values ​​of $E_n$ for two different $n (n=1, 2, 3, \dots)$ are determined with greater accuracy in the frequencies of the spectral lines observed in the experiment.

Let us denote by $q$ the generalized coordinate of the oscillator. Let's write down the energy of the oscillator:

$$E = \gamma q^2 / 2 + \beta q'^2 / 2.$$

One can imagine that the oscillator is a certain “particle” with mass $\beta$, oscillating outwardly with rigidity $\gamma$. In order to form the basic idea of ​​quantum mechanics for this object, we introduce the wavelength $\lambda$ of the wave process associated with our “particle”:

$$\lambda = 2\pi \hbar / \beta q'.$$

The denominator is the product of “mass” and its speed. Since the speed changes as the particle moves, the wavelength also changes - it is the minimum value of the equilibrium position and grows in those areas where the speed of the “particle” is low.

Let the “particle” move in the region from $-q_0$ to $q_0$. (Emphasizing that $2q_0$ is not the range of oscillator oscillations, but the region in which its coordinate “trembles.”) In order for a standing wave to form, the “length” $2q_0$ must contain an integer half-wave: $2q_0 = (n+1)\lambda / 2$ ($n=0, 1, 2, \dots$). As an estimate of the speed, we take the value of the speed at $q = q_0$, i.e. $(q'_0 = 2\pi \hbar / \beta \lambda = \pi \hbar (n+1) / 2\beta q_0)$. Substituting this value into the expression for kinetic energy, we find

$$W_n = \frac{\pi^2 \hbar^2}{8\beta} \frac{(n+1)^2}{q_0^2}.$$

For the total energy we get

$$E_n(q_0) = \frac{(\pi \hbar)^2}{8\beta} \frac{(n+1)^2}{q_0^2} + \frac{\gamma q_0^2}{2}.$$

For small values ​​of $q_0$ the energy is high due to the first term, and for large values ​​of $q_0$ - due to the second. The applicable value $q_0$, which gives the lowest energy, is obtained if both terms are equated. From this condition we find

$$q_0^2 = \frac{\pi \hbar (n+1)}{2\sqrt{\beta \gamma}}.$$

Substituting into the expression for energy, we get

$$E_n = \frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \sqrt{\gamma / \beta} (n+1) = \frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \omega (n+1) \quad (n=0, 1, 2, \dots).$$

The quantity $\omega = \sqrt{\gamma / \beta}$ represents the oscillation frequency of the classical oscillator.

An exact calculation for the energy yields the expression

$$E_n = \hbar \omega (n + \frac{1}{2}).$$

Thus, we made a mistake in the numerical factor ($\pi/2$ instead of 1), as well as in the numerical value of the energy in the lowest state, i.e. at $n=0$ ($\frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \omega$ instead of $\frac{1}{2} \hbar \omega$). But everything else worked out exactly! Now that the result has been obtained, we should think about what we used to obtain it and what follows from the expressions we obtained for the oscillator energy and for the value $q_0^2$.

First of all, we applied to our oscillator, without being interested in how it is structured, the principles of quantum mechanics, originally established in relation to electrons. Of course, it is natural to expect that the general principles should be the same for other particles whose mass differs from the mass of the electron. Indeed, such a generalization has been experimentally confirmed with great accuracy. But why can these principles be applied to something like an oscillatory circuit, where the role of “coordinate” is played by the charge on the capacitor plates? Here we come to an important assumption that was and is widely used in theoretical physics of the 20th century. If two systems have energy that equally depends on coordinates and speed, then such systems have the same properties, despite the fact that “coordinates” and “velocities” may have completely different meanings in these systems.

We have the right to consider that we have solved the problem of applying quantum mechanics to all possible oscillators at once.

Now let’s think about what will show us the results that are useful to us. How do they translate into the formulas of classical mechanics? First of all, we found that the energy does not change intermittently, but in portions of magnitude $\hbar \omega$. True, the value of $\hbar$ is very small, and for general macrosoptic oscillators this abrupt change in energy is practically not observed. However, there are special macroscopic systems where discontinuity plays a certain role (for example, lasers). The correctness of the expression we obtained for the oscillator energy has been verified with great accuracy for many types of oscillators.

There is one more important property of a quantum oscillator, which we also obtained. When the energy is at its minimum, the classical oscillator is at rest in the equilibrium position; Meanwhile, the quantum oscillator in its most energetic state makes oscillations (they are called “zero oscillations”). The kinetic and potential energy of these oscillations is of the order of $\hbar \omega$. In this case, the average value of the oscillator coordinate is zero, and the average value of the squared coordinate is given by the above formula for $q^2$. This remarkable property of quantum oscillators has been well tested in experiment and plays an important role in modern physics.

If we consider sound oscillations in a solid body as a set of quantum oscillators, then we find that at absolute zero temperature the atoms of the solid body are not motionless, but perform zero oscillations. And this fact was confirmed by experiments on light scattering at low temperatures! If we consider electromagnetic waves that can propagate in empty space as a set of oscillators, then we will come to the conclusion that in emptiness, even when there are no particles or quanta in it, zero oscillations of the electromagnetic field should be found. And these oscillations were also discovered during testing! But this is a more complex issue that requires detailed discussion.

Understanding arises in the process of work

We will introduce several incomprehensible words: quantization, wave process associated with a particle, quantum oscillator, etc. And we began to act, not really their concepts. Nevertheless, we have found how the energy of the hydrogen atom depends on the quantum number $n$; They learned that a quantum oscillator oscillates in its highest energy state, and they even began to apply the results of quantization of the oscillator to the oscillations of the electromagnetic field in the void. And as a result of these actions, at least partial understanding arose.

So, understanding arises in the process of work. After all, if we tried to achieve complete understanding before we began our calculations, nothing would work. Understanding does not arise suddenly, but as you progress in your work. Conversely, work progresses as understanding deepens.

In order to achieve a deeper understanding, you need to solve problems in this area of ​​physics yourself. Passive study gives only a faint idea of ​​the beauties that are discovered through independent work.
