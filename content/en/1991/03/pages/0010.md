$$\lambda = \frac{2\pi \hbar}{mv},$$
where $m$ is the mass of the particle, $v$ is its speed. Discrete values ​​of electron energy are obtained from the condition that an integer number of waves must fit into the length of the orbit along which the electron moves. If the orbital radius is $r$, then the $n$ state of the electron corresponds to the condition $2\pi r = \lambda n$ ($n=1, 2, \ldots$), or $mv_n = \hbar n/r$. From here it is not difficult to find the kinetic energy in the $n$-state:

$$W_n = m v_n^2 / 2 = \hbar^2 n^2 / 2 m r^2.$$

The total energy of the electron is the sum of the kinetic energy and the potential energy in the nuclear field, which is negative and equal to $U = -Ze^2 / r$. The total energy of an electron in a hydrogen atom is

$$E_n(r) = \hbar^2 n^2 / 2 m r^2 - e^2 / r.$$

In our derivation we assume that the orbital radius $r$ has a fixed value. According to quantum mechanics, the radius of the orbits is “scattered” in the vicinity of the classical orbital sources. As an estimate of the radius, we can take the value $r$, which corresponds to the minimum energy $E(r)$.

To find the minimum $E(r)$, we proceed as follows. Let's rewrite the expression for $E(r)$ as follows:

$$E = A x^2 - B x.$$

We used the following designations: $x = 1 / r$, $A = h^2 n^2 / 2 m$, $B = e^2$. It can be seen that for $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 = B / A$ the value of $E$ is zero. Inside the integral $|x_1, x_2| E$ is negative. Somewhere inside this integral lies the minimum $E$. For the sake of estimation, we will assume that $E_{\min}$ corresponds to the value $x$ in the middle of the integral, i.e. $x = B / 2 A$. Matching, $r = 1 / x = 2 A / B$, i.e.

$$r_n = \frac{\hbar^2 n^2}{me^2}.$$

(Note that for $n = 1$ this expression gives a correct estimate for the radius of the atom in its best state.) Substituting this value for $r_n$ into the expression for $E_n(r)$ gives

$$E_n = - \frac{me^4}{\hbar^2} \frac{1}{2 n^2},$$

i.e., an expression matching (**)!

In reality, an electron can be located at any distance from the nucleus with different probabilities. Our simplification consists in the assumption that this distance is defined, equal to $r$, and is found from the minimum energy condition. Of course, we acted as a group. Therefore, you cannot trust the forward numeric factor of this formula, even though it happens to be correct. But everything else can be trusted! And the set $me^4 / \hbar^2$, and, most importantly, the dependence on the “quantum number” $n$.

An exact solution would require the value of the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics (the Schrödinger equation) and very complex (according to school concepts) mathematics. What we got is a qualitative solution, when the result is obtained accurate to an unknown numerical factor, but the nature of the dependence on the parameters of the problem is conveyed accurately. Qualitative solutions make it extremely easy to obtain an accurate solution, since the main features of the phenomenon are clarified. Moreover, if there is a high-quality solution, but an exact one cannot be obtained analytically, it can be done without much loss in understanding the problem using computers.
