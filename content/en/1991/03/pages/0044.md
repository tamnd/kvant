First of all, let's discuss what the melting point (or melting point) of ice is and why it is interesting. As is known, when this temperature is reached, the ice stops heating, and the body absorbed by it is entirely used to melt the ice. If the liquid stops flowing, the remaining ice and water will be in thermal equilibrium. Thus, the melting point is the equilibrium temperature between water and ice at a given pressure. So, at a pressure of 1 atm it is equal to 0 °C. How much will this temperature change when the pressure increases to 1.01 atm?

It turns out that calculating the melting point of ice will help us...
Carnot's theorem. Yes, yes, the same theorem that talks about the maximum efficiency of heat engines. “What does the melting point of ice have to do with it?” - you ask. The fact is that Carnot proved a theorem about the maximum efficiency of a cyclic heat engine of any type, regardless of what is included in it as a working fluid: an ideal gas, ice or soap film. Only one condition must be observed: the machine must receive heat only at a temperature of $T_1$, and give it away only at a temperature of $T_2$ (heat exchange should not occur at intermediate stages). The maximum efficiency of such a machine (it is called an ideal Carnot machine) is realized in the slow, reverse mode of its operation and, regardless of the operating external machine, is equal to

$$\eta = \frac{A}{Q_1} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{T_1},$$

where $A$ is the work of the machine per cycle, $Q_1$ is the amount of heat received by the machine from heating.

Let's imagine our imaginary Carnot machine in the form of a cylinder with a piston (Fig. 1, a). Let's put ice in it with a mass of $m$ under a pressure of $p_1 = 1$ atm at a temperature of $t_1 = 0 °C$ ($T_1 = 273$ K). Constant pressure is provided by a weight resting on the piston. To maintain that water and ice are in balance, we depicted small amounts of water in the corner of the vessel.

Let us describe in stages how one cycle will occur in our Carnot machine.

1) Let's put the cylinder on a heater (thermostat) having a temperature $T_1$, and transfer the system to the heat $Q_1 = \lambda m$ necessary to melt all the ice ($\lambda$ is the specific melting temperature). In this case, the piston with the load will lower slightly (Fig. 1, b), since the volume of ice $V_\pi = m/\rho_\pi$ is greater than the volume of water $V_B = m/\rho_B$. On the graph in coordinates $p, V$ (Fig. 2), this process corresponds to line 1-2.

2) Let's remove the vessel from the thermostat and heat it (Fig. 1, s), after which we will slowly increase the pressure until it becomes equal to $p_1 + \Delta p = 1,01$ atm (to do this, we will gradually add sand to the piston). In this case, the temperature of the system will drop to a value $T_2 = T_1 - \Delta T$, equal to the melting point of ice at a pressure of 1.01 atm.
