Now we carry out calculations. The work per cycle can be easily found from the graph in coordinates $p$, $V$ - it is equal to the area of ​​the figure limited by the graph:

$$A = \Delta p \left( \frac{m}{\rho_{\pi}} - \frac{m}{\rho_{\mathrm{B}}} \right).$$

The amount of heat received from the heater is

$$Q_1 = \lambda m.$$

Pozny from Carnot’s theorem (1) we obtain

$$\Delta p \left( \frac{m}{\rho_{\pi}} - \frac{m}{\rho_{\mathrm{B}}} \right) / (\lambda m) = \Delta T / T_1,$$

or, for any temperature $T_1 = T$,

$$\Delta T = \Delta p \frac{T}{\lambda} \left( \frac{1}{\rho_{\pi}} - \frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{B}}} \right).$$

This correspondence is called the Clapeyron-Clausius equation. Substituting numerical values ​​into it, we find that at $\Delta p = 0,01$ atm $\Delta T = 9,2 \cdot 10^{-5}$ K. It can be seen that the effect is very weak. To change the melting temperature by 1 K, for example, creates a pressure of $\approx 133$ atm. Let's return, however, to our skates.

The pressure of a person wearing skates can be estimated as $p = mg / S \approx 600 \text{ H} / 2 \text{ cm}^2 = 30$ atm. Then the melting point displacement under the skates is $\approx 0,3$ K, which is of course very small on a frosty day. So the “naive” schoolboy turned out to be right: lubricant is formed primarily due to friction. What is the role of skates in this case? After all, they are still needed! Without discussing the “physics of skating” in detail, we note one very important fact: to lubricate the surface of a skate, the melt of which is small, you need to grow much less ice than to lubricate the sole of a boot.

Are there such pressures in the surrounding population that a noticeable shift in the melting point of ice occurs? Of course they do. Let's give just one example - overcoming a very heavy glacier sliding down a slope. In the place where the glacier rests on a piece of rock or stone, very high pressure is created, and the ice begins to melt. The glacier seems to float the stone in its movement, passing it through itself. After the pressure is “relieved,” the water on the glacier freezes again.

“This is, of course, interesting, but already very exotic,” you say. “Is this really all that we have achieved?” Of course not. Let's take a look at our result. We have learned to calculate how the equilibrium temperature of two phases—liquid (water) and solid (ice)—changes when external pressure changes. The best thing is that you can take any other phases together, as long as they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. For example, liquid - vapor, metal - melt, solid - vapor, etc. In other words, the Clapeyron-Clausius equation is valid not only for melting, but also for any other process of transition from one phase to another (evaporation, sublimation, etc.). Formula (2) then includes the densities of the substance in these phases and the corresponding specific heat of phase transformation.
