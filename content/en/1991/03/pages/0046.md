What is the difference between the processes of ice melting and water evaporation? Why does the equilibrium temperature of the phases decrease with increasing pressure in one case, and increase in the other? The thing is. When ice absorbs heat and melts, the volume of the system decreases (the density of water is greater than the density of ice), and when water absorbs heat and evaporates, the volume of the system increases (the density of saturated vapor is less than the density of water). However, the cycle graph in coordinates $p$, $V$ in both cases must go clockwise - otherwise the machine’s work per cycle will be negative. Compare the course of both graphs (see Fig. 2 and 3) and you will appreciate why in one case more pressure corresponds to less temperature, and in the other - vice versa. In addition, try to think through the sequence of actions that must be performed with a vessel containing water and steam in order to obtain the Carnot cycle shown on the graph.

We will complete our analysis with a numerical assessment of the boiling point mixing when the pressure increases from 1 atm to 1.01 atm. To do this, in formula (2), we replace the specific heat of boiling of ice with the specific heat of vaporization of water $r$, and the density of ice with the density of saturated steam $\rho_{\pi}$:

$$\Delta T = \Delta p \frac{T}{r} \left( \frac{1}{\rho_{\pi}} - \frac{1}{\rho_{\beta}} \right).$$

We find the density of saturated water vapor at $T=373$ K and $p=1$ atm from the Mendleev-Clapeyron equation:

$$\rho_{\pi} = \frac{pM}{RT} \approx 0,58 \text{ кг/м}^3.$$

Substituting numerical values, for $\Delta p=0,01$ atm we obtain $\Delta T \approx 0,28$ K.

As you can see, in this case the effect is quite noticeable: in order to increase the boiling point by 1 K, you need to increase the pressure by only 0.035 atm, which is completely feasible even under normal conditions.

What does the spectrum of the hydrogen atom say?

As is known, the simplest radiation is from isolated atoms, for example, atoms of monatomic gases or vapors of neoctor metals. Such spectra are a set of discrete spectral lines of different intensities, corresponding to different wavelengths. They are called line spectra.

When gases or vapors, whose molecules consist of several atoms, glow, positive spectra appear—collections of groups of spectral lines. Finally, radiation emitted by heated liquids and solids has a continuous spectrum that contains all possible wavelengths.
