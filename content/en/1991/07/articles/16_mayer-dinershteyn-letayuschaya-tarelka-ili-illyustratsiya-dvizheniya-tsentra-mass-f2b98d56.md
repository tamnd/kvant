Flying Saucer, or Illustration of the Motion of the Center of Mass

V. MAYER, V. DINERSTEIN

It would probably be correct to say: a falling plate. But the word “fall” has some kind of negative connotation, and I would like the experience offered in this article to give you only positive emotions.

Demonstration. You take a disk covered on one side with black cloth with a white circle in the center, raise it as high as possible in front of you and release it in a vertical position. Disk falls along a straight trajectory into a box with foam rubber or cotton wool standing on the floor (Fig. 1, a). You repeat the experiment, and again nothing happens. You raise the disk for the third time, release it, and... suddenly everyone sees that, as it falls, it describes a decaying sinusoid (Fig. 1, b).

This can't happen! After all, a body released without an initial velocity always falls down one hundred percent in a straight line, doesn’t it? No, not like that. Not the body, but its center of mass, when falling freely without initial speed, moves in a straight line. And you certainly know this.

On the movement of the center of mass. Recall that the coordinate of the center of mass (c) of a system of two point materials with masses $m_1, m_2$ and coordinates $x_1, x_2$ is given by the simple relation

$$x_c = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

From here you can find the position of the center of mass of any system. For this replacement point, an important theorem is proven that the center of mass moves as if the entire mass of the system is concentrated in it and all external forces are applied to it. Internal forces, i.e., interaction forces between parts of the system, do not affect the movement of the center of mass.

If a system of $n$ parts, the masses of which are $m_1, m_2, ..., m_n$, moves in the gravitational field of the Earth, then each particle is subject to the force of gravity, which is external to the system under consideration. Therefore, the sum of all external forces applied to the center of mass of the system is equal to

$$\vec{F} = m_1 \vec{g} + m_2 \vec{g} + ... + m_n \vec{g} = m \vec{g},$$

where $m$ is the mass of the system and $\vec{g}$ is the free pressure acceleration. According to the theorem on the motion of the center of mass of the system, its acceleration

$$\vec{a}_c = \vec{F} / m = \vec{g},$$

Consequently, in the Earth's gravity field, the center of mass of the motion system is with acceleration of free pressure.

Thus, the center of mass of a body thrown at an angle to the horizon describes a parabola, and one released from a certain height describes a straight line. However, individual parts of the system (body) can move in a more complex manner. For example, if they mix relative to each other.

Experimental device. It is now easy to understand the experience described at the beginning. Obviously, the disk visible to everyone, which oscillated when falling, is one part of the system, and inside there must necessarily be another part that also oscillates, and in such a way that the center of mass of the entire system moves in a straight line.

Indeed, turn the dial on the reverse side and we will see how the device works (Fig. 2). A ring of polystyrene foam 1 with an outer diameter of 30 cm, an inner diameter of 25 cm and a thickness of 2 cm is cut at the top. In slotted dioral pads 2, to which an elastic strip 3 made of vinyl plastic measuring $2 \times 15 \times 150$ mm is attached. A steel or brass weight 4 measuring $25 \times 25 \times 50$ mm is attached to the end of the shelf.

A clamp 5 made of copper wire with a diameter of 2 mm is attached to the load. The foam ring is tightly wrapped with electrical tape 6 - this winding secures the dioral pads to the ring and slightly increases the strength of the ring.

You can make such a device during receipt. Cut a ring from a sheet of foam plastic of suitable thickness using a hacksaw blade. Make an elastic strip from an old plastic ruler. Secure the weight, clamp and pads to the elastic strip with fasteners or bolts and nuts. Or you can simply tie them tightly with wire or electrical tape.

When demonstrating the experiment, tilt the load to the right, take the ring in your right hand and press the latch against the ring with your thumb. No one will notice this if the front of the device is covered with opaque fabric. By releasing the device, you will simultaneously release the deformed elastic strip with the load. In this case, the disk and the load will fall, oscillating. To make the vibrations more noticeable to the audience, attach a small circle of thick white paper to the dark cloth in the center of the disk with adhesive tape.

Elementary theory. To explain the experiment, consider the following simplified model. Let's take mass $M$ and stretch it along its horizontal diameter with an ideal spring with stiffness $k$. In the center of the hoop $O$ we attach an external load with mass $m$.

Let us first assume that the hoop is clamped motionless and there is no gravity. Let's place the origin of coordinates at point $O$, and direct the $X$ axis horizontally to the right (Fig. 3, a). Obviously, the center of mass of the system, consisting of a hoop and a load, coincides with the origin of coordinates $O$. Let us shift the load from the equilibrium position so that its coordinate becomes $x$ (Fig. 3, b). In this case, the clamp will deform by amounts $\Delta l = x$ and an elastic force $F$ will arise, the projection of which on the axis $X$ is equal to $F_x = -kx$. from the sides of the two halves of the spring, the stiffness of each of which is equal to $k/2$. So the total elastic force $F=2(k/2)x=kx.$ According to Newton’s second law, under the influence of this force the load acquires acceleration

$$a_x=-\frac{k}{m}x.$$

This equation, as is known, describes a harmonic oscillation, the frequency of which

$$\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$$

It is clear that in fact the oscillations of the load will be damped, since friction, which we silently neglected, always exists. Therefore, the last formula gives only an approximation of the oscillation frequency.

Now let's free the hoop and pull the load from the equilibrium position, as shown in figure 4, $a.$ Let the centers of mass of the hoop and the load be at points $C$ and $G$, respectively, and the origin of coordinates is at the center of mass of the system - at point $O.$ In the new coordinate system, the center of mass of the hoop has the coordinate $X$, and the load - $x.$

At the same time, let’s release the hoop and the weight so that they begin to fall freely. Since the acceleration of gravity does not have a component along the $X$ axis, the speed of the center of mass of the system being considered along this axis will not change. And since at the initial moment of time this speed was equal to zero, the coordinate of the center of mass of the system will not change during the entire time of free fall of the system.

As for the centers of mass of the hoop and the load, they will begin to move towards each other and at some point will coincide with the center of mass of the entire system (Fig. 4, 6). Then they are placed in places and so on, i.e. there will be coordinates relative to the center of mass of the system falling along a vertical straight line.

Substituting the values ​​$x_1=x, x_2=X, m_1=m$ and $m_2=M$ into formula (*) and taking into account that $x_c=0$, we obtain that for any moment of time the equality

$$\frac{mx+MX}{m+M}=0.$$

It follows that the coordinates of the centers of mass of the hoop and the load are related by the relation

$$X=-\frac{m}{M}x.$$

In this case, the spring is deformed by amounts

$$\Delta l=x+|X|=x-X=(1+m/M)x,$$

the load is acted upon by a force directed to the origin of coordinates, the projection of which

$$F_x=-k\Delta l=-k(1+m/M)x,$$

and the acceleration of the load is

$$a_x=-k\frac{m+M}{mM}x.$$

Comparing this expression with the expression for the acceleration of a load in a fixed hoop, we come to the conclusion that the load in a freely falling hoop oscillates with a frequency

$$\omega_1 = \sqrt{k \frac{m+M}{mM}} = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \sqrt{1 + \frac{m}{M}} = \omega \sqrt{1 + \frac{m}{M}}.$$
The hoop itself vibrates with the same frequency $\omega_1$, but its oscillations occur in antiphase with the oscillations of the load.
It is also easy to estimate the amplitudes of the initial vibrations of the hoop. Let us assume that the internal radius of the hoop is $R$, i.e., the maximum displacement of the load relative to the center of the hoop is $\Delta l_{\max} = R$. Then we find that the maximum value of the hoop oscillation amplitude is
$$X_{\max} = \frac{m}{M} x_{\max} = \frac{m}{M} \frac{\Delta l_{\max}}{1+m/M} = \frac{R}{1+M/m}.$$
Conclusion. The described experiment will be impressive if the hoop vibrates with a sufficiently large amplitude and a sufficiently high frequency. The latter will become just as significant as the first if we remember that a freely falling hoop moves quite quickly, and in a short period of observation one must be able to notice at least a few vibrations. The corresponding formulas obtained show that it is possible to increase the frequency and amplitudes of oscillations in only one way - by reducing the relative mass of the hoop and the load $M/m$. We hope that you will be able to perform specific calculations and experiments on your own.
