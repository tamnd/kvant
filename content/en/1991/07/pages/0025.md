I made an agreement with the client to align the top side of the square with the bottom, and the right side with the left. From a square you get a torus, and from columns and rows you get a ring. By replacing empty cells with zeros and canceled cells with ones, I discovered that one of the ring rows (and the corresponding column) turned out to be a $n$-ring (a solution to Problem 5).

Moreover, there is also a “$n$-torus”, each row and column of which is a $n$-ring. To obtain it, it is enough to write some $n$-sequence in the first line of the square-sweep, and in each subsequent line, shift it by one cell (Fig. 5). It is interesting that some diagonal strings turn out to be $n$-sequences. So I found a way to construct squares satisfying conditions 7 for any $n$ for which there is a $n$-ring.

Planmetric analysis of a universal ruler

I agreed on its existence a long time ago. If the universal ruler measured non-repeating lengths, then its planned analogue should measure non-repeating areas. This means that this is a kind of flat template with exact answers for the vertices of the measured squares. So,

Problem 8 (new). What is the minimum number of cells $p(n)$ that must be marked in square $n \times n$ so that among them there are all four cells whose centers lie at the vertices of squares $1 \times 1$, $2 \times 2$, ..., $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ on the side parallel to the sides of square $n \times n$?

The idea behind the solution is this. Let us assume that in the original marking no two squares have common vertices. Then it contains exactly $4(n-1)$ cells (4 cells per square). But such markings can be easily detected by combining parts of the squares so that the non-tortic ones of their vertices coincide. If these combinations are arbitrary within the original square $n \times n$, then, obviously, the requirement of the problem will still be met.

We will combine the squares so that their diagonals lie on the diagonal of the original square. Then for the total number of marking cells $p(n)$ the following estimate holds:

$$p(n) \leqslant 2(n-1) + \varphi(n),$$

where $\varphi(n) \leqslant 2(n-1)$ is the number of cells that are canceled on the diagonal of the square $n \times n$, and $(n-1)$ is the number of cells that are canceled symmetrically on both sides.

I would like to arrange the squares so that $\varphi(n)$ is minimal. But then a diagonal larger than a square turns into a universal ruler of Problem 2 (Fig. 6): it must have a minimum number of divisions and change all segments (diagonals of squares) from 1 to $(n-1)$.

With this marking method $\varphi(n) \leqslant (n+2)/2$. Her analysis is simple and is left to the reader.

So, $p(n) \leqslant 2(n-1) + (n+2)/2 = (5n-2)/2$ if $n$ is even, and $p(n) \leqslant (5n-1)/2$ if $n$ is odd.

A more accurate estimate of $p(n)$ is given by another way of marking a diagonal larger than a square (Fig. 7). First, we mark $m$ cells in a row. Then we move the remaining diagonal cells at intervals of $(n-1)$ cells. The last cell of the diagonal is displaced in any case (to change the square $n \times n$). (In addition, the first $m$ cells of each column in which the diagonal cell turns out to be displaced, and the $m$ cells symmetrical to them in the corresponding lines are displaced. But for now we will mark only the diagonal.) Let us express the side of the square $n$ in terms of $m$ and $\varphi(n)$:

$$n = m + (\varphi(n) - m) \text{ или}$$
$$n = m(\varphi(n) + 1) - m^2.$$

Using the division formula with a remainder, it is not difficult to prove that for any $m$ in such a cell layout, our “ruler” will change all the squares on the side 1, 2, ..., $(n-1)$.

How large can $n$ be made by changing $m$ with fixed $\varphi(n) = k?$ Let us write formula (2) in the form

$$n = (k+1)^2/4 - (m-k+1)/2^2.$$
