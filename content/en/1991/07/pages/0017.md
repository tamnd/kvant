Dividing a square into five parts

By analogy with the previous cases, it is natural to try to divide the square into five rectangles (for example, Fig. 8). However, such a division turns out to be insufficiently “small”: if the square is cut in the same way as the rectangle was cut into figure 1, we obtain less than the value of the maximum diameter of the part.

So, let's try to divide the square into two quadrangles and three pentagons, so that their diameters are equal. This requirement, naturally when searching for the required partition, is satisfied at $AE = \frac{13}{3}AB$ (Fig. 9). In this case, all diameters are equal to $\frac{5\sqrt{34}}{64} = 0,455$...

Let us prove that for any division of a square into five parts, the diameter of one of them is not less than this number.

Let us assume that the square was divided into five parts of diameter less than $\frac{5\sqrt{34}}{64}$. Obviously, all the vertices of the square belong to four different sets. It is also clear that the points of the fifth set can fall on no more than two (and adjacent) sides of the square. Of course, it may turn out that they are not on any of its sides or that they are on exactly one side.
