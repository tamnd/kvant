ON SMALL
PLATES

I. KOKOREV, L. KURLYANDCHIK

At the fifteenth All-Union Olympiad in tenth-class mathematics, the following problem was proposed: “There are 6 points in a rectangle $3 \times 4$ cm. Prove that there is a pair of points separated from each other by no more than $\sqrt{5}$ cm.”

In the book by N.B. Vasilyev and A.A. Egorov “Problems of the All-Union Mathematical Olympiads” (M., “Nauka”, 1988) its solution was found on two lines: “Of any six given points, at least two will end up in one of the figures shown on figure 1.”

However, this problem is a fragment of a very interesting and meaningful mathematical problem. We will talk about it in this article.

Figure diameter

Imagine a serving table, and on it there is a larger cake. There are $n$ guests sitting at the table, and in front of each there is a small plate. Therefore, you, as the owner, need to cut this cake into $n$ pieces so that their sizes are as small as possible.

By asking guests to wait a little, let's approach the problem scientifically. A piece of cake has the shape of some geometric figure. Let us introduce the concept of diameter for it.

What does the words “the diameter of a circle is $d$” mean? On the one hand, this means that the distance between any two points on the circle does not exceed $d$. On the other hand, one can find two of its points such that the distance between them is exactly equal to $d$.

By analogy with a circle, the diameter of an arbitrary figure $F$ is a number $d$ such that the distance between any two points of the figure $F$ does not exceed $d$, and there are two such points such that the distance between them is $d$.

It's easy to understand that the diameter of a polygon is the greatest distance between its vertices (check it out!).

Now let's get back to waiting patiently for the guests. So, you are faced with the task: cut the cake into $n$ parts so that the diameters of most of them are as small as possible.
