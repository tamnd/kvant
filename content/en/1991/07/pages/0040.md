On plane A, an additional positive charge will appear, and on plane B, a negative charge will appear. So, the pieoelectric effect consists in the fact that when some crystals are deformed, an electrostatic voltage arises between their edges.

Now that we know the principle of operation of a pie lighter, it is easy to turn it into a high voltage source. One of the possible methods is shown in figure 2. A metal tube (say, a worn-out ballpoint pen) is placed over the protruding electrode. The tube is reliably insulated from the housing, then wires are connected to it and to the housing - and the voltage source is ready. Keep in mind that 10 kilovolts is a very high voltage, so be careful and never handle both wires with your hands! If you promise to take all precautions, you can conduct a number of independent experiments.

Take an overheated light bulb, connect wire A to the cycle, and press wire B to the glass bulb (Fig. 3). Turn off the light in the room, press and release the lighter button, and sparks will appear in the light bulb between the filament and the glass of the bulb. With practice, you can receive discharges of various types, including the fires of Holy Elmo.

Hold a magnet close to the glass of the flask and you will be able to see how the glowing cords will deviate towards the pole of the magnet. What happens if you bring two magnets in?

You can even make an electronic digitizer - the dream of all science fiction writers. Remember how the Segner wheel (Fig. 4, a) works? Water escaping from the tubes provides the entire wheel with a rotational movement. Now imagine that electrons are flying from the tubes together with water - if there are a lot of them and they fly quickly, the wheel should also end.
