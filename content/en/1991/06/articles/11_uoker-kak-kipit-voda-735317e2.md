(Leidenfrost effect)
J. WALKER

The fact that a drop of water applied to a metal whose temperature is much higher than the boiling point of water lasts a long time was first described as early as 1732, but was not widely studied until 1756, when Johann Gottlieb Leidenfrost published his Treatise on Some Properties of Common Water. Because Leidenfrost's work was not translated from Latin until 1965, it became little known. Nevertheless, now it is his name that is associated with the phenomenon of the long life of a drop on a hot plate. In addition, the temperature corresponding to the peak of my plot of the lifetime of the droplet versus the surface temperature (Fig. 1) is called the Leidenfrost point.

Leidenfrost experimented with an iron spoon heated red-hot in a forge. By placing a drop of water in a spoon, he changed its lifespan using a swinging matrix. He noted that the drop seemed to suck up the light and heat from the spoon, leaving a stain on the surface that was duller than the rest of the spoon. The first drop lasted 30 seconds in the spoon, the second drop only 10, the subsequent drops only a few seconds.

Leidenfrost misunderstood the results of his experiments because he did not realize that the lingering drops were actually boiling. Let me explain this.

When the plate temperature is below the Leidenfrost point, water spreads over the plate and quickly removes heat from it, which ensures complete evaporation of the drop in a few seconds. When the temperature is at or above the Leidenfrost point, the bottom of the drop applied to the plate evaporates almost instantly, and the pressure of the resulting vapor prevents the rest of the drop from touching the plate. The steam layer is constantly replenished by additional water evaporating from the bottom surface, thanks to the heat from the plate that is radiated and conducted through the steam. Although the layer thickness is less than 0.1 mm at the outer boundary and about 0.2 mm at the center, it dramatically replaces the evaporation of the droplet (Fig. 2). This way the steam supports and protects the droplet for a minute or so.

To show the flow of steam from under a drop of Leidenfrost, I sprinkled it on the plate.

I also created large ambo-like drops of water by inserting a syringe into the top of the floating drop and adding a few shavings of water to it. Similar experiments can be carried out not only with water, but also with other liquids. For vinegar, for example, the Leidenfrost point corresponds to a temperature of about 250 °C, for spirit - about 150 °C.

After reading the translation of Leidenfrost's research, I remembered the description of an interesting trio that was shown at carnivals at the beginning of the century. It was said that the performer could dip his fingers into molten lead. Deciding that there was no trickery there, I suggested that this trio should be based on the Leidenfrost effect. As soon as the performer's wet fingers touched the hot liquid metal, part of the water evaporated, covering the brush with a layer of steam. If your fingers are immersed quickly, they will not heat up significantly.

I couldn't resist the temptation to test my explanation. Using a laborator burner, I melted a large piece of lead in a crucible. I then heated the lead to over 400°C, which is hot above its melting point (328°C). I wet my finger in tap water and prepared to touch the surface of the molten lead. I must admit that my assistant was standing next to the pharmacy. I must also admit that the first few attempts were unsuccessful, since my brain protested against this stupid experiment, pointing the finger of mismo lead.

When I finally got over my fear and quickly touched the lead, I was amazed. As I thought, some of the water on the finger evaporated, forming a protective layer. Since the contact was short, the radiation and conductivity of the body were not enough to meaningfully raise the temperature of the skin. I got carried away. Having wet my brush, I plunged all my fingers into the lead and touched the bottom of the vessel - when there was no such thing. Apparently, the effectiveness of Leidenfrost, or more precisely, the presence of film boiling, protected my fingers.

I still doubted my explanation. Is it possible to touch lead with a dry finger without getting burned? Throwing away all rational thoughts, I tried to do this and instantly realized my stupidity when I used my finger. I continued the experiment with a small dry pacifier, immersing it in molten lead for a few seconds. Its skin quickly turned black - it lacked the protection of filmy boils, like my dry finger.

You should not repeat this experience under any circumstances!

It must be said that immersing your fingers in molten lead poses a serious danger. If the lead's temperature is just above the scorching point, heat loss from water evaporation can cause the lead to harden on the fingers. If you pull the resulting “glove” of hot solid lead out of a vessel, it will be in contact with your fingers for so long that they will definitely be completely burnt. In addition, dirty fingers are very dangerous. When excess water evaporates, it can cause molten lead to burst into the surrounding area and, most importantly, into the eyes. Such explosive fumes left me with scars on my arms and face.

Film boiling can also be seen when liquid nitrogen is drunk - large and small drops curl into balls and roll along the floor. The temperature of liquid nitrogen is about - 200 °C. When a drop of soaked liquid approaches the floor, its lower surface evaporates, the resulting layer of steam supports the remaining liquid, allowing it to live for an amazingly long time.

I was told about a trioke, in which the performer poured liquid nitrogen into his mouth “without getting burned,” despite its extremely low temperature. The liquid immediately underwent film boiling on the lower surface and did not directly touch the tongue. Out of stupidity, I repeated this experiment. Repeatedly everything went smoothly and efficiently. With a large drop of liquid nitrogen in my mouth, I concentrated on not peeking through it while exhaling. The moisture in my cold breath condensed, creating a terrible stream that pulled out of my mouth for about a meter. However, the last time the liquid thermally damaged my two front teeth so much that the enamel turned into a “road map” of cracks. My dentist convinced me to stop this experience.

The Leidenfrost effect may also play a role in another daredevil experience: walking on hot coals. Sometimes the media, with great fuss and various mystical nonsense, report on a performer walking on hot coals. It is stated that protection from strong hunting is given by the power of the spirit over matter. In fact, during a successful “walk,” physics protects the feet. Particularly important is the fact that although the surface of the coals is very hot, its thermal conductivity is very low. If the performer walks at a moderate pace, the touch of the foot is so brief that it conducts very little energy from the coals. Of course, walking more slowly can cause burning, since longer contact will allow the tell to be transferred to the foot not from the surface, but from the inside of the coals.

If you wet your feet before walking, the liquid can also help seal them. For example, a performer might walk on damp grass before stepping onto hot coals. Or he could simply be sweaty from the heat of the coals or excitement before the performance. When the performer steps on the coals, the heat evaporates the moisture in the feet and less tella is transferred to the skin. In addition, there may be contact points where the liquid undergoes film boiling, which also provides protection from hot coals.

I walked on hot coals five times. Four times I was quite afraid, and neither were sweaty. However, for the fifth time I took my safety for granted, and nothing was dry. The burns I received turned out to be severe and very incomprehensible. The legs did not heal for several weeks.

My failure could have been caused by the lack of film boiling on my feet, but I also neglected an additional safety factor. In previous days, I had been clutching an early edition of this book to my chest out of anticipation*) to strengthen my faith in physics. Alas, on the day I was so badly burned, I forgot the book.

I argued for a long time that the last of the final exams for obtaining an academic degree was supposed to be “walking on fire.” If a candidate for a degree believes in physics so much that he is not burned, the chairman immediately hands him a diploma. This test will be much more revealing than traditional exams.

*) This refers to the book: D. Halliday, R. Resnics “Fundamentals of Physics”, Third edition extended, 1988 (John Willey & Sons, N.Y.).
