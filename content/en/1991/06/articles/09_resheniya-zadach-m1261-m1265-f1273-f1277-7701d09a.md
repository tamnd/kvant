M1263. Inside the circle there are two more circles touching the outer circle at points A and B, respectively, and intersecting each other. Prove that if one of the intersection points lies on the segment AB, then the sum of the radii between the circles is equal to the radius of the larger one. Is the opposite true?

Let $O, O_1, O_2$ be the centers of these circles; $r, r_1, r_2$, respectively, are their radii (see figure), with $O_1$ lying on OA, and $O_2$ on OB. If the point C of the intersection of circles $O_1$ and $O_2$ lies on AB, then the isosceles triangles OAB, O_1AC and O_2BC are similar to each other (they have equal base angles). From here it obviously follows that $OO_1CO_2$ is a parallelogram, and therefore $r = OA = OO_1 + O_1A = O_2C + O_1A = r_2 + r_1$.

The converse statement is also true: if $r = r_1 + r_2$, then the segment AB passes through the intersection point of the circles $O_1$ and $O_2$. To prove it, we construct a parallelogram $OO_1C'O'_2$ whose vertices $C'$ and $O'_2$ lie on the segments AB and OB. Triangles $O_1AC'$ and $O'_2BC'$ are similar to isosceles triangle OAB, so

$$O_1C' = O_1A = r_1,$$

which means $C'$ lies on the circle $O_1$, and

$$O'_2B = O'_2C' = OO_1 = OA - O_1A = r_2,$$

whence it follows that $O'_2 = O_2$ and that $C'$ also lies on the circle $O_2$.

F1273. A rope of length $l$ is fixed at one of its ends at the vertex of a sphere of radius $R$ (Fig. 1). At some point the rope is released. Find the acceleration of the rope immediately after this. Trenz is missing.

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

The only force that accelerates a rope is the force of gravity. This means that we need to find its component along the sphere.

Divide the rope into small sections of length $\Delta l$ (Fig. 2). Let the angle between the radius drawn to one of such sections from the center of the sphere and vertically be equal to $\varphi$. Then the component of the gravitational force along the sphere for this section is equal to

$$\Delta F = \Delta mg \sin \varphi = \frac{M}{l} \Delta lg \sin \varphi,$$

where $M$ is the length of the rope. But from figure it is clear that $\Delta l \sin \varphi = \Delta h,$

where $\Delta h$ is the difference in heights between the ends of this section, therefore,

$$\Delta F = \frac{M}{l} g \Delta h.$$

The required total accelerating force is

$$F = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta F_i = \frac{M}{l} g \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta h_i = \frac{M}{l} g H.$$

If the length of the rope is equal, for example, to four circumferences, i.e. $l = \pi R/2$, $H = R$, and the acceleration of the rope at the initial moment is equal to

$$a = \frac{F}{M} = \frac{2}{\pi} g.$$

F1275. A point particle having mass $m$ and charge $Q$ is placed at a distance $L$ from an infinite conducting plane and released. How long does it take the particle to reach the plane? There is also no strength. (Hint: You can use the mirror image method.)

When a charged particle is placed near a conducting plane, charges are induced on this plane that are attractive to the particle. Their action is equivalent to the action of an image charge, equal in magnitude - $Q$ and located at the same distance from the plane, but on its other side (see figure). The force acting on a particle located at a distance $x$ from the plane is found from Coulomb’s law:

$$F = \frac{kQ^2}{(2x)^2} = \frac{kQ^2}{4x^2}.$$

Let's imagine that the same force acts on our particle, which has experienced not electrical, but gravitational interaction with the exact mass $M$ placed at point $O$. This mass should be

$$M = \frac{Fx^2}{Gm} = \frac{kQ^2}{4mG}.$$

Now the motion of a particle can be described using Kepler's laws.

The trajectory of a particle can be considered a very elongated ellipse, having a major semi-axis $a = L/2$ and a minor semi-axis $b \ll a$ (in this case, the ellipse focuses at point $O$ and at the point of the first initial semi-axis of the particle). Let us replace the elliptical orbit with a circular one with radius $L$ and find the period of revolution $T_0$ of the particle around the mass $M$:

$$m \frac{4\pi^2}{T_0^2} L = G \frac{mM}{L^2} \Rightarrow T_0 = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L^3}{GM}}.$$

To find the period $T$ in the case of an ellipse with semi-axis $a = L/2$, we apply Kepler’s third law:

$$T = T_0 \left( \frac{a}{a_0} \right)^{3/2} = T_0 \left( \frac{L/2}{L} \right)^{3/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}} T_0.$$

Obviously, the time it takes for the particle to reach the plane is equal to half the period of revolution:

$$t = \frac{1}{2} T = \frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}} T_0 = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}} \sqrt{\frac{L^3}{GM}} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}} \frac{L}{Q} \sqrt{\frac{Lm}{k}}.$$

F1276. At a height of $H=20$ m above the floor plane, at the vertices of the square on the side $l=1$ m, there are four identical loudspeakers. They are fed a sinusoidal cycle with a frequency of $f=1$ kHz. At what distance from the loudspeaker's maximum point on the floor does the volume reach zero?

The point of maximum volume on the floor is located exactly under the center of the square, at the vertices of which the loudspeakers are located. (For simplicity, we will consider them to be exact radiative sounds; however, for the frequency $f=1000$ we have chosen, the wavelength is $\lambda=0,33$ m and our simplification is not entirely justified, although forced.) If we move away from the maximum point, then the results from the radiative wave will have different phases and the result of the addition will be less than at the center.

Let's make one more necessary simplification - we will assume that the minimum point of interest to us is located not far from the center (compared to the height of the hall) and the amplitudes of the incoming waves are practically the same (generally speaking, as we move away from the emitter, the amplitude decreases). It is clear that close to the center we will find a minimum point if we mix along the floor parallel to one of the sides of the square (see figure). The minimum condition is determined by the difference in the path of waves from one pair of emitters (at the same time from the other):

$$\sqrt{H^2 + (a/2+x)^2} - \sqrt{H^2 + (a/2-x)^2} = \lambda/2.$$

You can solve this equation either numerically - by selecting the desired value $x$ on a calculator, or use some more civilized

F1277. A closed figure was made from thin wires, shown in figure. The radius of the semicircle is R. Where is the center of gravity of this figure? What is the period of small vibrations relative to point O?

To determine the position of the center of mass (center of gravity) of the figure, we will use not direct calculation, but energy messages. Namely, by the property of the center of mass that the potential energy of an arbitrary body with mass $m$ can be calculated using the formula

$$E_p = mgh_\pi,$$

where $h_\pi$ is the height of the center of mass relative to the raised zero level.

Let’s rotate our figure by a small angle $\varphi$ (see figure) and find the change in its potential energy, and we’ll do this in two ways. First, let’s connect $\Delta E_p$ with the change in the height of the figure’s center of mass (taking into account the smallness of the angle $\varphi$):

$$\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h_\pi = mgl_\pi(1 - \cos \varphi) = mgl_\pi \cdot 2\sin^2(\varphi/2) = \frac{1}{2} mgl_\pi \varphi^2,$$

where $l_\pi$ is the distance from the center of mass to point O. Secondly, we will use the features of the wiring formula

Let's continue our calculations, and again use energy messages. Note that if the energy of the oscillatory system can be reduced to the form

$$E = E_p + E_k = \frac{Ax^2}{2} + \frac{Bx'^2}{2},$$

where $x$ is a parameter describing the deviation of the system from the equilibrium position, and $x'$ is the time derivative of $x$, then the period of oscillation of the system is

$$A = \frac{2mgR}{2 + \pi}.$$
