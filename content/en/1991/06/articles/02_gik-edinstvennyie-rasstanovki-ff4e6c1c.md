THE ONLY
ARRANGEMENTS

Martin Gardner is a classic of modern and intriguing mathematics, the author of more than two dozen books on this topic. Many of them have been translated into Russian. It is curious that almost every publication by M. Harder contains many mathematical problems, games and puzzles with chess addition. This also applies to the last two books recently published in our country: “Tic-tac-poly” (“World”, 1988) and “Time Travel” (“World”, 1990). Now we will discuss several problems from these books.

Let's start with the following simple task. Imagine that you and a friend have finished the game and want to arrange the pieces for a new game. What is the maximum number of pieces movements required for this?

In this record situation, there will be as many as 38 permutations. Let's look at one white piece first. The king and queen can be swapped in only three “moves”. The queen is placed on any free square, then the king is placed on the home square e1 and the queen on d1. It is not difficult to see that in four “moves” the pieces in the lower left corner and the same number in the lower right corner fall into place. Finally, in eight “moves” the pawns will be in place. In total, 19 “moves” of white pieces, and the same number of black ones.

An Amazon is a piece that moves as both a queen and a knight. Let's look at one puzzle based on the Amazons.

As is known, on an n×n board for any n>3 it is possible to place n queens that do not threaten each other. How many “peaceful” Amazons can you place?

H. Long discovered that on a 5x5 board only four amazons are reduced, and on a 10x10 board - ten, and the solutions in general cases are unique (up to the thresholds of the board and its mirror images). In the drawings, the Amazons are depicted as dots.

In the general case, this problem, each one, was not solved in any way. We are talking not only about finding the necessary arrangements, but also about counting their number for different values ​​of n. By the way, having gone through all 92 solutions to the classical problem of eight queens, it is not difficult to see that not a single one of them is suitable for the Amazons, i.e. it is impossible to place eight of these fabulous figures on the board so that they do not threaten each other.

In the book “Tic-Tac-Tac,” an entire chapter is devoted to chess; it is called “Chess Problems.” The reader can find most of the problem and puzzles contained in it in my book “Chess and Mathematics” (“Science”, 1983), but Harder also has several discoveries that are new to me. They are associated with the placement of queens on an n×n board that have certain properties.

For example, the following question: how to place n queens on an n×n board so that there is a maximum number of squares left on it free from the threat of queens? When n=1, 2 or 3 there are no such fields at all. With n=4 there can be only one field, and with n=5 there can be three, and the record position is again the only one - (board transformation does not count).

There is a hypothesis according to which the maximum number of squares not attacked by queens, for n = 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12, is equal to 5, 7, 11, 16, 22, 27 and 36 respectively. Finding the record formations (or breaking the record) is left to the reader.

In conclusion, two interesting facts. It turns out that there is only one way to place three queens on a 6x6 board so that all free squares are at risk (transforming the board does not count).

And on a 7x7 board, you can place four queens so that all free squares are under threat and not a single pair of queens attacks each other.

And again this location is the only one.

E. Geek
