DIELECTRIC
ANALOGUE MAGNETS

Doctor of Technical Sciences
G. EFASHKIN

Magnets in the form of pieces of iron ore are often found in nature and have been known since ancient times. When the connection between magnetic and electrical phenomena was discovered, the question arose: is it possible to obtain an electrical analogue of a magnet - a dielectric, which would create an electric field? This assumption was made by the English physicist O. Hevisyad in 1892. He also proposed a name for the analogue of a magnet - “electret”.

Electrets are not found in nature in their natural state. Is it possible to obtain electrium artificially, how are artificial magnets obtained? Magnetization of a magnetic material (magnet) is carried out by placing it in an external magnetic field. You can try to obtain electriet in a similar way, by placing the dielectric in an external electric field.

The matter turned out to be more complicated. Only in 1922, thirty years after Hevisyad's theoretical assumption, Mototoro Eguchi in Japan managed to obtain the first electrets. Eguchi placed a mixture of palm resin and rosin (Carnauba wax) between two metal electrodes, melted the mixture, turned on the direction and, without removing the direction, cooled it. The result was a dielectric plate that created an external electric field - an electret.

Soon after this, the first electrite microphone was created. The design of the microphone was extremely simple. The electret was placed between two electric ones, one of which was pressed tightly, and the second, which played the role of a membrane, was placed with a small gap. The microphone worked without a power source. This is an arbitrary impression of a miracle. In fact, when the membrane vibrated, the electric field between the electret changed, and a current was generated in the external circuit. The electret directly converts sound energy into electrical current.

Why are such simple microphones not widely used? As the designers say, carnauba wax was a low-tech material - it melted at a low temperature and was fragile.

But the idea was good. Therefore, starting with the search for the technology of this electretonic material. In the 50s, at the Physical Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences, G. I. Skanavi and A. N. Gubkin obtained durable ceramics with good electrical properties - calcium titanate - and designed a microphone on a ceramic electret.

In the 60s, electrical efficiency was discovered in thin polymer films of lavsan, fluoroplastic, and polycarbonate. The practical application of electrets began to develop rapidly.

In parallel with the search for new electrical materials, work has been carried out on the theory of the electrical state of dielectrics. The Soviet scientist Professor A.N. Gubkin created the phenomenological theory of electrites, which is now recognized throughout the world.

Only the surface charges will remain uncompensated. The dielectric becomes polarized. Charges of one sign appear on one surface, and charges of the opposite sign appear on the other. Free charges in the external field move (drift) and are also separated.

The orientation of dipfields and the drift of free charges occur in time. If the process occurs quickly, in a very short period of time (millionths of a second), the polarization is called instantaneous. Inter-polarization (polarization time is calculated in minutes, hours, days and even years) is called relaxation. To characterize the polarization process, the concept of relaxation times is introduced.

Relaxation polarization can be thought of as if the dielectric dipoles are slowly rotating in a viscous liquid. The relaxation time depends very much on temperature, and if the dielectric is heated, the strength of the “liquid” decreases, and the dipoles turn significantly faster.

The sign of the charge surface when the difields are oriented is opposite to the sign of the polarizing electrode. The pluses of the dipoles are attracted to the minus of the electrode. Therefore, surface charges are also called heterocharges (from the Greek word heteros - other).

Charges whose sign is the same as the sign of the electrode can “flow” (inject) from the electrode onto the surface of the dielectric. Such charges are called heterocharges (from the word homos - identical).

With relaxation polarization, if the external field is turned off, the warm motion does not immediately scatter the molecules. Depolarization of the dielectric is also slow. The dielectric remains filled for some time. Residual polarization occurs.

Let's place the electret material between two electrodes pressed to the material and apply a voltage $U_0$ to them; then a field $E_0 = U_0 / d$ will arise between the electrodes. In the field, relaxation polarization of dipoles occurs and a surface heterocharge is formed, the density of which is $\sigma_{\text{retero}}$ (Fig. 1). On figure difields are depicted in the form of elongated rectangles. Homocharges are injected from the electrodes onto the surface of the electret material. They are depicted in circles on figure. The density of homocharges is $\sigma_{\text{гомо}}$. The densities of heterocharges are not the same. The total charge density is $\sigma_{\text{сум}} = \sigma_{\text{retero}} - \sigma_{\text{гомо}}$.

Turn off the voltage, remove the external field, remove the electrodes. We got the electret. When the electret material was polarized, the charges separated, positive charges appeared on one surface, and negative charges appeared on the other. By analogy with magnets, the “north” and “south” poles were formed.

Let's consider a free (without electrodes) electret. The electric field of the electret is created by the difference between homo and heterocharges. Some heterocharges are overlapped by homocharges. Let's imagine that there is only excess charge (Fig. 2) on the surface. The picture is familiar. After all, this is a flat-plate capacitor. The difference is that in a flat capacitor the charge is on the metal plates and it is free, that is, it can easily drain from the metal. In this case, they say that the electret is considered in the approximation of a flat capacitor. It is known that the strip capacitor is concentrated inside the capacitor. There is an external margin only at the edges. Therefore, in this approximation, the electret has only an internal field. Due to the inflow of charges from the outside and the slow drift of unbound charges present in the dielectric, as well as the chaotic tell motion, a slow depolarization of the electret occurs. The lifetime of an electret largely depends on the conductivity of the dielectric. The lower the conductivity, the longer the relaxation time, the more stable the electret. For this reason, ferroelectric dipoles are quickly screened by free charges. Ferroelectric electrets have a short lifetime.

Theoretically, all dielectrics have electret properties, but the lifetime of most of them is very short - seconds or, at best, hours. And only some dielectrics (ceramics made from calcium titanium, films of fluoroplastic, polycarbonate, lavsan, as well as polymethyl methacrylate plexiglass) retain an electreton state for a long time - months, years and even ten years. *)

How to use an electret for practical purposes? To do this, the electret is again placed between two electrodes, which are connected to each other by a target. As they say, the electret is short-circuited. But if the lower electrode is pressed against the electret, then the upper one is placed with a gap. Then induced charges (Fig. 3) arise on the metal electrode, and in the “electret-electrode” interaction the working field $E$ appears.

If the plate of the upper electrode moves and changes its position, then the field also changes, the magnitude of the induced charge changes accordingly, and since the charges are free on the electrode, they either flow off the electrode or flow onto it; current is generated in the circuit. The electret converts the external mechanical energy of the electrode vibrations into electrical energy.

This is how the principles of a short-circuited electret are used in practice. Many foreign companies produce radio equipment with electret microphones. In the Soviet Union in Tula, the Oktava plant produces about twenty modifications of high-quality electrified microphones, among which miniature microphones for service devices attract attention.

Electrets operate on the same principle and are stable for changing vibration, speed (tachometers), alternating current generators, and pressure sensors. Using electrets, you can control the electron beam, change the penetrating radiation, air humidity, you can make changing equipment to change the charges and potentials of the electrostatic field, and design focusing and sorting systems.

In the flat capacitor approximation, we assume that the charge is distributed uniformly over the surfaces of the electret. This has led to such a situation that the concept of uniform distribution has become a stereotype. A stereotype is a person’s habitual idea of ​​a phenomenon. Stereotypes are very difficult to convey and interfere with the correct assessment of phenomena.

The “uniform distribution” stereotype made it difficult to explain failures of stable operation on electrets or instability. When it became clear that the cause of the failures was the uneven distribution of charges, it must have been assumed that the unevenness would occur accidentally, due to technical defects.

“Probably the technical imperfections,” said the free skaters. “Nothing, after some time the charge will spread evenly over the surface,” the scientists said. “After all, on a flat metal capacitor the charge is always distributed evenly.”

The electret series is in principle discrete. Charge inhomogeneities begin at the atomic level. The electric fields of discrete charges are nonuniform, and if the charges are arranged regularly, as in a crystal lattice, then the fields are periodic. If you move away from the surface, the periodicity smoothes out. The fields are homogenized, and quite quickly the field becomes homogeneous. Like in a television image: if we look at it closely, we see a system of dots of different brightness, and the product can see the smoothly changing color of the image.

The size of a thin near-surface layer (TS-layers), within which the discreteness of the charge and the inhomogeneity of the field is located, is a quantitative criterion understood as “uniform” and “inhomogeneous” distribution.

Thus, for charge elements arranged regularly (crystal lattice plane), the TP of layers is only 3A (1A = 10⁻¹⁰ m). If we examine the electret from a distance greater than 3A, then we can assume that the charges are distributed evenly and the field is uniform.

The model did not contradict the previously accepted flat capacitor approximation. Indeed, we can assume that the field outside the thin surface layer is zero, as for a flat capacitor.

The proposed model includes the parallel-plate capacitor approximation as a special case.

The electret was “willingly” polarized in the form of a system of round charge spots. Calculations have shown that, for round charge spots of TP layers, the value is on the order of three spot radii.

Near-surface fields can be very strong and can be used for practical purposes. By adjusting the size and pitch of the spots, you can form the required field values ​​(Fig. 5). The electret shown in figure is called an artificial discrete charge structure (IDS) electret.

From the model of an electret with a discrete near-surface charge, as if from a horn of an image, fundamentally new possibilities for the use of near-surface fields of electrets began to pour out.
