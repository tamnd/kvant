Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. SAVIN

We associate the words “perpetual motion machine” (in Latilian “perpetuum-mobile”) with a machine similar to the one depicted in figure 1: a wheel with rolling internal balls, which, according to the inventor, not only circles itself indefinitely, but also sets other machines and machines in motion. But from school, we know for certain that such a machine cannot work, since this would contradict the law of conservation of energy. True, hundreds of new (and not new) projects for such machines come to the academies of sciences around the world every day. Physicists call them perpetual motion machines of the first kind. So, there are also perpetual motion engines of the second kind? Yes. We'll talk about them.

The idea of ​​a perpetual motion machine of the second kind is to, having two equally heated bodies, transfer part of the body from one of them to the other without expending energy, and then obtain the energy of motion using heat engines that use the resulting temperature difference. As a result of the operation of the heat engine, body temperature is equalized again. Then we again transfer the tello from one body to another, get work - and so on ad infinitum. A perpetual motion machine of the second kind does not contradict the law of conservation of energy. Here, energy does not disappear or appear, but only moves first from one body to another, and then is spent on the operation of a heat engine that produces useful work. In this case, the temperature of both bodies becomes lower than initially, but this loss of the body can be compensated by heating from the heat of the environment.

However, another law prevents the implementation of perpetual motion machines of the second kind. His ideas were founded in the works of the remarkable French physicist Sadi Carnot, the son of the outstanding activity of the Great French Revolution, the famous mathematician Lazare Carnot, and developed in the works of the English physicist W. Thompson and the German physicist R. Clausius. This law is called the “second law of thermodynamics” and sounds like this:

It is impossible to transfer a part of a body from one body to another without expenditure of energy if the temperature of the first body does not exceed the temperature of the second.

This request doesn't look very convincing. Is he always faithful?
The great English physicist J. Maxwell expressed the idea to refute this request. It was called "Maxwell's demon." Let's, he says, take a box of gas, divide it in half with a partition, near which a demon will sit and let through the partition, from the left half to the right, only the fast gas molecules, leaving the slow ones in the left. Since the temperature of a gas is characterized by the average speed of its molecules, the gas in the right half of the box will heat up, and in the left half it will cool. Several designs were proposed that, according to the authors, were supposed to play the role of such a demon, but they revealed unaccounted effects due to which the designs did not work.

Where physicists give up, it turns out that mathematical conditions are added. I offer you a device free from demons and other innumerable forces, which can be made even in a school workshop. It, according to calculations, is capable of transferring heat from one body to another if the bodies were initially heated to the same temperature.

Before we begin to describe our perpetual motion machine, it is necessary to swallow a certain dose of mathematics so that you can convince yourself of the correctness of further reasoning and construction. We are talking about the elipis and its properties.

An ellipse is a curve consisting of all points of the plane, the sum of the distances from which to two given points $F_1$ and $F_2$ (they are called the foci of the ellipse) is a constant. Generally it is denoted by 2a. This property of the elipis is used by gardeners when marking oval climbs. They dig two sticks into the ground (in the elipis tricks), call the ends of the rope to them, then take another stick with a sharp end and use it to pull the rope (Fig. 2).

If you now move this stick, leaving the rope taut, then its sharp end will draw an ellipse on the ground. Its size and shape will depend on the length of the rope and the distance between the sticks stuck into the ground. You can do the same in a notebook if you take pins together with two sticks, and a pencil with the third.

After drawing several ellipses, you will come to the conclusion that every ellipis is a closed convex curve with a center of symmetry and two axes of symmetry, one of which is the line $F_1F_2$, and the second is a line perpendicular to it, passing through the middle of the segment $F_1F_2$. In addition, it is easy to notice that for points lying inside the ellipse, the sum of the distance to the foci is less than 2a, and for points lying outside it, this sum is greater than 2a.

This information is enough to prove an important and non-obvious property of the ellipiss:

If an arbitrary point $M$ lying on the elipiss is connected by segments with its foci, then these segments form equal angles with the straight line tangent to the elipiss at this point $M$.

Let us recall the law of light reflection - the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection - we can form this property as follows: every ray emerging from one focus of the ellipse, after reflection, falls into the second focus. This property is called the optical property of the ellipse. It is also observed in nature: there are caves with ellipsoidal vaults, in which two places are found that are quite distant from each other, but the voice of a person standing in one of these places is heard in the other as if the speaker was standing nearby. In some palaces and castles, the architecture designed halls with such an effect.

Since the optical property of the ellipse will be the main one in our reasoning, we present its proof, especially since it is quite short and simple.

Let $l$ be a line tangent to the ellipse at point $M$, and let the segments $F_1M$ and $F_2M$ form unequal angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (Fig. 3) with the line $l$. Let's construct the point $F'_1$, symmetrically to the point $F_1$ relative to the straight line $l$. The point $N$ of intersection of the straight line $l$ with the segment $F'_1F_2$ is located outside the ellipse (due to its convexity), and therefore the sum of its distances to the foci is greater than that of the point $M$ lying on the ellipse. But from symmetry it follows that $F_1M = F'_1M$ and $F_1N = F'_1N$. Therefore, $F_2M + MF_1 = F_2M + F'_1M$, which is less than $F_2N + NF_1 = F_2N + NF'_1$, i.e. $F_2F'_1 > F_2M + MF'_1$. But the segments $F_2F'_1$, $F_2M$ and $MF'_1$ are sides of the triangle $F_2MF'_1$, and in it the sum of two sides are large triangles, i.e. the inverse correspondence $F_2F'_1 < F_2M + MF'_1$ is satisfied. Thus, the assumption that the angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are different led us to a contradiction. Therefore, these angles are equal (point $N$ coincides with point $M$).

Now let's begin our project of creating a perpetual motion machine of the 2nd kind. Let's take a sheet of good drawing paper, mark two points $F_1$ and $F_2$, draw two ellipses with foci at these points, taking one longer and the second shorter. Let us also draw a straight line passing through the middle of the segment $F_1F_2$ perpendicular to it. Now let’s take an eraser and erase part of what was drawn so that we get a “mushroom” similar to the one chosen on figure 4.

Let's roll the paper into a tube and go to the nearest southern workshop. There we will ask you to use this drawing to make a shell made of iron, which is obtained if the drawn curve is rotated around its axis of symmetry, called an ellipsoid of revolution). We will ask you to cover the inside of the resulting shell with reflective scrap. We will take the completed structure home. You have become the owner of a perpetual motion machine! Don't believe me? Let's prove this strictly mathematically.

If this partition were not there, then this ray, reflected from the part of the ellipse that we erased, would fall into focus $F_1$. In the combination of our design, which is spaced at figure 6, these will be rays located inside the angle $F_2AB$ and the angle $F_2A'B'$ symmetrical to it.

Thus, the body at point $F_2$ will heat up, and the body at point $F_1$ will cool. You can even calculate what the body temperatures will be after the process stabilizes, if the initial temperatures were $T_0$ degrees on the Kelvin scale.

(By the way, the Kelvin scale is named after the already mentioned English physicist W. Thompson, who for his outstanding scientific work

the servant was given the title Lord Kelvin.)

It is known that the radiation of a body is proportional to the fourth power of its temperature (on the Kelvin scale). Therefore, if at point $F_1$ the temperature is $T_1$, and at point $F_2$ it is $T_2$, then the radiation from point $F_1$ is $kT_1^4$; the radiation from point $F_2$ is equal to $kT_2^4$, but only part of it reaches point $F_1$. Which? Consider a small sphere around point $F_2$. Those rays that return back to point $F_2$ form a ring on this sphere (Fig. 7). If we denote its area by $S_1$, and the area of ​​the sphere by $S$, we obtain that the fraction of radiation returning back to point $F_2$ is equal to $\frac{S_1kT_2^4}{S}$, and the fraction of outgoing radiation is equal to $(S - S_1)\frac{kT_2^4}{S}$.

If the process is established, then the energy leaving the point $F_1$ is equal to the energy falling there, i.e.

$(S - S_1)\frac{kT_2^4}{S} = kT_1^4$.

At the same time, the thermal energy of a body at a temperature $T$ is equal to the product of the mass of this body and the heat capacity and the temperature of the body: $W = m\lambda T$. From the law of conservation of energy it follows that

$2m\lambda T_0 = m\lambda T_1 + m\lambda T_2$.

All! You can check your reasoning from the very beginning and make sure that it is correct. Have we really refuted the second law of thermodynamics? Unfortunately (or fortunately), no. There is a mistake here. What is she wearing? Think for yourself, and then check your answer with what is written below. I think that not everyone will answer this question correctly.

Those who look for an error in physics, for example, in that the convection process is not taken into account here, because a vacuum can be created inside a cavity, will be wrong. But apart from physics and mathematics, there seems to be nothing here? The error is located, as it were, on the border of these two sciences - during the transition from a physical process to its mathematical model.

Let us remember that we were talking about bodies that we place at focal points. Thus, we ignored the size of these bodies. This procedure is accustomed to physical reasoning, and the phrase “the body is at point $M$” does not evoke a feeling of protégé. Such disregard for the size of the body is justified in many cases, since the movement of the body under the influence of given forces applied to its center by gravity does not depend on the shape and size of the body. Moreover, in a number of other cases such a replacement is legal. Thus, I. Newoton showed that an elephant-homogeneous body (a body composed of several homogeneous concentric words) attracts other bodies in the same way as a point with the same mass located in the center of this ball. So replacing a body with a point is a common procedure in wave physics.

But in this case it is precisely this that leads to the error. Let's see what the consideration of balls of non-zero radius, whose centers lie at foci, gives us.

Let the point $M$ lie on the ellipse in its left half. Let's consider a ray emerging from point $F_2$, reflected from an ellipse at point $M$ and further to point $F_1$, as well as two rays going to point $M$ and absent from point $F_2$ at a distance $r$ (Fig. 8). Let's see at what distance $R$ from point $F_1$ they will pass after reflection from the ellipse at point $M$. From the similarity of triangles $AF_2M$ and $A_1F_1M$, as well as triangles $BF_2M$ and $B_1F_1M$, we obtain that

$$
\frac{r}{R} = \frac{F_2M}{F_1M}.
$$

Therefore, if point $M$ lies in the left half of the ellipse, then the reflected ray will pass from focus $F_1$ at a distance greater than it was from focus $F_2$. Thus, not all rays leaving the body $\Phi_2$ will hit the body $\Phi_1$ or return to $\Phi_2$, but will unwind. This circumstance destroys all our reasoning and constructions.
