Magnets and electrets:
similarities and differences
Magnets have long attracted human attention. But understanding of the nature of magnets arose only after it was discovered at the beginning of the last century that a magnetic field is created by moving charges (taks). The magnetic properties of Ampere's substance are due to closed electric currents within the substance. According to Ampere's hypothesis, elementary electric currents circulate inside atoms and molecules (the movement of electrons in atoms). In a magnetic state, the elementary currents in the body are oriented in such a way that their actions add up.

The magnetic properties of a substance are characterized by magnetic permeability μ. Ferromagnets (iron, cobalt, etc.) have enormous magnetic permeability, the magnetic fields of which are created not by the movement of electrons around the nuclei, but as a consequence of the properties of the electron, they create a magnetic field of “its own rotation” (spin).

The theory of ferromagnetism was developed by the French physicist P. Weiss, the Soviet scientist Ya. I. Frenkel and the German scientist W. Heisenberg. According to Weiss, a ferromagnet has microsoptic regions of micron dimensions - domains that are spontaneously magnetized until saturation (all spins inside the domain are oriented in the same direction). If there is no external field, the domains are oriented xotically, their fields compensate each other, and the ferromagnet is not magnetized. When an external field is turned on, the domains are oriented along the field, and the ferromagnet is magnetized (in reality, the magnetization process is more complex).

Experience is very effective, which confirms the domain structure of ferromagnets. If another coil is put on the ferromagnetic core, which is magnetized in the field of the coil, and connected to a loudspeaker, then when the magnetizing coil is turned on, the domains, orienting themselves in the magnetic field, produce a strong rustling sound that can be heard by the entire audience.

Areas of spontaneous magnetization can be seen under a microscope if fine magnet powder is applied to the polished surface of a ferromagnetic magnet. Powder particles, oriented in the magnetic field of the domains, exhibit the shape of the domains.

Dielectrics began to be studied much later. The term “dielectric” was introduced by Faraday in 1839, and, essentially, from that same time the systematic study of dielectrics began.

In this case, an analogy between magnetic and electrical phenomena was used. An analogue of a ferromagnetic magnet, a ferroelectric, was discovered in 1918. As in a ferromagnet, a ferroelectric contains domains—regions of spontaneous polarization (the domain's electric diopoles are oriented in one direction). Under the influence of an external field, the domains are oriented and the dielectric is polarized. Let us note an important feature of magnets that distinguishes them from electrets: the poles of a magnet, including at the molecular level, are inseparable. In other words, magnetic charges (so-called magnetic monopoles) do not exist. These, in part, explain the stability of natural magnets.

In dielectrics, positive and negative charges are mainly in a bonded state, for example, they are part of polar molecules (each such molecule forms an electric diopole); however, the dielectric may also contain unbound, free charges.

If there is no external field, the dielectric is unpolarized, diopoles and free charges are dispersed, and the dielectric has no field of its own.
