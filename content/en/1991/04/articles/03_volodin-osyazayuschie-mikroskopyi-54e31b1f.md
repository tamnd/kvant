Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. VOLODIN

Man has always been attracted to the microworld - the world of objects that cannot be seen with the eye. Research into the nature of the microworld is invaluable. They saved humanity from a great many terrible diseases, allowed engineers to create miracles of microelectronics, and scientists - to understand the nature of most of the phenomena occurring in our visible world.

But to understand the structure of the microworld, you must first see it. It was in order to examine the invisible world that the Danish researcher A. Leeuwenhoek in 1674 invented an optical microscope, now well known to every schoolchild. It uses a system of light refracting lines to display an enlarged image of an object from the focal area. Centuries have passed; During this time, the optical microscope of the present good worked in various fields of science, which rightfully became one of its symbols. However, its capabilities turned out to be limited: it allows us to consider only a micron-sized world. Scientists were attracted by submicron objects measured in hundreds and tens of nanometers. However, from Leeuwenhoek’s first microsop, it was possible to obtain an magnification of only 200 times; modern optical microsops are not far behind - their magnification does not exceed 1000. What’s the matter?

Abbe ban
Over 100 years ago, the famous German physicist and optician E. Abbe established that for any microscope working with light or other radiation focused by lenses, there will be fundamental limitations, and the main one is due to the waves igniting the object; it “hides” details less than half the radiation wavelength. And since the wavelength of visible light is a fraction of a micron, an object of submicron dimensions cannot be seen in an optical microscope.

To advance into the submicron world, it is logical to use radiation of varying wavelengths, for example RENTen's, or a flow of electrons (remember that an electron, like any elementary particle, is also a wave). Thus, in the 30s of our century, electronic microsoppia arose.

Particles of radiation, regardless of its nature, have a universal characteristic - energy. The higher the energy, the shorter the wavelength. A device built on the principle of an optical microscope, but working with a flow of electrons, supposed to specialize in magnetic lenses, is called an electron microscope. Electron waves are about a thousand times shorter than light waves, so the magnification of the best electron microscopes reaches one million. But it’s quite difficult: an electron microscopy is thousands of times larger, more complex and expensive than an optical one, and has a significant drawback - it destroys the object under study. The fact is that under the influence of electrons with energies of tens of electrons, all living things die, and defects appear in crystalline materials - violations of the regular arrangement of atoms. Nevertheless, the electron microscope allows a big step in the study of the submicron world.
