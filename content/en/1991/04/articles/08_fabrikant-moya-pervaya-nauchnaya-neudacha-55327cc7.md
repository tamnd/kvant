Academician of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR
V. FABRIKANT

My first, but, unfortunately, not the last major scientific failure was associated with participation in the first work on the study of Raman scattering of light.

The phenomenon of Raman scattering of light was discovered in 1928 by Soviet physicists G. S. Landsberg and L. I. Mandelstam in crits and, independently, indications by C. Raman in liquids. The Raman effect was called the Raman effect, and Raman was awarded the Nobel Prize. Soviet physicists did not receive the prize, which is clearly unfair.

The history of this discovery is quite peculiar.

In 1923, the Austrian physicist Cmekal published a theoretical paper in which he made a very clear prediction of the phenomenon of Raman scattering of light. In 1926, the Dutchman Kramers and the German Heisenberg repeated Smekal's prediction in a more detailed form. French physicists Rocard and Cabani tried to experimentally confirm theoretical predictions, but failed due to the weakness of the effect.

Raman scattering is one of the types of molecular light scattering. Here it is worth recalling one venerable story related to the birth of the theory of molecular scattering.

About the blue color of the sky

Maxwell, who examined Rayleigh, the future famous physicist, suggested that as a task he should give an explanation of the blue color of the sky. It was said that Maxwell hinted to Rayleigh that the phenomenon must be related to the molecular structure of air. Even Leonardo da Vinci was clear that the blue color of the sky is explained by the “physicality of the air,” the scattering of sunlight by the atmosphere. Rayleigh considered this issue from the position of the wave theory of light. A light wave propagating in the atmosphere causes vibrations in air molecules (we will say “electron vibrations”; Rayleigh and Maxwell did not yet know about their existence). Molecular vibrations are accompanied by the emission of light waves. Molecules work like relay radio stations. Each molecule emits secondary waves, which, according to Rayleigh, constitute scattered light. At the same time, Rayleigh showed that the intensity of light scattered by each molecule is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength of the scattered light (this law was established by him in 1871). Thus, the blue color of the sky was explained - blue and blue rays have shorter wavelengths than red and yellow, so blue and blue rays scatter more strongly. The number of comparisons between the results of the experimental study and Rayleigh's calculations should be in excellent agreement. Maxwell, very indo, was pleased with his student, and Rayleigh's work was published. It’s as if everything is so detailed.

However, in 1907, Mandelstam drew attention to the fact that there was a weak point in Rayleigh's reasoning. There is a saying: “The winner is not judged.” This saying is not entirely consistent

In this case, Mandelstam drew attention to the fact that secondary waves scattered by individual molecules are correct among themselves. It was found out, however (Smoluchowski, 1908; Einstein, 1910) that this will not happen due to the fact that there are inconsistencies in the spatial distribution of molecules (they are called fluctuations). What is Raman scattering?

The scattering of light on molecules, which Rayleigh studied to explain the blue color of the sky, occurs without a change in frequency (the portion of the scattered light is equal to the frequency of the incident light). Such scattering can be called elastic, since the state of the molecule (or atom) after scattering is the same as before it. In Raman scattering, simultaneously with the act of scattering, the state of the molecule changes, and the scattered light has a frequency different from the frequency of the incident light. Two new lines (satellites) were experimentally discovered in scattered light: one with a frequency higher than the incident one, the other with a lower one. What is the physical explanation for this phenomenon?

The change in frequency during Raman scattering of light can be explained in both quantum and wave terms. As we will see, in this case the quantum language is simpler and correctly reflects the peculiarity of the phenomenon.

Quantum addition and subtraction

A. Smekal considered Raman scattering as inelastic collisions of photons with atoms and molecules.

As is known, according to quantum theory, atoms and molecules can only have certain reserves of internal energy - be at certain energy levels. If an inelastic collision of a photon (energy $hv_0$) occurs with an unexcited molecule (or atom) located at the lower energy level $E_1$, then the photon gives up part of its energy and excites the molecule to a higher energy level $E_2$. After such a collision, a photon with reduced energy $hv_k$ (the index “k” comes from the word “red”) leaves the molecule:

$$hv_k = hv_0 - (E_2 - E_1).$$

If an inelastic collision of a photon occurs with an already excited molecule, then it moves from the level $E_2$ to the level $E_1$ and a photon with increased energy $hv_\phi$ appears (the index “f” comes from the word “phylot”):

$$hv_\phi = hv_0 + (E_2 - E_1).$$

In the first case, the quantum of intramolecular energy is subtracted from the quantum of radiant energy, and in the second, the addition of these quanta occurs. In the first case, the scattered photon is less than the frequency of the photon incident on the molecule:

$$v_k = v_0 - (E_2 - E_1)/h,$$

in the second - more:

$$v_\phi = v_0 + (E_2 - E_1)/h.$$

Thus, in scattered light, breaking with frequency $v_0$ (elastic collisions of photons with atoms and molecules), frequencies $v_k$ and $v_\phi$ should appear. In the spectrum of scattered light, this corresponds to two spectral lines (satellites), symmetrically shifted relative to the central

Classic multiplication

In classical language, Raman scattering is similar to the process of transmitting information via radio. L. I. Mandelstam drew attention to this analogy immediately after the experimental discovery of the phenomenon. The transmission of information via radio involves the use of modulated radio waves. We will limit ourselves to one type of modulation that has the widest application - amplitude modulation.

The fact is that an ideal sinusoidal wave, repeating its shape with sad constancy in time, is not capable of transferring information from one place to another. It is necessary to disrupt the correct shape of the wave, make a “notch” on it and observe the transition of the “notch” from one point in space to another. This “notch” is a change in the amplitude of the wave. The singer uses a microphone to modulate the amplitude of the radio wave, and this wave carries information about the song he is singing. Sound waves with a cyclic frequency $\omega_{3B} = 2\pi v_{3B}$ cause vibrations in the microphone membranes, which are converted into electrical vibrations of the same frequency. With their help, amplitude modulation of radio waves is carried out.

Let us use the services of trionometry for a more detailed characterization of the wave. Let the amplitude of the wave $A$ not be constant, but equal to $1 + a \cos(\omega_{3B} t)$; then for the simulated wave we get the following expression:

$$E = (1 + a \cos(\omega_{3B} t)) \cos \omega_0 t,$$

where $\omega_0$ is the cyclic frequency of the carrier wave (radio frequency). Using Trionometric Compliance

$$\cos \alpha \cos \beta = \frac{1}{2} (\cos (\alpha + \beta) + \cos (\alpha - \beta)),$$

this formula can be rewritten as

$$E = \cos (\omega_0 t) + \frac{a}{2} (\cos (\omega_0 + \omega_{3B}) t + \cos (\omega_0 - \omega_{3B}) t).$$

Thus, the simulated wave can be considered as consisting of three waves, each of which has a constant amplitude. The frequencies of these waves are $\omega_0, \omega_0 + \omega_{3B}$ and $\omega_0 - \omega_{3B}$.

Similar phenomena occur during molecular scattering. When interacting with a vibrating molecule (or atom), the incident wave is “modulated” with a frequency corresponding to the frequency of the vibrating molecule itself. This means, similar to the quantum picture, two satellite plates appear in the spectrum of scattered light, symmetrically aligned relative to the central line at the frequency of the bulb molecule.

As can be seen from the resulting formula, the amplitudes of these satellite plates should be equal to each other, which contradicts experience and the quantum picture.

On March 3, 1991, at the age of 84, the remarkable scientist and teacher, laureate of the USSR State Prize, Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, professor, full member of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR Valentin Aleksadrovich Fabrikant died.

During his long creative life, Valentin Aleksadrovich managed to do a lot - make wonderful discoveries (he is the founder of work on parametric amplification of light, which laid the foundation for the creation of lasers), educate a galaxy of students, write wonderful books.

V. A. Fabrikant was also at the origins of the creation of our magazine. Being a permanent member of the editorial collection, he was a great friend of readers and editors. When Valentin Aleksadrovich came to the editorial office, his delicacy, charm and intelligence attracted those around him, created around him that atmosphere of the aristocracy of spirit that we associate today with university life at the beginning of the century.

Until his last days, Valentin Aleksadrovich did not excel in his scientific, pedagogical and educational activities. And now the sad news of his death found the editors preparing for publication of his article - the one that lies in front of you. In this article, V. A. Fabrikant turns to the data of his youth. His work is a striking example of a scientist’s critical attitude to the results of his work, an attitude that is inherent in talent and is the driving force of progress.
