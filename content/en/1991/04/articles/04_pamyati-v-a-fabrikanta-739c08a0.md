Is it possible to penetrate into the depths of the microworld in another way?

How Abbe's ban was overcome
More recently, in the mid-80s, the path of development of microcopying tools changed completely. If earlier proresis was achieved within the framework of the Abbe ban by reducing the wavelength of the radiation building the image in the microcosole, now this ban was simply bypassed. A new generation of micropipes appeared - scanning microprobes, which made it possible to examine surfaces from a very close distance. And if the previous micro-braces were based on the principles of “vision”, then the new generation devices operate on the principle of “sensation”, and they not only provide information about the shape and location of the smallest details of an object, but also characterize their properties. Thus, a magnetic probe microscope “shocks” the inhomogeneity of the magnetization of an object, an electric one - the microdistribution of electric fields, a thermal one - temperature differences, etc. So, all the main physical parameters of the thermal one correspond to the probe microscope. And microprobes, based on electron tunneling and the action of mechanical interaction forces, make it possible to examine even individual atoms.

But let's not get ahead of ourselves and start with the optical probe micro-array.

Touching with light
Let's imagine an opaque finite shell with a tiny hole at the top, such that its diameter is not even half the wavelength of light (Fig. 1). If light is passed through such a shell, it will not travel far, since the light wave will not “crawl” into a hole of such dimensions and will be reflected back. And yet, on the other side of the hole, light can be detected, but only in the immediate vicinity - again at a distance of half a wavelength. It is this kind of “sagging” light that is used in new generation optical microscopes. (The sag property is of a quantum mechanical nature, characteristic of any wave or particle, and is called tunneling.)

Let us place the object under study near the hole, at a distance less than its diameter. A light spot approximately equal to the diameter of the hole will appear on its surface. The light reflected by an object can be captured by a photoconverter - a device that turns weak streams of light into an electrical signal. Piezoelectric fingers of microbranch

But how can you scan the surface of an object with such high accuracy? For this purpose, precision space-electric manipulators have been designed, the simplest of which is shown in figure 2. It is made of special ceramics that slightly changes size when the applied electric field changes, for which the manipulator is placed between the plates of a capacitor. Often the plates themselves are applied to the surface of the ceramic manipulator in the form of electrodes made of a thin layer of metal. By changing the direction on the electrodes by 0.1 V, it is possible to remove such a rod by only 0.1 nm, i.e., by the size of the atoma diameter. (The metal on the surface of the manipulator is thin enough so that, when stretched, it does not interfere with this movement.)

A simple design of three manipulator rods connected at one point perpendicular to each other, as shown in figure 3, can move a probe placed at the connection in all spatial directions. Tunnel microcoppe
settles the atoms

Let us consider in more detail the operation of the processing communication system using the example of a scanning tunnel microcopper; it was the first to appear in the family of probe microcopps. For his invention, G. Binning and G. Rohrer, employees of the computer company IBM in Zorix, were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1986. In it, the probe is an extremely sharp metal game. If we draw an analogy with an optical probe microcouple, then the role of the probe response in a tunnel microcouple is played by the tip of the game, from which the quantum mechanical waves of electrons contained in the metal of the tip “sag” together (Fig. 4). The length of such electronic waves is approximately a thousand times shorter than light, and, accordingly, they can “illuminate” an area in a thousand times less time than an optical probe. When such an electron wave touches the conducting surface under study (and this happens at a distance between the probe and the surface of about 1 nm), the electron from the tip can jump to the surface, in other words, “tunnel”. Tunneling means the appearance of an electric current in the probe-surface circuit, albeit a very weak current - in billionths of an ampere, but controlling it with the help of advanced electronics does not seem to be a problem. It is important that it depends extremely strongly on the distance between the tip and the surface. So, reducing the distance by a couple of angstroms, i.e., with

equal to the size of an atom, increases the tunneling current by a thousand times. The function reflecting this dependence is exponential (exponential function with base $e = 2,718...$).

Let us now return to the processing communication system, which ensures high-quality operation of any probe microcopper. It is a complex and sensitive electronic circuit that detects changes in the tunnel current and changes the voltage $U_z$ applied to the vertical manipulator. A piezoelective manipulator moves the probe so that the tunnel current remains constant. This is only possible if the distance between the probe and the surface remains unchanged. Thus, the processing connection does not provide the probe with any reports from the surface, nor much with it. Due to the extreme sensitivity of the tunnel current to the distance to the surface, the accuracy of the processing connection is very high - 0.1-0.01 A. As a result, it moves along a trajectory that carefully follows the topography of the surface scanned by the probe. And since the stress $U_z$ is proportional to the height of the surface above which the tip is located in a given ment, it serves as a convenient measure of the relief. Information about the surface topography is recorded in the computer memory and, after processing, which consists of filtering, i.e., eliminating noise and spurious signals, is displayed on the display in the form of a “tographic map” of the surface (Fig. 5). Microcop feels the repulsion of atoms

And yet, for all its attractiveness, the scanning tunnel microcop has a significant drawback - only electron-conducting objects are suitable for it. Most materials are coated with an insulating layer of oxides. Biological objects that are tempting to study often do not conduct current either. Can a scanning tunnel microcop detect “non-conducting” atoms? It turns out it can. It is enough to place a tiny piece of diamond attached to a thin metal strip between its tip and the surface of the insulating object, as shown in figure 6.
