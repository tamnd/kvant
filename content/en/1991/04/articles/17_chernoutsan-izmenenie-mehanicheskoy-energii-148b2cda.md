Change in Mechanical Energy
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. CHERNOUTSAN

The mechanical energy of a system of bodies can change for two reasons. Firstly, the presence of external forces can lead to a change in energy (in the event that the work done by these forces is not zero). And, secondly, even if there are no external forces, i.e. the system of bodies is closed, its mechanical energy may not be conserved. For example, under the action of the internal system - between the bodies included in it - the forces of friction, resistance and other so-called distal forces will result in the transition of part of the mechanical energy into the internal, warm energy of the bodies. But the sum of mechanical and internal energy in the case of a closed system remains constant - this is the general principle of conservation of energy, which goes further beyond the scope of mechanics.

An example of a process in which a decrease in mechanical energy occurs under the influence of forces of a dispersive nature is an inelastic impact. An example of the opposite kind is the rupture of a projectile, in which the mechanical energy is also not conserved, but does not kill, but returns. The point here is that as a result of the chemical reaction of combustion of an explosive, a large amount of internal chemical energy very quickly turns into mechanical energy. A similar process occurs when an internal engine is running. A person can play the same role when he does work using his internal energy resources (example: you pick up a stone from the ground and then throw it).

For the sake of the picture, let’s remember what forces “do not threaten” the law of conservation of mechanical energy, that is, they do not lead to either the loss or production of this energy. These are forces that are well known to you: gravity, elasticity, Coulomb interaction and other so-called conservative forces, i.e. forces whose work along a closed trajectory is zero. Correspondence, the dispersive forces discussed above are called non-conservative.

It is useful to keep in mind that for any conservative interaction it is possible to determine the corresponding potential energy, but for a non-conservative interaction this is impossible. This means that if the potential energy is known for each interaction, then it is called conservative.

Everything that has been said, of course, is known to any schoolchild from a physics textbook. Therefore, seeing that the conditions of the task mention, for example, the friction force (or friction coefficient), the applicant often opens up to the idea of ​​using energy messages when solving the problem. And he is right in the sense that it would be wrong to try to write down the law of conservation of mechanical energy. But it is possible and necessary in such cases to apply a formula for changing mechanical energy, which connects the change in mechanical energy with the work of the forces leading to this:

$$\Delta E_{\text{мек}} = A_{\text{внешн}} + A_{\text{внутр}}^*.$$ (*)

Here $A_{\text{внешн}}$ is the work of external forces, and $A_{\text{внутр}}^*$ is the work of internal non-conservative forces (most often the work of friction or resistance forces).

Let us also note e. To correctly use this formula, it is useful to rely on the following simple rule: on the right side it is necessary to take into account the work of all forces that are not taken into account in the expression for the potential energy of the system. For example, if for some reason it is more convenient for you to take into account the force of gravity in the work on the right side of the equation (*), then the expression for energy should no longer contain the term $mgh$.

And now - a few specific tasks.

Problem 1. A body of mass $m$ lies on a horizontal plane, connected to a vertical wall by a thin spring with stiffness $k$ (Fig. 1). At the initial moment the spring is not deformed. A constant force $F$ begins to act on the body. Assuming that the friction coefficient between the body and the plane is $m$ and that $F > \mu mg$, find the maximum displacement of the body from the initial position and the maximum speed of the body during movement.

In this case, the change in the mechanical energy of the system - the kinetic energy of the body $mv^2/2$ and the potential energy of the deformed spring $kx^2/2$ - occurs under the action of an external force $F$ and the sliding friction force $F_{\text{тр}} = \mu N = \mu mg$:

$$\left( \frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{kx^2}{2} \right) - 0 = Fx - F_{\text{тр}} x$$

(initial mechanical energy is zero). The maximum displacement of the body corresponds to the moment when the speed of the body goes to zero, so we get

$$x_{\text{max}} = \frac{2}{k} (F - \mu mg).$$

To determine the maximum speed of the body, it is necessary to find a correspondence to this moment value $x$. Note that the speed of the body will be maximum

Problem 2. A small body is placed on an inclined plane making an angle $\alpha$ with the horizon and released (Fig. 2). At the bottom point of the plane, the body hits the stop, bounces off without losing speed and rises back along the inclined plane to a certain height. Find this height $h_2$ if the initial height of the body is $h_1$, and the coefficient of friction of the body on the plane is $\mu$ ($\mu < tg \alpha$).

Unlike the previous case, there are no external forces here (in relation to the body-Earth system), and the change in mechanical energy occurs only under the influence of friction.

The work of the friction force is calculated very simply, since the magnitude of the friction force does not depend on which direction the body is moving: $F_{\tau p} = \mu N = \mu mg \cos \alpha$. Considering that both in the initial and final states the kinetic energy of the body is zero, we write equation (*) in the form

$$mgh_2 - mgh_1 = -\mu mg \cos \alpha \left( \frac{h_1}{\sin \alpha} + \frac{h_2}{\sin \alpha} \right),$$

where $h_1/\sin \alpha$ and $h_2/\sin \alpha$ are the paths traveled by the body down and up the inclined plane, and the sign “—” on the right side means that the work of the friction force is negative. Thus, we get

$$h_2 = h_1 \frac{tg \alpha - \mu}{tg \alpha + \mu}.$$

In this problem, we will apply the law of change in the mechanical energy of the system together with the law of conservation of momentum.

The speed of the body $v_1$ at the base of the inclined plane will be found from the law of conservation of mechanical energy:

$$\frac{m v_1^2}{2} = m g h \Rightarrow v_1 = \sqrt{2 g h},$$

and the final speed of the joint motion of the body and the cart $v_2$ will be calculated using the law of conservation of momentum:

$$m v_1 = (m + M) v_2 \Rightarrow v_2 = v_1 \frac{m}{m + M}.$$

The change in the energy of the “body-cart” system is the work of the friction force acting between them:

$$\frac{(m + M) v_2^2}{2} - \frac{m v_1^2}{2} = - F_{\text{тр}} L,$$

where $L$ is the distance traveled by the body along the cart. Considering that $F_{\text{тр}} = \mu N = \mu mg$, we get

$$L = h \frac{M}{\mu (m + M)}.$$

The question arises: why do we calculate the work of the friction force as if the cart were ending and the body was moving along it at a distance of $L$? The whole point is that a person does work not only on a stone, but also on... the Earth, and in order to restore the correct balance of energy, it is necessary to take into account the change in the energy of the Earth when throwing a stone. These arguments seem very unusual, since it is usually tacitly assumed that changes in the Earth’s speed can be neglected (the Earth’s mass is very large), and only the kinetic energy of bodies moving in the Earth’s gravity field is taken into account. And yet, let us bring our reasoning to the end.

The increment of the Earth's speed $\Delta \vec{V}$ can be found from the law of conservation of momentum of the “rock-Earth” system (we denote the mass of the Earth by $M$):

$$m\vec{v} + M\Delta \vec{V} = 0 \Rightarrow \Delta \vec{V} = -\frac{m}{M}\vec{v}.$$

In a reference frame where the initial velocity of the Earth is zero, the increment in the kinetic energy of the Earth is nothing small, and we promise not to take it into account:

$$\Delta E_3 = \frac{M(\Delta V)^2}{2} = \frac{m}{M} \frac{mv^2}{2} \ll \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

However, in a reference frame moving with speed $\vec{v}/2$, the change in the Earth's kinetic energy is not negligible:

$$\frac{1}{2} M \left( \frac{v}{2} + \Delta V \right)^2 - \frac{1}{2} M \left( \frac{v}{2} \right)^2 \approx \frac{1}{2} M v \Delta V = \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

It can be seen that here too the total work of a person is equal to $mv^2/2$, only it is performed not on the stone, but on the Earth.

Thus, it is necessary to keep in mind that the change in the kinetic energy of a very heavy body (Earth, walls, etc.) can be considered negligible only in the frame of reference where this body ends at the initial moment.

Problem 6. A ball suspended with external rigidity $k$ is spring into a liquid. The density of the ball materials $\rho$ is greater than the density of the liquid $\rho_{ж}$. First, the ball is held in such a position that the spring is not deformed, and then it releases.

How much heat will be released in the system by the time the ball stops oscillating and it is left behind? The volume of the ball is $V$.

First of all, we note that the expression “such and such an amount of heat is released” cannot be called successful from an average point of view. It has been preserved and continues to be used according to purely historical principles. In modern language it means: “the internal, thermal energy of the body of the system increases to such an extent.”

Now - essentially. In our problem, the change in the internal energy of the “body-liquid-Earth” system (we denote it by $Q$), taken with the opposite sign, is equal to the work of the resistance force and can be calculated as a decrease in the mechanical energy of the system:

$$Q = -A_{\text{conp}} = -\Delta E_{\text{мек}}.$$

Kinetic energy is zero in both the initial and final states, so we only need to find the change in potential energy. An error often encountered here is that, taking into account the change in the potential energy of the spring $(kx^2/2)$ and the potential energy of the ball $(-mgx)$, they forget to take into account the change in the potential energy of the liquid. The meaning of this inclusion in the change in energy density is inherent in 6: simultaneously with the movement of the ball downwards, the volume of liquid moves upward from the place where the ball is at the final moment to the place where it is at the beginning.

So,

$$Q = -\frac{kx^2}{2} + mgx - m_{ж} gx.$$

Finally, the last thing. Just like the school experiment with a balloon that splits when air is pumped out from under the bell of an air pump, our experiment with the boiling of hexane at various temperatures well illustrates the work of the Le Chatel-Brown principle, well known in physics. It lies in the fact that if a system that is in a stable state is influenced by an external factor that seeks to bring the system out of this state, then processes begin to arise in it that compensate for the effect of the external influence. In our case, such a process was, very much so, the boiling of a liquid.
