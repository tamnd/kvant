Here is the strangest of hypotheses for free!
It was unexpected to see night in the midst of a clear day... but who would have thought that light, merging with light, could cause darkness!

D. Arago

The words given in the epigraph were written more than a century and a half ago and refer to Jung’s experience, which studied the scientific world, and he advanced the idea of ​​the interference of light. However, despite the outstanding achievements of interference optics in recent decades and the widespread penetration
its introduction into modern practice, obtaining an extended interference pattern from a thermal (non-laser) source, especially in the form of a colorful pattern of interference fringes in white light, today turns out to be just as difficult as it was at the dawn of the interference era. And if, nevertheless, the experience turns out to be successful, it, along with a meaningful effect, brings joy and satisfaction.

Among such experiments one can rightly include the experiment on interference in the air gap between two signs, first carried out by W. Herschel back in 1809. Let's consider a variant of this experiment, which allows us to observe interesting and instructive interference phenomena in a home laboratory. Scheme of image experiment on figure 1.

A beam of white light from a small incandescent light bulb L falls on a glass sign pressed to the edge of the table, the cross-section of which is a right triangle with an angle of 45°. For the central ray, let the angle of incidence on the face AD ​​be equal to the limiting angle of total internal reflection $i_0 = \arcsin (1/n)$, where $n$ is the glass assumption indicator. Part 1 of the incident beam, for which $i > i_0$, is completely reflected from AD, as from a mirror, and forms a brightly illuminated area $O'D'$ on the screen E. Part 2, for which $i < i_0$, experiences partial reflection and partial assumption on the edge AD,

so that it is also divided, in turn, into two parts. The reflected part $2_0$ forms a weakly illuminated (demi-dark) area $B'O'$ on the screen, adjacent to the area $O'D'$, and the prefield part forms a beam $2_n$, passing through the facets $AD$.

Let us reflect the beam $2_n$ in the direction of the beam $2_o$, using a glass plate as a reflector. This could be photographic plastic washed from the emulsion, or a piece of window glass, or black glass, or a plate of optical glass, or a second prism, or something else. Let's bring the reflector to the sign. Then, in the semi-dark area $B'O'$ of the screen, the beam $2_n$ will overlap the beam $2_o$, and the illumination of the screen will noticeably increase. This can be verified directly by moving the reflector up or down along the reflector, or by using a reflector of a smaller size than the reflector, or by tilting the reflector, forming a gap in the form of an air wedge (Fig. 2).

After making sure that there are overlapping beams, we again press the reflector to the target. However, no interface picture will emerge yet. Maybe the air gap is too thick? Let's try to reduce it. To this end, we clean the mating edge from dirt and dust, for example, by breathing on the edge and then thoroughly wiping the foggy surfaces with a dry, clean cloth. Let's apply the reflector to the mark again and with light circular movements and gentle pressure we will rub it against the edge $AD$. It is here that the interference spectra with a narrow dark boundary strip “flare up” on the screen in the region of beam overlap. How can one obtain such a picture in a home laboratory? It turns out there are two ways to do this. In the first, which we have already discussed in essence, the prism is covered with a triangular plywood cover and pressed against the edge of the desk with a clamp so that the hypotenuse face of the prism protrudes a few millimeters beyond the edge of the table. The light source can be a film projection lamp (for example, a K-12-30 lamp from a children's filter), and the observation screen can be a pillowcase thrown over the back of a chair. The picture is observed in reflected rays according to the scheme shown in figure 5.

In the second method, an unsecured device is simply placed on a desk, and the device layer is examined directly with the eye in diffused daylight in reflected or transmitted rays (according to the diagrams in Figures 6.6 and 6.a). This method is simpler and more accessible, and it also makes it possible to simultaneously observe and compare the picture in transmitted and reflected light. We will discuss this in more detail.

A piece of white paper or Vatan paper BB is placed on the table, two faceted cardans are placed on it, placing them parallel to each other at a distance of 4-5 cm, these “rails” are covered with photoplastic Pl 9x12 cm, washed from the emulsion, a prism P is placed on the plate, after carefully wiping the contacting surface, and the facets of the prism facing the window are covered a CN overlay made of black paper, cut to the size of this edge, with a side-bend 2-3 cm wide.

To observe the picture in reflected rays, you need to replace the white background with black and vice versa: place a piece of black paper on the table and directly on it - a glass plastic with a prism placed on it. A translucent PN overlay, for example made from tissue paper, can be applied to the grana facing the window.

Of particular interest is the simultaneous observation and comparison of both patterns - both in transmitted and reflected rays. For this purpose, the arrangement of the device is used according to figure 6, a, but under the glass plastic on one side (on top of one of the cardans) a sheet of black paper is inserted approximately half the width of the plate, and the prism is positioned so that half of its hypotenuse face is above the black lining. What can you see in the interlayer under the prism? It turns out that there is a lot. We are forced to limit ourselves to describing only one observation.
