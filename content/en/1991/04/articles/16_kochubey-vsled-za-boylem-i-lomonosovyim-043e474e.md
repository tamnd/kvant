Following Boylem and Lomonosov...
I. KOCHUBEY

The man turned the tap, and the air, with a sharp half-hiss, half-whistle, rushed under the bell of the ear pump. Taking a small bottle from under the bell, the man put it on the windowsill next to exactly the same one and threw something into both. After some time, a description of this experiment appeared in a laboratory journal, which began with the words: “I placed a glass vessel, half filled with water, under the bell of the ear pump and pumped out the air several times with repeated strokes of the piston; some air bubbles rose from the water..."

We can now read these words in the famous dissertation of M. V. Lomonosov “On the action of chemical solvents in general,” written by him in 1743. (The appearance of gas bubbles in a liquid during rarefaction was apparently first noticed by R. Boyle (1627-1691).)

Let's turn off this reading for a while and try to experiment with liquid pressure pongengos ourselves.

Take a small test tube (eg 7 ml) with a side outlet and pour hexane ($C_6H_{14}$) into it. Connect the outlet with a tube to the sock of a syringe (volume 100 ml) or some other device with which a vacuum can be obtained in the test tube. Dip a thermometer (for example, mercury) inserted into a rubber stopper into hexane, and without closing the opening of the test tube, immerse it in just boiled water (the heater must be turned off).

Of course, the design of the device may be different, the idea is important.

When the test tube warms up enough, the hexane boils and the thermometer stops rising - and this should occur at a temperature of 68.7 °C - quickly remove the test tube from the water and tightly close it with its stopper. The boiling will stop (the temperature will drop quite quickly). Now pull out the syringe piston with force - the hexane boils vigorously again. You may be able to notice how bubbles form and grow in the volume of liquid.

It is clear why boiling will occur in our experience. The liquid begins to boil when the pressure of its saturated vapor in the bubbles becomes slightly higher than the pressure in the liquid, which consists of external (atmospheric) pressure, hydrostatic pressure and pressure under the curved surface of the liquid, caused by surface tension.
