at the moment of boiling, the saturated vapor pressure of the liquid is equal to the external pressure. This means that boiling can be caused either by increasing the saturated vapor pressure or by decreasing the external pressure - which is what was done in our experiment.

As you know, saturated vapor pressure increases with increasing temperature, and it increases quite rapidly. In the case of hexane, this dependence, found experimentally, can be described analytically as follows:

$$\lg p_H = 6,88 - \frac{1171}{t + 224},$$

where pressure $p_H$ is expressed in mm pt. Art., and the temperature $t$ is in degrees Celsius.

Using this formula, you can estimate how much you managed to lower your blood pressure.
tag. By substituting into the formula the value of the lowest temperature at which you were still able to “make” hexane boil in your experiments, you will find the required saturated vapor pressure, and therefore the pressure inside the test tube.

Note that corrections for changes in atmospheric pressure in a given location compared to atmospheric pressure at sea level should be taken into account before working in research laboratories. (For example, in Kolarad, a region in the western United States, water boils at 90 °C.)

Finally, the last thing. Just like the school experiment with a balloon that splits when air is pumped out from under the bell of an air pump, our experiment with the boiling of hexane at various temperatures well illustrates the work of the Le Chatel-Brown principle, well known in physics. It lies in the fact that if a system that is in a stable state is influenced by an external factor that seeks to bring the system out of this state, then processes begin to arise in it that compensate for the effect of the external influence. In our case, such a process was, very much so, the boiling of a liquid.
