Currently, using scanning tunnel microcopies, detailed images of the surfaces of many crystalline and polymeric materials have been obtained with atomic resolution. The scanning tunnel microcop has an unprecedented magnification of 100 million!

Researchers have already established that piezoelectric manipulators can be mixed with precision corresponding to atomic dimensions. They have even learned to use the tip of a tunnel microcoppa as a tool for working in the nanometer microcosm - with a tip one atom thick, one can precisely hit the selected location of a molecule and cut it into pieces. Microcop feels the repulsion of atoms

And yet, for all its attractiveness, the scanning tunnel microcop has a significant drawback - only electron-conducting objects are suitable for it. Most materials are coated with an insulating layer of oxides. Biological objects that are tempting to study often do not conduct current either. Can a scanning tunnel microcop detect “non-conducting” atoms? It turns out it can. It is enough to place a tiny piece of diamond attached to a thin metal strip between its tip and the surface of the insulating object, as shown in figure 6.
