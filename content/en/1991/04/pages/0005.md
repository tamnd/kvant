Is it possible to penetrate into the depths of the microworld in another way?

How Abbe's ban was overcome
More recently, in the mid-80s, the path of development of microcopying tools changed completely. If earlier proresis was achieved within the framework of the Abbe ban by reducing the wavelength of the radiation building the image in the microcosole, now this ban was simply bypassed. A new generation of micropipes appeared - scanning microprobes, which made it possible to examine surfaces from a very close distance. And if the previous micro-braces were based on the principles of “vision”, then the new generation devices operate on the principle of “sensation”, and they not only provide information about the shape and location of the smallest details of an object, but also characterize their properties. Thus, a magnetic probe microscope “shocks” the inhomogeneity of the magnetization of an object, an electric one - the microdistribution of electric fields, a thermal one - temperature differences, etc. So, all the main physical parameters of the thermal one correspond to the probe microscope. And microprobes, based on electron tunneling and the action of mechanical interaction forces, make it possible to examine even individual atoms.

But let's not get ahead of ourselves and start with the optical probe micro-array.

Touching with light
Let's imagine an opaque finite shell with a tiny hole at the top, such that its diameter is not even half the wavelength of light (Fig. 1). If light is passed through such a shell, it will not travel far, since the light wave will not “crawl” into a hole of such dimensions and will be reflected back. And yet, on the other side of the hole, light can be detected, but only in the immediate vicinity - again at a distance of half a wavelength. It is this kind of “sagging” light that is used in new generation optical microscopes. (The sag property is of a quantum mechanical nature, characteristic of any wave or particle, and is called tunneling.)

Let us place the object under study near the hole, at a distance less than its diameter. A light spot approximately equal to the diameter of the hole will appear on its surface.
