Let us consider in more detail the operation of the processing communication system using the example of a scanning tunnel microcopper; it was the first to appear in the family of probe microcopps. For his invention, G. Binning and G. Rohrer, employees of the computer company IBM in Zorix, were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1986. In it, the probe is an extremely sharp metal game. If we draw an analogy with an optical probe microcouple, then the role of the probe response in a tunnel microcouple is played by the tip of the game, from which the quantum mechanical waves of electrons contained in the metal of the tip “sag” together (Fig. 4). The length of such electronic waves is approximately a thousand times shorter than light, and, accordingly, they can “illuminate” an area in a thousand times less time than an optical probe. When such an electron wave touches the conducting surface under study (and this happens at a distance between the probe and the surface of about 1 nm), the electron from the tip can jump to the surface, in other words, “tunnel”. Tunneling means the appearance of an electric current in the probe-surface circuit, albeit a very weak current - in billionths of an ampere, but controlling it with the help of advanced electronics does not seem to be a problem. It is important that it depends extremely strongly on the distance between the tip and the surface. So, reducing the distance by a couple of angstroms, i.e., with

equal to the size of an atom, increases the tunneling current by a thousand times. The function reflecting this dependence is exponential (exponential function with base $e = 2,718...$).

Let us now return to the processing communication system, which ensures high-quality operation of any probe microcopper. It is a complex and sensitive electronic circuit that detects changes in the tunnel current and changes the voltage $U_z$ applied to the vertical manipulator. A piezoelective manipulator moves the probe so that the tunnel current remains constant. This is only possible if the distance between the probe and the surface remains unchanged. Thus, the processing connection does not provide the probe with any reports from the surface, nor much with it. Due to the extreme sensitivity of the tunnel current to the distance to the surface, the accuracy of the processing connection is very high - 0.1-0.01 A. As a result, it moves along a trajectory that carefully follows the topography of the surface scanned by the probe. And since the stress $U_z$ is proportional to the height of the surface above which the tip is located in a given ment, it serves as a convenient measure of the relief. Information about the surface topography is recorded in the computer memory and, after processing, which consists of filtering, i.e., eliminating noise and spurious signals, is displayed on the display in the form of a “tographic map” of the surface (Fig. 5).
