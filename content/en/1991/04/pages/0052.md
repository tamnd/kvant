For the sake of the picture, let’s remember what forces “do not threaten” the law of conservation of mechanical energy, that is, they do not lead to either the loss or production of this energy. These are forces that are well known to you: gravity, elasticity, Coulomb interaction and other so-called conservative forces, i.e. forces whose work along a closed trajectory is zero. Correspondence, the dispersive forces discussed above are called non-conservative.

It is useful to keep in mind that for any conservative interaction it is possible to determine the corresponding potential energy, but for a non-conservative interaction this is impossible. This means that if the potential energy is known for each interaction, then it is called conservative.

Everything that has been said, of course, is known to any schoolchild from a physics textbook. Therefore, seeing that the conditions of the task mention, for example, the friction force (or friction coefficient), the applicant often opens up to the idea of ​​using energy messages when solving the problem. And he is right in the sense that it would be wrong to try to write down the law of conservation of mechanical energy. But it is possible and necessary in such cases to apply a formula for changing mechanical energy, which connects the change in mechanical energy with the work of the forces leading to this:

$$\Delta E_{\text{мек}} = A_{\text{внешн}} + A_{\text{внутр}}^*.$$ (*)

Here $A_{\text{внешн}}$ is the work of external forces, and $A_{\text{внутр}}^*$ is the work of internal non-conservative forces (most often the work of friction or resistance forces).

Let us also note e. To correctly use this formula, it is useful to rely on the following simple rule: on the right side it is necessary to take into account the work of all forces that are not taken into account in the expression for the potential energy of the system. For example, if for some reason it is more convenient for you to take into account the force of gravity in the work on the right side of the equation (*), then the expression for energy should no longer contain the term $mgh$.

And now - a few specific tasks.

Problem 1. A body of mass $m$ lies on a horizontal plane, connected to a vertical wall by a thin spring with stiffness $k$ (Fig. 1). At the initial moment the spring is not deformed. A constant force $F$ begins to act on the body. Assuming that the friction coefficient between the body and the plane is $m$ and that $F > \mu mg$, find the maximum displacement of the body from the initial position and the maximum speed of the body during movement.

In this case, the change in the mechanical energy of the system - the kinetic energy of the body $mv^2/2$ and the potential energy of the deformed spring $kx^2/2$ - occurs under the action of an external force $F$ and the sliding friction force $F_{\text{тр}} = \mu N = \mu mg$:

$$\left( \frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{kx^2}{2} \right) - 0 = Fx - F_{\text{тр}} x$$

(initial mechanical energy is zero). The maximum displacement of the body corresponds to the moment when the speed of the body goes to zero, so we get

$$x_{\text{max}} = \frac{2}{k} (F - \mu mg).$$
