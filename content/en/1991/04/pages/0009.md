The sharp edge of the grain will repel the electron clouds of surface atoms. Of course, it will be necessary to bring the microscope so close that the electron clouds of its edges and surfaces overlap. The strip of metal acts as a spring, attaching the grain to the surface.

Let us now scan the surface with this “sandwich”. Moving up and down, the grain will track the roughness of the relief. Its movement will be detected by changes in the tunneling current flowing from the tip to the metal strip. And in such a device, called a microcoaster based on atomic forces, STRAIN is equipped with a FEEDBACK SYSTEM, which works in much the same way as in a scanning tunnel microcoaster. By moving the entire “sandwich” probe vertically, this system maintains the deflection of the strip with a large (and therefore repelling atomic force) constant. Microscatter on atomic forces also has ultra-high atomic resolution. As an example, figure 7 shows a map of a layer of polymerized molecules of a complex organic substance obtained with its help. It is computer-colored into similar geographical ones: the color scale at the bottom figure shows the height of the relief.

Broken information
under microbraid

You, of course, know what a bit of information is. When information is recorded at high density on a magnetic computer disk, magnetized and non-magnetized areas of microcon or submicron sizes are formed in the surface layer of the disk. However, no matter what microscope you looked at the disk through, you wouldn’t see anything there. But now, in the family of probe microbeams, a device has appeared that “feels” these magnetic sections of the disk - the oven is a microbeam using magnetic forces.
