The light spot emerging from the probe will cover the entire surface under study. This procedure is called surface scanning. If you mark the path traversed by the probe on the monitor screen with dots of different brightness, an image of the surface will appear on the screen. The resolution characterizing the resulting image corresponds to the diameter of the spot that illuminated the surface, i.e., significantly less than $\lambda/2$. (Recall that the minimum, still observable size of the details of an object is called microconson resolution.) So, Abbe’s prohibition has been overcome! The new device with which this can be done is called the near-field optical scanning microcutter. Paradoxical as it may seem, such an optical microcosm allows one to examine details many times smaller than the light wavelength!

Piezoelectric fingers of microbranch

But how can you scan the surface of an object with such high accuracy? For this purpose, precision space-electric manipulators have been designed, the simplest of which is shown in figure 2. It is made of special ceramics that slightly changes size when the applied electric field changes, for which the manipulator is placed between the plates of a capacitor. Often the plates themselves are applied to the surface of the ceramic manipulator in the form of electrodes made of a thin layer of metal. By changing the direction on the electrodes by 0.1 V, it is possible to remove such a rod by only 0.1 nm, i.e., by the size of the atoma diameter. (The metal on the surface of the manipulator is thin enough so that, when stretched, it does not interfere with this movement.)

A simple design of three manipulator rods connected at one point perpendicular to each other, as shown in figure 3, can move a probe placed at the connection in all spatial directions. In voltage control, $U_x$, $U_y$ and $U_z$, set the probe mixing coordinates $x$, $y$ and $z$. The changing voltage $U_x$, $U_y$ moves the probe along the surface of the object under study, scanning it along parallel lines spaced from each other at a given distance (like a beam on a TV screen).
