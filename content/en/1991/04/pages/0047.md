The words given in the epigraph were written more than a century and a half ago and refer to Jung’s experience, which studied the scientific world, and he advanced the idea of ​​the interference of light. However, despite the outstanding achievements of interference optics in recent decades and the widespread penetration
its introduction into modern practice, obtaining an extended interference pattern from a thermal (non-laser) source, especially in the form of a colorful pattern of interference fringes in white light, today turns out to be just as difficult as it was at the dawn of the interference era. And if, nevertheless, the experience turns out to be successful, it, along with a meaningful effect, brings joy and satisfaction.

Among such experiments one can rightly include the experiment on interference in the air gap between two signs, first carried out by W. Herschel back in 1809. Let's consider a variant of this experiment, which allows us to observe interesting and instructive interference phenomena in a home laboratory. Scheme of image experiment on figure 1.

A beam of white light from a small incandescent light bulb L falls on a glass sign pressed to the edge of the table, the cross-section of which is a right triangle with an angle of 45°. For the central ray, let the angle of incidence on the face AD ​​be equal to the limiting angle of total internal reflection $i_0 = \arcsin (1/n)$, where $n$ is the glass assumption indicator. Part 1 of the incident beam, for which $i > i_0$, is completely reflected from AD, as from a mirror, and forms a brightly illuminated area $O'D'$ on the screen E. Part 2, for which $i < i_0$, experiences partial reflection and partial assumption on the edge AD,
