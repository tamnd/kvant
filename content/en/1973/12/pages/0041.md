SEARCHING FOR A SOLUTION
TASKS
Ya.I.Grugekhov

To find solutions to multi-tasks, the method of alternating movement on both sides is used - from the given tasks to the desired one and back. First, they try to obtain a series of consequences from the data, and then - statements from which the desired result would follow, then they return to the data again, and so on.

Generally speaking, from these tasks you can get many consequences that have nothing to do with its solution. However, most often we subconsciously dwell on precisely those of them that can be associated with what we are looking for, since our attention has just been fixed on it or on the statements from which it follows. The same thing happens when moving from the searched to the data.

It is advisable to carry out alternating movement on both sides until an idea for a solution (one or more) arises. Sometimes it seems that it appears unexpectedly. In fact, it is the result of painstaking work to bring down what you are looking for from the data. This can be clearly seen in the diagram (Fig. 1), in which let the solution pass through statements 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Let's look at an example.

Problem 1. Prove that in a straight sill, the bicsecting angle of the right angle bisects the angle between the median and the height drawn to the same angle.

We study the conditions. We make a drawing (Fig. 2), denoting some angles with numbers. We select the data ($\angle ABC = 90^\circ$, $BO \perp AC$, $AM = MC$, $\angle ABK = \angle KBC$) and the required (prove that $\angle 2 = \angle 3$). We check in advance that the arrangement of elements on figure 2 is the only possible one (the bisector lies between the median and the height); this is easily proven using the midpoint of the arc AC circumscribed about $\Delta ABC$ of the circle.
