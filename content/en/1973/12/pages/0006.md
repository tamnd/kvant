The criticism was caused by Cantor's bold views, which went against the long tradition. But this could not prevent the fruitful application of the new theory to numerous branches of mathematics. Since the beginning of the 20th century, set theory has become the basis of all mathematical disciplines, and now its methods and concepts are gradually penetrating school mathematics. The starting points of this science are no more difficult than those of arithmetic or elementary algebra and geometry. If they are nevertheless not so widely known, this is due to the fact that set theory arose relatively recently - only a few years ago. Its founder was one of the greatest mathematicians - Georg Cantor. We can even accurately name the “birthday” of this science - December 7, 1873. On this day, Cantor managed to prove the first non-trivial statement about infinite sets - the “meorem about the non-ness of the set of real numbers.” Thus, the way was opened to the construction of a new large mathematical discipline - the doctrine of infinite sets. We know how this happened from Kantor’s correspondence with Dedekind. It seems to us that a story about this with excerpts from Kantor’s letters is of undoubted interest to our readers.

In the early 70s, a young professor passed his centenary. At the ancient German University in Halle, Georg Cantor thought about the existence of natural numbers, which we obtain for counting objects in some kind of finite set, and whether it is possible for infinite sets, for example, the set of all points of a plane or the set of all integers, to attribute some “numbers” of an as yet unknown nature. Cantor's interest in such questions arose under the influence of his teacher, Professor Weierstrass. We have already said that the attention of the greatest mathematicians was focused on real numbers. This is explained by the fact that, in the final estimate, it was precisely the insufficiently precise definition of what a real number was that caused the difficulties that arose in the analysis. Gradually, mathematicians of this era moved to increasingly simpler concepts: from real numbers to rational numbers, integers and, finally, to natural numbers.

It can be assumed that natural numbers should not be reduced to simpler concepts. Some mathematicians still hold this opinion. But there is a lot to say about the fact that the concept of a natural number is not the simplest: there are simpler, more visual concepts. A child does not master this concept immediately, but learns it gradually. It is well known from the analysis of language that the common natural numbers appeared quite late.
