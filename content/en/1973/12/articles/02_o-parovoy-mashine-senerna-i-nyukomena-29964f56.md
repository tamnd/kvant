“At the beginning of the 18th century, the English Severney and Newcomen built a machine for pumping water out of a stone flax mine. It is presented in the drawing. At the end b, steam is generated, which passes through the tube e into the cylinder d and raises the piston; when the piston reaches the top, close the tap e, open h and through the tube i spray a stream of cold water into the cylinder from tank n, which extinguishes the vapor; a void is created inside the cylinder - the piston lowers due to atmospheric pressure; the water generated by the jet of steam and spewing out of the reservoir n is released through the tube m. Thus, by opening and closing valves e, h, m, you can make the piston move up and down. The piston has a rod, which is connected by a chain to a rocker arm, which has a fulcrum in the middle. At the other end of the rocker there is also a chain, and to it is attached a pump rod that pumps water out of the stone flax mine.

This description of one of the first steam engines was given in the book “Manual of Physics”, compiled by A. Malinny and K. Bureny. The seventh edition of this book for hypnasiums and real schools was published in 1883.

The principle of operation of heat engines is described in this issue of our magazine in an article by Yu. I. Sokolovsky (see p. 12).

To the 100th anniversary of Georg Cantor's set theory

If you want the tree to bear more fruit than usual, you have nothing to do with its branches, but need to loosen the ground and put new soil under the roots.

Francis Bacon

At the end of the 19th century, for mathematical analysis - the central field of pure and applied mathematics - it became necessary to clarify the meaning of such basic concepts as function, non-feather, convergence. To do this, it was necessary to strictly define what a real number is and, moreover, what a natural number is.

This set of questions was successfully solved, first of all, by the German mathematicians K. Weierstrass (1815-1897), G. Cantor (1845-1918) and R. Dedekind (1831-1916). Their works filled the foundations of modern analysis.

It should be noted that the general situation that had developed by that time was conducive to the development of new, fruitful ideas in science.

At the end of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th century, the scientific process reformed old ideas in literally all areas of knowledge. At the same time, new ideas were born outside of the old teachings.

We know how this happened from Kantor’s correspondence with Dedekind. It seems to us that a story about this with excerpts from Kantor’s letters is of undoubted interest to our readers.

In the early 70s, a young professor passed his centenary. At the ancient German University in Halle, Georg Cantor thought about the existence of natural numbers, which we obtain for counting objects in some kind of finite set, and whether it is possible for infinite sets, for example, the set of all points of a plane or the set of all integers, to attribute some “numbers” of an as yet unknown nature. Cantor's interest in such questions arose under the influence of his teacher, Professor Weierstrass. We have already said that the attention of the greatest mathematicians was focused on real numbers. This is explained by the fact that, in the final estimate, it was precisely the insufficiently precise definition of what a real number was that caused the difficulties that arose in the analysis. Gradually, mathematicians of this era moved to increasingly simpler concepts: from real numbers to rational numbers, integers and, finally, to natural numbers.

It can be assumed that natural numbers should not be reduced to simpler concepts. Some mathematicians still hold this opinion. But there is a lot to say about the fact that the concept of a natural number is not the simplest: there are simpler, more visual concepts. A child does not master this concept immediately, but learns it gradually. It is well known from the analysis of language that the common natural numbers appeared quite late. And yet, not knowing how to count, people could answer the question of which of the two sets had the largest elements. Of course, in this case, many specific elements are comparable: the number of livestock, the size of troops, and others. Now this method of comparing a set is called establishing a one-to-one correspondence. This is what it will consist of.

Let us have two finite sets $M_1$ and $M_2$. The elements of the first set will be denoted by dogs, and the elements of the second set by cats. To compare these sets, they do this: they extract one element from each set and compare them with each other, forming pairs (see figure).

If dogs and cats end at the same time, then we say that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the sets $M_1$ and $M_2$ and that these sets contain the same number of elements (that they are numerically equal or equivalent). It is easy to imagine a different situation: some elements of the set $M_1$ were left without a guide. Then we should assume that the set $M_1$ contains more elements than the set $M_2$. It is obvious that the described procedure for comparing finite sets does not require the concept of “natural number”. On the contrary, the concept of one-to-one correspondence seems to be simpler, and it is possible to define the concept of “natural number” based on it.

Mathematicians and logicians over the past centuries (G. Fregg, B. Russell and others) have proposed doing this on the basis of approximately the following reasoning.

Let us divide all finite sets into classes in such a way that each class contains those and only those sets that are equivalent to each other, regardless of the nature of the elements forming the given set. Then the general property that characterizes all finite sets from any class is defined as the number of elements in the sets of this class. So, for example, 5 is the general property that all finite sets have, equivalent to the set of fingers of my right hand. From the above it is clear how to compare the numbers defined in this way “by size”. Let $m_1$ and $m_2$ be two natural numbers, and $M_1$ and $M_2$ be two sets that characterize these numbers. If $M_1$ and $M_2$ are nonequivalent and there is a one-to-one correspondence between $M_1$ and some part of the set $M_2$, then $m_1 < m_2$. Thus, we defined the concept of “natural number” and the properties of these numbers ($n_1 = n_2; n_1 < n_2; n_1 > n_2$).

But with this method of comparison it is not at all necessary to assume that the sets $M_1$ and $M_2$ are finite. Therefore, Kantor had a problem

the idea that in the same way all infinite sets can be divided into classes.

All sets included in the same class, that is, having the same power, are called equal power.

Then all sets included in one class correspond to a certain number, which is called the cardinal number or cardinality.

From this it is clear that power is a generalization of the concept of a natural number.

The simplest example of an infinite set is the set $N$ of all natural numbers. Pozny Cantor began to compare various infinite sets with the set $N$. It soon became clear that for many infinite sets it is quite easy to establish a one-to-one correspondence with this set. Consider, for example, $N_2$, the set of all positive even numbers. Despite the fact that $N_2$ is part of $N$, a one-to-one correspondence can be established between $N_2$ and $N$: the number $2n$, included in the set $N_2$, is comparable to the number $n$, included in the set $N_1$. It is clear that such a correspondence will be one-to-one. At first, it seemed paradoxical to be able to establish a one-to-one correspondence between a set and its part: after all, for finite sets such a situation is impossible. But debate on this issue arose quite quickly - it was established that for an infinite set $A$ one can always specify a subset $B$ such that a one-to-one correspondence can be established between $A$ and $B$. Dedekind even proposed to consider this the definition of an infinite set.

Even more surprising was the fact that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of all positive rational numbers and the set $N$. Nevertheless, specifying such a correspondence is not so difficult. Try to do it yourself *).

The question naturally arises: are there infinite sets for which one-to-one correspondence with $N$ is impossible?

If there were no such sets, then all infinite sets would be of equal power, and the concept of a cardinal number (for infinite sets) would lose all meaning—it would be the same for all infinite sets.

In the early 1970s, Cantor suspected that an infinite set, not equal to the set $N$ of natural numbers, was the set $R$ of all real numbers (or even the set of all real numbers of any interval - say, the set of all numbers of the interval [0, 1]). But how can this be proven? Indeed, in all the cases considered above, the proof of the state in finding some operators of a well-defined one-to-one mapping. In this case, it is necessary to establish that constructing such a mapping is impossible. Statements of this kind are called “impossibility theorems,” and their proof usually requires squaring the circle and trisecting an angle, or proving the impossibility of solving algebraic equations of degree greater than four using the operations of addition, multiplication, subtraction, division, and taking roots of various powers). "Halle, November 29, 1873
... Let me ask you a question that is of some theoretical interest to me, but to which I cannot find an answer. Maybe you can do this and will be so kind as to write to me about it. We are talking about the following.

Consider the collection of all positive integer objects and denote it by $(n)$. In addition, consider, say, the set of all positive real numbers and denote it by $(x)$. The question is simply whether it is possible to put in correspondence $(n)$ and $(x)$ such that each individual of one population will be answered by one and only one individual of the other.

At first glance, it seems that no - this is impossible, since $(n)$ consists of discrete parts, and $(x)$ forms a continuum. But nothing is gained by such an argument, and no matter how much I am inclined to believe that $(n)$ and $(x)$ do not allow a one-to-one correspondence, I cannot find a reason for this, and that is exactly what I need. Perhaps the reason is quite simple.

Don’t you at first glance incline to the opinion that $(n)$ does not admit a one-to-one correspondence with the set $(p/q)$ of all positive rational numbers and nevertheless it is not at all difficult to show...》(that this is so. - L.K.).

On November 29, 1873, in Dedekind’s diary, where he noted the contents of the letters he received on scientific topics and his thoughts about them, the following entry appears: “... I immediately answered him that I could not solve his first question, but at the same time I expressed and completely proved the theorem that even the set of all algebraic numbers can be compared in the indicated way to the set of $(n)$ natural numbers...”.
