Modern life and modern production are unthinkable without machines. And to set machines in motion, we need engines, whose task is to supply the energy the machines need.

The earth's crust and the water of the oceans have enormous reserves of internal energy. But this energy cannot be directly used to set the machine in motion; it passes to other bodies only by heat transfer.

It is clear, therefore, that we need devices that, receiving energy in the process of heat transfer, would transfer it to other bodies (in particular, machines), performing work. In order for this device to operate indefinitely (and not until some of its internal resources are exhausted), it must operate cyclically, periodically returning to the same state. Such a device is essentially called a heat engine. In short, a heat engine converts heat supplied to it into work. Schematically, this can be represented as in figure 1.

Let us note that the opposite problem—to give back the energy received in the process of mechanical work in the form of heat—is solved quite simply. A kind of “mill” working in a fussy manner (Fig. 2) is suitable: behind the hand $P$ they turn a millstone $A$, which is the third against another, motionless, millstone $B$, so that both of them are heated. First, due to the work performed by external forces, the mill itself warms up - its internal energy increases, and with it the temperature. This stage lasts until the energy transferred by the miller to surrounding bodies in the form of heat is equal to the expended mechanical energy. After this, dynamic equilibrium occurs, the process becomes stationary (stationary process can be considered cyclic). Thus, the mill becomes a “mechanical stove,” that is, the direct opposite of a heat engine.

The processes occurring in the engine are graphical images on the diagram in coordinates $p$, $V$ (Fig. 5). Point $K$ is the initial state of the gas. Isotherm $KL$ - expanded gas at a constant (relatively high) temperature $T_1$ in contact with a “hot” thermostat. Isochore $LM$ - gradual cooling of the gas in contact with a “cold” thermostat to a temperature $T_2$ (gradually, which means the quasi-stationary process in the gas is ensured by the flat thermal conductivity of the cylinder). Isotherm $MN$—gas rolling at a relatively low temperature $T_2$. Isochore $NK$ - heating of gas from temperature $T_2$ to $T_1$ during thermal contact with a hot thermostat.

Work is performed only on sections $KL$ ($A_1$) and $MN$ ($A_2$). The amount of work performed during the entire cycle $A = A_1 - A_2$ is numerically equal to the area limited by the contour of the cycle. Heat is supplied to the gas from the “hot” thermostat during expansion in section $KL$ ($q_1$) and during heating in section $NK$ ($q$). Heat is separated by the gas to a cold thermostat during cooling in section $LM$ (the amount of this heat is $q$ and corresponds to the difference in internal energies of the gas at temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$) and during rolling in section $MN$ ($q_2$). In just one cycle, the hot thermostat will release $Q_1 = q_1 + q$ heat, and the cold thermostat will receive $Q_2 = q_2 + q$ heat.

In thermodynamics, a hot thermostat is called a heated thermostat, and a cold thermostat is called a cooler. As we have seen, for the cyclic operation of a thermal generator, a cooler is just as necessary as a heated one: by removing the uncooled gas, we would reduce to zero the weight coefficient obtained by expanding REN. At the same time, only part of the energy received from heating is converted into useful work; the rest of the energy is transferred to the cooler.

It is extremely desirable that the cooler expend as little energy as possible (since the engine is used to do work, and not at all to heat the cooler). The more heat is taken from heating, the more materials are needed to maintain its temperature (you have to, for example, discard more fuel). Transferring a certain amount of heat to a cold thermostat requires additional care to cool it (after all, if the cooler temperature rises to $T_1$, the thermostat will stop).

The technical performance of a thermal generator is characterized by its “thermal efficiency”, which, as is known, is determined by the ratio

$$\eta = \frac{A}{Q_1} = \frac{Q_1 - Q_2}{Q_1} = 1 - \frac{Q_2}{Q_1}.$$

Indeed, in accordance with the first law of thermodynamics, the work of a digator per cycle $A = Q_1 - Q_2$, since after each cycle the initial state of the gas is restored, and therefore the original value of its internal energy. (Work is done solely by heat.)

To increase the efficiency of the digitizer, it is desirable to reduce the amount of heat that is not converted into work, but only “flows” through the digitizer from heating to the cooler. From this point of view, the isochoric processes $LM$ and $NK$, not related to the performance of work, primarily attract attention. Isochoric cooling ($LM$), the gas is separated following the same heat $q$, which then comes to it during isochoric heating ($NK$).

Divide both isochores with intermediate (blue) isotherms into very small sections (Fig. 6). Then we can assume that during the cooling process in any section - say, in section $\alpha$ - heat is separated in the same quantity as is supplied in the heating section (section $\beta$) between the same isotherms, at the same temperature.

Under these conditions, is it advisable to “dump” the corresponding energy into the cooler, and then re-take the same amount from heating?

Isn't it better to store it in some kind of energy accumulator (or generator) during digitalization, and then use it again?

Such a generator could serve as a special thermostat, divided into many sections by adnabatic partitions (Fig. 7).

Each section has its own temperature (approximately corresponding to one pair of isochore sections $LM$ and $NK$), so that the sequence of all sections as a whole provides a more or less smooth transition from $T_1$ to $T_2$. In particular, at $T_1 = 450^\circ$ K and $T_2 = 300^\circ$ K sections can have, for example, temperatures $440^\circ$, $430^\circ$, ..., $320^\circ$, $310^\circ$ K (the more sections, the more the implementing process is closer to the theoretical one).

Cooling along the $LM$ isochore is carried out by isolating the cylinder from the hot thermostat and sequentially establishing thermal contact with increasingly colder sections of the generator - for example, moving it upward (see Fig. 7; TC - heat-conducting rod).

Similarly, when heating along the $NK$ isochore, hotter and hotter sections of the generator are alternately “connected” to the cylinder. As a result, at the end of the cycle, all sections will again have their original energy reserve, so the generator does not need any energy interaction with the outside world.

Thermal Diggaatel Conversion

The thermal diggaatel with regenerator is a reverse machine. This means that it can also function “reversely” along the same cycle, passing through all the same states, but only in the reverse sequence $KN \rightarrow NM \rightarrow ML \rightarrow LK$. At the same time, all energy transitions (work and heat transfer) change their direction. Waste cooling (KN) is accompanied by the transfer of heat from the gas to the regenerator; manufacturing, expanding (NM), the gas receives heat $q_2$ from the cold thermostat and does work $A_2$; exhausted heating (ML) occurs due to heat transfer from the regenerator (returning to the initial state); manufacturing roll (LK) at a higher temperature $T_1 > T_2$ requires external forces to perform work $A_1 > A_2$ with the simultaneous transfer of heat $Q_1 = A_1$ to the hot thermostat.

Carnot cycle

The regenerator increases efficiency by relieving the engine from external heat transfer when burning and increasing the temperature of the gas. But they can be avoided without such a bulky device. After all, it is not at all necessary to remove heat in order to cool the gas. Instead, force the gas to do work by allowing it to expand adnabatically (that is, without adding or removing heat).

When, during expansion, the gas reaches state $L$ (Fig. 10), the adnabatic dampers are closed and the gas is given the opportunity to expand, performing work due to internal energy (for this, of course, the piston must be gradually unloaded, which can be ensured in advance by an appropriate choice of cam mechanism products). During the process of adnabatic expansion, the temperature, volume, and pressure of the gas change. In the diagram $p$, $V$ this process is depicted by a curve called adnabata (at figure 10 adnabata - $LB$).

When the gas temperature during the expansion process rises to $T_2$ (point $B$), the lower damper opens and thermal contact with the cooler is established. After this, the gas is isothermally skimmed (isotherm $BD$)*.

Now we need to heat the gas to temperature $T_1$; this again is done without heat transfer, adnabatic skatium (adnabata $DK$). This is again followed by isothermal expansion ($KL$), the process is repeated (a second track is provided on the cam of the transmission mechanism for the return stroke of the piston).

The works on the adnabatic sections $LB$ and $DK$ are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. (after all, they are replaced by energy transfer $q$).

It is more difficult to calculate the efficiency of a Carnot engine than an engine with a generator. But there is no need for this, since the following theorem is valid for all reverse heat engines.

Carnot's theorem. At given heating and cooling temperatures, all reverse heat engines have the same efficiency (it is assumed that the engines do not come into thermal contact with any bodies other than heating and cooling).

The proof of Carnot's theorem is based on the second law of thermodynamics.

Second law of thermodynamics
The first law of thermodynamics, which expresses the law of conservation of energy, does not impose restrictions on the direction of processes (after all, during the transition from state A to state B, the energy remains unchanged, then it is also conserved during the reverse transition from B to A). If, for example, you immerse a warm stone in cold water, then both heating the water due to the cooling of the stone and further heating of the stone due to the cooling of the water are equally compatible with the law of conservation of energy - as long as the changes in the energy of the surrounding bodies are in magnitude and opposite in sign.

However, no one has observed that a warm stone becomes hot by itself due to the freezing of the water in which it is thrown! All human experience states that during thermal contact, heat always passes from a hotter body to a colder one, so that the temperatures of both bodies gradually equalize. It is with this property that the certain concept of “temperature” is associated.

But no matter how convincing these facts are, they relate only to heat exchange during simple thermal contact. We already know that a “tell pump” can take energy from a cold body and transfer it to a hot one. But for the “pump” to operate, the external body must do work, so at the end of the cycle, not everything returns to its original state.

The second law of thermodynamics states that even indirectly, an uncompensated transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter one is impossible. “Uncompensated” means not leaving behind any traces (that is, no changes in any bodies other than the heating of a hot body and the cooling of a cold one). It is impossible to improve a heat pump in such a way that it, through heat transfer, takes energy from a cold body and gives it to a hot one, returning all the processes of the bodies (including the pump itself) to the initial state.

It is clear that no experiment (or even a series of experiments) can prove this kind of negative statement (just as it is impossible to experimentally prove the impossibility of perpetual motion). Like many other laws, the second law of thermodynamics is accepted in physics as an axiom, from which numerous and distant consequences are derived. The unchangeable agreement of all consequences with experiments and practice and the successful prediction of new facts on their basis instills reasonable confidence in the truth of the law.

Based on the second law of thermodynamics, it can be shown that it is impossible to operate cyclically, which would receive energy through heat transfer and do work due to it, without leaving any other changes in the surrounding world.

Proof of Carnot's theorem
Let us now apply the second law of thermodynamics to prove Carnot's theorem.
We will prove it by contradiction. Let us assume that there are two reverse motors with different efficiencies. Performing the same work, they receive different amounts of heat from heating and release different amounts of heat to the cooler (Fig. 11).

Using reversibility, we turn the worst of the engines (in ours figure is the right one) into a heat “pump” mechanically driven from another engine (Fig. 12). The resulting complex unit does not produce any external effects other than the transfer of heat from a cold thermostat to a hot one (and it itself periodically returns to its original state). The contradiction with the second law of thermodynamics proves the inadmissibility of the assumption made, and therefore also proves Carnot’s theorem.

For a pair of irreversible engines, such a proof does not work - they may have different efficiencies.

What if one motor is reverse and the other is not? As can be seen from figure 12, the assumption about the worst efficiency of a reverse motor (turned into a pump) leads to absurdity. Consequently, an irreversible heat engine cannot have greater efficiency than a reverse one.

In accordance with Carnot’s theorem, the derived formula for the efficiency of a reverse heat engine with a generator is equal to

$$\eta = \frac{Q_1 - Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{T_1}$$

automatically applies to all reverse heat engines.

Consequently, for all reverse heat engines the condition is satisfied

$$\frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{T_2}{T_1},$$

or

$$\frac{Q_2}{T_2} = \frac{Q_1}{T_1}.$$

The ratio of the amount of heat to the temperature of the body involved in heat transfer is called reduced heat in thermodynamics. As you can see, the reverse engine separates the cooler as much heat as it receives from heating (while it simply separates less heat than it receives).
