SEARCHING FOR A SOLUTION
TASKS
Ya.I.Grugekhov

To find solutions to multi-tasks, the method of alternating movement on both sides is used - from the given tasks to the desired one and back. First, they try to obtain a series of consequences from the data, and then - statements from which the desired result would follow, then they return to the data again, and so on.

Generally speaking, from these tasks you can get many consequences that have nothing to do with its solution. However, most often we subconsciously dwell on precisely those of them that can be associated with what we are looking for, since our attention has just been fixed on it or on the statements from which it follows. The same thing happens when moving from the searched to the data.

It is advisable to carry out alternating movement on both sides until an idea for a solution (one or more) arises. Sometimes it seems that it appears unexpectedly. In fact, it is the result of painstaking work to bring down what you are looking for from the data. This can be clearly seen in the diagram (Fig. 1), in which let the solution pass through statements 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Let's look at an example.

Problem 1. Prove that in a straight sill, the bicsecting angle of the right angle bisects the angle between the median and the height drawn to the same angle.

We study the conditions. We make a drawing (Fig. 2), denoting some angles with numbers. We select the data ($\angle ABC = 90^\circ$, $BO \perp AC$, $AM = MC$, $\angle ABK = \angle KBC$) and the required (prove that $\angle 2 = \angle 3$). We check in advance that the arrangement of elements on figure 2 is the only possible one (the bisector lies between the median and the height); this is easily proven using the midpoint of the arc AC circumscribed about $\Delta ABC$ of the circle.

If you cannot solve the problem by alternating movement on both sides, you can turn to other methods. For example, solve a problem in a particular case. Remember whether a similar problem has been encountered, whether this problem can be reduced to an already known, simpler problem, whether a Japanese solution is obligated if only part of the data is taken into account.

Task 2. Calculate

$$M = \sqrt{\frac{44 \cdot 4}{200} - \frac{88 \cdot 8}{100}}$$

We study conditions a). It’s easy to extract the square root of the difference of two numbers; 6) which of the numbers contains the same digits; b) the minuend of fours subtracted from eights; d) the minuend contains 200 digits, the subtrahend - 100; e) in the minuend there are twice as many digits.

It is not possible to calculate this difference. Let's try to factor it into factors. To do this, let us first consider part of the application

measures, composing it so that only part of the data is executed: (a), (6), (c), (e) (except (d)).

1) $4444 - 88 = 4 \cdot 1111 - 8 \cdot 11 = 4 \cdot (1111 - 2 \cdot 11) = 4 \cdot (1100 + 11 - 11 - 11) = 4 \cdot (11 \cdot 100 - 11) = 4 \cdot 11 \cdot (100 - 1) = 4 \cdot 11 \cdot 99 = 4 \cdot (11)^2 \cdot 9$.

2) $4444 - 88 = (4400 + 44) - (44 + 44) = 44 \cdot 100 - 44 = 44 (100 - 1) = 44 \cdot 99 = 4 \cdot (11)^2 \cdot 9$.

Any of these methods can be applied to solve this problem; choose the second one:

$$M = \sqrt{\frac{44 \cdot 4}{200} - \frac{44 \cdot 4}{100}} = \sqrt{\frac{44 \cdot 4}{100} \cdot (10^100 - 1)} = \sqrt{\frac{44 \cdot 4}{100} \cdot (10^100 - 1)} = \sqrt{\frac{44 \cdot 4}{100} \cdot (10^100 - 1)} = \frac{66 \cdot 6}{100}$$

Problem 6. Prove that $\frac{R}{r} \geq \sqrt{2} + 1$, where $R$ is the radius of the ball described around a regular quadruple paramide, $r$ is the radius of the ball described in this paramid.

Guideline 1. Let $PABCD$ be a regular paramid, $PO$ its height (Fig. 7). Then the center $T$ of the ball described in the paramid lies at its height, and the center $M$ of the ball described near it lies at the height or on its extension.

Instruction 2 a. In order to use these problems when proving the inequality $\frac{R}{r} \geq \sqrt{2} + 1$, we express $R$ and $r$ through the elements of the paramida. This is easier to do if one element is linear, the second is an angle. Let $BC = a$, $\triangle PKO = \alpha$, where $K$ is the middle of $CB$, $\triangle PCO = \beta$. Note that $R$ and $r$ are easier to express in terms of $a$ and $\alpha$ than in $a$ and $\beta$.

Guideline 26. $T$ is the center of the ball described in a regular paramid, and $\triangle PKO$ is the linear angle of the dihedral angle $BC$, therefore $TK$ is the bisector of the angles $OKP$ and $r = OT = \frac{a}{2} \cdot tg \frac{\alpha}{2}$.

Instruction 2 a. To find $R = MP = MC = MB = \dots$, you can check. $ME \perp PC$ and consider similar triangles $PME$ and $POC$. And for this you need to find $PC$, $OP$ and $PE = \frac{1}{2} CP$.

Guideline 26. $OC = \frac{a \sqrt{2}}{2}$, $OP = \frac{a}{2} tg \alpha$. Therefore, $\Delta OPC$ will result in $PC = \frac{a}{2} \sqrt{2 + tg^2 \alpha}$. Then $PE = \frac{a}{4} \sqrt{2 + tg^2 \alpha}$.

From the similarity of triangles $OPC$ and $MPE$ we have: $\frac{PM}{PE} = \frac{PC}{OP}$, $PM = \frac{PE \cdot PC}{PO}$. Substituting the values ​​$PE$, $PC$ and $PO$, we get $R = PM = \frac{(2 + tg^2 \alpha) \cdot a}{4 tg \alpha}$.

Therefore, $\frac{R}{r} = \frac{2 + tg^2 \alpha}{2 \cdot tg \alpha \cdot tg \frac{\alpha}{2}}$.

Instruction 2a. The original inequality contained two parameters: $R$ and $r$. Using these problems, we replaced it with the inequality

$$\frac{2 + \text{tg}^2 \alpha}{2 \text{tg} \alpha \cdot \text{tg} \frac{\alpha}{2}} \geq \sqrt{2} + 1,$$

in which we can go to tg $\frac{\alpha}{2}$, that is, to only one parameter, which means it will be easier to prove.

Note 26. Transforming the ley part of the last inequality, we obtain

$$\frac{1 + \text{tg}^4 \frac{\alpha}{2}}{2 \text{tg}^2 \frac{\alpha}{2} \cdot \left(1 - \text{tg}^2 \frac{\alpha}{2}\right)} \geq \sqrt{2} + 1.$$

We denote $\text{tg}^2 \frac{\alpha}{2} = x$, the inequality to be proved takes the form

$$\frac{1 + x^2}{2x(1-x)} \geq \sqrt{2} + 1.$$

Instruction 2a. To prove the inequality it is enough to show that

$$A = \frac{1 + x^2}{2x(1-x)} - \left(\sqrt{2} + 1\right) \geq 0.$$

Instruction 26.

$$A = \frac{1 + x^2 - 2x\sqrt{2} - 2x^2 + 2\sqrt{2}x^2 + 2x^2}{2(1-x)} = \frac{(3 + 2\sqrt{2})x^2 - 2(1 + \sqrt{2})x + 1}{2x(1-x)}$$

The discriminant of a quadratic trinomial in the numerator is zero (check) and $3 + 2\sqrt{2} > 0$. Therefore, the trinomial is non-negative.

Instruction 2a. If it can be shown that the denominator of the fraction $2x \times (1-x)$ is positive, then the problem will be solved.

Instruction 26. $x = \text{tg}^2 \frac{\alpha}{2}$, but $0 < \alpha < \frac{\pi}{2}$, $0 < \frac{\alpha}{2} < \frac{\pi}{4}$, whence $0 < \text{tg}^2 \frac{\alpha}{2} < 1$, that is, $0 < x < 1$, $1 - x > 0$. This means $A \geq 0$ and inequality $\frac{R}{r} \geq \sqrt{2} + 1$ are proven.
