Experiments by Dike and Bragiinsky
More accurate experiments were carried out only in 1962. The American physicist Dicke conducted new experiments in which the gravitational force acting on the rocker arm of a torsion balance was the force of tension of the weights not by the Earth, but by the Sun. If you don’t touch the rocker and don’t turn it to $180^\circ$, as was done in Eötvos’s experiments, then the direction of twisting the thread of the torsion balance would seem to remain unchanged. Dicke, however, noted that if the principle of equivalence is not valid, then the yoke of the balance should be turned slowly due to the effect of the gravitational force on the samples from the Sun (Fig. 4). The orientation of the rocker relative to the Sun changes over time due to the rotation of the Earth, and the experimenter would have to observe the torsional oscillations of the thread with

period of 24 hours. No such fluctuations were detected. The advantage of Dicke's experiments compared to Eotvos's experiments was that in the new experiments the experimenter did not have to change the position of the rocker himself; this saved him from the danger of making additional mistakes.

The analysis showed that in Dicke's experiments the error was reduced to $10^{-10}$.

Dicke's result was improved by the Soviet physicist V. B. Bragiinsky. Bragiinsky used Dicke's idea, but lowered the possibility of error to $10^{-12}$.

It can hardly be expected that in the near future anyone will undertake new experiments: too much work must be spent on this, and the chances are too small that the principle of equivalence may turn out to be unfair if changed with even greater accuracy.

Galileo's error

Galileo reasoned like this: Let's take two stones, a large one and a small one. Suppose a large stone falls faster than a small one. Let's tie them together and throw these tied stones down from a height. If a larger stone falls faster than a small one, then the smallest one must slow down the movement of the larger one. But then the stones tied together fall faster, slower than one large one, but faster than the smallest one. On the other hand, the mass of the connected stones is greater than the mass of each of them, which means that they should fall faster. This results in a visible contradiction. The situation does not subside and counters the assumption that the larger stone falls more slowly. It would seem that this implies the law of equality of accelerations of all bodies.

Actually this is not true. The reasoning error lies in the naive assumption that the acceleration of bodies depends only on one physical quantity - “gravity” (that is, on the gravitational mass). If there were two masses (gravitational and inertia, which is not equal to it), then the additional “proof” could not be carried out.
