§ 1. Formulation of the problem
A fox runs in a straight line at constant speed. The dog chases the fox at the same speed, and runs in such a way that he sees the fox all the time in front of him. The fox runs from point L₀ (Fig. 1) in the direction indicated by the arrow. The dog is at the initial moment of being at point C₀. At what C₀ will the dog catch up with the fox?

Now it is difficult to establish who came up with this beautiful problem - it has been offered for many years at the Moscow State University to students who have not yet learned what a derivative is. After the theme “Derivative” was included in the mathematics school curriculum, they began to tell schoolchildren about the fox and the dog, warning them in advance that this was a “problem with two stars.”

At the beginning of 1972, we proposed a problem about a fox and a dog in the 10th “b” class of the 57th school in Moscow. The first in the class to solve it (in different ways) were Kolya Nadrashiel, Volodya Lidsky and Serge Glyadkov*). However, the solution (thought up by S. Glyadkov) was simplified so much that it became completely “elementary.”

This solution (which does not require familiarity with the concept of derivative) will be given below. Before reading it, try, of course, to solve the problem yourself. maybe it didn’t work out (I already warned you that the task is difficult) - then try to at least guess the answer. It's quite unexpected.

§ 3. In the moving coordinate system

Let's take a decisive step that will dramatically simplify all further calculations. Let us choose a direct coordinate system $x, y$ so that it moves uniformly along with the fox *) in the direction of the axis $y$. Let the fox be on the moving system at the initial moment (at point $O$). Then she will be at this point all the time.

How will the dog move relative to the system $x, y$?

Let us take some point $C$ (Fig. 5, a) on the plane $(x, y)$.

Let us prove that it is along this parabola that the dog will run (relative to the system $x, y$) if at the initial moment it is at point $C_0$.

To prove this, let us imagine that $OC_0$ and $C_0D_0$ are tight ropes; at point $D_0$ the rope $C_0D_0$ is tied to a peg, which is on the straight line $d$ and can freely slide along it; rope $OC_0$ is firmly tied at point $O$. Let’s also imagine that at point $C_0$ there is not a dog, but, say, a monkey (a dog is not eager enough to do what we are about to teach it). Let's give the monkey ropes and ask him to pull himself up both of them at once - simultaneously to the point $O$ and to the straight line $d$.

Anyone who has ever climbed a rope (hereinafter one at a time, not two at a time) can easily imagine what will happen. By moving its paws, our dog (that is, the monkey) will approach the point $O$ and the straight line $d$ at the same speed. After some time, it will clear at point $C_1$ (Fig. 9); a ring sliding without friction along a straight line $d$ will move to a position $D_1$ such that $C_1D_1 \perp d$; since both ropes will be shortened by the same piece, then the segments $OC_1$ and $C_1D_1$ will be equal.
