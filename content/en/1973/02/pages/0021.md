Rotating Earth

Let's consider a body located in a rotating coordinate system, for example, on Earth. It (from the point of view of an earthly observer) is acted upon by a centrifugal force of inertia $*$), directed perpendicular to the axis of rotation of the Earth. The magnitude of the centrifugal force of inertia is equal to

$$F_{цб} = m \omega^2 r.$$

($m$ — inertial mass, $r$ — distance from the rotation axis, $\omega$ — angular velocity).

In order to understand how the centrifugal force of inertia arises, consider the simple problem of a weight suspended on a thread. An observer outside the Earth will see that the weight is suspended along with the Earth. The weight has an acceleration $\omega^2\rho$ ($\rho$ is the distance to the Earth’s axis); this means that it is acted upon by a force $F_{цс} = m_n \omega^2\rho$ directed towards the axis of rotation ($m_n$ is an inert mass). This force is created by attraction to the center of the Earth $F_T = m_r g$ ($m_r$ - gravitational mass) and tension of the thread $F_r + F_H = F_{цс}$ (Fig. 1, a).

If we now rewrite the balance of power like this:

$$F_r + F_H - F_{цс} = 0,$$

then we can say that the load is in equilibrium (as seen by the observer on Earth) under the influence of three forces. The third force, $-F_{цс}$, is directed to the sides from the rotation axis, and is the centrifugal force of inertia $F_{цб}$ (Fig. 1, ​​b).
