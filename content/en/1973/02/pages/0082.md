1. 1. Note. In this example, we have to convert mixed-periodic and purely periodic infinite decimal fractions into ordinary fractions. Here is one practical example of such treatment.

Let a purely periodic fraction $x = 0$, $(a)$ be given, where the number $a$ contains $k$ digits. Let's rewrite the given number like this: $x = 0$, $a(a)$ and multiply by $10^k$. We get $10^k \cdot x = a$, $(a)$.

If we now subtract $x$ from $10^k x$, we get $x \cdot (10^k - 1) = a$, which opens $x = \frac{a}{10^k - 1}$.

If given a mixed periodic fraction $x = 0$, $b(a)$, where the number $b$ contains $n$ digits, and the number $a$ contains $k$ digits, then multiply $x$ by $10^n$, we get $10^n \cdot x = b$, $(a)$ or $10^n \cdot x = b$, $a(a)$. Now let’s multiply both sides of the last equality by $10^k$: $10^{n+k} \cdot x = \{b\} \{a\}$, $(a)$ (here $\{b\} \{a\}$ means the sequential writing of the digits forming the number $b$ and the number $a$). If we now subtract $10^n \cdot x$ from $10^{n+k} x$, we get $\{b\} \{a\} - b$, which opens $x$.
