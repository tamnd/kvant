Ancient ideas

In the unknown depths of centuries, the time passed when man first saw among the fixed stars the seventh celestial bodies, the position of which changed before his eyes. The Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn surprised and surprised the ancient observed skies. Their movement was preceded by anxiety and joy. The sun brought with it the seasons, the moon alternated days and nights, crimson Mars was a harbinger of war.

The Egyptians also learned that the movements of the Sun and Moon can be predicted. Those who knew how to do this occupied a special position - they were priests who knew the secrets of the gods. The priests announced the beginning of the year, holidays, and the timing of work in the fields. Since then, for many hundreds of years, the observed celestial bodies have been associated with the calendar. Copernicus himself delved into astronomy when Leo X fell in 1514 to correct the calendar. (This reform - the transition to a new style - was carried out later, in 1574.)

The Greeks went much further than Egypt. They thought about how the entire planetary system works, how the planets move. Back in the 3rd century BC, Aristarchus of Samos

Skyy expressed the idea of ​​a helium-centric system - a system of planets with the Sun in the center. In the 2nd century BC, on the island of Rhodes and Alexandria, Hipparchus observed the stars and was the first to learn to predict the position of the Sun and Moon, creating a theory of their movement. Astronomers saw that each planet moves according to its own special rules; therefore they talked about the theory of Mars, the theory of the Moon, etc. Only Kepler was able to unite these disparate theories into a single scheme.

The first consistent diagram of the planetary system was given by Claudius Potolemaus, who worked in Alexandria in the middle of the 2nd century AD. He relied heavily on the works of Hipparchus, which have not survived to this day. The Potolemaic system lasted 15 centuries and survived, almost unchanged, until Copernicus. Until the 16th century, I had no doubt about its truth *).

Potolemaus believed that the Earth is located at the center of the Universe, and there are seven spheres around it, which include seven planets behind them (Fig. 1)

*) Potolean’s work was called “Mathematical Treatise in Books XIII.” In the Arabic translation, in which medieval scholars became acquainted with it, it was called “Almagest” (from the Greek “μεγίζτη” - the greatest). It is still cited under this name.
