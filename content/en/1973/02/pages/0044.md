4. Let us take a point $C_0$ on the plane $(x, y)$, which does not lie on the axis $y$, but is otherwise arbitrary. Let us lay off the segment $C_0D_0 = OC_0$ (Fig. 8) from it in the direction opposite to the $y$ axis and draw a straight line $d$ through the point $D_0$, parallel to the $x$ axis. The distance between the axis $x$ and the straight line $d$ is denoted by $a$. According to item 2, parabola (A) is equidistant from $O$ and $d$ and passes through the point $C_0$.

Let us prove that it is along this parabola that the dog will run (relative to the system $x, y$) if at the initial moment it is at point $C_0$.

To prove this, let us imagine that $OC_0$ and $C_0D_0$ are tight ropes; at point $D_0$ the rope $C_0D_0$ is tied to a peg, which is on the straight line $d$ and can freely slide along it; rope $OC_0$ is firmly tied at point $O$. Let’s also imagine that at point $C_0$ there is not a dog, but, say, a monkey (a dog is not eager enough to do what we are about to teach it). Let's give the monkey ropes and ask him to pull himself up both of them at once - simultaneously to the point $O$ and to the straight line $d$.

Anyone who has ever climbed a rope (hereinafter one at a time, not two at a time) can easily imagine what will happen. By moving its paws, our dog (that is, the monkey) will approach the point $O$ and the straight line $d$ at the same speed. After some time, it will clear at point $C_1$ (Fig. 9); a ring sliding without friction along a straight line $d$ will move to a position $D_1$ such that $C_1D_1 \perp d$; since both ropes will be shortened by the same piece, then the segments $OC_1$ and $C_1D_1$ will be equal.
