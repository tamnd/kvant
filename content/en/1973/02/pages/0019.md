Gravitational mass
D. Borodin

Physicists deal with two masses - inertial and gravitational. The first is included in Newton's laws of motion, the second - in the law of universal gravitation.

According to the law of universal gravitation, the magnitude of the tension force between two material points is equal to

$$F = \gamma \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2},$$

(1)

where $\gamma$ is the gravitational constant $^*$), $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of the point materials, $r$ is the distance between them. The masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ included in this formula are the same quantities that appear in Newton’s second law

$$F = ma,$$

(2)

connecting force and acceleration of a body.

The role of mass in formulas (1) and (2) is completely different. In expression (2) it determines the inertness of the materials of a point, and in expression (1) it characterizes the gravitational field that a material point creates and the force with which this field acts on another/material point. Note that, by virtue of Newton's third law, we do not care which mass ($m_1$ or $m_2$) is considered to be creating the field and which is considered to be in this field: action is equal to reaction. That,

$$\gamma = 6,6732 \cdot 10^{-11} \frac{hm^2}{kc^2}$$

(the parentheses may have been read inaccurately in the last two characters).

that the mass included in the law of gravitation (1) - it is called gravitational (or gravitating) mass - turns out to be the same as the inertial mass included in formula (2), there is a great law of gravity.

It is called the principle of instance of gravitational and inertial masses.

In everyday life, we have become so accustomed to this principle that we do not pay attention to it. Meanwhile, we use it when we determine the mass of a body by weighing it on scales; Carrying out this operation, we, without thinking about physics, can easily assume that the force of tension to the ground is proportional to the inertial mass of this body. We said "proportional". In fact, in practice, both masses—gravitational and inertial—change in the same units and are numerically equal. Therefore, we never think about what kind of mass is physicizing in this or that case. The unit of mass change must be chosen so that there are no coefficients in Newton’s second law; then the numerical coefficient $\gamma$ appears in the law of universal gravitation. It is possible, of course, to introduce a new unit for gravitational mass such that $m' = \sqrt{\gamma m}$; then in formula (1) the coefficient $\gamma$ disappears, but $m'$ appears. In this way, the physical development of inertonic and gravitational masses can be emphasized; however, in practice this would be very inconvenient.
