The length $P$ of the broken line $L$ will tend to the length of the line $K$, the surface area of ​​$T$ will tend to the surface area of ​​$\Pi$, and the center of gravity of the broken line $L$ will approach the center of gravity of the line $K$. Since $L$ is true for the broken line, then, passing to the limit at $m \to 0$, we find that correspondence (1) is also valid for the line $K$. This completes the proof of Gülden's first theorem.

Let us now turn to the proof of Gülden's second theorem. First of all, let's consider a puck, that is, a body obtained by rotating a rectangle around a straight line that does not intersect the rectangle and is parallel to its two sides (Fig. 6). The volume of the washer can be easily calculated as the difference between the volumes of two cylinders. We invite the reader to independently calculate this volume $V$ and make sure that Gülden’s second theorem is valid for the puck,

$$V = S \cdot 2\pi r,$$

(3)

where $S$ is the area of ​​the rotating rectangle, and $r$ is the distance of its center of gravity from the axis of rotation $l$.

Now let $F$ be a plane figure of area $S$, and $l$ be a line lying in its plane that does not intersect the figure $F$. Let's take an arbitrary natural number $n$ and draw $n - 1$ straight lines, intersecting $l$ and dividing the figure $F$ into $n$ “slices”, each of which has the same area $\frac{1}{n} S$ (Fig. 7). Each “lobe” is limited by two segments perpendicular to $l$, and two arcs connecting the ends of these segments. (True, if the figure $F$ is not convex, then there may be more complex “lobes”, as, for example, on Fig. 8; however, the calculations carried out are applied to this case as well.) Let us replace these arcs with segments parallel to $l$, so that the area does not change (that is, so that the “lobe” turns into a rectangle of the same area $\frac{1}{n} S$). Having done this with each “lobe”, we will turn $F$ into a “stepped” figure $G$, not much different from the figure $F$.
