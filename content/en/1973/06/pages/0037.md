From the last equality and correspondence (6) it follows that the logarithm of a given positive number $x$ is the exponent to which the number $e$ must be raised to obtain the number $x$:
$$e^{\ln x} = \exp \ln x = x.$$

7. Exponential functions and logarithms with arbitrary bases

Now we can define $y = a^x$ for any $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$, and any $x$ by putting
$$a^x = \exp (x \ln a) = e^{x \ln a},$$
as well as the logarithm of the number $x > 0$ to base $a$, putting
$$\log_a x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a}.$$

It is easy to verify that these functions have the same basic properties:
$$a^{x_1} + x_2 = a^{x_1} \cdot a^{x_2},$$
$$\log_a x_1 x_2 = \log_a x_1 + \log_a x_2$$
(at $x_1 > 0$, $x_2 > 0$) and
$$a^{\log_a x} = x.$$

We invite the reader to verify for himself that these definitions coincide with the traditional ones. But in the future we will deal with such properties of logarithms and exponents, where it is important that the number $e$ is taken as the base, and not some other one.

8. Rate of change exponent of function and logarithm

Until now we have been talking about the properties of logarithms and exponential functions, which are known from the school course. Now we are talking about properties that are associated with the rate of change of these functions and which are especially important for applications in physics.

Let's start with some definitions. The increment $\Delta f$ of a function $x \rightarrow f(x)$ on the segment $[x_0, x_1]$ is the difference $\Delta f = f(x_1) - f(x_0)$, and the increment of the argument is the difference $\Delta x = x_1 - x_0$. The ratio $\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x_1) - f(x_0)}{x_1 - x_0}$ is naturally called the average rate of change of the function $x \rightarrow f(x)$ on the segment $[x_0, x_1]$ (if $x$ is time, and $f(x)$ is the path traveled by the moving body to the moment $x$, then $\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}$ is the average speed of movement for the period of time from $x_0$ to $x_1$). Now let $x_0$ be fixed and $x_1$ approach $x_0$. If in this case the ratio $\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}$ tends to a certain limit, then this limit value - the “instantaneous rate” of change of the function at the point $x_0$ - is called an arbitrary function $x \rightarrow f(x)$ at the point $x_0$ and is denoted by $f'(x_0)$. The value of an arbitrary characteristic allows the behavior of the function to lick the point $x_0$.
