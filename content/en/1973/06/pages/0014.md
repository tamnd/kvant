Let us denote the saints by $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_n$ (Fig. 9), and their distances from the straight line $l$ by $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$.

When the figure $F$ is expressed around the line $l$, the body $X$ is obtained, the volume of which needs to be found, and when the figure $G$ is expressed around the line $l$, the body $Y$ is obtained, which differs little from the body $X$. Since the figure $G$ is made up of $n$ rectangles, the body $Y$ is made up of $n$ washers. By calculating the volume of each washer using formula (3), it is easy to find the volume $V$ of the body $Y$:

$$V = \frac{1}{n} S \cdot 2\pi r_1 + \frac{1}{n} S \cdot 2\pi r_2 + \dots + \frac{1}{n} S \cdot 2\pi r_n,$$

that is

$$V = S \cdot 2\pi R,$$

(4)

where $R$ is calculated using formula (2). Let us now note that the center of gravity of the area of ​​the figure $G$, that is, the center of gravity of the system of point $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_n$, in each of which the mass $\frac{1}{n} S$ is concentrated, according to the lemma, is separated from the straight line $l$ at a distance $R$. Formula (4) means, therefore, that for the figure $G$ Golden’s second theorem is valid.

In this construction we will now assume that $n \to \infty$. Then the volume of the body $Y$, resulting from the expression of the figure $G$, will tend to the volume of the body $X$, and the center of gravity of the area of ​​the figure $G$ will tend to the center of gravity of the figure $F$. (Note that the areas of the figures $F$ and $G$ are the same.) Since for any $n$ the correspondence (4) for the figure $G$ is valid, then, going to the preposition at $n \to \infty$, we will find that the correspondence (4) is also valid for the figure $F$. These complete the proof of Golden's second theorem.

Golden's second theorem can be considered Golden's “three-dimensional” theorem, since it talks about the volume of a body of rotation. Golden's first theorem can be called "two-dimensional", since it talks about the surface area of ​​rotation. By analogy, we can formulate the “one-dimensional Golden theorem”:

Let several points be given in the plane on one side of the straight line $l$. Then the total length $C$ of the circle receiving this system of points around the line $l$ is equal to the product of the number of points by the length of the circle, the radius of which is considered from the axis of rotation $l$ to the center of softness of this system of points:

$$C = n \cdot 2\pi R,$$

food $n$ is the number of points, and $R$ is the distance from the center of softness to the axis of rotation.

The reader can easily prove this simple theorem himself.

It is curious that all three of Golden's theorems (three-dimensional, two-dimensional and one-dimensional) can be combined in one formulation. It's done like this. We will call a zero-dimensional figure any set consisting of a finite number of points. Further, the zero-dimensional volume of a zero-dimensional figure is the number of points of this figure. The condition line is called one-dimensional figures; the length of the line will be called its one-dimensional volume. Surfaces (partially flat figures) are called two-dimensional figures, and the area of ​​a surface is called its two-dimensional volume. Finally, we will call bodies three-dimensional figures, and the volume of a body will also be called its three-dimensional volume.

By $S$ we denote the $(k - 1)$-dimensional volume of the figure $F$, and by $R$ we denote the distance from its center of softness to the straight line $l$. When rotating the figure $F$ around the line $l$, a k-terminal figure is obtained, the k-terminal volume of which is satisfied by the formula:

$$V = S \cdot 2\pi R.$$
