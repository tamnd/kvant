It turns out that by considering the flow of “average” electrons, one can not only understand the processes occurring in metals, but also derive the correct formulas that describe these processes. Electronic gas
The fact that metals are good conductors of electric current has long led to the idea that there are free electrons in the metal.

There are many free electrons. There are about $10^{22} \div 10^{23}$ of them in one cubic centimeter of metal. Like molecules in a gas, they are in constant motion. That’s what they say: “electron gas” or “gas of conduction electrons.” True, the properties of this gas differ significantly from the properties of ordinary gases. Electron gas in metals obeys the law of quantum mechanics. Of all the necessary (quantum) properties of an electron gas, one thing is important for us: the average xaotic speed of electron motion in a metal weakly depends on temperature and for most metals is approximately equal to $<v> \approx 10^8 \text{ cm/c}^*$.

"Average" electron
If there is current flowing through a metal conductor, it means that a potential difference is applied to it, and there is a simple proportionality between the current $I$ and the potential difference $U$

$$I = \frac{U}{R}.$$

This is Ohm's law of signs.

*) We are talking about root mean square speed: $<v> = \sqrt{\frac{v^2}{c^2}}$; The bar over $v^2$ hereinafter denotes the arithmetic mean.

Resistance $R$ is proportional to the length of the conductor $L$ and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area $S$:

$$R = \frac{\rho L}{S}.$$

The proportionality coefficient $\rho$ (resistivity) depends only on the matrix of the conductor and on the conditions in which this conductor is located (what is its temperature, for example). The quantity $1/\rho$ is usually denoted by the letter $\sigma$. This is specific electrical conductivity. If we introduce the current density $j = \frac{I}{S}$ and the electric field voltage $E = \frac{U}{L}$, then Ohm’s law will be rewritten in differential form *)

$$j = \sigma E.$$

The quantities included in (1) do not depend on the geometric dimensions of the conductor. The letters $j$ and $E$ are in bold to show that the direction of the electric field and the current density are vectors.

$$\bar{v} = en \bar{v}.$$

Current flows through metal; has a directional flow of electrons; $\bar{v}$ is the speed of this flow.
