Lev Andreevich ARTSIMOVICH

L. A. Artsimovich was born on February 25, 1909 in Moscow. In 1928 he graduated from the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of the Belarusian University and in 1930 he entered the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology as a laboratory worker. Soon he began independent scientific research.

His main area of ​​scientific interest, in which he made the most important contributions, was atomic physics. He was the first to study the total internal reflection of RENTen rays. Later, he, together with I.V. Kurchatov, studied the “neutron + proton” reaction, which results in the formation of a heavy hydrogen nucleus. In 1936, L. A. Artsimovich, with ingenious experiments, refuted the proposal of the American physicist Shenkland that the interaction of electrons and positrons under certain conditions violates the law of conservation of energy. In 1936-1940, he investigated various phenomena accompanying the passage of fast electrons through matter. In doing so, he proved that all these phenomena, matters of opinion of some scientists, are correctly described by quantum mechanics.

During the war years, L. A. Artsimovich performed a number of important works in the field of electron optics. In subsequent years, L. A. Artsimovich began solving an important scientific and technical problem - the electromagnetic method of separating isotopes of atomic nuclei. The development of this problem required the creation of a large scientific team, which managed to create reliable industrial installations for isotope separation and ensure the production of pure isotopes of various elements for those in need in physics, technology, biology and medicine.

From 1951 until the last days of his life, L. A. Artsimovich headed experimental research on the physics of hot plasma and controlled thermonuclear reactions in our country. He and his collaborators succeeded for the first time in carrying out a thermonuclear reaction in laboratory conditions. In recent years, at the Tokamak installations, physics achievements have achieved record results both in the temperature of the hydrogen plasma and in the time of its stable existence.

Giving credit to the scientific merits of L. A. Artsimovich, the USSR Academy of Sciences elected him a corresponding member in 1946, and an academician in 1953. Since 1957, L. A. Artsimovich was the permanent academic secretary of the Department of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, and then of the Department of General Physics and Astronomy of the USSR Academy of Sciences.

Lev Andreevich's department spent a lot of time training the scientific shift. He studied at the Leningrad Polytechnic Institute, Leningrad University, and the Moscow Engineering Physics Institute. In his last lives he headed the department of atomic physics at Moscow State University.

L. A. Artsimovich was a man with the necessary wealth of interests that went far beyond the boundaries of his specialty. He had great human responsibility and a keen sense of humor. Many of his humorous statements have long become “physical aphorisms.”

Lev Andreevich did not go as far as creating a technical thermonuclear reactor. But grateful humanity will not forget him who made an invaluable contribution to the creation of future thermonuclear power plants.

In general, solutions to problems of calculating the volumes and surfaces of bodies of rotation are, as a rule, not complicated, but rather cumbersome. At the same time, solutions to such problems using Golden's theorems are written literally in a few lines.

Let us formulate these theorems.

Golden's 1st theorem (on the area of ​​rotation surfaces). The surface of a body formed by rotation of a flat line (closed or not) on an axis lying in the plane of this line and not intersecting it is equal to the product of the length of the rotating line and the length of the circle, the radius of which is the distance from the axis of rotation to the center of the line:

$$S = P \cdot 2\pi R,$$

food $P$ is the length of the turning line (or its perimeter, if it is closed), and $R$ is the distance from the center of the ball to the axis of rotation.

Golden's 2nd theorem (on the volume of a body of rotation). The volume of a body formed by rotation of a flat figure with an axis located in this plane and not intersecting the figure is equal to the area of ​​the figure multiplied by the length of the circumference, the radius of which is the distance from the axis of rotation to the center of gravity of the figure:

$$V = S \cdot 2\pi R,$$

food $S$ is the area of ​​the rotated figure, $R$ is the distance from the center of the softness to the axis of rotation.

We emphasize that in the first theorem we are talking about the center of gravity and masses uniformly distributed along the length of the line, and in the second theorem we are talking about the center of gravity and masses uniformly distributed over the entire area of ​​​​the figure.

Due to the fact that Golden's theorems are not proven in the school course (they are proved by methods of integral calculus) *), it is not possible to directly use them to solve problems in school or exam practice

Golden's second theorem can be considered Golden's “three-dimensional” theorem, since it talks about the volume of a body of rotation. Golden's first theorem can be called "two-dimensional", since it talks about the surface area of ​​rotation. By analogy, we can formulate the “one-dimensional Golden theorem”:

Let several points be given in the plane on one side of the straight line $l$. Then the total length $C$ of the circle receiving this system of points around the line $l$ is equal to the product of the number of points by the length of the circle, the radius of which is considered from the axis of rotation $l$ to the center of softness of this system of points:

$$C = n \cdot 2\pi R,$$

food $n$ is the number of points, and $R$ is the distance from the center of softness to the axis of rotation.

The reader can easily prove this simple theorem himself.

It is curious that all three of Golden's theorems (three-dimensional, two-dimensional and one-dimensional) can be combined in one formulation. It's done like this. We will call a zero-dimensional figure any set consisting of a finite number of points. Further, the zero-dimensional volume of a zero-dimensional figure is the number of points of this figure. The condition line is called one-dimensional figures; the length of the line will be called its one-dimensional volume. Surfaces (partially flat figures) are called two-dimensional figures, and the area of ​​a surface is called its two-dimensional volume. Finally, we will call bodies three-dimensional figures, and the volume of a body will also be called its three-dimensional volume.
