Let's take a square root - through history

$$\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356237309504$$

A set of numbers - this boundless alphabet of the powerful expressive language of mathematics - has been ordering the human imagination for thousands of years. The tradition of interest in very large numbers goes back at least to Archimedes, who, determined to determine how many grains of sand could fit in the Universe, developed a system of classes and orders of arithmetic quantities. He even proposed principles with the help of which one can “invent” names for any number of large numbers.

However, interest in the square root of two apparently arose even earlier. In the collection of Babylonian historical objects, stored at Yale University (New Haven, Connecticut), there is a round clay tablet dating back to 1750 BC. It depicts a square dissected by diagonals and three numbers written in clear cuneiform characters (see photograph). When they were read, it became clear that almost four thousand years ago in Babylon they were able to determine the diagonal of a square along its side, multiplying its length by the square root of two. the numbers on the table exactly represent this value, displayed accurate to the fifth digit: 1, 24, 51, 10. Well, this is a very good approximation to the truth, because $1 + 24/60 + 51/60^2 + 10/60^3 = 1,41421$.

I can’t help but want to repeat: this was calculated in the 18th century BC!

Five centuries BC, the school of Pythagoras made one of the greatest mathematical discoveries. The Pythagoreans tried to prove that any number could be derived by adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing positive integers. And the square root of two is an irrational number and a finite number of such operations cannot be obtained. This was discovered by the followers of Pythagoras. However, they loved all kinds of secrecy and “concealed” their discovery for many years.

His proof first appeared in Euclid's Elements around 300 BC. And then, around 140 AD, Theon of Smyrna managed to develop an interesting algorithm for calculating the square root of two; this algorithm became the forerunner of the entire technique of using continued fractions.

In the 19th century, mathematician J. M. Boorman brought the calculation of the square root of two to the 486th decimal place. His victory, adding “with his bare hands,” is marred by the fact that in the 316th sign Burman made a mistake, and then his calculation is no longer correct.

Recently, interest in such operations has become not only this tradition. “Unordered” quantities, such as the square root of two, can serve to model randomly occurring mass phenomena, for example, the emergence and disappearance of a market

Matematikam needs to know whether sequences such as, for example, 7, 7, 7, 7 or 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 appear anywhere in this value $\sqrt{2}$. The theory cannot yet predict such events, so it comes to adding an empirical answer, by approximating and approximating the truth. Therefore, since the advent of the speed of electronic computers, mathematicians spared no effort and brought the calculation of $\sqrt{2}$ to a sign of so many. A recent achievement was the titanic work of Professor Jacques Dutka (New York), a member of the Department of Mathematical Methods in Engineering at Columbia University. He specially developed a completely new algorithm and calculated the value of the notorious root to one million eighty-second decimal place! This is the longest of all computational quantities in the entire history of mathematics.
