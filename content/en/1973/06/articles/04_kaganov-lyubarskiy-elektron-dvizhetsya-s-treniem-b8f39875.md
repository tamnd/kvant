Introduction
The word “friction” brings to mind the moving parts of machines, sleds sliding over a snowy slope, and metorites heating up as they fly through the atmosphere. In one case, it is impossible to do without trenya, in the other, they do everything possible to get rid of it. The slalom skier and the design engineer have to reckon with everything; Because a passerby complains about the lack of traffic, and physicists at LAB are looking for ways to reduce traffic by at least a fraction of a percent. But, perhaps, no matter who thinks about friction, everyone imagines some kind of macroscopic body (a leg, a wheel, a steamboat) interacting with the environment or with other bodies.

Is it possible to talk about friction during the movement of a microsoptic particle, for example, an electron?

In this article we will look at the movement of electrons in a metal. It is known that electrons perform random, chaotic movement. But if a current flows through a conductor, then, in addition to the otic thermal movement, there is also a directed movement of electrons.

It is very difficult to describe the behavior of each electron individually. Therefore, we will replace the movement of all particles with a stream of identical “average” particles moving in the same direction with the same average speeds. Mobility

The main characteristic of particles in Aristotelian mechanics is their mobility $u$ - the speed of a particle under the influence of a unit force. According to formula (4), the mobility of the “average” electron is $\sigma/e^2n$.

Unlike average speed, mobility does not depend on the magnitude of the applied force.

It is nice not to express mobility through specific electrical conductivity, but, on the contrary, to express specific electrical conductivity through mobility:

$$\sigma = ne^2u. \tag{5}$$

*) See the article by M. I. Kaganov, G. Ya. Lyubarsky “On Aristotle’s mechanics” («Kvant» No. 8, 1972).

The question may arise: why express one characteristic of a metal (electrical conductivity $\sigma$) in terms of two others?

This is done because the quantities $n$ and $u$ determine different, one might say, independent characteristics of the metal. The number of free electrons per unit volume $n$ practically does not change with temperature and depends little on the state of the sample of a given metal (single crystal or polycrystal, very pure or with a small amount of impurities). Mobility $u$ characterizes the strength of the medium’s resistance to the movement of electrons. With temperature changes, it can change according to friction thousands of times; a significant increase in mobility when cleaning metal from impurities. The number of electrons $n$ and mobility $u$ allow independent experimental determination.

One more thing should be emphasized. The introduction of mobility means the definition of penetration in the application of electrical conductivity: formula (5) has the right to exist only if the current is carried by electrons. The entire set of experimental facts confirms this point of view.

Free trial time

The fact that a constant current flows through a conductor under the influence of a constant field shows that electrons, acquiring energy from the electric field, give it to the metal (more precisely, it would be said: the crystal lattice of the metal); In a metal, when a current passes, heat is released (it is called Joule heat). In order not to contradict the previously stated statement about the freedom of electrons in metals (we will check it soon!), let us assume that each electron moves for a time $\tau$ under the influence of an external field, and then, in a short act of collision with something, gives up the energy acquired from the electric field ($\tau$ - the time of free passage of the electron). Since the acceleration of the electron is $eE/m$, then the increase in speed over time $\tau$ is $\frac{e\tau}{m} E$, and the average current is **)

$$j = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m} E.$$ (8)

Comparing this formula with formulas (1) and (5), we find

$$\rho = \frac{m}{ne^2\tau}; \quad u = \frac{\tau}{m}.$$ (9)

These are very important formulas. They associate macroscopic quantities (resistivity, mobility) with parameters characterizing the movement of individual electrons. However, one should not forget that $\tau$ is also an average characteristic.

Instead of the free run time, it is sometimes convenient to use the free run time that is understandable to us:

$$l = <v>\tau,$$ (10)

$l$ is the distance that an electron travels (on average) between two collisions, $<v>$ is the average speed of the chaotic motion of electrons. For an electron gas, as we have already said, $<v>$ does not depend on temperature and the temperature dependences $l$ and $\tau$ coincide.

Now let's check whether electrons can actually be considered free. Above we calculated the electron mobility of copper. It turned out to be equal to $6 \cdot 10^{12} \, c/e$. From this and from formulas (9) and (10) it follows:

$$\tau \approx 2 \cdot 10^{-14} \, c; \quad l \approx 2 \cdot 10^{-6} \, cm,$$ (11)

since $m \approx 10^{-27} \, e$ and $<v> \approx 3 \cdot 10^8 \, cm/c$.

The values ​​of $\tau$ and $l$ completely confirm our idea of ​​​​the freedom of conduction electrons: between two stolnov electrons there is a distance that builds in the size of atoms! When the temperature decreases, the electrical conductivity of metals returns, the mobility of electrons returns, and, consequently, the free charge time returns - the freedom of the electron is more clearly manifested.
