⟦folio 40⟧

This article talks about the work done in the Kiev House of Pioneers by Andrey Varlamov and Dmitry Kazakovtsev when they were in the 9th grade of school No. 145 in Kyiv.

We invite you to carry out the description of the experiments yourself. Those who are engaged in growing crystals can recommend reading the article by M. O. Klia “How to Grow a Crystal” («Kvant» No. 5, 1970).

At figure 1 you see a photograph of a crystal grown in natural conditions. This crystal consists of small crystals of pyrite and calcide. Many crystals have rather bizarre shapes. In nature, crystals grow over millions of years. Is it possible to speed up this process? It turns out that it is possible. The industry has been supplying technically artificial crystals for a long time. It’s all the more interesting to get them yourself. This is exactly the task we have set for ourselves.

We will not present the theory of crystal growth; it can be found in R. Fullam’s article “Crystal Growth” (“Quantum No. 6, 1971”). Let's move straight to a review of methods for growing single crystals.

The simplest, but very important method is growing crystals from rasters. This includes, first of all, growing crystals by gradually lowering the temperature of the solution. This method is based on the property of many crystalline substances to change their solubility with changes in temperature. It is good because it does not require complex equipment and allows you to grow very multi-tailed crystals. However, it is only suitable for good soluble compounds. When growing crystals of poorly soluble substances, a bulky installation is needed to combine a sufficient amount of solution.

Another method is to evaporate the solvent. This creates a slight supersaturation of the solution, due to which crystallization occurs. One of the disadvantages of this method is the appearance of parasitic crystals where the walls of the vessel border the surface of the evaporating solution. But this method is very simple and therefore widely used. By adding new portions of the saturated solution as it evaporates, crystals of slightly soluble compounds can also be grown.

An interesting method is intended for growing crystals of insoluble compounds in the event that there are two good soluble components that give light as a result of the intersulce reaction: Both components are dissolved in separate vessels. Then, during non-primary placement, a liquid of them is injected dropwise into the liquid with the help of a biorette. The supersaturation formed during the reaction is sufficient for the crystallization of the substance we need.

We chose the simplest method - solvent evaporation. The installation is represented by a vessel made of organic glass with a capacity of about 750 ml. Approximately 600 ml of saturated solvent is poured into it. As it evaporated, new portions of the solution were added to the vessel. We lubricated the upper part of the walls with a thin layer of white oil. Therefore, the walls were not wetted by the solution, and parasitic crystals almost did not appear on them.

Initially, from the polycrystalline mass of copper sulfate, we selected seven crystals of more or less regular shape. Each was lowered on a thin (0.15 mm) line into a vessel with a saturated solution of copper sulfate. As they grew, the unsuccessful crystals were removed, formed by parasites and having lost their typical shape for single crystals of copper sulfate. After two weeks, only three of the best crystals remained, and after a month, only one. It was already quite large, so its linear growth slowed down due to the large crystallization surface. Instead of stirring the solution, which is usual in such cases, we decided to swirl the crystal itself. To do this, they hung it on a scaffolding (about 0.7 m long), the end of which was secured to the axis of the micromotor. During 10-12 seconds of operation, the engine of the scaffold twisted so much that after fixing the axis, it ensured the slow rotation of the single crystal for about half an hour. Please, it would be easier to simply mix the solution with a stirrer rotating from a microelectric motor. During the entire experiment, the vessel was covered with cellophane to prevent dust from getting into it.
