The following are excerpts from two original works by Thomas Young:
I - from a lecture he gave on November 24, 1803 at the Royal Society; II - from the 39th lecture of the Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy, published in 1807.
In these works, based on an analysis of the phenomena of interference and diffraction, the validity of the wave theory of light is rigorously and surprisingly logically proven.
Translation and preparation of the publication were carried out by S. R. Filonovich.

I. “... I discovered such a simple and easily demonstrable proof of the general law of interference of two streams of light, which I had already tried to establish earlier, that I decided to submit to the Royal Society a brief report on the fact, which seems to me to be conclusive. The statement that I am now going to make is simple: color stripes appear as a result of the interference of two streams of light. I think that the fact that it is possible to test this assertion by the simple experiments which I am about to relate, and which can easily be repeated on any sunny day with the help of objects at hand, will not be answered even by prejudiced people.

I continued the small answer in the window shutter and covered it with a piece of thick paper, which I pierced with a thin needle. For greater ease of observation, I placed a small glass at the window so that it reflected sunlight horizontally onto the opposite wall. At the same time, the diverging light flux passed over the table, on which there were several small screens made of paper cards. I introduced a strip of card 1/3 inch wide* into the light stream and observed its shadow both on the opposite wall and on other cards placed at different distances from the first. At the same time, colored stripes formed along the edges of the shadow, and the shadow itself was divided into a number of parallel stripes, while its middle remained light. These stripes in the shadow itself are a consequence of the combination of two portions of light coming from different sides of the card strip and diffusing in the shadow area. If a small screen is placed a few inches from the strip so that the edges of its shadow and the strip coincide, all the streaks previously seen in the shadow on the wall will immediately disappear, although the light passing on the other side of the strip will travel the same way. If you try to study the sizes of the stripes under different conditions, you can calculate the difference in the lengths of the paths traversed by the light streams. In this case, we will find that if the path lengths are equal, then the light always remains white. Where either the brightest light, or light of any color, appears again for the first, second or third time, the differences in the lengths of the paths of the two streams form an arithmetic progression, naturally, as accurately as experiments of this kind can be consistent with each other.

Observations of the effects of diffraction and interference can sometimes be useful from a practical point of view, making us more cautious in our conclusions regarding the appearance of the smallest bodies examined under a microscope. The shadow of an opaque fiber placed in a beam of light passing through a small hole is always slightly lighter in the center than at the edge. A similar effect may, to a certain extent, occur in relation to the image on the retina. In this way, an idea of ​​proergism can be created, which is not the case in reality. If a small beam of light actually passes through a substance, it can be destroyed by interference with the diffracted light, and the impression of partial opacity of this substance is created. Thus, the central spot and the light spot surrounded by a dark ring can respectively be obtained as images of translucent or opaque particles and give the impression of a complex structure that is not in reality. II. “The nature of light is not a very important subject from a material point of view, which concerns life and practice, but in many other aspects this subject is very interesting, since it enriches our knowledge of the nature of the senses and of the Universe in general. The study of the production of colors and the richness of their shades in dough is associated with the theory of their most important properties and their principles. Many of these phenomena will help us in forming an idea of ​​nature and the source of light in general.

It is assumed that light either consists of a stream of very small particles of luminiferous substances, the existence of which is denied (these particles move at the speed of light), or is the excitation of wave-like motion in a light and elastic medium filling the Universe, such that corresponds to sound. The opinions of scientists of all centuries were divided when it was necessary to give preference to one of these judgments. In addition, there are circumstances that force those who share the first point of view either to believe, along with Newoton, that the radiation of particles is always accompanied by a wave-like motion of the etheric medium, or to believe that the smallest particles themselves at the moment of radiation receive certain rotational and oscillatory movements, which they retain throughout the entire duration of their supposed movement. These additional messages, which were invented to explain certain specific phenomena, were never considered or accepted in general, although no attempt was made to adapt the theory to explain these phenomena in any other way.

Assuming that light of any given color is composed of vibrations of a given length or of a given particle, we conclude that these wave motions are responsible for the effects we have already studied in the case of water waves and sound pulses. It has been shown that two equal groups of waves, receiving from two centers located nearby, can at certain points cancel each other's influence, and at other points double it; the beating of the two sounds is explained by a similar interface. We will now apply these principles to variable addition and extinction of colors.

In order for the actions of two streams of light to be combined in this way, it is necessary that they are generated by the same source and that they reach the same point by different paths in directions that are not very different from each other *). This difference in path can be achieved by diffraction, reflection, refraction, or by

The simplest case is when a stream of uniform light falls on a screen with two very small holes or slits (Fig. 1), which can be considered centers of divergence, and from which the light diffracts in all directions *). In this case, when the two newly formed streams fall on the surfaces where they overlap, their light is separated by dark stripes, approximately equal in size, but becoming wider as the screen moves away from the hole. The middle between the two streams is always light, and the bright stripes on each side are at such distances that the light coming to them from one hole must travel a longer path than from the other, by an amount that is equal to the length of one, two, three or more waves, while the dark stripes lying between them correspond to a difference in path of 1/2, 3/2, 5/2, ... wavelengths.

From a comparison of the results of various experiments it follows that the wavelength for red light should be 1/36,000 of an inch in air, and for phylote light 1/60,000 of an inch; the average value over the entire spectrum is approximately 1/45000 inch **). Based on these dimensions and taking into account the above-known value of the speed of light, it can be calculated that even for long-wavelength light, 500,000,000,000 such waves enter the eye in one second.
