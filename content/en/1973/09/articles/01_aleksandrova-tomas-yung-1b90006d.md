THOMAS JUNG

and 200th anniversary of his birth

The outstanding English physicist Thomas Young was born in Milverton on June 13, 1773. Jung's childhood was unusual. At the age of two he read fluently, at the age of six he began to study Latin, at the age of eight he became interested in land surveying and learned to determine distances and calculate heights. At first he studied at home, then in private schools. By the age of fifteen, he had learned several modern and ancient languages.

In 1792, nineteen-year-old Jung decided to devote himself to medicine. A year later, he presented to the Royal Society*) his work “Observations on the Process of Vision,” which examined the question of accommodation*) of the eye. At that time there were different opinions on this matter. Some scientists believed that the eye as a whole is capable of deforming (stretching or contracting); others suggested that the lens could change its location; still others linked the ability to accommodate with changes in the curvature of the cornea; fourth - with a change in the curvature of the lens. Through numerous experiments on his own eyes, Jung refuted the first three points of view and showed that the accommodation of the eye is due to the deformation of the lens. Unfortunately, under pressure from the then authorities

*) Royal Society - Academy of Sciences of England.

*) Accommodation of the eye - adaptation of the eye to clearly seeing objects located at different distances.

At first he had to abandon his theory, but in 1800 he returned to it again and substantiated its correctness with new research.

After completing his medical studies (first in London, then in Edinburgh, Heatingen and Cambridge), Jung returned to London, where he opened part of his medical practice, while continuing to engage in scientific research. From 1801 to 1804, Jung was a professor of natural philosophy at the Royal Institution, from 1811 he was a doctor at St. George's Hospital in London, and from 1818 he was secretary of the Bureau of Longitudes, where he was entrusted with the publication of the Nautical Calendar.

“Any person can do what others do” was Jung’s motto. An excellent musician, an expert in literature and painting, he is interested in issues of mechanics, acoustics, optics, astronomy, botany, and zoology. His works were so numerous that some of them had to be published anonymously so as not to damage his reputation as a doctor. Despite this, the patients considered him too learned, and he was not popular with them.

“An Essay on Music and Painting”, “On the Stability of Local Arches”, “On the Calculation of Eclipses”, “Experience and Problems on Sound and Light”, “Theory of Light and Colors”, “Restoration and Translation of Various Greek Inscriptions”, “Experience and Calculations Relating to Physical Optics”, “Lecture on Natural Philosophy” - this is the title of some of his works. Jung’s colleague at the Royal Institute, Hefmy Deviskaal, about him: “Even if he limited himself to one area of ​​knowledge, he would undoubtedly be the first in this area. But he was an outstanding specialist both as a linguist and as a hieroglyph; he knew so much that it’s even difficult to say what he didn’t know.”

Jung is the founder of the theory of color vision. He also made significant contributions to management theories. But in the history of physics he is known primarily as a scientist who provided a reasoned and indisputable proof of the wave properties of light.

Light has been of interest to scientists for a long time, but only at the end of the 17th century were the first hypotheses about the nature of light made. Laws such as the law of rectilinear propagation, reflection and refraction of light have already found practical application in optical instruments (magnifying glass, telescope, microscope), when phenomena were discovered that require careful research and explanation. This is primarily the diffraction of light, which was first observed by the Ilyan scientist Grimaldi (1618-1663), and the formation of various colors of thin films, studied by the English scientist Hooke (1635-1703). Both Grimaldi and Hooke connected light with bulbs, and Hooke even mentioned that the directions of the light bulbs are perpendicular to the directional propagation of light, that is, the light bulbs are transverse (though this will be discussed later).

The wave theory of light was outlined in more detail and detail by the Dutch scientist Huygen (1629-1695) in his famous “Treatise on Light,” where many light phenomena are explained from the wave point of view. “There is no doubt that light consists of the movement of some substance,” Huygens wrote, obviously drawing an analogy with the process of sound propagation. That is, Huygens had to construct the existence of a special substance - ether, which fills all peaceful space and has amazing qualities: very high elasticity and very low density. The light source creates a flask in the ether, which, spreading at great speed, reaches the eye

Huygen's contemporary, the great English scientist Isaac Newton (1642-1727), made a significant contribution to the development of optics. He discovered the phenomenon of light dispersion, created a theory of colors, and showed that white sunlight is complex in its spectral composition. Newton continued his experiments with thin strips, and the experiment, which is now called the experiment on observing Newton's wheels, turned out to be especially effective. He also repeated Grimaldi's experiment on the diffraction of light.

To explain the observed phenomena, Newton created the so-called light source theory. According to this theory, each light source emits tiny particles (corpuscles), their sizes are different (the largest for red light and the smallest for phylote light); When particles enter the eye, they cause a sensation of light.

Some light phenomena, such as reflection and refraction of light, fit equally well into both theories.

It is interesting to note that Newoton himself, although he did not agree with Huygen’s wave theory, did not categorically insist on his theory, since he believed that it was not necessary to have a definite point of view on the nature of light. However, consistent Newton so actively promoted his theory of light that it took a lot of time and effort to return to the wave theory of light at the beginning of the 19th century. The first step towards this was Young's attribution of the phenomenon of interface on the basis of the wave representation and its significance to experience in interface and diffraction of light.

In one of his works, Jung formulates his discovery of the “simple and general law” of the interface of light: “Where two parts of the same light enter the eyes in different ways, either exactly or close in direction, the light becomes strong where the difference in paths is an integer multiple of a certain length, and least strong in the intervals of the states of the interfering parts; and this length is different for light of different colors.”

Unfortunately, the importance of work was not understood by contemporaries; As a result, the interface principle was abandoned for another ten and a half years, until it was rediscovered by the French scientist Fresnel (1788-1827).
