This issue contains articles about the VII All-Union Mathematical Olympidade. They contain all the problems proposed at this Olympidade, and solutions to a number of them. It is hardly appropriate to introduce mathematical (and physical) Olympids to the readers of the magazine here and talk in detail about the system of holding these competitions in our country - most readers are directly familiar with the Olympids or have participated in them. We will only point out that in the USSR, mathematical Olympids in their present form have been held since 1934. The first city Olympid in the country was held in Leningrad, its organizers and leaders were Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences V. N. Delone and Professor V. A. Tartakovsky; The first Moscow City Olympiad took place in 1935 under the leadership of corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences (now academician) P. S. Aleksadrov.
The oldest in the world seems to be the Hungarian Mathematical Olympids - the first all-Hungarian competition of this type took place back in the last century (in 1894). Apparently, it is impossible to connect the stable tradition of the Hungarian mathematical Olympiads with the lecture calculation of physical and mathematical sciences in Hungary in the 20th century, when this relatively small country had dozens of outstanding mathematicians and physicists. Nevertheless, there is probably still some kind of intimate connection here. The stable conditions of Hungarian schoolchildren at international mathematical olympids, as well as the appearance in Hungary in the 20th century of a number of prominent mathematicians with talent, so to speak, of the “Olympid” type (that is, scientists who have proven themselves primarily successful in solving complex and non-standard mathematical problems in terms of conditions and methods for solving them), are largely unknown. Such scientists include, for example, Dierd Pólya*, well known to our schoolchildren for his translations of a number of his books, and, especially, Pal Erdős**, a member of the Academy of Sciences of the Hungarian People's Republic. Of the Soviet mathematicians, the same type includes, for example, Yuri Matiyasevich, whose first successes were associated with school mathematical Olympids (the main accessibility of this scientist is described in «Kvant» No. 7, 1970 in an article by F. L. Varpakhovsky and A. N. Kolmogorov). Pojaa, who subsequently moved to the United States, is the main organizer of the “American Mathematical Olympiad,” which attracts schoolchildren from all over the country. This Olympid is held by Stanford University, where D. Pojaa was a professor for a number of years. In terms of the system of conducting and the types of problems proposed, the Stanford Olympidad was similar to the Hungarian Olympiads, which the schoolboy Pojaa once attended, and to the Soviet Olympids. The events held since 1950 as the “New York Olympids” (and since 1957, which have become “All-American”), organized by the Mathematical Association of America (“MAA - contests”) have a slightly different character. In recent years, hundreds of thousands (!) of schoolchildren from the United States of America and Canada have taken part in these competitions (these competitions, the tasks for which are compiled by the “central headquarters of the Olympiad,” are held simultaneously in many cities in the USA and Canada). Currently, mathematics Olympids are held in ten countries in Europe, Asia, North and Latin America

Great interest in mathematical olympids stimulated the publication of collections of “olympiad” problems in different countries. The fame and popularity of the useful solution is regularly published in the collections of problems in the Generic Olympiads. These collections were translated into a number of languages, for example, their two-volume translation was included in the New Mathematical Library series, which is close in nature to our series “Library of the Mathematical Circle”*, which was published in large quantities in the USA. In the same series, a collection of problems proposed at modern “MAA-contests” was published; Collections of problems from the Stanford Olympids were also published in the USA. I also saw collections of “olympic” problems published in the GDR and Bulgaria. Olympid problems are widely reflected in the open series “Library of the Mathematical Circle” books by D. O. Shklyarsky and others “Selected problems and theorems of elementary mathematics” (the first of these books has already gone through 4 editions), as well as in the latest books of D. O. Shklyarsky and others included in the same series (including in the book prepared for publication “Geometric estimates and problems from combinatorial geometry"), although these books are not collections of "olympics" for

Ore, “Graphs and Their Application” (M., “Mir”, 1965); E. Becken Bach, R. Bellman, “Introduction to Inequalities” (Moscow, “Mir”, 1965); A. N. The well-known and very interesting collection “International Mathematical Olympids” by E. A. Morozova and I. S. Petrakov has gone through three editions in our country; many collections of problems for “local” olympids were published in different cities of the country (but, unfortunately, not in Leningrad, which was the “cradle” of our olympids!) and in individual resource centers (for example, in Ukraine, Armenia).

However, the widest in coverage of materials and thus the most interesting of the books of this kind of quality was compiled by one of the veterans of the Moscow Olympiads, Andrei Leman, “Collection of problems of the Moscow Mathematical Olympidads” (M., “Prosveshchenie”, 1965; the book’s editor is Professor V. G. Boltyansky). The Moscow Olympiads, before the organization of all Russian and then all-Union Olympiads, ultimately became the decisive “first Olympiads of the country”; A lot of interesting experience was accumulated here, which played a very important role in the further development of mathematical competitions for schoolchildren. The book compiled by A. A. Leman reveals the history and nature of the Moscow Olympics; This is followed by “training” (“preparatory”) tasks and a complete list of tasks for all Moscow Olympiads, starting with I (1935) and up to XXVII (1964). Unfortunately, the few problems on this list today seem to be impossible to use in solving “training” problems and a significant part of “olympic” problems. The fact that some of the problems proposed at the Moscow Olympiads were left without solutions cannot be considered a shortcoming of the book, but this should encourage the reader to try to solve the problems independently.
