Physics Olympiad

From April 11 to 16, the final round of the VII All-Union Olympiad for Schoolchildren in Physics was held in Leningrad. The participants in this tour were winners of city, district, regional and national Olympiads. Representatives of all 15 allied resource cities, 7 hero cities (Brest, Volgograd, Kyiv, Leningrad, Moscow, Odessa, Sevastopol), and 8 special boarding schools came to the All-Union Olympiad.

A total of 566 schoolchildren took part in the final form: 126 eighth-graders, 174 ninth-graders and 266 tenth-graders.

The grand opening of the VII All-Union Physics Olympiad took place on April 11 in the House of Officers named after. S. M. Kirov. N.I. Kuzin, head of the Leningrad city department of public education, who opened the meeting, read out a welcoming telephone message from the USSR Minister of Education M.A. Prokofiev. The Chairman of the All-Union Body of the Olympiad, Academician I.K. Kirov, addressed “the best physicists among schoolchildren and the best schoolchildren among physics.” The chairman of the jury of the VII All-Union Olympiad in Physics, Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences K. Ya. Kondratyev, in his speech, exclusively noted the role that physics plays in the development of science and technology in the modern world.

April 12 is the first working day of the final stage of the Olympiad, which, as in the previous days, consisted of two rounds: theoretical and experimental. The theoretical tour took place in the New Day auditorium of the Faculty of Physics of Leningrad State University in Peterhof, near Leningrad. Before starting work, each participant received a sheet of paper with methodological instructions, which were supposed to remind them once again what scientific rules should be followed when performing a theoretical type task. There, in particular, it was said: “Look for the most direct path from the basic laws of physics known to you to a specific result for the physical phenomenon discussed in the problem... Each significant statement must be substantiated... Avoid an excessively “scientific” presentation of simple things - it will not replace physics...”

In the theoretical round, five problems were proposed for each class. The 8th graders were given four hours to solve them, and the 9th and 10th graders were given five hours.

Efficiency is defined as the ratio of useful power to all expended power. The useful power in both cases is the same, since the lamps in schemes a) and b) (see Fig. 3) burn in normal mode. And the power expended is different.

Let $U_0$ be the direction of the network, and $U$ be the direction in which the lamp is designed. On section $AB$ the direction is the same in both cases

$$U_{AB} = U_0 - U,$$

and the current in the second case is greater than in the first, since it is the sum of the lamp current and the current flowing through section BC. Consequently, in the section $AB$ in the second case, greater power is released. In addition, additional power is allocated to the BC section. Thus, the efficiency of circuit a) is greater than that of circuit b).

Consider the movement of a load in a reporting system associated with a wheel. Two forces act on it: the force of gravity $mg$ and the reaction force from the wheel rim $Q$ (Fig. 7). The sum of these forces $F_\mu$ is always directed radially to the center of the wheel and imparts centripetal acceleration to the load

$$a_\mu = \frac{v^2}{R},$$

where $R$ is the radius of the wheel, and $v$ is the linear speed of the load, equal to the speed of the translational movement of the cane.

The following forces (Fig. 8) act on the wheel: gravity $Mg$, pressure force from the load $Q' = -Q$, force $F$ from the cane and forces $F_{\tau p}$ and $N$ from the road. With uniform motion, the sum of these forces is zero.

Let's watch now when the wheel starts to bounce? It will lose contact with the road ($N = 0$) if the vertical component of the force $Q'$ balances the gravity force $Mg$. It is clear that this may not happen at any speed. Indeed, let us return to the load again and observe the change in the reaction force $Q$ as the load moves from the lowest to the highest position (Fig. 9). At the bottom point 1, the force $Q$ is directed vertically upward, then its horizontal component appears, and the vertical component decreases (since in general the force $Q$ decreases). At point 2, the vertical component goes to zero, and later it, changing direction, begins to grow. Let's write down the equation of vertical motion, for example, for point 3 (Fig. 10):

$$Q_B + mg = F_{\mu.B}.$$

From here

$$Q_B = F_{\mu.B} - mg = \frac{mv^2}{R} \cos \alpha - mg.$$

It is clear that when determining the speed $Q_B$ will be maximum at the highest point and equal to

$$Q_B \max = \frac{mv^2}{R} - mg.$$

When evaluating experimental work, taking into account independence in performing the experiment, reducing the need to correctly draw up a report on the work, which should describe all stages of the experiment, justify the methods used, process the experimental results, and evaluate the accuracy of the results obtained.

The maximum score that could be obtained for completing the experimental task was 15 points. Moreover, the jury developed a very detailed scale of main, deductible and penalty points. For example, when evaluating ninth-grader experimenters, the main points were given for the correct selection and calculation of the electrical circuit, the use of forced heating (maximum current through the resistance when the measuring device is turned off), for taking into account heat transfer losses, correct accounting of the heat capacity of the calorimeter, and for assessing the error of the experiment. Incentive points were given for maximum calculation of the heating rate, for explicit consideration of thermal inertia, and for correct calibration of the calorimeter (with great accuracy and minimal time consumption). Penalty (negative) points were awarded for conditions or actual pressure of any type, for using a thermometer as a measure, for errors in graphs, for computational errors.
