At first he had to abandon his theory, but in 1800 he returned to it again and substantiated its correctness with new research.

After completing his medical studies (first in London, then in Edinburgh, Heatingen and Cambridge), Jung returned to London, where he opened part of his medical practice, while continuing to engage in scientific research. From 1801 to 1804, Jung was a professor of natural philosophy at the Royal Institution, from 1811 he was a doctor at St. George's Hospital in London, and from 1818 he was secretary of the Bureau of Longitudes, where he was entrusted with the publication of the Nautical Calendar.

“Any person can do what others do” was Jung’s motto. An excellent musician, an expert in literature and painting, he is interested in issues of mechanics, acoustics, optics, astronomy, botany, and zoology. His works were so numerous that some of them had to be published anonymously so as not to damage his reputation as a doctor. Despite this, the patients considered him too learned, and he was not popular with them.

“An Essay on Music and Painting”, “On the Stability of Local Arches”, “On the Calculation of Eclipses”, “Experience and Problems on Sound and Light”, “Theory of Light and Colors”, “Restoration and Translation of Various Greek Inscriptions”, “Experience and Calculations Relating to Physical Optics”, “Lecture on Natural Philosophy” - this is the title of some of his works. Jung’s colleague at the Royal Institute, Hefmy Deviskaal, about him: “Even if he limited himself to one area of ​​knowledge, he would undoubtedly be the first in this area. But he was an outstanding specialist both as a linguist and as a hieroglyph; he knew so much that it’s even difficult to say what he didn’t know.”

Jung is the founder of the theory of color vision. He also made significant contributions to management theories. But in the history of physics he is known primarily as a scientist who provided a reasoned and indisputable proof of the wave properties of light.

Light has been of interest to scientists for a long time, but only at the end of the 17th century were the first hypotheses about the nature of light made. Laws such as the law of rectilinear propagation, reflection and refraction of light have already found practical application in optical instruments (magnifying glass, telescope, microscope), when phenomena were discovered that require careful research and explanation. This is primarily the diffraction of light, which was first observed by the Ilyan scientist Grimaldi (1618-1663), and the formation of various colors of thin films, studied by the English scientist Hooke (1635-1703). Both Grimaldi and Hooke connected light with bulbs, and Hooke even mentioned that the directions of the light bulbs are perpendicular to the directional propagation of light, that is, the light bulbs are transverse (though this will be discussed later).

The wave theory of light was outlined in more detail and detail by the Dutch scientist Huygen (1629-1695) in his famous “Treatise on Light,” where many light phenomena are explained from the wave point of view. “There is no doubt that light consists of the movement of some substance,” Huygens wrote, obviously drawing an analogy with the process of sound propagation. That is, Huygens had to construct the existence of a special substance - ether, which fills all peaceful space and has amazing qualities: very high elasticity and very low density.
