The formula defines the chord $S$ subtending the arc of the great circle passing through points $A$ and $B$:

$$S = \sqrt{\Delta \lambda^2 \cos \varphi_A \cos \varphi_B + \Delta \varphi^2},$$

where $\Delta \lambda$ and $\Delta \varphi$ are chords subtending arcs corresponding to the differences of debts ($\lambda_B - \lambda_A$) and latitudes ($\varphi_B - \varphi_A$). Knowing the chord $S$, we can calculate the great circle subtended by its arc passing through points $A$ and $B$, that is, the shortest distance between them.

Beruni paid great attention to the design of various astronomical and geodetic instruments.

He developed methods for determining the width of a river or ravine (inaccessible for direct measurement), the depth of a well, and also solved a number of other problems that have not lost their practical significance.

Fig. 4.

For example, to determine the width of the river $AB$ (Fig. 3), Beruni recommends constructing two right triangles $BAC$ and $QAC$. To do this, use the astrophilizer $AC$, the repredcular $AB$, and change the angle $ACB = \beta$ at point $C$, and then construct the angle $ACM = \beta$ at the same point $C$. Now it remains to continue the line $BA$ so as to obtain the point $Q$ lying on the line $CM$. Then the side $AQ$ of the triangle $QAC$ reached for direct measurement will be equal to the exclusive distance $AB$.

Beruni recommends using the astrophil to determine the depth $H$ of the well $ABCD$ (Fig. 4). To do this, it is enough to change the angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ at points $C$ and $M$, lying on the continuation of straight line $DC$, using an astrophilizer. From the triangle $ADM$ and $ADC$ it follows that $MD = AD$ sttg $\beta$, $H = CD = AD$ sttg $\alpha$. Next, $h = MD - DC$, $H = \frac{h}{ctg \beta - ctg \alpha}$ sttg $\alpha$.

This is a very brief overview of the works of Beruni, who gave his life to the service of science. He had an exceptional breadth of scientific interests, and it is not for nothing that historians of science called the first half of the 11th century in the history of world science the era of Beruni.

Far from his homeland, in the city of Ghazna in Afghanistan (south of Kabul), on December 11, 1048, the heart of Abu Raikhan Beruni, a classic of Uzbek culture, the greatest of the scientists of the Middle Ages, stopped beating. What he brought to the world, the Motherland, and the people was not forgotten or faded. His scientific discoveries remain an example for posterity.
