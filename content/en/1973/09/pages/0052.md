⟦folio 50⟧

Matboy
I. N. Bershtein

During the All-Union Olympiad, a traditional mathematical battle* was held between the teams of two physics and mathematics boarding schools - Leningrad and Moscow. This year, 11 people spoke from each boarding school; the jury proposed 11 problems.

After a refresher in-person vigil (the tasks were known to the participants the night before), the teams challenged each other to a battle. It was preceded by a competition for captains (Moscow - Arkady Vaintrob, Leningrad - Viktor Kozyrev), the winner of which won his team the right to choose the first move. The question had to be answered: how many roots does the equation $\log \frac{1}{16} x = \left( \frac{1}{16} \right)^x$ have?

This is not a task, but rather a riddle. The captains did not know its conditions in advance, and it was enough for them to guess the answer in any way. (Those who think this is very simple, let them time it and try it themselves. Keep in mind that if you name the wrong answer, then victory is automatically awarded to your opponent. On the other hand, slowly waiting for the opponent to name the wrong answer is also not the most reasonable tactic).

After this the battle began. First, Moscow called the Leningraders to solve the following problem.

*) Matboy is a game similar partly to KBH, partly to a psychological duel, partly to a mathematical Olympiad. Its rules were published in «Kvant» No. 10, 1972.

In the future, each task will be faced with (M) if called by Muscovites, and (L) if called by Leningraders.

No. 1 (M). n points are connected by n - 1 segments so that any two points are connected by exactly one chain of segments. There are arrows placed on the segments in some way. You can perform the following operation an unlimited number of times: if at some point there are no arrows entering it, and all arrows exit from it, then you can change the direction of all these arrows to the opposite. For example, $\leftrightarrow$ can be passed to $\rightarrow$ $\leftarrow$

Prove that such operations can change the direction of all arrows to any direction. Estimate from above and below the minimum number M of such operations, which depends on sufficiently that for any connection of n points by segments you can change any direction of the arrows to any other.

Both teams solved the first part of the problem, but were unable to give an accurate estimate of the number M.

No. 2 (L). Is there a polynomial in two variables P(x, y) such that

a) for all x, y P(x, y) > 0;

b) for any positive number c there exist x, y such that P(x, y) < c.

One of the most interesting problems in Math. At the beginning of the fight, Moscow was confident in the existence of such a polynomial, while the Leningraders were confident in the opposite.

No. 3 (M). There are 128 weights and analytical scales with an arrow. When weighing, the class is allowed to have an equal number of weights on each pan of the scale (and the arrow will indicate the difference in the weight of the weights on the two pans). The weight of the first weight is known exactly. What is the minimum number of weighings required to determine the total weight of the weights?

When proposing this problem, the jury members did not know its solution (this is allowed by the authorities). As a result of the attack, he was never recognized...
