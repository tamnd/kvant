“Collected Information for the Knowledge of Jewels,” briefly called “Mineralogy,” is Beruni’s last completed work. The book, to a certain extent, pursued practical goals related to the trade in precious colored stones. Most of the treatise is devoted to minerals that were known to Beruni, and to metals. The scientist gives the names of minerals in different languages, describes their color and shades, physical and medicinal properties. Of great interest for the history of physics and mineralogy is the fact that Beruni was the first to accurately determine the specific gravity of some minerals, which is far from his ability to classify minerals. Thus, minerals, still a purely descriptive science, first began to be based on digital data obtained as a result of physical experiments. “Mineralogy” was intended by Beruni not as a strictly scientific work, but as a scientific and literary work. This explains the large number of legends, stories and fictional stories about stones found in the text. Written in bright, expressive language, Mineralogy contains imaginative characteristics of minerals. “On the polished surface of the stone, in most areas, golden stars are visible, like specks of dust in the rays of the Sun,” is how Beruni poetically describes lapis lazuli.

Beruni is of significant interest in the field of geodesy, one of whose tasks is to determine the shapes and sizes of our planet. The study of the shapes and sizes of the Earth, along with its position and movement in space, was of great importance for man’s understanding of the reality around him, for solving the riddle of the universe.

Chaldean scientists in ancient times (VII-VI centuries BC), observing lunar eclipses, expressed thoughts about the sphericity of the Earth. History has also preserved for us the well-known definition of the radius of the globe (method and dimensions), made by the Greek scientist Ertosthenes (about 276-194 BC). According to his calculations, the radius of the globe turned out to be 6311 km.
