8th grade, task 2. Let's assume that it is convenient to find such a number. Let us denote it by $n$, and by $m$ the number of which it is a square. The last digit of $n$ is 5, so the last digit of $m$ is also 5, and can be represented as $m = 10m_1 + 5$. So, $n = (10m_1 + 5)^2 = 100(m_1^2 + m_1) + 25$. Therefore, the second digit from the right of the number $n$ is 2, and the third is the same as the last digit of the number $m_1^2 + m_1$. This digit is determined by the last digit of the number $m_1$: if the last digit $m_1$ is 2 or 7, then the result is 6, in all other cases the result is 0 or 2. Since the third digit of the number $n$ cannot be either a zero (by convention) or a two (it has already been used), then it is equal to six, and the second digit of the number $m$ is 2 or 7. But then $m$ is divisible by 25, and $n$ is divisible by $25^2 = 625$. But then $n = 625 = 1000$. $n_1$ is also divisible by 625. Hence $n_1$ is divisible by 5 and the last digit of the number $n_1$, which is the fourth digit of the number $n$, is zero or five. But both of these contradict the conditions of the problem. Consequently, it is impossible to find a square with the specified properties.
