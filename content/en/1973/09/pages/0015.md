If you try to study the sizes of the stripes under different conditions, you can calculate the difference in the lengths of the paths traversed by the light streams. In this case, we will find that if the path lengths are equal, then the light always remains white. Where either the brightest light, or light of any color, appears again for the first, second or third time, the differences in the lengths of the paths of the two streams form an arithmetic progression, naturally, as accurately as experiments of this kind can be consistent with each other.

Observations of the effects of diffraction and interference can sometimes be useful from a practical point of view, making us more cautious in our conclusions regarding the appearance of the smallest bodies examined under a microscope. The shadow of an opaque fiber placed in a beam of light passing through a small hole is always slightly lighter in the center than at the edge. A similar effect may, to a certain extent, occur in relation to the image on the retina. In this way, an idea of ​​proergism can be created, which is not the case in reality. If a small beam of light actually passes through a substance, it can be destroyed by interference with the diffracted light, and the impression of partial opacity of this substance is created. Thus, the central spot and the light spot surrounded by a dark ring can respectively be obtained as images of translucent or opaque particles and give the impression of a complex structure that is not in reality.
