Equation of gas state.
Work and heat of gas.

I. A. Zaitsev

Problems of changing the state of an ideal gas are solved using the equation of gas state - the Clapeyron - Mendeleev equation, which relates the parameters of the state - pressure, volume and temperature:

$$PV = \frac{m}{\mu} RT,$$

where $m$ is the mass of the gas, $\mu$ is the molecular mass (gram-mol) of this gas, $\frac{m}{\mu}$ is the number of moles, $R$ is the universal gas constant. We will start this article by solving several common problems. Then we will consider how the work done by the gas or on the gas is determined, and the heat of the gas in various processes.

Problem 1. How did the volume of a certain mass of gas change during its transition from state 1 to state 2 (Fig. 1)?

Solution. From the equation of gas state it follows that when constructing the volume of a gas, its pressure is proportional to temperature:

$$P = \frac{1}{V} \frac{m}{\mu} RT.$$

The graph of $P$ versus $T$ is a straight line whose equation is

$$P = aT,$$

where $a = \frac{1}{V} \frac{m}{\mu} R$. The larger $V$, the smaller the proportional coefficient $a$ and the smaller the angle $\alpha$ of the graphic to the $T$ axis. Let's draw two isochores passing through points 1 and 2. Since $\alpha_1 > \alpha_2$, then $V_2 > V_1$, that is, the volume of gas increases.

Problem 2. How did the mass of gas in a cylinder with a value of $V$ change if, when the gas was heated from temperature $T_1$ to temperature $T_2$, the pressure in the cylinder changed from $P_1$ to $P_2$? The molecular mass of the gas is $\mu$.

Fig. 1.

Solution. This problem can be solved using not the equation of gas state, but the laws of Boyle - Mariotte, Gay-Lussac and Charles. However, such a solution is cumbersome and requires a certain amount of ingenuity. Using the equation of gas state, the problem is solved quite simply.

Let us write down the equation of the gas in the cylinder at the beginning and at the end of the process:

$$P_1V = \frac{m_1}{\mu}RT_1,$$

$$P_2V = \frac{m_2}{\mu}RT_2.$$

⟦folio 44⟧

Here $m_1$ is the mass of gas in the cylinder before heating, and $m_2$ is after heating. Let us express $m_1$ and $m_2$ from these equations

$$m_1 = \frac{\mu V}{R} \frac{P_1}{T_1},$$

$$m_2 = \frac{\mu V}{R} \frac{P_2}{T_2}.$$

Then the change in mass $\Delta m = m_1 - m_2 = \frac{\mu V}{R} \left( \frac{P_1}{T_1} - \frac{P_2}{T_2} \right).$

Problem 3. Air is pumped out of a vessel with volume $V$, the pressure in the chamber is $P$. How much pumping should a piston pump be pumped about $v$ for the pressure in the seal vessel to be $k$ times? The air temperature does not change.

Solution. Before the piston makes the first “swing”, we can write for the gas in the vessel

$$PV = \frac{m}{\mu} RT.$$

When the pump piston is in its extreme position, the volume of gas is $V + v$, the pressure is $P_1$, and

$$P_1 (V + v) = \frac{m}{\mu} RT.$$

This means that

$$P_1 = P \frac{V}{V + v}.$$

Similarly, we find that after the second swing the pressure in the vessel will be equal to

$$P_2 = P_1 \frac{V}{V + v} = P \left( \frac{V}{V + v} \right)^2.$$

After $n$ swings the pressure becomes equal

$$P_n = P \left( \frac{V}{V + v} \right)^n.$$

According to the conditions of the problem

$$P_n = \frac{1}{k} P.$$

It follows that

$$\frac{1}{k} = \left( \frac{V}{V + v} \right)^n,$$

that is

$$n = - \frac{\lg k}{\lg V / v} = \frac{\lg k}{\lg \frac{V}{v} + V / V}.$$

If there is a mixture of several gases in a vessel, then according to Dalton’s law, the pressure of this mixture on the walls of the vessel is equal to the sum of the partial pressures. The pressure that each gas individually would create if the remaining gases were removed from the container is called partial pressure. This means that the state of each gas in the mixture is independent of the state of the other gases. However, only volume and temperature are constant (since the gases are in equilibrium). Therefore, for each of the gases it is possible to write the equation of gas state differently. Let's take an example.

Problem 4. Determine the density of a mixture consisting of $m_1 = 50$ oxygen and $m_2 = 20$ hydrogen at a temperature of $t = 20^\circ C$ and a pressure of $P = 0,9$ atmospheres.

Solution.

$$P = P_1 + P_2,$$

where $P_1$ is the partial pressure of oxygen and $P_2$ is the partial pressure of hydrogen.

Let us denote the volume of the mixture $V$, then

$$P_1 V = \frac{m_1}{\mu_1} RT,$$

$$P_2 V = \frac{m_2}{\mu_2} RT,$$

And

$$PV = \left( \frac{m_1}{\mu_1} + \frac{m_2}{\mu_2} \right) RT.$$

The density of the mixture is

$$\rho = \frac{m_1 + m_2}{V} = \frac{m_1 + m_2}{RT} \left( \frac{m_1}{\mu_1} + \frac{m_2}{\mu_2} \right) \approx 0,23 \text{ ке/м}^3.$$

Solution. The diffusion process will stop when hydrogen is evenly distributed throughout the entire volume. The first half of the vessel will contain only hydrogen, the second half will contain hydrogen and oxygen. Moreover, the hydrogen pressure

in the first half is equal to the partial pressure of hydrogen in the mixture in the second half of the vessel. If the hydrogen pressure denotes $P_1$, then

$$P_1 \frac{V}{2} = \frac{m_1}{2} \mu_1 RT = 73,8 \text{ amm}$$

($\mu_1 = 2 \text{ e/mоль} — молекулярная масса водорода$).

In the second half of the vessel the pressure is equal to

$$P = P_1 + P_2,$$

Where

$$P_2 \frac{V}{2} = \frac{m_2}{\mu_2} RT$$

($\mu_2 = 32 \frac{e}{mol} — молекулярная масса кислорода$).

Thus

$$P = \left( 2 \frac{m_2}{\mu_2} + \frac{m_1}{\mu_1} \right) \frac{RT}{V} \approx 123 \text{ amm}.$$

Now let's focus on another important issue - the work done by the gas. If a gas expands at constant pressure, it does work

$$A = P \Delta V,$$

where $\Delta V$ is the change in gas volume. It is not at all difficult to prove this formula. Let the gas be in a cylinder with a piston whose area is $S$. Then the force $F$ acting on the side of the gas is equal to $PS$ and when the piston is mixed over a distance $\Delta l$ the gas does work

$$A = F \Delta l = PS \Delta l.$$

Because

$$S \Delta l = \Delta V,$$

That

$$A = P \Delta V.$$

As the volume of gas decreases, $\Delta V < 0$ and the gas does negative work. This means that external forces acting on the piston do positive work.

What to do if the pressure in the vessel is not constant? For example, does it change with volume as shown in figure 2? The change in gas volume can be divided into small sections, such that during each of them the pressure can be considered constant. In each of these sections, the pressure exerted by the gas is equal to the product of the gas pressure and the change in its volume. This product is equal to the area of ​​the straight line allocated on figure. The work done by the gas during the entire process is equal to the sum of these products. At $\Delta V \rightarrow 0$ this sum tends to the area of ​​the figure limited by the graph $P(V)$ and the axis $V$.

So, to find the work done by a gas during a certain process, you need to draw a graph of its pressure versus volume and find the area of ​​the figure under the graph.

ski to figure 3. How much total work was done by the gas during the emode process?

Solution. Let's draw a graph of the dependence of $P$ on $V$ (Fig. 4). In section 1-2, the pressure changes according to the law $P = \alpha \cdot T^{\frac{1}{2}}$, where $\alpha$ is some constant. Let us express the gas temperature in terms of pressure $T = \frac{P^2}{\alpha^2}$ and substitute it into the Clapeyron-Mendeleev equation:

$$PV = RT.$$

We get

$$V = R \frac{P}{\alpha^2}; \quad P = \frac{\alpha^2}{R} \cdot V,$$

that is, pressure is directly proportional to volume. In this section, the gas does positive work. Process 2-3 is isochoric; during this process, work is zero. During the 3-1 isobaric process, work was done on the gas, that is, the gas did negative work. Thus, the total work done by the gas is equal to the area of ​​the triangle 1 2 3:

$$A = \frac{P_2 - P_1}{2} (V_2 - V_1).$$

Because

$$V_2 = \frac{1}{P_2} RT_2 \text{ и } V_1 = \frac{1}{P_1} RT_1,$$

That

$$A = \frac{P_2 - P_1}{2} \left( \frac{T_2}{P_2} - \frac{T_1}{P_1} \right) R.$$

The heat capacity of an ideal gas (as opposed to liquids and solids) depends on what process occurs with the gas. For example, that specific heat is the amount of heat that must be added to a gas in order to increase its temperature by $1^\circ$. When the mass of a gas is equal to one unit of mass, the heat is called specific, and when the process involves one mole of gas, it is called molar.

If the gas is given an amount of heat $Q$, then

$$Q = cm\Delta T$$

(c is the specific heat and $m$ is the gas mass).

According to the First Law of Thermodynamics, the amount of heat imparted to the gas is equal to the sum of the change in the internal energy of the gas and the work done by the gas:

$$Q = \Delta U + A.$$

Therefore, the heat of a gas is related to the amount of work done by the gas.

For example, if a gas becomes a gas due to a decrease in its internal energy, the specific heat of the gas is zero. The heat capacity of a gas can also be negative if the gas, due to a decrease in its internal energy, not only acts as a gas, but also gives off a certain amount of heat to the external environment, so that $Q$ is negative.

If the process occurs at a constant volume, then the gas does no work. In this case, the amount of heat imparted to the gas is equal to the change in its internal energy. The heat capacity of a gas during an isochoric process is called the heat capacity at constant volume and is denoted $c_V$.

Since in any process in which the initial and final temperature of the gas are the same, the changes in the internal energy of the gas are the same - the internal energy is determined by the temperature of the gas - then in any process the change in the internal energy of the gas is equal to $c_V m \Delta T$. Therefore, the first law of thermodynamics can be written in this form

$$cm \Delta T = c_V m \Delta T + A.$$

This means that in order to find the heat of a gas in each given process, you need to find an expression for the work done by the gas. And we already know how to do this.

Problem 7. Find the specific heat of a gas at constant pressure if its specific heat at constant volume is $c_V$.

Solution. We have shown that

$$c_P m \Delta T = c_V m \Delta T + A.$$

No gas work is equal to

$$A = P (V_2 - V_1).$$

Because

$$PV_1 = \frac{m}{\mu} RT_1$$ and $$PV_2 = \frac{m}{\mu} RT_2$$, then

$$V_1 = \frac{1}{P} \frac{m}{\mu} RT_1$$ and $$V_2 = \frac{1}{P} \frac{m}{\mu} RT_2$$ and

$$A = P \frac{1}{P} \frac{m}{\mu} R (T_2 - T_1) = \frac{m}{\mu} R \Delta T.$$

Fig. 6.

This means that

$$c_P m \Delta T = c_V m \Delta T + \frac{m}{\mu} R \Delta T.$$

From here

$$c_P = c_V + \frac{R}{\mu}.$$
