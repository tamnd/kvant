In the case of a continental climate, when there is little moisture in the atmosphere, the solar radiant energy of the city is quickly converted into the energy of the Earth's radiation. As a result, the earth's surface cools more rapidly during the night and the difference between daytime and hourly temperatures is greater than in a marine climate.

Let us estimate the characteristic time for processing solar radiant energy into Earth radiation energy. Suppose, starting from a certain point in time, the radiant energy of the Sun stopped hitting the surface of the Earth. In this case, the Earth will still emit long-wave radiation, but since the flow of energy on its surface has stopped, the Earth's surface will cool down. We will call the characteristic time for processing solar radiant energy into the energy of the Earth's radiation the time during which the intensity of the Earth's radiation has changed several times.

We wanted to set up a thought experiment, but such an experiment actually exists. Namely, the flow of solar radiation at the considered point on the Earth stops at night for a time t, and during this time the temperature drops by values ​​ΔT - the difference between daytime and hourly temperatures. This corresponds to a change in the flux of radiation from the Earth's surface.

Δj = σT⁴ - σ(T−ΔT)⁴ ≈ 4σT³ΔT.

The characteristic time of processing solar radiant energy in order of magnitude is equal to

τ/Δj ~ τ/T/4ΔT,

where j = σT⁴ is the flux of radiation from the Earth's surface. Assuming that the values ​​of ΔT are on the order of several degrees, τ is on the order of several hours, and taking T ≈ 300° K, we find that the characteristic time for processing solar radiant energy into Earth radiation energy is about 100 hours.

We are first trying to notify this time of processing radiant energy in practical life. For example, when constructing a greenhouse, it is covered with glass or film, which transmit solar radiation but reflect processed long-wave radiation. Therefore, the release of energy from the greenhouse is delayed and the temperature inside the greenhouse is higher than outside. Similarly, it protects fruit trees during sudden frosts. To do this, fires are lit, and the smoke does not transmit long-wave radiation. As a result, the Earth's surface cools much more slowly.
