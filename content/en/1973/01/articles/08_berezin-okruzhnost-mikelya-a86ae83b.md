Now we can solve the following problem: “Given 5 points of a circle. Using a line, draw another circle of this circle.”

Let the given points be $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$, $A_4$ and $A_5$. Having found the point $B$ as the intersection of $A_1A_3$ with $A_2A_4$, we choose an arbitrary point $C$ on $A_2A_5$. Then point $D$ is defined as the intersection of $BC$ with $A_1A_5$, and point $A_6$ is defined as the intersection of $A_4D$ with $A_3C$.

It is clear that in this way it is possible to construct as many corner points of the circle as possible.

Exercises

1. Inscribe a hexagon into a given circle along the middle sides, taken through one side.
2. Five consecutive angles of an inscribed convex hexagon are related as $6:5:8:9:10$. Find the values ​​of the sixth angle.
3. The hexagon $A_1A_2A_3A_4A_5A_6$ is inscribed in a circle, with $A_1A_2 = A_3A_4 = A_5A_6 = a$ and $A_2A_3 = A_4A_5 = A_6A_1 = b$. Prove that the diagonals $A_1A_4$, $A_2A_5$ and $A_3A_6$ enclose an equilateral triangle and find its area.
4. The major diagonals of an inscribed convex hexagon $A_1A_2$, $A_3A_4$ and $A_5A_6$ intersect at one point; $A_1A_2 = 6$ cm, $A_3A_4 = 16$ cm and $A_5A_6 = 18$ cm, and the other three sides are equal to each other. Find the perimeter of the hexagon.
5. A hexagon $A_1A_2A_3A_4A_5A_6$ is inscribed in a circle of radius 25 cm, three diagonals of which pass through the center of the circle. Meaning that $A_1A_2 = 14$ cm, $A_3A_4 = 30$ cm, determine the perimeter of the hexagon.

Miquel circumference

What is this ball of colored lines on the cover? Is it possible to discern any order in their placement?

Yes, you can. The circles described around triangles that do not have a red side are drawn in red. Blue circles are described around triangles that do not have a blue side, and so on. Each of the four circles, regardless of the angles between the line segments (as long as the middle lines are not parallel and no more than two lines intersect at one point), intersect at a point common to these circles (Wallace's point, discovered in 1799). All heels of Wallace's point lie on the Miquel circle (discovered in 1844). Mikel's circle on figure of the first cover page plays a central role. It is highlighted in white.

But that's not all. In 1871, the English mathematician William Clifford proved the following rules: not parallel to any of the five given lines and not passing through the intersection points of already constructed lines. Let's construct six Mikel circles (for each of the five lines) - they will intersect at one point!

Let's return to the four-line configuration. Let a circle be described around each of the four triangles. The German mathematician Jacob Steiner established in the last century that the centers of these circles and the Wallace points lie on the same circle.

It is interesting that evidence of these facts is described by its own swishins and central angles. Try to check all the evidence yourself.
