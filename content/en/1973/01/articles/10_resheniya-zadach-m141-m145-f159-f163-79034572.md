M141. Let us choose an arbitrary point P (Fig. 1) on the height BH of triangle ABC. Let K be the point of intersection of lines AP and BC, L be the point of intersection of lines CP and AB. Let us prove that the segments KH and LH make equal angles with the height BH.

This statement is easy to prove using the coordinate method. It is convenient to take the base AC and the height HB of the triangle as the coordinate axes (H is the origin). Let us denote the coordinates of the points as shown in figure 2 (in this case figure a < 0, c > 0). We will use the following lemma:

The equation of the straight line intersecting the Hx axis at the point (x₀, 0), and the Hy axis at (0, y₀), has the form

$$\frac{x}{x_0} + \frac{y}{y_0} = 1.$$ (1)

Since only one line can be drawn through two points, to prove the lemma it is enough to check that line (1) passes through each of these points.

It follows from the lemma that the coordinates (xₖ, yₖ) of point K—the intersection points of lines AP and BC—are obtained by solving the system of equations

$$\begin{cases}
\frac{x}{c} + \frac{y}{b} = 1, \\
\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{p} = 1.
\end{cases}$$ (2)

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

Similarly, coordinates (xₘ, yₘ) are the solution of the system

$$\begin{cases}
\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1, \\
\frac{x}{c} + \frac{y}{p} = 1.
\end{cases}$$

We are interested in the angular coefficients of the lines HK and HM - the tangents of the angle of inclination of these lines to the Hx axis. Subtracting one equation of system (2) term by term from another, we find:

$$x_K \left( \frac{1}{c} - \frac{1}{a} \right) + y_K \left( \frac{1}{b} - \frac{1}{p} \right) = 0,$$

where

$$tg \rightarrow KHx = \frac{y_K}{x_K} = \frac{(a-c) bp}{ac(b-p)}.$$ (3)

Likewise

$$tg \rightarrow MHx = \frac{y_M}{x_M} = \frac{(c-a) bp}{ac(b-p)}.$$ (4)

We see that these slopes are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. It follows that lines KH and MH make equal angles with line BH (and are symmetrical with respect to this line).

Of course, the same formulas (3) and (4) can be obtained without introducing a coordinate system, but by considering many pairs of similar triangles. This is what most of the institutions that solved this problem did. Many also submitted a solution using the theorem of sines. However, from the application of the analytical solution (using a coordinate system), in addition to the brevity of the notation, there is no need to worry about the location of the point: the solution is also suitable for the case when one of the angles is AP or C of type: it does not work only in the “expressed” case, when one of these angles is a straight line, that is, a or c is equal to 0; in this case, both points K and M lie on the line BH, so the statement of the problem is very indo.

Some readers have submitted geometric solutions using Ceva's theorem. Here is one such solution.

Let $K_1$ and $M_1$ be the points of intersection of $HK$ and $HM$ with the line $l$ parallel to $AC$ and passing through $B$ (Fig. 3). Then to solve the problem it is enough to prove that $BK_1 = BM_1$ (from here the equality $\Delta BK_1H = \Delta BM_1H$ will follow). But from the similarity of $\Delta BKK_1$ and $\Delta CKH$ it follows that

$$BK_1 = \frac{CH \cdot BK}{CK}$$

and similarly

$$BM_1 = \frac{AH \cdot BM}{AM},$$

so the necessary equality follows from the following statement.

Theorem (Chevy). If $P$ is any point in the plane of the triangle, $ABC$, $K$, $H$ and $M$ are the intersection points of the lines $AP$ and $BC$, $BP$ and $AC$, $CP$ and $AB$, respectively (Fig. 4), then

$$AH \cdot BM \cdot CK = CH \cdot AM \cdot BK.$$

Proof of the theorem. We will denote by $S(ABC)$ the area of ​​triangle $ABC$. Then

$$\frac{AH}{CH} \cdot \frac{BM}{AM} \cdot \frac{CK}{BK} =$$

$$= \frac{S(APH)}{S(CPH)} \cdot \frac{S(BPM)}{S(APM)} \cdot \frac{S(CPK)}{S(BPK)} =$$

$$= \frac{S(APH)}{S(BPK)} \cdot \frac{S(BPM)}{S(CPH)} \cdot \frac{S(CPK)}{S(APM)} =$$

$$= \frac{PA \cdot PH}{PB \cdot PK} \cdot \frac{PB \cdot PM}{PC \cdot PH} \cdot \frac{PC \cdot PK}{PA \cdot PM} = 1$$

(I discuss in detail the properties of the areas of triangles used here in the article by P.R. Kantor and J.M. Work “Areas of Polygons” in «Kvant» No. 2, 1972).

$$\frac{c}{T} = \sqrt{2}$$

F162. Industrial Mars is called the moment when Mars, the Sun and the Earth are on the same line. The Great Industrial is the moment when the distance between Earth and Mars is minimal. In blue, that the Earth’s orbit is a circle, and the orbit of Mars is an ellipse, find how many years it takes for the great industrial ones to turn around.

Mars makes a full revolution around the Sun in 687 days.

If great industrial events repeat after $k$ years, then the Earth will make $k$ full revolutions during this time, and Mars will make $n = \frac{365}{687} k$ full revolutions. This number must be an integer. Thus, we need to find the minimum integer $k$ such that the expression $$\frac{365}{687} k$$ is equal to an integer.

Since $$\frac{365}{687} \approx \frac{365}{685} = \frac{73}{137} \approx \frac{72}{135} = \frac{8}{15},$$, the minimal integer $k$ for which $n$ is also an integer is equal to 15. Moreover, $n = 8$.

Thus, great industrial events are repeated for 15 years. Mars makes 8 revolutions during this time.

In fact, great industrial events are known to repeat themselves after 15 or 17 years.

F163. According to one of the first models of the hydrogen atom (Thomson's model), it is a uniformly charged polymetric electric ball, in which there is an electron. In general, the atom is neutral. The maximum energy that must be imparted to an electron to remove it from an atom over a long distance is equal to $W$. The magnitude of the electron charge is $e$.

The value $W$ is equal to the work that must be expended in order to remove an electron from an atom.

It is easy to find the work that must be expended in order to remove an electron from the surface of an atom “to infinity” (infinitely far from it). This work is equal to the potential energy of the electron and the surface of a polymetrically charged ball, that is, the potential electric field on the surface of the ball $\varphi = \frac{e}{R}$ (e is the charge of the ball and $R$ is its radius), multiplied by the value of the electron charge

$$A_1 = \frac{e}{R} \cdot e = \frac{e^2}{R}.$$

In order to find the work that must be expended to move an electron from the center of the atom to its surface, divide the ball into thin spherical elephants of thickness $\Delta r$ so that throughout each of these elephants the force acting on the charge can be considered constant. To do this you need $\Delta r \ll R$. For example, the field created by the charges of each of the words is known to be zero inside this layer, and outside the layer it is the same as it would be if the weight of the charge were concentrated in the center of the ball. This means that the direction of the electric field inside an atom at a distance of $r$ from its center is

$$E = \frac{q}{r^2},$$

and the force acting on the electron in this field is equal to

$$F = \frac{qe}{r^2},$$

where $q$ is the charge that is inside the sphere of radius $r$.

Since the charge of the ball is $e$ and it is distributed uniformly over the ball, then the charge is concentrated in a unit volume of the ball

$$\frac{e}{4\pi R^3}.$$

This means that inside a ball with radius $r$ there will be a charge

$$q = \frac{e}{4\pi R^3} \cdot \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 = e \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^3.$$
