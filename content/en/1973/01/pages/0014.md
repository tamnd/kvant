Therefore, the mechanism of energy dissipation associated with the escape of atoms and molecules beyond the Earth's atmosphere turns out to be ineffective.

The second way to return solar energy into space is radiation. The temperature of the Earth's surface (≈300 °K) is significantly lower than the temperature of the solar surface (6000 °K). The maximum energy of solar radiation corresponds to wavelengths in the visible part of the spectrum, and the wavelengths of Earth's radiation lie in the far infrared region. Let's find out what temperature the Earth would have if it radiated energy with a power of 10^17 m as a completely black body. From the Stefan-Boltzmann law we find that the power of radiation emitted by the Earth in this case is equal to σT⁴4π R². In this case, the temperature of the Earth should be 240 °K.

The difference between the obtained value and the actual temperature of the Earth's surface is not so great, especially if we take into account that we can only very approximately assume that the Earth radiates as a completely black body.

The model used makes it possible to present a general qualitative picture of the processes occurring in the Earth's atmosphere during the conversion of solar radiant energy into Earth radiation energy. It is clear that the energy of the Sun is unevenly distributed over the surface of the Earth. In this regard, energy flows from warmer to colder areas appear in the Earth's atmosphere: winds arise. Wind movement concentrates approximately 2% of the radiant energy absorbed by the Earth.

Most of the radiant energy (approximately 4·10^16 Vm) goes to the evaporation of water. Water vapor is carried away by directed atmospheric currents, so that the amount of water evaporation and precipitation at each point on the Earth does not coincide. This is compensated by flows of water on the surface of the Earth - rivers, which carry considerable power - approximately 3·10^15 m.

Water plays an important role not only in absorbing solar energy, but also in absorbing radiation from the Earth. Water molecules effectively absorb photons emitted by the Earth's surface. As a result, thermal radiation is delayed. This explains the two types of climates - marine and continental.
