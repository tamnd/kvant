Some readers have submitted geometric solutions using Ceva's theorem. Here is one such solution.

Let $K_1$ and $M_1$ be the points of intersection of $HK$ and $HM$ with the line $l$ parallel to $AC$ and passing through $B$ (Fig. 3). Then to solve the problem it is enough to prove that $BK_1 = BM_1$ (from here the equality $\Delta BK_1H = \Delta BM_1H$ will follow). But from the similarity of $\Delta BKK_1$ and $\Delta CKH$ it follows that

$$BK_1 = \frac{CH \cdot BK}{CK}$$

and similarly

$$BM_1 = \frac{AH \cdot BM}{AM},$$

so the necessary equality follows from the following statement.

Theorem (Chevy). If $P$ is any point in the plane of the triangle, $ABC$, $K$, $H$ and $M$ are the intersection points of the lines $AP$ and $BC$, $BP$ and $AC$, $CP$ and $AB$, respectively (Fig. 4), then

$$AH \cdot BM \cdot CK = CH \cdot AM \cdot BK.$$

Proof of the theorem. We will denote by $S(ABC)$ the area of ​​triangle $ABC$. Then

$$\frac{AH}{CH} \cdot \frac{BM}{AM} \cdot \frac{CK}{BK} =$$

$$= \frac{S(APH)}{S(CPH)} \cdot \frac{S(BPM)}{S(APM)} \cdot \frac{S(CPK)}{S(BPK)} =$$

$$= \frac{S(APH)}{S(BPK)} \cdot \frac{S(BPM)}{S(CPH)} \cdot \frac{S(CPK)}{S(APM)} =$$

$$= \frac{PA \cdot PH}{PB \cdot PK} \cdot \frac{PB \cdot PM}{PC \cdot PH} \cdot \frac{PC \cdot PK}{PA \cdot PM} = 1$$

(I discuss in detail the properties of the areas of triangles used here in the article by P.R. Kantor and J.M. Work “Areas of Polygons” in «Kvant» No. 2, 1972).

M142. a) Prove that it is impossible to entangle the edges of a cube of numbers 1, 2, ..., 12马克 so that for each vertex the sum of the numbers of the three edges coming from it is the same. b) Is it possible to cross out one of the numbers 1, 2, ..., 12, 13 and use the remaining numbers to confuse the edges of the cube so that the same condition is satisfied?

a) Suppose that it is possible to arrange the numbers 1, 2, ..., 12 on the edges of the cube so that the sum of the three numbers at each vertex is equal to s. Let's add up all eight such sums corresponding to different vertices of the cube. In the resulting sum of 24 numbers, each of the numbers 1, 2, ..., 12 will appear twice, because each edge of the cube is adjacent to two vertices. Thus,

$$2(1 + 2 + \dots + 12) = 8s$$

whence $s = \frac{12 \cdot 13}{8} = \frac{39}{2}$; s turns out not whole! The contradiction that has arisen shows that it is impossible to designate and entangle the edges of the numbers 1, 2, ..., 12.

b) Answer: Yes.

From the solution to point a) it is clear that it makes sense to cross out only such a number c for which

$$s = \frac{1 + 2 + \dots + 13 - c}{4} = \frac{91 - c}{4}$$

is an integer, that is, c = 3, c = 7 or c = 11. In this case, s = 22, s = 21 or s = 20.

Many readers, having convinced themselves that with these c numbers s turns out to be an integer, conclude from this that the necessary arrangement exists. But this verification, of course, is not enough.

An example must be provided to justify your answer. Most readers sent an example of the required arrangement for c = 7 (Fig. 5). In this example, each pair of symmetrical relations of 7 numbers (k, 14 – k) is located on edges symmetrical to the relations of the center of the cube.
