However, a class of superconducting substances has been discovered that can create powerful magnetic fields and withstand strong currents. But the description of their properties is the subject of a separate story.

Speaking about the electromagnetic properties of superconductors, one more interesting feature should be noted, due to the presence of pairs of bound electrons.

The basic law describing direct current in a normal metal is Ohm's law, which can be written in the so-called local form: $j = E/\rho$ ($\rho$ is the resistivity of the conductor, $E$ is the electric field voltage, $j$ is the current density). According to Ohm's law, the magnitude of the current in a certain place in the metal is determined by the value of the electric field in the same place.

In superconductors, everything looks completely different. A constant superconducting current is created, as we have already noted, not by an electric field, but by a magnetic field. But this is not the only difference. Because of the interelectronic coupling, it turns out that if an external field acts on any electron in a superconductor, then this action will be “felt” by another electron associated with it in a pair.

Let's place the superconductor in an external magnetic field. The electrons located in the surface layer (the field does not penetrate into the thickness of the sample) begin to move, and a superconducting current is created. In this case, the electron moves both due to the direct action of an external magnetic field on it, and under the influence of other electrons associated with it, also being under the influence of the field. Therefore, the current arising in a given location of the superconductor is determined not only by the value of the external field at this location,

The thermal properties of superconductors at a temperature divided from $T_k$ turns out to be a very small value, much less than the thermal conductivity of a normal metal taken at the same temperature (the transition to the normal state at the same temperature can be accomplished using a magnetic field). Therefore, the temperature of superconductors changes very easily when heat is supplied or, conversely, when heat is removed.

The picture of heat transfer in a superconductor is very necessary. The thermal conductivity of ordinary metal changes with temperature. For example, the ability of alloys to conduct heat decreases with decreasing temperature. The superconducting alloy exhibits bonding behavior.
