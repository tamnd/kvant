If the state of one of the electrons in a pair changes under the influence of some force (for example, under the influence of a magnetic field), then this change will immediately affect the behavior of the other electron. Superconductors exhibit the rarest example of long-distance communication in inanimate nature.

As the temperature rises, the role of chaotic thermal motion increases. The interelectronic acceptance weakens, and, finally, at some temperature (this is the critical temperature $T_k$) the substance passes from the superconducting to the ordinary, normal state.

No electrical resistance. Superconductors are remarkable because electrons in them do not repel each other, but stick to each other.

Under the influence of attractive forces, the electronic system turns into a connected collective. Cohesive forces act between the parts.

In normal metals, electron gas can increase its energy in any small portions. In a superconductor, the electron gas cannot absorb any energy. The minimum portion of energy that it can absorb is the binding energy of an electron pair. The picture of the breaking of an electron pair is similar to the decay of an atomic nucleus. Neutrons and protons are bound into a single whole, and breaking the system requires finite energy - the binding energy of the atomic nucleus.

Electrical resistance is known to be associated with the scattering of electrons by thermal vibrations of the lattice or impurities *). The mechanical energy of moving electrons turns into warm energy
