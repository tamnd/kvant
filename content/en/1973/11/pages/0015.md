Open-loop control

As the technical complexity of control objects has developed, it has increased immeasurably. It becomes more and more difficult for man to control the machines he has created. For example, an operator of a high-speed rolling machine has to perform many (!) operations per minute - press buttons, turn steering wheels and handles, move levers. It is impossible for a person to work at such a pace without making mistakes.

Computers came to the rescue. A much more typical example of the use of a computer in an open-loop control system is the operation of computer-controlled machine tools. Here's how they work.

The initial information for drawing up a workpiece processing program is taken from the working drawing. The technologist outlines the required processing sequence, divides the geometric contour of the part into sections formed by straight or curved lines, determines the coordinates of reference points (joint points of individual sections of the contour, centers of arcs, etc.) and sets the parameters of the lines connecting the reference points. All this information is encoded (in a double or decimal number system) and applied to punched tape or punched cards, which are specially controlled by a computer control machine - an integrator.

The technologist sets the coordinates of only some (reference) points on the surface of the part. But the movement of the working parts of the machine must be ensured at some point in time. Consequently, information must enter the machine control system continuously in accordance with the law of tool motion along each coordinate. This stream of numerical information is created by an interpolator that calculates the position of the working parts of the machine for each element of the part.

The results of the interpolator calculation either directly enter the control system of the machine, or are pre-recorded on magnetic tape in the so-called unitary code (with a base equal to one) and only then transferred to the control system.

In a unitary code, each number is written as a sequence of units equal to the number being written. For example, the number 5 is written like this: 11111. The need to convert double-coded information into a unitary code, that is, into a sequence of single pulses, is determined by the design of the executive bodies of computer-controlled machines - stepper motors. The stepper moves in such a way that each single pulse entering its winding causes the shaft to rotate and move at a strictly defined angle. Consequently, the movement of the working body (cutter, milling cutter, etc.) associated with the shaft moves will be proportional to the number of pulses sent to the control device of the machine.

The transformation of information in the interpolator is carried out in such a way that the output single pulses are equal to the double-coded coordinate, and the time during which this number of pulses is issued is the specified processing time of the contour section from one point to another.

Computer-controlled machines, which first appeared in the late 50s, have now become increasingly widespread. They form the basis of future, fully automated enterprises.
