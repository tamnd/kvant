⟦folio 3⟧

The absence of electrical resistance means that there is absolutely no energy loss when current flows. After all, heat losses when current passes through normal, non-superconducting metals (such metals are called normal) are caused by the resistance of the conductor. This can be seen from the well-known formula expressing the Joule-Lenz law:

$$Q = I^2 Rt.$$

In a superconductor, electrons experience no resistance and do not lose energy. Therefore, the superconducting current does not dampen.

In 1959, such an experiment was carried out. A superconducting current was induced in the metal ring. After two and a half years, the instruments did not notice any decrease in this current. The absence of thermal losses leads to the fact that the time of current circulation through the ring is almost infinitely long.

Superconducting substances are the first to be discovered when using other substances. However, new superconductors were soon discovered.

Currently, about 25 elements are known that transform into a superconducting state when the temperature decreases. The highest critical temperature is for nubium ($T_K = 9,2^\circ$), and the lowest is for pridinium. In pridinium, superconductivity is detected at a temperature of only 0.140°. Temperatures of thousandths of a degree have now been reached. It is possible that many elements turn out to be superconductors at temperatures lower than $T_K$ pridinium.

The bulk of superconducting substances are not pure substances, but alloys and compounds. About 500 such superconducting substances are now known, and their number is increasing all the time. Many laboratories around the world are actively searching for new superconducting materials.

The highest temperatures at which the property of superconductivity is preserved are observed precisely in alloys. For many years, the “record holder” was Nizya alloys with Nb$_3$Sn ovum, the critical temperature of which is 18.1°. Currently, the highest $T_K$ is possessed by the complex compound Nb$_3$Al·Nb$_3$Ge, synthesized several years ago and retaining the property of superconductivity up to a temperature of $T_K = 20,1^\circ$.

Even a simple study of the properties of superconductors allows one to draw some interesting conclusions.

There are alloys (for example, an alloy of copper with sulfur CuS or gold with bicmut Au$_2$Bi), the components of which are not superconductors.

Beryllium becomes superconducting only if the sample is prepared in the form of a thin film, the thickness of which does not exceed 10$^{-5}$ cm.

Different metals conduct electricity differently. One would expect that such good conductors as copper, gold, silver, whose resistance under ordinary conditions is already very small, would more easily transition to the state of superconductivity than other substances. However, in experience a completely different picture is observed. It is in these metals that superconductivity has not been discovered. The importance of this fact was noted by physicists long before the creation of the theory of superconductivity. Thus, in the book by Ya. G. Dorfman and I. K. Kikon, published 40 years ago (*), we read: “The obstruction that neither Ag, nor Cu, nor other best conductors go into superconducting states, but, on the contrary, ... flat conductors ... have this amazing feature, indicates that the mechanism of superconductivity is completely different from the mechanism of ordinary conductivity.”

*) Y. G. Dorfman, I. K. Kikon. Physics of metals. GTTI, 1933.
