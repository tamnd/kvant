⟦folio 24⟧

APPLICATION

Fluxgate magnetometer

The field arising in the core is determined by the total field created by the current of the primary coil and that weak external constant (or little changing over time) field in which the magnetometer is located: $B = \mu B_{\text{cym}} = \mu(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)$. The magnetic permeability of the core materials—permalloy—depends on the magnitude of induction $B_{\text{cym}}$: $\mu = a - b B^2_{\text{cym}}$ ($a, b$—some constant coefficients, $a$—dimensionless, dimension $b$[$b] = \frac{1}{[B^2]}$). So the magnetic flux through the core, perpendicular to the confluence lines of the total magnetic field, is equal to

$$\Phi = S(a B_{\text{cym}} - b B^3_{\text{cym}}) = S[a(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0) - b(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)^3] = S(a B_1 \sin \omega t + a B_0 - b B_1^3 \sin^3 \omega t - 3b B_0 B_1^2 \sin^2 \omega t - 3b B_0^2 B_1 \sin \omega t - b B_0^3).$$

The principle of operation of a magnetometer: it “selects” the fourth term $(-3b B_0 B_1^2 \sin^2 \omega t) = \frac{3}{2} b B_0 B_1^2 (1 - \cos 2\omega t)$ and, according to the electromotive force it creates in the secondary winding, changes $B_0$. But how to suppress the remaining terms of the flow?

For this purpose, not one, but two primary windings, wound in opposite directions, are put on the core (see Fig. 8). These windings create fields $B_1 \sin \omega t$ and $-B_1 \sin \omega t$ in a vacuum, respectively. In the core, the total flux from the two windings $\Phi_{\text{cym}} = S[a(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0) - b(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)^3] + S[a(-B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0) - b(-B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)^3] = S(2a B_0 - 2b B_0^3 - 3b B_0 B_1^2 + 3b B_0 B_1^2 \cos 2\omega t)$ has a frequency twice as high as the frequency of the supply current. Accordingly, such a device is called a second harmonic sensor.

If the device operated in a linear mode (that is, the magnetic flux in the core was equal to $\Phi = S[a(B_0 + B_1 \sin \omega t)]$, then the magnetic flux $\Phi_1 = a B_0 S$ created by a weak mechanism by the field would be difficult to register due to its smallness. The time-varying flux $\Phi_2 = a S B_1 \sin \omega t$ would not give anything to change the field, it would not depend on the variable field $B_0$. Cubic (that is, nonlinear) dependence $\Phi = \Phi(B_{\text{cym}})$ introduces a term $(3b B_0 B_1^2 \cos 2\omega t)$ into the flux, proportional to both the induction $B_0$ of the variable external field and the square of the induction of the excited field. This is no longer a small value, moreover, it can be adjusted by changing the excitation current.

Why was permaloy chosen from a number of ferromagnetic materials? First of all, the permalloy magnetization curve contains nonlinear cubic terms. In addition, due to the high magnetic permeability of permalloy, which already reaches a value of $\mu = 100 000$ in weak fields, non-magnetizing fields create a large induction in the core. A change in this induction over time causes greater e. d.s. in the secondary winding, the amplitude of which is proportional to the magnitude of the variable field, and the frequency is equal to twice the frequency of the supply current. Created by this e. d.s. The current in the secondary winding is successfully rectified by a special circuit, and its constant component serves as a measure of the field $B_0$.
