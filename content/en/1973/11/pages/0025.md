The remanent magnetization field is on the surface, it quickly decreases with height, and to register it it is necessary to install a magnetometer on the surface of the Moon.

Such an experiment was carried out by astronauts of the Apollo 12 spacecraft on November 19, 1969. They installed a fluxgate magnetometer and put it into action. The device continued to operate for many months even in the absence of astronauts, and its readings were automatically transmitted by a radio transmitter to Earth.

Over the last year, 1973, as a result of changes obtained with the help of the Apollo 17 spacecraft, our views on the structure of the Moon have undergone an evolution. A lot of data has been obtained on the crawl of a hot ($t^\circ > 1000^\circ$ C) core. But there are no currents that would create a magnetic field in the core. The magnetic field of the Moon is due to the remanent magnetization of the crust and, possibly, processes in the layers under the crust, at depths of 100-200 km.

Mars, Jupiter, Venus

The Soviet fluxgate magnetometer SG, installed at the Venera-4 station, changed the magnetic field (~ 10 γ) at distances up to 200 km from the surface of Venus. Venus's own magnetic field was not detected at these distances.

Valuable information for the SG magnetometer installed on the artificial Martian satellites “Mars-2” and “Mars-3”. At an altitude of 2000 km above the surface of Mars, a field of 27γ was discovered, which is 8 times higher than the interplanetary field. If subsequent changes confirm that this is Mars’ own field, then this can only be the core field: the surface field disappears at altitudes of 1000-2000 km.

Along with changes on Mars, there are also changes in magnetic fields on the largest planet, Jupiter. Judging by the radio emission spectrum of Jupiter in the meter and centimeter wavelength ranges, it can be stated with sufficient confidence that Jupiter has a strong magnetic field - hundreds of times stronger than the Earth’s field.
