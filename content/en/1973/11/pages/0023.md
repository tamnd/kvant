In the region adjacent externally to the boundary of the magnetosphere, the magnetic field of the solar wind is distorted by the influence of the earth's field. This region (at figure 5 it is darker than the magnetosphere) is the region of the “disturbed” solar wind. Its outer boundary closest to the Earth lies at a distance of ~ 100,000 km from the Earth's surface. Beyond this boundary there is an undisturbed solar wind (black color on figure 5), moving from the Sun in a straight line.

The Earth's magnetosphere is an outer shield 60,000 km thick, protecting us both from the destructive effects of solar cosmic rays and from the less dangerous solar wind. The next, inner shield is formed by the earth's atmosphere.

Magnetic changes performed on the Luna 2 package showed for the first time that there is no strong magnetic field on the Moon. There is no second shield - atmosphere. Therefore, solar cosmic rays continuously bombard the lunar surface; The radiation danger on the Moon is much greater than on Earth.

Measurements on artificial lunar satellites

figure 6 shows the orbits of the artificial satellites of the Moon. All of these satellites were equipped with fluxgate magnetometers.

The sensitivity of the magnetometer installed on the first artificial lunar satellite in the history of Luna 10 (it was launched in the Soviet Union in April 1966) was 1γ. Magnetic surveys were carried out along the satellite's trajectory at altitudes from 350 to 1017 km above the lunar surface.

In 1967, the American Explorer 35 satellite was launched into orbit around the Moon. A fluxgate magnetometer similar to our SG was installed on it.

The results of measurements from Luna 10 and Explorer 35 confirmed the measurements from Luna 2. The Moon does not have its own magnetic field (core field) and behaves in relation to the magnetic field of the solar wind as a non-magnetic ball with low electrical conductivity (which indicates the absence of a liquid core). Solar wind ions and cosmic rays continuously reach its surface.
