Superconductors in a magnetic field. Critical field. If you increase the external magnetic field, then at some of its values ​​$B_K$ (this value is called the critical magnetic field), the superconducting state is destroyed, and the substance passes into the usual normal state. The value of $B_K$ depends on temperature. The closer we approach the critical temperature, the smaller the value of $B_K$ and the easier the state of superconductivity is destroyed.

figure 6 shows how the value of the critical field $B_K$ changes with increasing temperature. The most stable superconducting state is at absolute zero (value $B_K$, corresponding to $T = 0^\circ$, maximum). When the temperature becomes $T_K$, the magnitude of the critical magnetic field naturally becomes zero.

Thus, a metal can be transferred from a superconducting state to a normal state without increasing its temperature. Such a transition can occur when a superconductor is exposed to a magnetic field.

Therefore, one should not assume that a substance at a temperature below $T_K$ is necessarily a superconductor. It can also be a normal metal if the magnitude of the surrounding magnetic field exceeds $B_K$.

If we want to obtain very strong magnetic fields, then it is necessary to create sufficiently strong points in the windings; in this case, due to heat losses, the wires may simply melt.
