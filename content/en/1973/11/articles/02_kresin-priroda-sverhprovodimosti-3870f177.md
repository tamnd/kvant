Superconducting states of matter

Discovery of superconductivity. The phenomenon of superconductivity was discovered a little more than
60 years ago, in 1911. In the laboratory of the Dutch physicist Kamerlingh Onnes, the electrical resistance of metals at ultra-low temperatures was studied.

Kamerlingh Onnes showed how the ability of pure metals to conduct electric current changes with changing temperatures. Resistance to the movement of electrons in a pure metal arises due to thermal vibrations of ions located at the nodes of the crystal lattice. When the temperature rises, these fluctuations become less intense, which leads to a decrease in resistance. The absence of electrical resistance means that there is absolutely no energy loss when current flows. After all, heat losses when current passes through normal, non-superconducting metals (such metals are called normal) are caused by the resistance of the conductor. This can be seen from the well-known formula expressing the Joule-Lenz law:

$$Q = I^2 Rt.$$

In a superconductor, electrons experience no resistance and do not lose energy. Therefore, the superconducting current does not dampen.

In 1959, such an experiment was carried out. A superconducting current was induced in the metal ring. After two and a half years, the instruments did not notice any decrease in this current. The absence of thermal losses leads to the fact that the time of current circulation through the ring is almost infinitely long.

Superconducting substances are the first to be discovered when using other substances. However, new superconductors were soon discovered.

Currently, about 25 elements are known that transform into a superconducting state when the temperature decreases. The highest critical temperature is for nubium ($T_K = 9,2^\circ$), and the lowest is for pridinium. In pridinium, superconductivity is detected at a temperature of only 0.140°. Temperatures of thousandths of a degree have now been reached. It is possible that many elements turn out to be superconductors at temperatures lower than $T_K$ pridinium.

The bulk of superconducting substances are not pure substances, but alloys and compounds. About 500 such superconducting substances are now known, and their number is increasing all the time. Many laboratories around the world are actively searching for new superconducting materials.

The highest temperatures at which the property of superconductivity is preserved are observed precisely in alloys. For many years, the “record holder” was Nizya alloys with Nb$_3$Sn ovum, the critical temperature of which is 18.1°. Currently, the highest $T_K$ is possessed by the complex compound Nb$_3$Al·Nb$_3$Ge, synthesized several years ago and retaining the property of superconductivity up to a temperature of $T_K = 20,1^\circ$.

Even a simple study of the properties of superconductors allows one to draw some interesting conclusions.

There are alloys (for example, an alloy of copper with sulfur CuS or gold with bicmut Au$_2$Bi), the components of which are not superconductors.

Beryllium becomes superconducting only if the sample is prepared in the form of a thin film, the thickness of which does not exceed 10$^{-5}$ cm.

Different metals conduct electricity differently. One would expect that such good conductors as copper, gold, silver, whose resistance under ordinary conditions is already very small, would more easily transition to the state of superconductivity than other substances. However, in experience a completely different picture is observed. It is in these metals that superconductivity has not been discovered. The importance of this fact was noted by physicists long before the creation of the theory of superconductivity. Thus, in the book by Ya. G. Dorfman and I. K. Kikon, published 40 years ago (*), we read: “The obstruction that neither Ag, nor Cu, nor other best conductors go into superconducting states, but, on the contrary, ... flat conductors ... have this amazing feature, indicates that the mechanism of superconductivity is completely different from the mechanism of ordinary conductivity.”

A special state of matter. The word “superconductivity” reflects the ability of substances to conduct electric current without any resistance. But this is not the only feature of superconductors. The superconducting transition is not only the disappearance of electrical resistance. This is a transition to a super new physical state. Anomalies of almost all known physical properties are observed in superconductors. Whatever we are talking about - about heat capacity, about the absorption of light or sound, about an increase in a magnetic field - they always ignore the special properties of superconductors, which sharply differ from the properties of ordinary metals. When passing through the critical temperature $T_k$ we find ourselves in a world of special laws. That is why we will talk not just about superconductivity, but about the superconducting state of matter.

Let us first dwell on the main question: what is the nature of superconductivity? How is the phenomenon of superconductivity explained and what happens in a substance when its temperature approaches absolute zero?

Attraction between electrons. The title of this section may cause legal satisfaction. In fact, electrins, as particles with identical electric charges, should not attract, but repel each other. It turns out, however, that the superconductivity effect is determined precisely by the presence of forces between electrons.

The electron gas in a metal or semiconductor is under the influence of the crystal lattice, due to which mechanical attraction appears. Let's try to visualize the mechanism by which this attraction arises. The interaction distance of any two electrons $e_1$ and $e_2$ located in the metal (Fig. 2). Thus, the crystal lattice is the intermediate medium that creates attraction between electrons. The attraction itself can be imagined as follows. The electron attracts new crystal lattices. As a result of lattice deformation, the electron becomes a circular “cloud” of positive charge. At high temperatures, sufficiently intense thermal movement throws particles away from each other, erodes the nonna “coat,” which actually reduces the spirals of attraction. At low temperatures, attractive forces play a very important role.

The question may arise: does the existence of electron attraction between electrons contradict the known law of physics? If two electrons are in a vacuum, then, of course, they repel each other. In the medium, the force of their interaction is equal to

(ε is the dielectric constant of the medium). The medium may have a negative dielectric constant ε < 0. This leads to a change in the sign of the force, and electrons in such a medium will attract each other. The crystal lattice turns out to be the medium that makes the dielectric constant in a superconductor negative.

Thus, at temperatures close to absolute zero, Coulomb repulsion and additional attraction act between electrons. Everything else depends on the size. If repulsion predominates down to absolute zero, the metal remains normal and its properties do not radically change. If, at a certain temperature, attractive forces begin to predominate, then the substance goes into a superconducting state.

No electrical resistance. Superconductors are remarkable because electrons in them do not repel each other, but stick to each other.

At temperatures above $T_k$, magnetic field lines penetrate into the substance, as into any normal metal (Fig. 3, a). Let's start lowering the temperature without turning off the magnetic field. Once the critical temperature $T_k$ is reached, a transition to the superconducting state occurs; in this case, the lines of force are pushed out of the thickness of the sample (Fig. 3, b). This phenomenon, called the Meissner effect, was discovered in 1933.

How does the magnetic field line push out from a superconductor? The answer to this question is closely related to the effect of superconductivity itself, that is, with the possibility of continuous electrical currents flowing.

Let's assume that we want to maintain a constant electric current in a substance. In a normal metal, this requires creating a constant electric field. A magnetic field is not suitable for this purpose, since it does not do any work, and this is not necessary, since there is movement of electrons, heat losses occur, and therefore a continuous supply of energy is necessary.

Completely different processes develop in superconductors. They have zero resistance, so no work is required to maintain electric current. Direct current in superconductors can be created by a magnetic field. This is a very important feature of the electromagnetism of superconductors.

Let us now return to the Meissner effect. When a superconductor is exposed to an external magnetic field, a superconducting current caused by this field will arise in its surface. This current, as in the case of the movement of charges, creates its own magnetic field, which is opposite to the external magnetic field. The theory of superconductivity proves that inside the superconductor, these fields are completely compensated, so there is no magnetic field there. Thus, the surface layer of a superconductor is a magnetic shield that does not allow the magnetic field to pass through the substance.

Superconductors in a magnetic field. Critical field. If you increase the external magnetic field, then at some of its values ​​$B_K$ (this value is called the critical magnetic field), the superconducting state is destroyed, and the substance passes into the usual normal state. The value of $B_K$ depends on temperature. The closer we approach the critical temperature, the smaller the value of $B_K$ and the easier the state of superconductivity is destroyed.

figure 6 shows how the value of the critical field $B_K$ changes with increasing temperature. The most stable superconducting state is at absolute zero (value $B_K$, corresponding to $T = 0^\circ$, maximum). When the temperature becomes $T_K$, the magnitude of the critical magnetic field naturally becomes zero.

Thus, a metal can be transferred from a superconducting state to a normal state without increasing its temperature. Such a transition can occur when a superconductor is exposed to a magnetic field.

Therefore, one should not assume that a substance at a temperature below $T_K$ is necessarily a superconductor. It can also be a normal metal if the magnitude of the surrounding magnetic field exceeds $B_K$.

At first glance, it may seem that superconducting current, flowing without thermal loss, is ideal for creating extremely strong magnetic fields. Indeed, when using conventional electromagnets, Joule losses play a large role. If we want to obtain very strong magnetic fields, then it is necessary to create sufficiently strong points in the windings; in this case, due to heat losses, the wires may simply melt.

In superconductors, due to the absence of thermal losses, there is no such danger. Therefore, it may seem that by increasing the superconducting current we can obtain any required value of the magnetic field induction. However, as soon as the field

Speaking about the electromagnetic properties of superconductors, one more interesting feature should be noted, due to the presence of pairs of bound electrons.

The basic law describing direct current in a normal metal is Ohm's law, which can be written in the so-called local form: $j = E/\rho$ ($\rho$ is the resistivity of the conductor, $E$ is the electric field voltage, $j$ is the current density). According to Ohm's law, the magnitude of the current in a certain place in the metal is determined by the value of the electric field in the same place.

In superconductors, everything looks completely different. A constant superconducting current is created, as we have already noted, not by an electric field, but by a magnetic field. But this is not the only difference. Because of the interelectronic coupling, it turns out that if an external field acts on any electron in a superconductor, then this action will be “felt” by another electron associated with it in a pair.

Let's place the superconductor in an external magnetic field. The electrons located in the surface layer (the field does not penetrate into the thickness of the sample) begin to move, and a superconducting current is created. In this case, the electron moves both due to the direct action of an external magnetic field on it, and under the influence of other electrons associated with it, also being under the influence of the field. Therefore, the current arising in a given location of the superconductor is determined not only by the value of the external field at this location,

Josephson effect. A distance contact (Fig.8), consisting of two superconducting films separated by a thin (about $10^{-7}\text{cm}$) layer of insulating material. Electrons can move from one film to another. We will not discuss here the issue of the mechanism for the passage of electrons through a dielectric layer, since this is not directly related to the subject of our story. Let us only note the possibility of observing an amazing phenomenon, discovered only a few years ago.

If a constant potential is applied to a contact, then alternating current will be consumed through it. This alternating current, just like, for example, the current in an oscillating circuit, will emit electromagnetic waves, and this radiation is observed experimentally.

This effect is named after the English physicist Josephson, who theoretically predicted the possibility of its observation in 1962.

As we have already said, bound electron pairs appear in a superconductor. These pairs pass through the dielectric layer and acquire energy $2eU$ (U is the potential difference applied to the contact). In an ordinary metal, this energy would be converted into warm energy. When a superconducting current flows, there is no resistance, and the energy portion $2eU$ received by the electron pair is emitted in the form of a quantum with energy $hv = 2eU$. Radiation with a frequency of $v = 2eU/h$ is observed experimentally.

Only alternating current can emit electromagnetic waves. This is exactly the current that flows through the Josephon contact.

The expression for the frequency of radiation includes the satisfactory charge of the electron. This is due to the existence of electron pairs; the total charge of each is $2e$. A single electron, when passing through a potential difference $U$, acquires energy $eU$. The fact that radiation with a frequency of $v = 2eU/h$ is actually observed experimentally can be considered as experimental confirmation of the correctness of the idea of ​​the existence of bound electron pairs in superconductors.

The Josephson effect allows you to create alternating current using a constant potential difference.
