Briefly about the device

Even in ancient times it was known that the Earth acts on the magnetic needle as if it were a giant magnet. And scientists still cannot answer the question about the reason for the emergence of the earth’s magnetic field and the laws of its change over time (it changes slowly over a millennium). Therefore, of great interest to science is the question of whether other cosmic bodies have magnetic fields - the Moon, Mars, Venus, and so on - that differ from the Earth in their internal structure, size and daily rotation period. By comparing the magnetism of the planets and the magnetism of the Earth, it will be possible to understand what caused the appearance of the magnetic field on Earth.

Magnetic fields, although very weak - about 6000 times weaker than on Earth - also exist in interplanetary space. In the entire solar system, from the Sun to Neptune, it is impossible to indicate places where these weak fields do not exist.

To measure magnetic fields on planets and in interplanetary space, special instruments are installed on almost all spacecraft - so-called fluxgate magnetometers. This is one of the main space instruments.

This device is essentially a special type of transformer.

The described transformer consists of two windings, primary and secondary, wound on a common ferromagnetic core *. In the SG magnetometer, the core is a thin strip of permalloy, a special alloy of iron (20%) and nickel (80%).

Induction B of the resulting magnetic field in the core consists of two parts: induction $B_0$ of the field created by the current of the magnetizing coil, and induction $B_\phi$ of the field created by oriented elementary currents in the substance itself. The ratio $\frac{B}{B_0} = \frac{B_0 + B_\phi}{B_0} = \mu$, called the magnetic permeability of a substance, for ferromagnetic materials is much larger (that is, $B_\phi \gg B_0$) **).

The product of induction B and the area S of the core cross-section perpendicular to vector B, that is, BS, is called magnetic flux.

According to the law of electromagnetic induction, a change in magnetic flux over time generates an electromotive force of induction in the secondary winding, the magnitude of which is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux and the number of turns in the secondary winding. If the number of turns in the secondary winding is greater than in the primary, we will get a voltage in it greater than in the primary (step-up transformer). The purpose of a transformer is to increase (or decrease) the voltage applied to the primary winding.

*) Let us recall that ferromagnetic are materials that are capable of strongly magnetizing, even when they are in a relatively weak external magnetic field (mainly iron, nickel and some of their compounds and alloys).

**) The value of magnetic permeability can change greatly when the external magnetic field changes. In particular, permaloy differs in that, at very weak external fields, the value of μ changes sharply with changes in the external field.

In the magnetometer, as in the transformer, there are primary and secondary windings, wound on a common permalloy core (for the magnetometer diagram, see Fig. 8 in the Appendix). An alternating current with a frequency of 5000 2Hz is supplied to the primary winding from a special generator. This current creates an alternating magnetic flux F in the permaloy core, and a change in time of this flux induces e in the secondary winding. d.s. induction. Velichana e. d.s. modified by special instruments installed on the spacecraft and transmitted via radio to Earth.

However, the purpose of a magnetmeter is not to increase or decrease the voltage, but to change the time-constant weak external magnetic field in which the device is located.

The external field changes the magnetic permeability of the core and thereby affects the secondary voltage (the principle of operation of the magnetmeter is described in more detail in the Appendix). Transformer cores are made of transformer steel, an alloy of iron and silicon; weak external magnetic fields do not change the magnetic permeability of this material.

The magnetometer has three mutually perpendicular coils; they change the field components in three mutually perpendicular directions.

The sensitivity of the device is very high. For example, a magnetmeter installed on the artificial lunar satellite “Luna-10” records changes in the magnetic inductance of 1 gamma. (γ is a unit of measurement of magnetic induction; 1γ = 10⁻⁹ mL. The magnetic field at the Earth’s surface is 30÷60 thousand gamma, the interplanetary field is 5γ.)

What is the significance of this result?

First, it is important for understanding the internal structure of the Moon. The Earth's magnetic field is created, above all, by a complex system of flows of molten metals in the Earth's core (Fig. 3). The absence of a magnetic field on the Moon, close in magnitude to the Earth's, suggests that there are no currents in the Moon's core that create a magnetic field.

Secondly, the absence of a magnetic field in the interior of the Moon radically changes the nature of the interaction of the Moon with electrons and electrons falling on its surface.

In addition to visible light, ultraphyllet, infrared and RENTgen rays, the sun also emits streams of electrons and ions - the so-called solar wind. The speed of solar wind ions (95% are hydrogen ions - protons) is from 300 to 700 km/s, depending on the conditions on the Sun (its activity). In addition to this wind, the Sun also emits particles of high energy, the so-called solar cosmic rays. If the average energy of solar wind protons

In 1967, the American Explorer 35 satellite was launched into orbit around the Moon. A fluxgate magnetometer similar to our SG was installed on it.

The results of measurements from Luna 10 and Explorer 35 confirmed the measurements from Luna 2. The Moon does not have its own magnetic field (core field) and behaves in relation to the magnetic field of the solar wind as a non-magnetic ball with low electrical conductivity (which indicates the absence of a liquid core). Solar wind ions and cosmic rays continuously reach its surface.

Magnetization of lunar samples

There is no core magnetic field on the Moon. But then lunar rock samples were brought to Earth, and measurements showed that these samples were magnetized.

How did this magnetization occur?

The first natural explanation is the hypothesis that the Moon had a magnetic field in the distant past, which subsequently disappeared. Let us assume that the Moon once had a molten core, which created a strong magnetic field (Fig. 7a). This field magnetized rocks on the surface containing iron. Then, in the absence of the Moon and its daily rotation slowing down, the magnetic field of the core disappeared. However, the magnetized strata of the lunar surface retained their magnetic properties - the so-called remanent magnetization (Fig. 76).

The rock acquires the main share of residual magnetization when it remains: therefore, when the Moon remained, the core field was there. Based on the residual magnetization, knowing the composition of the sample, one can judge the magnitude of the magnetic field in which this sample cooled. On the other hand, the age of rocks can be determined by the relative daily average of radioactive elements and their decay products*).

Such combined changes in radioactivity and magnetization of lunar rock samples showed that approximately 3.1 billion years ago the Moon had its own magnetic field of at least 1000γ.

Mars, Jupiter, Venus

The Soviet fluxgate magnetometer SG, installed at the Venera-4 station, changed the magnetic field (~ 10 γ) at distances up to 200 km from the surface of Venus. Venus's own magnetic field was not detected at these distances.

Valuable information for the SG magnetometer installed on the artificial Martian satellites “Mars-2” and “Mars-3”. At an altitude of 2000 km above the surface of Mars, a field of 27γ was discovered, which is 8 times higher than the interplanetary field. If subsequent changes confirm that this is Mars’ own field, then this can only be the core field: the surface field disappears at altitudes of 1000-2000 km.

Along with changes on Mars, there are also changes in magnetic fields on the largest planet, Jupiter. Judging by the radio emission spectrum of Jupiter in the meter and centimeter wavelength ranges, it can be stated with sufficient confidence that Jupiter has a strong magnetic field - hundreds of times stronger than the Earth’s field. APPLICATION

Fluxgate magnetometer

The field arising in the core is determined by the total field created by the current of the primary coil and that weak external constant (or little changing over time) field in which the magnetometer is located: $B = \mu B_{\text{cym}} = \mu(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)$. The magnetic permeability of the core materials—permalloy—depends on the magnitude of induction $B_{\text{cym}}$: $\mu = a - b B^2_{\text{cym}}$ ($a, b$—some constant coefficients, $a$—dimensionless, dimension $b$[$b] = \frac{1}{[B^2]}$). So the magnetic flux through the core, perpendicular to the confluence lines of the total magnetic field, is equal to

$$\Phi = S(a B_{\text{cym}} - b B^3_{\text{cym}}) = S[a(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0) - b(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)^3] = S(a B_1 \sin \omega t + a B_0 - b B_1^3 \sin^3 \omega t - 3b B_0 B_1^2 \sin^2 \omega t - 3b B_0^2 B_1 \sin \omega t - b B_0^3).$$

The principle of operation of a magnetometer: it “selects” the fourth term $(-3b B_0 B_1^2 \sin^2 \omega t) = \frac{3}{2} b B_0 B_1^2 (1 - \cos 2\omega t)$ and, according to the electromotive force it creates in the secondary winding, changes $B_0$. But how to suppress the remaining terms of the flow?

For this purpose, not one, but two primary windings, wound in opposite directions, are put on the core (see Fig. 8). These windings create fields $B_1 \sin \omega t$ and $-B_1 \sin \omega t$ in a vacuum, respectively. In the core, the total flux from the two windings $\Phi_{\text{cym}} = S[a(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0) - b(B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)^3] + S[a(-B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0) - b(-B_1 \sin \omega t + B_0)^3] = S(2a B_0 - 2b B_0^3 - 3b B_0 B_1^2 + 3b B_0 B_1^2 \cos 2\omega t)$ has a frequency twice as high as the frequency of the supply current. Accordingly, such a device is called a second harmonic sensor.

If the device operated in a linear mode (that is, the magnetic flux in the core was equal to $\Phi = S[a(B_0 + B_1 \sin \omega t)]$, then the magnetic flux $\Phi_1 = a B_0 S$ created by a weak mechanism by the field would be difficult to register due to its smallness. The time-varying flux $\Phi_2 = a S B_1 \sin \omega t$ would not give anything to change the field, it would not depend on the variable field $B_0$. Cubic (that is, nonlinear) dependence $\Phi = \Phi(B_{\text{cym}})$ introduces a term $(3b B_0 B_1^2 \cos 2\omega t)$ into the flux, proportional to both the induction $B_0$ of the variable external field and the square of the induction of the excited field. This is no longer a small value, moreover, it can be adjusted by changing the excitation current.

Why was permaloy chosen from a number of ferromagnetic materials? First of all, the permalloy magnetization curve contains nonlinear cubic terms. In addition, due to the high magnetic permeability of permalloy, which already reaches a value of $\mu = 100 000$ in weak fields, non-magnetizing fields create a large induction in the core. A change in this induction over time causes greater e. d.s. in the secondary winding, the amplitude of which is proportional to the magnitude of the variable field, and the frequency is equal to twice the frequency of the supply current. Created by this e. d.s. The current in the secondary winding is successfully rectified by a special circuit, and its constant component serves as a measure of the field $B_0$.
