Surface tension draws a hyperbola

I. I. Vorobiev

One method for measuring the surface tension coefficient of a liquid is based on raising a column of wetting liquid in a capillary. However, capillary tubes and microcoppes are not always at hand to determine their internal diameter.

It turns out that capillaries can be completely replaced by two glass plates. Place the plates in a vessel with water and gradually bring them closer parallel to each other. Water rises between the plates - it is drawn in by the tension surface force (Fig. 1).

It is easy to calculate the tension surface coefficient σ from the height of water rise y and the gap between the plates d. The surface force of the tension surface is F = 2 σL, where L is the length of the plate (the two appears due to the fact that the water is in contact with the volumes of the plates). This force holds a layer of water of mass m = ρLdy,

where ρ is the density of water. Thus,

2 σL = ρLdy.

From this we can find the tension surface coefficient

σ = 1/2 ρgdy. (1)

But it’s more interesting to do this: roll the plates together at one end, and leave a small gap at the other (Fig. 2). The water rises and forms an amazingly regular surface between the plates (of course, if the glass is clean and dry).

It would be easy to say that the section of this surface by the hertical plane is a hyperbola. In fact, it is enough to substitute d into formula (1) together with a new expression for the gap at a given location. From the similarity of the corresponding triangles (see Fig. 2)

d = D x/L Here D is the end gap,

L is still the length of the plate, and x is the distance from the point of contact of the plates to the place where the gap and level height are determined. Thus,

σ = 1/2 ρgdy D x/L,

or

y = 2σL/ρgD · 1/x. (2)

Equation (2) is real and is a hyperbola equation.

To conduct the experiment, you can take 10× plates
