Fire in a sieve

The experiment we invite you to do is almost a magic trick. To perform this trick, you will need a smooth metal rod, paper, a candle and matches.

Cut a narrow strip of paper and wrap it around a metal rod with a screw. This must be done so that the cocked paper fits tightly to the metal. Now light the candle and insert a rod wrapped in paper into its flame. The paper doesn't burn! The rod is already hot in your hand, but the paper still doesn’t burn. And enough time will pass before it lights up.

What's the matter? After all, paper “by itself” burns beautifully.

This “trick” is explained by the high thermal conductivity of the metal. The metal rod quickly “takes away” the heat from the paper and within a few minutes prevents it from heating up to a temperature at which it can catch fire.

The property of metals to conduct heat well is very clearly demonstrated by this experiment. Let's take a mesh made of copper wire (in the places where the wires are intertwined, they should touch very tightly). Let's place this grid over the gas burner. If you light the gas above the mesh with the burner tap open, the gas under the mesh will not ignite. If you light the gas under the mesh, the flame will not “leak” through the mesh.

Now you can probably explain for yourself why this is happening.

In those days when there were no electronic miner's lamps, miners used the so-called Davy lamp. In its simplest form, it is a candle “planted” in a small metal cage. And yes-

if the mine is full of flammable gases, a burning Davy lamp will not cause an explosion - the flame does not “extend” beyond the lamp thanks to the metal mesh.

T. S. Petrova
