Surface tension
L. G. Aslamaozov

The processes of surface tension of liquids in nature and technology are surprisingly diverse. It creates drops of water, thanks to which we can blow a soap bubble and write with a pen. Surface tension plays an important role in the physiology of our body. Its use in space technology. Why does the surface of a liquid behave like a stretched elastic film? Let's try to figure this out.

Surface Energy
Molecules located in a thin layer of liquid near the surface are in special conditions. They have neighbors identical to it only on one side of the surface, in contrast to molecules inside a liquid, surrounded on all sides by the same molecules. Therefore, the resulting force acting on the molecule in the surface layer is different from zero. For example, on the free surface of a liquid (Fig. 1) this force is directed inside the liquid.

When a molecule moves from the surface into the bulk of a liquid, positive work is performed. This means that the molecules in the surface layer have excess potential energy compared to the molecules inside the liquid. Of course, the molecules of a liquid are in continuous thermal motion - one molecule leaves the surface, others, on the contrary, fall on it. But we can talk about the average additional energy of the surface layer of the liquid - about the surface energy proportional to the surface area of ​​the liquid.

It is known that of all possible states of a system of sources, the one in which the energy of the system is minimal. In particular, the surface of the liquid tends to take a shape in which its surface energy is minimal. As they say, a liquid has increased tension, which tends to contract and reduce its surface. The surface tension coefficient is the surface energy per unit area or force per unit length of the surface boundaries. It's easy to prove (do it yourself) that both definitions of surface tension are equivalent.

The tendency of surface tension to reduce the surface of a liquid is usually counteracted by other forces. For example, a drop of liquid is almost never a sphere, although a sphere has the smallest surface area of ​​all figures for a given volume. When a drop accumulates on a stationary horizontal surface, it becomes flattened. A drop falling in the air also has a complex shape. And only a drop in weightlessness takes on a perfect spherical shape.

The Belgian scientist J. Plateau first agreed to eliminate the effect of gravity when studying the surface tension of a liquid in the middle of the last century. Of course, at that time they did not even dream of real weightlessness, and Plato simply proposed balancing the force with gravity using the Archimedean buoyancy force. He placed the liquid under study (oil) in a solution of the same density, and, as his biographer writes, “he was surprised to see that the oil took on a spherical shape; he immediately accepted his rule “to be surprised in time,” and this phenomenon then served as the subject of long reflection for him.”

Plato used his method to study various phenomena. For example, he studied the process of formation and separation of a drop of liquid at the end of a tube.

Obviously, no matter how slowly we grow the drop, it comes off the tube so quickly that the eye cannot follow the details of this process. Plato placed the end of the tube into a liquid whose density was only slightly less than that of a drop. In this case, the effect of gravity is weakened; you can grow a very large drop and see how it comes off the tube.

Let's try to explain this phenomenon. As long as the drop grows slowly, we can assume that it is in equilibrium at every moment of time. Then, for a given volume of a drop, its shape is determined from the condition that the sum of the surface energy and the potential energy of the drop due to gravity is minimal. The larger the drop, the more role the potential energy of gravity plays. As the drop grows, the bulk of the mass gathers at the bottom, and the drop forms a neck (second photograph on figure 2). A secondary droplet is always formed (it is called a Plateau ball), but since the process of tearing off a droplet is very fast, we usually do not notice this secondary droplet.

Drop liftoff is used to measure the surface tension of a liquid. When the drop is still hanging on the neck, the force of gravity is balanced by the surface tension acting along the perimeter of the cross section of the neck:

$$mg = 2\pi r\sigma,$$

where $r$ is the radius of the narrowest point of the neck, in which the surface tension forces are directed vertically, $\sigma$ is the coefficient of surface tension of the liquid. The mass of the drop $m$ hanging on the neck, as already displaced, is practically equal to the mass of the detached drop. Therefore, by changing the radius of the neck $r$ and determining the mass of the detached drop $m$ we weigh, we can find the values ​​of the surface tension of the liquid:

$$\sigma = \frac{mg}{2\pi r}.$$

For greater accuracy, they usually collect many drops into a vessel and, dividing their total mass by the number of drops, find the average drop mass $m$. To more accurately determine the radius of the neck $r$, at which the drop has not yet come off, it is illuminated with a diverging beam of light and the size of the neck is determined by the size of the shadow on the screen and the magnification factor.

Soap films

Soap films are an excellent object for studying surface tension. Gravity plays almost no role here, since the films are extremely thin and their mass is absolutely insignificant. The main role here is played by surface energy, and therefore, in soap films, the shape of the surface is always such that its area is minimal.

Probably everyone has seen soap dust. They can float freely in the air and are perfectly spherical.

The pressure inside the dust is greater than atmospheric pressure, and the smaller the dust radius $R$, the greater the excess pressure inside the dust. This excess pressure $\Delta p_{c\phi}$ (for spherical dust) is determined by the well-known Laplace formula:

$$\Delta p_{c\phi} = \frac{2\sigma'}{R},$$

where $\sigma' = 2\sigma$ is the doubled surface tension coefficient of the liquid (the film has two surfaces).

The reciprocal of the radius of a sphere is called its curvature: $\rho = \frac{1}{R}$.

The formula written above means that the excess pressure is proportional to the curvature of the sphere.

A ball is not the only shape that soap dust can be given. If you place the dusty dumma in rings, then it can be stretched until it takes the shape of a cylinder with spherical “caps” (Fig. 3).

Interestingly, the formation of stable foam is only a side effect of the decrease in surface tension when detergents dissolve. Foam is formed from air bubbles that fall inside the water (when mixed with a stream of water). Then they float to its surface and find themselves surrounded by a film of liquid. If the surface tension is not high, then the excess pressure inside the bubble is low, and it should not burst. Of course, the high strength of soap films also plays a significant role here.

The mechanism of the washing action, which was discussed here, is also of interest in connection with other important technical tasks - coating surfaces with varnishes and paints, bonding, dyeing fabric with suspensions, making waterproof fabrics.

For example, in order to make a fabric water-repellent, its educational specialized substance that forms a thin film around each fiber. This film significantly increases the surface tension at the water-fabric interface, but little changes the tension at the fabric-air interface. If the formula for the contact angle θ is formed, then it is easy to see that the angle θ increases. As a result, water does not wet the fabric, but collects on it into drops that roll off the materials.

Adsorption films are used to reduce the evaporation of water from the surface of reservoirs, which is an important problem in arid areas. A protective film is easily created over the entire surface of a reservoir, since adsorption always reduces surface tension (otherwise adsorption would not produce surface energy at all). The surface tension force of pure water acts on the film boundary, tending to stretch the film, and

the tension force of the film itself is directed in the opposite direction. The surface tension of clean water is greater, and as a result the film covers the entire clean surface.

To create protective films, a special substance is used - hexadecanol. Under normal conditions, this is a solid substance that floats on the surface of the water. If the entire surface of the water is covered with an adsorbed monomolecular layer, then the substance is not consumed. However, if the film gets damaged somewhere. As the surface tension increases in this place, it pulls the film from neighboring areas, etc. As a result, the film is broken and near the floating piece of substance, which restores the film.

It was found that to create a protective film over an area of ​​1 EA, about 2 EA of hexadecanol is required, and the loss is on average 2-3 monomolecular layers per day. Therefore, the consumption of substance to maintain the film is only about 60 ea per day per 1 ea, and this method turns out to be economically profitable. For example, in Australia, it saves about 10 million liters of water per hectare of water surface every day.
