The larger the drop, the more role the potential energy of gravity plays. As the drop grows, the bulk of the mass gathers at the bottom, and the drop forms a neck (second photograph on figure 2). The surface tension force is directed vertically tangentially to the neck. It balances the force of gravity acting on the drop. Now, enough for the drop to increase quite a bit, and the forces of surface tension will no longer be able to balance the force of gravity. The neck of the drop quickly narrows (third photograph on figure 2), and as a result the drop breaks off (fourth photograph). In this case, a small drop is separated from the neck of the drop and falls after the large one. A secondary droplet is always formed (it is called a Plateau ball), but since the process of tearing off a droplet is very fast, we usually do not notice this secondary droplet.

Drop liftoff is used to measure the surface tension of a liquid. When the drop is still hanging on the neck, the force of gravity is balanced by the surface tension acting along the perimeter of the cross section of the neck:

$$mg = 2\pi r\sigma,$$

where $r$ is the radius of the narrowest point of the neck, in which the surface tension forces are directed vertically, $\sigma$ is the coefficient of surface tension of the liquid. The mass of the drop $m$ hanging on the neck, as already displaced, is practically equal to the mass of the detached drop. Therefore, by changing the radius of the neck $r$ and determining the mass of the detached drop $m$ we weigh, we can find the values ​​of the surface tension of the liquid:

$$\sigma = \frac{mg}{2\pi r}.$$

For greater accuracy, they usually collect many drops into a vessel and, dividing their total mass by the number of drops, find the average drop mass $m$. To more accurately determine the radius of the neck $r$, at which the drop has not yet come off, it is illuminated with a diverging beam of light and the size of the neck is determined by the size of the shadow on the screen and the magnification factor.

Soap films

Soap films are an excellent object for studying surface tension. Gravity plays almost no role here, since the films are extremely thin and their mass is absolutely insignificant. The main role here is played by surface energy, and therefore, in soap films, the shape of the surface is always such that its area is minimal.

Probably everyone has seen soap dust. They can float freely in the air and are perfectly spherical.

The pressure inside the dust is greater than atmospheric pressure, and the smaller the dust radius $R$, the greater the excess pressure inside the dust. This excess pressure $\Delta p_{c\phi}$ (for spherical dust) is determined by the well-known Laplace formula:

$$\Delta p_{c\phi} = \frac{2\sigma'}{R},$$

where $\sigma' = 2\sigma$ is the doubled surface tension coefficient of the liquid (the film has two surfaces).

The reciprocal of the radius of a sphere is called its curvature: $\rho = \frac{1}{R}$.

The formula written above means that the excess pressure is proportional to the curvature of the sphere.

A ball is not the only shape that soap dust can be given. If you place the dusty dumma in rings, then it can be stretched until it takes the shape of a cylinder with spherical “caps” (Fig. 3).
