The law of conservation of energy (along with the law of conservation of momentum and other conservation laws derived from physics, which are not studied in high school) is a powerful tool for solving physical problems. The main reason for this is that the law of conservation of energy is always valid, regardless of the course of the process and the nature of the acting forces. This law can also be used when the course of the process is generally unknown - only the initial and final states of the system are extracted. This is the case, for example, in elementary particle physics.
Now we will analyze several problems in which the application of the law of conservation of energy allows us to understand the situation in which diamantic patterns lead to very complex mathematical calculations, and sometimes even lead to a dead end.
All problems are taken from written work on application exams at MIPT in 1970-1972.
Problem 1. On an inclined plane from a height $h$, a hoop is crushed without piercing the monk. Determine its speed at the end of the inclined plane.
This problem can be solved using only Newton's laws. But

Such a solution is practically impossible to implement within the framework of a school course.
Let's use the law of conservation of energy.
Initially, the hoop has potential energy $mgh$ (m is the mass of the hoop); By the end of the path along the inclined plane, all this energy has turned into kinetic energy, which consists of the energy of translational motion $\frac{mv^2}{2}$ (v is the final speed of the hoop’s center of mass) and the energy of rotational motion. Since the hoop does not slip, the linear speed of all its points is equal to the speed of translational motion of the hoop as a whole, that is, $v$. Therefore, the kinetic energy of rotational motion, as well as translational motion, is equal to $\frac{mv^2}{2}$.
Thus,
$$2 \cdot \frac{mv^2}{2} = mgh$$
And
$$v = \sqrt{gh}.$$
