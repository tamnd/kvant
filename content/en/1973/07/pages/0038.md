⟦folio 36⟧

One is above the plane, and the other is below. The role of the free fall acceleration $g$ is played by the acceleration $a$. In order to answer other questions of the problem, we need to consider two cases: a) the plate is negatively charged and b) the plate is positively charged.

Let's consider case a). In this case, the direction vector of the electric field is directed toward the plane, and the acceleration $a$ of the electron is directed away from the plane. Several possible trajectories of electron motion (for different values ​​of $\sigma$) are shown in figure 2. If the acceleration of “free fall” $a$ is large, then the electron may not reach the plane. Since the vertical component of the initial velocity of the electron is equal to $v_y = v_0 \cos \alpha$, then the maximum height $H$ of its “rise” is equal to

$$H = \frac{v_y^2}{2a} = \frac{(v_0 \cos \alpha)^2}{2a} = \frac{(v_0 \cos \alpha)^2 \varepsilon_0 m}{\varepsilon \sigma}.$$

If $H < d$, then this means that the electron should not reach the plate. In this case, the minimum distance at which the electron approaches the plane is

$$h = d - H = d - \frac{(v_0 \cos \alpha)^2 \varepsilon_0 m}{\varepsilon \sigma}.$$

At $H = d$ the electron trajectory touches the plane, and at $H > d$ the electron will pass through the plane. Below the plate of electron motion trajectories there will be another parabola.

So, since here the acceleration is directed away from the plate, the electron will always move away from it.

Let's find at what point the electron will pass through the plate and its speed at this moment in time. To do this, we write down the equations of electron motion. The projection of the electron velocity onto the $X$ (Fig. 2) axis is constructed and is equal to $v_x = v_0 \sin \alpha$. Therefore, the coordinate $x$ of the electron changes with time according to the law

$$x = v_x t = v_0 \sin \alpha \cdot t.$$ (4)

Since the acceleration of the electron $a$ is directed along the axis $Y$, the coordinate $y$ changes with time as follows:

$$y = v_0 \cos \alpha \cdot t + \frac{at^2}{2}.$$ (5)

At time $t = t_1$, at which the electron crosses the plate, $y = y_1 = d$ and $x = x_1$. Substituting these values ​​$t$, $x$ and $y$ into equation (4) and (5), we obtain:

$$x_1 = v_0 \sin \alpha \cdot t_1,$$

$$d = v_0 \cos \alpha \cdot t_1 + \frac{at^2}{2}.$$ (6)

Solving this system of equations, we find

$$t_1 = \frac{-v_0 \cos \alpha + \sqrt{v_0^2 \cos^2 \alpha + 2ad}}{a}$$

$$x_1 = v_0 \sin \alpha \cdot \frac{-v_0 \cos \alpha + \sqrt{v_0^2 \cos^2 \alpha + 2ad}}{a}$$

*) Solving the second of equations (6), we discarded the second, larger root for $t_1$. This equation allows us to determine those moments in time when the frequency had a coordinate $y$ equal to $d$. If the plate were far away and the electron did not cross it during its movement, then this value $y$ would be repeated twice: the first time when the electron “rose”, and the second time when it “fell”. The value of the coordinate $y = d$ during the “return” of the electron corresponds to the second root for $t_1$, equal to

$$-v_0 \cos \alpha - \sqrt{v_0^2 \cos^2 \alpha + 2ad}$$
