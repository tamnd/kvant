In fact, per unit length of the circle along which the water comes into contact with the walls of the flask, only three forces of surface tension act: $\sigma_{2-3}$ - surface tension at the boundary of air and water, $\sigma_{1-2}$ - tension at the boundary of water and the stack, and $\sigma_{3-1}$ - tension at the boundary of the stack from the air. In equilibrium, the surface of the liquid forms such a contact angle with the wall that the resultant of these three forces does not have a component along the wall: $\sigma_{1-3} = \sigma_{1-2} + \sigma_{2-3}\cos\theta$ (the perpendicular component is balanced by the reaction force of the wall). That's why

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\sigma_{1-3} - \sigma_{1-2}}{\sigma_{2-3}}$$

We see that the contact angle depends only on the nature of the three contact media, namely on the surface tension at their boundaries. It does not depend on the magnitude of gravity and the shape of the vessel.

The surface of the liquid, the boundary from the air, in weightlessness should be minimal, and therefore spherical. Then the final picture of the location of the liquid in the flask in zero gravity becomes clear. The spherical surface of the liquid covers the air at the walls of the flask so as to maintain the contact angle (Fig. 86). Of course, there are no concepts of up and down in zero gravity, and this picture can be turned like a corner.

Why did the water in the glass flask surround the bubble on all sides, tearing it away from the walls of the flask? This is easy to understand - after all, clean water completely wets clean glass (Fig. 9, a). The contact angle is zero, and the liquid will be enclosed by acting in a spherical bubble, which should touch its surface to the stack of the flask. But then it can break away from them and settle inside the liquid (Fig. 9, b).

Of course, such a study of the behavior of liquids in weightlessness is only qualitative in nature, and real calculations are a complex physical and mathematical problem. However, such a qualitative study helped us to correctly imagine the behavior of a “weightless” liquid.
