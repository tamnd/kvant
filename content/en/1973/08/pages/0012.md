3. "Pascal wheel"

On January 2, 1640, the Pascal family moved to Rouen, where Etienne Pascal received the position of integrator of the pronunciers, actually in charge of all affairs under the governor.

This appointment was preceded by curious events. E. Pascal took an active part in the performances of the wig RANT believers, for which he was threatened with imprisonment in the Bastille. He was forced to hide, but at that time Jacqueline fell ill with wasp, and her father, despite the terrible threat, visited her. Jacqueline recovered and even took part in a performance attended by Cardinal Richelieu. At the request of the horse actress, the cardinal forgave her father, but at the same time appointed him to the position. The former troublemaker had to implement the cardinal's policy (the reader of The Three Mysketeers will probably not be surprised by this treachery).

Now Etienne Pascal had a lot of counting work, in which his son constantly helps him. At the end of 1640, Blaise Pascal came up with the idea of ​​​​building a machine to free the mind from calculations “with the help of a pen and tokens.” The main idea arose quickly and remained unchanged throughout “... each wheel or rod of a certain category, making a movement of ten digits of arithmetic, causes the next to move only one digit.” However, a brilliant idea is only the first step. Its implementation required incomparably greater efforts. Later, in the “Foreknowledge” to those who “will have the curiosity to see and use the harpmetic machine,” Blaise Pascal modestly writes: “I did not save any time, no labor, no means to bring it to a state of being useful to you.” Behind these words stood five years of hard work, which led to the creation of a machine (“Pascal wheel,” as contemporaries said), which reliably, although rather slowly, performed four operations on five-digit numbers. Pascal made about fifty copies of the machine; here is just a list of the materials he tried: wood, ivory, ebony, brass, copper. He spent a lot of effort searching for the best craftsmen who could handle “the lathe, the file and the hammer,” and many times it seemed to him that they were unable to achieve the required precision. The testing system is carefully thought out, including transportation of 250 liters. Pascal does not forget about advertising: he receives the support of the clerk Segier, adds “royal privileges” (something like a patent), demonstrates the car many times in showrooms and even sends a copy to the Swedish Queen Christina. It is unknown whether it is finally being made by machine-made ones, but eight copies have survived to this day.

It’s amazing how brilliantly Pascal could do a variety of things. Relatively recently it became known that in 1623 Schiccard, a friend of Kepler, built a harpmetic machine, but Pascal's machine was much more perfect.
