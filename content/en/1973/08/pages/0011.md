Pascal first formulates a theorem for a circle and limits the simplest numbering of points. In this case, this is an elementary, although not very simple task **). But the transition from a circle to any finite section is very simple. It is necessary to transform such a section into a circle using a central projection and take advantage of the fact that with central projection, straight lines turn into straight lines, and intersection points (in a generalized sense) into intersection points. Then, as has already been proven, the images of the points P, Q, R when projected will lie on the same straight line, and it follows that the points P, Q, R themselves have this property.

*) Form your own corollaries that can be obtained from this theorem when some of the points under consideration are at infinity.

**) Its solution can be found in the article L. M. Lopovok, Inscribed hexagon. «Kvant» No. 1, 1973, p. 18.

The theorema, which Pascal calls the “mystical hexagon” theorem, was not an end in itself; he considered it as the key to constructing a general theory of finite sections, covering the theory of Apollonius. The poster already mentions generalizations of important theorems of Apollonius, which Desargues was unable to obtain. Desargus praised Pascal's theorem, calling it "the great Pascalva"; he claimed that it contained the first four books of Apollonius.

Pascal begins work on the “Complete work on finite sections”, which in 1654 is mentioned as the final work of the “Significant Parisian Academy of Mathematics”. It is known from Mersen that Pascal obtained about 400 corollaries from his theorem. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) was the last person to see Pascal's treatise after his death, in 1675-1676. Despite Leibniz's advice, the family did not publish the manuscript, and over time it was lost.

As an example, we give one of the simplest, but also the most important corollaries from Pascal's theorem. The finite section is uniquely determined by any properties of the points.
