mercury empty. There was a widespread point of view that the apparent vacuum is filled by matter that “has no properties” (I recall Second Lieutenant Kizhe from the story by Yu. N. Tynyanov, “having no figure”). It is simply impossible to prove the absence of such matter. Pascal's clear statements are very important in posing a broader problem about the nature of evidence in physics. He writes: “After I have proven that none of the matter that is accessible to our senses and that is known to us fills this space, which seems empty, I wash it until they prove to me the existence of some matter filling it - that this space is in fact empty and devoid of all matter.” Less academic statements are contained in a letter to the non-suit scientist Nozl: “But we have more reasons to deny its (exact matter - S.G.) existence, because it cannot be proven, than to believe in it for the sole reason that it cannot be proven that it does not exist.” So, it is necessary to prove the existence of an object and cannot require proof of its absence (this is associated with the legal principle that the court must prove guilt and it is wrong to demand proof of innocence from the accused).

At that time, B. Pascal's elder sister Gilberte lived in Pascal's homeland in Clermont; her husband Florent Perrier, who served in court, devoted his free time to teaching. On November 15, 1647, Pascal sent Perrier a letter in which he asked to compare the levels of mercury in the Torricelli tube at the foot and at the top of the Pun-de-Dome mountain: “You understand, if the height of the mercury at the top of the mountain were less than at the bottom (I think so for many reasons, although everyone who has written about this subject has a different opinion), then from this it would be possible to conclude that the only cause of the phenomenon is gravity air, and not the press horror vacui (fear of emptiness - S.G.). It is clear, in fact, that at the bottom of the mountain the influences should be more concentrated than at the top, while it is not reasonable to assume in him a greater fear of emptiness at the foot than at the top.” The experiment was postponed for various reasons and took place only on September 19, 1648 in the presence of the heel of the “wetted inhabitants of Clermont.” At the end of the year, a pamphlet was published that included Pascal's letter and Perrier's reply, with a very scrupulous description of the experience. With a mountain height of about 1.5 km, the difference in mercury levels is 82.5 mm; this “filled the participants in the experiment with admiration and surprise” and, it is believed, was unexpected for Pascal. It is impossible to assume the existence of preliminary estimates, and the lightness of the air was very great. The result was clear that already one of the participants in the experiment, Abbot Maru, came up with the idea that the experiment could produce results on a much more modest scale. And, indeed, the difference in levels of the Notre-Dame-de-Clermont Cathedral, which has a height of 39 m, is 4.5 mm. If Pascal had admitted such a possibility, he would not have waited ten months. Having received what is known from Perrier, he repeats the experiments on the highest data in Paris, obtaining the same results. Pascal called this experiment "the great experiment of fluid equilibrium" (this name may be surprising, since it deals with the equilibrium of air and mercury and thereby influences Descartes).
