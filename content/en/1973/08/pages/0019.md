The problems considered by Pascal no longer allow elementary solutions (the area and center of gravity of an arbitrary segment of a cyclonde, the volumes of the corresponding bodies of rotation, etc.). Using these problems, Pascal developed essentially everything that was necessary to construct differential and integral calculus in a general form. Leibniz, who shares the credit with Newton as the creators of this theory, writes that when, on the advice of Huygen, he became acquainted with the works of Pascal, he was “illuminated with a new light,” he was surprised how close Pascal was to constructing a general theory, and suddenly stopped, as if “there were veils before his eyes.”

It was characteristic of the works that anticipated the advent of differential and integral calculus that the intuition of their authors was far ahead of the possibility of conducting rigorous proofs; the mathematical language was not developed enough to transfer the train of thought onto paper. A solution was found later by introducing new concepts and special symbols. Pascal did not resort to any symbolism, but he mastered the language so masterfully that at times it seems that he simply did not need it. Let us cite the statement of N. Bourbaki: “Walis in 1655 and Pascal in 1658 each compiled languages ​​of an algebraic nature for their own use, in which, without writing down a single formula, they give formulations that can be immediately, as soon as their mechanism is understood, written in the formulas of integral calculus. Pascal's language is particularly clear and precise; and if it is not always clear why he abandoned the use of algebraic notation not only of Descartes, but also of Vieta, one still cannot help but admire his skill, which could only manifest itself on the basis of perfect command of the language.” I would like to say that here Pascal the writer helped Pascal the mathematician.
