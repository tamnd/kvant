4. "Fear of Emptiness" and "The Great Experiment in Fluid Equilibrium"
At the end of 1646, rumors of amazing “Italian experiments with emptiness” reached Rouen.
The question of the existence of emptiness in nature worried the ancient Greeks; in their views on this issue, the diversity of points of view inherent in ancient Greek philosophy was manifested: Epicurus believed that emptiness can and does exist; Heron - that it can be obtained artificially, Empedocles - that it does not exist and has nowhere to come from, and, finally, Aristotle argued that “the nature of the bounty is emptiness.”
In the Middle Ages, calculations were simplified, since the truth of Aristotle’s teachings was established by practical people in a legal manner (back in the 17th century, speaking against Aristotle in France could lead to hard labor). The classic example of “Fear of Emptiness” shows water rising behind a piston without pressing into the empty space that forms. And suddenly an incident occurred with this example. When constructing the fountains at Florenzini, it was discovered that the water “did not want” to rise above 34 feet (10.3 meters). The perplexed parties turned for help to the death of Galius Galius (1564-1642), who believed that nature ceases to be rich in emptiness at a height exceeding 34 feet, but still offered to analyze the phenomenon to his students Evangelista Torricelli (1608-1647) and Vincenzo Vivani (1622-1703). It is likely that Torricelli (and perhaps Galius himself) came up with the idea that the height at which a liquid can rise in a pump is inversely proportional to its specific gravity. In particular, the water must rise to a height 13.3 times less than water, i.e. 76 cm. The experiment acquired a scale more favorable for laboratory conditions and was carried out by Vivani at the initiative of Torricelli. This experiment is well known, but let us remind you that a meter-long glass tube sealed at one end is filled with mercury, the open end is closed with a finger, after which the tube is turned over and lowered into a cup with mercury. If the report is a finger, then the level of mercury in the tube will drop to 76 cm. Torricelli makes two statements: firstly, the space above the mercury in the tube is empty (later it will be called the “Torricelli void”), and, secondly, the mercury does not pour out of the tube completely, since this is prevented by a column of air pressing on the surface of the mercury in the cup. By accepting these hypotheses, everything can be explained, but an explanation can be obtained by introducing special, rather complex, acting forces that prevent the formation of a vacuum. Accepting Torricelli's hypotheses was not easy. Only a few of his contemporaries accepted the effect of obtaining a vacuum on the air, but it was almost impossible to believe that the slightest exposure to heavy mercury in a tube. Let us mention that Galileo is trying to explain this effect by the properties of the liquid itself, and Descartes argued that the apparent vacuum is always filled with “the finest matter.”

Pascal enthusiastically repeats Italian experiments, coming up with many ingenious conditions for the creation. Eight such experiments are described in a treatise published in 1647. He does not limit himself to experiments with mercury, but experiments with water, oil, and red wine, for which he needed barrels along with necks and tubes about 15 m long. Effective experiments are carried out onto the streets of Rouen, delighting its residents. (Engravings with a wine barometer are still popularly reproduced in physics textbooks.)
