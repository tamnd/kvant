In the associations of statements that we encountered earlier (conjunction, alternative, disjunction), the order of statements did not play any role. For implication this is no longer true. Let's look at an example.

Offer
“If a number is divisible by 2³, mono is divisible by 2.”
expressed in the form “IF A, THEN B”*). This proposal is correct. The first part of such a sentence (before the comma) is called the premise (condition), the second part is called the conclusion (statement).

Reversing the premise and conclusion, we get the following sentence:
“IF a number is divisible by 2, THEN it is also divisible by 2³,” and this sentence is incorrect.

Consider again the rationale given by Hans and Klaus:
Hans “IF the sum of the digits is divisible by 3, THEN the number itself is divisible by 3.”

Klaus (wrote a reverse sentence): “IF a number is divisible by 3, THEN the sum of the digits is divisible by 3.”

Both sentences are of the form “IF A, THEN B.” But nevertheless, it cannot be argued that they have the same meaning (although in this case both sentences are true); they will differ significantly from each other.

The condition formed by Hans reads:

*) Here A means: “The number is divisible by 2³”, B means: “The number is divisible by 2”.
(Approx. Transl.)

The sum of the digits of the number 374111 is divisible by 3.”
Hence the conclusion is drawn: “The number 374111 can be divided by 3.”
Klaus, on the contrary, starts from the condition that the number is divisible by 3 and concludes that the sum of the digits of the number is also divisible by 3.

The reversal of a true statement may or may not be true, as was shown above. Therefore, it is always important to understand whether the appeal of a given statement will be correct.

If the reversal of the true statement is correct, then both statements can be combined using the phrase “THEN AND ONLY IF”*).

So, a number is divisible by 3 THEN AND ONLY THEN; WHEN the sum of the digits of a number is divisible by 3.

Let us now formulate the inversions of some statements and check whether they are correct.
“IF a number is divisible by 6, THEN it is divisible by 2.”
Appeal:
“IF a number is divisible by 2, THEN it is divisible by 6.”
It is clear that the second sentence is incorrect, since, for example, 14 is divisible by 2, but not divisible by 6.

a) IF a number is divisible by 12, THEN it is divisible by 6.
b) IF the sides of the rectangle ABCM are equal, THEN the rectangle ABCM is a square.
b) IF point P lies inside a triangle, then it also lies inside the circle circumscribed about this triangle.
