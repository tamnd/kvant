Since in fact the concentration of reactants changes continuously, our calculation will be more accurate the smaller the interval τ. To obtain the exact value of the concentration of reactants at a given time, you need to go to the limit at τ→0, taking into account that the reaction rate changes continuously.

Differential calculus allows you to solve this problem accurately. The concentration of reagents [N]₄ at each given time is determined by compliance

$$[N]_t = \frac{[N]_0}{1 + K[N]_0 t},$$

where [N]₀ is the initial concentration, t is time, the product from the beginning of the reaction *).

Substituting into equation (3) the values ​​of the rate constant and initial concentrations that we find, we find how the concentrations of the reactants and the rate of the process should change over time:

$$[R]_t = [S]_t = \frac{1}{1 + 0.1t} \text{ mol/l} = \frac{6 \cdot 10^{20}}{1 + 0.1t} \text{ cm}^{-3},$$

$$W_t = \frac{0,1}{(1 + 0.1t)^2} \text{ mol/l} \cdot c = \frac{6 \cdot 10^{19}}{(1 + 0.1t)^2} \text{ cm}^{-3} \cdot c^{-1}.$$

These dependencies are shown by red lines in Figures 2, a, b.

The true curves of changes in the rate of the chemical process and the concentration of reactants differ significantly from similar curves obtained by the approximate method.

Equation (3) allows you to calculate the concentration of reagents and the rate of the process at any time and for any initial concentration of reagents.

However, a chemical process does not always represent one elementary reaction of the interaction of starting substances with another. The overall process can be more complex; it consists of several elemental reactions occurring simultaneously.

For example, reaction products can interact with each other or react with the starting materials. Obviously, in this case, equation (3) will no longer describe the kinetics of the process, and it is necessary to derive other, more complex equations in which the rate of all elemental reactions occurring before the time of the process arises.

In this regard, it is possible not only to determine the rate constants of elemental reactions, but also to make sure that the process is actually assumed to be a set of these reactions.
