Further analysis. At $n > 2$, every wise man, and without the announcement of the guide, becomes aware not only that someone has gotten dirty, but also that everyone else knows about it. Let, for example, the boundaries of the sages be three: $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$. Then $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ sees the ugly face of $M_3$. Thus, $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ knows that someone got dirty, and if he didn’t come, then $M_3$ has no way to know about it.

The difference between the option when the conductor came or did not come, at $n > 2$ therefore becomes even subtler. Let us formulate it exactly for $n = 3$.

Case $n = 3$. If the guide came, then $M_3$ knows that $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ knows that someone got dirty, and if he didn’t come, then $M_3$ has no way to know about it.

General case. A famous philosopher once remarked:
If a person says a phrase
“I think about what I think about ...”, then already on the first - fourth turn we measure the meaning of what is being said.

In the general case, we will have to make not three or four, but $n$ revolutions. Let us denote by $U_k$ the following statement: $M_k$ knows that $M_{k-1}$ knows, ..., that $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ knows that someone got dirty.

Then the difference between the compared options can be formulated in the form of the following theorem:
Let the guide come, and the sages $M_1$, ..., $M_n$ (and maybe someone else) have their empty spaces dirty. Then statement $U_n$ is true.

Let the guide not come and the desert is dirty with the sages $M_1$, ..., $M_n$ (and only with them). Then statement $U_n$ is true.

Let us prove this theorem by indication.

With $n = 1$ it is very important: if the conductor came, then $M_1$ knows from his announcement that someone got dirty, but if the conductor did not come, and only $M_1$’s face was dirty, then he has no way of knowing about it; that is, in the first option $U_1$ is fair, but in the second it is unfair.

The transition from $n = k$ to $n = k + 1$ is carried out as follows.

Option I (the guide came). The faces of $M_1$, ..., $M_{k+1}$ are dirty. This means, in part, they are dirty at $M_1$, ..., $M_k$. This means that by the indicative assumption, statement $U_k$ is true. This, of course, is known to the wise man $M_{k+1}$, that is, $M_{k+1}$ knows that $M_k$ knows, ... that $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ knows that someone has gotten dirty. Thus, statement $U_{k+1}$ is true.

Option II (the guide did not come). The sage $M_{k-1}$ admits that he may have a clean face. In this case, from his point of view, the faces of only $k$ sages $M_1$, ..., $M_k$ are dirty, and, therefore, by the indicative assumption, statement $U_k$ is true. So, assuming that his face is clean, $M_{k+1}$ is forced to admit that $M_k$ does not know that $M_{k-1}$ knows, ... that $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ knows that someone has gotten dirty*).

And at the same time, it was finally clarified what the guide told the sage. The more $n$, the more the sages, it turns out, learned from his announcement - he who has udida hears!

In other words, $M_{k+1}$ does not know that $M_k$ knows, ..., that $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ knows that someone got dirty, that is, statement $U_{k+1}$ is true.

The theorem is proven.

*) In fact, $M_k$ knows about this, that is, in fact - and not from the point of view of $M_{k+1}$, who assumed that he has a clean face - statement $U_k$ is true. Check it out.
