§ 6. Dots over 1

Readers who have made it through all the obstacles to the end of § 5 are unlikely to find the following explanations. Still, “for the sake of order,” we give them.

1. In § 3 the transition from $k=4$ to $k+1=5$ is continued without errors. It is also true that for large $k$ the proof passes without changes. It is also not difficult to go from $n=2$ to $n=3$ and from $n=3$ to $n=4$. “Only” the transition from $n=1$ to $n=2$ does not work. He cannot get it because at $n=2$ the statement is false: not any two girls have the same color eyes.

If you still try to prove the proof “on your fingers” in this case, then nothing will come of it (in § 3 we connected $A$ and $E$ with a “bridge” $C$: both $A$ and $E$ have eyes the same color as $C$; if there are only two fingers, then such a bridge no).

The two-toed sloth from the epigraph was considered to draw attention to the exclusively indicated incident.

2. In § 2, statement $Y_2$ is true, but all statements $Y_n$, starting from the second, are false. The transition from $n=k$ to $n=k+1$ is justified correctly for $k \ge 2$. The transition from $n=1$ to $n=2$ contains an error: when we remove the $A$ card, there is one card left on the table; it has one number written on it, but not necessarily the number $X$.

3. If we leave aside the question of whether $M_2$ knew that $M_1$ knows that you need to wash yourself not in a pose, but in the parking lots (and if you knew, then did $M_3$ know about it, etc.), then the problem discussed in § 5 is equivalent to problem 1 from § 4. Thus, if the guide did not announce that “someone got dirty,” none of the wise men You won’t be able to wash yourself (neither at the $n$th stop, nor at any other stop).

No one will go to wash even in the case when $n>1$ and not all $n$ of the dirty sages are present during the announcement of the conductor (task 2 from § 4). The proof is similar to the wired one in § 5, only the induction needs to be found with $n=2$: with $n=1$ the only dirty sage can wash himself on the 1st os-

tanovka regardless of where he was at the time of the conductor's announcement.

4. In exercise 1 from § 5, statements a), b) and d) are true, but statements c) are false.

5. Along with the statement $U_e$ (true if the conductor came, and false if he did not come), to distinguish between the options comparable in § 5, we can use another $n!$ statements, which are obtained from $U_n$ by all possible permutations of the letters $M_1$, . . . , $M_n$. For example, $M_1$ knows that $M_2$ knows. . . that $M_n$ knows that someone has gotten dirty.

All these $n!$ statements contain $n$ turns. The “shorter” (content no more than $n-1$ turn) statement does not allow us to distinguish between the options when the guide came or did not come. For example, the statement $U_{n-1}$ is valid without declaring a conductor, since $M_{n-1}$ knows that $M_{n-2}$ knows. . . that $M_2$ knows that $M_1$ knows that $M_n$ is dirty.

6. The funny date from the epigraph to § 4 agreed that she had a dirty face, although no conductor made any announcements. The role of guide was played by a careless mixture of neighbors. Statutory sages, of course, would not allow themselves anything like that!
