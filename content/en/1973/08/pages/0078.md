“NOT ALL...” and “ALL... NOT”

We move on to consider the final mistake Klaus made in his written work. The task was to turn an incorrect statement into a correct one by introducing a negation.

Let us first note the following:

1. The logical negation of an incorrect statement leads to a correct statement;
2. From a true statement, logical negation can be used to obtain an incorrect statement.

Let's form the negations of the following sentences:

(a) “247 is a prime number”;
(b) «$2^4 + 2^2 = 2^5$»;
(b) “a is greater than 7”;
(g) “The product of 17·11 is an integer”;
(d) “All prime numbers are odd.”

Negations of sentences (a), (6), (c), (d) can be formed immediately:

(a') “247 is not a prime number”;
(b') «$2^4 + 2^2 \neq 2^5$»;
(b'_1) "and not more than 7" or
(b'_2) “a is less than 7 or equal to 7”;

(d') “The product 17·11 is an odd number.”

As you can see, negations can be formed in different ways. Therefore, it is not so easy to give a definite rule for composing the negation of each individual statement. You can, however, use the phrase “It is not true that...” before a previously formed statement. Thus, the statement “It is not true that all prime numbers are odd” will be a well-formed negation of statement (e). And the multi-school students surveyed formulated this denial as follows:

Ralph: “All prime numbers are not odd”;

Inga: “Not all prime numbers are odd”;

Peter: “No odd number is odd”;

Bernd: “There is at least one prime number that is not odd.”

We know that statement (e) is false. So the negation of this statement must be true. But it can be verified that only Inga and Bernd formed correct statements.
