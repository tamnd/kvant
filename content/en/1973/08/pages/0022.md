This happens because the water has the same height in all vessels, and the measure of this force is the weight of the water contained in the first vessel, which is uniform in shape. And if this quantity of water weighs one hundred pounds *), then a force of one hundred pounds is required to hold each of the stoppers, even at the fifth vessel, although the water contained in it does not weigh one ounce**).

To check this accurately, you need to close the hole of the fifth vessel with a round piece of wood and wrap it around it, like a pump piston. (The piston must enter and pass through the hole with such precision as not to get stuck and at the same time to prevent the escape of water.) Then attach to the middle of this piston a thread that would pass through this thin tube, tie it to one arm of the yoke of the scale, and hang a weight of one hundred pounds on the other arm. Then we shall see the complete equilibrium of this hundred-pound weight with the water in the thin tube, which weighs one ounce. If you slightly reduce the load of one hundred pounds, then the water will lower the piston, and the next amount.

*) 1 pound equals 453.6 g.
**) 1 ounce equals 28.35 g.

Consequently, the arm of the yoke of the scale to which it is attached will lift the one on which the load hangs of a little less than a hundred pounds. If the water freezes, but the ice does not stick to the vessel, then to keep it in balance, it will be enough to have only one ounce on the other arm of the scale. If we bring fire closer to the vessel and grow ice, then there are already a hundred pounds to balance the weight of this ice melted into water, although we have only one ounce of it.

The same thing will happen if the holes that are closed with stoppers are on the side or in the upper part of the vessels; It will be even easier to check this, exactly as follows.

You need to take a vessel, closed on all sides, make two holes in its upper part, one very narrow and the other wider, and attach tubes of the same size as the holes above both (Fig. VI). If you now insert a piston into a wide tube and a thin thread of water into it, it is easy to see that a larger weight will have to be placed on the piston so that the weight of the water in the thin tube does not push it up, just as in the first experiments a force of one hundred pounds was used to prevent the piston from being pushed down.
