Great contemporaries willingly communicated with de Mere, and only because of this his name was preserved in history. At the same time, he managed to write letters to Pascal admonishing him on various issues, not excluding mathematics. Now all this looks naive and, according to Sainte-Beuve, “such a letter is quite enough to destroy the person who wrote it in the opinion of posterity.” Nevertheless, for quite a long time Pascal willingly communicated with de Mere; he turned out to be a capable student of the gentleman in social life.

We move on to the story of how “the problem posed to the stern wisdom of secular man became the source of the theory of probability” (Poisson). Actually, there were two problems and, as the sources of mathematics found out, both of them were known long before de Mere. The first question is how many times must two dice be rolled so that the probability of getting two sixes at least once is greater than the probability of not getting two sixes at all? De Mere himself solved this problem, but, unfortunately... in two ways, which gave different answers: 24 and 25 throws. Convinced of the equal reliability of both methods, de Mere turns to the “inconstancy” of mathematics. Pascal, convinced that the correct answer is 25, does not even give a solution. Its main conditions were aimed at solving the second problem - the problem of “fair division of rates”. In the beginning of the game, all participants (their number can be more than two) first place bets in the “bank”; The game is divided into several games, and to win the bank you need to win a certain fixed number of games. The question is how the pot should be fairly divided among the players depending on the number of games won if the game is not completed (no one has won enough games to get the pot). According to Pascal, “de Mere...could not even begin to approach this issue...”.

No one from Pascal’s circle was able to understand the solution he proposed, but still a worthy owner was found. Between October 29 and 27, Pascal exchanges letters with Fermat (through the mediation of Pierre Carcavi, who inherited Mersenne's functions). It is often believed that the theory of probability was born in this correspondence. Ferm solves the betting problem differently than Pascal, and there is some initial disagreement. But in the last letter Pascal CONSTATES: “Our mutual depression has been completely restored,” and further: “As I see, the truth is the same in Toulouse and in Paris.” He is grateful that he has found a great like-minded person: “I would continue to share my thoughts with you as much as possible.”

In the same year, 1654, Pascal published one of his most popular works, “Treatise on the Trigonal Argument.” Now it is called the Pascal trigon, although it turned out that it was known in Ancient India, and in the 16th century it was rediscovered by Stiefel. It is based on a simple way to calculate the number of combinations $C_n^k$ by induction on $n$ (using the formula $C_n^k = C_{n-1}^k + C_{n-1}^{k-1}$). In this treatise it was first used earlier and is formulated in a form familiar to us.

In 1654, Pascal's "Notable Parisian Mathematical Academy" lists the works that he is preparing for publication, and among them is a treatise that "can rightfully claim the title of Mathematics of Chance."
