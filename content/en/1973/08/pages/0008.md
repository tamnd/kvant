The story of how Pascal himself constructed nested geometry is known from Gilberte's enthusiastic account of his system. This story gave rise to a very common misconception, which is that since Pascal discovered the 32nd sentence of Euclid’s Elements, he had previously discovered all the previous theorems and all the axions. This was often taken as an argument that Euclid’s axiomat is the only possible one. In fact, Vergono, Pascal’s geometry is at the “pre-Evkin” level, when intuitively insensitive statements are proven by reduction to very prominent ones, and the set of the latter is not fixed or limited in any way. Only at the next, significantly higher level is the great discovery made that one can limit oneself to a finite, relatively small set of very clear statements - axioms, the proposed truth of which can be left to be proven by a geometric statement. At the same time, along with intangible statements (such as, for example, theorems about replacement points of a triangle), it is necessary to prove “obvious” theorems, the validity of which is easy to believe (for example, simple tests for the equality of triangles). Actually, the 32nd sentence is the first sentence of “Began” that is insensitive in this sense. There is no doubt that young Pascal had neither the time for the enormous work of selecting axioms nor, most likely, the need for it.
