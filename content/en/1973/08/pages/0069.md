⟦folio 67⟧

VII city scientific conference for schoolchildren of Kyiv
A. N. Vilenkin

From March 29 to March 31, 1973, the Kyiv City Department of Public Education, the Kiev City Committee of the LKSMU and the Kiev Palace of Pioneers and Schoolchildren held the VII city scientific conference older than the classics.

The conference featured a city competition “For the best student scientific work”; over 2,000 schoolchildren took part in it. More than 100 works were displayed, the authors of which read reports in the sections of history, geography and local history, biology, literature, mathematics, physics (separately for grades 7-8 and 9-10), chemistry, astronomy, automation and cybernetics.

Sections of physics and mathematics worked in the Palace of Pioneers and Schoolchildren. On March 30, reports were heard from students in grades 7-8 in physics and students in grades 7-10 in mathematics, on March 31 - reports from students in grades 9-10 in physics. The jury included prominent scientists, the physics section (for students in grades 9-10) was headed by Corresponding Member of the Ukrainian Academy of Sciences, Director of the Institute of Physics of the Ukrainian Academy of Sciences, Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, Professor M. T. Shpak.

The mathematics section worked at the conference for the first time. Of the reports, the most interesting were the 10th grade students on Natasha Shvartsman’s scale 124 “Mathematical methods for calculating leaf areas.” In her work, a fairly simple method was found for determining the area of ​​leaves, say, of a maple tree (Natasha took up this work after a lesson in a biology club).

Natasha chose the following method. Two linear dimensions $l_1$ and $l_2$ are measured between certain points of the leaf (leaves are asymmetrical, so one leaf is not enough). Then the average is taken: $l = (l_1 + l_2)/2$. The area of ​​the sheet is determined by the formula $S = cl^2$ (this is natural, since the area of ​​similar figures is related to the squares of their linear dimensions). To find the coefficient $c$, the area $S_i$ and size $l_i$ were measured for several samples, and the coefficients $c_i$ were determined from them. The coefficient $c$ is defined as the average of these $c_i$.

What is the error allowed in this calculation of area? Natasha appreciated her too! Assuming that the error is distributed according to the so-called “normal law” (this is a term from probability theory), using studies of leaves, the probable error allowed when calculating the area was determined. This report was recognized as one of the best in the mathematics section.

The physics section already has extensive experience. If earlier the section was dominated by abstract reports, that is, a review of scientific literature (albeit carried out by a supervisor), now most of the guys presented the results of independent experiments, as well as calculations of various physical effects performed at the “student level”. Let's talk about some reports.

Everyone really liked the report of Pavel Trivaylo, a 9th grade student of 142 scale, “research on the friction of flexible bodies.” First, Pavel talked about Euler's formula. It lies in the fact that the friction force grows exponentially with increasing number of turns. This can be illustrated with this example. To hold the ship at the pier, the sailor winds a rope thrown from the ship onto a bollard. Let the sailor pull the rope with a force of 1 kts and, having wrapped the rope around the bollard once, the sailor will hold the rope, which pulls the other end with a force of 10 kts, then, having wound the rope twice, the sailor will hold 100 kts, three times - 1000 kts, ..., wrapping n times - 10ⁿ kts.

Then Pavel demonstrated a stand he had compared for determining the friction coefficient of flexible bodies. In his work, Pavel used this stand to determine (and compare) the friction coefficients of different types of tapes, paper and metal tapes, and nylon fishing line on steel and brass.

Galina Varskaya, a 9th grade student of scale 178, read the report “Study of nontell types of electron emission” (note that this title does not fully correspond to the work done). Galya has designed a device that allows one to determine the randomness of cells; it can be used biologically in the study of cancer tumors.
