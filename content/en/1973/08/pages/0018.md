7. Amos Dettonville

《I spent a lot of time studying abstract sciences; The lack of information he provided discouraged me from visiting them. When I began to study man, I saw that these distractions were not natural for him and that I was even more confused, delving into them, than others, not knowing them.” These words from Pascal characterize his mood in the last years of his life. And yet, for a year and a half of which he studied mathematics...

It began one night in the spring of 1658, when, during a terrible attack of toothache, Pascal remembered one of Mersen’s unsolved problems about the cyclode. He notices that focused thinking distracts from pain. By the morning, he had already proven his goal with a series of results about the cyclide and... was healed of his toothache. At first, Pascal considers randomness a sin and is not going to write down the results. Later, under the influence of the Duke of Roanne, he changes his mind; for eight days, according to Gilberte Perrier, “he did nothing but write while his hand could write.”

Then in June 1658, Pascal, as was often done then, organized a competition, inviting the greatest mathematicians to solve six problems about the cyclode. The greatest successes were added by Christan Huygens (1629-1695), who solved four problems, and John Wallis (1616-1703), for whom all problems were solved with some problems. But the work of the unknown Amos Dettonville was recognized as the real one. Huygens later admitted that “this work is done so subtly that it is impossible to add to it.” Note that “Amos Dettonville” consists of the same letters as “Louis de Montalte”. This is how a new pseudo-mime for Pascal was invented. Dettonville's works were published for the premium price of 60 pistoles.

First of all, let us quote Pascal’s words about the curve called a cyclude or roulette: “The roulette is so linear that after the straight line and the circle there is no more frequently occurring line; . . . or it is nothing other than the path described in the air by the nail of the wheel, when it rolls with its own motion from the moment the nail began to rise from the ground, until the non-prime quality of the wheel brings it back to the ground after the end of a whole revolution, considering that the wheel is a perfect circle, the nail is a point on its circumference, and the earth is ideally flat” (see Fig. 7). Pascal believed that Mersenne discovered the cycle, although in fact it was Galileo who discovered it. Initial interest in this curve was stimulated by the fact that a number of interesting problems for it could be solved in an elementary way.
