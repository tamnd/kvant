It is quite difficult to theoretically calculate the rate constant of a chemical reaction, so in most cases it should be determined based on experimental data.

Task 2. Determine the rate constant of the reaction between substances $R$ and $S$, if it is known from the experiment that their initial concentrations are 1 mol/L and in one second the act of chemical transformation indicates 10% of each substance.

In one second, 0.1 mol of a substance is consumed in 1 liter of volume; This means that in each cubic centimeter 6·10$^{19}$ acts of chemical transformation occur, that is, the reaction rate

$$W = 6 \cdot 10^{19} \text{ cm}^{-3} \text{c}^{-1}.$$

Substituting the value of the initial concentrations and reaction rate $W$ into equation (2), we determine the values ​​of the reaction rate constant:

$$K = \frac{W}{[R]_0 [S]_0} = 0,1 \frac{\lambda}{mol \cdot c} = 1,7 \cdot 10^{-20} \frac{cm^3}{c}.$$

We calculated the value of the constant $K$ at constant concentrations $[R]_0$ and $[S]_0$. If the speed of the process were constant, substances $R$ and $S$ would have to be completely consumed in 10 s, since, as already mentioned, 10% of the total amount of starting substances is consumed in 1 s. However, in reality this is not the case. The concentrations of reagents during a reaction decrease over time, and the rate of their consumption, that is, the reaction rate, decreases accordingly. During the same period of time, different amounts of time are spent at different stages of the reaction; the reaction rate is constant.
