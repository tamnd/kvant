A chemical reaction is the transformation of one substance into another. However, we may be interested not only in the products of chemical transformation, but also in the general laws according to which the chemical process occurs: how the concentration of substances changes, how temperature, pressure and other external conditions influence the course of the reaction.

To solve these problems, you need knowledge not only of chemistry, but also, perhaps even to a greater extent, of physics and mathematics.

We are publishing an article that examines one of the fundamental principles of chemical physics—the law of mass action, which can be used to describe the course of a chemical reaction over time.

In a chemical reaction, some substances can be converted into others. Combustion leads to the formation of water and carbon dioxide, and when acid and alkali are mixed, salt is formed. However, when studying chemistry, we seem to close our eyes to the time when molecules interact with each other, and open them to the outside when the products of a chemical reaction have already been formed. Meanwhile, it is precisely those processes that occur during the interaction of molecules with each other that determine the composition of the products of a chemical reaction.

The chemical reaction consists of two main stages. First, the particles must react with each other, and then the chemical transformation itself occurs, in which the electron shells of the molecules are rearranged, and reaction products are formed from the original particles.

In accordance with this, chemical physics is divided into two large sections: chemical kinetics and the theory of elementary acts.

Chemical kinetics studies questions about how a reaction

These particles can meet with each other, what external forces act on them, what equations can describe the change in the concentration of reacting particles over time, how the speed of a chemical process depends on the concentration of reactants (that is, substances that react), on temperature, and other conditions in which the chemical process takes place.

The theory of the elementary act of chemical transformation is considered as a result of the interaction of particles, changes in the configuration of electron shell molecules and the distances between the atoms included in the molecules.

In this article we will limit ourselves to discussing the kinetics of chemical reactions.

The main question of chemical kinetics is the question of the particle meeting of substances.

The particles encountered in the reaction are not necessarily molecules. These can be neutral atoms, ions, and any other particles. Therefore, as long as we are not talking about any specific substances -

Let a reaction take place in a certain volume between particles of two types $A$ and $B$, practically uniformly distributed throughout the entire volume. This reaction is called bimolecular. Both particles have the shape of a ball of radius $r$. Let each cubic centimeter contain $a_0$ particles of type $A$ and $b_0$ particles of type $B$. It is known that particles in a gas move chaotically, that is, their speeds change all the time both in magnitude and direction. However, the mean square speed of movement of the part $<v>$ is practically unchanged and is determined only by the temperature of the medium $T$ and the mass of the particle $m^*$):

$$<v> = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}}.$$

Thus, each part of the variety $A$ in time $t$ will cover a path equal on average to

$$s = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} t.$$

We will assume that particles of type $B$ are motionless, and the speed of part of type $A$ does not change after the next generation.

During the movement of a part of the type $A$ will collide with all particles of the type $B$, the centers of which are at a distance less than $2r$ from the trajectory of movement of its center, that is, with those particles that seem to fall into a cylinder offset by a point at figure 1. During the time $t$ one part of the type $A$ will collide with all particles of type $B$ located in the volume

$$V = 4\pi r^2 s.$$

A particle of type $A$ can, of course, encounter not only particles of type $B$, but also with other particles of type $A$, as well as with all other particles located in the medium. But these meetings are not of interest to us, since they cannot lead to a chemical reaction between substances $A$ and $B$.

We have already said that in every cubic centimeter of the medium there are $b_0$ particles of the $B$ variety; therefore, each part of the $A$ variety encountered $b_0V$ particles of the $B$ variety. In total, $1cm^3$ contains $a_0$ particles of the $A$ variety. Thus, the total number of $n$ encounters of particles of the $A$ and $B$ variety during the time $t$ will be

$$n = Va_0b_0.$$

There are $\frac{n}{t}$ such meetings per unit of time:

$$\frac{n}{t} = 4\pi r^2 \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} a_0b_0.$$

When calculating, we did not take into account that not only particles of the $A$ type move, but also particles of the $B$ type, that the speed of movement of a part changes after a new phase, that the distribution of a part over the volume can only be considered uniform on average. But a more accurate calculation taking into account these factors leads to a change in the found number of meetings by no more than 10%.

Of course, not every time parts $A$ and $B$ meet, a chemical transformation occurs; one part of the variety $A$ results in a change proportional to the number of encounters.

The number of chemical transformations per unit volume per unit time is usually called the rate of a chemical reaction. Chemical speed

Relationship (1) expresses the law of mass action: the rate of a chemical reaction at any time is proportional to the product of the concentration of the reacting substances at that time. The proportionality coefficient before the concentration product is called the rate constant of a chemical reaction at a given temperature; Let's denote it by the letter $K$.

In the reaction of interaction of two particles with the same mass, the value $K$ is equal to

$$K = 4\pi r^2 \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}}.$$

Equation (3) allows you to calculate the concentration of reagents and the rate of the process at any time and for any initial concentration of reagents.

However, a chemical process does not always represent one elementary reaction of the interaction of starting substances with another. The overall process can be more complex; it consists of several elemental reactions occurring simultaneously.

For example, reaction products can interact with each other or react with the starting materials. Obviously, in this case, equation (3) will no longer describe the kinetics of the process, and it is necessary to derive other, more complex equations in which the rate of all elemental reactions occurring before the time of the process arises.

In this regard, it is possible not only to determine the rate constants of elemental reactions, but also to make sure that the process is actually assumed to be a set of these reactions.
