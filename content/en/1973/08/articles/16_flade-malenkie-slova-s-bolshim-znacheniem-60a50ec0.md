Little words with big meaning

Small, almost imperceptible words, such as AND, OR, EXACTLY, can have a big meaning. And not only the small words themselves, but also their place in the sentence is very important (for example, the place of the words IF, SO, NOT).

In order not to make the same mistakes as our Klaus made, let's consider these small words with great meaning.

NO MORE, NO LESS, EXACTLY, ONE AND ONLY ONE, AT LEAST ONE

Consider the statement:
Which natural number corresponds to a point on the number axis.
This statement is correct. It expresses the same meaning as the statement:
Which natural number corresponds to a point on the number axis.
These sentences do not establish that each natural number corresponds to only one point on the number line. There may be several of these points, but in no case less than one.

As you can see, the information in these statements is much less than in the sentence:
Which natural number corresponds to a point on the number axis.
Now it is easy to find out whether the following sentences are true:

1. There are EXACTLY 3 single-digit natural numbers that are divisible by 3.
2. There are 3 single-digit natural numbers divisible by 3.
3. There are at least three single-digit natural numbers divisible by 3*).

The first statement, of course, is incorrect, since it has more than 3 single-digit natural numbers divisible by 3, namely 4 numbers: 0, 3, 6, 9. Statements 2 and 3 are correct, they have the same meaning.

In the same way, we can establish that the statement between 1 and 100 has at least 50 numerical natural numbers - incorrect, since in fact there are only 49 of them (that is, less than 50).

Surely there are readers among you who think that the statement

The GDR on January 1, 1971 had no more than 50 million inhabitants - this is incorrect, since the number of inhabitants of the GDR is only about 17 million. This argument is unconvincing: the turnover is “no more than 50 million.” means that it may be less, but in no case more, which means this statement is correct.

AND

We came across the conjunction “AND” in a sentence about dividing natural numbers (in Klaus’s written work). With this word we can combine several separate statements into one compound statement. Such a connection of statements is called defusion or conjunction. Let's look at an example.
The number 3 satisfies the inequality x < 7 And the number 3 satisfies the inequality x > 2.
There are two parts to this statement:
1) Number 3 satisfies the inequality x < 7 (true),
2) The number 3 satisfies the inequality x > 2 (true).
A union (conjunction) of statements will be true if and only if both parts from which it is formed are also true. In the case when one of the parts is true and the other is true, or, even more so, when both parts are true, the unification will be true. For example, the statement
The number 300 immediately following the number 200 AND the number 1000 immediately preceding the number 2000 is incorrect because both parts are incorrect.
0 is the smallest natural number AND 10000000000 is the smallest natural number - false because the second statement being combined is false.
Let's return to the question of dividing natural numbers.
Division a : b in the field of natural numbers is possible if the number a is a multiple of the number b AND b ≠ 0.
In this case, it is impossible to put OR together with AND.
It's easy to understand why the teacher was not satisfied with Klaus's answer:

"Division a : b in the domain of natural numbers is possible if a is a multiple of b OR if b is not zero." Indeed, empty, for example, a = 15, b = 4. Here b ≠ 0; the condition formed by Klaus is fulfilled, but division is completely impossible.

Check the following statements for yourself:
a) 461·7² = 3217 AND 414: 6 = 54.
b) Each triangle has at most one right angle AND at least one acute angle.
b) The number 19 satisfies BOTH the inequality 17 < x < 27 AND the inequality 19 < x < 29.
d) The number 714 satisfies AND, IN ADDITION, is divisible by 7.
We see that there are other connecting words, for example: AS... SO AND, AND, IN ADDITION, NOT ONLY... BUT ALSO others, which, when used together with the conjunction AND, do not change the validity of the statements.

IF A, TO B and IF B, TO A

When Klaus argued in his work that the number 374111 is divisible by 3, he wrote:

“If a number is divisible by 3, then the sum of its digits is also divisible by 3.

Hans wrote the opposite:

“Since the sum of the digits of a number is divisible by 3, then the sum is also divisible by 3,” and received the highest mark.

Klaus believed that both statements expressed the same meaning. Is this true?

First of all, we note that the above expressions are again combinations of statements formed using the expression “IF A, TO B”. This relationship of statements is also called implication.

In the associations of statements that we encountered earlier (conjunction, alternative, disjunction), the order of statements did not play any role. For implication this is no longer true. Let's look at an example.

Offer
“If a number is divisible by 2³, mono is divisible by 2.”
expressed in the form “IF A, THEN B”*). This proposal is correct. The first part of such a sentence (before the comma) is called the premise (condition), the second part is called the conclusion (statement).

Reversing the premise and conclusion, we get the following sentence:
“IF a number is divisible by 2, THEN it is also divisible by 2³,” and this sentence is incorrect.

Consider again the rationale given by Hans and Klaus:
Hans “IF the sum of the digits is divisible by 3, THEN the number itself is divisible by 3.”

Klaus (wrote a reverse sentence): “IF a number is divisible by 3, THEN the sum of the digits is divisible by 3.”

Both sentences are of the form “IF A, THEN B.” But nevertheless, it cannot be argued that they have the same meaning (although in this case both sentences are true); they will differ significantly from each other.

The condition formed by Hans reads:

The sum of the digits of the number 374111 is divisible by 3.”
Hence the conclusion is drawn: “The number 374111 can be divided by 3.”
Klaus, on the contrary, starts from the condition that the number is divisible by 3 and concludes that the sum of the digits of the number is also divisible by 3.

The reversal of a true statement may or may not be true, as was shown above. Therefore, it is always important to understand whether the appeal of a given statement will be correct.

If the reversal of the true statement is correct, then both statements can be combined using the phrase “THEN AND ONLY IF”*).

So, a number is divisible by 3 THEN AND ONLY THEN; WHEN the sum of the digits of a number is divisible by 3.

“NOT ALL...” and “ALL... NOT”

We move on to consider the final mistake Klaus made in his written work. The task was to turn an incorrect statement into a correct one by introducing a negation.

Let us first note the following:

1. The logical negation of an incorrect statement leads to a correct statement;
2. From a true statement, logical negation can be used to obtain an incorrect statement.

Let's form the negations of the following sentences:

(a) “247 is a prime number”;
(b) «$2^4 + 2^2 = 2^5$»;
(b) “a is greater than 7”;
(g) “The product of 17·11 is an integer”;
(d) “All prime numbers are odd.”

Negations of sentences (a), (6), (c), (d) can be formed immediately:

(a') “247 is not a prime number”;
(b') «$2^4 + 2^2 \neq 2^5$»;
(b'_1) "and not more than 7" or
(b'_2) “a is less than 7 or equal to 7”;

(d') “The product 17·11 is an odd number.”

As you can see, negations can be formed in different ways. Therefore, it is not so easy to give a definite rule for composing the negation of each individual statement. You can, however, use the phrase “It is not true that...” before a previously formed statement. Thus, the statement “It is not true that all prime numbers are odd” will be a well-formed negation of statement (e). And the multi-school students surveyed formulated this denial as follows:

Ralph: “All prime numbers are not odd”;

Inga: “Not all prime numbers are odd”;

Peter: “No odd number is odd”;

Bernd: “There is at least one prime number that is not odd.”

We know that statement (e) is false. So the negation of this statement must be true. But it can be verified that only Inga and Bernd formed correct statements.
