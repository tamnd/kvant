On May 27, 1973, a member of the editorial collection of the magazine «Kvant», one of the largest Soviet scientists, an outstanding organizer of science and social activities, Chairman of the Supreme Council of the RSFSR, Vice-President of the USSR Academy of Sciences, Deputy Director of the Institute of Atomic Energy named after. I. V. Kurchatova, Deputy Chairman of the Council for Coordinating the Activities of the Academies of Sciences of the Union Republics, Editor-in-Chief of the journal “Bulletin of the USSR Academy of Sciences”, Chairman of the Scientific Council on the complex problem “Methods of direct conversion of thermal energy into electrical energy”, Hero of Socialist Labor, laureate of the Lenin and State Prizes, Academician Mikhail Dmitrievich Millionshchikov.

M. D. Millionshchikov was born on January 16, 1913 in the city of Grozny. In 1932 he graduated from the Grozny Oil Institute, and in 1938 he completed graduate school at the Moscow Aviation Institute. Subsequently, he worked at the Central Aerodynamic Institute named after N. E. Zhukovsky, and from 1949 until the last days of his life - at the Institute of Atomic Energy named after I. V. Kurchatov.

Mikhail Dmitrievich began independent scientific research early. Even at the age of twenty, he investigated a number of problems in the theory of oil and gas filtration in a porous medium, directly related to the needs of the developing oil industry of the Soviet Union. While studying and working at the Moscow Aviation Institute, M. D. Millionshchikov began studying the theory of turbulence under the guidance of Academician A. N. Kolmogorov. The turbulent movement of liquids and gases is a weight propagation natural phenomenon. With this movement, the particles of the medium move in a disorderly manner, forming vortices. Turbulent motion plays a vital role in many technical processes (flow around an airplane wing or ship hull, fluid movement through pipes, etc.). However, theoretical analysis of such a movement is associated with great difficulties. M. D. Millionshchikov has developed a subtle mathematical apparatus that allows the study of turbulence to be advanced.

In 1949, at the invitation of Academician I.V. Kurchatov, M.D. Millionshchikov became involved in the development of problems of using nuclear energy. The research he conducted made a significant contribution to the development of Soviet nuclear technology. In particular, he obtained very important results in the field of creating high-temperature nuclear reactors.

M.D. Millionshchikov spent a lot of time on the issues of magnetic hydrodynamics, that is, the study of the movement of conducting gases in a magnetic field. Research on these issues is of great importance in the development of methods for the direct conversion of thermal energy into electrical energy.

In the works of M.D. Millionshchikov, the intuition of an engineer with a deep mathematical culture was considered.

Taking into account the major contribution made by M.D. Millionshchikov to science, the USSR Academy of Sciences elected him in 1953 as its corresponding member. In 1962 he was elected academician and vice-president of the Academy.

For more than thirty years, M.D. Millionshchikov spent time in a number of high educational institutions, training scientists.

The activities of M.D. Millionshchikov as Chairman of the Supreme Council of the RSFSR, as well as as Chairman of the Soviet Pagush Committee, uniting those studying in the struggle for peace, disarmament and international détente, enjoyed international fame.

The bright memory of Mikhail Dmitrievich Millionshchikov remains in the hearts of Soviet people.

3. "Pascal wheel"

On January 2, 1640, the Pascal family moved to Rouen, where Etienne Pascal received the position of integrator of the pronunciers, actually in charge of all affairs under the governor.

This appointment was preceded by curious events. E. Pascal took an active part in the performances of the wig RANT believers, for which he was threatened with imprisonment in the Bastille. He was forced to hide, but at that time Jacqueline fell ill with wasp, and her father, despite the terrible threat, visited her. Jacqueline recovered and even took part in a performance attended by Cardinal Richelieu. At the request of the horse actress, the cardinal forgave her father, but at the same time appointed him to the position. The former troublemaker had to implement the cardinal's policy (the reader of The Three Mysketeers will probably not be surprised by this treachery).

Now Etienne Pascal had a lot of counting work, in which his son constantly helps him. At the end of 1640, Blaise Pascal came up with the idea of ​​​​building a machine to free the mind from calculations “with the help of a pen and tokens.” The main idea arose quickly and remained unchanged throughout “... each wheel or rod of a certain category, making a movement of ten digits of arithmetic, causes the next to move only one digit.” However, a brilliant idea is only the first step. Its implementation required incomparably greater efforts. Later, in the “Foreknowledge” to those who “will have the curiosity to see and use the harpmetic machine,” Blaise Pascal modestly writes: “I did not save any time, no labor, no means to bring it to a state of being useful to you.” Behind these words stood five years of hard work, which led to the creation of a machine (“Pascal wheel,” as contemporaries said), which reliably, although rather slowly, performed four operations on five-digit numbers. Pascal made about fifty copies of the machine; here is just a list of the materials he tried: wood, ivory, ebony, brass, copper. He spent a lot of effort searching for the best craftsmen who could handle “the lathe, the file and the hammer,” and many times it seemed to him that they were unable to achieve the required precision. The testing system is carefully thought out, including transportation of 250 liters. Pascal does not forget about advertising: he receives the support of the clerk Segier, adds “royal privileges” (something like a patent), demonstrates the car many times in showrooms and even sends a copy to the Swedish Queen Christina. It is unknown whether it is finally being made by machine-made ones, but eight copies have survived to this day.

It’s amazing how brilliantly Pascal could do a variety of things. Relatively recently it became known that in 1623 Schiccard, a friend of Kepler, built a harpmetic machine, but Pascal's machine was much more perfect.

4. "Fear of Emptiness" and "The Great Experiment in Fluid Equilibrium"
At the end of 1646, rumors of amazing “Italian experiments with emptiness” reached Rouen.
The question of the existence of emptiness in nature worried the ancient Greeks; in their views on this issue, the diversity of points of view inherent in ancient Greek philosophy was manifested: Epicurus believed that emptiness can and does exist; Heron - that it can be obtained artificially, Empedocles - that it does not exist and has nowhere to come from, and, finally, Aristotle argued that “the nature of the bounty is emptiness.”
In the Middle Ages, calculations were simplified, since the truth of Aristotle’s teachings was established by practical people in a legal manner (back in the 17th century, speaking against Aristotle in France could lead to hard labor). The classic example of “Fear of Emptiness” shows water rising behind a piston without pressing into the empty space that forms. And suddenly an incident occurred with this example. When constructing the fountains at Florenzini, it was discovered that the water “did not want” to rise above 34 feet (10.3 meters). The perplexed parties turned for help to the death of Galius Galius (1564-1642), who believed that nature ceases to be rich in emptiness at a height exceeding 34 feet, but still offered to analyze the phenomenon to his students Evangelista Torricelli (1608-1647) and Vincenzo Vivani (1622-1703). It is likely that Torricelli (and perhaps Galius himself) came up with the idea that the height at which a liquid can rise in a pump is inversely proportional to its specific gravity. In particular, the water must rise to a height 13.3 times less than water, i.e. 76 cm. The experiment acquired a scale more favorable for laboratory conditions and was carried out by Vivani at the initiative of Torricelli. This experiment is well known, but let us remind you that a meter-long glass tube sealed at one end is filled with mercury, the open end is closed with a finger, after which the tube is turned over and lowered into a cup with mercury. If the report is a finger, then the level of mercury in the tube will drop to 76 cm. Torricelli makes two statements: firstly, the space above the mercury in the tube is empty (later it will be called the “Torricelli void”), and, secondly, the mercury does not pour out of the tube completely, since this is prevented by a column of air pressing on the surface of the mercury in the cup. By accepting these hypotheses, everything can be explained, but an explanation can be obtained by introducing special, rather complex, acting forces that prevent the formation of a vacuum. Accepting Torricelli's hypotheses was not easy. Only a few of his contemporaries accepted the effect of obtaining a vacuum on the air, but it was almost impossible to believe that the slightest exposure to heavy mercury in a tube. Let us mention that Galileo is trying to explain this effect by the properties of the liquid itself, and Descartes argued that the apparent vacuum is always filled with “the finest matter.”

Pascal enthusiastically repeats Italian experiments, coming up with many ingenious conditions for the creation. Eight such experiments are described in a treatise published in 1647. He does not limit himself to experiments with mercury, but experiments with water, oil, and red wine, for which he needed barrels along with necks and tubes about 15 m long. Effective experiments are carried out onto the streets of Rouen, delighting its residents. (Engravings with a wine barometer are still popularly reproduced in physics textbooks.)

It began one night in the spring of 1658, when, during a terrible attack of toothache, Pascal remembered one of Mersen’s unsolved problems about the cyclode. He notices that focused thinking distracts from pain. By the morning, he had already proven his goal with a series of results about the cyclide and... was healed of his toothache. At first, Pascal considers randomness a sin and is not going to write down the results. Later, under the influence of the Duke of Roanne, he changes his mind; for eight days, according to Gilberte Perrier, “he did nothing but write while his hand could write.”

Then in June 1658, Pascal, as was often done then, organized a competition, inviting the greatest mathematicians to solve six problems about the cyclode. The greatest successes were added by Christan Huygens (1629-1695), who solved four problems, and John Wallis (1616-1703), for whom all problems were solved with some problems. But the work of the unknown Amos Dettonville was recognized as the real one. Huygens later admitted that “this work is done so subtly that it is impossible to add to it.” Note that “Amos Dettonville” consists of the same letters as “Louis de Montalte”. This is how a new pseudo-mime for Pascal was invented. Dettonville's works were published for the premium price of 60 pistoles.

First of all, let us quote Pascal’s words about the curve called a cyclude or roulette: “The roulette is so linear that after the straight line and the circle there is no more frequently occurring line; . . . or it is nothing other than the path described in the air by the nail of the wheel, when it rolls with its own motion from the moment the nail began to rise from the ground, until the non-prime quality of the wheel brings it back to the ground after the end of a whole revolution, considering that the wheel is a perfect circle, the nail is a point on its circumference, and the earth is ideally flat” (see Fig. 7). Pascal believed that Mersenne discovered the cycle, although in fact it was Galileo who discovered it. Initial interest in this curve was stimulated by the fact that a number of interesting problems for it could be solved in an elementary way. The problems considered by Pascal no longer allow elementary solutions (the area and center of gravity of an arbitrary segment of a cyclonde, the volumes of the corresponding bodies of rotation, etc.). Using these problems, Pascal developed essentially everything that was necessary to construct differential and integral calculus in a general form. Leibniz, who shares the credit with Newton as the creators of this theory, writes that when, on the advice of Huygen, he became acquainted with the works of Pascal, he was “illuminated with a new light,” he was surprised how close Pascal was to constructing a general theory, and suddenly stopped, as if “there were veils before his eyes.”

It was characteristic of the works that anticipated the advent of differential and integral calculus that the intuition of their authors was far ahead of the possibility of conducting rigorous proofs; the mathematical language was not developed enough to transfer the train of thought onto paper. A solution was found later by introducing new concepts and special symbols. Pascal did not resort to any symbolism, but he mastered the language so masterfully that at times it seems that he simply did not need it. Let us cite the statement of N. Bourbaki: “Walis in 1655 and Pascal in 1658 each compiled languages ​​of an algebraic nature for their own use, in which, without writing down a single formula, they give formulations that can be immediately, as soon as their mechanism is understood, written in the formulas of integral calculus. Pascal's language is particularly clear and precise; and if it is not always clear why he abandoned the use of algebraic notation not only of Descartes, but also of Vieta, one still cannot help but admire his skill, which could only manifest itself on the basis of perfect command of the language.” I would like to say that here Pascal the writer helped Pascal the mathematician.
