Fluid Equilibrium Tract

We place here the first chapter from Pascal's treatise, in which he formed his landmark law on the conditions of fluid equilibrium, substantiated the principle of operation of hydraulic machines and unleashed the so-called hydrostatic paradox (all these problems are discussed in the physics course for the sixth grade). The full title of this work is as follows: “Treats on the equilibrium of liquids and all masses of air, the content of the explanation of the principles of various natural phenomena that have not yet been well known and, in particular, those that are attributed to the richness of the void.” It was published in 1663, that is, a year after the death of the author. Pascal wrote it in 1651-1653.

In Russian, this treatise, translated by A. N. Dolgov, was published in the book “Principles of Hydrostatics; Archimedes, Stavin, Galileo, Pascal,” published in 1933 by the State Technical Publishing House. The figure given in the article are taken from the treatise. The publication was prepared by V. A. Leshkovtsev.

CHAPTER I

That liquids have a weight corresponding to the height of their structure

If you attach several vessels to the wall, one like the one on figure the first, another inclined, as on the second, then a wider one, as on the third, then a narrow one, as on the fourth, then one that is nothing more than a narrow tube, adjacent at the bottom to a wide, but having almost no height, vessel, as on figure the fifth, fill them all with water to the same height, make the same holes at the bottom, which are closed with stoppers to retain water, experience will show that the same force is required to prevent these stoppers from falling out, although the water in these different vessels is in the balance

various quantities. This happens because the water has the same height in all vessels, and the measure of this force is the weight of the water contained in the first vessel, which is uniform in shape. And if this quantity of water weighs one hundred pounds *), then a force of one hundred pounds is required to hold each of the stoppers, even at the fifth vessel, although the water contained in it does not weigh one ounce**).

To check this accurately, you need to close the hole of the fifth vessel with a round piece of wood and wrap it around it, like a pump piston. (The piston must enter and pass through the hole with such precision as not to get stuck and at the same time to prevent the escape of water.) Then attach to the middle of this piston a thread that would pass through this thin tube, tie it to one arm of the yoke of the scale, and hang a weight of one hundred pounds on the other arm. Then we shall see the complete equilibrium of this hundred-pound weight with the water in the thin tube, which weighs one ounce. If you slightly reduce the load of one hundred pounds, then the water will lower the piston, and the next amount.

*) 1 pound equals 453.6 g.
**) 1 ounce equals 28.35 g.

Consequently, the arm of the yoke of the scale to which it is attached will lift the one on which the load hangs of a little less than a hundred pounds. If the water freezes, but the ice does not stick to the vessel, then to keep it in balance, it will be enough to have only one ounce on the other arm of the scale. If we bring fire closer to the vessel and grow ice, then there are already a hundred pounds to balance the weight of this ice melted into water, although we have only one ounce of it.

The same thing will happen if the holes that are closed with stoppers are on the side or in the upper part of the vessels; It will be even easier to check this, exactly as follows.

You need to take a vessel, closed on all sides, make two holes in its upper part, one very narrow and the other wider, and attach tubes of the same size as the holes above both (Fig. VI). If you now insert a piston into a wide tube and a thin thread of water into it, it is easy to see that a larger weight will have to be placed on the piston so that the weight of the water in the thin tube does not push it up, just as in the first experiments a force of one hundred pounds was used to prevent the piston from being pushed down.

If you drink water into a tube at double height, then to balance the water, a double weight will act on the piston. In the same way, if you make the answer in which the piston is inserted twice the size, then you will need to double the force required to hold the double piston. From this it is clear that the force needed to prevent water from flowing out of the answer is proportional to the height of the water, and not to the width of the vessel, and that the measure of this force is always the weight of the water contained in a column with a height equal to the height of the water, and a base equal to the size of the answer.

What I said about water applies to all other types of liquid.
