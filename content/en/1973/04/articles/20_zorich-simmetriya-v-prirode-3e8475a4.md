Symmetry in nature

Have you ever wondered why a horse, a hare, a lizard, a fish has a right and a left, but a jellyfish or, say, a daisy does not? Or why does the left hemisphere of our brain control the right side of the body and vice versa? Why are the marble tiles that cover the walls and floors of metro stations laid out in the form of an ornament - monotonous, repeated many times figure? Is a symmetrical building always beautiful? If these and similar questions have not yet arisen in your mind, they will certainly arise as soon as you get acquainted with the first chapters of the book by V. Rydnik “From the Chamomile to the Antiworld” *). As you continue reading the book, the questions will become more and more profound and will concern such complex problems as the concepts of space and time, the structure of molecules and atoms, the taxonomy of elementary particles, the properties of the world and the antiworld, etc.

Together with the ninth-grader, on whose behalf the story is told, and his friends, united in a “Seminar on the Study of Symmetry,” you will try to measure a square while looking in the mirror, determine the nature of symmetry figure on both sides of your room, admire samples of crystals in the geological museum, and even read palindromes - “reverse” poems.

This effect of the reader’s participation in what the book talks about is its great advantage. How did the author manage to achieve such an effect? First of all, the chosen method of presentation. The book is written in the style of conversations, dating back to ancient scientific and philosophical*

Always related either to the answers of the father, an experienced theoretical physicist, to his son’s irrelevant questions, or to a heated argument between friends, or to the children’s attempts to express information in simple, sometimes naive words, gleaned from books. Lively speech, dialogue, non-trivial comparisons, author's remarks - all this allows the reader to be untempted without overworking himself, arriving at complex, sometimes dry and formal sections of physics.

The book cannot be read through, which is called “absorbed”; Each chapter requires attention, comprehension, and often work with a pencil. These are, for example, three chapters under the same title “On the Laws of Symmetry,” as well as the final sections, which introduce, unfortunately, too briefly, some aspects of the physics of the microworld. When reading the book, questions arise and are explained in subsequent chapters; others remain open for the simple reason that science has not yet answered them, as the author openly reports. His book, unlike many popular science books, does not create the impression of completeness of science; on the contrary, it focuses on solved problems (for example, why in our world there are incomparably more particles than antiparticles, or why only “left-handed” amino acids survived in the process of evolution on our planet) - this is its second important advantage.

The book gives a clear idea of ​​the unity of science, of the inextricable connection of all its fields: mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, geology, humanities, right down to physics and science. In fact, did the young Évariste Galois think, when creating an abstract section of mathematics - group theory, that she would try to find now regerograd-biology, which would deny the need to use physics, chemistry, mathematics, in particular, the same theory of symmetry.

But related sciences, as well as chemistry and biology, are touched upon here only to a minimum—the book is based on physics, and physics is widely represented in it. Taking symmetry theory as the core not in the narrow, “geometric” sense, but in a broader and deeper, “physical” sense, the author examines the sections of mechanics, electrodynamics, relativity theory, atomic physics, and elementary particle physics.
