About prime numbers

A. D. Bendukidze

This note talks about coefficients about prime numbers. People became interested in these numbers back in ancient times. Many interesting questions have been raised regarding prime numbers. It is noteworthy that the answers to some of them are not known even today.

1. Let's take a series of natural numbers:

1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ...
There is no most number in this series. How is this clear? This means that no matter how large the natural number $n$ is, there is an even larger number. In fact, this will be the next natural number, the number $n+1$. So, there are infinitely many natural numbers. This is precisely what mathematicians mean when they say that the set of natural numbers is infinite.

2. The smallest number in the series of natural numbers is the number 1. It has only one divisor - this is 1. The next numbers are 2. This number has two divisors: 1 and 2. Next comes 3. It also has two divisors: 1 and 3. But the next number, the number 4, has three divisors: 1, 2, 4. The heel has two divisors, the six has four, and so on. A number can have many divisors. For example, the number 60-12 has divisors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60.

A number that has only two divisors: 1 and this number itself is called prime; if a number has more than two divisors, then it is called composite. So, for example, the numbers 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 are prime, and 4, 6, 8, 9, 10 are composite. What about the number 1? After all, it only has one divisor! This number is neither prime nor composite.

Thus, the set of natural numbers naturally develops into three parts or, as they say, into three subsets. The first of these subsets consists of one number, namely, the number 1; the second contains all the prime numbers, and the third contains the composite numbers. Note that each natural number falls into one and only one of these subsets, that is, as is usually said, these subsets are pairwise disjoint.

3. It is easy to prove that the set of composite numbers is infinite. In fact, there are already numbers of the form $2^n$, where $n$ takes the values ​​2, 3, 4,..., infinitely many, but besides these there are other composite numbers!

Well, what can we say about prime numbers - is their set finite or infinite? The famous ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, who lived in the 3rd century AD, proved that the set of prime numbers is infinite.

Look at how Euclid argues when proving this fact.

Let $p$ be some prime number. Let us prove that there exists a prime number greater than $p$. In fact, all prime numbers up to $p$ are variable, including $p$ itself, and to this product we add one. We get the following number:

$$2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \dots p + 1.$$
