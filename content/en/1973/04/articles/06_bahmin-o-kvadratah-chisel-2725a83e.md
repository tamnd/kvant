⟦folio 29⟧

Figures of constant width have many remarkable properties. For example, the length of the curve bounding any figure of constant width 1 is equal to π, that is, equal to the length of a circle with diameter 1 (Barbier’s theorem). Further, of all figures of constant width 1, the circle has the largest (and not the smallest!) area, and the Reuleaux triangle has the smallest area; in any case, it is not difficult to verify that the area of ​​the Reuleaux triangle is equal to $\frac{\pi - \sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0,7048$ - noticeably less than the area of ​​the circle, equal to $\frac{\pi}{4} \approx 0,7854$. Let us also note that two parallel support lines drawn to a figure of constant width 1 have two common points with this figure, and the segment connecting these points is perpendicular to the support line (and, therefore, has length 1; Fig. 14). It follows from this that in any figure of constant width 1, a segment of length 1 can make a full revolution. The proof of the statements formulated here can be found in the above-mentioned book “Convex Figures” (see also magazine «Kvant» No. 3, 1971, p. 21).

So, if the above assumption were true, then the smallest (by area) convex figure in which a segment of length 1 could make a full revolution would be the Reuleaux triangle (and not a circle at all). But it turns out (as it may seem to countries at first glance) that the Reuleaux triangle is not the smallest figure. Such the smallest figure is an equilateral triangle with height 1 (its area is equal to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 0,5773$, that is, the smallest area of ​​the Reuleaux triangle). Proof of this fact can be found on pages 256-258 of the book Convex Figures.

About squares of numbers

Here are some interesting facts about squared numbers. If the last two digits of the square are the same, but not zero, then these must be two fours. The smallest such square is 144. There can be three fours at the end - this is the number 1444. But squares with four fours at the end no longer exist. There were not many numbered squares with all the same numbers and those in which all the numbers were odd. Try to prove it.

Among two-digit numbers there is a number that has any properties. This number is 13. Its square is 169. Reading this number from right to left, we get 961 - a number whose square root is 31, that is, 13 read backwards. And finally, the sum of the digits of 169 is equal to 16, and the sum of the digits of 13 is 4, that is, the square roots of 16. Among the two-digit numbers there is only one number with the same set of properties, try to find it.

There are squares that contain all the numbers, and every number on pages 256-258 of the book “Convex Figures”.
