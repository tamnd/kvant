Pothenot's problem

L. S. Khrenov

If three points A, B and C are given on the plane (that is, the relative position of these points is known), then to determine the position of the fourth point M it is usually enough to know two angles: $\angle ABM = \alpha$ and $\angle BMC = \beta$. This problem is known as the Potenot problem. It will arise, for example, when determining the location of a ship from which three lighthouses are visible, the coordinates of which are known, as well as in many other cases.

Geometric solution of the Potenot problem

Potenot's problem coincides with the construction of the arc AB (Fig. 1), from any point M of which the segment AB is visible at an angle $\alpha$, and the arc BC, from the point of which the segment BC is visible at an angle $\beta$. To do this, it is enough to set aside an angle $\alpha$ from the straight line AB and construct a perpendicular AP to the resulting straight line AV, and construct a perpendicular to the segment AB in its middle. The point $O_1$ of intersection of these perpendiculars will be the center of the circle passing through points A, B and M, and the line AB will be the chord of the arc, together at the angle $\alpha$. Similarly, the center $O_2$ of a circle passing through points B, C and M with another BC is constructed, together at the angle $\beta$.

Intersect two circles constructed in this way and determine the position of point M, since they cannot have more than two common points. However, of these, according to the construction, point B is the second, the sought-after point M is the second. Consequently, the Potenot problem has a wave definition and a unique solution, except for the case when both circles merge into one passing through points A, B, C and M (Fig. 2). In this case, the required point M lies on a circle passing through points A, B and C, and to determine the position of point M, you need to know some other distances.

However, despite the simplicity of the geometric solution to the Potenot problem, it is not used in practice.

In practice, to solve the Potenot problem, similar *) or graphical methods are used using mensula, kipregel or aldada and compass.

*) The first similar solution was proposed in 1616 by the Dutch astrologer and mathematician V. Sneglyus (1580-1626); later, in 1692, the French mathematician L. Potenot (1660-1732) proposed a more successful solution to this problem, which is why this problem is called his.

The mensula is a table for drawing a plan. It consists of a shativ and a tablet - a square drawing board, which is mounted on a stand. The stand has three lifting screws allowing for alignment on the stand. The stand together with the tablet is attached to the tripod using a screw. Stands can be wooden or metal. The centering fork (see Fig.nok) is used to center a point on the tablet above the corresponding point on the terrain: a plumb line is suspended from the lower end of the fork, the upper end of the fork is on the same vertical as the lower one.

And yes, yes, I licked it for sighting from the mensula point to the terrain point. It consists of a ruler on which the transverse scale is engraved, and two diopters with slits and hairs; A cylindrical level is usually placed on the alidade ruler to bring the scale tablet into a horizontal position. Telery, together with alidads with diopters, uses Cypregel.

The kipregel consists of a ruler with a transverse scale and level, and a rubbing tube rotating on the axis of the column so that the plane described by the sighting axis of the tube (collimation) passes through the beveled edge of the kipregel ruler. On the mensula, a horizontal position of the terrain should be obtained, therefore, to change the angles of inclination, kipregels with vertical circles are made, with the help of which the angles of inclination are changed with an accuracy of 1°. Two additional threads (rangefinder), located symmetrically about the middle axis, are visible in the field of view of the kipregel tube. They are used to determine distances on the ground.

The compass (improved compass) is used to orient the compass according to the cardinal points. The compass has the form of a round or rectangular box with degree divisions; a magnetic needle rotates in the center. The compass is always constructed in such a way that the line connecting the divisions 0°-0° or 0°-180° and passing through the center of the compass is parallel to the outer edges of the box.

Graphical solution to the Potenot problem

If there are three points on the scale tablet corresponding to those points on the terrain (that is, if there is, for example, a map of the area), you can determine the position of the fourth point by observing only in it, that is, solve the Potenot problem.

More than 100 different methods have been proposed for graphically solving the Potenot problem *). They are based on the orientation of the scale tablet. Let the three points $a, b$ and $c$ (Fig. 3), which make up the triangle $abc$ on the scale tablet $Q$, correspond to the threes $A, B$ and $C$ on the terrain. A tablet $Q$ is oriented if it is positioned so that $ab \parallel AB, ac \parallel AC$ and $bc \parallel BC$.

In this case, the directions passed through $a, b$ and $c$ when sighting at the corresponding terrain points will intersect at one point. To prove this, we construct the point $m$ as the intersection point of the lines $Aa$ and $Bb$. Similarly, by reducing $\Delta ABC$ so that $AB$ goes into $ab$, we get $\Delta abc'$, but since $ac \parallel AC \parallel ac'$ and $bc \parallel BC \parallel bc'$, the points $c'$ and $c$ must coincide, and the lines $Aa, Bb$ and $Cc$ will all intersect in point $m$, corresponding on the tablet to point $M$, from which we sight at points $A, B, C$.
