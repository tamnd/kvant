State awards
1972

In 1972, the Central Committee of the CPSU and the Council of Ministers of the USSR awarded 23 State Prizes for outstanding research in the field of science and technology. Two of them were awarded for work in the field of physics.

Academician of the Academy of Sciences of the Byelorussian SSR Boris Ivanovich Stepanov, together with his collaborators Anatoly Nikolaevich Rubinov and Vasily Andreevich Mostovnikov, were awarded the State Prize “for a series of works on the study of the phenomenon of optical generation in disorders of complex organic compounds and the creation on their basis of a new type of lasers with a continuously tunable radiation frequency in a wide range of the spectrum.” These works relate to one of the youngest areas of modern physics - quantum electronics, which has provided humanity with fundamentally new sources of electromagnetic radiation, the so-called lasers.

A laser differs from all other radiation sources in that it produces a narrow, almost non-diverging beam of rays. In this case, the laser radiation is confined to an unusually narrow wavelength range. Therefore, it can be considered, as physicists say, almost monochromatic (which means “one-color”). The energy concentration in the laser beam is extremely high. Further, the metal cannot be exposed to such a beam. Since the divergence of the laser beam is very small, such a beam is almost not attenuated in irradiated media and can be detected at enormous distances from the source.

 Until recently, physicists dealt only with lasers in which the radiation source was atoms of various crystals (for example, ruby) or atoms of various gases (for example, carbon dioxide or argon). However, back in 1960, that is, before the creation of the first lasers, ideas were expressed that led to the amplification of light by complex molecules of organic substances. Developing these ideas in extensive theoretical research, Academician Stepanov showed in 1964 that the source of laser radiation in liquid media can be the formation of complex organizational crystals (rhodamine, cryptocin, etc.). In 1966, Stepanov and his collaborators managed to confirm their conclusions experimentally. They were the first to apply laser radiation to organic dye molecules. Subsequently, liquid lasers “Rainbow” were designed and experiments were carried out at the All-Union Exhibition of National Economic Achievements in Moscow and were highly appreciated.

The theory of liquid lasers created by V. I. Stepanov allows us to imagine which of the many different types of organic dyes may have the properties necessary to generate laser beams. It turned out that the substance also added radiation at any wavelength in the region of visible light, as well as invisible infrared and ultraviolet rays. By changing the dye concentration, solution temperatures, or optical characteristics of the laser device, you can smoothly change the length of the emitted electromagnetic waves (as they say, altering part of the liquid laser radiation).

Liquid lasers also have a number of important advantages. The efficiency coefficient (that is, the proportion of energy emitted by the laser beam relative to the energy of the exciting radiation) for such lasers is very high; about 30-40%; and in some cases it reaches 60%. The energy of a single impulse can reach 100 joules.

Liquid lasers will certainly find many useful advantages in spectroscopy, photochemistry, biology, medicine and engineering.
