About the profession of mathematics

Below we publish excerpts from the well-known brochure by A. N. Kolmogorov “On the Profession of Mathematics” *)
The publication was prepared by M. Smolyansky and T. Kislova

1. The importance of mathematical methods in sciences such as mechanics, physics or astronomy is well known. Everyone also knows that mathematics is necessary in the practical work of engineers and technicians. Basic knowledge of geometry and the ability to use alphabetic formulas are necessary for almost every craftsman or skilled worker. But less clear to many is the question of what it means to have a specialization in mathematics and to engage in mathematics itself as a primary profession.

Many people imagine the matter in such a way that textbooks and mathematical reference books have already collected a wave of sufficient formulas and rules for solving all kinds of mathematical problems encountered in practice. Further, very educated people often express bewilderment: is it really possible to do anything new in mathematics?

Pozmota and mathematicians are sometimes imagined as a boring person who has memorized a large number of formulas and theorems, and they believe that his task is to transfer the memorized, ready-made knowledge to others.

In all this, the only thing that is true is that the mathematical information imparted in secondary school and at the first stages of studying mathematics in higher education was added by man a long time ago. But even these simple mathematicians

The proposer of mathematics in both higher and secondary schools is required not only to have a thorough knowledge of the science he proposes. Only a person who is passionate about it and perceives it as a living, developing science can offer a good proposal for mathematics. Probably, many high school students know how exciting and, thanks to this, easy and accessible mathematics becomes with such students.

To an even greater extent, independence and the ability to take a new approach to the mathematical formulation of a task are necessary for those who use mathematics in solving technical problems. This applies to every engineer's job. But since this requires mathematical knowledge and not everyone has the ability, most of our research technical institutes and even some large factories began to successfully attract mathematicians to work together with engineers on technical problems.

The idea of ​​mathematics as a complete science, once built on its theoretical foundations, is erroneous. In fact, mathematics is enriched with completely new theories and rebuilds in response to new requests from mechanics (nonlinear wheels

In addition, in the depths of mathematics itself, after the accumulation of a large number of disparate special problems solved by partial methods, new general theories are created that illuminate these problems from other points of view and make it possible to solve them using monotonous methods. For example, the methods of “functional analysis” emerging before our eyes relate to mathematical analysis (which was created back in the 17th-18th centuries and is taught in all higher technical institutions) approximately in the same way as algebra relates to arithmetic. The so-called “operator methods” of functional analysis have already found wide application in modern physics and technology.

At present, there is a particular shortage of mathematicians capable of supervising large-scale computational work.

There are many problems that require calculations beyond the capabilities of one person to obtain a numerical result. Calculation of elastic stresses in dense materials, filtration of water under dense materials, resistance experienced by aircraft during flight, or projectile trajectories are typical examples of such problems. For a long time now, at scientific institutes, design organizations and factories in need of solving similar problems, computing bureaus with many tens of advanced engineers began to appear, equipped with arithmeters and computers that require, in order to perform arithmetic operations on multi-digit numbers, just typing them using keys and pressing the corresponding button (+, -, ×, :). However, modern science and technology are faced with such tasks that, at the same time, the level of organization of computational work requires many months, and sometimes years of dozens of computational work. This situation caused the rapid development of modern “machine mathematics”.

The design and maintenance of modern computers has become a broad engineering specialty that students receive at the relevant departments of technical schools. To work in old-style computer offices or to enter data into a modern computer, general secondary education and industrial training are sufficient. In order to bring the solution of mathematical problems to the stage after which they can be transferred to a computing office or to a computer to obtain numerical results, many people with deep mathematical knowledge are needed.

The theory of “computational methods” of mathematics has now developed into a big science, and the need for specialists proficient in these methods is increasing with the development of “machine mathematics”. We are faced with unique problems of “programming,” that is, bringing the calculation process to a form that allows it to fully automate the solution of a certain type of problem on machines.

But one should not think that in mathematics it is more difficult than in other sciences to achieve the possibility of doing something new. Experience speaks rather differently: capable mathematicians, as a rule, begin independent scientific research at a very young age. Numerical average human abilities are quite enough to, with good guidance or good books, not only master high school mathematics, but also understand, for example, the beginnings of differential and integral calculus. However, when it comes to choosing mathematics as a major, it is quite natural to want to test your “mathematics aptitude.” After all, there is no doubt that different people perceive mathematical wonders, solve mathematical problems, or, at a higher level, come to new mathematical discoveries with different speed, ease and success. What are these abilities? First of all, it should be emphasized that success in mathematics is least of all based on mechanical memorization of a large number of facts, individual formulas, etc. A good memory in mathematics, as in any other matter, is useful, but most renowned mathematicians did not possess any special, outstanding memory.

In particular, magicians who fill in long series of multi-digit numbers and add or change them in their heads cannot at all serve as an example of people with good mathematical abilities in the service sense of the word.

The ability to perform algebraic calculations, that is, the ability to transform complex alphabetic expressions, find successful ways to solve equations that do not fit standard rules, and the like, is more in line with the abilities that are often required of a mathematician in service scientific work.

It is even pleasant to think that an exceptionally large development of such computational or, as is sometimes said, “algorithmic” abilities is characteristic of one of several basic types of mathematical talent. This kind of ability is required to overcome the difficulties of school algebra, and above all - in factoring algebraic expressions.

Further, the main application of this type of ability is solving equations. However, wherever possible, mathematicians strive to make the gaps they study geometrically visual. In high school it is quite clear how useful graphs are for studying the properties of functions. Therefore, the reader will not be surprised by the statement that geometric imagination, or, as they say, “geometric intuition” plays a big role in research work in almost all areas of mathematics, even the most important ones.

At school, it is usually very difficult to visualize spatial functions. For example, you must already be a very good mathematician in order to close your eyes and clearly imagine what the intersection of the surface of a cube with a plane passing through the center of the cube and perpendicular to it looks like.

Dams of large power plants take up space and even years of work of special computing bureaus. This situation naturally led to the need to improve computer technology. First of all, along with ordinary arithmetic machines, “small computers” have become widespread, automatically performing even arithmetic operations on multi-digit numbers. Variables of two eight-digit numbers take forty seconds on such a machine.

When using these machines, the computer is also forced to record the results of each action and therefore re-enter them into the machine. Over the past twenty years, work has expanded widely to create “large computers” that, without human intervention, perform long series of arithmetic operations.

The operating program of such a machine is set by punched paper tape. The machine itself performs arithmetic operations in the specified order, records the process results, uses them in further calculations and, finally, produces the final result, printed on tape or cards, or even printed.

At first, such complex computers used mechanical elements such as the wheels of a conventional adding machine and electromagnetic relays that closed and opened the current driving the elements of the machine. A complete revolution in computer technology took place about ten years ago, when it was shown that it was possible to do without mechanical movement of machine elements altogether, replacing them with electronic tubes (dummies, triodes, etc.) and their combinations (triggers, etc.). Thanks to this, it became possible to perform, for example, several thousand operations of multiplying multi-digit numbers in one second. Somewhat later, vacuum tubes began to replace the resulting elements with significant size; to “memorize” a large number of intermediate data (up to several hundred thousand), magnetic bases were introduced, etc. It became possible to make calculations requiring, for example, 20 million operations to predict the weather for the next day based on data from meteorological stations, calculate the trajectory of a projectile in a time less than its flight time, etc.

Large computers were sometimes used and built specifically for one purpose (for example, to predict the weather), but more often they were of a universal nature, that is, they were intended to solve a wide variety of problems. In this case, they are located in “computer centers” serving various scientific and technical institutions that do not have their own large computers. Often computers are connected to devices that automatically control one or another process.

If managing a fast-paced process requires complex calculations based on data obtained during this process, then without the speed of a computer such a thing would not be feasible at all. The scope of application of such machine controls is growing rapidly.

Control machines are in many ways similar to control mechanical elements that arose naturally and are studied by recently emerging sciences: computer science theories and cybernetics, which are largely mathematical and pose many new demands on pure mathematics.
