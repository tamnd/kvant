On the other hand, the rock may contain lead, which appeared simultaneously with other elements and got into the mineral during the formation of the solid earth's crust. This lead is called primary. On the face of primary lead leads to an overestimation of the age of the rock. Therefore, to correctly determine the age, it is necessary to make sure that radon has not leaked from the mineral, and to be able to determine the proportion of primary lead in the rock.

Lead from radioactive production (radiogenic lead) accumulates not only after the decay of the isotope $^{238}$U, but is also formed as a result of radioactive transformations $^{235}$U and $^{232}$Th. The $^{206}$Pb isotope is a $^{238}$U flux, and the $^{207}$Pb and $^{208}$Pb ​​isotopes complete the decay target $^{235}$U and $^{232}$Th, respectively.

Natural lead also contains the light isotope $^{204}$Pb, which does not accumulate for the purpose of radioactive transformations of any of the natural radioactive elements. Where did the “light” lead come from? There is only one answer - lead with a mass number of 204 is formed simultaneously with other elements on Earth.

The isotopic composition of lead without radiogenic impurities was determined by analyzing iron meteorites. Such meteorites do not contain uranium and thorium, sources of radiogenic lead. Meteoric lead contains 10 parts of $^{206}$Pb, 10 parts of $^{207}$Pb and 29 parts of $^{208}$Pb for one part of the $^{204}$Pb isotope.

Isotope analysis allows us to estimate the amount of primary lead in the rock based on the amount of the $^{204}$Pb isotope. Thus, if lead lacks the $^{204}$Pb isotope or contains a small fraction of it, then almost all of the lead is radiogenic.
