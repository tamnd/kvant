Pothenot's problem

L. S. Khrenov

If three points A, B and C are given on the plane (that is, the relative position of these points is known), then to determine the position of the fourth point M it is usually enough to know two angles: $\angle ABM = \alpha$ and $\angle BMC = \beta$. This problem is known as the Potenot problem. It will arise, for example, when determining the location of a ship from which three lighthouses are visible, the coordinates of which are known, as well as in many other cases.

Geometric solution of the Potenot problem

Potenot's problem coincides with the construction of the arc AB (Fig. 1), from any point M of which the segment AB is visible at an angle $\alpha$, and the arc BC, from the point of which the segment BC is visible at an angle $\beta$. To do this, it is enough to set aside an angle $\alpha$ from the straight line AB and construct a perpendicular AP to the resulting straight line AV, and construct a perpendicular to the segment AB in its middle. The point $O_1$ of intersection of these perpendiculars will be the center of the circle passing through points A, B and M, and the line AB will be the chord of the arc, together at the angle $\alpha$. Similarly, the center $O_2$ of a circle passing through points B, C and M with another BC is constructed, together at the angle $\beta$.

Intersect two circles constructed in this way and determine the position of point M, since they cannot have more than two common points. However, of these, according to the construction, point B is the second, the sought-after point M is the second. Consequently, the Potenot problem has a wave definition and a unique solution, except for the case when both circles merge into one passing through points A, B, C and M (Fig. 2). In this case, the required point M lies on a circle passing through points A, B and C, and to determine the position of point M, you need to know some other distances.

However, despite the simplicity of the geometric solution to the Potenot problem, it is not used in practice.

In practice, to solve the Potenot problem, similar *) or graphical methods are used using mensula, kipregel or aldada and compass.

*) The first similar solution was proposed in 1616 by the Dutch astrologer and mathematician V. Sneglyus (1580-1626); later, in 1692, the French mathematician L. Potenot (1660-1732) proposed a more successful solution to this problem, which is why this problem is called his.
