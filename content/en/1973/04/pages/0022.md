Fig. 1. The main chain of radioactive transformations of uranium. The abscissa axis shows the number of neutrons in the nucleus, and the ordinate axis shows the number of protons (atomic number of the element). Arrows pointing down correspond to the α decay process. In this process, the atomic nucleus emits a fast gel nucleus (α particles), losing two protons and two neutrons. Arrows pointing up indicate β decay. In this process, the nucleus becomes a proton and the atomic number increases by one.

In order to measure periods of the order of billions of years, there must be such an order of magnitude. Only under this condition are radioactive atoms preserved in the mineral to this day.

The first radioactive decay process that was used to determine the age of minerals was the conversion of uranium isotopes to lead.

Natural uranium consists of three types of atoms of different masses - the isotopes $^{238}$U, $^{235}$U and $^{234}$U. The most common isotope of uranium is $^{238}$U. In uranium isolated from any rock, it accounts for 99.3%.

Let's follow the chain of radioactive transformations of the isotope $^{238}$U

(Fig. 1). The $^{238}$U atoms slowly turn into lead ($^{206}$Pb) and gel ($^{4}$He) atoms. It takes 4.5 billion years for the initial amount of $^{238}$U to be converted into lead and gel. In this case, 14 radioactive transformations occur, as a result of which each decayed atom of uranium produces one atom of stable lead and eight atoms of gel:

$$\frac{238}{92}U \rightarrow 8_{2}^{4}He + \frac{206}{82}Pb + 6_{-1}^{0}e.$$

If a piece of dense rock, for example, a boundary, is ground, the resulting powder is dissolved in acid, lead and uranium are isolated from the solution, and then we determine how many atoms of the isotope $^{206}$Pb come to one atom $^{238}$U, then from these data we can determine the age of granite. Let us denote by the letter $N$ the number of uranium atoms, and $N_1$ the number of lead atoms $^{206}$Pb contained in the mineral at the time of analysis. Then $N_0 = N + N_1$ is the number of uranium atoms at the moment of granite formation. (We believe that all the lead in granite was formed from uranium by radioactive decay.) According to the law of radioactive decay

$$\frac{N}{N_0} = \frac{N}{N + N_1} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}},$$

where $t$ is the age of granite. Taking the logarithm of this equality, we obtain a formula for calculating the age of the mineral:

$$t = \frac{T_{1/2}}{\ln 2} \ln \frac{N_0}{N}.$$

In the calculations, it was assumed that there was no built-up lead in the sample and that exchange of matter with the external environment was excluded. However, this is not always the case. The element radon, which appears on the path of transformations between uranium and lead, belongs to the group of noble gases, and if the mineral being studied is not dense enough, radon can evaporate.
