It is also necessary to prove that there cannot be a case in which all rectangular lines are closed, even “number 0”. Before we prove this, we note that the area of ​​the closed rectangle number $k$ is less than the area of ​​the square number $k$ (this is the main idea of ​​our laying method!). Indeed, it is clear that as long as this side of the rectangle $k$ is equal to the side of the square $k$, it cannot yet be closed (even the $k$th square fits into it).

Now suppose that at some $n$-th step the rectangle 0, which had dimensions $c \times d$ ($c \geq d$) before this step, is closed. It is clear that at the $n$th step $m = 0$, that is, all rectangles with numbers $m > 0$ are closed, and their area is less than the area of ​​the corresponding squares.

Let $u$ and $v$ be the sides of squares numbered $n$ and $n+1$. If the square $v \times v$ does not fit into the rectangle $d \times (c - u)$ (Fig. 6), then $v \geq c - u$, so

$$2(u^2 + v^2) \geq (u + v)^2 \geq c^2 \geq cd,$$

that is, the sum of the areas of the $n$-th and $(n+1)$-th squares is not less than half the area of ​​the rectangle $c \times d$. It turns out that already the first $(n+1)$ squares make up more than half the area of ​​the $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2}$ square. Contradiction.

We propose several problems that clarify and generalize the statements of the square packing problem.

1. Two equal squares on side $a$ cannot be placed without intersecting in a square whose side is less than 2$a$.

2. Squares whose sum of square areas is $S$ and the longest side is $x$ can be packed into a square with side $x + \sqrt{S - x^2}$.

3. These squares can be packed into a rectangle $a \times b$ if $a \geq x$, $b \geq x$ and $ab \geq 2S$.

4. If any system of squares with total area 1 can be packed into a rectangle $a \times b$, where $a \geq b$, then at least one of two conditions is true: 1) $a \geq \sqrt{3}$ and $b \geq 1$ 2) $a \geq \sqrt{2}$ and $b \geq 2/\sqrt{3}$.

D. Kleitman and M. Krieger proved that the rectangle $1 \times \sqrt{3}$ can accommodate any system of squares with total area 1. It is plausible that a similar statement is true for the rectangle $\sqrt{2} \times 2/\sqrt{3}$.

5. Rectangles, the sum of the areas of the squares is equal to $S$ and the most of the sides of the squares is equal to $x$, can be packed into a rectangle $a \times b$, if $a \geq x$ and $ab \geq 2S + a^2/8$.

6. Cubes with a total volume of $V$ can be packed into a cube of volume 4$V$.
