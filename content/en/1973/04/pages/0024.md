However, 89% of the $^{40}$K isotope decays in this way, and 11% decays in a different way, experiencing, as physicists say, $K$-capture. The process of $K$-capture is that an atomic nucleus captures an orbital electron and becomes the nucleus of an element whose atomic number is one less. Thus, $^{40}$K transforms into the argon isotope $^{40}$Ar (Fig. 2).

Mineral studies have shown that in some rocks, such as mica, argon survives billions of years without loss. During the formation of the earth's crust, primary argon “boiled away” into the atmosphere, so minerals contain only radiogenic argon.

Thus, by isolating argon from mica contained in granite, it is possible to determine the increase obtained by the lead-uranium method.

The half-life of the isotope $^{40}$K can be determined using a beta particle counter and an ordinary clock, the number of atoms $N$ - as a result of chemical analysis. The value of $N_0$ is equal to $N + N_p$, where $N_p$ is the number of $^{40}$K atoms that decayed during the existence of granite. $N_p$ can be calculated if the number of argon atoms contained in mica is known.

Let's denote this number by the letter $A$. Then $N_p = \frac{A}{0,11}$ (since the share of transformations with potassium - argon accounts for 11% of the total number of decays of the radioactive isotope of potassium). Therefore, $N_0 = N + \frac{A}{0,11}$. Substituting this expression into formula (1), we obtain

$$t = \frac{T_{1/2}}{\ln 2} \ln \left(1 + \frac{A}{0,11}\right).$$

Rubide-strontium radioactive clocks are also of great interest. The rubidium isotope $^{87}$Rb is radioactive and emits beta particles, transforming into the strontium isotope $^{87}$Sr. Natural rubidium contains 28% radioactive isotope. The principle of determining the birth of minerals using the rubide-strontium assessment method is simple. Chemical analysis determines the total amount of rubidium, and then the content of the isotope $^{87}$Sr. After such procedures, calculations are performed using radioactive decay formulas.

All the methods we talked about apply to the growth of minerals from the moment of their crystallization. Only in solids do radioactive decay products remain close to the parent nucleus. In a liquid melt, atoms mix freely, and since the chemical properties of the substance consisting of daughter nuclei differ from the properties of the parent substance, the daughter products are concentrated in other places.

As a rule, the oldest rocks lie under enormous layers of sediment. Only in some areas do they come close to the surface.
