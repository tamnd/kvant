BALLISTIC
TASK
IN SPACE

Let's imagine that a spaceship takes off from the surface of the Earth, and at the moment of stopping (in the system of absence associated with the Earth's center of mass), identical bodies are thrown out of it with the same speed $v_0$ in different directions. The question arises: what part of the near-Earth space can these bodies fall into?

This article is devoted to solving this problem and a number of related problems. It is special to warn the reader that in such a formulation the problem was considered in the last century, namely, in the article of Professor B.K. Mlodezevsky “On the enveloping orbits at Newtonian acceptance”, published in 1886, and then, with a simpler conclusion, in the article of the famous Russian mechanic N.E. Zhukovsky “Remarks on the article of B.K. Mlodezevsky”, published in the same year.

Below we will give an even more elementary presentation of these issues, which will require the reader to know the simplest properties of the ellipse, as well as two conservation laws for motion under the action of the Newtonian force of acceptance and Kepler's first law *).

*) All this information can be found, for example, in the article by Ya. A. Smorodinsky “Movement of the Planets”, «Kvant» No. 1, 1971.

1. Statement of the problem.
Original proposals

When solving the problem, we will neglect the resistance of the Earth's atmosphere; We will consider the selected reporting system associated with the Earth as inertial, the bodies as material points of mass $m$, and the Earth as a homogeneous ball of mass $m_0$.

As is known, such a ball accepts a material point with a force directed towards its center and equal to $F = \gamma \frac{m_0 m}{r^2}$, where $\gamma$ is the gravitational constant, and $r$ is the distance of the material point to the center of the ball.

Finally, the problem we are considering is reduced to an idealized problem studied by Newton:

In the motionless *) ceptre O the mass $m_0$ is concentrated, and we apply the material point of mass $m$ with a force $F = \gamma \frac{m_0 m}{r^2}$. It is required to investigate the possibility of motion of this material point under the influence of gravity; there are two conservation laws: mechanical energy and angular momentum.

The law of conservation of total mechanical energy says that energy

$$E = E_K + E_n = \frac{mv^2}{2} - \gamma \frac{m_0 m}{r}$$

(1)

maintains a constant value, that is, at any time $E = E_0$, where $E_0$ is the initial energy

$$E_0 = \frac{mv_0^2}{2} - \gamma \frac{m_0 m}{r}$$

(2)

*) Strictly speaking, as a result of mutual attraction, both the material point and the Earth will move relative to their common center of mass. But since the mass $m$ is negligible compared to the mass $m_0$, the Earth can be considered motionless.

If $E_0 < 0$, then $r$ remains limited. It is known that in this and only in this case the material point describes an ellipse, and one of its foci coincides with the center $O$ (Kepler's first law). Therefore, the initial speed $v_0$, for which $E_0 < 0$, is called elliptic. If $E_0 = 0$, the point moves along a parabola, and the corresponding initial speed is called parabolic. If $E_0 > 0$, the motion of the point follows a hyperbola, and the speed is called hyperbolic.

3. Getting from the initial position to the specified one.
"Safety" ellipse

Let's solve the following problem.
What direction of the initial velocity must be reported at a given value $v_0$ in order for the material point to move from the initial position $M_0$ to the given position $M$?

Point $M$ must lie on an ellipse, so the initial initial velocity means finding

This is the direction of the tangent to the ellipse at the point $M_0$. This can be done by using the optical property of the ellipse, according to which the initial velocity must be directed (in one direction or another) along the bissertic of the angle adjacent to the angle ($r_0, r'$) (Fig. 2), where $r_0$ and $r'$ are radius vectors drawn from the foci to the point $M_0$.

One of the ellipse's foci is known (coincides with the point $O$), and the other needs to be determined. It is obvious that the distances $r'$ and $r'$ from the point $M_0$ and $M$ to this focus satisfy the conditions

$$r_0 + r' = 2 a,$$

$$r + r' = 2 a.$$

It follows that

$$r' = 2 a - r_0$$ and $$r' = 2 a - r,$$

that is, $r_0$ and $r'$ are satisfied by the position of the point $M_0$ and $M$ and the magnitude of the initial speed. Therefore, the desired focus lies at the intersection of two circles with centers at points $M_0$ and $M$ and with radii equal to the corresponding $r'$ and $r'$.

If the distance is $M_0 M > r' + r' = 4 a - r_0 - r$, then the problem has no solution, since the circles do not intersect.

If $M_0 M < r' + r'$, then the above circles will have two intersection points $F_1$ and $F_2$ as shown in figure 2 and the problem will have two solutions.
