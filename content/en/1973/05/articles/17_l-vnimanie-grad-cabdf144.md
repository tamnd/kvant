3. Let's touch on one more issue related to prime numbers. Let us take a segment of a series of natural numbers from 1 to some $n$ inclusive. There is a certain number of prime numbers on this segment. Their number is usually denoted by $\pi(n)$*. What is $\pi(n)$ equal to for individual values ​​of $n$?

For small $n$ this is easy to calculate. For example, $\pi(1) = 0$, $\pi(2) = 1$, $\pi(3) = 2$, $\pi(4) = 2$, $\pi(5) = 3$, $\pi(6) = 3$, $\pi(7) = 4$, $\pi(8) = 4$, $\pi(9) = 4$, $\pi(10) = 4$.

The peregular change $\pi(n)$ is immediately replaced. In general, no simple formula for $\pi(n)$ can be written.

However, by increasing $n$, one can replace that the "average density" of primes, that is, the ratio $\pi(n):n$, becomes smaller and smaller. This can be clearly seen from the following table:

| $n$ | $\pi(n)$ | $\pi(n):n$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 10 | 4 | 0,4 |
| 100 | 25 | 0,4 |
| 1 000 | 168 | 0,17 |
| 10 000 | 1 229 | 0,12 |
| 100 000 | 9 592 | 0,096 |
| 1 000 000 | 78 498 | 0,078 |
| 10 000 000 | 664 579 | 0,066 |
| 100 000 000 | 5 761 455 | 0,057 |
| 1 000 000 000 | 50 847 478 | 0,051 |

It has been proven that as $n$ increases, the ratio $\pi(n):n$ approaches zero. This fact was first proven by Leonhard Euler, the greatest mathematician of the 18th century. Subsequently, the great Russian mathematician Pafunty Lvovich Chebyshev clarified Euler’s results by proving a more general theorem (see «Kvant» No. 5 for 1971, pp. 1-3), but we will not talk about this.

*) $\pi$ is the Greek letter “pi”, $n$ is the Latin letter “en”, $\pi(n)$ is read like this: “pi from en”.

On June 9, 1926, unusually large hail fell in Odessa. Individual hailstones reached 300 $z$, and the light cover that formed on the ground reached 30 $cm$.

In July 1928, hail fell in the city of Potter (USA); the hailstones reached 10 $cm$ in diameter, and the mass of individual hailstones reached 600 $z$. The hailstones were smooth, spherical and consisted of concentric layers of ice frozen onto the central core. They fell quite further from one another and were completely buried in the ground.

The mechanism of hail formation is not difficult to understand.

If you consider that the air temperature in the troposphere falls on average by 5-6°C with every kilometer of rise, then it is easy to calculate that at an altitude of 6-8 km above the Earth, that is, the altitude characteristic of the upper boundary of a thundercloud, the temperature is significantly below zero even at midday in July. Droplets of supercooled water and individual ice crystals, colliding with each other, form the nuclei of future hailstones. Passing through a multi-oclimeter thick cloud (6-7 km), the cores are formed by layers of transparent ice. The thickness of the growing shell depends on the process of ignition of water vapor with the surfaces of hailstones and, to an even greater extent, on the intensity of the ascent of air flows inside the cloud. These streams, firstly, “throw” new supercooled drops of water towards the hailstone flying down, which immediately freeze onto the hailstone. Secondly, if the flows become powerful, thereby significantly lengthening the ice ball in the supercooled liquid. In these cases, the size of the hailstones falling to the ground can become catastrophically large.
