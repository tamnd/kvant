So, we will talk about a new approach to the problem of searching for minerals, more precisely, about the problem of searching for oil and gas deposits.

What to look for?

The surface of the Earth, many kilometers deep, looks like a multilayer sandwich: through

There are layers of sand, clay, limestone, etc. Oil or gas can only occur in porous layers where there are adequate spaces between the rock particles. Under the influence of all kinds of catalytic layers of sedimentary rocks changed their geometry, peaks and rises, mountain ridges and faults appeared. In most cases, oil and gas fields are confined to underground hills with a seal impermeable to liquid or gas; it can be clayey or salty. Under this dense dome there are porous layers in which liquid or gas lies. Therefore, the first task of geologists when searching for oil or gas is to detect underground domes, or, as geologists call them, local *) uplifts. This is what we are talking about now about searching for local uplifts.

Model

How to get an idea of ​​the structure of the earth's layers at a depth of several kilometers on the Earth's surface? The answer is clear: it is necessary to drill wells, select pieces of rock - core - from different depths, and then compare the results of studies of different wells. But drilling many wells is very expensive; drilling a well to a depth of 2-4 km costs hundreds of thousands of rubles, and now deeper wells are being drilled. And therefore, to obtain preliminary information, it uses various indirect methods, such as gravity surveying or electrical prospecting. The most informative among them is now freeze-breeding.

How could this happen? Who is to blame for this? And most importantly: how to avoid such mistakes?

Formalization

Much has been written about treasure hunters, and I am sure the reader can easily recall the corresponding stories. Whenever a treasure hunter is faced with the difficulties associated with the possibility of different paths to achieve his goal, he somehow evaluates his chances on each of the paths. But this is an intuitive, qualitative assessment. If the treasure hunter turned out to be a cyberneticist, then he would first of all make efforts to develop a criterion for quantitatively assessing the chances of achieving success, and then choosing a path at each step would no longer present difficulties for him: he would have to act in such a way that the criterion would always give the highest possible value.

In our problem of finding underground storehouses - local uplifts - the situation is the same: we need to construct a criterion for choosing the best sequence of actions or, as they say now, for choosing the best strategy,

So, we will talk about an approach to the search process with mathematical positions. But for this, the process itself must be properly formatted.

First of all, you need to give a clear definition of the object being studied - a local uplift.

Let us define at each point $Q$ with coordinates $(x, y)$ on the studied part of the plane (day surface) a function (distance from the day surface to the surface of the dome) $h = H(Q) \equiv H(x, y)$, the values ​​of which describe the surface of the horizon being studied. The level line is a set of those points $Q$ whose coordinates satisfy the equation $H(x, y) = h_0$, where $h_0$ is the definition of number.

A local rise is an area on the plane, orangish the closed level lines at some fixed value $h_0$, inside which everything is $H(x, y) > h_0$.

Two points should be made here.

Firstly, it follows from the definition that inside a local uplift there are infinitely many other local uplifts, or inside the level lines we have chosen there are other also closed level lines. However, this diversity does not suit us.

Secondly, it is necessary to limit the size of the local uplifts being studied. Indeed, very small domes have no industrial significance, but very large ones may have a different meaning altogether.

In practice, in almost every territory we are interested in local uplifts, the diameter $D$ (that is, the largest chord) of which is contained within certain limits (between the minimum, less than which

Probabilistic approach

According to the definition, the point $Q_0$ either belongs to the local lift or does not belong. Now the question arises of how to construct a reliability function based on the information available to geophysicists. Deviations of the depth values ​​obtained as a result of survey work from the actual values ​​of the depth of the surface under study at each point $Q$ are random variables. To study their probabilistic characteristics, they were compared with data from subsequent drilling, which, being much more accurate, was accepted as valid. Data processing in large regions of the Volga-Ural oil and gas province for the occurrence of della is an opportunity to find out the probabilistic characteristics of these random variables.

In accordance with the last definition, it is necessary to find at least one closed polygon enclosing the point $Q_0$, in all nodes $Q_i$ of which the values ​​of the functions $H(Q_i)$ will be less than $H(Q_0)$. Since the quantities $H(Q_i)$ themselves are random, then the own “there is at least one closed polygon enclosing the point $Q_0$” is also random. Its probability is the probability that the point $Q_0$ belongs to a local lift, that is, the value of the confidence functions $d(Q_0)$.

Knowing the probabilistic properties of the random variables $H(Q_i)$, we can write formulas for calculating the values ​​of the reliability functions. However, these formulas are very cumbersome and are not practical to use for calculations.

Some results and prospects

The thought is directed by itself: the values ​​of the reliability functions are also plotted on the structural map (that is, on the map of the level lines of the horizon being studied, constructed according to the data of the georeconnaissance survey). You can even use the general methods of interpolation, drawing lines of the level of reliability functions (at Fig. 2 and further the level lines of reliability functions are drawn with red lines).

Now we can begin to discuss the new things that the reliability function brought to the structural map.

It turned out that in areas where the values ​​of the reliability functions were quite high (more than 0.8), there is, as a rule, a coincidence of the local uplift constructed according to geological survey data (we will henceforth call it the geophysical uplift) with the real uplift (see Fig. 4, where the local uplift is shaded).

At the highest values ​​of the reliability functions of the order of 0.5 - 0.6, a mixture of the geophysical uplift and the real one was often noted, and the areas with the highest values ​​of the reliability functions tended in location to the real uplift (Fig. 5 and 6). An important practical conclusion follows from this: the exploration well should be located not at those points that turned out to be the highest according to the geophysical construction (that is, in the arch of the geophysical uplift), but at the points with the highest values ​​of the reliability functions, or with the most confidence in the surveyed area belong to the real local uplift.

Finally, geophysical structures for which the maximum values ​​of the confidence functions were more than 0.35, as a rule, turned out to be false; their presence was refuted by subsequent drilling. In this case, all the costs of drilling out such false structures turned out to be involuntary.
