Experiments with infrared radiation

A homemade phototransistor can serve as an infrared radiation receiver. It is not difficult to make by sawing off the head of the metal case of any powerful transistor (for example, P-210). Such a phototransistor reacts practically only to infrared rays directly adjacent to the visible region of the spectrum.

If you direct light from an accumulating lamp that contains an alternating current to the phototransistor you have made, an alternating voltage with a frequency of 100 $2\mu$ will appear at its terminals. The magnitude of this voltage is very small; it should be increased.

The simplest representation diagram is shown in figure. Any low-frequency transistors can be used as transistors $T_2$ and $T_3$. The amplifier is powered by a flashlight battery KBS-L-0.50.

$$R_1 = 2 \text{ ком}; R_2 = 100 \text{ ком}; R_3 = 10 \text{ ком}; R_4 = 100 \text{ ком}; R_5 = 10 \text{ ком}; T_2 \text{ и } T_3 \text{- любые низкочастные трансисторы.}$$

Transistor $T_1$ can be turned on in another way - use the emmeter - base terminals. In this case, the base output is connected to the battery plate, and the resistance $R_1$ should be taken equal to 30 ÷ 50 kohms.

When assembled correctly, the device starts working immediately when the power is turned on. The adjustment will consist of selecting the value of resistor $R_1$.

The output of the preliminary successor should be connected using a shielded wire to the “sound pickup” sockets of any industrial radio receiver or player (you can also use a homemade low-frequency successor). When the phototransistor is illuminated by the light of the accumulation lamp included, you will hear a sound with a frequency of 100 $2\mu$. (If a strong “background” of alternating current is audible even without illuminating the transistor, then the preliminary successor should be enclosed in a metal box connected to the “ground” of the device - this will reduce interference from the network.)

Now that you have an infrared receiver, try answering the following questions:

1. Why, when illuminating a phototransistor with an accumulating lamp containing alternating current, made of diamine, a loud sound (“background”) with a frequency of 100 $2\mu$ is amplified?

2. How can you significantly enhance the sound without changing the receiver circuit?

3. Make sure that the action sensor responds well to infrared rays (Zbonite plastic with a thickness of 0.5-1 mm completely blocks visible light and transmits infrared rays).

4. Try to find some kind of infrared filter (try using, for example, different types of photographic filters).

5. In the path of the light beam incident on the phototransistor, place two polaroids (they can be taken from the school kit on polarization of light). Rotate one of the polaroids around its axis. What do you observe? What conclusions can you draw from your observations?

6. Prove the law of reflection for infrared rays.

7. Check if the infrared rays are focused by the lens?

8. What other experiments can you perform to study the properties of infrared radiation?
