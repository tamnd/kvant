Mathematics exams in GDR schools

Curricula and programs vary from country to country. In this article we will talk about the features of education in 10-year and 12-year schools in the GDR, about the mathematics course in these schools, and about the level of examination requirements.

The Unified Socialist Education System is being successfully implemented in the German Democratic Republic, the law on which was adopted by the People's Chamber of the Republic in 1965. Along with all educational ten-year educational programs, training in extended (12-year) secondary schools, as well as secondary specialized and higher educational institutions, the system also provides for broad vocational and technical education.

There are no “dead-end” schools in this system. The Constitution of the GDR (Article 26) states that “the state shall ensure the possibility of transition to the next higher level of education... in accordance with the principle of success and social needs.”

In the first class of PRIMARYS children who turned 6 years old by May 30 of this year, that is, almost a year earlier than in our schools. But the mathematics course in GDR schools differs significantly from the course in the corresponding classes in the Soviet school. For example, the mathematics program for the fifth grade of the GDR school almost completely coincides with the program for our fourth grades.

In the rural class, a lot of time is spent on calculations with percentages and drawing up ratios, using a slide rule; In the same class, the extraction of square roots and elements of descriptive geometry are studied. Solving quadratic equations is first covered in ninth grade, and the concept of trigonometric functions in tenth grade. On the whole, the ten-year school in the GDR provides only slightly more mathematical training than our eight-year school. Most ten-year graduates know how to apply the acquired knowledge: quickly and accurately calculate on a slide rule, confidently use tables and reference books. But solving the simplest word problem sometimes causes them insurmountable difficulties. And some sections familiar to us are not studied at all in a ten-year school: there are no limits, programmers are not considered, trigonometric equations are almost not solved - this is recognized as optional for a general ten-year education and is transferred to a twelve-year, extended school.

In the extended school, 300 lessons are allocated for the study of mathematics (5 lessons per week in grades XI and XII). In addition to the traditional sections, mathematical indications, the theory of limits, vector algebra and analytical geometry, as well as the fundamentals of differential and integral calculus are studied - approximately in the scope of the first year of some of our colleges.

The grading system adopted in the GDR school is very interesting. Solving each problem starts with a few points; If the problem is not solved completely or with an error, then the number of points is reduced or not awarded at all. Attached to the text of the written work in mathematics for grade 10 (see below) (for teachers only!) are assessment instructions. It states that problem No. 1 is worth 6 points, with one point each for composing the first equation, composing the second equation, eliminating one unknown, finding the first number, finding the second number, and checking.

The remaining tasks are “assessed” in exactly the same way; All works will be assessed 40 points. The instructions below describe the school:

39 and 40 points - mark “1” - “very good”;

from 32 to 38 points – mark “2” – good;

from 24 to 31 points - mark “3” - “satisfactory”;

from 14 to 23 points – mark “4” – “sufficient”;

from 0 to 13 points – mark “5” – “insufficient”.

Written work per year is assessed in the same way; Marks are rarely given for oral answers (further in physics, chemistry, biology, history). As a rule, a mark of “3” is given for 60%, and a mark of “4” for 33% of the task. This mark allows you to move from class to class, but absolutely excludes the possibility of getting into a 12-year school, and especially a university.

After completing the 8th grade, each student receives a recommendation for further education.

At the same time, 15-20% of the best students are recommended for a 12-year school, the completion of which gives the right to enter a university (without exams, but on the recommendation of the school), 10-15% of the weakest students are recommended for three-year vocational schools, which provide a specialty and a general ten-year education. The rest continue their studies in grades XI and X.
