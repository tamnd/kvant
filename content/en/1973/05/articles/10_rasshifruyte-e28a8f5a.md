It is for periods, and not for main periods.

Theorem 3. If $T_1 > 0$ is the period $f_1(x)$, $T_2 > 0$ is the period $f_2(x)$, the reception $T_1$ and $T_2$ are co-dimensions and the sucestium of the value $x$, belonging to the simultaneous domain of definition $f_1$ and $f_2$, then $f(x) = f_1(x) + f_2(x)$ is a periodic function whose entire period has the number $T$, equal to the common multiple (including the smallest) of the periods $T_1$ and $T_2$.

Proof. Let $T$ be the least common multiple of commensurate segments $T_1$ and $T_2$. Then there are natural numbers $k$ and $l$ such that $T = kT_1$ and $T = lT_2$. If $x$ is any of the domain $f(x)$, then

$$f(x) = f_1(x) + f_2(x) = f_1(x + kT_1) + f_2(x + lT_2) = f_1(x + T) + f_2(x + T) = f(x + T).$$

All the restrictions present in the conditions of the theorem were used in the given chain of equalities.

Generally speaking, a similar theorem can be proven for a much wider class of combinations of the functions $f_1$ and $f_2$, including the product quotient. However, consideration of specific practical situations here is apparently much more effective than constructing any rigorous theory.

Where is the error?

— How many roots does a quadratic equation have?
“Two, of course,” you answer.
- But is it always?

Consider this equation:

$$\frac{(x-a)(x-b)}{(c-a)(c-b)} + \frac{(x-b)(x-c)}{(a-b)(a-c)} + \frac{(x-a)(x-c)}{(b-a)(b-c)} = 1$$

(here $a, b$ and $c$ are pairwise distinct numbers).

Let's substitute $x=a$. The first and third terms will go to zero. The second gives one. Substituting $x=b$, we see that the first two terms become zero, and the third gives one.

Substitution $x=c$ produces the same result. Thus, although we have a quadratic equation, there are at least three roots! Popular solution: a second degree equation has more than two roots!
