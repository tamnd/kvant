⟦folio 26⟧

Regular polyhedra - tetrahedron, octaedron, hexahedron (cube), dodecahedron and icosahedron - are usually called Platonic solids *).

Platonic solids are discussed in school textbooks, including in A.P. Kiselev’s textbook “Geometry,” part two.

Let us recall the definition of a regular polyhedron given in this textbook: “A polyhedron is called regular if all its faces are equal regular polygons and all polyhedral angles are equal.” From this definition it follows that in regular polyhedra all plane angles, all dihedral angles and all edges are equal. The textbook proves that there cannot be any other regular polyhedra except the Platonic solids (see page 4 of the cover).

The proof is given in full. And yet there are not five regular polyhedra, but nine. You see them on the cover of our magazine. The fact is that the remaining four polyhedra (we will further call them star-shaped) have faces that intersect regular polygons, and for two of them, each of the faces is a self-intersecting polygon. Such polygons and polyhedra are excluded from consideration in A.P. Kiselev’s course, although the above definition of a regular polyhedron, as well as its corollary, remains in force.

Now a little source. Two star polyhedra were constructed by Johannes Kepler (1571–1630). Only years later, the French geometer Louis Poinsot (1777-1859) pointed out the existence of two more regular stellate polyhedra.

*) The ancient Greek philosopher Plato (427-347 BC), who mentioned regular polyhedra in one of his works, was not actually their discoverer. The tetrahedron, hexahedron and dodecahedron were known long before Plato. For example, during excavations, a model of a dodecahedron was found, which served as a children's toy more than 2,500 years ago.
