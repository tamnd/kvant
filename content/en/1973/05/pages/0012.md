When the surface is represented by a dome, then its level lines are closed curves, and the inner, smallest line corresponds to the vicinity of the top of the dome.

So, the result of sowing georeconnaissance work is a map of the level lines of the horizon being studied, and if somewhere on this map there is a series of closed level lines nested within each other, with the internal lines corresponding to shallower depths, then this part of the map corresponds to a dome - a local uplift.

Now we need to drill a well to find out if there is oil or gas in this uplift. And it is clear to everyone that it is necessary to drill at the top of the dome, that is, the internal lines correspond to shallower depths, then this part of the map corresponds to the dome - a local uplift.

Sometimes they drill the first well, but it does not produce any oil or gas, then they drill another one, two, three, and then it turns out that there is no local uplift, the geophysical information about the presence of a dome turned out to be a mistake. Consequently, these wells were drilled in vain, more money and considerable time were wasted.

How could this happen? Who is to blame for this? And most importantly: how to avoid such mistakes?

Let us remember the words highlighted above: at and zhenno. Depths, and therefore the shape and location of level lines, are determined by geophysicists not accurately, but with errors, and sometimes very significant ones.

There is no need to blame them for this. The fact is that errors, as I said above, are a consequence of both hardware errors and the method itself. And if instrumental errors can be noticeably reduced, then the errors of the method cannot be fundamentally eliminated. If we knew exactly the speed characteristics of the medium, that is, the speed of propagation of an elastic wave in all layers along the path of this wave, then the problem of local uplifts would not exist at all: getting to a local uplift would in principle be as easy as getting from Moscow to Kyiv, using a compass and an accurate geographical map.

However, when a linear level map - it is called a structural map - has already been drawn (and it was not given in vain - a lot of skilled work, money and time were spent), then it is very tempting to treat such a map with confidence. Such is human nature: it is difficult to distrust everything around you all the time, especially if it is easier to believe it. And what can be done here: there seems to be no other information at the disposal of a geophysicist or geologist.

Much has been written about treasure hunters, and I am sure the reader can easily recall the corresponding stories. Whenever a treasure hunter is faced with the difficulties associated with the possibility of different paths to achieve his goal, he somehow evaluates his chances on each of the paths. But this is an intuitive, qualitative assessment.
