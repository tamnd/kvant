⟦folio 27⟧

cov. (All four star polyhedra are shown on page 2 of the cover.) Poinsot was unable to prove that there are no other regular polyhedra.

A year later (in 1811), the French mathematician Augustin Louis Cauchy (1789-1857) did this. He took advantage of the fact that, according to the definition of a regular polyhedron, it can be superimposed on itself so that an arbitrary face of it coincides with a previously chosen one. It follows from this that all faces of the star polyhedron are equidistant from a certain point - the center of the sphere inscribed in the polyhedron.

The planes of the faces of a star polyhedron, intersecting, also form a regular convex polyhedron, that is, a Platonic solid described around the same sphere. Ksshi called this Platonic solid the core of this stellate polyhedron. Thus, a star polyhedron can be obtained by continuing the planes of the faces of one of the Platonic solids.

Can any Platonic solid serve as the core of a regular stellate polyhedron? To understand this, let's turn to the polyhedral angles of a stellated polyhedron. The plane angles of such a polyhedral angle are equal and, thanks to the ratio, correspond to two facet angles, they can be combined with each other by rotating the polyhedral angle about the axis passing through its vertex. At the same time that the plane angles align and match the edges of the core, rotate it along with the star polyhedron.

Figures 1, 2 and 3 highlight the faces of the nuclei - dodecahedron and icosahedron, which generate into each other during such rotations, and show the polyhedral angles to which the continuation of the faces leads.

On figure 4 the case is considered when the continuation of the edges of a regular polyhedron, an octahedron, leads to the formation of not one, but two regular polyhedra - intersecting tetrahedra.

Prove on your own that no other rotations of the Platonic solids and the corresponding extensions of their faces lead to the formation of new regular polyhedra.
