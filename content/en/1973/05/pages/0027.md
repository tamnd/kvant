Proof. Let us put $a_k = (k+1)^q$, $k=0, 1, \dots, n-1$ in identity (7) and first consider the values ​​of $n \leq q+1$. Since in this case $C_p^n = 0$ is for $p > q+1$, formula (8) follows from (7) under the condition $1 \leq n \leq q+1$. However, it follows and on the right we have polynomials of degree $q+1$ with respect to $n$ without a dummy term and therefore they must coincide for all $n$. Indeed, otherwise their difference is a polynomial of degree no higher than $q+1$ – we have $q+2$ real roots: $0, 1, 2, \dots, q+1$.

Try to prove on your own that then this polynomial is also effectively zero *).

Formula (8) represents the sum $S_q(n)$ as a linear combination of combinations $C_n^1, C_n^2, \dots, C_n^{q+1}$.

$$S_0(n) = C_n^1,$$
$$S_1(n) = C_n^1 + C_n^2,$$
$$S_2(n) = C_n^1 + 3C_n^2 + 2C_n^3,$$
$$S_3(n) = C_n^1 + 7C_n^2 + 12C_n^3 + 6C_n^4,$$
$$S_4(n) = C_n^1 + 15C_n^2 + 50C_n^3 + 60C_n^4 + 24C_n^5,$$

etc.

We leave it to the reader to explore the curious patterns that have arisen between the coefficients of these combinations, for example, the laws of formation of coefficients for capitals.

Let us conclude with a few more problems on the sums $S_q$.

Exercises
1. Show that
$$S_1 = S_1,$$
$$2(S_1)^2 = 2S_3,$$
$$4(S_1)^3 = 3S_5 + S_3,$$
$$8(S_1)^4 = 4S_7 + 4S_5,$$

*) If you failed to do this, we can find the proof in «Kvant» No. 12, 1972, page 37

and in general, that at $k=1, 2, 3, \dots$

$$2^{k-1}(S_1)^k = C_k^1 S_{2k-1} + C_k^3 S_{2k-3} + C_k^5 S_{2k-5} + \dots,$$

and the last term of the expression on the right is equal to $S_k$ or $kS_{k+1}$, depending on whether $k$ is odd or even.

2. Show that

$$3S_2 = 3S_2,$$
$$6S_2S_1 = 5S_4 + S_2,$$
$$12S_2(S_1)^2 = 7S_6 + 5S_4,$$
$$24S_2(S_1)^2 = 9S_8 + 14S_6 + S_4$$

and in general, that at $k=1, 2, 3, \dots$

$$3 \cdot 2^{k-1}S_2(S_1)^{k-1} = (C_k^0 + 2C_k^1)S_{2k} + (C_k^2 + 2C_k^3)S_{2k-2} + \dots,$$

and the last term of the expression on the right is equal to either $(k+2)S_{k+1}$ or $S_k$, depending on whether $k$ is odd or even.

3. Show that

$$S_3 = (S_1)^2,$$
$$S_5 = (S_1)^2 \cdot \frac{4S_1 - 1}{3},$$
$$S_7 = (S_1)^2 \cdot \frac{6(S_1)^2 - 4S_1 + 1}{3},$$

and in general that $S_{2k-1}$ (where $2k-1 \geq 3$) is a polynomial in $S_1 = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ of degree $k$ divisible by $(S_1)^2$.

4. Show that

$$S_4 = S_2 \cdot \frac{6S_1 - 1}{5},$$
$$S_6 = S_2 \cdot \frac{12(S_1)^2 - 6S_1 + 1}{7},$$
$$S_8 = S_2 \cdot \frac{40(S_1)^3 - 40(S_1)^2 + 18S_1 - 3}{15},$$

and in general that $\frac{S_{2k}}{S_2}$ is a polynomial of degree $k-1$ in $S_1$.

5. Prove that for $k \geq 1$ (but not for $k=0$)

$$S_k (-x-1) = (-1)^{k-1}S_k(x).$$

6. Find the sum: $1 + 27 + 125 + \dots + (2n-1)^3$.

7. Find the sum: $2^2 + 5^2 + 8^2 + \dots + (3n-1)^2$.
