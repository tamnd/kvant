Let us now note that the information obtained during the search process is statistical in nature, and therefore, when constructing a criterion and choosing strategies, it is advisable to use a probabilistic approach.

So, we will talk about an approach to the search process with mathematical positions. But for this, the process itself must be properly formatted.

First of all, you need to give a clear definition of the object being studied - a local uplift.

Let us define at each point $Q$ with coordinates $(x, y)$ on the studied part of the plane (day surface) a function (distance from the day surface to the surface of the dome) $h = H(Q) \equiv H(x, y)$, the values ​​of which describe the surface of the horizon being studied. The level line is a set of those points $Q$ whose coordinates satisfy the equation $H(x, y) = h_0$, where $h_0$ is the definition of number.

A local rise is an area on the plane, orangish the closed level lines at some fixed value $h_0$, inside which everything is $H(x, y) > h_0$.

Two points should be made here.

Firstly, it follows from the definition that inside a local uplift there are infinitely many other local uplifts, or inside the level lines we have chosen there are other also closed level lines. However, this diversity does not suit us.

Secondly, it is necessary to limit the size of the local uplifts being studied. Indeed, very small domes have no industrial significance, but very large ones may have a different meaning altogether.

In practice, in almost every territory we are interested in local uplifts, the diameter $D$ (that is, the largest chord) of which is contained within certain limits (between the minimum, less than which

In practice, the initial data of a georeconnaissance survey make it possible to determine the depths of the horizon being studied only at some individual points, and even then with errors. Then if the surface of this horizon is determined by interpolation (that is, a restoration approximation), and of course the positions of the point of this surface will be even less accurate.

So, the position of a point on the surface under study will be determined with errors, and, consequently, the position of a point of its level line will also be determined with errors.

For further study it is useful for us: we will not be interested in the presence or absence of a local uplift in a given area, but we will find out regarding individual points whether they belong to a local uplift.
