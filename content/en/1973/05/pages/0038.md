Electron is transal, then the charges above and below the “equatar” have different symbols. Consequently, at the second pole $C$ the density of surface charges is equal to $-\sigma_0$, and at the equator it is zero.

Since charges on the surface of a conductor are always distributed in such a way that their density is proportional to the electric field voltage, we can assume that the density of induced charges $S$ at each point of the ball will be proportional to the external field voltage $E$:

$$\sigma \sim E.$$

(This also follows from the messages of dimensions - the dimensions $\sigma$ and $E$ are the same).

Let's use the principle of superposition and decompose the field with stress $E$ at point $B$ into two components (Fig. 11)—in the direction $OB$ (vector $E_1$) and perpendicular to $OB$ (vector $E_2$):

$$E_1 = E \cos \alpha,$$
$$E_2 = E \sin \alpha.$$

We also represent the charge density $S$ at point $B$ as the sum of the charge densities that arise at this point: $\sigma = \sigma_1 + \sigma_2$, and $\sigma_1$ is created by a field with intense $E_1$, and $\sigma_2$ by a field with intense $E_2$.

But in a field with a tense $E_2$, the point $B$ lies on the “equatar” of the sphere, therefore $\sigma_2 = 0$. In a field with a tense $E_1$ point $B$ is the “pole” of the sphere. Therefore $\sigma_1 \sim E_1$

$$\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} = \frac{E_1}{E}.$$

From here

$$\sigma = \sigma_1 = \sigma_0 \frac{E_1}{E_0} = \sigma_0 \cos \alpha.$$

F181. The satellite moves around the Earth in a steep orbit with a speed of $v$. The change in its orbit is due to the fact that the frictional force $F = av^\alpha$ acts on the satellite from the side of the microescissa, while $a$ and $\alpha$ are constants. Named $\alpha$ if it is known that the radius of the satellite’s orbit changes very slowly.

Let us immediately stipulate that we will solve the problem for the case when the orbital radius changes at a constant speed.

Work against the friction force is performed due to a decrease in the mechanical energy of the satellite.

Let a satellite of mass $m$ move with speed $v$ in a circular orbit of radius $R$.

Its kinetic energy is equal to $W_K = \frac{mv^2}{2}$, and its potential energy is $W_\pi = -\gamma \frac{mM}{R}$, where $M$ is the mass of the Earth, $\gamma$ is the gravitational constant (see, for example, the article by N. M. Speransky “Potential energy of bodies in a gravitational field”, «Kvant» No. 6, 1972).

Centripetal acceleration imparts to the satellite due to gravity. Therefore, according to Newton's second law

$$\frac{mv^2}{R} = \gamma \frac{mM}{R^2},$$

where

$$v = \left( \gamma \frac{M}{R} \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.$$

The Theler expression for $W_K$ can be written as follows:

$$W_K = \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \gamma \frac{Mm}{R}.$$

Then the total mechanical energy of the satellite is

$$W = W_K + W_\pi = -\frac{1}{2} \gamma \frac{Mm}{R}.$$

If the orbital radius during time $\Delta t$ decreases by a small amount $\Delta R (\Delta R \ll R)$, then the change in the energy of the satellite will be equal to

$$\Delta W = -\frac{1}{2} \gamma \frac{Mm}{R} - \left( -\frac{1}{2} \gamma \frac{Mm}{R - \Delta R} \right) =$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} \gamma \frac{Mm}{R(R - \Delta R)} \Delta R \approx \frac{1}{2} \gamma \frac{Mm}{R^2} \Delta R.$$

The work against the friction force during this time can be calculated as follows:

$$A = F_{\text{тр.}} v_{\text{cp}} \Delta t = av^{\alpha+1} \Delta t \approx$$

$$\approx a \left( \gamma \frac{M}{R} \right)^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}} \Delta t = \frac{1}{2} \gamma \frac{Mm}{R^2} \Delta R.$$
