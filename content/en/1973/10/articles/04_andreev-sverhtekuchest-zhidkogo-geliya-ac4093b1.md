One of the most important achievements of modern physics is the special properties of liquid gel. These properties are unexpected and paradoxical. Currently, they are all united by the understandable superfluidity - a phenomenon discovered by the Soviet physicist P. L. Kapitsa in 1938. The phenomenon of superfluidity, of course, cannot be reduced to a purely quantitative assessment, that is, to the fact that liquid gels significantly better than other liquids. The properties of a superfluid gel *), as shown in 1941 by the Soviet theoretical physicist L. D. That is why the influence that research on liquid gel had on other seemingly distant areas of physics was so great.

The quantity characteristic of thermal conductivity is the thermal conductivity coefficient associated with the ratio of heat flow to temperature difference. The greater the coefficient of thermal conductivity, the greater the heat flow transferred in a fluid from one area to another at the same temperature difference, or, in other words, the smaller the temperature difference required to provide the same heat flow.

Convection heat transfer is associated with the movement of the liquid itself. The cause of the heat flow in this case is the movement of the fluid, and not the temperature difference. Convective heat transfer can thus occur even with the same readings of thermometers $T_1$ and $T_2$.

In ordinary liquids, convection is associated with fairly large values ​​of heat flux. For liquid gel II, this cannot, of course, be the movement of individual atoms of the gel *), if only because it is precisely on this idea of ​​thermal motion that the physics of ordinary liquids is based, the laws of which, as we have seen, the properties of gel II clearly contradict. The elementary possibilities we need should be sought among some other types of thermal motion. L. D. Now the proposal about the sound nature of elementary excitations has been fully confirmed by numerous experimental studies.

There are also simple messages showing a natural such sentence. Indeed, in ordinary substances the process of freezing is the movement of individual molecules connected with the solidification of the liquid. In a solid body, molecules can perform, as noted above, only small vibrations causing certain equilibrium positions. But these are not independent vibrations of individual particles. The vibration of any molecule is immediately transmitted to its neighbors, and as a result, a vibration of the entire group of molecules, that is, the entire solid body, will arise. And these are sound waves. In ordinary substances, therefore, the process of freezing the movement of individual molecules is marked by a transition to a sound type of movement. Sound can also propagate in a liquid, but in ordinary liquids it always eventually decays simply because its energy is converted into the energy of thermal motion of individual molecules as it propagates in the liquid.

Some properties of sound waves in liquid

The walls do not entrain the liquid, but “entrain” the sound waves present in the liquid. Indeed, when sound waves “collision” with a moving rough wall, they receive from it some impulse in the direction of the wall’s movement. It is clear, therefore, that the movement of the walls leads to the appearance of a preferential direction of propagation of sound waves. In this case, as we know, a transfer of liquid mass occurs. Thus, in reality, moving walls carry away some mass with their movement, but at low temperatures the energy of sound waves is very small, and therefore the mass transferred by it is much less than the total mass of the liquid.

One can imagine that gel II consists of two components capable of moving independently of each other. The movement of one of them is not accompanied by friction, and it is therefore called the superfluid component. When another component moves, entrained by the walls, as in ordinary liquids, friction occurs; this component is called normal. The sum of the masses of both components is equal to the total mass of the liquid. It must, of course, be borne in mind that the division of Heli II into two components is purely conditional. In fact, as we have seen, two types of movements can exist in gels II, each of which is accompanied by the transfer of its own mass, and the sum of the masses is equal to the true mass of the liquid. However, of these movements it is associated with the movement of sound waves and is accompanied by friction, the second has the property of superfluidity. All atoms gels participate in both movements; in this case, there can be no talk of dividing atoms into superfluid and normal ones.

As the temperature increases, the energy of sound waves increases and at the same time the mass associated with normal motion increases. Finally, at a certain temperature it becomes equal to the true mass of the liquid.

In this case, superfluid motion disappears, since the mass it carries disappears, and gels II turns into gels I, which behaves like ordinary liquids, since only normal motion is possible in it.

If any body moves uniformly in gels II at a speed that changes the sound speed, then the mass it carries appears, and gels II transforms into gels I, which behaves like ordinary liquids, since only normal motion is possible in it.

If the resistance confluence is different from zero, then to move the body, a force must be applied to it, which will do work. However, this work can only be converted into heat, that is, almost into the radiation of sound in a liquid, which, as we know, is impossible. This result can also be formulated as the absence of pressure force on a body streamlined by superfluid motion. When sound waves meet the surface of solid bodies immersed in a liquid, they will exchange momentum with them, which we have already seen in the example of their interaction with the walls of a slot. Therefore, the flow around the normal movement of the resistance will arise by shifting the pressure force.
