What is a "centroid"?

Any modern science has its own specific language, which uses a lot of specialized terms. The great development of science leads to an increase in its vocabulary, so that often scientists of the same specialty, but of different specializations have difficulty understanding each other. Very clearly, and perhaps most clearly, this property of modern science is manifested in mathematics. Sometimes it is difficult for a mathematics teacher to understand modern work, for example, in mathematical logic. This is especially evident in connection with the introduction of a new mathematics program. In such cases, only a dictionary can help.

The product “Prosveshcheniye” has taken a valuable initiative by publishing the《Explanatory Dictionary of Mathematical Terms》*. The main purpose of this dictionary is to help mathematics teachers and schoolchildren become familiar with mathematical terminology. Enough time has passed since the publication of this book, so that it is already possible to sum up some results and evaluate to what extent the authors of the dictionaries succeeded in solving the very difficult task they faced.

First of all, the facts: published in a circulation of 102 thousand (the physics and mathematics editors only publish manuals for applicants in large editions), the dictionary disappeared from the appendices literally in 2-3 weeks. And, for example, the author of this note, having failed to purchase it in a store, was forced to resort to the help of friends. Disappeared quickly*

*) Manturov O. V., Solntsev Yu. K., Sorkin Yu. I., Fedin N. G. Explanatory dictionary of mathematical terms. M., “Enlightenment”, 1965.

The introduction of the dictionary from bookstore appendices testifies, first of all, to the urgent need for such a publication. But the fact that over the past 8 years this dictionary has been a very rare guest of second-hand bookstores (I had to go out of my way and go around several Moscow second-hand bookstores; the dialogue was something like this: “Do you have a dictionary of mathematical terms?” - No.” - “Do they exist?” - “Yes, they do, but rarely”), allows us to conclude that the dictionary turned out to be a useful owner, and, as mathematicians say, it follows that the authors of the dictionary have achieved their intended goal. Although, again, maybe the point is only that there are simply no other publications that can replace the dictionary, and “if there’s no fish, then there’s fish.”

Let's try to evaluate the content of the dictionary on its merits. Recently, in various studies, the so-called “method of examination of assessments” has become widespread, the essence of which, in a nutshell, is the following: if any object or phenomenon is difficult or even impossible to give an accurate quantitative assessment, they turn to several experts, deduce something from the assessments given by these experts, which the experts of this study of experts will consider this note. Before we give our assessment, we will try to justify it at least a little. Writing any dictionary begins with compiling a list of words that are going to be included in it. Since the dictionary is intended to help teachers and schoolchildren, the full representation of terms related to elementary mathematics in the dictionary is completely natural. Limited by the volume of the book, the authors of the device of terms related to higher mathematics are inevitably forced to be guided by their own tastes. It seems reasonable to me that the authors gravitate toward those sections of higher mathematics that are studied in the junior courses of most higher educational institutions (differential and integral calculus, higher algebra), as well as to geometric disciplines, i.e., to sections directly adjacent to elementary school mathematics and that are currently already included in sample school textbooks. Although here you can find many terms, unfairly, in my opinion, by the authors (for example, “codimension,” “signs of convergence of series.” This, however, is largely due to the size of the dictionary (approximately 1800 terms). For comparison, we note that the small two-volume mathematical dictionary published in the GDR in 1961 contains about 200 thousand words. But most German terms has Russian equivalents.
