Mechanical properties of crystals

Properties of solids such as elasticity, strength, surface tension are determined by the strength of interactions between atoms and the structure of crystals (type of crystal lattice). By studying the forces of atomic interaction, it is possible, for example, to determine the values ​​of the elastic modulus, the tensile strength of materials, the binding energy of the crystal and the surface tension coefficient.

The characteristics of any solids can be assessed in this way, but it is easiest to do this for ideal nonhydrostatic crystals. In the lattice of such crystals, positive and negative ions are periodic.

To evaluate, it is first necessary to determine the magnitude of a single mechanical bond, which in nonhydrostatic crystals is determined by the force of interaction between two ions.

Silim of mechanical interaction
The dependence of mechanical interaction forces *) on the distance between the centers of atoms in solids is shown schematically in figure 1.

From this figure one can judge some features of the forces of mechanical interaction.

1. Between atoms, gravitational and repulsive forces act simultaneously. The resultant force between atomic interactions is the sum of these two forces.

2. As the distance between atoms decreases, the repulsive force increases significantly faster than the gravitational force; therefore, there is a certain distance $r_0$ at which the gravitational and repulsive forces are balanced and the resulting force becomes equal to zero. In a crystal left to its own devices, they are located precisely at a distance of $r_0$ from each other. If the distance between atoms is less than the equilibrium one ($r < r_0$), then repulsive forces predominate; if $r > r_0$, then gravitational forces predominate.

These properties of mechanical forces allow us to conditionally consider the particles forming a crystal (for example, Na and Cl ions in a coli crystal) as solid yarns interacting with each other. Deformation of the crystal distance leads to an increase in the distance between the centers of neighboring balls and the predominance of gravitational forces, and deformation of the skatium leads to a decrease in this distance and the predominance of repulsive forces.

Estimation of the binding energy value

Knowing the magnitude of the single force of interatomic bonding, one can find the magnitude of the interaction energy of two atoms. The binding energy of two atoms is determined by the work required to remove these atoms from each other at such a distance at which the interaction forces are vanishingly small. This distance (see Fig. 1) is approximately equal to 2 $r_0$.

For simplicity, we will assume that the average value of the interaction force when the distance changes from $r_0$ to $2r_0$ is equal to $\frac{1}{2} F_{\text{max}}$. then the binding energy (per one bond) is equal to

$$W_1 = A = F \cdot l \approx F_{\text{cp}} (2r_0 - r_0) \approx \frac{F_{\text{max}}}{2} \cdot r_0 = \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2} \cdot 3 \cdot 10^{-10} = 3 \cdot 10^{-19} (\partial \chi).$$

It is interesting to compare the obtained value of the potential energy of interaction of particles in a solid body (binding energy) with the kinetic energy of their solid motion, determined on average by the value $kT$. At room temperature

$$W_K \sim kT = 1,38 \cdot 10^{-23} \cdot 300 \approx 4 \cdot 10^{-21} (\partial \chi).$$

It can be seen that $W_1 \gg W_K$, that is, in a solid body, the energy of interatomic interaction is much greater than the energy of solid motion.

Using the known value of the energy of one bond, we will find the binding energy of the crystal, that is, the energy that must be expended to divide the crystal into individual atoms, and compare the resulting value with experimental values. In experiment, the binding energy of a crystalline substance is the heat of evaporation of a solid.

The binding energy of one mole of a substance is equal to

$$W_{\text{cb}} = W_1 N' N_A,$$

where $W_1$ is the energy of one bond, $N'$ is the number of bonds coming per atom, $N_A$ is Avogadro’s number.

In the simplest lattice under consideration (see Fig. 2) there is one connection for each atom. Consequently, the magnitude of the binding energy

$$W_{\text{cb}} = 3 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 1 \cdot 6,02 \cdot 10^{23} \approx 18 \cdot 10^4 (\frac{\partial \chi}{\text{моль}}) \approx 40 (\frac{\kappa \alpha L}{\text{моль}}).$$

This value is close to the experimentally found binding energies of various crystals (several tens of kilocalories per mole).

Surface Tension Calculation

Using the considered model, it is possible to estimate the value of the specific energy surface - the energy surface coefficient - the energy surface coefficient, which a unit of surface at the boundary of a substance has ($\alpha = \frac{W}{S}$). When a crystal of section $S$ breaks, a new surface of the solid body rupture plane is formed equal to $W' = W_1 \times N_{\text{cb}}$, where $W_1$ is the energy of one bond, and $N_{\text{cb}}$ is the number of bonds per square meter. This energy is received both by the layers lying on one side of the discontinuity plane and by the layer lying on the other side.
