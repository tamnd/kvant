Evariste Galois was born on October 26, 1811 in the town of Bourg-la-Reine in the family of the director of a panson. His mother Adelaide-Marie, daughter of a doctor of laws at the University of Paris, gave her son a good liberal arts education. At the age of twelve, Evariste entered the Parisian office of Louis the Great. There he became one of the best - crushed leaves and prizes of Latin verses and translations from Greek rained down on him one after another. However, Evariste rather quickly loses interest in literature and history and remains in the second year in the classification. His work was rated as mediocre, his behavior as absent-minded, his intelligence as too young. Galois took advantage of his return to repeat the course to study in mathematics class at the same time. There his exceptional mathematical abilities were immediately revealed. Elementary books on algebra did not satisfy the young man: they lacked two scientific studies. He reads Legendre's geometry textbook without stopping, like a novel, and when he finishes reading, the entire length of the series of theorems remains firmly in his memory.

He gets real mental pleasure from reading the works of Lagrange, one of the greatest mathematicians of the 18th century. With amazing ease, Galois masters mathematical analysis. But most of all I was interested in the work of Lagrange, in which the great analyst studied the problem of solvability in radicals of algebraic equations.

Back in the 16th century, the Ilyuyan mathematicians Tartaglia and Cardno derived formulas for solving equations of the third degree, and Ferrari, Cardno’s young student, developed formulas for solving equations of the fourth degree. But then things stalled: it was not possible to derive a formula for solving fifth-degree equations. Mathematicians at that time had no doubt that such a formula existed. It seemed to everyone that the only thing was to find this formula, to create a magic combination of equation coefficients, arithmetic symbols and radicals, which would be used to solve any equation of the fifth degree. Lagrange has a peculiar way of thinking. In each issue, he sought to create broad theoretical concepts that would link into a single whole the entire multitude of individual problems, proposals and principles. He applied the same approach to the question of solving the equation in radics. He did not make up various combinations, but decided what was the reason for the success of the prims used by Cardanov, Ferrari, Euler, Tschirghausen and many others to solve equations of the 3rd and 4th degrees.

It turned out that they all did the same thing - they made up expressions from the roots of the equation, which, when the roots are rearranged, take on relatively few different values. For example, if you take a rational expression made up of 4 roots $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$ of an equation of the 4th degree, then under various rearrangements of the roots it will apply, generally speaking, 24 different values ​​(because 4 objects can be represented in 24 ways).

With such adjustments, the monomial $x_1$ will take 4 values: $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$, that is, the same as the degree of the equation; work

$$\left( x_1 - x_2 \right) \left( x_1 - x_3 \right) \left( x_1 - x_4 \right) \times \left( x_2 - x_3 \right) \left( x_2 - x_4 \right) \left( x_3 - x_4 \right)$$

with these adjustments, only 2 different values ​​are used; binomial $x_1 x_2 + x_3 x_4$ - only 3 different values. Lagrange proved that if $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are the roots of an equation of $n$th degree, then the number of rearrangements $k$, which do not change the form of some rational expression $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$, is a divisor of the number $n!(n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot \dots \cdot n)$, and this expression itself satisfies an equation of $n! / k$th degree, the coefficients of which can be expressed through the coefficients given equation.

By analyzing all possible expressions made up of the roots of a given equation, and permutations that leave these expressions unchanged, Lagrange proved that if $p$ is a prime number, then the solution of any equation of degree $p$ indicates the solution of an equation of degree $(p - 2)!$. When $p = 3$ we have $(p - 2)! = 1$, the first degree equation is solved. If $p = 5$, then $(p - 2)! = 3! = 6$, that is, the solution of an equation of the fifth degree indicates the solution of an equation of the sixth degree. “It follows from this,” Lagrange wrote, “that it is necessary that the methods we have considered can give a complete solution to the fifth degree equation.” The main thing in Lagrange’s work was that he established a connection between the solution of an equation in radicals and permutations of roots. He called this connection “the true philosophy of solving the equation.”

Lagrange's work opened up a new world for Galois. All his thoughts are directed towards mathematics. Stuffed with history, literature, rhetoric; teachers of these sciences write that “his abilities are nothing more than a legend that it’s time to stop believing...”. Further, the mathematics teacher Verni, who had a very high opinion of Galois’s talents, considers Evariste’s behavior very bad, and himself as hidden and proud. Are there such permutations for other equations? After all, for them too you can make correspondences between the roots and see under what permutations the correct correspondences will turn into correct ones. Of course, the best ones are equations whose roots are rational numbers. Such equations are solved without extracting roots. For such equations there are very simple correspondences between the roots $x_1 = b_1, \dots, x_n = b_n$, and the only "permutation" that preserves these correspondences is the identity permutation. It is unlikely that, having used this name, Evariste thought that he saw in mathematics a new concept that was the essence of duty and a glorious life, that the number of works associated with groups would amount to many thousands, that the methods of group theory would reveal the secrets of crystal solutions, the atom, and much more. He just wanted to see what happened to groups of permutations of roots in the process of solving equations.

Of course, Galya was not the first to deal with groups of root permutations. Both Lagrange and Gauss were already working on them. Galya himself realized that the ideas he expressed were contained in a neutral form in the works of his predecessors.

But great is the merit of the one who clarified such ideas, formulated the essential properties of concepts, and applied them to the solution of new and difficult problems. This is what Galya did for the concept of a group - only after his work did it become a subject of study for mathematicians.

However, let's return to Galya's line of reasoning. Now we need to carefully look at what correspondences there may be between the roots of the equation. It depends on what coefficients are considered acceptable in such correspondences. Let's take the equation

$$x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1 = 0.$$

If only rational coefficients are allowed in a correspondence, then for its roots there are only Vieta correspondences, correspondences of the form $x_k = x_1^k$ and those that are obtained from them using rational operations. But if we assume coefficients of a more complex form, then new correspondences will appear.

The wider the set of coefficients, the greater the correspondence between the roots. And then permutations that keep all the old correspondences true may already turn out to be non-productive: they can violate the new correspondences. This means that with the expansion of the set of coefficients, the group of the equation decreases. But if the equation is solved, then its group turns into a unit group, that is, into a group consisting of one identical permutation. So this is the secret of solving the equation! It remains to find out what the group of the equation must be so that it can be solved in radics. A similar result appeared in 1829 in the Berlin Krehl's Journal. Its author was the famous Abel.

He proved that an equation has a solution in radicals if all its roots can be expressed as rational functions of one root $x_k = F_k(x_1)$, and these functions must have the following commutative property: $F_k(F_l(x_1)) = F_l(F_k(x))$.
