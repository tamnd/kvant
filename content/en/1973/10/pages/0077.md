⟦folio 75⟧

To the article “Estimates of angles”

2. If the point $D$ is inside the triangle $ABC$, then at least one of the 6 angles into which $AD$, $BD$ and $CD$ divide the angles of the triangle is not greater than $30^\circ$. Because of this, it is enough for us to consider the case when the convex hull of points does not contain a single point inside itself. Next, notice that the sum of all the angles of a $n$-gon is $180^\circ$ ($n - 2$), and the diagonals divide these angles into $n$ ($n - 2$) parts. See also instructions for task 3.

3. Suppose that the points $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ are numbered so that the $A_nA_1A_2$ angle of the $k$-gon $M$ ($k \leq n$), which is the convex hull of these points, and that the rays $A_1A_2, A_1A_3, A_1A_4, \dots, A_1A_{n-1}, A_1A_n$ follow one after the other in exactly this order (all these rays pass inside or along the boundary of the angle $A_2A_1A_n$; some of them may match). If $B$ is a point on the extension of ray $A_1A_n$ beyond $A_1$, then $180^\circ = BA_1A_n = BA_1A_2 + BA_2A_1A_3 + A_3A_1A_4 + \dots + A_{n-1}A_1A_n = A_1A_2A_n + A_1A_nA_2 + A_2A_1A_3 + \dots + A_{n-1}A_1A_n$. Therefore, if all angles $A_iA_jA_k$ are not less than $\beta$, then $180^\circ \geq n\beta$, hence $\beta \leq \frac{180^\circ}{n}$.

The value $\frac{180^\circ}{n}$ of angle $\beta$ is achieved for a system of $n$ points coinciding with the vertices of a regular $n$-gon. Moreover, in order for the above inequality to turn into equality for each vertex of the polygon $M$, it is necessary and sufficient that $M$ be a regular $n$-gon.

4. a) The convex hull of 4 points can be either a quadrilateral, or a triangle containing the fourth point, or a segment. In the first case, at least one of the angles of the quadrilateral is not less than $90^\circ$; in the second case, at least one of the “internal” angles is not less than $120^\circ$; in the third case, the angles are not less than $120^\circ$.

6) The convex hull of 5 points can be either a pentagon, or a quadrilateral, or a triangle, or a segment (the latter case is not of interest, here $\alpha = 180^\circ$). In the first case, at least one of the angles of the pentagon is not less than $\frac{3}{5} \cdot 180^\circ = 108^\circ$, in the third case, at least one of the angles is not less than $120^\circ$. Finally, if the convex hull is a quadrilateral, then the fifth point lies inside one of the two triangles into which the quadrilateral divides its diagonal; Thus, this case is reduced to what has already been discussed.

b) If the convex hull is a hexagon, then at least one of its angles is not less than $\frac{4}{6} \cdot 180^\circ = 120^\circ$; otherwise, it will find either 3 points lying on the same line (then $\alpha = 180^\circ$), or a point lying inside a triangle formed by three other points (this triangle is either the convex hull of these points, or one of the parts of the convex hull, on which it is cut by diagonals emerging from one vertex).

For the case of a quadrilateral, $\alpha = 90^\circ$ is achieved for the vertices of any rectangle; similarly, in Problems 46 and 48, the smallest value of $\alpha$ is achieved for the vertices of a convex polygon with equal angles.

5. Since for a hexagon the angles formed by it are no less than $120^\circ$, this is also true for any large number of points. At the same time, the value $\alpha = 120^\circ$ for a larger number of points is not enough, since otherwise the points from the considered (different!) points must be the vertices of a hexagon with angles of $120^\circ$, which is impossible.

Next, it is enough to consider a (say, centrally symmetric) hexagon $A_1A_2A_3A_4A_5A_6$ with angles of $120^\circ$, and such that $A_1A_2 \ll A_2A_3 \ll A_3A_4$ (the sign “means “much less”), point $A_7A_8$ is located on the bisectors of angles $A_3$ and $A_6$ of the hexagon at the same and very small distance from the vertices $A_3$ and $A_6$; then the most angles formed by points $A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4, A_5, A_6, A_7, A_8$ will slightly exceed $120^\circ$ (make an approximate drawing).

6. a) If the convex hull $M$ of these points is a segment, then $\alpha^{(2)} = 180^\circ$; if $M$ is a triangle, then at least two “internal” angles are at least $90^\circ$. Now let $M$ be the quadrilateral $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ and $A_1$ be the largest angle of this quadrilateral; then $A_1A_2A_3 + A_2A_3A_4 + A_3A_4A_1 + A_4A_1A_3 + A_2A_1A_3 = 360^\circ$, and therefore at least one of these angles is not less than $\frac{1}{5} \cdot 360^\circ = 72^\circ$. The value $\alpha^{(2)} = 72^\circ$ is achieved if all angles considered are equal to $72^\circ$.

6) If the convex hull $M$ of these points is a segment, then $\beta^{(2)} = 0^\circ$; if $M$ is a triangle, then the two smallest angles $M$ are acute, each of them is divided into two parts, the smaller of the parts is not larger than $45^\circ$. If $M$ is the quadrilateral $A_1A_2A_3A_4$, then $A_1A_2A_4 + A_2A_4A_1 + A_2A_1A_3 + A_4A_1A_3 = 180^\circ$ and $A_4A_2A_3 + A_2A_4A_1 = 180^\circ$; in each of these sums at least one of the angles is not greater than $45^\circ$.

The value $\beta^{(2)} = 45^\circ$ is implemented, for example, for a square.

7. Consider $n$ points lying on the same line.
