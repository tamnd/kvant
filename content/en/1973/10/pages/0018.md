In order for the conductive heat flux to be different from zero, it is therefore necessary that thermometer $T_1$ shows a higher temperature than thermometer $T_2$. The quantity characteristic of thermal conductivity is the thermal conductivity coefficient associated with the ratio of heat flow to temperature difference. The greater the coefficient of thermal conductivity, the greater the heat flow transferred in a fluid from one area to another at the same temperature difference, or, in other words, the smaller the temperature difference required to provide the same heat flow.

Convection heat transfer is associated with the movement of the liquid itself. If in the experiment under consideration the liquid, due to some principles, moves in the direction from left to right, then heat transfer occurs in this direction, since the moving liquid has thermal energy. The cause of the heat flow in this case is the movement of the fluid, and not the temperature difference. Convective heat transfer can thus occur even with the same readings of thermometers $T_1$ and $T_2$.

In ordinary liquids, convection is associated with fairly large values ​​of heat flux. If the heat flux is small, then the mechanism of re-ossification of the body is purely thermally conductive, and we can determine the thermal conductivity coefficient of the liquid by experimentally measuring the heat flux and temperature difference.

If we carry out this experiment with body II, we will find that any small convection of heat is re-ossified through the liquid with absolutely equal readings of thermometers $T_1$ and $T_2$. Two conclusions can be drawn from this result.
