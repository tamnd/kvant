⟦folio 9⟧

there is a rearrangement

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 3 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

This will result in 4 different permutations of roots, including the identical permutation

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix},$$

in which all roots remain in place. These permutations, and only they, transform the correspondences $x_k = x_1^k$ into other correspondences, which holds for the roots of equation (1) at $n = 4$. And here's what's interesting. If you first make one such permutation, and then another, the result will be a permutation that also transforms correct correspondences into correct ones. These permutations can be multiplied like a number.

Are there such permutations for other equations? After all, for them too you can make correspondences between the roots and see under what permutations the correct correspondences will turn into correct ones. Of course, the best ones are equations whose roots are rational numbers. Such equations are solved without extracting roots. For such equations there are very simple correspondences between the roots $x_1 = b_1, \dots, x_n = b_n$, and the only "permutation" that preserves these correspondences is the identity permutation. This means that the equation is simpler, the smaller the set of permutations that preserve the correspondence between the roots! Such collections of permutations should be named somehow. These permutations come together, are grouped. Shouldn't we call their totality *erpnoi*?

It is unlikely that, having used this name, Evariste thought that he saw in mathematics a new concept that was the essence of duty and a glorious life, that the number of works associated with groups would amount to many thousands, that the methods of group theory would reveal the secrets of crystal solutions, the atom, and much more. He just wanted to see what happened to groups of permutations of roots in the process of solving equations.

Of course, Galya was not the first to deal with groups of root permutations. Both Lagrange and Gauss were already working on them. Galya himself realized that the ideas he expressed were contained in a neutral form in the works of his predecessors.

But great is the merit of the one who clarified such ideas, formulated the essential properties of concepts, and applied them to the solution of new and difficult problems. This is what Galya did for the concept of a group - only after his work did it become a subject of study for mathematicians.

However, let's return to Galya's line of reasoning. Now we need to carefully look at what correspondences there may be between the roots of the equation. It depends on what coefficients are considered acceptable in such correspondences. Let's take the equation

$$x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1 = 0.$$

If only rational coefficients are allowed in a correspondence, then for its roots there are only Vieta correspondences, correspondences of the form $x_k = x_1^k$ and those that are obtained from them using rational operations. But if we assume coefficients of a more complex form, then new correspondences will appear.

The wider the set of coefficients, the greater the correspondence between the roots. And then permutations that keep all the old correspondences true may already turn out to be non-productive: they can violate the new correspondences. This means that with the expansion of the set of coefficients, the group of the equation decreases. But if the equation is solved, then its group turns into a unit group, that is, into a group consisting of one identical permutation. So this is the secret of solving the equation! It is necessary to expand the set of coefficients and monitor how the group of the equation changes (after many decades).
