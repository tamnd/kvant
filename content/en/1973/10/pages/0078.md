6) By distance is the “convex hull” of our points, that is, the smallest convex polyhedron $M$, containing within itself or on the boundary all these points ($M$ can be imagined as a polyhedron limited by a “rubber film” stretched over our points and trying to reduce its surface).

In the case under consideration, none of the points can be located inside $M$ and all faces $M$ are acute triangles (due to the result of Problem 16a).

Let now five points satisfy the conditions of the problem. In this case, $M$ has five vertices, that is, if $P$ and $\Gamma$ are the number of edges and faces of the polyhedron $M$, then

$$5 - P + \Gamma = 2$$

(Euler’s theorem; see, for example, «Kvant» No. 4, 1972, articles by A.P. Savin “Maps and coloring pages”). On the other hand, since each of the $\Gamma$ faces contains an edge path and each edge belongs to two faces, then $3\Gamma = 2P$, that is

$$P = \frac{3}{2}\Gamma,$$

from where you can immediately find that $\Gamma = 6$ and $P = 9$. On the other hand, if $B_3$ and $B_4$ are the number of 3-sided and 4-sided angles of $M$, then $B_3 + B_4 = 5$ and $3B_3 + 4B_4 = 2P = 18$, which implies that $B_3 = 2$ and $B_4 = 3$. This establishes that $M$ is necessarily a “triangular bipyramid”, that is, it consists of two triangular paramids $ABCD$ and $ABCE$, folded by a common base $ABC$.

Now, using the fact that all 10 triangles $ABC, ABD, \dots, CDE$ are acute triangles, using the cosine theorem it is established that, for example,

$$AB^2 + AC^2 > DB^2 + DC^2$$

(this inequality will be needed later).
