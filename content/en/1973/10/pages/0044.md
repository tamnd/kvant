How to determine the poles of a magnet?

B. I. Aleinikov

This question is not at all as simple as it seems at first glance. After all, we have no certainty that the poles of the magnet were not painted in different colors simply to spread them, that is, without connection with their true magnetism. In addition, the magnet may not be marked at all.

For the experiment, you need two things - a permanent horseshoe-shaped magnet, not necessarily painted in two colors (further it is more interesting if the poles of the magnet are not marked in advance), and... a television. It is best to carry out the experiment in the morning or afternoon, when the television broadcasts a grid for tuning televisions.

Let's bring the magnet to the screen of the switched on TV as shown in figure. The image will not slowly become distorted. There is a small circle in the center of the grid. It will move noticeably up or down, depending on how the magnet's poles are positioned.

The image on the TV screen creates an electron beam that searches from the depths of the tube to the eritel. Our magnet deflects the moving electrons, so the image shifts. The direction of action of the magnetic field on a moving charge is determined by the right of the left hand. You need to position your palm so that the drain lines enter it, and extend your fingers to indicate the direction of the current; then the finger bent at an angle of 90° indicates the direction of displacement of the driving charge. The power lines run from the server magnet to the south pole. And the direction of the current in the left-hand rule is considered to be the “technical” direction - from pliosa to minute. This is how positive charges would move. But electrons move in the film, and they fly towards us. This is equivalent to the fact that positive charges are flying away from us. Therefore, extend the fingers of your left hand should be directed towards the screen. The rest is clear. By the displacement of the central circle of the television grid, we determine which strip of the magnet is brought to the screen, north or south. So, using a TV you can easily and quickly determine the location of the poles of an unmarked magnet.

The TV can also help in determining the pole sign of an unmarked battery. For this experiment you need to have a battery, an electromagnet with a double core, a TV, a resistance, and a conductor.

Let us connect an electromagnet and a resistance in series to the battery, selected so that the current does not exceed the permissible value. When bringing an electromagnet to the TV screen, we determine its poles using the left-hand rule. Next, using the gimlet rule, we will find the direction of the current and, therefore, the signs of the battery poles.
