Galya explores how the continued fraction expansions of the two roots of an equation are related to each other.

This note is just the first problem. At the age of seventeen, Galya made much more important discoveries in the theory of equations and sent work to the Academy of Sciences. The most famous of the French mathematicians of that time, Cauchy, undertook to present his work, but the academician was too busy, and the work of the young artist was simply lost.

Galya no longer had time to continue these studies: it was necessary to prepare for a second attempt to build a Polytechnic School. This time, neither Evariste nor his teacher Richard had any doubts about success - the exams should not be taken by an arrogant schoolboy, but by a deep knowledge of mathematics, the author of scientific works.

Once again Evariste stands before the examiners. Brown hair, myopically squinted eyes. Worried and pale, he looks younger than his seventeen and a half years. Hands nervously crumble chalk. “Tell me what you know about logarithms.” And Evarist’s appearance gives way to anger: “I’m not a schoolboy! I won’t answer such a simple question!” Then Galya was asked to solve the most difficult equation. In a few minutes he sketched out an original solution. Not understanding what was written, the examiner took over. After a short argument, an infuriated Evarist threw a body-wiping cloth at the examiner. It was not only a gesture of anger, but also a gesture of despair: Galya realized that what he passionately dreamed of was eluding him forever.
