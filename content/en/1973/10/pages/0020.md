For liquid gel II, this cannot, of course, be the movement of individual atoms of the gel *), if only because it is precisely on this idea of ​​thermal motion that the physics of ordinary liquids is based, the laws of which, as we have seen, the properties of gel II clearly contradict. The elementary possibilities we need should be sought among some other types of thermal motion. L. D. Landu proposed that the elementary excitations of gel II are not the movements of individual atoms, but the collective movements of atoms of the liquid, that is, sound vibrations, and showed that on the basis of this proposal it is possible to construct a consistent theory of superfluidity. Now the proposal about the sound nature of elementary excitations has been fully confirmed by numerous experimental studies.

There are also simple messages showing a natural such sentence. Indeed, in ordinary substances the process of freezing is the movement of individual molecules connected with the solidification of the liquid. In a solid body, molecules can perform, as noted above, only small vibrations causing certain equilibrium positions. But these are not independent vibrations of individual particles. The vibration of any molecule is immediately transmitted to its neighbors, and as a result, a vibration of the entire group of molecules, that is, the entire solid body, will arise. And these are sound waves. In ordinary substances, therefore, the process of freezing the movement of individual molecules is marked by a transition to a sound type of movement. Sound can also propagate in a liquid, but in ordinary liquids it always eventually decays simply because its energy is converted into the energy of thermal motion of individual molecules as it propagates in the liquid.
