And you need to expand the set of coefficients by adding to it the roots of some auxiliary equations. It is now clear which auxiliary equations are good - only those whose addition of roots to an admissible set of coefficients reduces the Galois group.

It remains to find out what the group of the equation must be so that it can be solved in radics. A similar result appeared in 1829 in the Berlin Krehl's Journal. Its author was the famous Abel.

He proved that an equation has a solution in radicals if all its roots can be expressed as rational functions of one root $x_k = F_k(x_1)$, and these functions must have the following commutative property: $F_k(F_l(x_1)) = F_l(F_k(x))$. In this case, permutations preserving the correspondences $x_k = F_k(x_1)$ have the same properties of commutativity - their product does not depend on the order of the communitarians (this is not the case for any permutations).

With a powerful effort of mind, Galois solves the problem to the end. It turns out that an equation has a solution in radicals if and only if its group has properties defined by the image of commutative permutation groups (we will not say here more precisely what this means). At the same time, Galois introduces the concepts of a normal divisor, a coset, a solvable group, and so on, which are fundamental for the entire theory of groups. The magic key has been found - group theory reveals the secrets of the equation.

Before Galois unfolds a grand plan for a whole series of scientific works that will revolutionize mathematics... And here he faces a moral problem, which we learned about from a note preserved on a piece of paper:

“A person who has an idea has a choice: either to enjoy the colossal reputation of a scientist during his life, or to leave a larger name in the future. The first occurs if you apply your idea without expressing it; second - if it is published...”

For Evariste, the choice is clear - his ideas belong to the entire world of science. He wants to present all the most important things in his research, to present them conscientiously, without subterfuge, indicating the path that led him to these results, noting all the difficulties and unresolved questions.
