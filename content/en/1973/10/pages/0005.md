“I don’t have time... I don’t have time!” These words, scribbled on a May night in 1832 in almost indecipherable scrawls on a piece of paper covered entirely with algebraic formulas, scream about the most amazing and tragic fate that has ever befallen a scientist - the fate of Evariste Galois. An unrecognized genius, an outcast scientist, lonely and unapologetically honest. On the night before the duel, the twenty-year-old young man wrote for the last time, writing, saying goodbye to friends, to science, to life.

Evariste Galois was born on October 26, 1811 in the town of Bourg-la-Reine in the family of the director of a panson. His mother Adelaide-Marie, daughter of a doctor of laws at the University of Paris, gave her son a good liberal arts education. His favorite authors - Plutarch and Titus Livy, Corneille and Racine - taught that there is no greater happiness than to give your life for the freedom of your homeland.

At the age of twelve, Evariste entered the Parisian office of Louis the Great. There he became one of the best - crushed leaves and prizes of Latin verses and translations from Greek rained down on him one after another. However, Evariste rather quickly loses interest in literature and history and remains in the second year in the classification. His work was rated as mediocre, his behavior as absent-minded, his intelligence as too young. Galois took advantage of his return to repeat the course to study in mathematics class at the same time. There his exceptional mathematical abilities were immediately revealed. Elementary books on algebra did not satisfy the young man: they lacked two scientific studies. He reads Legendre's geometry textbook without stopping, like a novel, and when he finishes reading, the entire length of the series of theorems remains firmly in his memory.

He gets real mental pleasure from reading the works of Lagrange, one of the greatest mathematicians of the 18th century. With amazing ease, Galois masters mathematical analysis. But most of all I was interested in the work of Lagrange, in which the great analyst studied the problem of solvability in radicals of algebraic equations.

Back in the 16th century, the Ilyuyan mathematicians Tartaglia and Cardno derived formulas for solving equations of the third degree, and Ferrari, Cardno’s young student, developed formulas for solving equations of the fourth degree. But then things stalled: it was not possible to derive a formula for solving fifth-degree equations. Mathematicians at that time had no doubt that such a formula existed. It seemed to everyone that the only thing was to find this formula, to create a magic combination of equation coefficients, arithmetic symbols and radicals, which would be used to solve any equation of the fifth degree. But centuries passed, and the combination failed
