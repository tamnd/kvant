Echolocation

M. S. Livits

Inhomogeneities encountered along the path of waves scatter them in all directions, including the opposite direction. In other words, inhomogeneities in the medium themselves become sources (secondary) waves, and from them, as they say, comes an echo.

The phenomenon of echo is used for location, that is, determining the location of various objects using radio waves (radiolocation) or sound waves (soundlocation).

Waves. Colbation. Radiation

When they talk about waves, we involuntarily imagine waves on water. This is the only truly visual form of waves. Sound waves—waves of inclination and rarefaction in an elastic medium—are no longer easy to imagine; and even more hearing is required to represent radio waves and light waves.

It turns out that it is unexpectedly difficult to even give an exact definition of the concept of a wave. Please, the most general one would be:

A wave is a process in which the spatial distribution of some physical quantity periodically changes over time.

In the simplest case, which we observe on water, the waves are a periodic crossing of humps and vadines. For waves of other physical nature, the “hump” should be understood as the place in the wave where a given physical quantity (for example, the density of the medium in the case of sound waves, the directivity of the electrical

ric or magnetic field in the case of electromagnetic waves) reaches its maximum value, and the “valley” is the place where this value reaches its minimum value.

The distance between two adjacent humps (or valleys, or other points that are in the same states) is called the wavelength and is denoted by λ (Fig. 1). Wavelength is a very important characteristic.

If we focus our attention on some point in space where waves exist, we will discover a periodic change in the corresponding physical quantity only in time, called an oscillation. There is a connection between oscillations and waves. It turns out that a wave, which is a set of oscillations at all its points, will not arise on its own. There are some special points at which oscillations are sources of waves. In this regard, it is worthwhile to give another definition of a wave.

A wave is a process of distribution of vibrations in a medium.

Indeed, in order for waves to exist on water, you need to throw a stone into it or immerse some kind of vibrator (for example, an oscillating rod) into it. Sound waves exist if we speak or if a violin string sounds. Radio waves exist if there is a radio transmitter with an antenna. And so on.

The reverse process also takes place: if on the path of a wave, distribution in a certain medium, there is an oscillating one, the properties of which differ -

Now let's turn to the main question: how can you notice the presence of secondary waves (echoes)? After all, if we cannot identify this or that echo, then we will not be able to detect its source, which is called the target in echolocation.

It is difficult to come up with a simpler answer than this: it is necessary that no other waves enter the receiver during the arrival of this echo.

This is how the main feature of the vast majority of locors arises: operation in pulse mode. This means that the primary periodic emitter emits a group of waves (a pulse of duration τ, length cτ (Fig. 4)), and then does not work for a long time (compared to the duration of the pulse). It is during this period of “silence” that they come

reflected impulses from various targets.

The shorter the pulse emitted by the radar antenna (probe pulse), the greater the number of targets in the same volume of space that can be detected separately. Indeed, if the range to two targets differs by such an amount that the secondary (reflected) trains (that is, groups) of waves from them do not overlap, the targets will be observed separately by the radar (Fig. 5).

Thus, the pulse duration determines the so-called range resolution δD ~ τ.

In addition, long-term observation of several targets, the directions of which are different, ensures the use of emitters with a narrow directional pattern (Fig. 6).

Here we are already talking about angular resolution. Indeed, if the directions on the target differ by an angle greater than the width of the direction diagram (angle α), then they, even at the same range, will not be visible as one target. Selecting a wave range for a location

Now we are sufficiently prepared to understand why ultrashort radio waves (VHF) are used in radar, that is, waves whose wavelengths lie in the meter, decimeter and centimeter ranges.

Let's look at a few reasons.

1) For VHF, it is easy to ensure acceptable sizes of radar antennas that have a narrow radiation pattern (to ensure good angular resolution) and sufficiently intense radiation, since the main element of such antennas is the resulting vibrator.

2) The sizes of ordinary targets (airplanes, ships) will be quite large compared to acceptable wavelengths, which favors an increase in the intensity of the reflected signals (in accordance with the diagram of the curve at $d \gg \lambda/2$ to figure 2).

3) It is technically difficult to generate a pulse shorter than at least a tenth of a wavelength, so the smaller $\lambda$, the easier it is to ensure the formation of such a pulse, and therefore to obtain good range resolution.

In it, the position of the point is determined by the following coordinates: radius vector $D$ (in radar called overhead range); azimuth $\varphi$ (angle in the horizontal plane); elevation angle $\varepsilon$ (angle in the vertical plane).

Thus, for a location it is necessary to change the data and angular coordinates.

When the locator antenna emits a probing pulse, it is necessary to determine the time $t'$ for this group of waves to reach the target. As soon as the waves reach the target, the target becomes a secondary emitter, and part of the energy of the scattered (reflected) waves goes back, that is, to the locator antenna. When the speed of wave propagation is much greater than the speed of movement of the target, the displacement of the target during this time can be neglected. The reflected (secondary) pulse must travel from the target to the locator in the same time $t'$, be absorbed by its receiving antenna and arrive at the receiver input. In this case, the wave energy is converted into electric current energy and amplified by the receiver for subsequent transmission of the locator measuring devices. Note that the beneficial process here is the absorption of wave energy by the antenna.

What time intervals and with what accuracy does radar measure?

Typically, distances of tens of kilometers should be measured with an error not exceeding tens of meters. This means, as can be calculated using formula (1) (speed of light $c = 300\ 000\ \text{km/c}$), one must be able to measure time intervals of the order of hundreds of microseconds ($1\ \text{mkc} = 10^{-6}\ \text{c}$) with an accuracy of tenths of microseconds. No mechanical watch is capable of making such measurements: the mass (inertia) of the parts of its mechanism is too large; Only electronic devices, a consequence of the low inertia of electrons, can perform this task. Indicators

For a clearer idea of ​​how coordinates are measured in radars in accordance with the considered principles of measuring range and angle, let’s look at the indicators used for this purpose.

As examples, let's look at two types of indicators.

The first one provides a visual representation of the entire area in the surrounding space; the second grateful downtime can be used in individual radar devices (as radars are often called).

1. Almost all locators have an all-round visibility indicator. In it, the electron beam moves so that its “edge” draws a radial line on the screen (from the center to the edge of the screen). This is carried out by the so-called range sweep: the position of the illuminated point in the center of the screen corresponds to zero range (the location of the locator at the moment the probing pulse is sent), and the position of the illuminated point on the edge of the screen

corresponds to the maximum detection range. Between these points the entire range scale of the locator is located.

The direction along which the radial straight line is scanned is determined by the azimuth of the radiation of radio waves by the radio antenna, that is, when the radiation pattern of the antenna (“beam” of the antenna) is rotated, the sweep rotates synchronously (at the same time). The elevation angle is not determined in such an indicator.

In the absence of reflective objects, the electron beam is “extinguished.” The signal reflected from the object “supports” the beam, and a light spot appears in the corresponding place on the screen (a typical image on the screen is shown at figure 10). In order to see the whole picture at the same time, these indicators use a luminescent screen such that the glow excited anywhere on it lasts the entire time the antenna “beam” makes one revolution.

2. The simplest indicator is an indicator on the screen of which a range sweep is carried out along the horizontal diameter, and the magnitude of the vertical deviation of the spot (pulse amplitude) is determined by the magnitude of the signal from the target (Fig. 11).

This indicator uses a screen without afterglow, and the picture changes as the antenna “beam” rotates in space, so the angle of the “beam” must be counted on the antenna scale at the moment when the amplitude of the corresponding pulse on the screen reaches its maximum.

“Locals”, passive and active landowners

Each technical achievement does not come with its own weakness, to overcome which one comes to delve even deeper into the physics of the using phenomenon. This can be seen in several positional examples from radar.

1. In mountainous areas, the indicator screens turn out to be “clogged” with so-called “local” impulses, that is, impulses reflected from cold weather, rocks and other uneven terrain. This makes it difficult or even impossible to detect useful signals from the target. The same thing is observed near large populated areas.

Large rooms are also created by reflections from various areas, clouds and the like.

2. As we have already said, when placing defects close to

It turns out that a few hundred grams of foil or metallized fiberglass is enough to create the same reflected impulse as from an airplane.

Does this mean that nothing can be done either with the “locals” or with passive premises? Of course not.

The fact is that the sources of “local” sources are generally motionless, and clouds and passive spaces move at wind speed, which is usually much less than the speed of an airplane. This is what is used to set up the room. The principle of operation of one of the corresponding communication devices with the so-called interspersed subtraction. The essence of the latter is as follows: if the signals received during two successive soundings are applied to the indicator simultaneously, but with opposite signs, then the localists will be destroyed, and the signals from fast-moving targets that have changed their position during the repetition period will be preserved.

3. Signals from targets arriving at the radio receiver are many orders of magnitude weaker than the probing pulse. Consequently, a radiar is characterized by a combination of high power of the transmitter probing pulse with high sensitivity of the receiver. This is the only way to ensure a greater target detection range.

But this strength of radium immediately turns into its weakness.

Firstly, powerful probing pulses from the locator can actually be detected long before the aircraft is detected by the locator (since in this case the radio waves travel “one way”).

Secondly, the highly sensitive locator receiver can easily be “clogged” with local signals from indoor transmitters installed on an airplane (these transmitters can also be triggered by a parachute and the like).

This will create another way to combat radar - with the help of active missiles. However, there are ways to overcome them.

Features of sound provocation

For sound provocation, it exists that the speed of propagation of sound waves is many orders of magnitude less than the speed of propagation of electromagnetic waves (in air, the speed of sound under normal conditions is 331 m/s, that is, almost a million times less than the speed of light). It makes no sense, for example, to try to use sound provocation to determine the coordinates of a modern supersonic aircraft: the sound simply will not catch up with it. For hydrolocation (sound calling in water), the situation is better: the speed of sound in water is approximately 1500 m/s, and the speed of movement of the “target” under water is much less (about 10 m/s). In addition, for the benefit of the message: electromagnetic waves propagate very poorly in water (they are strongly attenuated, since water, especially sea water, is a fairly good conductor). Therefore, you cannot do without sound in water.

As for living nature, not only in water, but also in air, the speed of sound fully ensures the success of echolocation. This is why “live locators” use sound waves (ultrasound to be precise). For example, that sounds audible to humans cover the range of vibration frequencies from 20 to 20,000 Hz. Sounds higher in frequency than 20,000 Hz are called ultrasounds.

Some of the most advanced “living locators” - bats - use ultrasonic waves in the air for location, corresponding to the principle from 50 kHz to 100 kHz. And fishermen who use ultrasound in water “work” at even higher frequencies.
