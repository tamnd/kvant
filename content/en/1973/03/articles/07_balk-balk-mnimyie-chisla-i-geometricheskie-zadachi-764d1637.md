Imaginary numbers and geometric problems

G. D. Balk, M. B. Balk

What relation can square roots of negative numbers have to those given in the field of geometry? Is it possible for a person solving a planning problem to realize any benefit from the fact that in algebra numbers of the form $a + b\sqrt{-1}$ are considered?

Euler's formula and Myabra's formula
An expression of the form $\cos \varphi + i \sin \varphi$ appears frequently in mathematics and its applications. Various abbreviations are used for it. For example, in potatoes it is denoted by the sign $1_\varphi$, and in works on mathematics - by $exp(i\varphi)$ or $e^{i\varphi}$. Thus, by definition

$$e^{i\varphi} = \cos \varphi + i \sin \varphi.$$

Rule 10. What complex numbers are given by the expressions:

$$e^{i\cdot 0}; e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}; e^{i\frac{\pi}{3}}; e^{i(-\frac{\pi}{4})}?$$

Notation (1) is determined by the similarity in the properties of expressions of the form $e^x$ (a power with a real exponent $x$) and expressions of the form $e^{i\varphi}$. In fact, it is known that for real $x$, $x_1$ and integer $n$ the following formulas are true:

$$e^x \cdot e^{x_1} = e^x + x_1$$

$$e^{-x} = \frac{1}{e^x},$$

$$(e^x)^n = e^{nx}.$$

In other words, with a variable degree with the same base, the indicators are added; When any power is raised to a whole power, the indicators change. For example, for an expression of the form $e^{i\varphi}$, similar formulas (below $\varphi$, $\varphi_1$ are arbitrary real numbers, $n$ are any integer $^*$):

$$e^{i\varphi} \cdot e^{i\varphi_1} = e^{i(\varphi + \varphi_1)}$$

$$e^{-i\varphi} = \frac{1}{e^{i\varphi}},$$

$$(e^{i\varphi})^n = e^{in\varphi}.$$

Are these equalities true? It turns out yes. You can verify this by using the formulas for adding trionometric functions. Validity of formulas $(2') - (4')$ and definition, introduction of notation (1).

Let us prove the formulas $(2') - (4')$.

$$e^{i\varphi} \cdot e^{i\varphi_1} = (\cos \varphi + i \sin \varphi) (\cos \varphi_1 + i \sin \varphi_1) = (\cos \varphi \cdot \cos \varphi_1 - \sin \varphi \cdot \sin \varphi_1) + i (\sin \varphi \cdot \cos \varphi_1 + \cos \varphi \cdot \sin \varphi_1) = \cos (\varphi + \varphi_1) + i \sin (\varphi + \varphi_1) = e^{i(\varphi + \varphi_1)}$$.

$$(\cos \varphi + i \sin \varphi)^n = \cos n \varphi + i \sin n \varphi.$$

One geometry problem

Task 1. Draw an arbitrary straight line through the center of a regular n-unit and calculate the sum S of squared distances from the vertices of the polygon to this straight line. Will the resulting amount be affected by the choice of the straight line?

Solution. Let (Fig. 7) $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ be the given polygon, $R$ the radius of the circumcircle, $O$ its center, $MN$ the line passing through O. Let $\Rightarrow NOA_1 = \alpha$. Poling $\varphi = \frac{2\pi}{n}$, we have

$$S = R^2 \left\{ \sin^2 \alpha + \sin^2 (\alpha + \varphi) \right. + \sin^2 (\alpha + 2\varphi) + \dots + \sin^2 [\alpha + (n-1)\varphi] \}.$$

We need to find out whether S depends on $\alpha$. It is clear that $S = \frac{1}{2} R^2 (n-T)$, where

$$T = \cos 2\alpha + \cos (2\alpha + 2\varphi) + \cos (2\alpha + 4\varphi) + \dots + \cos [2\alpha + (2n-2)\varphi].$$

Let us calculate the sum T. From Euler’s formula it is clear that T is the real part of the sum

$$P = e^{i \cdot 2\alpha} + e^{i (2\alpha + 2\varphi)} + e^{i (2\alpha + 4\varphi)} + \dots + e^{i [2\alpha + (2n-2)\varphi]}.$$

First, let's calculate P, resulting in a formula for the sum of the terms of a geometric progression:

$$P = e^{i \cdot 2\alpha} \frac{1 - e^{i 2n\varphi}}{1 - e^{i 2\varphi}}.$$

But $2n\varphi = 2n \cdot \frac{2\pi}{n} = 4\pi$, and $e^{i \cdot 4\pi} = 1$, therefore $P = 0$. But then also $T = 0$, that is, $S = \frac{1}{2} R^2 n$.

This shows that the sum S does not depend on the choice of straight line MN.

Complex set as rotation and stretching operator

Let us dwell on one more important geometric interpretation of a complex number.

Let there be a vector OZ (Fig. 9) on the plane, and let $z$ be its complex coordinate. Let's rotate the OZ vector around its origin $O$ by an angle $\alpha$ (counterclockwise if $\alpha > 0$, and clockwise if $\alpha < 0$) and stretch this vector (without changing its origin) with a stretch factor $\rho$. As a result, we will get a new vector OW; We denote its complex coordinate by $\omega$. How is the number $\omega$ expressed through $z$, $\alpha$, $\rho$?

Let $z = re^{i\varphi}$. It is clear that $|\omega| = \rho r$, Arg $\omega = \alpha + \varphi$. Therefore $\omega = (\rho r)e^{i(\alpha + \varphi)} = (\rho e^{i\alpha})(re^{i\varphi})$. Let us denote by $c$ the complex number $c = \rho e^{i\alpha}$. Conversely, if the complex coordinate $z$ of some vector OZ is multiplied by the complex number $c = \rho e^{i\alpha}$, then the new number $w = c \cdot z$ represents the coordinate of the OW vector obtained from the OZ vector by rotating by an angle $\alpha$ and stretching by a factor of $\rho$.

In short, multiplication by a complex number $c$ acts as a rotation operator by an angle $\alpha = \text{Arg } c$ and a stretching operator with a stretch factor equal to $|c|$.

where $p, q$ are “objective”, that is, real numbers, according to the well-known formula sometimes led to numbers of the form $A + \sqrt{B}$, where $B$ is a negative number. But no one knew what the “square root of a negative number” was, what meaning should be given to such an expression. However, there were several ways out of this difficulty: it was simply declared that the expression $\sqrt{-1}$ (or $\sqrt{B}$, where $B < 0$) does not make sense. And this seemed completely reasonable: each time it was possible to show that for $B < 0$ the root of equation (1) cannot be a positive number, a negative number, or zero. When it came to solving cubic equations (that is, equations of the third degree), it turned out to be impossible to simply brush aside the square roots of negative numbers.

And it was like this. More than 400 years ago, several Italian mathematicians learned to solve equations of the third degree. The method outlined in the algebra textbook of one of them, Hieronymus Cardan (1545), boils down (in modern notation) to the following: the roots of an equation of the form
$$x^3 + px + q = 0$$
(2)

can be calculated using the formula (now called the Cardan formula)
$$x = \sqrt[3]{-q/2 + \sqrt{D}} + \sqrt[3]{-q/2 - \sqrt{D}},$$
(3)

Where
$$D = \left(\frac{p}{3}\right)^3 + \left(\frac{q}{2}\right)^2.$$

Alas, this formula misfires in the case when equation (2) has three different real roots! For example, it is easy to check that the roots of the equation $x^3 - x = 0$ are the numbers 0,1, -1. But if we tried to solve this equation using the Cardan formula, we would get
$$x = \sqrt[3]{V - \frac{1}{27}} + \sqrt[3]{V - \frac{1}{27}}.$$

How can you extract the numbers 0, 1, -1 from this formula? To answer such questions, a mathematician of the 16th - 17th centuries had to learn how to handle expressions of the form $A + \sqrt{B}$, where $B < 0$, and, in particular, cube roots from such expressions.

Mathematicians were extremely reluctant to study such expressions. They call them “imaginary” numbers, non-existent, impossible, “sophistical” numbers, “secret solutions to equations.” It was considered very surprising that they had no real content.

One of the greatest mathematicians in the world, G. W. Leibniz (1646-1716), who admired minimum numbers, at the same time called them “the degenerate idea of ​​the world,” “an almost dual being, located between “to be” and “not to be”; saying

Meanwhile, these “impossible”, “non-existent” numbers, all real, were knocking on the doors of mathematical science. Back in the 17th-18th centuries, it was discovered that many complex and cumbersome expressions found in elementary and higher mathematics were easy to calculate if you used minimal numbers. But the distrust of these numbers was so great that in the past mathematicians did this: first they calculated using minimal numbers, and then they proved the formula “one hundred” obtained in this way, without involving these numbers or other means.

Over the past 200 years, melex numbers (as the famous German mathematician K. F. Gauss suggested calling minimal numbers) have found numerous and completely unexpected applications. So, for example, with the help of a complex of numbers, Gauss (in 1796) was able to find an answer to a purely geometric question: for what natural numbers n can one construct a regular n-unit with a cyclel and a ruler *)?

Number complexes have found wide application in cartography, electronics, hydrodynamics, the theory of soil filtration, and theoretical physics.
