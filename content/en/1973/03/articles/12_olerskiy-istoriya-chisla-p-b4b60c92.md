History of the number pi

Under this title, a book by F. Kymena was published in 1971 (translation from Rusyn by M. G. Minole and Y. M. Frenk, edited by B. A. Rosenfeld and B. V. Biryukov, Nauka Publishing House, Main Editorial Office of Physics and Mathematics Literature).

This book tells in an interesting, accessible and lively way the development of the concept of the number π, from its empirical application in ancient times to the revelation of its true mathematical nature at the end of the last century.

The author of the book is Florica Campan, a professor at the University of Iasi, a well-known historian of mathematics and popular writer in Romania. She has written articles in special journals, research works on the history of mathematics, and popular books on this topic.

The book is intended for a wide readership. It is available to persons with mathematical knowledge and a previous high school program. At the same time, it is also of interest to readers with more serious mathematical training.

A quarter of the book's content is devoted to applications, in which the author of the book sets out in sufficient detail facts from the history of science and the history of mathematics that have undeniable educational value.

However, we won’t rehash this book; you probably already agree that it is quite interesting.

In conclusion, here are a few facts about the hot race. Avram Sharp again turns to Gregory's formula and, taking the value $\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}$ for the other x, finds the series $\frac{\pi}{6} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{3}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{3 \cdot 3} + \frac{1}{3^2 \cdot 5} - \frac{1}{3^3 \cdot 7} + \dots \right)$.

By summing the terms of this series, he gets 72 exact decimal places of the number π. Seventy-two decimal places! These are real numbers drunks. Indeed, to calculate the length of a circle, the radius of which would be equal to the distance from the Earth to the most separated nebula, with an error of less than 1 mm, the first 40 decimal places of the number π are sufficient. Despite this, astronomer John Machin, after obtaining the indicated result, calculated 100 decimal places, and then Lagny - 128 decimal places of π. After a short period of time, the great Euler, who was not only an outstanding mathematician, but also a substitute calculator, discovered another series, using it to verify Lagny's calculation of the 128 decimal places of the number π. He completed this test in a record time - 80 hours, discovering at the same time that Lanyi made a mistake: the 113th digit is not 7, as it was for him, but 8. To more clearly imagine the record short calculation time achieved by Euler, we will suggest that he continuously does this 8 hours a day; then, based on the general rate of calculation, it would take him 10 days to establish 128 decimal places of π. It turns out that in an hour Euler did the same work that an ordinary computer scientist needed 3 hours to do. This means that Euler's record-breaking time corresponds to an average month of computational work, which is a huge advance compared to the results of computation in the 16th and 17th centuries, which labored for years to determine only one-third of that many decimal places (pp. 113-115).
