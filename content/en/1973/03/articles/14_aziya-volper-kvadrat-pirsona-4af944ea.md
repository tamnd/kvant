Pearson square

A. P. Asia, I. M. Volper

In Ya. I. Perelman’s “Entertaining Algebra” there is an interesting problem called “At the Hairdresser’s.”
In this problem, the author says that, once looking into a hairdresser, he saw a master trying to flawlessly prepare a 12% hydrogen peroxide solution from two available solutions - three and thirty percent *).

The problem described by Perelman occurs not only in hairdressing salons.

For example, to charge batteries, it is sometimes necessary to prepare an electrolyte that must contain 24% sulfuric acid from two solutions containing 92% and 10% sulfuric acid. At canning factories, it becomes necessary to prepare 6% vinegar for marinade from two batches of vinegar of different strengths: 3% and 10%, etc.

To solve such problems, it is convenient to use the “Pearson Square”. Here's how it's done. Draw a square and draw two diagonals (Fig. 1). In the upper left corner, write down the larger strength indicator of the starting substances (a), and in the lower corner - the second indicator (b), and at the intersection of the diagonals write down the required indicator of the mixture (c).

Then subtract along the first diagonal (a − c) and find the number

*) Let us recall that the content of a substance in a solution is the ratio of the mass of this substance in “pure form” to the mass of the solution.

quality of the second part of the mixture (y). Subtract from the center along the second diagonal (c − b) and find the amount of the first part of the mixture (x). The x and y values ​​are written along the same line as the indicators. For x parts of the first substance, you need to take y parts of the second substance, then you get a mixture with exponent c.

Fig. 1. Fig. 2.

For example, suppose there are two batches of cream: one contains 36% fat, and the other contains 18%. It is necessary to determine how much of these and other creams should be taken to obtain a mixture with a fat content of 30%. We solve according to the image above using the method (Fig. 2) and get

$$y = a - c = 36 - 30 = 6,$$
$$x = c - b = 30 - 18 = 12,$$

that is, for 6 parts by weight of the second batch of cream you need to take 12 parts of the first.

This method is based on the specific type of quantity of the resulting mixture, it is equal to the difference in the indicators of the starting substances. This assumption is completely possible, since we are not interested in abscid values, but in the relative quantities of the two parts of the mixture.

In fact, we get $x + y = (c − b) + (a + c) = a − b$ parts of the mixture. There will be “pure” substance in it

$$\frac{x \cdot a}{100} + \frac{y \cdot b}{100} = \frac{(c − b) a + (a − c) b}{100} = \frac{ac − bc}{100}$$

parts, and the strength of the mixture will be equal to $\frac{ac − bc}{100(a − b)} = \frac{c}{100}$, that is, c%.
