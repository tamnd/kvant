Face. We provide the calculations (simpler than for balls) to the readers and present only the results: $t_1 \sim \frac{l}{u}, F_1 \sim ER^2 \frac{v}{u}$. The force of interaction when the cylinders collide turns out to be greater than when the balls collide: $\frac{F_1}{F} \sim \left(\frac{u}{v}\right)^{1/5}$, and the time at $l \sim R$ is less: $\frac{t_1}{t} \sim \left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{1/5}$. For table cylinders, standard speed is $5 \text{ cm/s}$, $\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)^{1/5} = 10$. Please, the most interesting difference between these results and the estimates for balls is the independence of time $t_1$ from the speed of movement of the cylinders.

It must, however, be kept in mind that the phenomena that occur when rods collide are much more complex than when balls collide. A deformed region can expand in a substance not instantly, but at the speed of sound, equal in order of magnitude to $u$. Therefore, it covers the rods in time $\sim \frac{l}{u}$, that is, during the entire time of impact, traveling compression waves propagate along the rods.

When the balls collide, the deformation spreads to the “spring” areas in time $\tau \sim \frac{r}{u} \sim \frac{R}{u}\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{2/5}$, much less than the impact time $t: \frac{\tau}{t} \sim \left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{3/5} \ll 1$. Therefore, assume that the deformation spreads to the “spring” areas instantly (which is what we did).

Sound orientation in dolphins

The conquest of the World Ocean by dolphins was greatly facilitated by their speed and sound orientation, and the appearance of an excellently developed sonar, which allows them to perfectly navigate in the aquatic environment. Of the sensory organs in cetaceans, hearing develops best. Sound for aquatic mammals is the most important source of information, since sound vibrations travel almost 5 times faster in water than in air. Cetaceans perceive not only sounds, but also infrasounds and ultrasounds (up to 170 kHz), lying far beyond the sensitive target ear.

The signals emitted by dolphins are used by immi both for communication with their kin and for orientation based on reflected sounds (echolocation).

Using echolocation signals, dolphins can navigate in space with great accuracy, frolic in minefields, determine the depth of the bottom and the proximity of the shore, the different nature of the soil, the size and quality of submerged objects. The effectiveness of sonar in dolphins has been well studied in aquariums. Soviet hydroacousticist N.A. Dubrovsky showed that a “blinded” bottlenose dolphin (with special eyecups) reliably distinguishes a lead ball with a diameter of 5 cm from a steel ball of the same size from a distance of 11 m and a steel ball from a dioral one from a distance of 8 m. American studies by Evans and Pauzl proved the ability of a “blinded” dolphin to distinguish two identical plates consisting of different metals, or the same metal, but of different thickness. “Blinded” dolphins in aquariums can freely swim, small-sized sonars and echolocators, small devices for orienting the blind as they walk along city streets and should lead to the implementation of an assessment of the possibilities of using dolphins in the future of human practice, in his marine research.

(From the article by A. G. Tomilin “Dolphins and Science”, published in the international yearbook “The Future of Science” for 1972)
