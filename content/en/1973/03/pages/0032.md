⟦folio 30⟧

Real Applicable
imaginary numbers

Imaginary numbers owe their birth to one wave of the real mathematical problem - solving a third-degree equation. True, mathematicians have encountered square roots of negative numbers even when solving quadratic equations. Solving a randomly chosen equation of the form
$$x^2 + px + q = 0,$$
(1)

where $p, q$ are “objective”, that is, real numbers, according to the well-known formula sometimes led to numbers of the form $A + \sqrt{B}$, where $B$ is a negative number. But no one knew what the “square root of a negative number” was, what meaning should be given to such an expression. However, there were several ways out of this difficulty: it was simply declared that the expression $\sqrt{-1}$ (or $\sqrt{B}$, where $B < 0$) does not make sense. And this seemed completely reasonable: each time it was possible to show that for $B < 0$ the root of equation (1) cannot be a positive number, a negative number, or zero. When it came to solving cubic equations (that is, equations of the third degree), it turned out to be impossible to simply brush aside the square roots of negative numbers.

And it was like this. More than 400 years ago, several Italian mathematicians learned to solve equations of the third degree. The method outlined in the algebra textbook of one of them, Hieronymus Cardan (1545), boils down (in modern notation) to the following: the roots of an equation of the form
$$x^3 + px + q = 0$$
(2)

can be calculated using the formula (now called the Cardan formula)
$$x = \sqrt[3]{-q/2 + \sqrt{D}} + \sqrt[3]{-q/2 - \sqrt{D}},$$
(3)

Where
$$D = \left(\frac{p}{3}\right)^3 + \left(\frac{q}{2}\right)^2.$$

Alas, this formula misfires in the case when equation (2) has three different real roots! For example, it is easy to check that the roots of the equation $x^3 - x = 0$ are the numbers 0,1, -1. But if we tried to solve this equation using the Cardan formula, we would get
$$x = \sqrt[3]{V - \frac{1}{27}} + \sqrt[3]{V - \frac{1}{27}}.$$

How can you extract the numbers 0, 1, -1 from this formula? To answer such questions, a mathematician of the 16th - 17th centuries had to learn how to handle expressions of the form $A + \sqrt{B}$, where $B < 0$, and, in particular, cube roots from such expressions.

Mathematicians were extremely reluctant to study such expressions. They call them “imaginary” numbers, non-existent, impossible, “sophistical” numbers, “secret solutions to equations.” It was considered very surprising that they had no real content.
