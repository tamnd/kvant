What time intervals and with what accuracy does radar measure?

Typically, distances of tens of kilometers should be measured with an error not exceeding tens of meters. This means, as can be calculated using formula (1) (speed of light $c = 300\ 000\ \text{km/c}$), one must be able to measure time intervals of the order of hundreds of microseconds ($1\ \text{mkc} = 10^{-6}\ \text{c}$) with an accuracy of tenths of microseconds. No mechanical watch is capable of making such measurements: the mass (inertia) of the parts of its mechanism is too large; Only electronic devices, a consequence of the low inertia of electrons, can perform this task. A very convenient device, for example, is an electron ray tube

(similar to those used in oscilloscopes and televisions). Its application in radar is usually based on the well-known truth that “it is better to see once than to hear it once.” Everything that hits the radar is converted by electron beam indicators into a visible picture of the corresponding region of space.

There are various methods for determining angular coordinates, but the general principle is to use the direction of radiation and reception of waves.

A much simpler method, based on this general principle, is to fix the angular position of the rotating antenna with some kind of meter at the moment when the pulse reflected from the target reaches its maximum (Fig. 9).
