So, in every inscribed convex quadrilateral, the sum of the products of opposite sides is equal to the product of its diagonals.

Ptolemy's theorem has been proven.

Problem 3. Can the product of the diagonals of a convex quadrilateral $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ be greater than the sum of the products of opposite sides?

Solution. We have already used pointdest, which is true for any complex numbers $z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4$:

$$(z_2 - z_1)(z_4 - z_3) + (z_3 - z_2)(z_4 - z_1) = (z_4 - z_2)(z_3 - z_1).$$

Operation on the properties of models of complex numbers, we get

$$|z_4 - z_2| \cdot |z_3 - z_1| \leq |z_2 - z_1| \times |z_4 - z_3| + |z_3 - z_2| \cdot |z_4 - z_1|.$$

that is, always $A_1A_3 \cdot A_2A_4 \leq A_1A_2 \times A_3A_4 + A_2A_3 \cdot A_1A_4$.

We have proved this addition to Ptolemy's theorem: in each quadrilateral the product of the diagonals is not greater than the sum of the product of its opposite sides.

Complex set as rotation and stretching operator

Let us dwell on one more important geometric interpretation of a complex number.

Let there be a vector OZ (Fig. 9) on the plane, and let $z$ be its complex coordinate. Let's rotate the OZ vector around its origin $O$ by an angle $\alpha$ (counterclockwise if $\alpha > 0$, and clockwise if $\alpha < 0$) and stretch this vector (without changing its origin) with a stretch factor $\rho$. As a result, we will get a new vector OW; We denote its complex coordinate by $\omega$. How is the number $\omega$ expressed through $z$, $\alpha$, $\rho$?

Let $z = re^{i\varphi}$. It is clear that $|\omega| = \rho r$, Arg $\omega = \alpha + \varphi$. Therefore $\omega = (\rho r)e^{i(\alpha + \varphi)} = (\rho e^{i\alpha})(re^{i\varphi})$. Let us denote by $c$ the complex number $c = \rho e^{i\alpha}$. Conversely, if the complex coordinate $z$ of some vector OZ is multiplied by the complex number $c = \rho e^{i\alpha}$, then the new number $w = c \cdot z$ represents the coordinate of the OW vector obtained from the OZ vector by rotating by an angle $\alpha$ and stretching by a factor of $\rho$.

In short, multiplication by a complex number $c$ acts as a rotation operator by an angle $\alpha = \text{Arg } c$ and a stretching operator with a stretch factor equal to $|c|$.
