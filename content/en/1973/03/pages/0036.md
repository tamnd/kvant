Boron lominophore

V. V. Mayer

Some substances have amazing properties. If you keep them in the light and then place them in a dark room, they will glow for some time. Such substances are called lominophores.

To prepare Boric lominophore, you will need pine concentrate and Boric acid powder. Both can be purchased at any pharmacy.

Dissolve 1 e of pine concentrate in 50 ml of water. Add several identical portions of Boric acid (2 - 3 units each).

If you do not have scales and a beaker, then equal portions of Boric acid can be measured, for example, with a teaspoon. And to obtain the desired solution of pine concentrate, gradually add the concentrate to the water until the greenish solution takes on a distinct yellow tint.

Pour one portion of Boric acid onto an aluminum plate and drop 10 drops of prepared pine concentrate into it. Mix it all until smooth. Level the resulting mass on the plate so that you get a layer 2 - 4 mm thick. Place the record on the hotplate that is turned on. As it heats up, water first evaporates from the mass, the mass hardens, and then begins to melt. The resulting bubbles should be pierced with a pointed stick. Once everything is melted, remove the melt from the hotplate and let it cool completely. The boron lominophore is ready.

Bring the lominophore to the burning lamp. After a while, turn off the lamp. In the dark, you may see a bright blue or greenish glow for a few seconds.

The brightness and color of the glow change depending on the amount of pine concentrate dissolver that you poured into boric acid before preparing the lominophore. Therefore, tie different amounts of pine concentrate into the remaining portions (for example, 20, 30, 40 drops). Prepare lominophores and determine the best match. After this, you can proportionally increase the components and produce a sufficient amount of lominophore.

Now, using the preparation of lominophore, create an experiment illustrating an important physical law. Lominophore on an aluminum plate is usually obtained in the form of a “cake”. Cover half of this “cake” with a red light filter and, having irradiated the lominophore, turn off the light. What happened?

Replace the red filter with yellow, green, film and phyllot filters in sequence. What do you observe?

When illuminated with red, yellow and green light, sequential boron lominophore is absent. On the cover of our magazine there is a photograph of the status of “Maenad” by the Greek sculptor Skopas. The statue was covered with lominoforum, illuminated, and then shaped in a dark room. On the right you see the consequence of the object. On the left is the same statue, illuminated through a red filter; there is no consequence.

If the lominophore is produced by light or phylote light, then in the dark it emits green or blue light. This observation illustrates Stokes' law, according to which the wavelength of the light of lominescence is greater than the wavelength of the light causing lominescence.
