Meanwhile, these “impossible”, “non-existent” numbers, all real, were knocking on the doors of mathematical science. Back in the 17th-18th centuries, it was discovered that many complex and cumbersome expressions found in elementary and higher mathematics were easy to calculate if you used minimal numbers. But the distrust of these numbers was so great that in the past mathematicians did this: first they calculated using minimal numbers, and then they proved the formula “one hundred” obtained in this way, without involving these numbers or other means.

Over the past 200 years, melex numbers (as the famous German mathematician K. F. Gauss suggested calling minimal numbers) have found numerous and completely unexpected applications. So, for example, with the help of a complex of numbers, Gauss (in 1796) was able to find an answer to a purely geometric question: for what natural numbers n can one construct a regular n-unit with a cyclel and a ruler *)?

Number complexes have found wide application in cartography, electronics, hydrodynamics, the theory of soil filtration, and theoretical physics. Already in our century, number complexes and function complexes (that is, functions for which both the values ​​of the argument and the values ​​of the functions are number complexes) were successfully used by Soviet mathematicians and mechanics N.
