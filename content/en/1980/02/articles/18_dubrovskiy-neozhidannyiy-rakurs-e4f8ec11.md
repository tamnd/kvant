They usually try to make drawings in stereometry as clear as possible, choosing an angle so that all important points, lines, and planes are clearly visible. However, for a broad group of problems, another perspective is advantageous: one in which non-tortic lines and planes degenerate, that is, they are represented, respectively, by points and lines. We will tell you about such tasks.

The idea of ​​the proposed technique is a special choice of parallel projection. Therefore, we first formulate the basic property of parallel projection, which directly follows from the properties indicated in § 12 of “Geometry 9”:

Let $A', B', C', D'$ be the images of points $A, B, C, D$ under parallel projection onto the plane $\pi$ along the straight line $l$. Then, if $AB = kCD$, then $u A'B' = kC'D'^*$ (Fig. 1, a).

Now we can formulate a rule, on the basis of which all the analyzed problems were solved: in order to find out in what respect the plane π (respectively, the line l) divides a segment that is not parallel to it, we should consider the projection along the plane π or along some known line lying in the plane π (respectively, along the line l).

As an example, the application of this rule in plane trilogy will be proven.

Menelaus's theorem. If line l intersects sides AB, BC and CA of triangle ABC or their extension at points K, L, M respectively, then
$$\frac{AK}{KB} \cdot \frac{BL}{LC} \cdot \frac{CM}{MA} = -1^*$$.

Proof (Fig. 5). Let's project a triangle parallel to line l onto some line intersecting it. Then
$$\frac{AK}{KB} \cdot \frac{BL}{LC} \cdot \frac{CM}{MA} = \frac{A'P}{PB'} \cdot \frac{B'P}{PC'} \cdot \frac{C'P}{PA'} = (-1)^3 = -1.$$
(Note that the converse theorem is also true; it can be proven by contradiction.)

Solution. Let $MN$ be the common perpendicular to the edges $AB$ and $CD$ of the tetrahedron $ABCD$ with equal-sized boundaries (Fig. 7, a). It is easy to understand that the orthogonal projection of a tetrahedron along the line $AB$ is a triangle $ADC$ (Fig. 7, b), in which the side $AD$ is congruent to the height $DD_1$ of the face $ABD$, and the side $AC$ is congruent to the height $CC_1$ of the face $ABC$, and the segment $MN$ is projected to the height $AN$ of triangle $ADC$. Since triangles $ABC$ and $ABD$ have the same areas and a common base $AB$, their heights dropped onto this base are equal, therefore, at figure 7, b $|AD|=|AC|$; which means that point $N$ (the base of the altitude of an equilateral triangle) divides the segment $CD$ in half. Using the useful property of projection and repeating this reasoning in relation to the remaining edges of the tetrahedron, we conclude that the common perpendiculars of all pairs of crossing edges of the tetrahedron $ABCD$ pass through the midpoints of the corresponding edges and, therefore, are the symmetry axes of the tetrahedron. For example, with axial symmetry with the axis $MN$ (Fig. 7, a), the vertex $A$ will go into $B$, $B$ - into $A$, $C$ - into $D$, $D$ - into $C$, so the face $ABC$ is congruent to the face $ABD$, and the face $ACD$ is congruent to the face $BCD$. In the same way it is proved that $\triangle ABD \cong \triangle ACD$.
