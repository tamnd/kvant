Up an inclined plane

We offer you an experience to evaluate the old and very similar to the trick.

Take two identical cones, for example, made of wood, plastic or metal (the material does not matter at all). The cones can be solid or hollow, as long as they are not too light. Connect the cones firmly with the bases (for example, glue them together), and so that the cones necessarily coincide. For the experiment, you will also only need an old book and two identical long rods.

Place the book on the table, and arrange the rods so that one end rests on the edge of the book, the other on the table, and in the same place. In short, the rods and edge of the book should form a kind of “inclined plane” in the shape of an equilateral triangle (Fig. 1).

Now take the fastened cones and place them on the rods, while the axis of the cones should be horizontal. As soon as you do this, you will see that the cones themselves, without pushing, will appear on an inclined plane... not whizz, but up, questioning the usual experience and common sense.

What is the secret of this trick? Of course, there are no miracles here. It simply turns out that if certain conditions are met, the centers of the cones in the process of “rolling” upward will not rise, but fall. It is the force of gravity that is the cause of such, at first glance, unusual movement.

What conditions must be met? Let's consider this issue in more detail. Let us denote the angle of inclination of the plane to the horizon by α, the angle between the rods by 2β, and the angle at the apex of the cones by 2γ (Fig. 2). Let the cones move upward along the guide rods from position I to position II, l₁ and l₂ are the corresponding distances between the points of contact of the cones with the rods.

From figure 2, a it is clear that in relation to the points of contact the center of gravity of the cones is lowered (vertically) by amounts

$$H = \frac{l_2 - l_1}{2} \text{tg} \gamma.$$

The points of contact themselves naturally rise, the height of their rise is equal (see Fig. 2, b)

$$h = |MN| \sin \alpha = \frac{l_2 - l_1}{2} \text{ctg} \beta \sin \alpha.$$

Now it is not difficult to formulate the conditions for the cones to “roll” up an inclined plane. Obviously, for this the requirement H > h must be met:

$$\frac{l_2 - l_1}{2} \text{tg} \gamma > \frac{l_2 - l_1}{2} \text{ctg} \beta \sin \alpha,$$

or

$$\sin \alpha < \text{tg} \beta \text{tg} \gamma,$$

Only in this case will the weight of the cones decrease as the cones move up the plane.

If the opposite inequality holds

$$\sin \alpha > \text{tg} \beta \text{tg} \gamma,$$

the cones will truly roll off the plane, that is, go down.
