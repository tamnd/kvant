A. Bendukidze, A. Savin

Derived proportions

- Hello, Ira!
- Oh, it’s you, Vitya! Come in, sit down.
- How is your sore throat?
— The doctor says that you can go to school on Monday. What's new in the class?
- Today was a laugh! Natalya Nikolaevna called Seryozha Kazakov to the board. I don’t remember what problem he solved, but along the way he needed to add $2/3$ and $5/4$. He took and added the numerator with the numerator, and the denominator with the denominator, and the result was $7/7$, that is, one. And Natalya Nikolaevna says: “Give me, Seryozha, a diary, I’ll put this unit there for you as a keepsake.”
- Wait a minute, Vitya, this is interesting!
- That's for sure! No one has received one from us for a long time.
- That's not what I'm talking about. Look, one fraction was less than one, and the second was more, and the result was one. Please hand me the notebook and pen from the table! Thank you. Come on, take $2/3$ and $2/5$. So... Add up the numerators, then the denominators...
— Do Kazakov’s laurels haunt you?
— Wait, we get $4/8$, that is $2/4$, that is $1/2$. Yeah! $1/2$ is greater than $2/5$ but less than $2/3$. Hooray! Vitya, I came up with a new theorem: if you add the numerators of two fractions, and then the denominators, you get a fraction that is larger than the smaller fraction, but smaller than the larger one.
- Wait, how do you know that this is true?
- I don’t know yet, I feel that this is so.
- Let's check it out. If two fractions are equal, then this “addition” according to your theorem should give a fraction equal to them. Let's take $2/3$ and $4/6$. So... We get $6/9$, that is, again $2/3$.
- Why should it be a special case? Let's take the irreducible fraction $a/b$.
- Why irreducible?
- Okay, I’ll multiply the numerator and denominator by some number $n$. Now I’ll take another fraction equal to it. To do this, I will multiply the numerator and denominator by the number $m$. We get

$$\frac{na}{nb} \quad \frac{ma}{mb}.$$

Add the numerators and denominators and divide. It worked

$$\frac{na+ma}{nb+mb}.$$

Now I see what needs to be done! Let us take the set $a$ into the numerator, and $b$ into the denominator. Let's write this down:

$$\frac{a(n+m)}{b(n+m)}$$

Now you can shorten it by $(n+m)$; it is clear that the resulting fraction is equal to the original one.

Vitya, this is also a wonderful theorem! Listen to the wording:

$$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}, \quad \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a+c}{b+d}$$

And I think it’s even better to formulate it like this: $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}, \quad \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} = \frac{na+mc}{nb+md}$$

On Monday, Ira and Vitya showed Natalya Nikolaevna their discovery.

Well, they’re young,” said Natalya Nikolaevna, “the property of which you discovered is, of course, known.” It is called derived proportion. In the future, it can be prepared for you more than once. And of course, you should write about it in our mathematical wall newspaper. We will also place your theorem there, Ira: $$\frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d}, \quad \frac{a}{b} < \frac{a+c}{b+d} < \frac{c}{d}$$. You guessed right. Just prove it yourself.

On my own behalf, I will add a few more theorems on derived proportions. Write it down, Vitya. Let the first theorem be this:

$$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}, \quad \frac{a+b}{b} = \frac{c+d}{d}$$

Well, Natalya Nikolaevna, it’s quite simple! Let's divide the left side by $b$ term by term, and the right side by $d$, we get $$\frac{a}{b} + 1 = \frac{c}{d} + 1.$$ But the fractions are equal, which means that by adding one to both sides of the true equality, we will again get the true equality.

The second one will be the last one: $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}, \quad \frac{a+b}{a-b} = \frac{c+d}{c-d}$$.

But here’s a proportion for you not even with two, but with four arbitrary numbers: $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \quad u, n, m, p, r$$ are arbitrary numbers, then

$$\frac{na+mb}{pa+rb} = \frac{nc+md}{rc+rd}$$.

Natalya Nikolaevna,” said Ira, “but your previous theorems are part of the cases of this one.” In the first theorem, $n$, $m$ and $r$ are equal to one, and $p$ are equal to zero, in the second, $m$ is equal to one, and the remaining numbers are equal to one. And in the third, $n$, $m$ and $p$ are equal to one, and $r$ is minus one. Therefore, having proven this theorem, I will prove all the others.

No,” Vitya drawled, “I’d rather try to consider partial cases, and then, look, and it will become clear how to prove the theorem in general form, rather than immediately rack your brains over a difficult theorem, especially since I’ve already proven one.”

And you, readers, are invited to think about the theorems discussed here.
