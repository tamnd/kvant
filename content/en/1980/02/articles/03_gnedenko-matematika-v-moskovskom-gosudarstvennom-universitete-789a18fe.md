Mathematics
at Moscow State University

January 25, 1980 marked 225 years since the signing of the decree on the establishment of Moscow University. At the university, two imazias were created: one for the children of the commoners, the other for the children of commoners. The grand opening of the university took place on April 26, 1755. But this was only the opening of immazii. Classes with students began only in the summer of 1755, when 30 young people seconded for spiritual seminars gathered at the university (there were no other prepared applicants for university studies in the country at that time).

Moscow University was opened thanks to the vigorous activity of M.V. Lomonosov, which added to the widespread spread of education in Russia, not associated with class restrictions. However, at that time the government of the serfdom empire categorically denied the peasantry the opportunity to receive higher education. Lomonosov argued that “at the university, the student who has learned more is more respectable: and whose son he is, there is no need for that.”

At first, the university consisted of three faculties: philosophy, law and medicine - and, formally, two gymnasiums. In fact, there was only one gymnasium. Only classes were held separately for Dvoyayan children and children of commoners. Association was allowed only in the last, so-called “rector’s” class. This last class provided additional preparation for entering the university, and classes lasted the same year.

All students must take classes at the Faculty of Philosophy, which is a free general education student for transfer to the Faculty of Medicine and Law. Classes at the Faculty of Philosophy lasted three years, and at the Faculty of Medicine and Law lasted Thursday of the year. Thus, studying at the university lasted 7 years. The medical and law faculties were called “highest” faculties at that time.

Teaching at the university was lecture-based; The lecture lasted two hours. In the first years, lectures to students

were read in Latin, French and German, and only from 1767, when many expatriates from Moscow University itself began to work at the university as professors, teaching began to take place in Russian.

Already in the first year of its existence, the university gymnasium accepted several hundred students, and by the end of the 18th century their number increased to three thousand. In 1758, the present Moscow University opened a gymnasium in Kazan. It was actually an integral part of Moscow University. The university provided her with funds, teaching aids, and teaching staff. It is interesting that at that time there was no gymnasium even in St. Petersburg, the capital of the Russian Empire.

In the last quarter of the 18th century, Moscow University took an active part in organizing gymnasiums in a number of provincial cities of Russia - Yaroslavl, Tver, Vladimir, Kostroma, Vologda. University graduates became the first teachers of the newly created gymnasiums. Thus, at first the university was the main center for training scientific and teaching personnel.

Initially there was no department of mathematics at the university. Mathematics was the responsibility of the Department of Physics and was taught only at the Faculty of Philosophy. The content of the mathematics course did not go beyond elementary mathematics and included arithmetic, elementary algebra, geometry and basic trigonometry. One of M. V. Lomonosov’s students, Anton Alekseevich Barsov (1730-1791), was invited to teach this course.

But just three years later (in 1758), a decision was made to create a special department of numerical mathematics.

In the early years, mathematics was taught by non-professionals. Thus, the first professor of mathematics A. A. Barsov was specialized in the field of syntax of the Russian language. The teaching of the applied mathematics course was entrusted to Ivan Akimovich Rost, who taught him English.

In the first fifty years of the existence of Moscow University, the lecturer usually strictly adhered to the textbook he had chosen once and for all. Even the days and hours of lectures remained traditional. Thus, A. A. Barsov, back in 1757, chose Wednesday and Saturday for lectures on pure mathematics. In the same days and hours after A. A. Barsov was read by his student Dmitry Sergeevich Anichkov (1733-1788), and then by another university graduate Vasily Kondratyevich Arshenevsky (1758-1807). The same is the case with lectures on applied mathematics: I. A. Rost read them on Mondays, Tuesdays, Thursdays and Fridays.

D. S. Anichkov accepted the Department of Pure Mathematics from A. A. Barsov. For him, teaching mathematics was his life's work. In this regard, he not only conveyed, passing on the contents of textbooks already written by other authors, but sought to filter the presentation as best as possible to the psychological characteristics of the students. He wrote four textbooks that were widely known at that time: “Arithmetic” (1764), “Geometry” (1780), “Trigonometry” (1780) and “Algebra” (1781). At that time, books were not named so simply; their titles were verbose and sounded very solemn. This is what the title of a textbook on geometry sounds like: “Theoretical and practical geometry, in creeping and use not only by youth, but also by those who are involved in earthworks, fortification and artillery, collected from various authors... by Dmitry Anichkov.”

No matter how widely Anichkov’s textbooks were distributed in the 18th century, the main benefit was brought to him by his dissertation on the topic “Discourse from natural richness about the beginning and production of natural rich reading,” which, for atenciónical purposes, was burned at the Execution Ground by order of the Synod.

Now, two centuries later, we see more clearly the shortcomings of textbooks of that time, first of all, their formalism and isolation from real-life problems that worried practice at that time. Even the leading thinkers of that time failed to point out this lack of books on mathematics. In particular, M.V. Lomonsov repeatedly pointed out the need for theoretical scientific subjects of nature, but you will find there not nature, explained by mathematics, as it should be, but mathematics, imposing its own laws on nature: nature does not take into account mathematics, but, on the contrary, mathematics - from nature, which is smarter and more excellent than all the sciences.”

In the 19th century, Russia experienced years of rapid industrial and economic development. This caused the emergence of a large number of gymnasiums in provincial and narrow towns. There is a great need for teachers of the natural cycle.

All this led to the development of a new university charter (1804). According to this charter, Moscow University was already divided into four departments (faculties): 1) practical and political sciences, 2) verbal sciences, 4) medical or medical sciences, 4) physical and mathematical sciences.

At that time, as a rule, a mathematics teacher also taught natural disciplines - physics, astronomy, botany, zoology, geography. This circumstance inevitably led to the uniqueness of training at the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of that time. The average student of the faculty had only six to eight lectures on mathematics, three large lectures on physics, botany, minerals, as well as other subjects of the natural scientific cycle. In those years, they tried to give school and university education a practical character, so that a young person, after graduating from high school, could immediately begin work in the field of commerce or industrial production. Note that the new university charged the professor with the responsibility for teaching education, supplementing the courses with the results of new discoveries. This circumstance significantly changed the direction of the university’s activities.

Already at the beginning of the 19th century, a number of professors at Moscow University began to lecture on new mathematics. Thus, Professor I. A. Ide (1775-1806) began teaching courses in analytical geometry and mathematical analysis. Professor V.K. Arshenevsky said that at this time the business will “provide by chance its highest geometry, in which it will show useful and inter-calculus.” In addition, students took courses in artillery and ballistics, and mathematical geography. As a result, the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics gave the country outstanding

investigators in the fields of mathematics, biology, physics, chemistry, mechanics, external ballistics and artillery.

The Patriotic War of 1812 had a hard impact on Moscow University: Napoleonic artillery plundered and burned the collections, library, and buildings. Many students and teachers left to defend the country. Classes at the university stopped. It took a long time to restore an accessible level of teaching, and further progress in the mathematical life of Moscow University dates back to the twenties. This progress is largely associated with the change of Professor Dmitry Matveevich Perevoshikov (1790-1880), the author of a number of textbooks on mathematics and the thirteen-volume “Manual Mathematical Encyclopedia”, which served as a real book for mathematicians, engineers, artillerymen, natural astronomical observatories, and then from 1830 to 1851 he was its director, combining this work with the work of the dean, professor, rector. He owns more than seventy scientific (mathematical and astronomical), pedagogical and popular science works.

Among Perevoshikov’s direct students, Nikolai Efimovich Zernov (1804-1862) should be mentioned first. A significant process of mathematical education and its renewal is associated with his name. In particular, it was Zernov who, after his teacher, first began teaching a course on probability theory at the university. The topic of Zernov’s master’s thesis, “On the daily and annual motion of the Earth,” was suggested to him by his teacher, and the topic of his doctoral dissertation, “A Discourse on the Interration of an Equation with Partial Differentials” (1837), was chosen by him himself. It is interesting to note that this was the first doctoral dissertation in mathematics defended at Moscow University.

The events of December 1825 greatly affected the life of the university. Suffice it to say that among the university students there were at least sixty Decembrists and a third of them were students and former students of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics. Full surveillance was established over the university, and together with the program installation of 1804, a new so-called censorship ustav was introduced. According to this charter, the university was prohibited from publishing scientific works on natural sciences, philosophy and medicine. Only textbooks were allowed to be printed (of course, after the corresponding doctorate).

The famous work “The Past and Thoughts” by A. I. Germen, a graduate of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Moscow University, contains memoirs about professors, classmates and the self-importance of university life in 1829-1833. Germen highly appreciated the role of Moscow University in the development of Russian culture:

“In the history of Russian education and in the life of the last two generations, Moscow University and Tsarskoye Rural Face play a significant role.

Moscow University grew in importance along with Moscow after 1812; demoted by Emperor Peter from the tsarist capitals, Moscow was promoted by Napoleon (either willingly or unwillingly) to the capital of the Russian people... In it, the university is increasingly becoming the center of Russian education.”

According to Germen, Nicholas I did not like Moscow University and called it a “free nest.” His presence had a sharply negative impact on the life of Moscow University: admission to the university was difficult, and the number of students at the university decreased. This especially affected

children of commoners. However, due to government policy issues, the youth of the university developed scientific and pedagogical ideals with the help of the support program and sought to implement them in life.

The spirit of creative quest and renewal of mathematical education was included in Moscow University, along with Perevoshikov, a native of Moravia, Nikolai Dmitrievich Brashman (1796-1866). Almost immediately after graduating from the University of Vienna, he moved to Russia. At first he worked for a very short time in St. Petersburg, then moved to Kazan University and, finally, in 1834 he moved to Moscow and became a professor at Moscow University. Moscow became like his second home. Here he showed his abilities as a scientist, organizer of science and educator of scientific youth. He published scientific articles, wrote original study guides, gave lectures, and truly sought out talented students who sought to encourage independent scientific Japanese. He managed to educate a number of outstanding mathematical scientists and teachers. Among his students, first of all, academicians P. L. Chebyshev (1821-1894) and I. I. Somov (1815-1876), as well as professor A. Yu. Davidov (1823-1886), should be mentioned. They have made a significant contribution to the development of mathematical culture in Russia. From Brashman comes the interest of Moscow mathematicians in problems of mechanics, in particular in problems of hydrodynamics. To some extent, this circumstance influenced the gradual formation of the Moscow School of Mechanics, which in the second half of the 19th century produced serious results in the development of hydraulics, solid mechanics and hydrodynamics.

N.D. Brashman’s views on mathematics are well outlined by him in his speech “On the influence of mathematical sciences on the development of mental abilities,” which he delivered at the university on June 17, 1841: “A geometer does not work simply to satisfy his amateurism: a rich supply of the form of geometry, symbols of analysis and its complex actions is not a simple Russian monstrosity of mental ingenuity, not a collected rarity for amateurs; on the contrary, it is a powerful arsenal from which researchers of nature and technology take their best resources.”

In 1864, Brushman retired, but he did not want and psychologically could not break with the university. For him, the university was everything: home, family, life itself. He obtained permission to establish a prize for the best student scientific work in mathematics, to which he transferred all his information. This prize was awarded regularly for fifty years.

Just before his retirement, he invited his students, colleagues and other mathematicians to gather at his home to exchange scientific and pedagogical thoughts, as well as to review the most interesting studies that appeared in print. At a meeting on September 15, 1864, it was decided to transform this private circle into a regularly operating mathematical society. Brashman was the only one elected president of the society. Members of the society divided among themselves the areas of mathematics, which they undertook to regularly monitor and promptly inform their comrades about all the more or less interesting new results.

In addition to the “reciprocal content in the studies of mathematical sciences,” the newly organized society developed a wide range of popularization, educational and literary activities. Soon the need arose to publish a press organ of the society. This decision was made in April 1865. It was assumed that two issues of a new journal called “Mathematical Collection” would be published annually. This was the first special mathematical journal in Russia.

There are a lot of things for mathematical studies in the next few years. First of all, we should note the remarkable research of Nikolai Egorovich Zhukovsky (1847-1921) in the field of hydrodynamics and aerodynamics. He created the foundations of the theory of flight of heavier-than-air vehicles and created the foundations of Soviet aircraft construction. No wonder V.I. Lenin called Zhukovsky “the father of Russian aviation.” Further, it should be noted that research on the theory of differential equations and geometry, traditional for Moscow University, continued to develop throughout the 19th and the past decades of the current 20th century. We'll talk about this later.

Now I would like to note the works of Viktor Viktorovich Bobinin (1849-1919), dedicated to the history of mathematics. For the first time, he perceived the problems of the history of science as the Japanese laws of development of scientific ideas, and not just as a presentation of scientific biographies of outstanding scientists. He paid much attention to studying the development of mathematics in our country. In particular, he is responsible for the scientific description of a number of ancient Russian mathematical manuscripts. After the last October Revolution, Bobinin began activities with Soviet educational organizations. The People's Commissariat for Education commissioned him to write the one-volume “History of Russian Mathematics” and the three-volume work “General History of Mathematics.” Unfortunately, this work was not completed due to the death of the author.

Serbez work on the history of mathematics was revived at Moscow University already in the thirties, when it was headed by Professor Sofya Aleksandrovna Yanovskaya (1896-1966).

A significant shift in the content and nature of mathematical education will occur at the beginning of the 20th century and is associated with the names of professors Dmitry Fedorovich Egorov (1869-1931) and Boleslav Kornelevich Mlodezevsky (1859-1923). They began, in addition to lectures and exercises, to conduct special seminars on new areas of mathematics. Participants in the collection are tasked with familiarizing themselves with certain works and preparing reports based on them. This innovation turned out to be extremely useful, since it made it possible to very effectively bring undergraduates and graduate students closer to active scientific activity. At the seminar, each report, and with it each mathematical idea, will be supported by a comprehensive discussion. As a result, new questions arose that required additional research, teaching seminars gave the opportunity to actively engage in independent creativity. This form of work currently occupies a prominent place in the life of the university.

Already in 1901, Mlodezevsky gave the first special course devoted to the scientific theory of functions of a real variable, which was developing at that time. In the next decade, this area of ​​mathematics became the main focus of creative intersex young mathematicians at Moscow University. The further development of this direction is associated with D. F. Egorov and N. N. Luzin (1883-1950). The school of theory of functions of a real variable is one of the most important pages in the history of mathematics at Moscow University. If only the aforementioned course of Mlodezevsky and the work of Egorov led to the creation of this school, then its founder and long-term inspirer is undoubtedly Nikolai Nikolaevich Luzin. His research 1911-1915 made up

Great progress was made by scientists at Moscow University in the theory of differential equations - both general and particular arbitrary. The works of V.V. Stepanov (1889-1950), who developed the strong scale in the qualitative theory of differential equations, I.G. Petrovsky (1901-1973) and A.N. Tikhonov, who from different positions developed the theory of differential equations in particular arbitrary computational orders, are considered classic.

Significant successes have been achieved by university mathematicians in the field of number theory. A. Ya. Khinchin (1894-1959) obtained a number of functional results in the field of metric number theory; L.G. Shirelman (1905-1938) proved in a very elementary way a substitute fact on the way to solving Goldbach's problem: every integer can be represented as the sum of a limited number of simple terms. A. O. Gelfond (1906-1968) developed a substitute research method that allowed him to prove the following conjecture, expressed at the beginning of the century by D. Hilbert: any number of the form $a^\beta$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are algebraic numbers, and $\alpha \neq 0$ and $\alpha \neq 1$, and $\beta$ irrational, is transcendental.

Beginning in 1923, the circle of interests of Moscow mathematicians included the theory of probability. A. Ya. Khinchin was a pioneer in this field. Two years later, A. N. Kolmogorov became interested in questions of probability theory. From now on

This is how the Moscow school of probability theory arose, which transformed the face of this science. In just over fifty years, it has been possible to produce numerous studies that have successfully developed both the theoretical problems of probability theory and its numerous applications. Suffice it to say that only through examination did they become professors of higher educational institutions, elected academicians and corresponding members. Currently, graduates of Moscow University have created strong theoretical schools outside Moscow State University: in the German Democratic Republic, Bulgaria and some allied resource countries.

Through the efforts of O. Yu. Schmidt (1891-1956) and A. G. Kursh (1908-1971), a community school of modern algebra was created at the university and far beyond its borders, which had and is having a strong influence on the development of practically all areas of algebraic research.

One of the greatest conditions of modern mathematics should be the creation of the mathematical theory of optimal control by L. S. Pontryagin and his students. His previous work has deservedly earned him the position of one of the leading topologists.

At Moscow University, numerous other areas are also successfully developing, which have brought new ideas and profound results to mathematics: functional analysis, mathematical logic, mathematical cybernetics, geometry, optimization problems and control issues.

A few words need to be said about computational mathematics. Before the Great Patriotic War, classical approaches were developed here, including nomographic methods. Since the late forties and early fifties, it has become clear that the main path to approximate computing is associated with electronic computing and programming. Literally from scratch, in an exceptionally short time, it was possible to create a powerful school of modern computational mathematics, which developed new methods for programming computational and logical problems for putting them on a computer, methods for statistical modeling and automation of programming. Through the efforts of A. N. Tikhonov, a special faculty of computational mathematics and cybernetics was allocated from the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics in the early seventies, which every year is gaining strength and technology with the challenges of our time.

Matematika at Moscow University is currently developing not only in two mathematical faculties, but also in the physics and economics faculties, which have special mathematics departments, as well as in the biological faculty, where a special laboratory of mathematical statistics has been created.

Currently, each of the two mathematical faculties of Moscow University has a significantly larger number of students than the entire tree-resolution university. Moscow University now admits more mathematics students every day than there were mathematics students in all of Russia before the revolution. It is this circumstance - the constant influx of the model - that makes the university forever young, capable of revival and expansion.

Moscow University came to its two hundred and twentieth anniversary full of strength and scientific knowledge. University employees can look back on the path they have traveled with pride. Students of Moscow University took part in activities and direct participation in the formation of Soviet power in the country, the fight against devastation, the creation of the scientific and technical base of our country, on the fronts of the civil and Great Patriotic Wars and in the improvement of the country's defense. Its workers and supporters work successfully in all areas of the national economy, education, science and culture; in many areas of scientific creativity they continued new paths and brought previously started functional research to its logical conclusion.

Moscow University knows that its future is technically connected with the revival of interest in science and applied activities among today's schoolchildren. That is why academic scientists pay great attention to working with school students, school and college teachers, as well as scientific and propaganda work.
