Finite sections and Apollonius' problem

In the history of science, the name of the Greek geometer and astronomer Apollonius of Perga for many centuries was put almost on a par with the name of Archimedes.

When the algebraist F. Viet, who lived in the 16th century, found his solution to Apollonius’ problem, he was delighted to reveal: “I am Apollonius of Gali!” (i.e. French). I already talk about the problem of Apollonius from the approved manuscript “On touching”, familiar to us only from the comments of his contemporaries, in «Kvant» (1971, No. 8). Here are its conditions: construct a circle tangent to the theme of other circles randomly located on the plane.

Apollonius also introduced a lot of new things into the theory of finite sections. We nominate that end sections are defined as lines representing sections to the plane of the cone surface. The influences of these lines - ellipse, hyperbola and parabola - were invented by him («Kvant», 1975, No. 1, 3, 4).

However, now other definitions of these lines are more common. Ellipse is a set of points of the plane P located at distances r1 and r2 from the foci F1 ∈ P and F2 ∈ P such that |r2−r1| = 2a. Finally, a parabola is a set of points in the plane P located at equal distances from the focus F ∈ P and from the line (the so-called directrix) d ∈ P.

You can do without a cone and it doesn’t necessarily go into space in order to find a mathematical EMENT that connects these three so different curves (the ellipse is closed, the parabola consists of one “infinite branch”, the hyperbola - of two).

Namely, let us consider on the plane two intersecting circles *) ε1 and ε2 with centers at points F1 and F2. It turns out that the centers of all possible circles touching one of the circles ε1 ε2 internally, and the other externally, form an empty circle with a center in F1 has a radius R1 (Fig. 1), and a circle with a center in F2 has a radius R2. Let T1 and T2 be, respectively, the points of tangency of these circles with the circle with center M. Then it is clear that

$$R_1 + R_2 = |F_1 T_1| + |F_2 T_2| = |F_1 T_1| + |M F_2| + |T_1 M| = r_1 + r_2 = const.$$

And in what cases does a parabola appear as a family of centers of circles? To answer this question, we will try to imagine a straight line as a circle of infinite radius. The set of centers of circles tangent to a straight line of an arbitrary position and some circle is a parabola (Fig. 3). Prove it!

The representation of finite sections adopted here as a family of centers of circles turns out to be useful, for example, for solving the Apollonius problem given above. Indeed, the center of the circle is tangent to the critical three circles. The set of centers of circles touching them is again a finite section. The center of a circle tangent to three data is thus obtained as the intersection of two end sections. Theler connects the centers of the desired circles with the centers of the given line segments and obtains the third at the intersection of the point of tangency of the desired circle with the data.

Of course, such a solution to Apollonius's problem could not satisfy the ancient Greek geometers. Why? Yes, because it is not clear how to construct the intersection point of two curves using only a ruler and a compass. This is why F. Viet was so happy when he managed to find a solution to Apollonius’ problem using only a compass and a ruler. Can you do this?
