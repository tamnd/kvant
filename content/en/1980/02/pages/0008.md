The spirit of creative quest and renewal of mathematical education was included in Moscow University, along with Perevoshikov, a native of Moravia, Nikolai Dmitrievich Brashman (1796-1866). Almost immediately after graduating from the University of Vienna, he moved to Russia. At first he worked for a very short time in St. Petersburg, then moved to Kazan University and, finally, in 1834 he moved to Moscow and became a professor at Moscow University. Moscow became like his second home. Here he showed his abilities as a scientist, organizer of science and educator of scientific youth. He published scientific articles, wrote original study guides, gave lectures, and truly sought out talented students who sought to encourage independent scientific Japanese. He managed to educate a number of outstanding mathematical scientists and teachers. Among his students, first of all, academicians P. L. Chebyshev (1821-1894) and I. I. Somov (1815-1876), as well as professor A. Yu. Davidov (1823-1886), should be mentioned. They have made a significant contribution to the development of mathematical culture in Russia. From Brashman comes the interest of Moscow mathematicians in problems of mechanics, in particular in problems of hydrodynamics. To some extent, this circumstance influenced the gradual formation of the Moscow School of Mechanics, which in the second half of the 19th century produced serious results in the development of hydraulics, solid mechanics and hydrodynamics.

N.D. Brashman’s views on mathematics are well outlined by him in his speech “On the influence of mathematical sciences on the development of mental abilities,” which he delivered at the university on June 17, 1841: “A geometer does not work simply to satisfy his amateurism: a rich supply of the form of geometry, symbols of analysis and its complex actions is not a simple Russian monstrosity of mental ingenuity, not a collected rarity for amateurs; on the contrary, it is a powerful arsenal from which researchers of nature and technology take their best resources.”

In 1864, Brushman retired, but he did not want and psychologically could not break with the university. For him, the university was everything: home, family, life itself. He obtained permission to establish a prize for the best student scientific work in mathematics, to which he transferred all his information. This prize was awarded regularly for fifty years.

Just before his retirement, he invited his students, colleagues and other mathematicians to gather at his home to exchange scientific and pedagogical thoughts, as well as to review the most interesting studies that appeared in print. At a meeting on September 15, 1864, it was decided to transform this private circle into a regularly operating mathematical society. Brashman was the only one elected president of the society. Members of the society divided among themselves the areas of mathematics, which they undertook to regularly monitor and promptly inform their comrades about all the more or less interesting new results.

In addition to the “reciprocal content in the studies of mathematical sciences,” the newly organized society developed a wide range of popularization, educational and literary activities. Soon the need arose to publish a press organ of the society. This decision was made in April 1865. It was assumed that two issues of a new journal called “Mathematical Collection” would be published annually. This was the first special mathematical journal in Russia.
