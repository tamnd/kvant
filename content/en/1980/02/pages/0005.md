Already in the first year of its existence, the university gymnasium accepted several hundred students, and by the end of the 18th century their number increased to three thousand. In 1758, the present Moscow University opened a gymnasium in Kazan. It was actually an integral part of Moscow University. The university provided her with funds, teaching aids, and teaching staff. It is interesting that at that time there was no gymnasium even in St. Petersburg, the capital of the Russian Empire.

In the last quarter of the 18th century, Moscow University took an active part in organizing gymnasiums in a number of provincial cities of Russia - Yaroslavl, Tver, Vladimir, Kostroma, Vologda. University graduates became the first teachers of the newly created gymnasiums. Thus, at first the university was the main center for training scientific and teaching personnel.

Initially there was no department of mathematics at the university. Mathematics was the responsibility of the Department of Physics and was taught only at the Faculty of Philosophy. The content of the mathematics course did not go beyond elementary mathematics and included arithmetic, elementary algebra, geometry and basic trigonometry. One of M. V. Lomonosov’s students, Anton Alekseevich Barsov (1730-1791), was invited to teach this course.

But just three years later (in 1758), a decision was made to create a special department of numerical mathematics.

In the early years, mathematics was taught by non-professionals. Thus, the first professor of mathematics A. A. Barsov was specialized in the field of syntax of the Russian language. The teaching of the applied mathematics course was entrusted to Ivan Akimovich Rost, who taught him English.

In the first fifty years of the existence of Moscow University, the lecturer usually strictly adhered to the textbook he had chosen once and for all. Even the days and hours of lectures remained traditional. Thus, A. A. Barsov, back in 1757, chose Wednesday and Saturday for lectures on pure mathematics. In the same days and hours after A. A. Barsov was read by his student Dmitry Sergeevich Anichkov (1733-1788), and then by another university graduate Vasily Kondratyevich Arshenevsky (1758-1807). The same is the case with lectures on applied mathematics: I. A. Rost read them on Mondays, Tuesdays, Thursdays and Fridays.
