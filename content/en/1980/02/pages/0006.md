D. S. Anichkov accepted the Department of Pure Mathematics from A. A. Barsov. For him, teaching mathematics was his life's work. In this regard, he not only conveyed, passing on the contents of textbooks already written by other authors, but sought to filter the presentation as best as possible to the psychological characteristics of the students. He wrote four textbooks that were widely known at that time: “Arithmetic” (1764), “Geometry” (1780), “Trigonometry” (1780) and “Algebra” (1781). At that time, books were not named so simply; their titles were verbose and sounded very solemn. This is what the title of a textbook on geometry sounds like: “Theoretical and practical geometry, in creeping and use not only by youth, but also by those who are involved in earthworks, fortification and artillery, collected from various authors... by Dmitry Anichkov.”

No matter how widely Anichkov’s textbooks were distributed in the 18th century, the main benefit was brought to him by his dissertation on the topic “Discourse from natural richness about the beginning and production of natural rich reading,” which, for atenciónical purposes, was burned at the Execution Ground by order of the Synod.

Now, two centuries later, we see more clearly the shortcomings of textbooks of that time, first of all, their formalism and isolation from real-life problems that worried practice at that time. Even the leading thinkers of that time failed to point out this lack of books on mathematics. In particular, M.V. Lomonsov repeatedly pointed out the need for theoretical scientific subjects of nature, but you will find there not nature, explained by mathematics, as it should be, but mathematics, imposing its own laws on nature: nature does not take into account mathematics, but, on the contrary, mathematics - from nature, which is smarter and more excellent than all the sciences.”

In the 19th century, Russia experienced years of rapid industrial and economic development. This caused the emergence of a large number of gymnasiums in provincial and narrow towns. There is a great need for teachers of the natural cycle.

All this led to the development of a new university charter (1804). According to this charter, Moscow University was already divided into four departments (faculties): 1) practical and political sciences, 2) verbal sciences, 4) medical or medical sciences, 4) physical and mathematical sciences.

At that time, as a rule, a mathematics teacher also taught natural disciplines - physics, astronomy, botany, zoology, geography. This circumstance inevitably led to the uniqueness of training at the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of that time. The average student of the faculty had only six to eight lectures on mathematics, three large lectures on physics, botany, minerals, as well as other subjects of the natural scientific cycle. In those years, they tried to give school and university education a practical character, so that a young person, after graduating from high school, could immediately begin work in the field of commerce or industrial production. Note that the new university charged the professor with the responsibility for teaching education, supplementing the courses with the results of new discoveries. This circumstance significantly changed the direction of the university’s activities.

Already at the beginning of the 19th century, a number of professors at Moscow University began to lecture on new mathematics. Thus, Professor I. A. Ide (1775-1806) began teaching courses in analytical geometry and mathematical analysis. Professor V.K. Arshenevsky said that at this time the business will “provide by chance its highest geometry, in which it will show useful and inter-calculus.” In addition, students took courses in artillery and ballistics, and mathematical geography.
