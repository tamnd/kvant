Zhenya's body. And Galileo decides to find a mathematical exact description of this change in speed. Here it should be said that Galileo initially saw his task as mathematizing Aristotle’s physics: “Philosophy is written in the greatest book that is constantly open to our eyes (I’m talking about the Universe); but it is impossible to understand it without first learning to understand the language and distinguish the signs with which it is written. It is written in mathematical language, and its signs are triangles, circles and other mathematical figures.” However, it soon became clear that mathematization required a systematic revision of all the facts.

How to find the law of change in the speed of free fall? The experiment was just beginning to enter the practice of scientific research. For Aristotle and his followers, he was considered a mere and unworthy occupation both in establishing the truth and in verifying it. Galileo could have tried to do a series of experiments with freely falling bodies, checking careful measurements and looking for a pattern that explained them. Thus, Galileo's contemporary Kepler, drawing on numerous observations by Tycho Brahe, discovered that the planets move in ellipses. But Galileo chooses a different path. He decides to first indicate the law from general considerations, and only then test it experimentally. The previous one did not do this, but gradually this research plan will become one of the leading ones in establishing scientific truths.

Now about how Galileo tried to indicate the law. He decides that nature “strives to use the simplest and easiest means in all its devices,” and therefore the law of increasing speed must be sought “in the simplest and clearest form for everyone.” But since the speed increases with the distance traveled, what could be simpler than the assumption that the speed is proportional to the path: $v = cs$, $c$ is a constant number. This assumption frightened him at first: it turns out that the fall begins with zero speed, and that the speed is high from the very beginning. But here's some reasoning that convinced him that there was no contradiction (Fig. 2): “If a load falling on its own from a height of four cubits stinks the latter into the ground approximately four inches, when falling from a height of two cubits it stinks it into the ground less and, of course, even less when falling from a height of one cubit or one span, and when, finally, the load falls from a height of no more than the thickness of a finger, then does it produce greater effects on itself? than if it had been laid down without any blow? The action of a load raised by the thickness of the sheet will be even smaller and completely independent. Since the effect of the blow depends on the speed of the striking body, can one doubt that the movement is extremely slow and the speed is minimal if the effect of the blow is completely independent?

Galileo spent a long time exploring various consequences of the assumption made and unexpectedly discovered that... according to such a law, movement cannot be found at all! Let's try to understand what's the matter. The coefficient of the proportional c depends on the choice of time units. For simplicity, we will assume that $c = 1$, let it change in meters, and time in seconds. Then at all times $v = s$.
