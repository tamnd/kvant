Galileo's discovery shocked his contemporaries. Konchesne sections (ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas) - the pinnacle of Greek geometry - seemed to be the fruit of mathematical fantasy, unrelated to reality. And so Kepler discovered that the planets move from ellipses, and Galileo showed that parabolas arise in a completely “earthly” situation. (Even in the 19th century, Laplace cited the use of Conches sections as the most necessary of the applications of pure mathematics.) Taking advantage of the properties of the parabola, Galileo compiled tables that, in particular, allow experimental verification. He proved Tartala's assertion that an angle of 45° corresponds to the greatest flight range, and showed that for angles that add up to 90°, the flight ranges are the same (at a fixed speed).

Galileo's work on mechanics was completed in Padua, but was published only at the end of Galileo's life in the book Discourses and Mathematical Proofs Concerning Two New Branches of Science Relating to Mechanics and Local Motion.
It was published in 1638, and was written after the abdication of Galileo, when he was seriously ill and honored.

“Although I am silent, I do not spend my life entirely idle,” he wrote. The book is written in the form of dialogues conducted over six days by Salviati (providing the author’s point of view), Sagredo and Simpliuno (a supporter of Aristotle, his name is translated as “simpleton”). On the third and fourth days, they read the treatise of the academician (Galileo) “On Local Movement” and discuss it in detail. This discussion allows us to learn a lot about how Galileo went to his discoveries.

Epilogue

There is another side to the story told: it is a story not only about a discovery that has been made, but also about a discovery... that was missed. After Galileo realized that according to the law $v(t) = cs(t)$ motion could not be generated, he lost interest in this law. His interest is only in natural movements that originate in nature, and not in those speculative movements “which in reality do not occur in nature, but may correspond to assumed conditions.”
Soon Lord Napier of Wortland became interested in a movement that arose according to a similar law.

Napier considered a rectangular motion that occurred according to the law $v(t) = l(t)$, where $v(t)$ is the instantaneous speed at time $t$, and $l(t)$ is the distance of the point at time $t$ from a fixed point 0 on the straight line. Listen, considered by Galileo, answers the situation when the moving point is at the initial moment $t = 0$ at point 0, that is, $l(0) = 0$, $l(t) = s(t)$. Napier has $l(0) > 0$, $l(t) = l(0) + s(t)$.

It turns out that motion with such a property can, in principle, originate and has remarkable mathematical properties (although “it will not originate in nature”!).
