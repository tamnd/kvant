D. Izaak, T. Utkina

Circles
Moshkin's steppes

After an elective in mathematics, seventh-grader Styopa Moshkin went to see his classmate Oleg. He decided to take advantage of the Steppe parish.

- Let me copy off the geometry, otherwise I don’t understand anything.

- Do you think that after this you will understand her better? - Styopa unexpectedly objected, deciding this time to show consciousness.

- Well, how do you figure out this geometry? - Oleg exclaimed. - In algebra, wherever you go, keep counting until you get the answer. And in the geometry of movements you can’t see or make jokes, and now there are some circles, these, what’s their name, compositions...

— During the elective, we were just analyzing compositions of movements.

And you know, with him it’s just like in algebra: we count, we count, and we get the answer. So great! Let me tell you!

- Why do I need your elective? You would better tell me how to solve homework.

The guys didn’t argue for long: Styopa’s enthusiasm turned out to be stronger than Oleg’s. Soon, sheets of paper for the draft, pens, and a blank double-marked notebook with a square appeared on the table. At first, Styopa tried to imitate Maria Vasilyevna, but quickly fell into a more excited speech:

“You understand, a circle is kind of like multiplication in algebra, an operation between letters,” Styopa began. “Only the letters do not represent numbers, but permutations.” And just like in algebra, it doesn’t matter how you put parentheses.

Styopa wrote in a clean notebook

$$R \circ (Q \circ P) = (R \circ Q) \circ P.$$  (1)

- This is called the law of associativity. And the same movement $E$ is when all the points remain in place, like units们

$$P \circ E = E \circ P = P.$$  (2)
