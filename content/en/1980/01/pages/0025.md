In order to achieve this, he draws a number of consequences from the uniform speed of motion of a point on an inclined plane, which are already convenient for LAB oratory testing (if the angle of inclination is small, then the rolling time is long). The central place is occupied by the statement that if the inclined planes have the same height $AB$, then the rolling times are treated as the distances traversed $AB_j$ (Fig. 4).

Movement on an inclined plane was of independent interest to Galileo. He makes a series of observations. For example, if the points of motion along the chords of the circle $AE_i, BF_j$ (Fig. 5), $AB$ are the vertical diameter, then all rolling times are equal to the free fall times along $AB$ (prove it!). A rather complex argument leads Galileo to prove that if $A, B, C$ are successive points on the circle (Fig. 6), then a point along the broken line $ABC$ rolls faster than along the chord $AC$. Galileo’s well-known error is associated with this statement: he believed that the fastest point from $A$ to $B$ rolls along the fourth circle, while the arc of a cyclid has this property (see «Kvant», 1975, No. 12, p. 29).

In his experiments with inclined planes, Galileo needed a means of comparing time. To do this, he used a slowly flowing stream of water, that is, a certain version of a water clock.
