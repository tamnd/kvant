The condensation process occurs with the release of heat of vaporization. And this heat is not small - about 2.5 · 10⁶ J are released per kilogram of water formed from steam at a temperature of 18°C. Due to this heat release, the temperature of moist air decreases as it rises more slowly than that of dry air. If the clouds, together with the air, crossed the ridge without losing a drop of water, then later, when descending along the windward slope and the air temperature increased, the water would evaporate again, and this would consume the previously released heat. The air at the foot on the windward side would have the same temperature and humidity as at the foot on the leeward side of the ridge.

If the wind is humid and the ridge is quite high, then a significant proportion of the water settles with fog or falls with jam and snow. Already dried air descends, the temperature of which rises faster during descent than it fell for humid air during ascent. (The ridge must be high so that the increase in air temperature with rise is sufficient for condensation.) Therefore, at the same altitude, the air temperature on the west side is higher than on the leeward side.

Let us now estimate the amount of temperature difference. Let's consider a curved layer of air, the side surface of which follows the directional speed of the wind, and the ends are located on opposite sides of the ridge. After a short time, a certain air mass M of this layer will leave the volume V₁ on the leeward side at the foot of the mountain, where the temperature is T₁ and the pressure is p₁. On the leeward side of the air, descending, it will occupy a new volume V₂ at temperature T₂ and pressure p₂. (We will assume that at each fixed location the air temperature, pressure, and wind speed remain unchanged over time. In particular, this means that the same mass of air passes through any section of the layer at the same time.)

The internal energy of one mole of a monatomic gas with temperature T is, as is known, equal to $\frac{3}{2}RT$. Air consists mainly of diatomic molecules of nitrogen and oxygen. For a diatomic gas, the internal energy of one mole is $\frac{5}{2}RT$.

The change in the internal energy of the air layer we have isolated is equal to

$$\Delta U = \frac{5}{2} \frac{M}{\mu} R (T_2 - T_1).$$

Let us now calculate the work done against external pressure forces. The air that descended on the north side flowed out of the volume V₂ of the air that was previously there with a pressure p₂, performing positive work p₂V₂; on the leeward side of the air, volume V₁ left at pressure p₁, performing negative work -p₁V₁. The total work is equal to

$$A = p_2V_2 - p_1V_1.$$

Using the equation of state for an ideal gas ($pV = \frac{M}{\mu}RT$), we express the work in terms of temperatures T₂ and T₁:

$$A = \frac{M}{\mu} R (T_2 - T_1).$$

(Since the volumes V₁ and V₂ are at the same height, the potential energy of the air does not change in the final case.)

Let us assume that the mountain is so high that when the air rises on the leeward side, almost everything condenses and falls out in the form of jedr. If the mass of precipitation is Δm, then the heat Q released during condensation is equal to λΔm (λ is the specific temperature of vaporization).

In the final case, it is this heat Q that goes to change the internal energy ΔU of the air and to perform work A:

$$Q = \Delta U + A.$$
