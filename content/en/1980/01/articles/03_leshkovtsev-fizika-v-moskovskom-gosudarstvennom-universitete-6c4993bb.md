at Moscow State University

It is not without reason that Moscow State University (MSU) bears the name of the great Russian scientist Mikhail Vasilyevich Lomonosov. The university was created 225 years ago on his advice and design. “My main reason,” Lomonosov wrote about this project, “is that the plan should serve for all future years.” There was this hope - the university arose and soon became one of the main charms of Russian education and science. Lomonosov did not take any active part in the activities of Moscow University. He had no students in the field of physics, so he practically could not have any influence on the development of physics at Moscow State University.

Moscow University was formed by decree of Empress Elizabeth Petrovna on January 12, 1755. At first there were only three faculties: physics, law and medicine; In the last course I took a physics course. Only in 1804 was the Faculty of Physical and Mathematical Sciences formed. Life itself (the development of production, the needs of the army) began to make demands on the training of specialists in these fields of science.

Even Peter I, striving for the development of domestic science, pointed out the need not only to teach science, but also to “produce it.” But while foreign scientists were invited to the Academy of Sciences (Euler, Bernoulli, Lenz and others), there was nothing of the kind for Moscow University.

The first major physicist to work at Moscow University was Aleksandr Grigorievich Stoletov. He gave more than 30 years of his life to the university. Stoletov was a student of Moscow University, from which he graduated with honors in 1860. Soon he was sent abroad for three years, where he continued his education in laboratories in Berlin, Heidelberg, Gottingen, and Paris. During these years he studied with Helmholtz, Kirchhoff, Weber, Magnus and other major scientists.

In 1865, Stoletov returned to his homeland and soon began teaching physics at the university - this activity continued until the last days of his life (he died in 1896). He was the first to teach a course in mathematical physics. With enormous efforts, in 1872 he managed to create the first physical laboratory in which he could conduct independent scientific research. Until this moment, university teachers, including Stoletov himself, conducted scientific research only in foreign laboratories. Stoletov also founded the first permanent scientific seminar on problems of physics, from among the participants of which such prominent scientists as N. A. Umov, P. N. Lebedev, N. E. Zhukovsky grew up.

Stoletov's work laid the foundations of theoretical electrical engineering; they have received wide international recognition. In 1871, Stoletov began researching the magnetic properties of iron, the main material for electrical engineering.

Under the influence of a magnetic field, iron acquires magnetic properties - it becomes a magnet. The magnetization of body I and its magnetic permeability μ depend on the magnitude of the magnetic induction of the magnetizing field. In addition, these values ​​also depend on the shape of the body, which greatly complicates the study. Before Stoletov, the values ​​of I and μ were changed only along the length of the rods. Stoletov created a new method for measuring these values ​​of body shapes and obtaining patterns characterizing the materials from which it is made. Based on these principles, the creators of electric machines found the key to solving many practical problems.

In 1887, the German physicist Hertz discovered the mysterious coupling of electrical breakdown in gases (the passage of a spark between electrodes connected to an electrical circuit); he discovered that irradiating the electrodes with ultraviolet rays facilitates the breakdown. Stoletov also showed that the strength of the saturation photocurrent is proportional to the light flux incident on the cathode, or, in modern terms, to the number of light quanta incident on the cathode. The laws of the photoelectric effect, discovered by Stoletov, played an important role in substantiating the quantum theory of radiation and found many valuable practical applications.

At the beginning of 1874, Umov published the work “Equations of Energy Motion in Bodies.” It contains the idea of ​​the flow of different types of energy in different media. Umov showed that many physical processes (for example, the propagation of light waves or vibrations in a solid) are associated with a continuous flow of energy. He was the first to introduce the concept of energy density, the speed of its propagation, the direction of energy flow, giving them strict physical definitions. Based on the law of conservation of energy, Umov obtained the general law of energy propagation. The physical properties of this law are as follows: the change in energy in a given volume of some medium per unit time is equal to the energy flow through the surface limiting this volume. Today these ideas of the Minds have become generally accepted. But at that time, Umov encountered deep misunderstanding on the part of senior physicists. Only ten years later, in 1884, the English physicist Poyntig published a widely known article on the transfer of electromagnetic energy, the main conclusions of which coincided with the conclusions of the more general theory of Minds.

The second problem studied by Umov, which we want to talk about here, is terrestrial magnetism. It is known that the Earth is a huge magnet. The nature of the Earth's magnetic field is very complex, and scientists are still arguing about the origin of this field. The formal mathematical theory of the earth's magnetic field was developed by the great German mathematician Gauss. He came up with a formula that can be used to calculate the magnitude of magnetic induction for any point on the surface of the globe. It is a sum of 24 terms with constant coefficients, determined from experience. Umov was the first to reveal the physical meaning of individual terms and coefficients in the Gauss formula.

The last outstanding physicist to work at Moscow University before the October Revolution was Pyotr Nikolaevich Lebedev. He was not a student of Moscow University, since he received his higher education at the Moscow Higher Technical School. Like Stoletov and Umov, he also improved his education abroad - in Strasbourg and Berlin. In 1891, Lebedev received a doctorate from the University of Strasbourg. The work he presented for defense was devoted to the theory of dielectrics and the determination of the dielectric constant of vapors of certain substances.

The theory of electromagnetic phenomena, created by Maxwell in the middle of the 19th century, did not immediately receive scientific recognition. Stoletov actively fought for the approval of this theory. (He conducted a series of experiments to accurately determine the correspondence between the coefficients that determine the force of interaction of electrical charges and the force of interaction of electric currents. Umov also took part in this struggle, whose work on the flow of energy confirmed and developed some conclusions arising from Maxwell’s theory. From Maxwell's theory it follows that light falling on the surface of a body exerts pressure on it. However, attempts to verify this conclusion experimentally by several scientists were unsuccessful. They came to the conclusion that if light pressure exists, its magnitude is very small and is overlapped by other, many times more noticeable physical effects that are inevitably present in such experiments. First of all, this is a radiometric effect associated with the fact that the body heated by light is separated differently from the illuminated and unlit sides. The second side effect that complicates the experiment is convection—the emergence of heat flows in air heated by light; Warm air rises, cold air descends.

Lebedev was the first to overcome all these difficulties. The device he built achieved a record degree of air rarefaction at that time. Platinum wings, on which light alternately falls from one to the other; on the other hand, they were very thin, which practically eliminated the influence of the radiometric effect. And the glass cylinder, inside of which the changing device was placed, was large enough to significantly weaken the influence of convection air flows. The value of light pressure on the light platinum wings changed by Lebedev coincided with the value calculated using Maxwell’s theory. This work was supplemented by Lebedev in Paris at the World Congress of Physicists in 1900 and immediately received universal recognition. The outstanding English physicist William Thomson (Lord Kelvin, to whom the absolute temperature scale is named), learned about Lebedev’s work and said to his acquaintance, Russian biologist Klement Arkadyevich Timirzev: “You may know that all my life I fought with Maxwell, not recognizing his light pressure, and now your Lebedev made me surrender to his experiments.”

In 1909, Lebedev completed three years of experiments to prove the existence of light pressure on gases, which required even greater ingenuity. It turned out that light exerted pressure on the gases and this is what determines the shape of cometary tails.

After the October Revolution, the Physics Faculty of Moscow State University continuously developed and improved. The creation of the Department of Theoretical Physics, the first head of which was the talented young scientist S. A. Boguslavsky, invited from Saratov, played a very important role in its development. Prodatov studied at Moscow State University for two years, he died of tuberculosis. Then, in his place, professor (and later academician) Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam was invited from Odessa University, who arrived together with his collaborator (later also academician) Igor Evgenievich Tamm. Both of them were outstanding Soviet scientists who had a great influence on the development of all Soviet physics. Gradually, the level of physics teaching at Moscow State University became equal to the best foreign higher education institutions.

By far the largest of the discoveries he made was the discovery of Raman scattering of light. If you illuminate a body with light of a strictly defined wavelength, then the light scattered by this body will have the same wavelength. This truth was long ago and reliably established by opticians. But L.I. Mandelstam and G.S. Landsberg decided to find out whether, when light is scattered in crystals, some new light waves do not arise that are not present in the light incident on the crystal. To do this, they performed the following experiment.

Monchromatic light, obtained from a ground lamp with the help of a filter, fell on a crystal of the most pure and homogeneous quartz. The light scattered by the crystal was analyzed using a spectrograph. One of the main difficulties of the experiment is that of the total amount of light energy incident on the crystal, only about one ten-millicard fraction is scattered, and almost all of the scattered light coincides in wavelength with the incident light. It is difficult to detect any new wavelengths in the non-teaching light reflected by the crystal.

Nevertheless, after an extremely long experiment, Mandelstam and Landsberg found in reflected light two additional spectral lines, symmetrically located at equal distances from the apex. The differences between the frequencies of these lines and the frequency of the incident light are identical and coincide with the frequency of internal vibrations of the molecules of the scattering substance.
