The first foundations of dynamics were laid by Galileo. The action of forces do was considered exclusively in the case of their equilibrium; and although the accelerated movement of freely feeding bodies and the curved movement of thrown bodies also assigned a constant acting force to gravity, it was not possible to establish the laws of this combined phenomenon, depending on such a simple reason. This discovery... now constitutes the most significant and indelible part of the merit of this great man. Free fall

As for Galileo's scientific interests, he remained interested in movements and, above all, the free falling of bodies, one of the most common natural movements. As was customary at this time, we need to start with what Aristotle argued about this. The latter’s opinion was based on two axioms: the speeds of falling bodies are proportional to their weight and inversely proportional to the “density of the medium.” Obvious difficulties arose with the second statement: it turned out that in emptiness, the “density” of which is zero, the speed of falling should be infinite. To this, Aristotle added that there is no emptiness in nature (“nature is bound by emptiness”).

In 1585, Galileo had the opportunity to familiarize himself with the immovable treatise of Benedetti, who allowed himself to disagree with Aristotle’s first statement. And here's the thing. Let there be two bodies - heavy and light (Fig. 1); the first one must fall quickly. Now let's connect them. It is natural to assume that a light body takes precedence over a heavy one and the speed of falling becomes intermediate between the falling speeds of the constituent bodies. But according to Aristotle, the speed must become greater than the speed of each body. With Benedetti's work in mind, Galile begins his reflections. But they didn’t really believe in reasoning, and, according to Vivania, “he proved this with repeated experiments carried out from the top of the Leaning Tower of Pisa in the presence of other lecturers, philosophers and the entire scientific fraternity.” Galileo is still often painted with kidoshim stones from the Leaning Tower of Pisa.

Even more appealing to Galileo's take-out was another observation by Benedetti, that the speed of free fall increases as one moves.

Here it should be said that Galileo initially saw his task as mathematizing Aristotle’s physics: “Philosophy is written in the greatest book that is constantly open to our eyes (I’m talking about the Universe); but it is impossible to understand it without first learning to understand the language and distinguish the signs with which it is written. How to find the law of change in the speed of free fall? The experiment was just beginning to enter the practice of scientific research. For Aristotle and his followers, he was considered a mere and unworthy occupation both in establishing the truth and in verifying it. Galileo could have tried to do a series of experiments with freely falling bodies, checking careful measurements and looking for a pattern that explained them. Thus, Galileo's contemporary Kepler, drawing on numerous observations by Tycho Brahe, discovered that the planets move in ellipses. But Galileo chooses a different path. He decides to first indicate the law from general considerations, and only then test it experimentally. The previous one did not do this, but gradually this research plan will become one of the leading ones in establishing scientific truths.

Now about how Galileo tried to indicate the law. He decides that nature “strives to use the simplest and easiest means in all its devices,” and therefore the law of increasing speed must be sought “in the simplest and clearest form for everyone.” But since the speed increases with the distance traveled, what could be simpler than the assumption that the speed is proportional to the path: $v = cs$, $c$ is a constant number. This assumption frightened him at first: it turns out that the fall begins with zero speed, and that the speed is high from the very beginning. But here's some reasoning that convinced him that there was no contradiction (Fig. 2): “If a load falling on its own from a height of four cubits stinks the latter into the ground approximately four inches, when falling from a height of two cubits it stinks it into the ground less and, of course, even less when falling from a height of one cubit or one span, and when, finally, the load falls from a height of no more than the thickness of a finger, then does it produce greater effects on itself? than if it had been laid down without any blow? The action of a load raised by the thickness of the sheet will be even smaller and completely independent. Since the effect of the blow depends on the speed of the striking body, can one doubt that the movement is extremely slow and the speed is minimal if the effect of the blow is completely independent?

Galileo spent a long time exploring various consequences of the assumption made and unexpectedly discovered that... according to such a law, movement cannot be found at all! Let's try to understand what's the matter. The coefficient of the proportional c depends on the choice of time units. For simplicity, we will assume that $c = 1$, let it change in meters, and time in seconds. Then at all times $v = s$.

Consider a point $A$ located at a distance of 1 m from the origin $O$. Let's estimate how long it takes from the beginning of the body's movement to reach this point. At point $A$ the speed is 1 m/s. Let's take the point $A_1$, which lies halfway between the beginning $O$ and $A$. On segment $A_1A$ the instantaneous speed will be less than 1 m/s, and for a segment 1/2 m long it will take more than 1/2 s. Let us now take the point $A_2$ - in the middle between $O$ and $A_1$. On the segment $A_1A_2$ the instantaneous speed will be less than 1/2 m/s (all points located from $O$ are less than 1/2 m away), and the segment $A_1A_2$ with a length of 1/4 m will again take more than 1/2 s. You have, of course, already proven, as we will argue further: point $A_3$ is the middle of the segment $OA_2$, into a segment $A_2A_3$ 1/8 m long at a speed less than 1/4 m/s, again it will take more than 1/2 s, etc. The division process can be continued indefinitely, and we can collect any number of segments, the passage of which takes more 1/2 s, never add up to $O$. This means that a body from $O$ cannot get to $A$ at all!

We proposed that $A$ is 1 m away from $O$. But in a similar way it is shown that the body from $O$ cannot reach any point at all. This is the remarkable reasoning with which classical mechanics began!

Well, Galileo had every reason to be offended by the treachery of nature, which did not choose the simplest path. However, as a result, Galileo’s nature did not fade away. He considers an equally simple assumption that the increase in speed will occur in proportion to time: $v = ct$. Such movement is called accelerated, but the term “uniformly accelerated movement” has taken root.

Galileo considers the speed graph on the time interval from 0 to $t$ (Fig. 3) and replaces that if the moment of time $t_1$, $t_2$, equilateral from $t/2$, is taken, then how much in $t_1$ the speed is less than $ct/2$, the present in $t_2$ it is greater. From this he concludes that on average the speed is $ct/2$, and the distance traveled is $ct/2 \cdot t = ct^2/2$ (don’t think too harshly!). This means that if we consider equilateral time segments $t=1$, 2, 3, 4,..., then the segments of the path taken from the beginning will be related as the squares of the natural numbers 1, 4, 9, 16,..., and the segments taken between adjacent moments of the report will be related to the odd numbers 1, 3, 5, 7,....

It is this conclusion that Galileo considers to be the main one and he wants to check it. But how to do this? You can’t keep throwing stones from the Leaning Tower of Pisa (although it may be convenient for observation that it is divided into floors), and in the laboratory the fall will happen too quickly. Galileo and here is the exit. It shows that if the free fall is uniformly accelerated, then the motion of bodies along an inclined plane will also be uniformly accelerated. He conducts a series of very cleverly selected experiments.

Epilogue

There is another side to the story told: it is a story not only about a discovery that has been made, but also about a discovery... that was missed. After Galileo realized that according to the law $v(t) = cs(t)$ motion could not be generated, he lost interest in this law. His interest is only in natural movements that originate in nature, and not in those speculative movements “which in reality do not occur in nature, but may correspond to assumed conditions.”
Soon Lord Napier of Wortland became interested in a movement that arose according to a similar law.

Napier considered a rectangular motion that occurred according to the law $v(t) = l(t)$, where $v(t)$ is the instantaneous speed at time $t$, and $l(t)$ is the distance of the point at time $t$ from a fixed point 0 on the straight line. Listen, considered by Galileo, answers the situation when the moving point is at the initial moment $t = 0$ at point 0, that is, $l(0) = 0$, $l(t) = s(t)$. Napier has $l(0) > 0$, $l(t) = l(0) + s(t)$.

It turns out that motion with such a property can, in principle, originate and has remarkable mathematical properties (although “it will not originate in nature”!).
