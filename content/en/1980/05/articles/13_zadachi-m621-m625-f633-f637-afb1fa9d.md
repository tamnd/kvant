M624. Find the sequence $(a_n)$ defined by the conditions $a_1 = 1$,
$$1 + \sum_{d} (-1)^{n/d} a_d = 0,$$
where the sum $\sum$ is taken over all divisors $d$ of $n$ (including $d = 1$ and $d = n$).

For example, if $n = p$ is a prime number, then (*) takes the form $1 + (-1)^{p/1} a_1 + (-1)^{p/p} a_p = 0$, hence $a_p = 2$ if $p = 2$, and $a_p = 0$ if $p > 2$.

M567. The natural numbers $p$ and $q$ are coprime. The segment $[0; 1]$ is divided into $p+q$ identical segments (Fig. 1). Prove that each of these segments, except the two extreme ones, contains exactly one of $p+q-2$ numbers

$$\frac{1}{p}, \frac{2}{p}, \dots, \frac{p-1}{p},$$

$$\frac{1}{q}, \frac{2}{q}, \dots, \frac{q-1}{q}.$$

Let's give two solutions.

First decision. It follows from the condition that each of the numbers $p$ and $q$ is coprime with the number $n=p+q$, so no two of the point $i/p, j/q, k/n$ (other than 0 and 1) coincide. Since $1/p > 1/n$ and $1/q > 1/n$, any two from the point $i/p$ lie in different segments $\left[\frac{k}{n}; \frac{k+1}{n}\right]$ and any two from the point $j/q$ too. You just need to prove that some two points $i/p$ and $j/q$ cannot fall into the same segment $\left[\frac{k}{n}; \frac{k+1}{n}\right]$ ($k=1, 2, \dots, n-2$). But this immediately follows from the fact that the fraction $\frac{k}{n} = \frac{i+j}{p+q}$ lies between $\frac{i}{p}$ and $\frac{j}{q}$ (see, on Fig. 1).

$p=8, q=5, n=8+5=13$

Second solution. Let's draw a rectangle with dimensions $p \times q$ cells on checkered paper and draw a diagonal $OE$ (Fig. 3) ​​in it - it will play the role of segment $[0; 1]$ of our problem. The lines of one direction (blue) divide it into $p$ equal parts, the other (red) - into $q$ equal parts. Let's draw another series of parallel straight lines through the tops of the cells - at an angle $45^\circ$ to the grid lines (on figure these are black straight lines $x+y=k$, where $k=1, 2, \dots, p+q-1$). They divide $OE$ into $n=p+q$ equal segments. The statement of the problem becomes almost obvious. In fact, on $OE$ there is necessarily a black dot between any two synchromatic points: after all, crossing some cell, $OE$ must also intersect its black diagonal. (You can say this instead: between any points of intersection of $OE$ with adjacent black lines there is a point of intersection with a blue or red line.)

In this solution, the mutual primality of the numbers $p$ and $q$ ensures that $OE$ does not pass through grid nodes other than $O$ and $E$ (looking at our little figure one might doubt this).

Problem M567 allows for a substitutional generalization. Let α and β be any positive numbers related by the relation 1/α + 1/β = 1.

Let us mark on the number line free numbers of the form iα and jβ (i ∈ Z, j ∈ Z). Then each segment [k; k+1] axis (k ∈ Z), neither of whose ends contains the offset number, contains exactly one of the offset numbers iα, jβ.

Our problem is equivalent to this fact for rational α and β: we need to take α = n/p, β = n/q (the role of the segment [0; 1] will now be played by the segment [0; n]). The same fact (for inrational α and β) was mentioned recently in the solution to problem M538 («Kvant», 1979, no. 11), very similar to our second solution to M567.

It is also possible, very indo, to obtain any double double-rational fraction, that is, a fraction of the form p/2^m, p<2^m (Fig. 1).

To get the fraction 1/q, it is enough to represent 1 as the sum of q different proper double-rational fractions: their arithmetic mean will be exactly 1/q. Let us prove by induction that this is possible. 1 can be represented as the sum of two such fractions (for example, 1=3/4+1/4).

From representation 1 as a sum of n-1 different double-rational fractions, we can obtain its representation as a sum of n such fractions:

$$1 = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_{n-1} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{a_1}{2} + \frac{a_2}{2} + \dots + \frac{a_{n-1}}{2}.$$

In particular, following this construction, we obtain

$$1 = \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{8} =$$
$$= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{16} + \frac{1}{16} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{3}{32} + \frac{1}{32},$$

which gives the representation of the number 1/5.

Geler can go about proving the second statement of the problem in many different ways. Let's give two of them.

1. Let us prove that if a natural number p can be represented as a sum of n-1 different proper double-rational fractions:

$$p = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_{n-1} \quad (0 < a_{n-1} < \dots < a_2 < a_1 < 1),$$

then it can be represented as a sum of n such fractions. Let 2^m be the lowest of the values ​​of the terms a_i. Replacing a_n-1 with the sum $\left(a_{n-1} - \frac{1}{2^m+2}\right) + \frac{1}{2^m+2}$, we get the desired representation. So, each of the fractions $\frac{1}{q}, \frac{2}{q}, \dots, \frac{q-1}{q}$ can be represented as the arithmetic mean of various proper double-rational fractions, then the fractions $\frac{1}{q+1}, \frac{2}{q+1}, \dots, \frac{q-1}{q+1}$ can be represented in a similar form. To prove the proof by induction (the base of induction is the representation of the number 1/2), it remains to represent the fraction $\frac{q}{q+1}$ in the required form. It can be obtained from the representation of the number $\frac{1}{q+1}$ by replacing all fractions a_i with 1—a_i.

2. Let's consider the three numbers already obtained: 0, 1/q, 1. However, of the two middle ones, $\frac{0+1}{2}, \frac{1/q+1}{2}$ are fractions of the form p_0/q, 1<p_0<q (если q>2). However, of the two middle ones $\frac{0+p_0/q}{2}, \frac{1/q+p_0/q}{2}$ -
