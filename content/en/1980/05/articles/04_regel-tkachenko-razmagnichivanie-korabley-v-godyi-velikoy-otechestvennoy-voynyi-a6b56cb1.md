Demagnetization of ships during the Great Patriotic War

On June 24, 1941, at 2:41 a.m., the destroyer Gnevny was struck by a mine at the mouth of the Gulf of Finland. At 4 hours 21 minutes, the cruiser Maxim Gorky was blown up by a mine in the same area, but reached Tallinn under its own power. It was assumed that the mines were non-contact (magnetic). Already in the first days of the war, the enemy created a server mine threat of escape from Soviet naval bases and on the main sea routes of communication, using a total of 1060 anchor strike mines and up to 160 données non-contact mines. The situation was created by a threat. What about our fleet? Was he prepared for the possibility of a mine blockade or was it just an accident and completely unexpected?

Among the many tasks of defense importance, demagnetization of ships occupied an important place. In a short article, it is impossible to cover in detail the entire epic of demagnetization and list all the participants, since in addition to scientists, many military sailors and shipbuilders were involved in solving this problem.

For a magnetic mine to explode, it does not require direct contact with the ship's hull. Explode due to the impact of the ship's magnetic field on it. The idea of ​​making such a fuse is so simple that it could have arisen immediately after the invention of the compass and the appearance of stable ships. Everyone knows that the compass needle is deflected if an iron object is brought to it. The deflection of such an arrow placed inside a mine can be used to close the fuse contact. With an appropriate increase in sensitivity, the mine explosion will occur at the moment when the ship is above it, which means that its most unprotected part, the bottom, will be affected. Further more ships, as a rule, perish when such mines explode underneath them. In addition, magnetic mines lying on the bottom are not supplied by the usual trawling method, designed to cut and detonate anchor mines. The installation of magnetic mines can be carried out not only from ships, but also from aircraft - on the open sea, in bays, harbors, and on the fairway.

These advantages of magnetic mines over conventional anchor contact mines for the basis of naval specialists were predicted in advance by their use in the upcoming war, especially since our sailors had to get acquainted for the first time with the magnetic mines of the English invaders back in September 1919 on the Northern Dvina. Even then, several of our ships were blown up while the English were loading magnetic mines. However, military sailors quickly cleared the fairway of these still comparatively primitive mines.

It was natural to expect that in a new war improved magnetic mines would be used: more sensitive, with attachments that would make them more difficult to mine and destroy. All this forced the commanders of the Navy

The USSR Fleet was given a special task - to develop methods for protecting ships from non-contact magnetic mine and torpedo weapons.

This important work was entrusted to the Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute (LPTI) of the USSR Academy of Sciences back in 1936. Anatoly Petrovich Aleksadrov (now an academician, director of the Institute of Atomic Energy named after I.V. Kurchatov, president of the USSR Academy of Sciences) and Boris Aleksadrovich Gaev (recent Doctor of Technical Sciences, deputy director of the LFTI named after A.F. Ioffe of the USSR Academy of Sciences, died in 1974) took on its implementation. A.P. Aleksadrov organized a special group in his laboratory, headed by B.A. Gaev, which dealt with the problem of demagnetization of ships.

The idea underlying the work to protect ships from non-contact magnetic mines was to demagnetize the ship. It was assumed that this could be done by compensating the ship's magnetic field using special windings attached to it, through which direct current is passed (see figure). In this case, the magnetic field of the ship can be accumulated by the magnetic field of the current to such an extent that the passage of the ship over the mine will not cause a comparative explosion, which has limited sensitivity.

The simple idea proposed by A.P. Aleksadrov, B.A. Gaev and the engineer of the Leningrad Baltic Plant A.A. Kartkovsky did not immediately receive support from specialists. Many of the miners believed that if the ship was already demagnetized, then its magnetic field would need to be completely concentrated to zero. And since this is impossible due to the weight of the complex field configuration, demagnetization becomes pointless, and all efforts must be concentrated on creating and improving traline methods. Some specialists even believed that the ship should not be demagnetized, but magnetized even more strongly, so that the increased magnetic field would cause an explosion of a magnetic mine at a great distance from the ship. By the way, as it became known after the war, the British, who began work on mine protection for ships also in 1936, took precisely this wrong path (after all, the enemy can cause both sensitive and hardened mines). As a result, by the beginning of the war, their ships were not protected from German magnetic mines and torpedoes, and the British fleet suffered very significant losses. Already during the war, the British had to demagnetize demagnetizing devices and promptly equip their ships with them.

The group of A.P. Aleksadrov had to prematurely identify the feasibility of identification. Initially, research was carried out on a laboratory model of a ship made of wood and common leaves and iron. As a result of these experiments, the most optimal types of demagnetizing windings were found. It was decided to move on to experiments on floating ships.

The first changes in the magnetic fields of ships and experiments to compensate for them were carried out by LPTI employees in 1937 in the dry dock of Kronstadt on the destroyers Yakov Sverdlov and Artem, and then on the Leningrad Lyceum. The results were very positive. In May 1938, in the Orange seaport, a temporary demagnetizing winding was applied to the Dozorny ship, and by changing the field under the ship, the optimal current in it was selected. Then the ship made more passes with the winding turned off and on over disarmed non-contact magnetic mines installed at different depths. It was recorded that the mines were reliably set off when a ship passed over them with the winding turned off and did not react at all when the optimal current was turned off in the winding.

Thus, the task of mine protection of a small ship was successfully solved. It was time to move on to the experience of demagnetizing large ships. In October 1938, the LINKOR “Marat” was allocated for experiments. And on this largest ship of our Navy, with the help of a temporary demagnetizing winding, it was possible to reduce the magnetic field tenfold in an unprecedented proximity to the keel.

To obtain all the necessary data about the ship's field for designing a protection system, it was necessary to change the magnetic induction values ​​of this field at a large number of points at different depths. To do this, an aluminum rod with a magnetometer attached to it on a special trolley was lowered under the ship *). The rod was moved under the ship and installed at different depths. For each position of the rod, the magnetic induction was changed at several points under the keel and outside the sides of the ship. The LFTI group learned how to quickly perform this labor-intensive work, which requires good debugging of the instrument and device, so as not to delay the ships any longer in the roadstead.

Thus, by the beginning of the Great Patriotic War, reliable methods of protecting our ships from enemy magnetic mines had been created.

The first morning of the war found A.P. Alexandrov’s group aboard the LINKOR “Marat”. Already at 4 o'clock in the morning, a combat alert was announced on it: enemy planes appeared from the Finnish coast. Having checked the compliance of the calculated ampere-turns in the sections of the windings and made sure that the direction of the currents in them was correct, the commission commissioned LINKora protection devices, went ashore in Kronstadt and immediately received the command’s order to promptly equip several minesweepers with mine protection systems. Without limiting this, the group of A.P. Alexandrov mounted two electromagnetic trawls from the available cable, and already on June 27, 1941

Yes, these minesweepers went out on a combat mission.

The forecasts of the LPTI and navy specialists were justified: the very first action of the fascist German command in the naval theaters of war after the treacherous attack on the Soviet Union was an attempt to block our ships in their bases and tie up their military operations with massive laying of magnetic mines. The Nazis had high hopes for the effectiveness of these new naval weapons and were confident that Soviet sailors and specialists would not be able to quickly find ways and means to protect ships.

It was in these difficult conditions of the beginning of the war that the enormous work that was done in the pre-war years by scientists, sailors and shipbuilding specialists began to tell. In the very first days, a number of ships of the Baltic Fleet were burned and time was closed on the deck along the sides.
