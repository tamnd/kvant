In the presence of lateral force, the automatic tire “goes” at a certain angle to its symmetry plane. This is called the tire slip phenomenon. To study the phenomenon of withdrawal, such an experiment is used. The tire under test is fixed on a stationary axle and pressed against a rotating drum with a force G corresponding to the load, that is, the weight of the machine “attached” to one wheel (figure 2, a). The wheel is fixed so that its plane of symmetry (Pl. M) makes some angle δ, called the slip angle (figure 2, b), with the plane of symmetry of the drum (Pl. N).

When the drum rotates, the friction force $\vec{F}_{\text{тр}} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2$ (figure 2, c) will act on the wheel. The term $\vec{F}_1$ is the force that “spins” the wheel, and the term $\vec{R}_2$ is that force; which, if not “compensated”, will mix the wheel “across” the drum. Therefore, in order for the wheel to “go” at an angle δ to the plane N, a force $\vec{P} = -\vec{F}_2$ must be applied to it.

By measuring the magnitude of the force $\vec{P}$ using the diameter at different slip angles, it is possible to construct a graph of the dependence $\|\vec{P}\|$ (δ). The general appearance of such a graph is shown in figure 3. At small slip angles ($\delta < 5^\circ$), the dependence of $\|\vec{P}\|$ on δ turns out to be almost linear, so we write $\|\vec{P}\| = k\delta$. (1)

The coefficient k is called the lateral slip resistance of a given tire. The value of k depends on the tire design and the magnitude of the load $\vec{G}$. For modern tires the resistance is approximately equal.

$$k \approx 5 \|\vec{G}\|.$$

For example, for an automatic Zhiguli, the load on one wheel is approximately 2200 N. Therefore, $k \approx 11000$ N/rad.

At slip angles $\delta > 5^\circ$, the lateral force increases with increasing δ. The section of the curve $\|\vec{P}\|$ (δ) at $\delta > 5^\circ$ corresponds to the rolling of a wheel with full side slip. The corresponding friction force is the so-called ultimate friction force - $\|\vec{P}_0\| = \mu \|\vec{G}\|$, where μ is the overall friction coefficient

“Straight” forces you to turn the steering wheel a little from time to time. During one certain period of time, while the steering wheel is motionless, the trajectory of the center of gravity of the automaton is close to a circular arc of a very large radius (compared to the length of the automaton). Thus, the real trajectory of the center of gravity of an automaton moving “straight” consists of a friend surrounded by a large radius and only appears to be a straight line.

Let us consider the movement of the automaton along a circle of radius $R$ with a stationary steering wheel position and constant speed $v$. This movement is called a steady repetition.

Let the steering (front) wheels of the automatic vehicle be turned at some constant angle $\varphi$ relative to the “straight” position (figure 5). The center of gravity of the automaton (point $C$) moves along a circular arc of radius $R$ with centripetal acceleration

$$|\vec{w}_C| = v^2/R.$$

This centripetal acceleration is reported by the equal lateral forces applied to the wheels of the automaton (in figure 5 only the components $\vec{P}_A, \vec{P}_B, \vec{P}_D$ and $\vec{P}_E$ of these forces, the variable of the axis of the automaton, are shown; their continuous components are insignificant in the case under consideration). If the turning radius $R$ is large compared to the length of the automaton, then we can accept

$$\vec{P}_A = \vec{P}_B$$

However, the total force is $\vec{P}_D = \vec{P}_E$. However, the total force $\vec{P}_1 = 2\vec{P}_A$ applied to the front axle and the total force $\vec{P}_2 = 2\vec{P}_D$ applied to the rear axle are not equal. Their eligibility

For a passenger car with identical front and rear wheels and with the center of gravity located approximately in the middle ($a \approx b$), the steering is close to neutral and the critical speed, if it exists, is quite high. If, however, one of the rear tires “deflates” in such a car, then the drag resistance of the rear axle will be determined, roughly speaking, by only one of the two wheels and therefore will decrease by approximately half. It is easy to check using formula (7) that in this case the critical speed may turn out to be quite low, on the order of $20 \div 30$ km/h. (In the case of $a = b$, $k_2 = \frac{1}{2} k_1$ and $k_1 = \frac{5}{4} gm$ the formula for $v_{\kappa}$ takes the form $v_{\kappa} = \sqrt{\frac{5}{2}} gL$.) If, before the rear tires were deflated, the car had a speed of $60 \div 70$ km/h, then after the unexpected tire deflation it finds itself beyond the critical speed. The situation when moving at “supercritical” speed is very interesting. From formula (6) it is clear that at $v > v_{\kappa}$ the denominator is negative. This means that “reversibility of control” occurs. That is, if the steering wheel is turned “to the left”, then the automile will end “to the right”, and vice versa. It is true that a sharp drop in pressure in one of the rear tires leads in many cases to accidents, since the driver cannot quickly “change lanes” and loses control. In special tests, when the driver is prepared, he controls the car and can move at a speed greater than the critical one. But when tires go flat unexpectedly, an accident is almost inevitable.

There is an opinion that the most dangerous thing is that the front tires rupture. This, however, is not entirely true. When the front tire starts up quickly, the force on the steering wheel increases sharply. But if you manage to “hold” the steering wheel (this does not require very much effort, it’s just a matter of surprise), then nothing dangerous will happen.
