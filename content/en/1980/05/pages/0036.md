How to come up with a combinatorial identity

In various problems, and even in the problem book «Kvant», identities containing biominal coefficients $C_k^i$ are often encountered. Quite often it is possible to prove them by the method of mathematical induction, sometimes by various cunning transformations, but these proofs, as a rule, leave a feeling of dissatisfaction: it is completely unclear from them how this identity was invented, how new identities were found in general.

Here we will indicate several prims that allow you to come up with such identities. In particular, we will explain how we came up with problem M470; Dagestan schoolboy A. Gabibov asked about this in a letter to «Kvant».

Let's imagine that we need to find $\sum_{k=1}^{n} kx^{k-1}$. Let's try to use where we came across the expression $kx^{k-1}$. Of course, in the formula $(x^k)' = kx^{k-1}$. Our contract “kills” the task; really,

$$\left( \sum_{k=1}^{n} x^k \right)' = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (x^k)' = \sum_{k=1}^{n} kx^{k-1}.$$

On the other side,

$$\left( \sum_{k=1}^{n} x^k \right)' = \left( \frac{x^{n+1}-x}{x-1} \right)' =$$

$$= \frac{((n+1)x^n-1)(x-1)-(x^{n+1}-x)}{(x-1)^2} =$$

$$= \frac{1}{(1-x)^2} + \frac{nx-n-1}{(1-x)^2} x^n.$$

(In this calculation we naturally assumed that $x \neq 1$.) So,

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} kx^{k-1} = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2} + \frac{nx-n-1}{(1-x)^2} x^n. \quad (1)$$

Now let's try to find

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} k(k-1)x^{k-2}. \quad (\text{Особенности человекого характера побуждают повторить процедуру, которая помогла нам ранее в анализной ситуации...})$$

(D. Poya “Mathematical discovery”, M., “Nauka”, 1970, p. 88). So let's repeat it!

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} k(k-1)x^{k-2} =$$

$$= \sum_{k=1}^{n} (kx^{k-1})' = \left( \sum_{k=1}^{n} kx^{k-1} \right)' =$$

$$= \left( \frac{1}{(1-x)^2} + \frac{nx-n-1}{(1-x)^2} x^n \right)' = \frac{2}{(1-x)^3} +$$

$$+ \frac{n(1-x)^2+2(1-x)(nx-n-1)}{(1-x)^4} x^n +$$

$$+ \frac{n(nx-n-1)}{(1-x)^2} x^{n-1}. \quad (2)$$

Equalities (1) and (2) are cumbersome. Based on them, we can obtain (at $|x| < 1$) a more beautiful formula

$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} C_{k+n}^n x^k = \frac{1}{(1-x)^{n+1}},$$

but its conclusion goes beyond the school curriculum.

Let us now talk about one technique that allows us to obtain “new” identities from “old” ones. Good extraction distance Newton's formula

$$(1+x)^n = \sum_{i=0}^{n} C_i^n x^i.$$

Let us differentiate this equality. We get

$$n(1+x)^{n-1} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} iC_i^n x^{i-1}. \quad (3)$$

Putting $x=1$ and $x=-1$ in equality (3), we find.
