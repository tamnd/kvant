The following abbreviations are used for them: ∀ and ∃. To illustrate the use of these symbols, consider the sentence “He who hesitates is lost.”* In pedagogical mathematical language, this can be said as follows: “For all X, if X hesitates, then X is lost.” Using K(X) and Π(X) as shorthand for “X wobbles” and “X disappears,” we can write (∀ X) (K(X) ⇒ Π(X)).

If we doubt this statement, that is, if we are inclined to believe that it is entirely possible to oscillate without subsequent destruction, we can express our skepticism in the form (∃ X) (K(X) & (∏ Π(X)).

And finally, Frege and many quantifiers
An important part of Aristotelian contractions is the use of additional symbols like the above symbol X. Such symbols play a role of pronouns in the language of logic. Recall that in the example above, X took the place of the word “he.” Symbols used in this way are called individual variables. The next historical figure worthy of mention is the man who first decided to use them. His name is Frege, and he also lived in the 19th century. Today we appreciate him for what he emphasized: one variable, that is, one pronoun, is a poor tool for most scientific and mathematical purposes. For example, if we want to express a very modest statement that there are more than two numbers, that is, that there are at least three different numbers, we can do it like this:

(∀ X) (∃ X') (∃ X'') (∃ X''') (∃ X'''')
(X = (X' × X') + ((X'' × X''') +
+ ((X'''' × X''''') + (X''''' × X'''''')))).
