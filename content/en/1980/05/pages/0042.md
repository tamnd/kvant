Problem M567 allows for a substitutional generalization. Let α and β be any positive numbers related by the relation 1/α + 1/β = 1.

Let us mark on the number line free numbers of the form iα and jβ (i ∈ Z, j ∈ Z). Then each segment [k; k+1] axis (k ∈ Z), neither of whose ends contains the offset number, contains exactly one of the offset numbers iα, jβ.

Our problem is equivalent to this fact for rational α and β: we need to take α = n/p, β = n/q (the role of the segment [0; 1] will now be played by the segment [0; n]). The same fact (for inrational α and β) was mentioned recently in the solution to problem M538 («Kvant», 1979, no. 11), very similar to our second solution to M567.

N. Vasiliev

M569. Several numbers are now sent to. It is allowed to assign to these numbers a number equal to the arithmetic mean of the length or several of them, if it is different from all the numbers already written. Prove that, starting with two numbers 0 and 1, using the described operations you can get
a) number 1/5;
b) any rational number between 0 and 1.

$$\frac{1}{5} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{3}{32} + \frac{1}{32}}{5}$$

Let's start by representing the number 1/5 and 1/q in general. The numbers 1/2, 1/4, 3/4 are very easy to obtain:

$$\frac{1}{2} = \frac{0+1}{2}, \frac{1}{4} = \frac{0+\frac{1}{2}}{2}, \frac{3}{4} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}+1}{2}.$$

It is also possible, very indo, to obtain any double double-rational fraction, that is, a fraction of the form p/2^m, p<2^m (Fig. 1).

To get the fraction 1/q, it is enough to represent 1 as the sum of q different proper double-rational fractions: their arithmetic mean will be exactly 1/q. Let us prove by induction that this is possible. 1 can be represented as the sum of two such fractions (for example, 1=3/4+1/4).

From representation 1 as a sum of n-1 different double-rational fractions, we can obtain its representation as a sum of n such fractions:

$$1 = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_{n-1} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{a_1}{2} + \frac{a_2}{2} + \dots + \frac{a_{n-1}}{2}.$$

In particular, following this construction, we obtain

$$1 = \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{8} =$$
$$= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{16} + \frac{1}{16} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{3}{32} + \frac{1}{32},$$

which gives the representation of the number 1/5.

Geler can go about proving the second statement of the problem in many different ways. Let's give two of them.

1. Let us prove that if a natural number p can be represented as a sum of n-1 different proper double-rational fractions:

$$p = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_{n-1} \quad (0 < a_{n-1} < \dots < a_2 < a_1 < 1),$$

then it can be represented as a sum of n such fractions. Let 2^m be the lowest of the values ​​of the terms a_i. Replacing a_n-1 with the sum $\left(a_{n-1} - \frac{1}{2^m+2}\right) + \frac{1}{2^m+2}$, we get the desired representation. So, each of the fractions $\frac{1}{q}, \frac{2}{q}, \dots, \frac{q-1}{q}$ can be represented as the arithmetic mean of various proper double-rational fractions, then the fractions $\frac{1}{q+1}, \frac{2}{q+1}, \dots, \frac{q-1}{q+1}$ can be represented in a similar form. It can be obtained from the representation of the number $\frac{1}{q+1}$ by replacing all fractions a_i with 1—a_i.
