It turns out that during normal automatic operation, the slip angles are in the linear portion of the characteristic |P| (δ). Further, in such “extreme” operating modes of the tire, such as automatic turning “with a letter”, the slip angle is still slightly less than the “critical” value δ = 5°.

When driving on a good, level road, the values ​​of the slip angles change all the time, since the driver constantly “corrects” the steering wheel automatically, but at the same time they remain very small (δ≈0.5÷1°).

The linear section of the slip characteristic, described by formula (1), is determined, as numerous studies have shown, by the lateral elasticity of the tire. For a very rigid wheel (for example, the wheel of a children's bicycle or stroller with a solid rubber tire), the critical slip angle is very small - about 0.5÷1°.

From here it is clear that at the same slip angle, and therefore, at the same deformations, the stiffer the tire, the greater the lateral force.

Determining the dependence of the value of k - the resistance to lateral slip of the tire - on the design parameters of the tire (air pressure in the tire, its geometric dimensions, rubber hardness, etc.) is a very difficult task. In addition, we considered dependence (1) to be valid only in cases where the slip angle is constant over time or changes relatively slowly.

In cases where it is necessary to resolve rapidly changing slip angles, the dependence turns out to be more complex. This dependence was studied by Academician M.V. Keldysh when studying the rapid vibrations of a wheel with a spring tire relative to a vertical axis. Oscillations of this type are called “shimmies” (after the name of the American dance). Such vibrations in many cases led to damage to automatic steering controls and aircraft landing gear. The theory of M. V. Keldysh makes it possible to create special devices, devices for these vibrations.

For studying the stability and controllability of the automation as a whole, dependence (1) is sufficient.
