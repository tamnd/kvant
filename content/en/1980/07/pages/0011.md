Existence can be proven by actually constructing a regular polyhedron in coordinates. Let us place the origin of coordinates at the center of the sphere described around the future polyhedron. Let us assume, for simplicity, that the vertex $A$ lies on the positive semi-axis of abscissa, that is, has coordinates $(R; 0; 0)$, and the neighboring vertices $B_1, \dots, B_k$ have the abscissa $x = R \cos \alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle at which the edge is visible from the center of the circumscribed sphere. Since the polygon $B_1 \dots B_k$ is regular and is located in a plane perpendicular to $(OA)$, we can assume that the point $B_l$ has coordinates $(R \cos \alpha; r \cos \frac{2\pi l}{k}; r \sin \frac{2\pi l}{k})$; $l = 1, \dots, k$, where $r$ is the radius of the circle circumscribed about the polygon $B_1 \dots B_k$. In the case, for example, searches this gives the vertices $A, B_1, \dots, B_5$. The remaining vertices are symmetrical to this relative to the origin (Fig. 9). The simplest expressions for coordinates are obtained at $R = \sqrt{5}$ (then $R \cos \alpha = 1, r = 2$): $A = (\sqrt{5}; 0; 0)$; $B_5 = (1; 2; 0)$; $B_1 = \left(1; \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2}; \sqrt{\frac{5 + \sqrt{5}}{2}}\right)$; $B_2 = \left(1; -\frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}; \sqrt{\frac{5 + \sqrt{5}}{2}}\right)$.

 the coordinates of the remaining points are obtained by changing the signs. To check that the real one has turned out to be a correct icoastra, it is enough to make sure that all the edges are constructive; this is easy to do using the formula for the distance between points in coordinates.
