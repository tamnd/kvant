figure 6, in the minimum image of objects we see point $B''$ further than point $A''$. This is exactly how the points $A$ and $B$ were mutually located when obtaining the headgram. In the actual image, point $B''$ is located closer to us than point $A'$. Thus, the actual image of an object obtained using a head frame is distorted: the bulges of the place are perceived as wavy and vice versa. This distortion is called pseudoscopicity.

The scheme for obtaining the headgram we considered was proposed and first implemented by Gabor himself. This scheme has significant disadvantages. When viewed from the front, the real and minimal images of the object appear to be on the same straight line and create mutual interference.

To separate real and minimal images, American scientists E. Leith and J. Yu. Upatnikes proposed in 1961, and in 1963 implemented, a two-beam headlighting scheme. According to this scheme, the laser beam is divided into two beams - the reference and the signal (figure 7, a). The signal beam is directed at an object, reflected from it and hits the photographic plastic, where it interacts with the reference beam. As can be seen from figure 7, 6, the real image of an object does not prevent the observer from considering the minimal image of this object.

Fig. 7. P - object, L - laser, Z - mirror, S.P. - signal beam, O.P. - reference beam; G - head frame, D.I. - real image, M.I. - minimal image.
