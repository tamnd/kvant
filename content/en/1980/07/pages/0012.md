V. Orlov

Holography

Who, the waves, stopped you,
Who bound you without mochi,
Who is silent and dreary in the pond
Have you turned the militia flow?

Blow up, winds, blow up the waters,
Destroy the zipper stronghold!
Where are you, rose - the symbol of freedom?
Promise over unwavering waters.

A. S. Pushkin

A. S. Pushkin, writing in 1823 the lines that we included in the epigraph, certainly did not think about holography. But modern physicists could answer the questions posed in the poem as follows. The English physicist Denis Gabor “stopped” the waves and “fettered” their “mighty run” in 1947, proposing a method for recording and restoring the wave front. “Silent and dreary pond” is a hologram. It is enough to look at the appearance of the hologram (figure 1) to make sure that Pushkin’s words are correct; it seems impossible to understand the “dense” interweaving of the complex interference pattern on the hologram. “The thunderstorm is a symbol of freedom” is a laser beam that destroys the “disastrous stronghold” and restores the image hidden in the hologram.

Gabor received the Nobel Prize for his discovery in 1971.

What is holography?

Holography is a method of recording and restoring images. But the same can be said about photography. How does holography differ from photography?

When photographing an object of registration, essentially, the distribution in the plane of the photoplastic of the amplitudes of light waves reflected by the object—after all, the blackness of the plate is determined by the intensity of the light, and the intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude of the light wave.

When holographing on a plate, the distribution in the plane of the plate of amplitudes, phases and frequencies of vibrations in light waves reflected by an object is recorded.

To obtain the source wave pattern in the plane of the plate, Gabor proposed using a wave pattern that is constant in time. To calculate the amplitude, the easiest way is to use the vector diagram method (see “Physics 10”, p. 51).

When two oscillations with the same frequency are added, $\omega$ and the initial physical oscillations are different, but at each point the amplitude remains unchanged in time. To calculate the amplitude, the easiest way is to use the vector diagram method:

$$X_p^2 = X_1^2 + X_2^2 + 2X_1X_2 \cos(\omega t + \varphi_1) + X_2 \cos(\omega t + \varphi_2) = X_p \cos(\omega t + \varphi)$$

(see figure 2). The amplitude of $X_p$ and the physical-resulting oscillation can be found from the vector diagram:

$$X_p^2 = X_1^2 + X_2^2 + 2X_1X_2 \cos(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)$$

tg $\varphi = \frac{X_1 \sin \varphi_1 + X_2 \sin \varphi_2}{X_1 \cos \varphi_1 + X_2 \cos \varphi_2}$.
