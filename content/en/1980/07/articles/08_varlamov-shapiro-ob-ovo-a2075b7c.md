Let us now consider the process of colliding two eggs. We will assume that the eggs are exactly the same - both in size and shape, and their shells are “designed” for the same maximum load $T$. The eggs collide with different ends - one blunt, the other sharp.

According to Newton's 111th law, the forces acting during the collision from one egg to another are equal in absolute magnitude and directed in opposite directions. Let us imagine the forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ as the sum of forces $\vec{f}_1, \vec{f}_1'$ and $\vec{f}_2, \vec{f}_2'$, respectively directed along tangents to the contacting surfaces of the eggs. The absolute magnitude of the force $\vec{f}$ depends on the angle between the tangents: $f = \frac{F}{2} \cos \frac{\alpha}{2}$. Thus, the greater the angle between the tangents, the greater the “tearing” forces generated during the collision. So, in fact, it is more advantageous to level by holding the egg with the sharp end towards the enemy. There is another reason for this strategy: in the egg there is an “air sac” at the blunt end, and because of this the blunt end is less durable (try to explain this fact).

One of my acquaintances, Vasya, a sixth-grader, explained the need for salting water like this: “If you pour salt into a pan with an egg by the spoonful, then if there is enough salt dissolved in the water, the egg will float, since the density of the salted water becomes greater than the density of the egg. And the egg will not hit the bottom.”

Kolya, a member of the circle of young physicists, offered his explanation: “The presence of salt in water leads to an increase in its thermal conductivity, and this contributes to more efficient boiling and uniform heating of the egg.”

A keen biological scientist, Valya attributed the influence of salt to a completely different area of ​​the phenomenon: “The presence of salt in water leads to better protein coagulation. Therefore, if the egg cracks, a plug of coagulated protein will immediately form in the salt water, which will plug the crack, and the egg will not leak out.”

...So far we have seen how the laws of mechanics, hydrodynamics and molecular physics are manifested in the properties of a chicken egg. What electronic phenomena can be observed using an egg?

An egg is a dielectric. It was this property of eggshells that Michael Faraday used to demonstrate the phenomenon of electrification.

Take a raw egg and poke two holes in it with a needle. By blowing into one of them, you can pour out the entire contents of the egg, and you will be left with an entire shell empty in your hands. Now bring to it an electrified ebonite stick or an ordinary plastic comb that you just combed your hair with. Now, wherever you move the stick or comb, the shell, like a dog on a leash, will relentlessly follow them. Think why?

...Is it possible to find out whether it is fresh or not without breaking an egg? Can. Place a raw egg in water. If an egg sinks, it is fresh; if it floats, it is spoiled. The fact is that in a stale egg, the protein and yolk decompose. These processes are accompanied by the release of hydrogen sulfide, which “evaporates” from the egg through the smallest pores in the shell. As the egg progresses, the density of the egg decreases.

But you don’t come to the store with your own saucepan to check whether the eggs are fresh or not?! You can do it simpler: look at the egg “up to the light”; If it is translucent, it means it’s fresh; if it’s dark, it means it’s stale. Hydrogen sulfide released in a spoiled egg reduces its pronunciation.

In conclusion, try the following trick experiment. Take two egg cups and place them close together. Place an egg boiled to taste in one of them. Blow strongly into the gap between the egg and the wall of the rim. The egg will jump out of the hole and jump into the next one. Try to explain this trick.
