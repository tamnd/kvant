A. S. Pushkin, writing in 1823 the lines that we included in the epigraph, certainly did not think about holography. But modern physicists could answer the questions posed in the poem as follows. The English physicist Denis Gabor “stopped” the waves and “fettered” their “mighty run” in 1947, proposing a method for recording and restoring the wave front. “Silent and dreary pond” is a hologram. It is enough to look at the appearance of the hologram (figure 1) to make sure that Pushkin’s words are correct; it seems impossible to understand the “dense” interweaving of the complex interference pattern on the hologram. “The thunderstorm is a symbol of freedom” is a laser beam that destroys the “disastrous stronghold” and restores the image hidden in the hologram.

Gabor received the Nobel Prize for his discovery in 1971.

What is holography?

Holography is a method of recording and restoring images. But the same can be said about photography. How does holography differ from photography?

When photographing an object of registration, essentially, the distribution in the plane of the photoplastic of the amplitudes of light waves reflected by the object—after all, the blackness of the plate is determined by the intensity of the light, and the intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude of the light wave.

When holographing on a plate, the distribution in the plane of the plate of amplitudes, phases and frequencies of vibrations in light waves reflected by an object is recorded.

To obtain the source wave pattern in the plane of the plate, Gabor proposed using a wave pattern that is constant in time. To calculate the amplitude, the easiest way is to use the vector diagram method (see “Physics 10”, p. 51).

When two oscillations with the same frequency are added, $\omega$ and the initial physical oscillations are different, but at each point the amplitude remains unchanged in time. To calculate the amplitude, the easiest way is to use the vector diagram method:

$$X_p^2 = X_1^2 + X_2^2 + 2X_1X_2 \cos(\omega t + \varphi_1) + X_2 \cos(\omega t + \varphi_2) = X_p \cos(\omega t + \varphi)$$

Interference analysis allowed Gabor to prove that the amplitude and phase of a monochromatic wave scattered by an object can be detected photographically if this wave integrates with a reference monochromatic wave of the same particle. A photographic plate with an interference pattern recorded on it is called a hologram. Literally translated from Greek, this means “full recording” - a recording of the spatial structure of the wave reflected by an object.
A light wave scattered by any object can be considered as a set of secondary waves, the sources of which are individual points of this object. Consequently, in order to form a hologram of a complex object, it is necessary to understand in detail how a process hologram is obtained - a hologram of a point.

Let's consider the process of restoring the image of a point using a zone plate. Let us illuminate the plate with the same plane wave (the same length) as the reference one, with the help of which the geography of the point was obtained. In this case, we will position the plate so that it is parallel to the front of the plane wave (figure 5).

Any small section of the zone plate can be considered as a diffraction grating. Select a narrow strip on the plate. It is a lattice, the period of which is not constant - it is equal to $d_k = \Delta r_k = r_{k+1} - r_k$ and, as can be seen from expression (*), decreases with distance from the center of the plate to the periphery. Let's consider a small section of the lattice. Since $r_k$ changes slowly with increasing $k$, we can assume that in a small section of the grating its period is constant and equal to approximately constant value $d = \Delta r_k$ - the distance between two adjacent light stripes (that is, the radial distance between two adjacent rings).

Each point in the grating slit on which a light wave falls is a source of secondary light waves that propagate in all directions. For example, propagating along which secondary light waves from the section of the grating we have chosen strengthen each other, the conditions $d \sin \alpha_k = \pm n \lambda$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$) (**) are determined

(See Physics 10, p. 183). Maximum energy of arrival at light beams of zero ($n=0$) and first ($n=1$) orders. Consequently, the most successful secondary light waves occur in directions determined by the angles

$$\alpha_0 = 0, \sin \alpha_k = \pm \frac{\lambda}{d} = \pm \frac{\lambda}{\Delta r_k}.$$

The value $\Delta r_k$ will find, use (*):

$$\Delta r_k = r_{k+1} - r_k = \frac{r_{k+1}^2 - r_k^2}{r_{k+1} + r_k} \approx \frac{2a \lambda}{2r_k} = \frac{a \lambda}{r_k}.$$

Thus,

$$\sin \alpha_k = \pm \frac{r_k}{a}.$$

As can be seen from the expression for $\sin \alpha_k$, as $k$ increases, the value of $\alpha_k$ increases.

In other words, the further away from the center of the plate, the greater the angle at which rays from first-order beams propagate. As a result, all rays deflected by angles $\sin \alpha_k = +\frac{r_k}{a}$ intersect at one point $S'$, creating a real image of point $S$; rays deflected by angles $\sin \alpha_k = -\frac{r_k}{a}$ intersect at their extensions at point $S''$, creating a minimal image of point $S$. In this case, points $S'$ and $S''$ are located at a distance $a$ from the plate.

Let's prove it. As can be seen from figure 5, the distance $x$ from the zone plate to the point $S'$ and $S''$ is

$$x = r_k \text{ ctg } \alpha_k = \frac{r_k \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 \alpha_k}}{\sin \alpha_k} = \frac{r_k \sqrt{1 - r_k^2 / a^2}}{r_k / a} \approx a.$$

Thus, when the headframe of point $S$ is illuminated by a reference plane wave, we obtain two images of point $S$: the real image $S'$ and the minimal image $S''$, located on opposite sides of the plate at a distance $a$ from the plate.

The ability of zone plates to focus waves, creating a real image of an object, was well known back in the 19th century. Their ability to create a minimal image was appreciated only in golovrafini, since it is the diverging rays of light that restore the undistorted image of the object, being an exact copy of the light wave signals.

Holography with recording in a three-dimensional environment

In 1962, Soviet physicist Yu. N. Denisok proposed recording an interference pattern on photographic plastic with a thick emulsion layer. The thickness of the emulsion layer can be on the order of 20 wavelengths of light.

At figure 3 straight-thick PQRT is a section of thick-layer emulsion (section by plane figure). If the emulsion layer is thick enough, the reference and signal waves interfere with the inner layer. In this case, in the places of interference maxima, the maximum amount of middle is released. The set of maxima points on figure 3 (red curves) are parabolas*). So in space (in the volume of the emulsion) the points of interference maxima form parabolids. In other words, in the emulsion layer a system of coaxial semi-opening parabolic mirrors with a common focus - point S - is formed.

A holograph obtained on a plate with a thick-layer emulsion is called volumetric. To obtain an image of point S using a volumetric hologram, it is necessary to illuminate the hologram with a plane light wave, the front of which is parallel to the plane of the platinum. In this case, the length of the plane wave does not necessarily have to be equal to the length of the reference wave. This state is the main advantage of a volumetric hologram and it leads to remarkable consequences. As you can see, with the help of a volumetric glogram, either a real or a minimal image of an object is created separately, and not both together, as is the case in flat glograms. The actual image is pseudoscopic, so they usually work with a minimal image.

In principle, to restore the wave front, it is sufficient to form one surface of interference maxima (one parabolic mirror) in the emulsion layer. The fact that several such surfaces are formed in a thick emulsion makes it possible to reconstruct the image using white light. In this case, the image will be obtained in the same color as the color of the reference wave. waves of exactly this color component of white light, when reflected from different mirror parabolides, will always come into focus in phase and, adding up, will intensify. The waves of all other color components reflected from the mirrors will come into focus in different phases, and there will be no effort.

Very effective volumetric glograms when obtaining color images. For this purpose, the object is illuminated with light from three lasers, the wavelengths of which $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ are selected so that you observe the full color of the object. Typically uses a combination of red, yellow and blue. Each laser creates a system of translucent mirror surfaces in its emulsion layer. When such a glogram is illuminated with light from the three lasers used to create it, the “one-color” images appear combined in space and form a color image of the object.

A color image is also obtained if such a glogram is illuminated with sunlight. In this case, a system of mirrors, highlighting and reflecting components of sunlight with wavelengths $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$, creates a color image of an object in a certain place in space.

For the creation of the theory of volumetric glograms, Yu. N. Denisok was awarded the Lenin Prize in 1970.

Properties and features of glograms

Displaying images reconstructed using glograms will show the imagination of any audience. The image of the present is “real” that you want to touch it to make sure that it is only an image and not the object itself. The image can be viewed from different angles; You can refocus your eye, examine close and far parts of an object, and observe the play of light reflections on an object. Figures 9 and 10 show photographs of the glogram image taken from various observation points.

A very important property of glograms is the ability to restore the entire image using individual sections of the glograms and the low sensitivity of glograms to dust particles, scratches and minor defects in the registration of the medium. These properties are due to the fact that each point of the plate during exposure is irradiated by light scattered by all points of the object, and the light reflected by each point of the object illuminates the entire plate. These properties of glograms can be easily checked using a homemade zone plate: the image obtained with the plate retains parts of the plate when exposed.

The substitutive properties of glograms that we have listed provide the possibility of widespread use of the glographic method not only in the field of optical lengths

Conclusion

In conclusion of the article, I would like to answer the following question: why was holography - in principle a simple method of Fotoeplicity of an interference pattern formed by two coherent points - created only very recently? Indeed, interference and diffraction were well studied back in the 19th century; photography was invented in the mid-19th century; so Gabor did not need knowledge of any new achievements of science to open the holographic. However, it took a deep understanding of the phenomena of interference and diffraction and, of course, an inventive mind to combine all the known elements of holography into one bright idea. The example of the creation of holography has been well used, that in our morphologie new and valuable ideas and discoveries are born on the basis of old, well-known concepts.

In addition, it must be emphasized that along with the principles of difficulties in the development of holography, there are also purely technical ones. Further, in 1947, Gabor's idea was still premature, since sources of monochromatic corrected light were needed to produce high-quality holograms. Such sources—lasers—appeared only in 1960. To create high-quality holograms, high-resolution photographic materials are required. As we have seen, when obtaining volumetric holograms, the distances between parabolic mirrors inside the emulsion layer turn out to be on the order of the wavelength. It is this value that should determine the resolution of the emulsion. For visible light $\lambda \sim 10^{-6}$ m; this means that on one millimeter of photoplastic material $\sim 10^3$ lines should be resolved (“see” separated). Such materials have been created only in recent years. A large number of technical difficulties are currently facing the creators of holographic cinema and television. But a lot has already been done to overcome these difficulties.
