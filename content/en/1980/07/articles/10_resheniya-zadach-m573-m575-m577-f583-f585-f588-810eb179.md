M573. Through point $O$ a) on the plane; b) there are 1979 straight lines in space, no two of which are perpendicular to others. An arbitrary point $A_1$, different from $O$, is taken on the line $l_1$. Prove that you can choose on each of the remaining lines at the point $A_i \in l_i$ ($i=2, 3, \dots, 1979$) so that the following 1979 pairs of lines are mutually perpendicular:

$$(A_1 A_3) \perp l_2,$$
$$(A_2 A_4) \perp l_3, \dots,$$
$$(A_{i-1} A_{i+1}) \perp l_i, \dots,$$
$$..., (A_{1977} A_{1979}) \perp l_{1978},$$
$$(A_{1978} A_1) \perp l_{1979},$$
$$(A_{1979} A_2) \perp l_1.$$

M573, M575—M577; F583—F585, F588

On each straight line $l_i$ we choose a single vector $e_i$. Then for points $A_i \in l_i$ we can write $\overrightarrow{OA_i} = a_i \overrightarrow{e_i}$. The condition of perpendicularity of lines $A_i A_{i+2}$ and $l_{i+1}$ can be written in the form $\overrightarrow{A_i A_{i+2}} \cdot \overrightarrow{e_{i+1}} = 0$, or, since
$$\overrightarrow{A_i A_{i+2}} = \overrightarrow{OA_i} + \overrightarrow{OA_i},$$
$$(a_{i+2} \overrightarrow{e_{i+2}} - a_i \overrightarrow{e_i}) \cdot \overrightarrow{e_{i+1}} = 0,$$
that is
$$a_{i+2} \overrightarrow{e_{i+1}} \cdot \overrightarrow{e_{i+2}} = a_i \overrightarrow{e_i} \cdot \overrightarrow{e_{i+1}}.$$

Let us put $\overrightarrow{e_i} \cdot \overrightarrow{e_{i+1}} = c_i$ ($i=1, 2, \dots, 1979$; $c_i$ is the costus of the angle of the vector $\overrightarrow{e_i}$ and the next by number of the vector $\overrightarrow{e_{i+1}}$; $c_i \neq 0$). We get the formula
$$a_{i+2} = a_i \frac{c_i}{c_{i+1}},$$
(*)

which allows you to sequentially determine from the point $A_1$ the points $A_3, A_5, \dots, A_{1977}, A_{1979}, A_2, A_4, \dots, A_{1978}$, and, finally, again the point $A'_1$ on the line $l_1$, which will coincide with the point $A_1$: by virtue of the formula (*)
$$a'_1 = a_1 \frac{c_1}{c_2} \cdot \frac{c_3}{c_4} \cdot \dots \cdot \frac{c_{1979}}{c_1} \cdot \frac{c_2}{c_3} \cdot \dots \cdot \frac{c_{1978}}{c_{1979}} = a_1.$$

(Formula (*), of course, can be obtained by also considering the projections of the points $A_i A_{i+2}$ onto the line $l_{i+1}$ - these projections must coincide, since $(A_i A_{i+2}) \perp l_{i+1})$.

M575. On a line no, the points $A_0, A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ are located in order so that the lengths of the segments $A_0 A_1, A_1 A_2, \dots, A_{n-1} A_n$ do not convert to 1. It is required to mark $k-1$ of the points $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_{n-1}$ in red so that the lengths of any two of the $k$ parts into which the segment $A_0 A_n$ is divided by red dots differ by no more than 1. Prove that this can always be done a) for $k=3$;
b) for every natural $k<n$.

First, let's formulate the condition of the problem several times. We will consider all possible arrangements of $k+1$ chips $M_0, M_1, M_2, \dots, M_k$ at points $A_0, A_1, \dots, A_n$. We call a consideration correct if $M_i$ is located more wrongly than $M_{i+1}$, and the distances between $M_i$ and $M_{i+1}$ differ by no more than $1 (0 \leq i < k)$. Then the original problem becomes this: prove that there is a correct arrangement of chips $M_0, M_1, \dots, M_k$ such that $M_0$ is in $A_0, a M_k - b A_n$. It is possible, of course, that in this arrangement $M_i$ and $M_j$ hit the same point, but then, obviously, $|A_0 A_n| < k-1$ (think for yourself what needs to be done in this case).

Let us show how from an arbitrary correct arrangement of chips we can obtain another correct one, in which some of the chips move to the right, and $M_0$ remains in place. Let us first introduce some notation.

Let us have a placement of chips $M_0, M_1, \dots, M_k$. We will also denote by $M_i$ the point $A_j$ at which the token $M_i$ is located, and by $M'_i$ the point $A_{i+1}$ (if it exists, see figure). Specifically, $|M_i M'_i| < 1$. We will show how to rearrange some of the chips from $M_i$ to $M'_i$ while maintaining the correct placement.

Let us choose the minimum among the distances $|M_i M_{i+1}|$; let it be $|M_s M_{s+1}| = a$. Then $a \leq |M_i M_{i+1}| \leq a+1$ for all $i$ (since the arrangement is correct). If we give some of the chips to the right while maintaining these inequalities, then the new arrangement will be correct. Let's see if among the chips $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_k$ there is a chip $M_i$ for which $|M_{i-1} M'_i| \leq a+1$, that is, one such that when shifted to the right, all new distances between the chips will not exceed $a+1$. We will call all such chips left-moving. It turns out that left-moving chips are essential. Indeed, this is, for example, the chip $M_{s+1}$: $|M_s M'_{s+1}| = |M_s M_{s+1}| + |M_{s+1} M'_{s+1}| = a + |M_s M'_{s+1}| \leq a+1.$

Now let's see if among the chips $M_0, M_1, \dots, M_{k-1}$ there is one for which $|M'_i M_{i+1}| \geq a$, that is, one such that when shifted to the right, none of the distances between the chips will become less than $a$. We call such chips *right-moving*. Naturally, the $M_k$ chip will also move to the right.

Note that if $M_i$ is not left-movable, then $|M'_{i-1} M'_i| > a$ is. In fact,
$$|M'_{i-1} M'_i| = |M_{i-1} M'_i| - |M_{i-1} M'_{i-1}| > (a+1) - |M_{i-1} M'_{i-1}| \geq (a+1) - 1 = a.$$
And if the chip $M_i$ is not right-moving, then
$$|M'_i M'_{i+1}| = |M'_i M_{i+1}| + |M_{i+1} M'_{i+1}| \leq a+1.$$
Now, of all the left-moving chips, let’s choose the right one. Let it be $M_l$. Among all the right-moving chips from $M_l, M_{l+1}, \dots, M_k$ we choose the leftmost one. Let it be $M_r$ (perhaps $l=r$). Now we merge the $M_l, M_{l+1}, \dots, M_r$ chips accordingly and it is shown that the sequence $M_0, \dots, M_k$ remains correct.

$$|M_{l-1} M'_l| > |M_{l-1} M_l| \geq a \text{ и } |M_{l-1} M'_l| \leq a+1,$$
since the chip $M_l$ is left-moving.
$$|M'_l M_{r+1}| < |M_r M_{r+1}| \leq a+1 \text{ и } |M'_l M_{r+1}| \geq a,$$
since the chip $M_r$ is right-moving.
For $l \leq i < r$, according to the remarks made above regarding the properties of stationary tokens, $a < |M'_i M'_{i+1}| \leq a+1$, since the token $M_{i+1}$ is not left-movable (it stands to the right of $M_l$ - the rightmost left-movable token), and $M_i$ is not right-movable (it stands to the left of $M_r$ - the most left-handed right-movable token). Therefore, for all distances $|M_{i-1} M_i|$ the inequality $a \leq |M_{i-1} M_i| \leq a+1$, $i=1, 2, \dots, k$ remains true. It is also necessary to prove that the $M_i$ chip cannot be to the right of the $M_{l+1}$ chip, but this is understandable, since if $M_i = M_{i+1}$ are equal, then the $M_{i+1}$ chip is left-moving and, therefore, the $M_i$ chip could not move. So, we have achieved the following: from any correct sequence, by shifting some part of the chips $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_k$ to neighboring points ($M_i$ shifts in $M'_i$) to the right, a new correct sequence is obtained.

Let us note the interesting role that the $M_s$ and $M_k$ chips played in this process. They themselves may not have participated in the operation, but their presence ensured the existence of the chips $M_l$ and $M_r$ we needed.

Now completing the proof is quite simple. Let's place all the $M_0, \dots, M_k$ chips in $A_0^*$ - this sequence is very correct - and teach you how to apply the process.

The placement of chips on the points changes all the time, with each chip moving only to the right. It is clear that after a certain number of such operations the token $M_k$ will arrive at point $A_n$, which is what was required.

Other known solutions to the problem also use rupedura (speaking in terms of the above solution): first, the chips $M_0$ and $M_k$ are closed at points $A_0$ and $A_n$, respectively, and the rest are placed arbitrarily; then we move the chips $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_k-1$ and add the required ones. (This approach immediately seems the most natural, which is why the idea of ​​the solution presented is so unexpected: do not cover the $M_k$ chip.)

**M576. On a plane at a given point. For some pairs ($A; B$) of these points, vectors $AB$ are taken, and in such a way that at each point there are as many vectors beginning as there are endings at it. Prove that the sum of all chosen vectors is equal to $\vec{0}$.**

This problem can be solved in multiimplicit ways. Let's give two of them.

First decision. Let us apply induction by the number of vectors. For $n=2$ we have vectors $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{BC}$, the sum of which is $\vec{0}$. Let the statement be true for $n$ vectors. Distance system of $n+1$ vectors. We will replace it in this system

**F583. Observer of motion with constant speed along a certain inclined straight line. A body thrown at an angle to the horizon intersects the trajectory of the observer of motion with a time interval t. Both times the body is located**

In the reference frame associated with the Earth, we denote by $\vec{v}_0$ the speed of the observer, and $\vec{v}_1$ the speed of the body at that moment of time $t_0$ at which it first intersects the trajectory of the observer. In a reference frame moving uniformly with speed $\vec{v}_0$, the observer is motionless, and the body at time $t_0$ has speed $\vec{v} = \vec{v}_1 + (-\vec{v}_0) = \vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_0$. Since the frame of reference for motion relative to the Earth is uniform, the acceleration

After the second intersection, there is a change in the paths traversed by the body in successive equal time intervals of duration t. Find the relationship of these paths.

Consequently, in a reference frame moving with a constant speed $\vec{v}_0$ relative to the Earth, the body moves along a parabola.

According to the conditions of the problem, the body intersects the inclined line both times at the same distance from the observer in front of it. This means that from the point of view of the observer, the body moves in a vertical straight line, that is, like a body thrown vertically upward.

The vectors $\vec{v}_0$ and $\vec{v}_1$ are such that the vector $\vec{v}$ is vertical (see figure).

In a moving reference frame, the equation of motion of a body in projection on the vertical axis $Y$, directed upward,

$$y = y_0 + vt - \frac{gt^2}{2}.$$

At $t = t_0 = 0$ (we start counting time from the moment the body first crosses the trajectory observed) and at $t = \tau$ the values ​​of the body coordinates coincide: $y = y_0$, that is

$$y_0 = y_0 + vt - \frac{g\tau^2}{2}.$$

From here

$$v\tau - \frac{g\tau^2}{2} = 0 \Rightarrow v = \frac{g\tau}{2}.$$

Consequently, the equation of the body in a moving frame of reference has the form

$$y = y_0 + \frac{g\tau}{2}t - \frac{gt^2}{2}.$$

Let us find the ratio of the paths traversed by the body in successively equal time intervals t. For $t = n\tau$

$$y_n = y_0 + n\frac{g\tau^2}{2} - n^2\frac{g\tau^2}{2};$$

at $t = (n + 1)\tau$

$$y_{n+1} = y_0 + (n + 1)\frac{g\tau^2}{2} - (n + 1)^2\frac{g\tau^2}{2};$$

Thus,

$$\Delta y_n = y_{n+1} - y_n = ng\tau^2.$$

During the first period of time t, the change in the coordinate of the body is zero: $\Delta y_0 = y_1 - y_0 = 0$ ($n = 0$); for the second period of time t (after the second intersection of the body with the trajectory is observed) - $\Delta y_1 = g\tau^2$; $\Delta y_2 = 2g\tau^2$, etc.

Consequently, the ratio of the paths traversed by the body after the second intersection of the trajectory is observed in successively equal time intervals t is equal to

$$\Delta y_1 : \Delta y_2 : \Delta y_3 \dots = 1 : 2 : 3...$$

F588. Figure 1 (top view) is a photograph of smoke plumes coming from three locomotives moving along a straight section of railway track. The speed of the first locomotive is $|\vec{v}_1| = 50 \text{ km/ч}$, and the second one is $|\vec{v}_2| = 70 \text{ km/ч}$, the directions of their movement are indicated on figure by arrows. What is the speed of the third locomotive?

First of all, let's find out what determines the direction of the smoke plume. A cloud of smoke released by a locomotive at point $A$, after time $t$ will be blown by the wind to point $C$ such that $\vec{AC} = \vec{ut}$, where $\vec{u}$ is the wind speed (Fig. 2). The train, after time $t$, ends up at point $B$ such that $\vec{AB} = \vec{vt}$, where $\vec{v}$ is the speed of the train. It is obvious that the direction of the smoke plume goes along the vector of the difference between the vectors $\vec{AC}$ and $\vec{AB}$, or, what is the same, along the vector $d$, equal to the difference between the vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$.

Let's find the wind speed. Let's draw the vector $\vec{v}_1$ on an arbitrary scale; then from the beginning $O$ of the vector $\vec{v}_1$ we draw the vector $\vec{v}_2$ on the same scale; from the ends of the vectors $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$:
