2. Well-known and classic examples: Newtonian mechanics at ordinary earthly speeds objectively correctly reflects realizable phenomena, but at speeds comparable to the speed of light, it ceases to be applicable - it leads to a contradiction with experience. Beyond the limits of suitability of Newtonian mechanics, another one operates—the relative mechanics of Einstein’s theory. But it is extremely important to illustrate this dialectical process of transition from some models, objectively correct within certain limits, to new ones using simpler examples. I will analyze in detail one example from mechanics that I discussed with schoolchildren at a summer school in Pushino this year.

The physics course for class VIII correctly states that in many problems a sufficiently small material body can be considered a “material point” - its dimensions should be neglected. This is what a ball thrown up looks like, moving according to the law

$$z = v_0 t - \frac{gt^2}{2}.$$

Its speed varies according to the law

$$v = v_0 - gt,$$

and the acceleration is constant

$$a = -g.$$

The coordinate $z$ and speed $v$ change continuously. The textbook says that this should be the case in the general case of motion of a material point. The forces acting on a body are always finite. Therefore, the acceleration is finite, which already implies the continuity of the time dependence of the velocity and coordinates $z$. But in practice, the ball, when it hits the floor, immediately changes direction and bounces up. Since the ball is not perfectly elastic, during the second jump its initial speed will be

$$v_1 = kv_0,$$

where the coefficient $k$ is less than one. Assuming that the coefficient $k$ is constant for successive jumps, we can calculate the weight of the further course of the process. Schoolchildren were asked to do calculations and draw graphs of the dependence of $z, v$ and $a$ on time for various coefficients $k$. They coped with this task perfectly, although the results surprised them and caused a lot of controversy.

It turned out that successive moments of impact on the floor $t_n$ when calculating $n$ will converge to the final limit $T$: over the time interval [0; $T$] there is an endless sequence of jumps of ever decreasing height.

Of course, this model is idealized; for jumps to a height of the same order as the diameter of the ball, it is not applicable. But the model is quite realistic. Quickly catching the moment of hitting the floor and stopping the ball completely after time $T$ is observed quite simply “by ear”. figure shows schematic graphs of $z$ and $v$ versus time $t$.

At the moment of removal $t_n$, the speed changes abruptly from the adjective value $-v_{n-1}$ to the value $v_n = kv_{n-1}$. Acceleration is equal to $-g$ all the time, except for moments of daring $t_n$. At these moments, the ball receives instantaneous impulses of finite magnitude.
