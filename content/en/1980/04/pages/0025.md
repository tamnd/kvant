Optical experiments with the eye

Experimental study

After conducting model experiments and their careful analysis (see «Kvant» No. 3, p. 19), you can attend the experiments directly with the eye.

1. Light a table lamp with a frosted shade (or some other extended and rather sharply defined light source) and place it about a meter away from you. Place a small flat mirror-label in front of your eye so that it does not block the direct rays falling from the lamp onto the eye. In the mirror you will see an image of a lamp on the pupil of the eye.

It is clear that this image is created by rays reflected from the eye. But why is there only one? After all, modeling experiments have shown that there should be even images!

Let's compare the rages of all possible images. To do this, we substitute specific values ​​of the refractive indices of the cornea, aqueous humor, lens and vitreous body into the formula for the light reflection coefficient $k$. We find that 2.5% is reflected from the front surface of the cornea, 0.02% is reflected from the back surface of the cornea, and 0.03% of the light flux incident on the eye is reflected from the front and back surfaces of the lens. This means that the brightness of the first image is approximately one hundred times greater than the brightness of the remaining images. That is why in the experiment there is only one image - obtained from the anterior surface of the light from the anterior surface of the cornea.

Place a ruler close to the eye (as shown in figure 1) and, together with the ruler and mirror, move it closer to the lamp (or away from it) until the width $l_1$ of the lamp image becomes equal to, for example, 1 mm. Measure the distance $d$ between the eye and the lamp and the transverse dimension $l$ of the lamp. Using the formula for object magnification $\Gamma = \frac{l_1}{l} = \frac{R}{2d}$, calculate the radius of the cornea $R$ of the anterior surface of the cornea.

In one of our experiments, the length changes were as follows: $l = 120$ mm, $l_1 = 1$ mm, $d = 450$ mm; hence the radius of the kivizna

$$R = \frac{2dl_1}{l} = 7,5$$ mm.

In a darkened room, at a distance of about a polymeter from you, sit your partner and ask him, without looking up, to look, for example with his left eye, at some object located at a distance of several meters behind you. To the side of his eye, at a distance of 10-20 cm, place a light bulb for a flashlight and light it. With your right eye, you will immediately see a small, rather bright image of a light bulb on the pupil of your partner’s eye.
