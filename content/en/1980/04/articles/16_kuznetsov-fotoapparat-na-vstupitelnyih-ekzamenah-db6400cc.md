Camera for entrance exams

The basic diagram of the Foto camera can be represented as shown in figure 1.

As you know, a Foto camera is a light-transmitting camera (very often a Foto camera is called a camera). At the front of the camera is the O object. The device's objects are quite complex: it often contains up to twenty different lenses. However, for solving large problems, the object can be considered a thin converging lens. This is what we will do in this article.

A shutter is used to open and close an object. The time during which objects remain open is called experiment time, or simply experiment. The duration of the experiment is carried out using the signal of the signal object and the sensitivity of the photographic film.

The flow of light energy passing through the object is controlled by the aperture D. By changing the diameter of the aperture, a more or less significant part of the object can be exposed. It turns out that this changes not only the illumination of the image, but also the depth of field of the image of space (we will talk about this in more detail a little later).

At the back of the camera there is a photographic film (or photographic plastic) FP, on which the image h of the photographed object H is obtained (see Fig. 1). In order for the image to appear exactly in the plane of the photographic film, most cameras are equipped with the ability to move the object within certain limits, along its optical axis. This is done by focusing.

Let's look at several specific tasks related to the Foto camera and offered to applicants during the entrance exams.

Problem 1. A point object was formed twice on the same kadar at two positions of the Foto camera. In the second position, the apparatus was moved h=10 cm relative to the first position. The distance between two images of the object on the film turned out to be equal to p=2 mm. Determine the distance to the object if the focal length of the Foto camera object is F=5 cm.

Let the object S and its first image S₁ be located as shown in figure 2. Let us find the position of the object image in the second case.

Since the Foto camera was shifted perpendicular to the main optical axis of the object, the second image S₂, just like the image S₁, should lie in the plane of the photographic film. Let's draw a secondary optical axis, passing through the object and the optical center of the object in the second position of the Foto camera. At the point of intersection of this axis and the film plane the image S₂ is located.

As can be seen from figure 2,

$$\frac{h}{p} = \frac{d}{f},$$

where d is the required distance from the object.

Problem 2. When photographing an object from a distance of $d_1 = 15$ m, the height of its image on the film turned out to be equal to $h_1 = 30$ mm, and when photographing from a distance of $d_2 = 9$ m, it was $h_2 = 51$ mm. Find the focal length of the camera object.

By definition, linear magnification of an object

$$\Gamma = \frac{h}{H},$$

where $h$ is the linear size of the image, $H$ is the linear size of the object.

On the other hand (this follows, for example, from figure 1),

$$\Gamma = \frac{f}{d},$$

where $f$ is the distance from the image to the object, $d$ is the distance from the object to the object.

Thus,

$$\frac{h}{H} = \frac{f}{d},$$

or, taking into account the lens formula

$$\frac{1}{d} + \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{F} \quad (F \text{ — фокусное расстояние}),$$

$$\frac{h}{H} = \frac{F}{d-F}.$$

Let us write down the last correspondence for the two cases specified in the conditions of the problem:

$$\frac{h_1}{H} = \frac{F}{d_1-F} \quad \text{и} \quad \frac{h_2}{H} = \frac{F}{d_2-F}.$$

From here we get

$$F = \frac{d_2h_2-d_1h_1}{h_2-h_1} \approx 0,43 \text{ м} = 43 \text{ см}.$$

Task 3. For azrophotostems, a camera is used, the lens of which has a focal length of $F = 8$ cm. The minimum size of image details placed on photographic film (sculpting resolution) is $\delta = 10^{-2}$ mm. At what height should tree sheets measuring $l = 5$ cm be placed? At what speed will the independent still not be blurred if the exposure time is $\tau = 10^{-3}$ s?

First of all, let's talk a little about the resolution of photographic film. The photosensitive emulsion of photographic film consists of crystals of silver bromide suspended in a gelatin layer. These crystals are the photosensitive centers of the photolayer. If light hits the crystal, when the photographic film is developed, it decomposes into metallic silver (which has a black color) and bromine.

The crystals, although they are small, still have finite sizes. Therefore, if the images of two different points fall on the same crystal, we will get one point on the photographic film - one blackened crystal. In other words, photographic film does not resolve these two points.

There is one more feature. Silver bromide crystals not only absorb, but also scatter light, which is why nearby neighboring crystals that do not receive direct light are also detected. Because of this, the minimum area of ​​pores corresponding to the image of a luminous point always turns out to be larger than the size of one crystal.

One way or another, any film has a certain resolution.

In our case, the film will resolve two dots if the distance between their images exceeds $\delta = 10^{-2}$ mm, otherwise after development on the film you will get one dot (more precisely, one small spot).

Now let's find out what the phrase “you can see the leaves of trees in a photograph” means. Generally, the criterion of difference is a matter of agreement. Let's agree to assume that the leaves of the trees will be different on the film if the image of one leaf has the size of the minimum area of ​​the tree. (Of course, in this case we can only talk about whether there is a leaf in this place or not, and not about what kind of leaf it is.)

This means that in this problem, a sheet of $l = 5$ cm in size must be depicted on an area of ​​at least $\delta = 10^{-2}$ mm in size, otherwise two adjacent sheets will produce one red spot.

The magnification created by the lens is determined by the formula

$$\Gamma = \frac{f}{d} = \frac{\delta}{l},$$

where $d$ is the distance from the object to the object, $f$ is the distance from the object to the film.

Since the plane at the time of aerial photography is flying at altitudes no lower than several tens of meters, we can assume that $d \gg f$. then from the lens formula $\frac{1}{d} + \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{F}$ it follows that $f = F$, that is, the image is in the focal plane of the lens of the device.

Thus,

$$d = f \frac{l}{\delta} = F \frac{l}{\delta} = 400 \text{ м.}$$

Regarding the second question, the problem of contracts is this. We will consider the image different if during the exposure time the image of one sheet moves no more than the size of the minimum spot on the side:

$$|\vec{v}| \tau \frac{F}{d} \leq \delta,$$

where $\vec{v}$ is the desired speed of the aircraft.

From here

$$|\vec{v}| \leq \frac{\delta d}{\tau F} = 100 \text{ м/c} = 360 \text{ км/ч.}$$

Problem 4. The closest point at which the camera can be focused (pointed) is at a distance of $d = 2$ m from the lens. Where will this point move if a thin positive line with an optical power of $D = +5$ ditre is pressed closely to the lens?

As we have already said, focusing the camera (focusing) is carried out by mechanically moving the lens. But this movement is limited, so the device cannot be focused on points located close to a certain distance. In this case, this distance is $d = 2$ m.

Let the object be located at a distance $d_1 < d$ from the object. Obviously, the attached lens should be represented by an image of this object at a distance $d$, at which the lens is attached without an additional lens.

According to the lens formula

$$\frac{1}{d_1} - \frac{1}{d} = D$$

(the minus sign is taken because the image is minimal), from where

$$d_1 = \frac{d}{Dd+1} \approx 0,18 \text{ м.}$$

This problem can be solved by podgrut. Let us denote by $D_0$ the optical power of the object. According to the lens formula

$$D_0 = \frac{1}{d} + \frac{1}{f},$$

where $f$ is the distance from object to object. It is known that if thin lenses are located close to each other, their optical powers add up. Therefore, for an objective with a linear attachment, the lens formula can be written as follows:

$$D_0 + D = \frac{1}{d_1} + \frac{1}{f}.$$

Subtracting the previous one from this equality, we find $d_1$.

Task 5. When photographing on film, you will get an equally sharp image not only

those objects (located at a distance $d_0$) at which the camera lens is pointed, but also objects located somewhat close and somewhat far from this distance. In other words, the mode will result in objects lying inside some area $d_1 \div d_2$ ($d_1 < d_0$, $d_2 > d_0$); $d_1$ is called the near limit of the depth of field, $d_2$ is the far limit. It turned out that when pointing the camera lens at objects located at a distance of $d_0 = 10$ m, the near limit of the depth of field is equal to $d_1 = 7,8$ m. Find the far limit.

Let us assume that using the lens $DE$ images of three points lying on the optical axis of the lens at different distances from it (Fig. 3) are obtained. Let these be points $C_0$, $C_1$ and $C_2$. Let's place the film at point $C_0$. We will assume that the size of the minimum area of ​​the film film is equal to $|AB|$ (at figure it is greatly enlarged for clarity). If the diameter of the lens is such that the rays forming points $C_1$ and $C_2$ do not extend beyond the area $AB$, when photographing any of these points, this entire area will be distorted, that is, the images of these points will be equally sharp.

Obviously, the smaller the lens diameter, the easier this condition is met. This is why when you stop down your lens, your depth of field increases.

Let us now move on to quantitative calculations. From the similarity of triangles $DC_2O$ and $AC_2C_0$ it follows

$$\frac{|AC_0|}{|DO|} = \frac{|C_2C_0|}{|OC_2|}.$$

Fig. 3.

Similarly, for triangles $DC_1O$ and $AC_1C_0$

$$\frac{|AC_0|}{|DO|} = \frac{|C_1C_0|}{|OC_1|}.$$

From here we get

$$\frac{|C_2C_0|}{|OC_2|} = \frac{|C_1C_0|}{|OC_1|},$$

or

$$\frac{1}{|OC_2|} + \frac{1}{|OC_1|} = \frac{2}{|OC_0|}$$

(since $|C_2C_0| = |OC_0| - |OC_2|$ and $|C_1C_0| = |OC_1| - |OC_0|$).

According to the lens formula

$$\frac{1}{d_0} + \frac{1}{|OC_0|} = \frac{1}{F},$$

where

$$|OC_0| = \frac{Fd_0}{d_0 - F},$$

where $F$ is the focal length of the lens. Similarly we get

$$|OC_1| = \frac{Fd_1}{d_1 - F} \text{ и } |OC_2| = \frac{Fd_2}{d_2 - F}$$

Substituting the resulting expressions for $|OC_0|$, $|OC_1|$ and $|OC_2|$ in accordance with (*) and carrying out simple transformations, we obtain

$$\frac{1}{d_1} + \frac{1}{d_2} = \frac{2}{d_0}.$$

From here we find $d_2$:

$$d_2 = \frac{d_0 d_1}{2 d_1 - d_0} \approx 13,9 \text{ м.}$$
