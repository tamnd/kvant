Dialectal-materialistic worldview in school courses in mathematics and physics

Vladimir Ilyich Lenin owns the words that express the first requirement for a real scientist: “... without a solid philosophical foundation, no natural sciences, no materialism can withstand the struggle against the onslaught of bourgeois ideas and the restoration of the bourgeois worldview. In order to withstand this struggle and carry it through to the end with complete success, the natural must be a modern materialist, a conscious side of the materialism that is presented by Marx, that is, he must have a dialectical-materialist worldview. These words fully apply to young readers «Kvant», lovers of physics and mathematics: their task is not only to accumulate specific scientific facts and knowledge, but also to cultivate a dialectical-materialistic worldview. The questions included in school courses in mathematics and physics provide quite a lot of opportunities to get acquainted at an elementary level with the materialistic concept of scientific problems, with elements of dialectical thinking.

Some of these issues are discussed in the report of Academician A. N. Kolmogorov, read in 1977 during the IV captivity of the Scientific Methodological Council of the USSR Ministry of Education. Below is (with minor abbreviations) the text of this report. Although the report was not intended for schoolchildren, we are sure that it will interest them too.

Discussing the dialectical-materialist worldview in school courses in mathematics and physics, I want to emphasize the more difficult side of the matter: familiarity with the elements of dialectical thinking. Discussion of this topic is usually treated with some distrust, since often the conversation about dialectics remains very superficial.

For example, they cite the fact that, according to Engels, with the advent of variable quantities in mathematics, motion and dialectics entered mathematics. From this they conclude that the early introduction of variables in modern textbooks is an achievement in the sense of introducing dialectics into school mathematics. At the same time, they forget that a variable in these textbooks is treated simply as a letter, together with which you can put the name of any element of a certain set. The dialectic here lies deeper - behind this formal definition. Understanding it requires developing a historical approach to the matter.

I want to illustrate my idea with specific examples.

1. You all know well the differential equation of exponential growth or decrease (“Algebra and the beginnings of analysis 10”, paragraph 110)

$$f'(t) = kf(t),$$

in a short note

$$y' = ky.$$

As a first example, the textbook gives the radioactive decay of a substance: $f(t)$ denotes the amount of decaying substance that has been preserved by the time point $t$. Here the coefficient $k$ is negative. As a second example with a positive $k$ distance, the country's population grows by 2% per year. It is this second example that causes bewilderment or even sharp protest among many. How, mathematically speaking, can we differentiate a function that accepts only integer values! The perturbation is replaced by substitution after filling, which in the case of radioactive decay we are also talking about the whole number of atoms preserved at time $t$.

Meanwhile, this is a good reason to emphasize the mathematical model of the phenomenon; As a rule, this phenomenon is schematized. Therefore, she gives the correct pred-

2. Well-known and classic examples: Newtonian mechanics at ordinary earthly speeds objectively correctly reflects realizable phenomena, but at speeds comparable to the speed of light, it ceases to be applicable - it leads to a contradiction with experience. Beyond the limits of suitability of Newtonian mechanics, another one operates—the relative mechanics of Einstein’s theory. But it is extremely important to illustrate this dialectical process of transition from some models, objectively correct within certain limits, to new ones using simpler examples. I will analyze in detail one example from mechanics that I discussed with schoolchildren at a summer school in Pushino this year.

The physics course for class VIII correctly states that in many problems a sufficiently small material body can be considered a “material point” - its dimensions should be neglected. This is what a ball thrown up looks like, moving according to the law

$$z = v_0 t - \frac{gt^2}{2}.$$

Its speed varies according to the law

$$v = v_0 - gt,$$

and the acceleration is constant

$$a = -g.$$

The coordinate $z$ and speed $v$ change continuously. The textbook says that this should be the case in the general case of motion of a material point. The forces acting on a body are always finite. Therefore, the acceleration is finite, which already implies the continuity of the time dependence of the velocity and coordinates $z$. But in practice, the ball, when it hits the floor, immediately changes direction and bounces up. Since the ball is not perfectly elastic, during the second jump its initial speed will be

$$v_1 = kv_0,$$

where the coefficient $k$ is less than one. Assuming that the coefficient $k$ is constant for successive jumps, we can calculate the weight of the further course of the process. Schoolchildren were asked to do calculations and draw graphs of the dependence of $z, v$ and $a$ on time for various coefficients $k$. They coped with this task perfectly, although the results surprised them and caused a lot of controversy.

It turned out that successive moments of impact on the floor $t_n$ when calculating $n$ will converge to the final limit $T$: over the time interval [0; $T$] there is an endless sequence of jumps of ever decreasing height.

Of course, this model is idealized; for jumps to a height of the same order as the diameter of the ball, it is not applicable. But the model is quite realistic. Quickly catching the moment of hitting the floor and stopping the ball completely after time $T$ is observed quite simply “by ear”. figure shows schematic graphs of $z$ and $v$ versus time $t$.

At the moment of removal $t_n$, the speed changes abruptly from the adjective value $-v_{n-1}$ to the value $v_n = kv_{n-1}$. Acceleration is equal to $-g$ all the time, except for moments of daring $t_n$. At these moments, the ball receives instantaneous impulses of finite magnitude.

3. It is well known that the education of a scientific materialist worldview is impossible without familiarizing students with the history of science and students understanding the main stages of the development of science. It was from these messages that the concept of the simplest differential equations was introduced into the new mathematics program. Only three equations are studied in detail: uniformly accelerated motion

$$y'' = a,$$

harmonic vibrations

$$y'' = -k^2 y$$

and exponential growth or decline

$$y' = ky.$$

Let me remind you that for Newton there were two main problems of analysis: 1) finding their arbitrary functions, 2) finding these functions themselves by correspondence between functions and their arbitrary ones. The second problem is the problem of interposing differential equations. Newton considered the problem of finding antiderivatives as a simple special case.

The entire ideology of the mathematical natural from Newton to Laplace is inextricably linked with the concept of a differential equation. Laplace set out the general principle of determinism based on the fact that the basic laws of nature are expressed in the form of differential equations, and their interpretation allows one to truly imagine the future. Internalization of the corresponding differential equations, as is known, is a scale problem that can be solved only by numerical methods. But it is not difficult to give a clear idea of ​​the problem. Without a fairly specific understanding of this stage of development of the mathematical natural, it is impossible to understand further stages, the emergence of statistical physics, quantum physics.
