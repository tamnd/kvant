Optical experiments with the eye

Experimental study

After conducting model experiments and their careful analysis (see «Kvant» No. 3, p. 19), you can attend the experiments directly with the eye.

1. Light a table lamp with a frosted shade (or some other extended and rather sharply defined light source) and place it about a meter away from you. Place a small flat mirror-label in front of your eye so that it does not block the direct rays falling from the lamp onto the eye. In the mirror you will see an image of a lamp on the pupil of the eye.

It is clear that this image is created by rays reflected from the eye. But why is there only one? After all, modeling experiments have shown that there should be even images!

Let's compare the rages of all possible images. To do this, we substitute specific values ​​of the refractive indices of the cornea, aqueous humor, lens and vitreous body into the formula for the light reflection coefficient $k$. We find that 2.5% is reflected from the front surface of the cornea, 0.02% is reflected from the back surface of the cornea, and 0.03% of the light flux incident on the eye is reflected from the front and back surfaces of the lens. This means that the brightness of the first image is approximately one hundred times greater than the brightness of the remaining images. That is why in the experiment there is only one image - obtained from the anterior surface of the light from the anterior surface of the cornea.

Place a ruler close to the eye (as shown in figure 1) and, together with the ruler and mirror, move it closer to the lamp (or away from it) until the width $l_1$ of the lamp image becomes equal to, for example, 1 mm. Measure the distance $d$ between the eye and the lamp and the transverse dimension $l$ of the lamp. Using the formula for object magnification $\Gamma = \frac{l_1}{l} = \frac{R}{2d}$, calculate the radius of the cornea $R$ of the anterior surface of the cornea.

In one of our experiments, the length changes were as follows: $l = 120$ mm, $l_1 = 1$ mm, $d = 450$ mm; hence the radius of the kivizna

$$R = \frac{2dl_1}{l} = 7,5$$ mm.

2. In a darkened room, at a distance of about a polymeter from you, sit your partner and ask him, without looking up, to look, for example with his left eye, at some object located at a distance of several meters behind you. To the side of his eye, at a distance of 10-20 cm, place a light bulb for a flashlight and light it. With your right eye, you will immediately see a small, rather bright image of a light bulb on the pupil of your partner’s eye.

Move the light bulb up and down, right and left (while your partner should continuously look at the distant object, and not move their eyes after the light bulb). If you observe carefully, you will notice two more, although much weaker, images in the pupil: larger and smaller compared to the first.

Notice that when the light bulb moves, the second image moves in the same direction as the first, and the third image moves in the opposite direction.

Compare the result of this experiment with the results of model experiments. Obviously, the first - direct and brightest - image is created by the anterior surface of the cornea (as in the previous experiment). A warm image (flip) can only be created with a mirror, that is, the back surface of the lens. The second image (direct, like the first) is created either by the posterior surface of the cornea or the anterior surface of the lens. What exactly?

As we have already said, in reality there should be four images in the eyes, but in experience only three are observed. Why? It turns out that the whole point is that the cornea is very thin and its back surface has almost the same curvature as the front. Therefore, the images produced by both surfaces must be very close to each other. Since the brightness of the image created by the anterior surface of the cornea is much greater than the brightness of the image obtained from its posterior surface, it is a practical necessity to see the latter image against the background of the first. Precise changes showed that for the average eye, the thickness of the cornea is 0.5 mm, and the radii of curvature of its anterior and posterior surfaces are 7.7 mm and 6.8 mm, respectively. (Compare the first value of the radius of curvature with the one you obtained in experiment 1.)

Thus, the second image of the light bulb filament (see Fig. 2) is due to the reflection of light from the front surface of the lens. But from this and from the fact that the third image is obtained by reflecting light from the back surface of the lens, it follows that the lens really exists!

3. To carry out the following experiment, preliminary preparation is required. Place a small object at a distance of 20-30 cm from your partner’s eye (for example, a white borer disk with a diameter of about 1 cm, secured with a plate at the end of a pencil). Ask your partner to look first at a distant object, then at a close object, while you watch his eye. If the pupils of the eye and both objects are on the same straight line, when the partner’s eye accommodates, the eye itself will remain motionless. Try to achieve this, otherwise the experiment will be inconclusive.

Using rubber rings, attach two flashlight bulbs to a wooden stick (or pencil) at a distance of 5-10 cm from each other. The light bulbs will indicate the ends of the object, the image of which you will observe when the light is reflected from your partner’s eye.

Ask your partner to look at a distant object. By moving the burning light bulbs to the side of your partner's eye, get three images of a light bulb in his pupil. Analysis of the results obtained

Let's summarize. What can be proven by continuing these experiments? Firstly, there is a lens in the eye. Secondly, that the cornea of ​​the eye is very thin and the curvature of its physical features is practically the same. Thirdly, that the refractive indices of the aqueous humor and vitreous body differ very little from the refractive indices of the cornea and lens. Fourthly, that accommodation of the eyes is caused by a change in the curvature of the surface of the lens.
