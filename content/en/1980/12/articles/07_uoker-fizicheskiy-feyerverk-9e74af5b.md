Physical fireworks

The problem-questions published below are taken from the book “Physical Fireworks” by J. Walker, recently published by the Mir publishing house.

1. With a pencil in the bath
If you want a little fun while taking a bath, grab a pencil and sit behind its shadow at the bottom of the bath. When you submerge the pencil halfway in water, you will see that its shadow has nothing in common with it - it rather looks like two rods with rounded ends that are separated by a light gap (Fig. 1). Where did this light gap come from and what determines its width?

2. Image of a coin in water
Place a coin at the bottom of a wide jar filled with water,

Fig. 2.

and look at the coin through the water from different angles. At some angles you will see an image of a coin on the surface of the water (Fig. 2). Now place your hand on the back of the jar. This will most likely not affect the image of the coin, however, if your hand is wet the image will disappear. Why?

3. "Ghosts" in double-glazed windows
Why do separated objects appear to be entertaining when viewed through a double-glazed window?
In some cases, for example when watching through the window of an airport control tower, such image diversion is not only unpleasant - it can lead to very dangerous situations. Try, at least approximately, to estimate the angular distance between the true object and its “ghost”. How does it depend on the time of day and weather conditions?

4. glowing rain
You may have had occasion to observe even in direct sunlight, you will notice that above some missing horizontal line the city newspaper should be lighter than below. How can this be explained?

5. Sunglasses and Apparent Motion Distortion
Cover one eye with a dark filter, such as a glass from sunglasses, and watch the swing of a simple pendulum. Although you know for sure that the pendulum swings in one plane, you get the impression that it describes an ellipse (Fig. 3). To the uninitiated, observing this reality may seem surprising, even mysterious.

If you ever have to sit behind the wheel of an automatic while wearing single-lens sunglasses, you will find...

You may think that the speed of the automatic vehicle coming to your left is noticeably different from the speed of the automatic vehicle coming to your right, although in fact they are moving at the same speeds. You estimate the automatic speed incorrectly.

Moreover, in considering the case, you will incorrectly estimate the distances to surrounding objects. The error in judgment even depends on whether the object is to your right or left.

What explains the apparent three-dimensional movement of the pendulum? What role does the light filter or glasses play in assessing this movement, automatic speed and distance to objects?

6. Rotating fiber in front of a TV screen

If in a dark room in front of a TV screen, the image on which remains unchanged, you run a flat fiber with a pattern on the upper surface, then mind-blowing pictures will appear on the wolf. There is no doubt that they are due to the pattern on the surface of the wolf, but what does the light of the television screen have to do with it?

7. "Floating" TV images

Looking at the TV screen in a dark room, quickly move your eyes first half a meter to the left of the screen, and then immediately half a meter to the right. You will see a detailed recognized image of the television frame "hanging" in the air to the right of the screen (Fig. 4). Sometimes even three-quet images appear, each in the form of a parallelogram inclined to the right. Why do these images appear and why are they tilted? Will their tilt remain the same if you move your eyes to the other side? Will similar recognized images appear if you move your eyes up or down?

8. Black and white disc colors

Can any colors be distinguished on a black and white surface? As a rule, no. However, make a disk with alternating black and white sectors and, rotating it at a relatively low speed, look closely at it, trying not to notice individual sectors. After a few minutes, the edges of the white sectors in front of the direction of rotation will turn red, and the edges of the back ones will turn blue. (The shades will be different at different light levels.) When rotated at higher speeds, the white parts turn red and the black parts turn greenish. With a further increase in the rotation speed, the colors can no longer be distinguished, but sparkles of pink and greenish filters begin to jump across the disk. In the disk shown at figure 5, all three effects can be observed simultaneously. Why do we see these flowers? Why do you have to look at it prematurely for several minutes for the disc to “color”?
