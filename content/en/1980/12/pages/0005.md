When a quartz plate, cut in a certain way from a crystal, is deformed, opposite charges arise on opposite faces. The magnitude and sign of the charge on the face depends, respectively, on the magnitude and direction of the deformation (figure 1). This phenomenon, called the direct piezoelectric effect, is reversible: if a potential difference is applied to a piezoquartz plate, then the plastic, depending on the polarity of the voltage, will either collapse or expand. Such a plate, connected to an alternating voltage source, performs forced oscillations with a frequency equal to the frequency of electrical oscillations. When the frequency v of electrical oscillations coincides with the natural frequency v₀ of oscillations of the plate, the amplitude A of its elastic oscillations will be greatest (figure 2). The natural frequency of the plate, determined by its size and shape, weakly depends on temperature and external influences.

From the point of view of radio engineering, a piezoquartz plate is equivalent to a general resonant circuit containing inductance and em-capacity, but differs from it in a very sharp resonance. This property of piezoelectric quartz has long been used in radio engineering to stabilize the frequency of generators in radio transmitters. The same property of piezoquartz—high constancy of its own frequency of oscillation—was used to create astronomical quartz clocks. The main component of such a clock is the electric generator.
