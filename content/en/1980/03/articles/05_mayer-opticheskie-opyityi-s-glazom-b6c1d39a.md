Research has shown that the lens of the eye is responsible for accommodation. If a blurry image of a nearby object appears on the retina of the eye, the corresponding signal travels through the optic nerve to the brain, is formed there and sent to the so-called cilinary muscle 7, which, contracting or stretching, changes the curvature of the surfaces of the lens until a sharp image is obtained on the retina.

Is it possible to experimentally prove that it is the lens that carries out accommodation? How can we be sure that accommodation is caused by a change in the curvature of the lens surfaces?

Probably, first of all, you need to make sure that the lens really exists. Take a mirror and look at your eye in it. You will easily find a white dense shell in it - the sclera 1, the primary part of this shell - the cornea 2, the iris 4 (which serves as a regulator of the light flux that creates the image and determines the color of the eyes).

Discussion of the problem
Stand on the tip of a pencil located at a distance of 15-30 cm from the eye, while you will see more distant objects blurred. Shift your gaze to distant objects - the tip of the pencil will become blurry. Obviously we don’t notice this: all the objects we look at seem quite sharp to us. This means that the eye itself, regardless of our consciousness, seems to adjust for sharpness. This process is called accommodation of the eye. How is it carried out?

Since the optical system of the eye creates images of objects on the retina, the refractive indices of the elements of the system must differ from the refractive index of the environment. (And indeed, according to the variable refractive index of the cornea, lens, aqueous humor and vitreous body, the correspondences $n_p = 1,376$, $n_{xp} = 1,386$, $n_{B.x} = n_{c.t} = 1,336$ are equal.) When light passes from one medium to another, in addition to refraction, reflection of light is also observed. This means that if you place a light source in front of your eye, you can expect to see its image in reflected light.

To a first approximation, the interfaces between different media in the eye are spherical surfaces. Therefore, they should act like spherical mirrors.

So, we come to the conclusion that the optical system of the eye not only forms an image of an object on the retina, but also creates several similar images due to the reflection of light from the surface of the cornea and lens. By discovering these images on the field, you will be able to prove the existence of the lens. And by examining the change in these images when the eye is “focused,” one can understand the optical essence of accommodation.

Before you begin an experimental study of the eye itself, try to stimulate its cornea and lens.

As a model of the eye lens, you can use a double beam with a magnification of 2-3.5 (it is called a clock beam and is still available in photo stores). At a distance of 10-20 cm from the beam, place a flashlight connected to a battery. Looking at the beam from the side of the light bulb, you will see two images of it.

On figure 3 the course of rays is presented when images $l_1$ and $l_2$ of an object are inverted by ray surfaces (the construction was carried out without taking into account refraction in the ray). The upright image $l_1$ is created by the front surface 1 of the beam, acting as a convex mirror, and the inverted image $l_2$ is created by the back surface 2, which is a wavy mirror.

As a rule, in an experiment one image of a source is larger than the other. Let's try to explain why.

For spherical mirrors, all the same correspondences are valid as for a thin lens. The magnification of the object given by the front surface of the beam (by a convex mirror) is equal to (see Fig. 3)

$$\Gamma_1 = \frac{l_1}{l} = \frac{f_1}{d_1}.$$

la and the distance $f_1$ from the mirror to the image are related by correspondence

$$\frac{1}{d_1} - \frac{1}{f_1} = -\frac{1}{F_1}.$$

Considering that the focal length of the mirror is equal to half the radius of curvature $R_1$ of the first spherical surface of the magnifying glass, we obtain

$$\Gamma_1 = \frac{F_1}{F_1 + d_1} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + 2d_1}.$$

If the object is located at a large distance from the mirror compared to its radius of curvature ($d_1 \gg R_1$), in the denominator of the previous formula the first term compared to the second can be neglected and we can assume that the magnification of a convex mirror is equal to

$$\Gamma_1 = \frac{R_1}{2d_1}.$$

Similarly, the magnification of an object given by the back surface of the magnifying glass (concave, mirror) is expressed by the same formula, only instead of $R_1$ there should be $R_2$, and together $d_1 - d_2$:

$$\Gamma_2 = \frac{R_2}{2d_2}.$$

Since the magnifying glass is a thin lens, that is, its thickness is small compared to the radii of curvature, the difference in distances $d_1$ and $d_2$ can be neglected. Then for the magnification of the object given by the surface of the magnifying glass, we have

$$\Gamma_1 = \frac{l_1}{l} = \frac{R_1}{2d} \text{ и } \Gamma_2 = \frac{l_2}{l} = \frac{R_2}{2d},$$

where $d = d_1 = d_2$ is the distance from the object to the magnifying glass.

Consequently, if images $l_1$ and $l_2$ are obtained in different sizes, then the radii of curvature of the magnifying glass are different. By measuring the distance from the object to the magnifying glass and changing the magnification given to the surface of the magnifying glass, you can determine the radii of curvature of its surface.

We advise you to check another experiment with a model of the lens of the eye.

Make a small rectangular chamber out of plasticine; insert a magnifying glass into the side wall of the chamber. Get images of burning llamas -

buds in the light reflected from the surface of the magnifying glass and pay attention to their brightness. Now pour water into the camera - the brightness of the image created by the back surface of the magnifying glass will then decrease. It turns out that the intensity of light reflected from the interface between two media depends on the refractive indices of these media *). In this case, when replacing air with water, the intensity of the reflected light decreased.

We have analyzed in sufficient detail what should be observed in reflected light if the optical system of the eye consisted of a single crystal. However, the eye also has a cornea and two chambers filled with aqueous fluid and vitreous humor.

You can simulate the action of the cornea by placing in front of the magnifying glass, at a distance of 2-4 mm from it, window glass with an optical power of +1 diopter (sold in all pharmacies). Direct the light from a flashlight bulb onto the resulting optical system. The wires are observed from the side of the light bulb, you see four images of it, three of which are straight and one is inverted. Please note that the images formed by the reflection of light from the surface of the window glass are located very close to each other.

Fill the cavity between the window glass and the magnifying glass with water, as well as the plastic chamber behind the magnifying glass. Observe how this changes the brightness of the images created by the various spherical surfaces of your optical system.

He advises you not to rush under any circumstances when setting up model experiments. The more carefully you carry them out, the better you will have an idea of ​​what should be observed in the living eye, and the easier it will be to reveal the desired images.

*) Rigorous calculation for the light reflection coefficient $k$ from the interfaces of two media $n_1$ and $n_2$ - refractive indices of the boundary media.
