Plane movements

It is natural to imagine the movement of a plane from the point of view of mechanics as the movement of one inextricable sheet on another. It turns out that there were only four types of such movements. This article and the properties of displacements useful for solving problems are discussed in this article.

1. Visual description of movements
Let $g$ be an arbitrary mapping of the plane onto itself. A mapping $g$ is called a displacement of a plane if it preserves distances, that is, for any point $A, B$ of this plane the equality
$$|g(A)g(B)| = |AB|.$$

3. Change and orientation

Let us continue the obvious discrepancies associated with the model discussed at the beginning of the article. The conditions are to assume that the positive direction of cutting off corners is specified on the plane. (Usually in drawings, the counterclockwise direction is taken as the positive direction.) In order not to forget about this convention, you can draw a circle in the corner of the drawing, on which the positive direction of cutting off the corners is indicated by an arrow.

For example, rotation and parallel translation can be carried out as a result of the plane sliding on itself, that is, they are orientation-preserving movements.

If, to carry out the movement, we shift the top (transparent) sheet to the reverse side, then the circle on which the counterclockwise direction of traversal was initially depicted will turn into a circle with the clockwise direction of traversal depicted on it. In this case, we say that a movement has been made that changes the orientation. For example, axial symmetry is a displacement that changes orientation (Fig. 5).

It is easy to understand that if each of the displacements $f, g$ preserves orientation, then their composition $g^\circ f$ will be an orientation-preserving displacement. In fact, the circle $l$, on which the traversal direction is counterclockwise, will transform when moving $f$ into the circle $l_1$ with the traversal direction also counterclockwise (since $f$ retains the orientation). In turn, when moving $g$, circle $l_1$ will turn into circle $l_2$ with the direction of traversal also counterclockwise (since $g$ maintains orientation). When moving $g^\circ f$, circle $l$ (with a counterclockwise traversal direction on it) will turn into circle $l_2$ with the same traversal direction. Thus, moving $g^\circ f$ maintains orientation. Reasoning similarly, we obtain the following statement:

Theorem 2. If each of the displacements $f, g$ preserves orientation or if each of them changes orientation, then their composition $g^\circ f$ is an orientation-preserving displacement. If one of the movements $f, g$ preserves and the other changes orientation, then $g^\circ f$ is a movement that changes orientation.

To make it easier to remember this formulation, the conditions are to consider movements that preserve orientation as “positive”, and movements that maintain less orientation as “negative”. Then the same sign rule will apply to the composition of displacements as for the multiplication of real numbers: if $f$ and $g$ have the same “signs”, then the result is “positive”, and if they are different, then the result is “negative”.

Note that sliding symmetry (it was defined as the composition of axial symmetry and parallel translation) is, according to Theorem 2, a displacement that changes orientation. Thus, of all the plane movements that are listed in Theorem 1, rotation and parallel translation preserve the orientation, while rotation and sliding symmetry change the orientation.

5. Applying displacements to problem solving

Problem 4. Two circles with centers $O_1, O_2$ intersect at points $A$ and $B$. Prove that the line $O_1O_2$ redistributes the segment $AB$ and divides it in half.

Solution. Let us denote by $l_1, l_2$ the semicircles lying on one side of the straight line $O_1O_2$, by $l'_1, l'_2$ the semicircles lying on the other side (Fig. 9), and by $s$ the symmetry with respect to $(O_1O_2)$. Then $l'_1 = s(l_1), l'_2 = s(l_2)$. Since $s(l_1 \cap l_2) = s(l_1) \cap s(l_2) = l'_1 \cap l'_2$, we get $s(A) = B$, that is, the points $A$ and $B$ are symmetrical with respect to $(O_1O_2)$. This is where the required statement follows.

In this solution we took advantage of the fact that for any movement $f$ and any figures $M, N$ the correspondence $f(M \cap N) = f(M) \cap f(N)$ is valid. Prove it yourself.

Problem 5. On the sides $AB$ and $BC$ of an arbitrary triangle $ABC$ squares $ABMN$ and $BCPQ$ are constructed outside it. Point $K$ is the middle of segment $MQ$. Prove that the segment $BK$ redistributed the base $AC$ and twice its crown.

Solution. Let $L$ be the midpoint of the segment $AC$ (Fig. 10). It is necessary to prove that the segments $BK$ and $CL$ redistributed the circle and are congruent. To do this, it is most convenient to establish that the first segment is obtained from the second by rotating by $\pm 90^\circ$ around a certain point. So, we would like to find a point, a rotation by $90^\circ$ or $-90^\circ$ around which transforms the point $C$ into $B$, and the point $L$ into $K$. But it is clear that turning $r$ by $-90^\circ$ around the center of the square $BCPQ$ takes the point $C$ to $B$. This means that it remains to establish that $r(L) = K$.

We have $r(C) = B, r(B) = Q$; let's put $r(A) = D$. Then $r([BA]) = [QD]$, that is, the segments $AB$ and $DQ$ have been redistributed and are congruent. This means that the segments $BM$ and $QD$ are parallel and congruent, that is, $BMDQ$ are parallel. Therefore, the point $K$ (the midpoint of the diagonal $MQ$) is also the midpoint of the segment $BD$. Since $r([CA]) = [BD]$, we get $r(L) = K$. So, $r(C) = B, r(L) = K$, that is, $r([CL]) = [BK]$, and therefore the segments $CL$ and $BK$ have been redistributed and are congruent.
