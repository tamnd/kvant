The poet is right when he points out that “both light and heat come from a bottle,” but, as befits a poet, he exaggerates when he compares an incandescent lamp to the Sun. Both differences are caused by the same reason: the temperature of the filament (“hair”) of an incandescent lamp (3000°C) is approximately two times lower than the temperature of the surface of the Sun (6000°C).

Let's move from position to numbers, and we come across an interesting paradox. An incandescent lamp is an almost ideal converter of electrical energy into radiation energy (with an efficiency close to unity) and at the same time an incandescent lamp is a very uneconomical light source. How is this possible?

Cold light

The disadvantages of the incandescent lamp as a source of visible light have forced scientists to look for new light sources that are more economical in the sense of converting electrical energy into light and light, closer to the sun in color properties. Thus, the task was to both increase light output and increase light quality.

S.I. Vavilov had the idea of ​​using the cold glow phenomenon called lominescence for these purposes (see, for example, Physics-10, pp. 210-212). If the Sun or an incandescent lamp represent heat sources in which radiation occurs due to heating to a high temperature, then the glow of the lominophore (lominescent body) is, as a rule, not associated with its heating.

We will be interested in the so-called photolominophores, the glow of which is caused by irradiation with light from an external source. Photolominophores convert radiation of one wavelength into radiation of another wavelength. Many dyes (for example, rhodimine orange dye) exhibit bright laminescence under the influence of a daylight candle.

The modern doctrine of literal lominescence owes much to S.I. Vavilov. In particular, S.I. Vavilov showed that lominophores - as a radiation energy converter - can have a very high efficiency. This property is very important for the practical use of lominophores.

Mechanism of radiation generation in a gas discharge

As already mentioned, the source of ultraviolet radiation in a luminescence lamp is an electrical discharge in other vapors at low pressure*). Electrons, aligned with ka汰a and accelerated by the electric field, meet mercury atoms on their way and experience collisions with it.

The nature of the collision turns out to depend on the energy of the electrons**). If the electron energy is less than 4.9 eV, its collision with a mercury atom is elastic in nature - the kinetic energy of the electron practically does not change, but only the direction of its movement changes. At an energy equal to (or greater than) 4.9 eV, the collision of an electron with a mercury atom becomes inelastic - the electron will be separated in just

(or almost all) of its energy to the mercury atom. This is one of the manifestations of the quantum properties of atoms.

According to quantum concepts, each atom can only have certain reserves of energy, or, as they say, it can only be at very specific energy levels. Consequently, an atom can only accept certain portions of energy. Thus, for a mercury atom such a minimum portion is an energy of 4.9 eV. The mercury atom does not accept less energy (its “quantum pride” manifests itself). What happens to the atom next?

figure 5 shows a very rough diagram of the energy levels of a mercury atom. As a result of an inelastic collision with an electron, the atom goes into an excited state corresponding to energy level 3, “lives” at this level for approximately 10⁻⁷ s and returns back to level 1.

Note that argon atoms, although their number exceeds the number of mercury atoms by hundreds of times, are not excited at all. This is due to the fact that the minimum exciting energy of argon atoms is significantly higher than that of mercury atoms.

Color vision is associated with the complex structure of the retina of the human eye. It consists of rods and cones - light-sensitive cells associated with the endings of the optic nerve branched in the retina (Fig. 9). Rods are more sensitive and work even in low light conditions (night vision), but colors are practically indistinguishable (“all cats are gray at night”). Cones are less sensitive and only work when the light is high enough (daytime vision), but this allows for color discrimination. For comparison, we point out that only sticks will fit, so they should make eye contact. Chickens, on the other hand, have only cones, which is why they go to bed at sunset.

The human eye is thought to have three types of cones.

When one of these types of cone light is absorbed, it produces a sensation of blue, another of green, and a third of red. The color of an object we perceive depends on the correspondence of the excitation intensities of the three indicated types of cones. This correspondence in turn depends on the spectral composition of the light reflected from the object and entering the eye. The spectral composition of reflected light is determined by the dependence of the reflectance of an object on the wavelength (Fig. 10) and the spectral composition of the light incident on the object. So, many of these have completely different colors in daylight and in incandescent light.
