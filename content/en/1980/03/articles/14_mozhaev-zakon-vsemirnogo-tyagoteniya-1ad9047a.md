Law of Gravity

Among the various forces operating in the Universe, the most fundamental and majestic is the force of gravity. Under the influence of this force, the planets of the solar system, including our Earth, move in their orbits around the Sun. This force attracts all bodies on it to the center of the Earth. The entire power of the spacecraft’s engines was directed against this force when, on October 4, 1957, for the first time in the world, the first Soviet artificial Earth satellite was launched into low-Earth orbit.

The first person to understand the role of gravity was Newton. Analyzing Kepler's laws, which describe the movement of the planet in its orbits around the Sun, he came to the conclusion that in order to keep the planet in orbit, there must be forces in the direction exactly from the planet to the Sun and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. However, Newton's main task is that he was able to understand that the force of attraction of a planet to the Sun is a special case of gravity acting between any and two bodies. It is no coincidence that the law discovered by Newot is called the law of universal gravitation. This is one of the most fundamental laws of nature.

In the simplest case, when the interaction of a body can be considered material points (the size of the bodies is compared to the distance between them), the law of universal gravitation is formed as follows - any two material points attract each other with a force $\vec{F}$, directed along the line connecting them, directly proportional to their masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ and inversely proportional to the square of the distance $r$ between them:

$$|\vec{F}| = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}.$$

Here $G = 6,67 \cdot 10^{-11} \text{H} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{кг}^2$ is the gravitational constant; it was first measured by Cavendish.

If the size of the bodies cannot be neglected, the bodies must be mentally divided into small areas so that they can be considered material points. For each pair of material points, you need to write the law of gravity in the form (*) and find the corresponding force of attraction, and then add up all the results obtained. It turns out that for centro-symmetric bodies (for example, for a ball or a spherical layer), the force of attraction can be calculated directly using formula (*), taking $r$ to be the distance between the centers of mass of interacting bodies.

As is known, gravitational fields (gravitational fields) are potential, that is, the work of the field to move a body from point 1 to point 2 does not depend on the shape of the trajectory, but is determined only by the difference in the potential energies of the body at points 1 and 2 of the corresponding:

$$A_{12} = E_{p1} - E_{p2}.$$

From this equality it is clear that only the difference in potential energies at different points of the field has a certain physical meaning. The numerical value of potential energy at a single point does not have much meaning; it is always determined with an accuracy up to a certain constant value. That is why, when solving specific problems, the zero level of potential energy can be chosen arbitrarily, at the most convenient point.

Typically, to determine the potential energy of a body of mass $m$ raised to a height $h$ above the zero level, one uses the formula

$$E_p = mgh.$$

However, this equality is valid only for values ​​of $h$, much less than the Earth's radius $R_3(h \ll R_3)$. If this condition is not met, the potential energy must be calculated differently. Let's illustrate this with a specific problem.

Problem 1. Giving the potential energy of the body equal to zero at infinity (that is, at a great distance from the Earth, where the force of gravity is negligible), find the dependence of the potential energy $E_p$ on the distance $r$ from the center of the Earth.

Let's limit ourselves to the region $r \geq R_3$.

The potential energy $E_p$ of a body with mass $m$, located at a distance $r$ from the center of the Earth, is equal to the force that the gravitational field makes, moving this body from a given point to infinity. Since the rotation in a potential field does not depend on the shape of the trajectory, we will assume that the body moves along the radial direction. When a body moves from point 1, located at a distance of $r_1$ from the center of the Earth (Fig. 1), to point 2, located at a distance of $r_2$ from the center of the Earth, the field rotates

$$A_{12} = \int_{r_1}^{r_2} |\vec{F}(r)| \, dr = \int_{r_1}^{r_2} -G \frac{mM_3}{r^2} \, dr = G \frac{mM_3}{r_2} - G \frac{mM_3}{r_1}.$$

Provided that point 2 is at infinity, the first term is equal to zero (at $r_2 \to \infty \frac{1}{r_2} \to 0$). Thus, the dependence of potential energy on the distance $r$ from the center of the Earth has the form

$$E_p(r) = -G \frac{mM_3}{r},$$

where $M_3$ is the mass of the Earth. The graph of this dependence is shown in figure 2.

Let's look at a few more problems.

Task 2. Determine what minimum speed must be imparted to a body located on the Earth’s surface in order for it to leave the sphere of action of the Earth’s gravitational field.

First of all, we note that the desired speed is called the second cosmic speed $\vec{v}_{2k}$. To determine its model $v_{2k}$ we use the law of conservation of energy.

Immediately after launch, that is, directly at the Earth's surface, the kinetic energy of the body is $mv_{2k}^2/2$, and its potential energy is $-GmM_3/R_3$. Total mechanical energy

$$E = \frac{mv_{2k}^2}{2} - G \frac{mM_3}{R_3}.$$

When a body moves away from the Earth to an infinitely large distance (goes beyond the action of the Earth’s gravitational field), the potential energy of the body becomes equal to zero. Obviously, in this case the kinetic energy will also go to zero (we are looking for the minimum initial speed of the body). Since the total energy of the body does not change, we obtain

$$\frac{mv_{2k}^2}{2} - G \frac{mM_3}{R_3} = 0.$$

From here

$$v_{2k} = \sqrt{2G \frac{M_3}{R_3}} = \sqrt{2gR_3} \approx 11,2 \text{ km/c}$$
($g = 9,8 \text{ m/c}^2$ is the acceleration of free pressure at the Earth’s surface).
