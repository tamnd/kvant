The challenger-challenger quarterfinal matches are due to end in March. Figures 1, 2 show diagrams on which you can mark the results of these matches. By the end of the year, the question marks will be removed, and then we found out with whom Anatoly Karpov and Maya Chiburdanise will have to defend their champion titles in the World Championship matches in 1981.

Let us note that of all the stages of the fight for the world championship, only the applicants are carried out according to the cup (Olympic) system. Zonal and international tournaments are held according to a cool system, in which all chess players play with each other. In the article “Who will go to Rio?” («Kvant», 1972, No. 8) a number of interesting problems about cup tournaments are considered under the assumption that the “transitivity principle” is true: if A plays stronger than B, and B plays stronger than C, then A plays stronger than C. Unfortunately, in practice this principle is not always fulfilled. Thus, any failure of the third pair (see Fig. 1) has a higher Elo coefficient than the participants of the second pair, but, alas, only one of the two outstanding chess players - M. Tal or L. Polugaevsky - will continue the fight for the world crown after the quarterfinals.

4. The proof was proved by the method of mathematical induction. For $n = 1$ the statement of the problem is obvious. Let us assume that it is true for some $n = k$. Let us prove that then it is true for $n = k + 1$. Let there be $3(k + 1)$ cities, for communication between which $k + 1$ modes of transport are used. Let's take some city $C$. He's connected to $3(k + 1)$! cities. Let us prove that there are at least $3k!$ cities from which city $C$ is connected by the same modes of transport. Let us assume that among these $3k!$ cities there are cities $A$ and $B$, connected to each other by the same type of transport as with city $C$. This means that, having left the city $C$, we, without changing the type of transport, increase in the cities $A$ and $B$ and return back to $C$. If there are no two such cities, then we come to the case where there are $3k!$ cities and $k$ modes of transport, which gives the statement an answer to the question of the problem by the induction assumption.
