Mechanism of radiation generation in a gas discharge

As already mentioned, the source of ultraviolet radiation in a luminescence lamp is an electrical discharge in other vapors at low pressure*). Electrons, aligned with ka汰a and accelerated by the electric field, meet mercury atoms on their way and experience collisions with it.

The nature of the collision turns out to depend on the energy of the electrons**). If the electron energy is less than 4.9 eV, its collision with a mercury atom is elastic in nature - the kinetic energy of the electron practically does not change, but only the direction of its movement changes. At an energy equal to (or greater than) 4.9 eV, the collision of an electron with a mercury atom becomes inelastic - the electron will be separated in just

(or almost all) of its energy to the mercury atom. This is one of the manifestations of the quantum properties of atoms.

According to quantum concepts, each atom can only have certain reserves of energy, or, as they say, it can only be at very specific energy levels. Consequently, an atom can only accept certain portions of energy. Thus, for a mercury atom such a minimum portion is an energy of 4.9 eV. The mercury atom does not accept less energy (its “quantum pride” manifests itself). What happens to the atom next?

figure 5 shows a very rough diagram of the energy levels of a mercury atom. As a result of an inelastic collision with an electron, the atom goes into an excited state corresponding to energy level 3, “lives” at this level for approximately 10⁻⁷ s and returns back to level 1.

Note that argon atoms, although their number exceeds the number of mercury atoms by hundreds of times, are not excited at all. This is due to the fact that the minimum exciting energy of argon atoms is significantly higher than that of mercury atoms.
