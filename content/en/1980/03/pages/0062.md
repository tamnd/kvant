What is new, then, in applied mathematics compared to classical mathematics? After all, classical mathematics (sometimes very unsuccessfully trying to call it “pure”) arose from the practical needs of people, dealt with mathematical models, and solved mathematical problems.

In the past, when solving problems, the main method was to obtain the necessary consequences from given premises using the laws of formal logic, and the main goal was to introduce into the derivation an “exact formula” that clearly connects the initial quantities with the given ones. Meanwhile, in most cases it is impossible to find an explicit formula in most cases; an exact answer based on various principles is impossible in principle. Then it is natural to try to indicate the correct execution of an infinite sequence of computational operations that allows one to obtain an approximate solution with required accuracy in a finite number of steps. However, this approach is used very rarely - not because mathematicians considered calculations an unworthy occupation, but because they are extremely labor-intensive and even practically impracticable on existing computing environments.

The creation of modern computers has radically changed the situation: now there is a real opportunity to carry out an exhaustive analysis of wide classes of mathematical problems, not only by forming logical conclusions, but also on the basis of the actual implementation of the calculation. The second main element of applied mathematics is precisely the development of algorithms - precise descriptions of sequences of computational operations that make it possible to obtain approximate solutions to problems using a computer. Having selected several fairly typical problems, the authors trace the entire path from the preliminary understanding of the problem and the emergence of the identity that forms the basis of the numerical method, to the final formation of the algorithm, its justification and comparison with other algorithms for solving the same problem. This material is of undoubted interest for equine logical mathematics, since it contains the structure and simultaneous execution for understanding the presentation of important mathematical issues. Pay special attention to the chapter containing the introduction of a certain interval and a description of numerical interpolation algorithms.

The authors of the book convincingly show that problems of applied mathematics are complex and server-based mathematical problems that require broad knowledge in a wide variety of areas of theoretical mathematics and reduce all the capabilities of computer technology.

A small but clearly written chapter is devoted to the third basic element of applied mathematics—electronic computers. Here we discuss the history of computer technology (but without describing the technical side of the matter), the problems of human-machine communication, and the most diverse applications of computers.

Electronic computers are one of the most significant creations of science and technology of the 20th century, opening up enormous program capabilities. However, computers themselves cannot solve unambiguous problems; they do not work without the direction of human influence. Therefore, today applied mathematics in the machine is actively attracting the interest of talented young people - those who in a few years will be able to turn the possibility of a program into the reality of life. The wonderful book by A. N. Tikhovov and D. P. Kostomarov serves precisely this lofty goal.

N. Rozov
