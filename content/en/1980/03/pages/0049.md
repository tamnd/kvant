⟦folio 47⟧

Twisted polygons

The pattern featured on the second cover page is made up of “twisted polygons.” A twisted polygon consists of a chain of right polygons: each subsequent polygon $M'$ is inscribed in the previous $M$ (the vertices $M'$ lie on the side $M$), and $M'$ is subset $M$ (the similarity coefficient is fixed for this chain).

Such chains look especially effective if they are built on a family of identical polygons; covering the plane (without overlap or runs), say, on a family of squares, right triangles or hexagons.

How does the computer that drew our pattern construct twisted polygons? The machine is “given” the initial polygon $P_1P_2...P_n$ (let’s call it a polygon of the first rank), that is, a list of vertices specified by their coordinates

$$P_1(x_1; y_1), P_2(x_2; y_2), ..., P_n(x_n; y_n).$$

Next, using a plotter, the machine draws the sides of the polygon, sequentially connecting the vertices with segments. Then it finds the coordinates of the vertices $P_1', P_2', ..., P_n'$ of the next polygon (second rank) using this rule:

$$(x'_i; y'_i) = (x_i; y_{i-1}) + k(x_{i+1} - x_i; y_{i+1} - y_i)$$

(we advise the reader to make a drawing, for example, for $n=6$, and make sure that the coefficient $k$ in the limiting formula is equal to

$$k = \frac{|P_i P'_i|}{|P_i P_{i+1}|}.$$

Next, the machine sequentially connects the vertices $P_1', P_2', ..., P_n', P'_i$, thereby obtaining figure of a polygon of the second rank. From a polygon of the second rank, the computer obtains a polygon of the third rank. And so on.

To avoid a blob (which occurs when small polygons accumulate towards the center of a third-rank polygon), the program limits the number of polygons in the chain.
