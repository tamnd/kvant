Let now two pairs of points $A, B$ and $A_1, B_1$ be given in the plane, and $|AB| = |A_1B_1| \neq 0$. The visual movement model described above suggests that there are exactly two plane movements that take the point $A$ to $A_1$ and the point $B$ to $B_1$.

In fact, by sliding the pronunciation sheet over an opaque one, one can find only one position of the pronunciation sheet, in which the points $A, B$ placed on it will coincide, respectively, with the point $A_1, B_1$ on the opaque sheet (Fig. 1). You can do it differently: first turn the pronunciation sheet over to the reverse side, and then combine the points $A, B$ placed on it with the point $A_1, B_1$ on the opaque sheet. There are no other movements (except for the two indicated) that would transform the points $A, B$ to $A_1, B_1$, respectively. We see that:

If $|AB| = |A_1B_1|$ (and $A \neq B$), then there are exactly two movements of the plane that take the point $A, B$ to $A_1, B_1$, respectively.

Of course, a “thought experiment” conducted with the described visual model cannot be considered a mathematical proof of the formulation of a statement - it can only be considered an experimental confirmation. In mathematics, the formulation of a statement is accepted (within the framework of school geometry) without proof, that is, it represents an axiom; it is called the axiom of mobility*) of the plane.
