Color vision is associated with the complex structure of the retina of the human eye. It consists of rods and cones - light-sensitive cells associated with the endings of the optic nerve branched in the retina (Fig. 9). Rods are more sensitive and work even in low light conditions (night vision), but colors are practically indistinguishable (“all cats are gray at night”). Cones are less sensitive and only work when the light is high enough (daytime vision), but this allows for color discrimination. For comparison, we point out that only sticks will fit, so they should make eye contact. Chickens, on the other hand, have only cones, which is why they go to bed at sunset.

The human eye is thought to have three types of cones.

When one of these types of cone light is absorbed, it produces a sensation of blue, another of green, and a third of red. The color of an object we perceive depends on the correspondence of the excitation intensities of the three indicated types of cones. This correspondence in turn depends on the spectral composition of the light reflected from the object and entering the eye. The spectral composition of reflected light is determined by the dependence of the reflectance of an object on the wavelength (Fig. 10) and the spectral composition of the light incident on the object. So, many of these have completely different colors in daylight and in incandescent light.
