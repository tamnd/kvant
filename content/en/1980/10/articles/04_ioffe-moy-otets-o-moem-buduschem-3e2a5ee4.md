My father -
about my future

Below are the memoirs of the daughters of Abram Fedorovich Ioffe, Valentinna Abramovna Ioffe, Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, senior researcher at the Institute of Silicate Chemists of the USSR Academy of Sciences.

When I finished school and had to decide what to study next, my father often talked to me about my future. He really wanted me to become a physicist, but there was no pressure from him. He only sought to describe to me the future of a scientific physicist, and he left it to me to decide whether such a prospect tempted me.

My father was a man of very broad interests. He loved and knew music well, and often attended classical music concerts at Filmonini. He appreciated the talent of young Shostakovich from his first steps. He also loved the theater - opera, drama; I could watch performances of the Vakhtangov Theater and the Art Theater several times. He took great interest in all experiments in the field of theatrical art.

He knew and loved poetry, especially Pushkin and Lermontov. I read a lot, knew the classics well, but also followed new literature until very recently.

My father loved nature very much; trees and flowers especially aroused his admiration. He loved long walks and used to go to the mountains - in his youth in Switzerland, and then, in adulthood, in the Crimea and the Caucasus. He swam well and played tennis.

He loved life in all its manifestations and knew how to get joy from different sources. But everything paled before the joy brought to him by the pursuit of science - a successful agreement, clearly established by experience. He often said that in his passion for physics there was an excitement that was somewhat similar to the excitement of a gambler—the excitement of a contract, as a result of which the player receives a win, and the scientist receives an explanation for an unknown phenomenon. He believed that studying physics did not require any special talents—everyone could study physics and enjoy it. It is important to feel this joy, and then life will be filled with lively interest, very little dependent on external circumstances.

My father was an ardent supporter of the involvement of women in scientific work. He always spoke with admiration about Marie and Irene Curie, about Litza Meitner and other women physicists at the forefront of modern science.

In conclusion, I would like to quote an excerpt from my father’s letter to me. To a fifteen-year-old student in her last year of school:

“...You will see that if you are interested in some problems, visible contradictions, for which you need to find a solution, having first found the original root that gives rise to this contradiction...

You don't seem to have much confidence in your talents. Now, of course, it is impossible to predict them. But different types of scientists created our modern worldview. It is impossible to describe exactly what life “should” be like for a person who wants to become a scientist. The truth is revealed in different ways...

Then, be able to see things clearly, understand clearly and recognize the innocuous spiritually develops and compares with people of the highest culture.

Finally, seeking and finding new paths and new understandings is one of the greatest satisfactions.”
