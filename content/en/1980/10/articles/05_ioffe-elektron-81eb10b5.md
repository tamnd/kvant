Electron

One of the constituent parts of the atom, the negatively charged electron, was discovered back in the 19th century. In 1897 it was possible, albeit roughly, to measure the charge of an electron; later its mass was measured and even later its other properties were discovered - rotational and magnetic moments.

When illuminated, especially with ultraviolet light, a negatively charged body gradually loses its charge, and a neutral one becomes positively charged. This phenomenon, called the photoelectric effect or, in short, photoelectric effect, was studied by Stoletov back in the 19th century. The nature of the charges emitted by the body during the photoelectric effect - electrons - is especially clearly visible in the experiment I conducted in 1912, which was a modification of previously used devices.

Small charged metal dust o fell into the space between two parallel horizontal plates A and B (figure 1). If the particle turns out to be negatively charged, then it can be kept from falling by charging the top plate positively and the bottom plate negatively.

A positively charged dust particle is held by a field of the opposite sign. It is possible to select such a potential difference $U_1$ between the plates so that the electric force just balances the force of gravity. Then the speck hangs motionless for many hours.

The electric force that acts on the charge $e_1$ of the dust is directed upward and is equal to $e_1 \frac{U_1}{d}$, where $d$ is the distance between the plates $A$ and $B$. Let us denote the force of gravity acting on the dust particles by $P$. The condition for the equilibrium of dust in the space between the plates is the equality of gravitational force and electrical force:

$$P = e_1 \frac{U_1}{d}.$$

In this state, I illuminated the pollen with a light source that caused the photoelectric effect. After some time, the previously motionless dust began to fall. Since the gravity $P$ could not change due to illumination, $U_1$ and $d$ did not change, then, consequently, the charge $e_1$ decreased to some value $e_2$.

By increasing the potential difference $U_1$ to a new value $U_2$, it was possible to stop the dust again; in this case the equality $e_2 \frac{U_2}{d} = P$ must be satisfied.

By charging and discharging the dust again, this process could be repeated hundreds of times, each time at the corresponding potentials $U_1, U_2, U_3, \dots$. It turned out that the various charges $e_1, e_2, e_3, \dots$ that the dust had were precisely expressed by the following relationship:

$$e_1:e_2:e_3:... = \frac{1}{U_1}:\frac{1}{U_2}:\frac{1}{U_3}:... = 1:2:3:...$$

The charges of a speck of dust changed like a series of whole numbers. When there was only one negative charge left on a speck of dust, it lost it entirely when illuminated and remained uncharged. Indeed, no potential difference $U$ (neither positive nor negative) could replace the fall of a particle. But if the illumination is continued during the fall, then after some time the dust particle, losing another charge, becomes positively charged, and then it can be left again, but only with the opposite potential difference, when the potential of the upper layer is negative, and the lower one is positive.

Thousands of such experiments with dust particles from various materials have shown that the charge of any dust particle always consists of an integer number of defined portions, equal to the most precise modern changes $4,8024 \cdot 10^{-10}$ absolute electrostatic units (a unit is taken to be a charge that repels an equal charge placed at a distance of 1 cm, with a force of 1 dyne) $^*$). Every time a speck of dust lost its negative charge during illumination, it turned out to be equal to - $4,8024 \cdot 10^{-10}$ absolute.

These single negative charges are called electrons.

Never and nowhere has it been possible to observe charges that change the charge of an electron or are equal to a non-integer number of electron charges. The same negatively charged particles - electrons - are emitted by heated bodies. This phenomenon is called thermionic emission; it is widely used in radio tubes.

When an electric charge is passed through highly rarefied gases, a stream of negative charges called cathode rays emerges from the negative pole (kathad). It was possible to show that

By placing catalog rays or a stream of photoelectrons in a magnetic field, it is possible to determine the ratio of the charge of electrons $e$ to their mass $m$, and, consequently, the mass of electrons itself.

It turned out that not only the charges, but also the masses $m$ of electrons of very different origins exactly coincide and that

$$m = 9,1060 \cdot 10^{-28} \text{ г.}$$

During our first decade, its vector was the only “elementary” particle known at that time. Physicists thought that the electron was the last and simplest element of nature, and imagined it as equal atoms, in the form of a ball, over the volume or surface of which an electric charge is distributed.

However, even then, in 1908, V.I. Lenin pointed out that the principles of dialectical materials about the inexhaustible diversity of nature leads us to expect that the electron is a complex formation.

This prediction was later confirmed, although we still cannot be sure of the complete existing picture of the electron.

It turned out that the electron has a rotational moment (spin) $s$ and a magnetic moment $μ$.

The magnetic moment of an electron determines the maximum moment of forces acting on an electron in a magnetic field with induction $B$:

$$M_{\text{max}} = |B| \mu.$$

For a turn with current $I$

$$M_{\text{max}} = |B| IS,$$

where $S$ is the area limited by the coil (see Physics 9). Therefore, the magnetic moment of the coil with current is equal to $μ = IS$. Magnetic moment is a vector quantity. Vector $\mu$ is directed perpendicular to the plane of the coil.

The magnetic properties of the electron are clearly revealed in experiments that were performed by Stern and Gerlach using the molecular beam method. This method studies the flow of molecules in the form of a thin ribbon, isolated using a system of narrow slits in the space in which the air is created.

High vacuum is possible. Any deviation of such a flow of molecules from a straight path can only be caused by the action of forces, and these forces can be calculated from the initial speed of the molecules and the magnitude of the deviation. This method is used to detect a wide variety of influences, including even such weak ones as gravitational forces.

If you pass a molecular beam through a strong and, moreover, sharply inhomogeneous magnetic field, then individual molecules will deviate the more strongly, the greater their magnetic moment and the smaller the angle between the direction of the magnetic moment and the direction of the magnetic field through which the molecules pass.

The magnetic moment can come from both the magnetic properties of the electron itself and from the electric current that the electron creates while moving in a closed orbit. If, for example, an electron moves along a circle of radius $r$ with a speed $v$, then the electron passes through the cross section of the circle $v/2\pi r$ times per second, each time transferring its charge $e$. Thus, rotating around a circle of radius $r$ the electron represents a current $i = ev/2\pi r$. The magnetic moment $\mu$ of an electron moving in a circular orbit is equal to

$$\mu = \pi r^2 i = \frac{evr}{2}.$$

The mechanical angular momentum of such an orbiting electron is $L = mvr$.

Thus, for an electron to move in a circular orbit

$$\frac{\mu}{L} = \frac{e}{2m}.$$

In hydrogen, lithium and silver atoms, the mechanical rotational moment of electrons when moving in orbit is zero: $L = 0$. Therefore, the magnetic moment $\mu$ must be equal to zero.

Meanwhile, for the flux of all these atoms, a good change occurred when passing through a magnetic field (figure 2). In this case, the entire flow was divided into

$$\frac{\mu}{s} = \frac{e}{m}$$

— the ratio $\mu/s$ is twice as large as the ratio $\mu/L$.
