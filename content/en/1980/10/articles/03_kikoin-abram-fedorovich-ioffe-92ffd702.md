Abram Fedorovich Yuffe

When starting this article, I did not intend to give a scientific biography of the outstanding Soviet physicist Abram Fedorovich Yuffe, whose 100th birthday anniversary is celebrated on October 30, 1980. Yuffe's biography is well known and is presented in a number of books and articles about him. I just wanted to share with the readers «Kvant» my personal memories of the founder of the Soviet school of physics, whose student I had the good fortune to be.

When a crystal is deformed, for example, when it is stretched, enormous mechanical stresses arise on these cracks, significantly exceeding the external stresses applied to the crystal as a whole. Because of this, it is precisely in those places where there are microsoptic cracks that the crystal ruptures when it is deformed under external stresses much lower than the calculated values ​​of the maximum permissible stresses.

Abram Fedorovich Ioffe agreed that if a crystal is placed in a liquid that dissolves it, then the surface layer with its defects will be removed; Consequently, the surface of the crystal becomes quite perfect, practically without defects, and the strength of such a crystal in a liquid should approach the theoretical one.

The easiest way was to test this assumption on crystals of table coli, well dissolved in water. A rock coli crystal immersed in water should be much stronger than a crystal in the air. The experiments of A.F. Ioffe brilliantly confirmed this idea, and the very phenomenon of crystal simplification in a solvent was called the Ioffe effect.

In the above-mentioned report, Abram Fedorovich demonstrated the idea of ​​​​reducing the strength of a body under the influence of small cracks on its surface with the following simple experiment. He took a piece of paper by the ends and tried to stretch it - the paper did not tear. Then he made a small cut across the strip, and then the paper tore with insignificant effort.

Following this experiment, he demonstrated another one. In a large glass vessel with water there were glass rods, which “wet” for several days. The same dry glass rods lay on the demonstration table. Joffe took the sticks from those lying on the table and bent it. As expected, the wand immediately broke. After that, he took the sticks out of the vessel with water and tried to bend it in the same way; at the same time he managed to bend it into a ring. This was explained by the fact that water, although to a small extent, dissolves glass; after prolonged exposure to water, the damage to the glass rods becomes free from cracks, and therefore they are easily bent. Thus, with simple experiments in a relatively short report, Abram Fedorovich explained to his readers the complex problem of the theory of crystal strength.

I could once again be convinced that Abram Fedorovich had an extraordinary talent for finding ways to present complex issues of physics in simple and clear language, without sacrificing the rigor of presentation, by listening to a number of lectures from the general course of physics, which he gave to the first year of our faculty, when I was a second-year student. Ioffe did not have oratorical talent, but his presentation of the subject was distinguished by extraordinary clarity, was accompanied by brilliant demonstrations, and it was difficult not to be carried away by the physics in his presentation.

Later I learned that in most advanced countries of the world, general physics courses are taught by prominent scientists. This is understandable, because it is in the general physics course that students are given an idea of ​​physics as a whole, and only outstanding scientists with a broad scientific horizon can do this. Abram Fedorovich Ioffe was such an outstanding physicist. Along with the great scientific work that he constantly carried out, Abram Fedorovich showed increased concern for young researchers and, naturally, students of his department.

...Since the beginning of the 30s, A.F. Ioffe became interested in the physics of semiconductors. I thought that some kind of intuition told Abram Fedorovich that semiconductors, which were very little studied at that time, could have important technical applications. The paucity of experimental data on semiconductors was explained by the fact that the electrical properties of a semiconductor with the same chemical composition, such as electrical conductivity, varied widely from sample to sample, and therefore experimental physicists, eager to obtain unambiguous data, gave up on semiconductors.

Abram Fedorovich was a real mastermind of this class of substances, so much so that he switched a significant part of the institute’s staff to their research. Of course, this was not done administratively. Abram Fedorovich always shows interest, and therefore enthusiasm, for the study of new areas of physics.

Already the first data obtained showed that the large scatter of data on the electrical properties of semiconductors of the same chemical composition observed in the experiment was explained by insignificant, from a chemical point of view, applications. Therefore, Abram Fedorovich focused special attention on studying the influence of small amounts of impurities on the electrical properties of semiconductors. Note that the term “small impurities” refers to fractions and even millionths of a percent in relation to the main substance, which are usually not detected by chemical analysis.

Among the issues in this area of ​​physics, Abram Fedorovich became interested in the problem of receiving alternating current. It was discovered that metal-semiconductor contact has a receptive property. When current passes through such a contact, the electrical resistance depends significantly on the direction of the current. If in one direction of current the resistance is low, then in the opposite direction of current it increases sharply. Thus, a semiconductor with a layer of metal deposited on its surface is a receiver, a table needed for many technical purposes. Quite soon after research carried out at LPTI on semiconductors made of copper oxide (Cu₂O) and selenium (Se), which were “fashionable” in the 30s, cuprous oxide and selenium receivers began to be used in industry.

Abram Fedorovich is trying to find a physical explanation for the effect at the metal-semiconductor interface. Together with Yakov Ilch Frenkel, he published a theory of this phenomenon. Although the number of predictions of the theory did not coincide with experimental data, its physical foundations have largely been preserved to this day.
