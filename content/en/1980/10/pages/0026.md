⟦folio 24⟧

High vacuum is possible. Any deviation of such a flow of molecules from a straight path can only be caused by the action of forces, and these forces can be calculated from the initial speed of the molecules and the magnitude of the deviation. This method is used to detect a wide variety of influences, including even such weak ones as gravitational forces.

If you pass a molecular beam through a strong and, moreover, sharply inhomogeneous magnetic field, then individual molecules will deviate the more strongly, the greater their magnetic moment and the smaller the angle between the direction of the magnetic moment and the direction of the magnetic field through which the molecules pass.

The magnetic moment can come from both the magnetic properties of the electron itself and from the electric current that the electron creates while moving in a closed orbit. If, for example, an electron moves along a circle of radius $r$ with a speed $v$, then the electron passes through the cross section of the circle $v/2\pi r$ times per second, each time transferring its charge $e$. Thus, rotating around a circle of radius $r$ the electron represents a current $i = ev/2\pi r$. The magnetic moment $\mu$ of an electron moving in a circular orbit is equal to

$$\mu = \pi r^2 i = \frac{evr}{2}.$$

The mechanical angular momentum of such an orbiting electron is $L = mvr$.

Thus, for an electron to move in a circular orbit

$$\frac{\mu}{L} = \frac{e}{2m}.$$

In hydrogen, lithium and silver atoms, the mechanical rotational moment of electrons when moving in orbit is zero: $L = 0$. Therefore, the magnetic moment $\mu$ must be equal to zero.

Meanwhile, for the flux of all these atoms, a good change occurred when passing through a magnetic field (figure 2). In this case, the entire flow was divided into

$$\frac{\mu}{s} = \frac{e}{m}$$

— the ratio $\mu/s$ is twice as large as the ratio $\mu/L$.
