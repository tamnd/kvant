The fact is that until 1946, academician Vladimir Leontyevich Komarov was president, but his state of health and age did not allow him to effectively manage the activities of this important institution. The Soviet government invited the entire Presidium of the USSR Academy of Sciences to a meeting at which the issue of electing a new president was discussed. At this meeting, the candidacy of Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov was proposed.

The branches of the USSR Academy of Sciences were immediately assembled. At a meeting of the department of physical and mathematical sciences, its academic secretary Abram Fedorovich Ioffe received a government recommendation and asked the members of the department to speak out on this issue. After several speeches, one of the oldest members of the department, Academician Alexey Nikolaevich Krylov, took the floor. He said that before the meeting of the department, knowing the agenda, he specifically looked at the charter of the Academy of Sciences to find out what requirements were presented to the president of the Academy of Sciences. It turned out that in the charter then in force, the words “president of the Academy of Sciences” are mentioned only once, namely: “The President of the Academy of Sciences is elected by the general meeting of the members of the Academy of Sciences.” Therefore, it is difficult for him personally to discuss the issue of a presidential candidate, since he does not know what requirements should be presented to him. As an example of a model charter, Krylov, drawing up the judicial charter of Peter I, read the following points:

1. Theft is the taking of someone else's property in secret.
2. Robbery is the theft of someone else's property, carried out openly.
3. Robbery is the taking of someone else's property, carried out with violence."
Having completed this quotation, Academician Krylov said: “As you can see, in the judicial charter everything is formulated as perfect and clear, but what the president of the Academy of Sciences is is not clear.” The last phrase, of course, caused confusion. Abram Fedorovich Ioffe did not lose his temper and answered Krylov something like this: “I understand that there is a gap in the current charter of the Academy of Sciences, but, apparently, the drafter of the charter assumed that the president would not engage in theft, robbery, or robbery.” These words were met with applause. The department decided to recommend to the general meeting of the Academy of Sciences the candidacy of Academician S.I. Vavilov for the post of president. Soon he was elected president of the USSR Academy of Sciences.

...It cannot be said that Abram Fedorovich’s life path was a success of roses. I witnessed several important events that, of course, greatly upset Abram Fedorovich Ioffe. One of these events was the failure to solve the problems of thin-layer insulation, on which Abram Fedorovich spent a lot of time and effort. It was purely a scientific failure. He bravely endured it and admitted in print that his proposals were wrong.

In 1936, a general meeting of the Academy of Sciences was held in Moscow to discuss the scientific activities of the LPTI, headed by Abram Fedorovich Ioffe. At this meeting, Abram Fedorovich made a corresponding report. Physicists who took part in the discussion of the report sharply criticized the activities of Abram Fedorovich Ioffe himself.

I thought that Abram Fedorovich was very upset by the biased speakers, among whom were his students. All the speeches sounded very tendentious. Those meeting participants who could make an objective positive assessment of the institute’s activities were not given the floor (the author of these lines was among them).

Time has shown how unfair this criticism was.
