1. Let's move on to the “unnumbered” notations: $a_1 = a$, $a_2 = b$, $a_3 = c$, $a_4 = d$, $a_5 = e$. Let's start writing out our numbers, taking into account that $a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = d^2 = e^2 = 1$:
   $a, b, c, d, e, ab, bc, cd, de, eab, ac, bd, ce, abd, bce, abcd, bcde, abcde, acde, ade, ae, a, b, c, d, e, \dots$

We see that starting from $a_{22}$ the numbers are repeated, that is, the period is 21. Since 1980 when divided by 21 leaves a remainder of 6, $a_{1980} = a_6 = ab = -1$.

2. It is best to advise you to stop solving the problem, since the desired layout of cards is impossible.
If it were possible, then card 5 should occupy the extreme upper (or lower) position and touch only cards 1 and 9. Then none of the remaining cards can be placed in the center, since for each of them there are no more than five candidates for neighbors (its “old” connections are prohibited - there are 3 or 4 of them - and itself), while the central card should have six neighbors.

3. January has 31 days. The “January calendar” can be represented as in figure 1. According to the condition, both “Monday” and “Friday” should appear in the bottom four rows of the “Days of the Week” column.
Putting “Monday” in each of these lines in turn, we will see that it can only appear in the bottom line. So the first line says “Tuesday”.

4. After the first deletion, there will be numbers left in the given number in four places, after the second - numbers in places divisible by four; after the third - in places that are multiples of eight, etc. The largest power of two, not exceeding a hundred, is 64. Therefore, the last figure to be crossed out will be the one in the given number in the 64th place, that is, 4.

5. Let us prove that the required coloring is impossible to implement. Let's assume the opposite and consider the cell with 3 (see Fig. 2) - it will be empty and red. Let's take any cell that can be reached from the square with the 3rd move of the knight, for example cell e2; then e2 by condition is also red. Let's expand the remaining cells of two columns and two rows, in which cells with 2 and e2 are located. Obviously, the filled cells can no longer be red.

One of the unshaded cells in row 1 should be red. You can only get to line 1 from lines 2, 3. From e2 you can no longer get to line 1. So, let the red cells lead to line 1 from c3. Therefore, one of the cells b1, d1 must be red. If red is b1, then in column a only cell a4 can be red (cell a5 can only be reached with a knight’s move with a filled-in, that is, unpainted square). Now in line 5 only cell d5 can be red, but then it is impossible to bypass the red cells by hitting each one once. Listen to d1, figure it out for yourself.
