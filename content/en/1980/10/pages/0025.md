⟦folio 23⟧

The charges of a speck of dust changed like a series of whole numbers. When there was only one negative charge left on a speck of dust, it lost it entirely when illuminated and remained uncharged. Indeed, no potential difference $U$ (neither positive nor negative) could replace the fall of a particle. But if the illumination is continued during the fall, then after some time the dust particle, losing another charge, becomes positively charged, and then it can be left again, but only with the opposite potential difference, when the potential of the upper layer is negative, and the lower one is positive.

Thousands of such experiments with dust particles from various materials have shown that the charge of any dust particle always consists of an integer number of defined portions, equal to the most precise modern changes $4,8024 \cdot 10^{-10}$ absolute electrostatic units (a unit is taken to be a charge that repels an equal charge placed at a distance of 1 cm, with a force of 1 dyne) $^*$). Every time a speck of dust lost its negative charge during illumination, it turned out to be equal to - $4,8024 \cdot 10^{-10}$ absolute.

These single negative charges are called electrons.

Never and nowhere has it been possible to observe charges that change the charge of an electron or are equal to a non-integer number of electron charges. The same negatively charged particles - electrons - are emitted by heated bodies. This phenomenon is called thermionic emission; it is widely used in radio tubes.

When an electric charge is passed through highly rarefied gases, a stream of negative charges called cathode rays emerges from the negative pole (kathad). It was possible to show that

cathode rays are a stream of the same electrons.

By placing catalog rays or a stream of photoelectrons in a magnetic field, it is possible to determine the ratio of the charge of electrons $e$ to their mass $m$, and, consequently, the mass of electrons itself.

It turned out that not only the charges, but also the masses $m$ of electrons of very different origins exactly coincide and that

$$m = 9,1060 \cdot 10^{-28} \text{ г.}$$

During our first decade, its vector was the only “elementary” particle known at that time. Physicists thought that the electron was the last and simplest element of nature, and imagined it as equal atoms, in the form of a ball, over the volume or surface of which an electric charge is distributed.

However, even then, in 1908, V.I. Lenin pointed out that the principles of dialectical materials about the inexhaustible diversity of nature leads us to expect that the electron is a complex formation.

This prediction was later confirmed, although we still cannot be sure of the complete existing picture of the electron.

It turned out that the electron has a rotational moment (spin) $s$ and a magnetic moment $μ$.

The magnetic moment of an electron determines the maximum moment of forces acting on an electron in a magnetic field with induction $B$:

$$M_{\text{max}} = |B| \mu.$$

For a turn with current $I$

$$M_{\text{max}} = |B| IS,$$

where $S$ is the area limited by the coil (see Physics 9). Therefore, the magnetic moment of the coil with current is equal to $μ = IS$. Magnetic moment is a vector quantity. Vector $\mu$ is directed perpendicular to the plane of the coil.

The magnetic properties of the electron are clearly revealed in experiments that were performed by Stern and Gerlach using the molecular beam method. This method studies the flow of molecules in the form of a thin ribbon, isolated using a system of narrow slits in the space in which the air is created.
