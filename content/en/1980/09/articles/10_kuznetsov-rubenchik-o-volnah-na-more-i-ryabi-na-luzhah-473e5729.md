About waves on the sea and fish in puddles

When throwing pebbles into the water, look at the circles they form; otherwise such throwing will be empty fun.

Kozma Prutkov

Of all the many types of waves studied by physicists, water waves are the most accessible to observation. Concepts of amplitude, wavelength, etc. for them are visual, and those that arise during their distribution are familiar and interesting. At the same time, many of the patterns of their propagation are the same as for light or sound waves, and therefore, by “throwing pebbles into the water,” a lot can be understood.

Waves on water are a very wide range of phenomena. This is the sea surf and fish in puddles, huge storm surges and the slow rise of water during high tides, ship waves and tsunamis crushing everything in its path.

The study of water waves began a very long time ago. The first theory - the theory of tides - goes back to Newton and is a direct application of his theory of universal gravitation. Further, in the study of phenomena on water there were periods of lull, and its own “golden age” associated with the names of Rayleigh and Stokes. But what’s surprising is that very interesting results in this classical branch of science were obtained quite recently. With their help, it was possible to explain experiments carried out in the last century.

In this article we would like to consider some phenomena that have arisen on the surface of a liquid, in particular water. Maybe after this you will look a little differently at the circles diverging from a thrown stone.

About the excitation of waves

Let us now consider how waves are excited on the surface of a liquid.

The first attempts to understand the processes of wave generation by wind were made in the middle of the last century. This problem was solved by the outstanding English physicist Thomson (Lord Kelvin) on the assumption that the movement of air is laminar, as if consisting of individual jets. Above the water hump, these air jets collapse (Fig. 3, a), the flow speed increases, and the pressure in this place, in accordance with Bernoulli’s law, drops. When the wind speed is sufficiently high, the resulting lift force overcomes the stabilizing effects of gravity and surface tension, and waves are excited on the surface of the water. Interestingly, the calculated critical speed turned out to be very high, ten times higher than the observed one. This stinking discrepancy between the results of theory and experiment has been one of the discussed problems in hydrodynamics for many years. Through various complex and cunning conditions of the theory, it was possible to reduce the critical speed several times. However, the paradox remained.

An explanation for it appeared only at the end of the fifties of this century. It is based on the fact that the layer of air adjacent to the water is not laminar. It consists of many small winreys (Fig. 3, b), or, as physicists say, it is turbulized. These vortices move at the speed of the wind. If the wind speed is less than the wave speed, the wave slope catches up with the winry, only them and, therefore, gives energy to them. In this case, the surface of the liquid remains calm. Then the waves are excited, they quickly fade. The situation changes if Winry catches up with the wave. They “push” it, give it energy and, therefore, damage its amplitude.

It is no coincidence that such a process of wave excitation is called resonant. And indeed, this is a general resonance. You encounter exactly the same resonance, for example, when a train moves quickly and tracks at junctions. The shaking frequency v is determined by the train speed u and the rail length l: v = u/l. If the frequency v matches one of the varon's natural frequencies, the varon swings wildly. The same is true when waves are excited by wind. Here the role of the length l is taken by the wavelength λ, and the speed u represents the wind speed, that is, at resonance, the wave speed must be equal to the wind speed.

Posmot, if the wind speed exceeds the minimum wave speed v_min, randomly exciting waves of small amplitude will intensify and grow. The calculated value of v_min is in good agreement with the observed data.

If the wind speed is close to v_min, waves with a length close to λ_min, which is several centimeters, are excited. It is precisely these waves that are excited in puddles at light

On the destruction of superfluidity

In conclusion, we would like to note the following. It is interesting that the reasoning given above is suitable for explaining a completely different phenomenon, quite far from a wave in the sea - the destruction of superfluidity. First of all, let us declare what superfluidity is. Superfluidity is the property of a liquid to flow without friction through narrow capillaries. It is inherent in the only liquid - liquid gel at very low temperatures close to absolute zero ($T<2,2\mathrm{K}$).

It turns out that waves also exist in liquid helium. At long wavelengths these are sound waves, their speed is constant and does not depend on the wavelength. As the wavelength decreases, the speed decreases, and at very short wavelengths it increases again. Thus, the dependence of wave speed on wavelength for gel is similar to the same dependence for surface waves (see Fig. 1). The most important thing is that both of these curves have a minimum.

Let us assume that the velocity of the gel in the capillary exceeds the minimum wave velocity. Obviously, then oscillations will be excited. In this case, the energy of the translational motion of the gel will be transferred to these waves, that is, friction will arise and superfluidity will be destroyed. Thus, the maximum speed at which the gel retains its superfluidity property is equal to the minimum wave speed.

It is curious that this phenomenon was discovered by the Soviet physicist P. L. Kapitsa in 1937-1941 and found its explanation in the work of another Soviet physicist L. D. Landdu in 1941, long before the mechanism of wave generation by wind was understood.
