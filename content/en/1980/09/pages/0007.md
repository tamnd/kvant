The theoretical basis of modern electrical engineering, radio engineering and optics are Maxwell's famous four equations. On this basis, James Clerk Maxwell (1831-1879) is rightly called the Newoton of electricity. Maxwell published his first work when he was barely 15 years old. At the age of 25 he became a professor at the University of Aberdeen, and at the age of 29 he became a professor at King's College in London. Soon he published one of his most important works, entitled “Dynamic Theory of the Electromagnetic Field.”

Dmitry Ivanovich Mendleyev (1834-1907), whom portraitists portrayed as a generally gray-haired old man with a beard, published his first scientific work at the age of 21, while still a student at the St. Petersburg Main Pedagogical Institute. At the age of 29 he was elected professor. Mendleev discovered his famous periodic law, which brought him worldwide fame, when he was 35 years old.

Pavel Nikolaevich Yablochkov (1847-1894) in 1876, that is, at the age of 29, patented an arc lamp - the first electric light source - which, under the name of “Russian light”, went around almost all the capitals of the world.

The foundation of all modern nuclear physics and technology was laid by the discovery of radioactivity, in the study of which Marie Sklodowska-Curie (1867-1943) played a major role. She was about 30 years old then. At the age of 36, Marie Sklodowska-Curie received, together with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel, one of the first Nobel Prizes.

Pyotr Nikolaevich Lebedev (1866-1912), at the age of 29, while doing research in the field of crystalloptics, showed a complete analogy between light and electromagnetic waves in the millimeter range (at that time the identity between “artificial” electromagnetic waves and “natural” light waves had not yet been firmly established). Further development of this work led him to a landmark experimental document proving the existence of light pressure.

The founder of the Soviet school of physics, Abram Fedorovich Yuffe (1880-1960), whose centenary of birth we celebrate this year, initially received a technical education at the St. Petersburg Technical Institute in 1902. Having become interested in physics, in the same year he went to the University of Munich, to the Znamenny Roentgen Laboratory, where, at the age of 25, he successfully defended his doctoral dissertation. Returning to Russia in 1906, Juffe carried out a series of brilliant experimental works that earned him worldwide fame. Thus, he proved that catalog rays represent an electric current and that the electric charge changes discretely (Juffe did the latter at the same time as Millick).
