The first step towards turning the model into a robot is assembling a steering control circuit, which automatically turns the wheels to the desired angle and automatically returns them to the center position when the control signal is removed.

The next step is to introduce a command decryptor. Various combinations of the four input signals (set by the positions of the toggle switches on the control panel) provide 16 different commands, such as “sharp left” or “slow reverse”. This completes the construction of Buster I - a simple mechanism, not yet a robot, but rather an improved toy.

Then the mechanism is further improved. First, a farmer of commands is introduced, ensuring circulation among the commands arriving at the input of the destructor, those that would specifically respond to the current situation. Here the so-called integrative function, characteristic of the human brain, is realized. However, one of the manifestations of such a function is establishing the priority of action. For example, the highest priority is assigned to the commands of the “departure” block, which moves the robot away from walls and other obstacles. The next priority is for the commands of the “hunger” block, which detects that the batteries are low and need to be recharged, otherwise the robot will “die a head death.” When the robot gets into a dead end and cannot get out on its own, or finds itself with low batteries, it sends a distress signal until help comes from the operator. He still cannot help himself. This is the stage that the author of the book calls Buster II.

Now the designer can begin to complete the stage - the creation of Buster III, which is characterized by the ability to perform tracking actions. The author limits it to two such actions: moving along the floor along the white line and searching for a charger.
