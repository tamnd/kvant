In equilibrium, the surface of a liquid is usually horizontal. If you remove it from this state (for example, throw a stone), a force appears that tends to return the surface of the liquid to its original state. The liquid begins to move, but does not remain at the same level, but leaks through it by inertia. The resulting vibrations of the liquid will spread over its surface - waves appear.

What force tends to make the surface of the liquid flat? Obviously, it consists of the force of gravity and the force of surface tension. But it turns out that the role of these forces is different for different waves. At long waves, the force of gravity predominates, therefore long waves are called gravitational. In the case of very short waves, the force of surface tension dominates, and therefore short waves are called capillary. A little later we will understand what “long” and “short” waves mean. For now, let’s look at gravitational and capillary waves separately and find out how the speed of wave propagation depends on its length. Let's use the dimensional method.

In the case of graviton waves, their speed can depend on the wavelength, the depth of the liquid, its density and the acceleration of gravity. (In fact, the speed of wave propagation also depends on air density, Lunyi gravity and many other factors. But their influence of weight is small and insignificant.) In other words, wave speed $v$ is a function of wavelength $\lambda$, fluid depth $H$, density $\rho$ and gravitational acceleration $g$:

$$v = f(\lambda, H, \rho, g).$$

From the messages of dimensions it follows that density should not be included in the answer, since it is the only one of all five quantities that contains the dimension of mass. In addition, the dimensions $\lambda$ and $H$ are the same, and the dimension of time is contained only in the acceleration of gravity.

Therefore, the speed of wave propagation can be written as

$$v = \sqrt{gH} \varphi \left( \frac{\lambda}{H} \right)$$

or

$$v = \sqrt{g\lambda} \psi \left( \frac{H}{\lambda} \right),$$

since $\lambda$ and $H$ are equal and do not stand out in relation to each other.

Let us consider, for example, expression (1) and specify it in two limiting cases - when the wavelength is small and when it is large compared to the depth. In the first case, they talk about short waves, or waves in deep water. In the second - about long waves, or waves on shallow water. Here are some examples. When you throw a stone into a river, the length of the resulting waves will be of the order of the size of the stone, which is small compared to the depth. Therefore, the waves can be considered short. Ripples in deep puddles, storm waves are also short waves, that is, waves in deep water. On the other hand, there are waves for which the ocean is shallow water. For example, the length of tidal waves is about the size of the globe, so for them the depth of the Marina Trench is some 11 km! Or another example is a tsunami, giant waves that arise during a “seaquake”.

In the case of short waves, the liquid moves only in a surface layer with a thickness of the order of the wavelength, so the wave speed should not depend on the depth of the liquid. Therefore, $\varphi \left( \frac{\lambda}{H} \right) = \alpha \sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{H}}$

$$v_{\text{кор}} = \alpha \sqrt{g\lambda}.$$

An exact calculation gives that $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

In the opposite case - wavelengths - the entire liquid is involved in the movement, right down to the bottom, so the speed of the waves should not depend on the wavelength: $\psi \left( \frac{H}{\lambda} \right) = \beta \sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{H}}$
