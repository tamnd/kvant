On the destruction of superfluidity

In conclusion, we would like to note the following. It is interesting that the reasoning given above is suitable for explaining a completely different phenomenon, quite far from a wave in the sea - the destruction of superfluidity. First of all, let us declare what superfluidity is. Superfluidity is the property of a liquid to flow without friction through narrow capillaries. It is inherent in the only liquid - liquid gel at very low temperatures close to absolute zero ($T<2,2\mathrm{K}$).

It turns out that waves also exist in liquid helium. At long wavelengths these are sound waves, their speed is constant and does not depend on the wavelength. As the wavelength decreases, the speed decreases, and at very short wavelengths it increases again. Thus, the dependence of wave speed on wavelength for gel is similar to the same dependence for surface waves (see Fig. 1). The most important thing is that both of these curves have a minimum.

Let us assume that the velocity of the gel in the capillary exceeds the minimum wave velocity. Obviously, then oscillations will be excited. In this case, the energy of the translational motion of the gel will be transferred to these waves, that is, friction will arise and superfluidity will be destroyed. Thus, the maximum speed at which the gel retains its superfluidity property is equal to the minimum wave speed.

It is curious that this phenomenon was discovered by the Soviet physicist P. L. Kapitsa in 1937-1941 and found its explanation in the work of another Soviet physicist L. D. Landdu in 1941, long before the mechanism of wave generation by wind was understood.
