⟦folio 8⟧

V. Boltyansky

Optics of black holes

From the editor
Currently, intensive work is being carried out to study distant space objects. The development of modern methods of astrophysical research makes it possible to accumulate enormous experimental material and expand the field of astronomical observations.

Along with experimental research, a lot of work is being done to create various theoretical models that explain the emergence and development of such, in many ways still mysterious, astronomical objects as supernovae, nebulae, quasars, pulsars, etc.

Black holes attract special attention. According to modern concepts, black holes are huge clumps of matter in a state of monstrous decline. All particles falling into a certain vicinity of a black hole are irrevocably drawn into it and “disappear.” No information reaches the observer from the vicinity of the black hole.

In this issue of the journal we publish an article by Corresponding Member of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR, Professor V. G. Boltyansky, in which one of the possible models of this phenomenon is described in a popular way. The proposed model was mathematically strictly calculated by the author; the conclusions that follow from this mathematical model largely coincide with known experimental data.

According to Einstein's general theory of relativity, the presence of masses changes the properties of the surrounding space, as if space is curved. Light passing near a massive body, such as the Sun, does not move in a straight line, but along a curved path.

This theoretical conclusion is confirmed by experiments.

For example, during a total solar eclipse, you can observe stars that, according to accurate calculations, at this time are located behind the edge of the Sun and should be inaccessible for observation from Earth. They turn out to be visible because the light rays coming from the stars, passing near the Sun, are bent (solid blue lines at figure 1). As a result, the stars appear to be at a greater angular distance from each other than in the absence of the Sun (dotted lines). The bending of a light beam as it passes close to the surface of the Sun is approximately 1.75 arcseconds.

If the entire mass of the Sun were localized in a very small volume (say, in a ball with a radius of 1 km), then with such a strong slope (collapsed) “Sun”, the bending of light rays passing through it would be more and more noticeable as the distance from the “Sun” to a nearby point in the light trajectory decreases.
