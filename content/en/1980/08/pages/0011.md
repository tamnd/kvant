Figure 2 schematically shows these curved trajectories: those of them that pass closest to the “Sun” can even make one or several turns around it before setting off on a further journey.

Of course, the reader may say, all this is “if only...”. After all, in fact, the Sun does not reach a size slightly larger than the Moscow Kremlin. However, this “if only...” is not so unrealistic. Theoretical conclusions made on the basis of the general theory of relativity show that the existence of a clump of matter in such a collapsed state is possible. Such a strongly rolled clot of matter - it is called collapse - behaves in a peculiar way: it attracts all nearby particles to itself (if they “had the levity” of flying close enough and at not very high speed), without letting go of anything, not even radiation. Everything nearby irrevocably “fails” inside the collapse region, which due to this received the name “black hole”. The proper horizon is the surface that limits the region of “irrevocability” and is represented as a sphere. This sphere is called the Schwarzschild sphere - its radius was theoretically expanded by the German astronomer Schwarzschild; it depends on the mass localized inside the black hole. Beyond the horizon, the rays of the own cluster behave as described above (figure 3).

The bending of light trajectories close to a black hole leads to a phenomenon that can be called focusing of light by a black hole. Figure4 illustrates this phenomenon. It is observed that, being at point $E$, not only direct rays from the star $\gamma$ come, but also those rays that, passing close to the black hole $O$, were to “turn” it.
