⟦folio 10⟧

Comes to observed $E$ as if from the vicinity of a black hole. Thanks to this, the black hole will be perceived as an object of extreme brightness. At the center of such an object, a small black spot should be observed, the angular size of which is equal to $\varphi = 2r/d$, where $r$ is the radius of the Schwarzschild sphere, and $d$ is the distance from observed $E$ to the black hole $O$. Of course, this black spot can be detected if the resolution of the instruments is $E$ greater than $\varphi$ (that is, the instruments allow two points to be observed separately, the angular distance between which is $\varphi$).

Is it not the focusing effect that explains the enormous energy of the giant quasars observed by astrophysicists? Aren't these quasars huge black holes that don't emit anything themselves, but only shine with someone else's light?

Another optical phenomenon associated with a black hole occurs if the black hole is strictly between the observed star $E$ and the star $S$. In this case, in each plane containing the line $ES$, there is a (theoretically) infinite number of trajectories along which light from the star $S$ comes to the observed star $E$. The two outermost of these trajectories are rays coming from the star, which are only slightly bent, passing near the black hole $O$; the remaining rays on the way from $S$ to $E$ make different numbers of turns around the black hole (figure 5).

Such a picture of light trajectories will be observed in any plane containing the straight line $ES$. Therefore, the observed $E$ will see the star $S$ in the form of a system of concentric rings, most of which have a visible angular diameter $2\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle that the ray coming from the star to the observed edge forms with the straight line $ES$. If the distance $ES$ is so great that the observed does not resolve (does not see separate) points, the angular distance between which is $2\varphi$, but only fixes the brightness, then he, unaware of the existence of a black hole, seems to see the star $S$ through it. In this case, the observed star sees a star much brighter than if it is observed indirectly (in the absence of a black hole).

If a moving star, during its movement, crosses the straight line $EO$, which connects the observed black hole (at figure 5 the red arrow indicates the direction in which the star is moving), then for some time this star will be seen as a super-bright object. This object will appear externally when the star approaches line $EO$, and after some time it will go out. Maybe this is what explains the rarely observed hail astronomical phenomenon - a supernova explosion?

We examined a mathematical model that describes ultra-bright astronomical objects not as sources of ultra-high energy, but as optical phenomena. How does one feel about this model? Is this science fiction or reality?
