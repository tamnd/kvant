They are visible especially clearly when there is a light translucent cloud between the Moon and the eye. Moonlight is scattered by randomly located cloud imperfections and will give a diffraction pattern in the form of rainbow circles.

If you are impatient to see rainbow lines, but for some reason there is no Moon in the sky, a diffraction pattern can be created artificially. To do this you will need pieces of cut film and a regular needle.

Run the point of a needle across the photographic emulsion - you will get a so-called diffraction slit. Bring the slit close to your eye and look through it at some bright light source (for example, the Sun or a lamp). You will see a bright rainbow stripe. Rotate the film - the multi-colored strip will also rotate, each time remaining perpendicular to the slit. Now scratch a small circle on the emulsion - the diffraction pattern will take the form of rainbow wheels. Draw another set of lines and see what happens.

3. Rainbows can also be observed using a drop of water. Place the drop on a stick or blade of grass. Insert with your back to the Sun and carefully lift the drop. When the rays of the Sun form an angle of about 42° with the direction of the eye - the drop, the emerging drop will suddenly flare up with a color extremely pure in tone. Which one? Anyone! If the drop is gently stirred in a circular arc, you can see all the colors of the rainbow.

One day, while picking mushrooms in the forest, I witnessed a funny scene. My son carefully examined the dew drops on the grass and on the pine needles. From the outside, his actions seemed somewhat strange: the boy slowly crouched, looking into a seemingly empty space. At this time, another mushroom appeared in the clearing, a quite respectable man. For a long time he watched in bewilderment the slow and squashy movements of his son, but in the end he could not stand it and turned to him for clarification. Now I looked with satisfaction at both of them, frozen at the spruce tree in awkward poses.

4. It turns out that you can make a small microjuice from a drop of water. To do this, you need to take thick paper, pierce a hole in it with just a needle and carefully place a drop of water on it. The microjuice is ready.

Bring the drop as close to the newspaper text as possible. You will see a greatly enlarged letter. The smaller the droplet, the greater the magnification. Now, after “setting up the device” in this way, you can examine any small objects.

By the way, we note that in the first microjuice invented by Leeuwenhoek, everything was done exactly like this, only the drop was glass.
