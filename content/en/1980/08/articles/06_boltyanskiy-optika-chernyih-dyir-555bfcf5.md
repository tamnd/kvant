Optics of black holes

From the editor
Currently, intensive work is being carried out to study distant space objects. The development of modern methods of astrophysical research makes it possible to accumulate enormous experimental material and expand the field of astronomical observations.

Along with experimental research, a lot of work is being done to create various theoretical models that explain the emergence and development of such, in many ways still mysterious, astronomical objects as supernovae, nebulae, quasars, pulsars, etc.

Black holes attract special attention. According to modern concepts, black holes are huge clumps of matter in a state of monstrous decline. All particles falling into a certain vicinity of a black hole are irrevocably drawn into it and “disappear.” No information reaches the observer from the vicinity of the black hole.

In this issue of the journal we publish an article by Corresponding Member of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR, Professor V. G. Boltyansky, in which one of the possible models of this phenomenon is described in a popular way. The proposed model was mathematically strictly calculated by the author; the conclusions that follow from this mathematical model largely coincide with known experimental data.

According to Einstein's general theory of relativity, the presence of masses changes the properties of the surrounding space, as if space is curved. Light passing near a massive body, such as the Sun, does not move in a straight line, but along a curved path.

This theoretical conclusion is confirmed by experiments.

For example, during a total solar eclipse, you can observe stars that, according to accurate calculations, at this time are located behind the edge of the Sun and should be inaccessible for observation from Earth. They turn out to be visible because the light rays coming from the stars, passing near the Sun, are bent (solid blue lines at figure 1). As a result, the stars appear to be at a greater angular distance from each other than in the absence of the Sun (dotted lines). The bending of a light beam as it passes close to the surface of the Sun is approximately 1.75 arcseconds.

If the entire mass of the Sun were localized in a very small volume (say, in a ball with a radius of 1 km), then with such a strong slope (collapsed) “Sun”, the bending of light rays passing through it would be more and more noticeable as the distance from the “Sun” to a nearby point in the light trajectory decreases. Comes to observed $E$ as if from the vicinity of a black hole. Thanks to this, the black hole will be perceived as an object of extreme brightness. At the center of such an object, a small black spot should be observed, the angular size of which is equal to $\varphi = 2r/d$, where $r$ is the radius of the Schwarzschild sphere, and $d$ is the distance from observed $E$ to the black hole $O$. Of course, this black spot can be detected if the resolution of the instruments is $E$ greater than $\varphi$ (that is, the instruments allow two points to be observed separately, the angular distance between which is $\varphi$).

Is it not the focusing effect that explains the enormous energy of the giant quasars observed by astrophysicists? Aren't these quasars huge black holes that don't emit anything themselves, but only shine with someone else's light?

Another optical phenomenon associated with a black hole occurs if the black hole is strictly between the observed star $E$ and the star $S$. In this case, in each plane containing the line $ES$, there is a (theoretically) infinite number of trajectories along which light from the star $S$ comes to the observed star $E$. The two outermost of these trajectories are rays coming from the star, which are only slightly bent, passing near the black hole $O$; the remaining rays on the way from $S$ to $E$ make different numbers of turns around the black hole (figure 5).

Such a picture of light trajectories will be observed in any plane containing the straight line $ES$. Therefore, the observed $E$ will see the star $S$ in the form of a system of concentric rings, most of which have a visible angular diameter $2\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle that the ray coming from the star to the observed edge forms with the straight line $ES$. If the distance $ES$ is so great that the observed does not resolve (does not see separate) points, the angular distance between which is $2\varphi$, but only fixes the brightness, then he, unaware of the existence of a black hole, seems to see the star $S$ through it. In this case, the observed star sees a star much brighter than if it is observed indirectly (in the absence of a black hole).

If a moving star, during its movement, crosses the straight line $EO$, which connects the observed black hole (at figure 5 the red arrow indicates the direction in which the star is moving), then for some time this star will be seen as a super-bright object. This object will appear externally when the star approaches line $EO$, and after some time it will go out. Maybe this is what explains the rarely observed hail astronomical phenomenon - a supernova explosion?

We examined a mathematical model that describes ultra-bright astronomical objects not as sources of ultra-high energy, but as optical phenomena. How does one feel about this model? Is this science fiction or reality?
