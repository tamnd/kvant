2) f has no fixed points. Take any point A; let $A' = f(A)$; let's put $\vec{a} = A'\vec{A}$. then the point A will be a fixed point of the mapping $\vec{a}^\circ f$ and we can refer to the first case already considered. We obtain (for some straight line l and angle φ)

$$\vec{a}^\circ f = R_l^\phi$$

Taking the displacement $(-\vec{a})$ to both sides of this equality and using the utf statement of 5°, we obtain

$$(-\vec{a}) \circ (\vec{a}^\circ f) = (-\vec{a}) \circ R_l^\phi$$

$$((-\vec{a}) \circ \vec{a})^\circ f = f = W_{l',e}^\phi$$

for some $l'$ and $\vec{e} (l' \parallel l)$. The theorem has been proven.

To formulate a similar theorem about displacements that change orientation, we need another definition. A sliding symmetry with respect to the plane π onto the vector $\vec{a}$, where $\vec{a} \parallel \pi$, is called the composition $\vec{a}^\circ S_\pi (S_\pi - \text{симметрия относительно π})$.

Theorem 2. Any movement f of space that changes orientation is either a central symmetry, or a symmetry relative to a plane, or a sliding symmetry.

I will not prove this theorem - limit myself to a hint. There are two ways: 1) take an arbitrary plane π, consider the displacement $S_\pi^\circ f$ and apply Theorem 1; 2) reason in two proofs of Theorem 1 (the same two cases, the same idea $A \rightarrow A' \rightarrow A''$).

We have come to the end of our story: now, finally, I can tell you what movements are like. There are six types of spatial displacements: parallel translation, rotation around a straight line, helical displacement, central symmetry, symmetry relative to a plane and sliding symmetry.

3. Given are the five bisectors of the five faces of a convex pentahedral angle. Construct its edges.
