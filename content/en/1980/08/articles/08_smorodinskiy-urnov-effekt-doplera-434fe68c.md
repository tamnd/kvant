Doppler effect

In 1842, an article entitled “On the colored light of double stars and some other constellations of the sky” appeared in the journal of the Prague Scientific Society. The author of the article, authorial mathematics professor Christan Doppler, proposed a new and unexpected explanation for the coloring of celestial objects. Doppler based his theory on the statement that the frequency of wave oscillations should depend on the speed of movement of the sources relative to the observable (that is, the device that perceived these waves). In other words, the same sound will have a different pitch, and the light will have a different color (color) for a stationary and moving source.

Doppler imagined the reason for the color of stars as follows. Light itself is white because it is a mixture of different colors in equal proportions. As the source moves, the colors will mix; for example, as the star approaches the Earth, the yellow color should turn into green and then into blue and violet; When the star is removed, the yellow color turns into orange and red. Since the eye is capable of perceiving only certain colors (from violet to red), and some color components, when mixed, “go beyond” the sensitivity of the eye, in the visible set of colors some of them are in advantage, which explains the color of the star.

For Doppler, as for almost all natural scientists over the centuries, light was a wave in a certain “luminiferous” medium - ether, which filled all space and all “pores” in bodies. The propagation of light in the ether was similar to the propagation of sound in the air (or in another medium), therefore light phenomena should have had an analogy in sound phenomena *). So the effect predicted by Doppler should have been observed for both light and sound.

Quite unusual experiments were carried out to test the Doppler principle. In the background, moving at high speed, there were musicians who made a sound of a certain tone. At several railway stations along the train route there were “observer” musicians who could determine the pitch by ear. As a result of these experiments, it was discovered that the pitch of the sound perceived by the observer does change as the source moves, in accordance with Doppler's predictions.

Nevertheless, Doppler's article "On Colored Light..." was supported by harsh criticism, and, despite Doppler's attempts to defend his point of view, his theory was rejected by a large body of known physical science.

Doppler's explanation of star color was indeed incorrect. However, it was based on the fruitful idea of ​​a change in the color (wavelength) of radiation as the source moves relative to the observer. This effect was used and it was called the Doppler effect. The correct explanation of this effect was made only after the nature of the radiation was understood. During Doppler's time, the significance of Doppler's idea was never essentially realized - neither by the opponents of his theory, nor by himself. Only Bolshano, a mathematician from Prague, in his country dedicated to the work of Doppler, wrote that this work gives much more than its general title, both for acoustics and optics and the whole doctrine of wave-like motion. Not believing in Doppler’s calculations, Bolshano nevertheless concluded her article with the words: “I expected with confidence that over time it (Doppler’s theory) will be used to resolve, on the basis of changes in the color of celestial bodies, the questions of whether these latter are moving, where and at what speed they are moving, what distances separate them from us and from each other, as well as to resolve many other questions.”

The possibilities of using the Doppler effect in physics and practical application were assessed much later, after the discovery of spectral analysis. Its deep connection with such fundamental concepts of physics as space and time was established by Albert Einstein. In his 1905 work on the theory of relative light, Einstein was the first to obtain the correct formula for changing the frequency of light when the source and observer are relative.

Since the testing of the Doppler effect in optics began to be firmly associated with the mixing of spectral lines, a lot of experimental evidence has been accumulated in its creep. It is curious that the first evidence was obtained by studying the spectra of stars. In LABoratory conditions, the phenomenon was observed by the Russian physicist A. Belopolsky, who carried out rather ingenious experiments with rotating mirrors (1900). The existence of the so-called transverse Doppler defect (which will be discussed further) was reliably established only half a century later (1948).

After the discovery of the quantum properties of light, the need arose to “reconcile” the already attractive phenomenon with new unusual ones.

Both sound and light are wave phenomena. Waves are vibrations that propagate through space over time. Sound (acoustic) waves in a medium (matter) are the propagation of fluctuations in the density of the medium. Light optical waves are the propagation of electromagnetic vibrations in space.

To describe any movement process, it is necessary first of all to select a reporting system - a coordinate system along with a clock resting in this system. The physical content of the Doppler effect lies in the fact that the frequency of oscillations depends on the reporting system in which the observation is made. Frequency conversion formulas when moving from one reporting system to another, moving relative to the first, provide a mathematical description of this phenomenon. It is extremely important, however, that these formulas are different for acoustic and optical waves.

Doppler effect for sound

In order to understand how the frequency of light changes when the source moves or is observed, let us analyze the following problem. There are two boats floating on the lake. From one of them, a device is lowered into the water, which can emit sound vibrations of frequency $v_0$. From another boat, a receiver is lowered into the water, which can record the frequency of the incoming sound.

What part of the sound will be recorded by the receiver in the following cases:

1. both boats are at rest on the water;
2. the boat with the source is at rest, the boat with the motion receiver;

3. Are the boat with the source of motion and the boat with the receiver at rest?

Let's consider each case separately.

1. The answer to this question is very clear: the receiver registers sound frequency $v_0$. Indeed, if the speed of sound in water is $c_0$, the thickness of the sound wave emitted by the source is $\lambda_0 = c_0/v_0$. The observer in the second boat, changing the speed of sound and wavelength, will record exactly these quantities - $c_1 = c_0$ and $\lambda_1 = \lambda_0$. So the frequency of the sound that registers the observed will be equal to

$$v_1 = \frac{c_1}{\lambda_1} = \frac{c_0}{\lambda_0} = v_0.$$  (1)

2. Now let the boat with the observed beginning of motion be along a straight line connecting both boats. Let us denote the speed of the boat with the observable by $v_h$ and assume that it is positive if the distance between the boats decreases. The speed of sound changing the observables will now be equal to $c_0$, and

$$c_2 = c_0 + v_h = c_0 \left( 1 + \frac{v_h}{c_0} \right).$$

The wavelength of sound in water does not, of course, depend on whether the object being observed is moving or not. Therefore $\lambda_2 = \lambda_0 = c_0/v_0$. On the other hand, $\lambda_2 = c_2/v_2$; means $c_0/v_0 = c_2/v_2$, that is

$$v_2 = \frac{c_2}{c_0} v_0 = \left( 1 + \frac{v_h}{c_0} \right) v_0.$$  (2)

So, if the distance between the boats decreases, then the receiver will register a higher frequency than the frequency of the sound source. On the contrary, if the boat with the observed person moves away from the boat with the source, then the frequency of the received sound decreases:

$$v'_2 = \left( 1 - \frac{v_h}{c_0} \right) v_0.$$  (2')

3. Distance is the case when the boat with the source is moving towards the observed one with a speed of $v_h$. The speed of sound in water is still $c_0$. The wavelength that will change the observed one will now be less than in the case when the boats were at rest. Indeed, the wavelength is the dis-

You can also write a general formula for the case when both boats are moving - both the source and the destination. This formula will be obtained by sequentially applying the two formulas obtained above:

$$v = \frac{1 \pm v_n/c_0}{1 + v_n/c_0} v_0,$$

or approximately, given that $(1 + \alpha)(1 + \beta) \approx 1 + \alpha + \beta$ at $\alpha, \beta \ll 1$,

$$v \approx \left( 1 \pm \frac{v_n \pm v_h}{c_0} \right) v_0.$$

It is easy to see that $v_n \pm v_h$ is the relative speed of the source and the target. The "+" signs correspond to their movement in opposite directions, the "-" signs - in the same direction. Therefore, the formula for the Doppler effect in the low-velocity approximation can be written as follows:

$$v \approx \left( 1 + \frac{v}{c_0} \right) v_0,$$

where $v$ is the relative speed of the source and application.

The general formula we obtained can be generalized once again - to the case when the medium of motion. (This generalization means that in our example the boat is not on the lake, but on the river.) In this case, the general form of the formula does not change, but the values ​​$v_n$ and $v_h$ should now be understood as the speeds of movement of the observed and the source of the relative river; in accordance with this, the value $v$ should also be substituted into the general formula.

And one more note. We considered the case when the source and applicator move along one straight line and the front of a sound wave propagates along this same straight line. If the direction of sound propagation makes an angle $\theta$ with the direction of the relative speed $v$, then the frequency recorded by the device is equal to

$$v \approx \left( 1 + \frac{v}{c_0} \cos \theta \right) v_0.$$

The change in frequency $v_0$ is determined by the projection $v \cos \theta$ of the relative velocity onto the direction of sound propagation. If $\theta = 90^\circ$, then $v = v_0$ - the so-called transverse Doppler effect is absent in conventional acoustics.

Doppler effect for light

In the past, it was believed that a change in the frequency of light when a source or object moves is described by the same formulas as a change in the frequency of sound. At that time, it was believed that light propagates in the ether - a hypothetical medium that fills all space, but does not offer resistance to the movement of bodies. For such a medium, the formulas derived for sound should remain unchanged, if only under $c_0$ the speed of propagation of light in the ether is understood. In the theory of relative ether, it disappeared, and with it the concept of absolute motion (movement in relation to the ether, as was previously thought) disappeared.

One of the main conclusions of the theory of relative light was the conclusion that no phenomenon can depend on absolute speed; formulas can only include relative speed. The formulas for the active Doppler effect, as we have seen, contain such an “absolute” speed - the speed of sound relative to the medium. It already follows from this that these formulas cannot be valid for light.

Indeed, in relative theory there is another formula for the Doppler effect:

$$v = \frac{1 + \frac{v}{c} \cos(1)}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} v_0.$$

In this formula, $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum, $v$ is the relative speed of the source and receiver, and $\theta$ is the angle between the speed $v$ and the direction of light propagation, modified in the reporting system associated with the radiation source.

If the speed $v$ is very small, so that its square can be transformed compared to the square of the speed of light (that is, $v^2/c^2 \ll 1$), then this formula is no different from the formula for changing the frequency of sound. The difference in these two formulas only appears if the quantities $v^2/c^2$ cannot be transformed.

Almost all formulas of the theory of relative light include many

$$\gamma = \left( 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2} \right)^{-1/2} (\gamma \geqslant 1).$$

This set - it is called the Lorentz set - determines the change in time in the driving system of the report, the reduction in the length of movement of its rod, the increase in the mass of movement of its body. It is not surprising that this set is also included in the Doppler formula. This becomes natural if we remember that the energy of a quantum is related to the frequency according to $E = hv$, and therefore in the moving coordinate system the frequency should change in the same way as the energy.

This explanation, of course, does not replace the derivation of the Doppler formula, but makes it clearer.

The existence of different acoustic and optical formulas of the Doppler effect manifests itself in the case when $\theta = 0$, that is, when the source emits waves in a direction perpendicular to its movement. For such a case, the classical (acoustic) formula predicts the absence of an effect ($v = v_0$).

The relativistic (optical) formula predicts the presence of a transverse effect. Observations of the double star PSR 1913-16$^*$ confirmed the existence of this effect.

$^*$) The number that denotes a star is its celestial coordinates: 19 hours 13 minutes - right ascension, $16^\circ$ - declination, PSR - catalog code.

Application of effective Doppler

If you ask a student studying mechanics how to change the speed of a body, he will answer that to do this it is necessary to change the time it takes the body to travel a given path. This is, in any case, what we do if we want to change the speed of the train, looking out of the Varona window. You can do it differently - change the path (the number of kilometer signs) traversed in a given time. In general, you need to “divide the path by time.”

The Doppler formula makes it possible to change speed in a completely different way. If a laser is sent from a railway station to a train, and a device for changing the frequency of change is carried into the train, then by comparing the changed frequency with the frequency for which the laser was built at the station, one can find the speed of the train relative to the station; more precisely, find the ratio of the speed of the train to the speed of light.

This method of changing speed differs from the standard, “school” one in an amazing feature. We do not need either length scales (kilometer stols) or time scales (stopwatches).

The Doppler effect allows us to discover one of the most powerful laws of the Universe - Hubble's law.

The American astronomer Hubble discovered that in the spectra of distant celestial objects - spiral galaxies - the known spectral lines appear to be an expanding nebula, in the center of which there is sometimes a pulsar - a source emitting a formation of clear radio pulses following each other. However, the model we have considered is plausible as a possible approach to explaining astronomical phenomena.

This means that as the distance to a galaxy increases by a million light years, the speed of its removal from us increases by 20 km/s.

The border of our Galaxy is located at a distance of about 2 million light years from us, the cluster in the constellation Virgo is at a distance of 35 million light years, the cluster in the constellation Hydra is 200 million light years away. An assessment of the distances to the most distant radio galaxies and quasars shows that they are billions of light years away from us (this corresponds to speeds of removal from us close to the speed of light - about 90% of the speed of light).
