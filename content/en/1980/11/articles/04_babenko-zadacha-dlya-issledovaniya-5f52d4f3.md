there are at least 1+f miks, that is, at least half.

Further, since the number of chemists exceeds the total number of alchemists according to the conditions of the problem, the number of chemists in the group of $N-2f=2(k-f)+1$ scientists must also exceed the number of alchemists. The number $N-2f$ is obviously less than $N$, therefore, by induction, there is a way to find out in $q_2=3(k-f)$ questions who the group of scientists standing is a chemist and who is an alchemist. Let us now select an arbitrary chemist from this group (obviously there is one) and ask him (this will take $q_3=1$ question) who the first scientist is.

If he is an alchemist, then those $t$ scientists who claimed that he was a chemist are alchemists. Therefore, we can only find out from the chemist we have chosen “who is who” among those $f$ scientists who claimed that the first scientist was an alchemist (this will take another $q_4=f$ questions). As a result, we will restore the complete picture of the division of conference participants into chemists and alchemists and spend $q=q_1+q_2+q_3+q_4=2f-1+3(k-f)+1+f=3k$ questions on this, which is what was required.

If the first scientist turned out to be a chemist, then those $f$ scientists who claimed that he was an alchemist are themselves alchemists. Therefore, we only have to find out from the chemist we have chosen “who is who” in the group of $t$ scientists who claimed that the first scientist is a chemist. We will address $q_4=t$ questions on this. Total number of questions $q=q_1+q_2+q_3+q_4=2f-1+3(k-f)$ —

Task
for research

a) For which natural numbers $k$ the following theorem is true: There are six points on the sphere so that the rays connecting the center of the sphere with these points form three mutually perpendicular straight lines. Then the sum of the $k$ powers of distances from any point on the sphere to these six points is the same.

b) And if together with these six points we take four vertices on the sphere correctly from any point on the sphere that was constructed, and describe all (or the most symmetrical) locations.
