Physical fireworks

The problem-questions published below are taken from the book “Physical Fireworks” by J. Walker, recently published by the Mir Publishing House.
Most problems are associated with various natural phenomena encountered practically at every step. To answer all the questions posed, you need to conduct appropriate experiments or observations.
The answers given at the end of the magazine, as a rule, only clarify the problem or suggest a path to its solution.

1. Magic propeller

The notched propeller on a stick is a wonderful toy that you can easily make yourself. Small transverse cuts are made along the entire length of one stick; At the end of it, a freely rotating propeller (Fig. 1, a) is secured with a nail. The second stick is passed along the cutouts.

Taking the notched stick with your left hand so that your thumb is facing you and your index finger is on the opposite side of the stick, begin to move the other stick back and forth along the notches. Teler press your index finger to the notched stick. The propeller begins to spin. Release the pressure of your index finger and press your thumb against the stick, while continuing to move the other stick along the notches. The propeller will spin in the other direction.

While demonstrating this trick to the uninitiated, discreetly press the stick alternately with your thumb and forefinger: the audience will be amazed at the unexpected change in the direction of rotation of the propeller. You can give the most fantastic explanations for this phenomenon. For example, say that a change in the direction of rotation of the propeller is associated with fluctuations in the intensity of cosmic radiation.

The first question to be answered is: why does the propeller spin at all? The next question is: why does the direction of its rotation depend on which side you press on the stick?

If you want to make this trick more interesting, attach four propellers to a stick (Fig. 1, b). True, the difference here is not great, because they will all turn in one direction. And one more design: two propellers are fixed behind each other (Fig. 1, c). Here it is possible that the straight line spins to the right or left, or, what is much more interesting, you can make one propeller spin in one direction and the other in the other.

9. Whirlwind in a cup of coffee

Gently swirl the spoon in the cup of hot coffee until the coffee begins to swirl evenly. Now carefully pour cold milk into the center of the cup in thin shavings. You will see a small vortex forming there. You may also notice a small indentation in the middle. If you jelly hot milk into coffee, there will be no whirlwind. Why does a vortex appear in the first case and not in the second?

10. “Interaction” of sinking objects

When several objects are simultaneously immersed in a viscous liquid, such as an oil or sugar solution, a great deal of interaction can occur between them.

Let's give examples.

1) Lower the two cylinders (Fig. 7) one after the other into the viscous liquid. At certain values ​​of fluid viscosity, cylinder sizes and speeds, the second cylinder can catch up with the first and go around it so that they are located at the same level. Then they will begin to rotate and, plunging, will disperse in different directions.

2) Drop one disk into the liquid, followed by two more. You will see that the latter will catch up with the former and all three will form a stable butterfly configuration (Fig. 8).

3) Now throw several balls (three - six) into the viscous liquid in a pile. They will diverge in such a way that they end up at the vertices of a horizontal regular polygon, which will increase in size as it descends.

How can this be explained?
