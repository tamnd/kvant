All-Union Olympiad for schoolchildren in mathematics not included in the Problem Book «Kvant».
8th grade
Problem 4

Dividing the quadrilateral ABCD diagonally AC, we see that
$$S_{ABCD} = 2S_{AMCP},$$
It is enough to prove the inequality
$$S_{AMCP} < a^2/4.$$

Let K be the intersection point of the segments AM and BD (Fig. 1). Triangles MCP and MKP have a common base MP and equal heights (MP is the midline of triangle BCD), so their areas are equal. Further, due to the convexity of the quadrilateral ABCD, point K lies between A and M, therefore the area of ​​triangle MKP is less than the area of ​​triangle AMP.
So,
$$S_{AMCP} = S_{AMP} + S_{MCP} = S_{AMP} + S_{MKP} < 2S_{AMP}.$$
Denoting |AM| through x, we get |AP| = a−x, $2S_{AMP} = x(a−x) \cdot \sin \widehat{MAP}$.
It remains to replace that $\sin \widehat{MAP} \leq 1$ and $x(a−x) = \frac{a^2}{4} - \left(x - \frac{a}{2}\right)^2 \leq \frac{a^2}{4}$.

Problem 5

Let us denote by $\vec{a}$ the sum of all 1980 given vectors $\vec{a}_1, \vec{a}_2, \dots, \vec{a}_{1980}$. Then the sum of all vectors except $\vec{a}_i$ is written as $\vec{a}−\vec{a}_i$ and, if $\vec{a}_i \neq 0$, it follows from the condition that $\vec{a}−\vec{a}_i = k_i\vec{a}_i$, that is, $\vec{a} = (1+k_i)\vec{a}_i$ for some number $k_i$. If $\vec{a} \neq 0$ then $1+k_i \neq 0$, so the vector $\vec{a}_i = \frac{1}{1+k_i}\vec{a}$ is co-peirent with $\vec{a}$. Obviously, this contradicts the condition that the middle vector is neocolipeiren. Therefore, $\vec{a} = 0$.

Problem 1

This equation has no solutions in prime numbers. One way to prove this is as follows. Let's assume the opposite. Firstly, at least one of the numbers x, y, z must be even, that is, equal to 2. Secondly, considering the equality $(3a \pm 1)^2 + (3b \pm 1)^3 = (3c \pm 1)^4$, we are convinced that it is impossible and therefore at least one of the numbers x, y, z must be divisible by 3, that is, equal to 3. It remains to make sure by substitution that then what is required cannot even be an integer (a distance of 6 cases).

Another solution to this problem can be based on the expansion of the original equation:
$$y^3 = z^4 − x^2 = (z^2 − x)(z^2 + x),$$
whence, in the case of simple y, we get two possibilities:
$$\begin{cases} z^2 − x = 1, \\ z^2 + x = y^3; \end{cases} \begin{cases} z^2 − x = y, \\ z^2 + x = y^2. \end{cases}$$

One equation in each of these systems is again expanded, after which we quickly arrive at a contradiction with the prime numbers x, y, z.

Problem 4

Let's put all the points in the order they follow along the shore of the lake: 1, 2, 3, ..., n (point n is followed by point 1). It follows from the condition that of any two “neighboring pairs of neighbors” k−1 and k, k and k+1, exactly one pair is connected by a flight, and therefore neighboring pairs of points connected by flights pass through an unconnected one. Let, for example, be points 1 and 2, 3 and 4, ..., 2k−1 and 2k, ... (Fig. 2) connected by flights. Obviously, it remains to prove the existence of a solution between any two pairs of points 2k−1 and 2k, 2l−1 and 2l, and this immediately follows from the condition: either points 2k−1 and 2l−1, or points 2k and 2l are connected by a flight. The statement of the problem is proven.

Note that our reasoning gives a restriction (a necessary condition!) on the possibility of the number of points: n is even and n ≥ 4.
