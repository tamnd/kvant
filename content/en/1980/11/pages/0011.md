Let us clarify that each of the two questions can be asked to any of the three persons encountered by the mathematician at the fork, and the question is answered only by the one to whom this question is asked. In addition, everyone he meets knows “who is who” among them and which road leads to A and which to B.

The solution to problem 1 is called to the thought: is it possible with the first question to select from three people the one who is not a deceiver? Then task 2 will be reduced to task 1 and, by asking the selected person the same question as in task 1, we found out the way to A. It turns out that such a first question can be asked, although it seems to the author that it is not easy to guess.

Let us renumber all three people randomly for convenience. The question is asked first. Here it is: “Suppose that each of you three now goes to A or B according to the following principle: a resident of city A will go to city A, a resident of city B will go to city B, and the deceiver - unless the deceiver is you yourself - will go with you; if you are a deceiver, then you will go anywhere. Under these conditions, will this person (pointing to the second) go to But?”

We argue that if the answer is “yes” the third person is not a deceiver, and if the answer is “no” the second person is not a deceiver. Indeed, if we asked a question to a deceiver, then the second and third of those interviewed are not a deceiver and, therefore, the third is not. On the contrary, his answer “no” indicates that the second person is a liar (after all, only a liar does not go with him). If we asked a question to a liar, then his “yes” means, since the deceiver goes to B, and the person telling the truth goes to A, that the second person is a deceiver, and the third is a person telling the truth. “No” means that, on the contrary, the second is a person telling the truth, and the third is a deceiver.

Having analyzed all the possibilities, it is not difficult to see that if the answer is “yes” the third person, and if the answer is “no” the second person depends, they are not deceivers. Thus, in both cases we can indicate a person who is not a deceiver, and thus problem 2 is solved.

Note that, as in problem 1, the main idea is to force the liar to make a double negative, that is, to ensure that his answers have the same meaning as those of the person telling the truth, and so that they can distinguish between the deceiver and the non-deceiver.

Task 3. N scientists came to the chemical concept; some of them are Khimki, the rest are alchemists, and it is known that there are more Khimki. A chemist answers all questions truthfully, but an alchemist always lies. A mathematician came to the concept and set out to establish about all the scientists which of them were chemists and which were alchemists. To do this, he is allowed to ask any scientist about any other scientist who he is. State a way in which a mathematician can figure out “who is who” in (N—1) questions.

Solving problem 3 is relatively easy. Namely, let’s ask any scientist (let’s call him first for definition) about each of the others. As a result, these (N-1) scientists will be divided into two groups: the groups of those who, according to the first scientist, are chemists, and the groups of those who, according to the first scientist, are alchemists. Let's classify the first scientist as the first group. Let's choose more from these groups. Then the scientists of this group are chemists, the scientists of the other group are alchemists (prove it!). Thus the problem is solved.
