Problem 6

Let us prove that the sequence $(n_k)$ is always bounded. Indeed, $n_k \leq n_{k+1} \leq n_k + 9^{c(n_k)}$ for all $k$, where $c(n_k)$ denotes the number of digits of the number $n_k$. Let us assume that for some choice $n_1$ the sequence $(n_k)$ is unlimited. Let us choose a natural number $N$ so that two conditions are satisfied: $10^N > n_1$ and $9^N < 10^{N-1}$ (since $10^N$ increases without limit, and $(9/10)^N$ tends to 0 at $N \to \infty$, such a choice is possible). From the unboundedness of $(n_k)$ it follows that, starting from some number $k$, $n_k > 10^N$, therefore the average number $n_k < 10^N$ exists for large ones - let it be $n_p$. But then $10^N \leq n_{p+1} \leq n_p + 9^{c(n_p)} < 10^N + 9^N < 10^{N-1}$, which means that $n_{p+1}$ starts with the numbers 10 and $P(n_{p+1}) = 0$. Consequently, $n_{p+2} = n_{p+1}$, $n_{p+3} = n_{p+1}$ and in general $n_k = n_{p+1}$ for all $k \geq p+1$, which contradicts the assumption that the sequence $(n_k)$ is unbounded.

Note that from the above reasoning it follows that, in fact, for any choice of $n_1$ the sequence $(n_k)$ is stabilized, that is, the value from some number $k$, $n_k$ does not change.

Problem 1

Since 999 is divisible by 37, a six-digit number

$$\frac{a_1 b_1 c_1 a_2 b_2 c_2}{a_1 b_1 c_1 + a_2 b_2 c_2} = \frac{a_1 b_1 c_1}{(999+1)} + \frac{a_2 b_2 c_2}{(999+1)}$$

is divisible by 37 if and only if the number is divisible by 37

$$\frac{a_1 b_1 c_1}{a_2 b_2 c_2} + \frac{a_2 b_2 c_2}{(999+1)} = (a_1+a_2) 100 + (b_1+b_2) 10 + (c_1+c_2).$$

Since the numbers $a_1$ and $a_2$, $b_1$ and $b_2$, $c_1$ and $c_2$ are included in the last expression in a symmetrical manner, rearranging the numbers within these pairs allows us to obtain seven more numbers from this number, divisible by 37.

Next we note that

$$10(100a+10b+c) = 999a + (100b+10c+a),$$

Along with $\frac{a_1 b_1 c_1 a_2 b_2 c_2}{b_1 c_1 a_2 b_2 c_2}$, numbers are also divisible by 37

$$\frac{b_1 c_1 a_1 b_2 c_2 a_2}{c_1 a_1 b_1 c_2 a_2 b_2}.$$

The previous permutations allow us to obtain seven more numbers from these numbers; dividing into 37 six-digit numbers, by permuting their digits we can obtain more than 23 other numbers; for example, from the number 123,876 you can get 47 numbers!

Let's suggest the opposite. From the fact that for any $a$ this equation has a finite number of solutions, it follows that for some number $M$ for any $a$ this equation has more than $M$ solutions in natural numbers. Let us take an arbitrary natural number $N$ and for each of the $N^2$ pairs $(x; y)$ natural numbers satisfying the conditions 1≤x≤N, 1≤y≤N, we calculate the value of the expression $[x^{3/2}] + [y^{3/2}]$. According to the assumption, each value will turn out to be no more than $M$ times, so the result will be no less than $N^2/M$ different natural numbers. $$[x_0^{3/2}] + [y_0^{3/2}] \geq \frac{N^2}{M}.$$
