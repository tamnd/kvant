⟦folio 30⟧

E. Gik

Sea battle

It is difficult to imagine a person who has never played naval battle in his life. Each of the two players draws two 10x10 boards on a checkered piece of paper. On one of them he places his ships, and on the second he seeks to remove the location of enemy ships. The flotilla consists of ten ships: one battleship (a ship measuring 4x1), two cruisers (3x1), three ships (2x1) and four boats (1x1). Ships can occupy any square of the board, but must not touch each other with either sides or corners.

After placing their fleet, the players begin to take turns “shooting” at the non-spinner ships, that is, naming the board squares - a3, b7, k9, etc. (horizontals of the board are designated by numbers from 1 to 10, and verticals by letters from a to k; see figure 1). After each shot, the player receives the following information from his partner: “hit” if the shot fell on the field with the ship, “sank” if this is the last field of the ship (that is, the other fields occupied by it were hit earlier), and, finally, “miss” if the field is empty. In the first two cases, the player receives the right to an additional shot, etc., until the first miss, after which the turn of the move is transferred to the partner. The winner is the player who manages to be the first to sink all ten enemy ships.

Obviously a shot in a naval battle is indicated by a dot, and when it hits a ship, the dot turns into a cross (in this case, the sunken ship is outlined in a rectangle). Of course, dots are also placed on those fields about which it is already known for sure that they cannot be part of any of the ships (they lie diagonally from the “damaged” fields or surround a sunken ship).

Obviously, the traditional shape of the board in naval combat, the type of ships and the composition of the flotilla are of special significance
