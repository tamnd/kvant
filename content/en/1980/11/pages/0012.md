It was supposed to find out “who is who” at the conference in a) 4N, 6) 2N-2, c) [3N/2] questions.

This task is incomparably more difficult than task 3, and again this is due to the fact that the liar's answers, although incorrect, are, so to speak, “constantly incorrect,” which allows us to extract almost as much information from them as the correct answers of a truthful person. The deceiver’s answers are arbitrary, and extracting any information from them is a more difficult task for the city.

The solution given below makes it possible to find out who is a chemist and who is an alchemist, even in less than [3N/2], the number of questions, namely in $q=3k$ questions if $N=2k+1$ is an odd number, and in $q=3(k-1)$ questions if $N=2k$ is an even number.

We'll look at the odd $N$ first. We will determine the required method by induction on $k$. If $N=1$ scientist ($k=0$) was present at the conference, then he is very/no chemist, since there should be more chemists; There is no need to ask any questions in this case: $q=0=3\cdot0$.

Let us now assume that for all odd numbers less than $N=2k+1$, we already have a way to solve the problem in the required number of questions. Let us indicate this method for the number $N=2k+1$. For the convenience of all conference participants, let’s renumber them randomly and start asking the second, third, etc. scientists who is the first scientist. We will stop this request as soon as one of two events occurs:

Sobtiye A. Among the scientists surveyed, the majority were in favor of the fact that the first scientist was an alchemist.

Event V. The number of scientists, it was argued that the first scientist was a chemist, is equal to $k$.

It is clear that if event $A$ occurred and by this moment $t$ scientists claimed that the first scientist was a chemist, and $f$ that he was an alchemist, then $f=t+1$. (Indeed, $f>t$, and if we assume that $f\geq t+2$, then the event $A$ must occur at least one question earlier.) It is also clear that in this case the survey asked $q_1=f+t=2f-1$ questions. (A special case of the event $A$ is the situation when the second scientist said that the first one is an alchemist - here $t=0$, $f=1$.)

If event $B$ occurred and at the same time $f$ scientists claimed that the first scientist was an alchemist, then the total number of questions asked is $q_1=k+f$.

It is not difficult to see that the poll turns before all the scientists attending the conference are polled. In fact, let's assume the opposite. This means that before the last scientist is interviewed, none of the events $A$, $B$ will occur. Let at this moment among the surveyed scientists $t$ a person speak out that the first scientist is a chemist and $f$ that he is an alchemist. Since the event $A$, $f\leq t$ did not occur. Since the event $B$, $t\leq k-1$ did not occur. Therefore, the total number of respondents is $f+t\leq 2(k-1)$. Adding the first and last scientists to them, we get that the total number of conference participants does not exceed $2k$, while there are $2k+1$. The resulting contradiction proves that one of the events - $A$ or $B$ - will occur before the last scientist is polled.

Let event $A$ occur. Then we assert that in the group of scientists, the state of the first scientist and all the scientists surveyed, the number of alchemists is not less than the number of chemists.

Indeed, if the first scientist is a chemist, then those $f$ scientists who claimed that he was an alchemist are alchemists themselves. Since the total number of scientists in the group under consideration is $1+t+f=2f$, the number of alchemists in the group in this case is not less than the number of chemists.
