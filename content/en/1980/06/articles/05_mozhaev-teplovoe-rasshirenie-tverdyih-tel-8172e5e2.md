Thermal expansion of solids

It is well known that almost all solids expand when heated. This is a universal property of solids. The relative elongation of most solids when heated by 1°C is ~10⁻⁵. To understand the nature of this, at first glance, obvious phenomenon, it is necessary to understand the characteristics of the interaction between atoms in a solid body.

Any solid body consists of a stable system of parts - atoms, ions or molecules - interacting with each other. We will not dwell in detail on the nature of these interactions, but look at their general nature.

For the stability of the ensemble of parts forming a solid body, the presence of two types of forces is necessary: ​​attractive forces, which do not give distance to such a system, and repulsive forces, which do not allow particles to move. The smaller the distance, the greater the attractive and repulsive forces. However, as the distance decreases, the absolute value of the repulsion force returns much faster than the absolute value of the attractive force.

figure 1 shows a general view of the dependence of the attractive force (a) and the repulsive force (b) on the distance between the particles of a solid body. Curve c is the result of these forces, which describes the nature of the interaction force. At a distance r = r₀, the repulsive forces balance the attractive forces, and the resulting interaction force becomes zero. The distance r₀ between particles corresponds to a stable equilibrium state. Indeed, the displacement of a part from an equilibrium position results in the appearance of a force that tends to return them to an equilibrium position.

To describe the interaction between particles it is common to use the concept of interaction energy. figure 2 shows a graph of the interaction energy versus the distance between particles. The numerical value of energy at a distance r between particles 1 and 2 is equal to the work that must be done to move particle 2 from infinity, where the interaction energy is zero, to a point at

walking at a distance $r$ from particle 1. When particles approach a distance $r_0$, the work is done by fusions of attraction - the interaction energy in this region is negative. When approaching from distance $r_0$ to distance $r < r_0$, it is necessary to do work against the repulsive forces - in this region the interaction energy is positive. In the stable equilibrium position (at $r = r_0$), the total interaction energy reaches a minimum value.

Near absolute zero, particles in a solid are located at distances $r_0$ from each other, being at the bottom of the potential well (in the energy sense of the word). When a solid body is heated, the total energy $E$ of the particles increases. This energy, absent from the bottom of the potential well, includes the kinetic and potential energy of the particles. The only available formulation of particles in this case is oscillatory motion around the position of equilibrium. The total energy $E$ of an oscillating particle in a solid, as well as the energy of gas molecules, proportional to the temperature $T$.

Let us consider the oscillations of a particle located in a potential well (in a section protected by figure 2) and having a total energy $E$. For simplicity, empty particle 1 is motionless, and particle 2 oscillates. At the moment of passing the equilibrium position (point $O$ on figure 3), particle 2 has a maximum kinetic energy equal to $E$. When a particle moves to the left from the equilibrium position, kinetic energy is spent on overcoming the forces of repulsion from particle 1 and turns into potential interaction energy. Deviation to the left occurs until all the kinetic energy of the particle is converted into potential energy. This occurs at point $A$ at a distance $x_1$ from the equilibrium position. At this point the particle stops and begins to move in the opposite direction. When moving to the right from the equilibrium position, the kinetic energy of particle 2 is spent on overcoming the forces of attraction towards particle 1 and also turns into potential interaction energy. At point $B$, located at a distance $x_2$ from the equilibrium position, all kinetic energy turns into potential,

We have described the general behavior of particle 2. Now let's look at it in more detail.

Let us turn again to figure 1. In the neighborhood of the point $r_0$ the dependent part $F(r)$ can be approximately, with a certain degree of accuracy, considered linear. In this case, the force $F(x)$, which arose when the particle deviated from the equilibrium position by a distance $x$, is proportional to this deviation and is directed towards the equilibrium position. (We believe that such forces arise during elastic deformations of solid bodies. It is from this proposal that Hooke’s law follows.) In this approximation, the change in the potential energy of a particle in the vicinity of the point $r_0$ is described by the dependence

$$U(x) = \frac{Kx^2}{2},$$

(1)

where $K$ is a non-actor constant coefficient, and the particle performs harmonic oscillations with frequency $\omega = \sqrt{K/m}$. The poseable approximation is also called harmonic.

Is it possible, remaining on the harmonic approximation, to explain the thermal expansion of a body? No. And here's why. The potential curve $U(x)$ in the harmonic approximation is a parabola, symmetrical with respect to the vertical $x = 0$ (see figure 3). This means that with increasing temperature, that is, with an increase in the total energy of the particle, only an increase in the amplitude of oscillations of particle 2 can occur, and the average distance between particles 1 and 2 remains unchanged. This means that the size of the body remains unchanged when heated.

So, we need to describe more accurately than the harmonic approximation the nature of the change in the potential energy of particle 2 in the vicinity of the point $O$.

In fact, the potential asymmetry curve—its left branch “grows” much faster than the right. To take into account the symmetry of the potential curve in the vicinity of the point $O$, we represent the potential energy of the particle in this form:

$$U(x) = K \frac{x^2}{2} - \beta \frac{x^3}{3},$$

(2)

where $x$, as before, is the deviation from the equilibrium position. By adding $\beta x^3/3$, we bring our model potential curve closer to the real curve. (As a first approximation, we replaced the actual interaction potential energy curve with a parabola (1); now we make the next approximation.)

The asymmetrical nature of the potential curve (2) leads to the fact that the maximum deviations of particle 2 to the right $(x_2)$ and to the left $(x_1)$ turn out to be unequal:

$$|x_2| > |x_1|.$$

Therefore, the average position of the particle no longer coincides with the equilibrium position - it will move to the right of the point $O$ to a distance $\bar{x} = \frac{|x_2| - |x_1|}{2}$. In other words, the average distance between particles 1 and 2 increases by $\bar{x}$, which means that heating the body, that is, an increase in the energy of its particles, leads to an increase in the linear dimensions of the body.

Let us now estimate the linear coefficient of thermal expansion $\alpha$. Let the body be heated to a neoctor temperature $T$, at which the average thermal energy of its atoms is $E$. Then for the left extreme position of particle 2 (point $A$ on figure 3) we can write:

$$E = \frac{Kx_1^2}{2} + \beta \frac{|x_1|^3}{3}.$$

(3)

(We have equated the total energy of the particle to its potential energy in the left extreme position.) Similarly for the right extreme position (point $B$)

$$E = \frac{Kx_1^2}{2} - \beta \frac{|x_1|^3}{3}.$$

(4)
