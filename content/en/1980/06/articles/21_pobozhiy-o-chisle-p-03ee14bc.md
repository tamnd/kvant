About the number P

What is easier, what is preferable - to guess the correct answer to a mathematical problem, then strictly prove it, or to look for it in complete ignorance? Preferable, of course, is the first one. Guessing is made easier by successful transformations. Funny example

$$S = \pi R^2$$

Such transformations are demonstrated by the figure placed here. Imagine, reader, that you do not yet know how the area of ​​a circle is expressed in terms of its radius. Let's try to guess the corresponding formula. Let's cut the circle into twelve equal sectors and cut each sector to a triangle, from

$$3R^2$$

throwing segments as indicated by figure in red. We cut every fourth of the resulting triangles into one equilateral and two isosceles triangles, as shown in figure. From the resulting triangles, add three squares on a side equal to the radius of the circle $R$. So, approximately, up to discarded segments, the area of ​​the circle is equal to $3R^2$. Taking into account the discarded segments forces us to take a slightly larger coefficient in front of $R^2$ in this formula. What number, slightly larger than three and related to the circle, could be useful here? Of course, $π = 3,14...$. So we guessed the formula for the area of ​​a circle with radius $R$ - here it is: $S = πR^2$.

For the sake of this, we would like to estimate the total area of ​​the segments, which is approximately equal, according to our agreement, to $0,14R^2$. As shown in figure, replace the segment with triangle ACD. The area of ​​such a triangle is equal to the product $2AB \cdot BD$, the factor is easily expressed through the trigonometric functions of the angle $α$, and they in turn can be estimated by taking into account that the sine of the small approximate angle is equal to the angle itself, expressed in radian measure. The calculations given next to figure give, with an accuracy of two decimal places, the value $0,11R^2$, a weight close to the estimate. After this, we have reason to believe that the area of ​​a circle with radius $R$ is indeed $πR^2$.
