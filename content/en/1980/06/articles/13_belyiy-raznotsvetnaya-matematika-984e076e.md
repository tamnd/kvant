Colorful mathematics

“The creativity of a mathematician is to the same extent a creation of beauty as the creativity of a painter or a pose - the totality of an idea, like the totality of paints or words, must have internal harmony. Beauty is the first touchstone for a mathematical idea; there is no place in the world for ugly mathematics,” said the famous English mathematician Godfrey Harold Hardy:

However, there are also more obvious connections between mathematics and painting: the mathematician very often helps... color. Yes, yes - color! Surely, more than once in your geometry lessons, your math teacher used colored crayons, using them to mark congruent segments, equal-sized figures, identical angles... Take a look, for example, at figure 1. There, the central rectangular triangle is highlighted in red, and six more congruous ones are highlighted in red. Now it is easy to see that the sum of the areas of two squares protected by green is equal to the area of ​​the square protected by it. The Pythagorean theorem has been proven!

But it turns out that coloring can even become a method for solving problems.

Let's start with a puzzle task: we will find a passage to the center of the labyrinth, shown in figure 2. To do this, we will paint over all the “dead ends” of the labyrinth, that is, those parts of it from which there is no way (see Fig. 3). Then what remains unpainted will be the desired path. Try this method on the maze shown in figure 4. Identify all the paths leading to the center; count how many there are and find the shortest one.

The following example calls the object knight across the chessboard and returns to the original square. She has many different solutions. But is it possible to bypass a board of a different shape? For example, a rectangular board measuring 7x9 fields? It turns out you can't. And “coloring” will again help us prove this.

Coloring a board of 7x9 squares in a checkerboard pattern, starting with the corner square: black square, white square, black square, white square, ... Then all the corner squares of the board will be black; Moreover, there will be 32 black fields and 31 white fields (for what?). But after each move the knight changes the color of the field on which it stood. Therefore, if the knight went around all the squares of the board and returned to the original square, then it would beat on the same number of black and white squares. Since there are more black squares on our board than white ones, at least one black square will remain unvisited by our traveling knight.

Well, is it possible to move a knight around a board measuring 4x8 squares? It turns out that it’s also impossible. A beautiful proof of this fact is obtained if we paint this board not in two, but in four colors - as in figure 5.

Note that from blue-colored fields a horse can only get to yellow-colored fields, and vice versa - it can only get to blue-colored fields from yellow-colored fields. Therefore, on the horse’s path, the blue field must appear to the yellow field and directly follow the yellow one: ...g - s - g - s - g... (see Fig. 6).

From the yellow fields the horse can move to the green fields. On the other hand, if a knight moves from a yellow square to a green square, then in order to move to a blue square, he will first have to play on a yellow square. It follows that in a closed route the number of yellow fields exceeds the number of blue ones (figure 6 will help you understand this!). But since in reality the number of yellow and blue fields is the same,

Another example: can a 6x6x6 cube be made from 27 1x2x4 "creeps"?

Let's assume that a 6x6x6 cube is made up of 27 "blocks" of size 2x2x2, colored blue and white and arranged in a "checkerboard pattern" (Fig. 7); let there be 14 blue “blocks” and 13 white “blocks”. Now note that no matter how we arrange the 1x2x4 “kripich” inside a large cube, half of the unit cups that make it up (1x1x1) will be blue and half will be white. Therefore, if a cube of size 6×6×6 could be folded from 27 1×2×4 bricks, then if this cube was “chessboarded” in 2×2×2 “blocks”, there would be as many white single cups in it as there were blue ones. In fact, we have 108 white single cups and 112 blue ones. Therefore, a 6x6x6 cube from 27 1x2x4 creeps cannot be folded.

“Any map on a plane can be colored in no more than four colors so that any two neighboring countries are colored in different colors” (countries are considered neighboring if they have a common section of borders); countries that have only a few common points are not considered neighboring). This problem was first mentioned in a letter to Hamilton by the English mathematician De Morgan (1852). Then it was formed from the rostrum of the British Geographical Society by the outstanding English mathematician Arthur Calley (1878). Since then, the problem of four colors has plagued scholars and mathematicians. For almost a hundred years it seemed impenetrable. As a result of many years of painstaking work, it was proven that any map containing no more than 41 countries can be colored in four colors in the required manner; but this, of course, did not solve all the problems. Only quite recently, in September 1976, in the journal of the American Mathematical Society “Bulletin of American Mathematical Society” a message appeared from the American mathematicians Apel and Haken, who proved the significance of this hypothesis (it is remarkable that the proof “took part”, and a very significant one, in the computer).

At the same time, at the end of the 19th century it was established that any map on a plane can be correctly colored with five colors (“correctly” means so that no two neighboring countries are painted in the same color). Try to find your own proof of this fact. But first, experiment.

1. Draw a map of four countries that could not be colored correctly with two or three colors.
2. Spend a few spicy ones; prove that the map they form can be correctly colored with two colors.
