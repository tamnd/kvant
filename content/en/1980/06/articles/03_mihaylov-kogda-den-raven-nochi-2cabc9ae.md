When does day equal night?

It would seem that the answer is obvious: the lengths of day and night are the same during equal periods, that is, on March 21 and September 23 daily. (It is interesting to note that in Latin, and from there in a number of European languages, instead of the word “equinox” the word “equinox” is used - equinoctium, which, of course, means the same thing.) It is on these days that the Sun, in its annual movement along the ecliptic, passes through the celestial equator, which intersects with the horizon along the diameter of the celestial sphere, so that half of the equator is always above and half below the horizon (Fig. 1). Consequently, during equalities, half of the Sun's daily path passes above the horizon and half below it; in other words, the length of the day is equal to the length of the night.

Let's check this by writing out from the calendar the times of sunrise and sunset on days close to the equinoxes. In this case, it is better to choose the year farthest from the high-flying year in order to reduce the influence of the uneven flow of calendar years caused by the inclusion of an additional day in every fourth year - February 29. (This addition is necessary in order not to diverge over time from the year of nature - the so-called tropical year, containing 365, 2422 days.)

First of all, we note that the moment of sunrise (sunset) is considered the moment when the upper edge of the solar disk appears (disappears) on the line of an ideal horizon, such as, for example, at sea. In this case, the center of the Sun is below the horizon by an apparent angular radius of the solar disk equal to 16', which lengthens the day by twice the time required to raise (or lower) the Sun by such an angle.

There are still other significant effects on increasing the length of the day. This is astronomical refraction. Let us explain its essence.

Rays of light from a celestial body, before entering the eye, are observed passing through the earth's atmosphere, refracting in it, as in a prism. As the density of the atmosphere increases towards the Earth's surface, rays are refracted more and more as they approach the Earth.

Let them be observed at point O (Fig. 2). Light from a distant object, such as the Sun, arrives at Earth along an almost parallel path. If there were no refraction of light, the Sun would be seen in the direction OS. But, having entered the atmosphere, the rays are refracted, approaching the perpendicular to the plane-parallel layers, and the Sun is observed in the direction of the tangent OR to the last element of the ray. In this case, the actual position of the Sun is point S' (BS' parallel to OS).

Thus, the Sun appears to be elevated above the horizon above its actual position by the angle of refraction ROS. If the luminary is at the zenith, no refraction of light occurs and the angle of refraction (or simply refraction) is zero. Refraction reaches its greatest value—35'—when the Sun appears above the horizon or sets below the horizon. Refraction raises the Sun above the horizon by 35', while the diameter of the solar disk is visible from Earth at an angle of only 32'.

The rapid change in refraction near the horizon itself leads to another curious phenomenon, which, apparently, was noticed by many readers - at the horizon the Sun seems to us to be noticeably continuous vertically (Fig. 3). Indeed, consider the moment when the lower edge of the solar disk is seen touching the horizon, but in fact is located 35' below. The upper edge of the disc is raised less by refraction, by only 29'. This difference leads to a distortion of the shape of the sun.

Let's return to the question of the length of day and night. Both of these phenomena lengthen the day and shorten the night: the Sun rises earlier in the morning and sets later in the evening than the “geometric” sunrise and sunset, determined by the moments when the center of the Sun crosses the horizon. The advance of sunrise and the delay of sunset are determined by the time required to change the height of the Sun at the horizon by 16′+35′=51′. This time, in turn, depends on the geographic latitude of the place.

The celestial equator intersects the horizon at an angle of 90°-f. When f = 0, that is, at the earth's equator, the celestial equator is located vertically. There, the Sun, in its apparent daily movement caused by the rotation of the Earth, rises in the morning and descends in the evening perpendicular to the horizon, traveling 51′ in 3.4 minutes (based on the calculation that a complete revolution of the Earth by 360° occurs in 24 hours).

In Moscow, whose latitude is close to 56°, the celestial equator is inclined to the horizon at an angle of 90°—56°=34°. Here the Sun rises at sunrise and descends obliquely at sunset, so its height changes slowly. In a straight line which is close to 56°, the celestial equator is inclined to the horizon at an angle of 90°—56°=34°.

It is gratifying to note that we are in the light season, when the day was longer than the night, longer than the dark. If we take a non-historic year of 365 days and assume that the day is equal to the night of March 18 and September 25, then the interval between these dates is 191 days, and from September 25 to March 18 there are 174 years, that is, the light season is 17 days longer than the dark season. This difference consists of ten days associated with the phenomena described above, and seven days, which are caused by the uneven movement of the Earth in its orbit. The Earth moves faster near perihelion, which it passes annually in early January, and slower near aphel, which occurs in early July. If you count the number of days between astronomical equivalences, then from March 21 to September 23 there will be 186 of them, and from September 23 to March 21 there are a year - a total of 179. But the first interval occurs in spring and summer in the northern hemisphere and in autumn and winter in the southern hemisphere. Therefore, we, the inhabitants of Sverma, are in a more favorable position, since we have a long and long cold and dark season, in contrast to the inhabitants of the southern hemisphere, where autumn and winter are longer and on average cold.
