so that the clock is transparent. But then both the clock and the mirror would have the same numbers. If the clock was opaque, then I would either not see what it showed at all, or (if I myself were sitting between the clock and the mirror) I would have to turn my head to look at it from the other side. Meanwhile, the condition only says “turned his gaze.” What happens to the book? And what happens to a person “through the looking glass”? Here two factors must be taken into account: (1) symmetry in space is impossible, (2) but two symmetries can be fulfilled simultaneously. And the real “face-back of the head” symmetry, by performing two symmetries, immediately turns into the “right-to-left” symmetry that we supposedly observe. Here's how it happens. The book, unlike the number plate, is opaque. In order to show yourself her text in the mirror, you must turn her text towards the mirror, i.e. swap its “front” and “back” positions (you no longer hold the book towards you in the same way as when reading: not with the text, but with the cover open). But we cannot change only the front and back: in our three-dimensional space it is impossible to make a reflection in only one plane, but we can make it simultaneously in two planes. Reflection in two planes perpendicular to each other at once is a rotation by $180 ^\circ$. So, instead of mirroring the book (which is impossible), you rotate the book by $180 ^\circ$, changing firstly the front and back, and secondly the right and left. As a result, a person who looks at the text in the mirror sees that the front and back of the book in the mirror are positioned in a normal way: the open page is in front of the eyes, as it should be, but the right and left have actually changed places. It would be possible to turn the book differently, so that the right remains on the right, but the top and bottom (Fig.6) would change, but usually no one holds it upside down. If this became a habit among us, we would perhaps be inclined to think that up and down are reversed in the mirror. But it’s unlikely. After all, there is another main argument: the man in the mirror. Does he really seem to have his right hand on his left? But here it’s the same story as with the book. A person cannot find himself in a looking glass. But it’s easy to imagine how to go around the mirror and stand on the other side. Then the psychology begins. A person is almost symmetrical relative to the plane dividing him into right and left halves. And it is asymmetrical relative to the plane dividing it into the front and back halves. The back of the head is located symmetrically to the face, but is completely different from it. But the left hand is quite similar to the right. Let’s imagine that Vitya stands in front of a certain plane, which we will conventionally call a “mirror,” but through which one can pass, and his twin brother Mitya passed through this plane. He stands exactly the same as Vitya (with his nose to the north, the back of his head to the south). “But,” Vitya thinks, “he is completely different from my reflection: after all, in the mirror I see my nose, and not the back of my head.” - Come on, turn around! - he commands Mitya. Mitya turns around... This is where the most important thing happens. A person cannot make a reflection (so that the heart is on the right), but he can easily make two reflections at once: after all, the result (composition) of two reflections is a turn, in this case a turn by $180^\circ.$ So, Mitya turns, i.e. reflected simultaneously in two planes: “front-back” (north and south change places) and “right-left” (west and east change). “Now,” Vitya thinks, “Mitya stood up correctly, exactly like my reflection. Only his right hand is opposite my left - in the mirror it would not be so. This means that in my mirror double, it is the right and left that change places.” No, dear Vitya! The mirror double's forehead and back of the head change places; You, comparing him with Mitya, did not notice that Mitya performed a double reflection. This is where your mistake comes from.

Did you know that:

1. The plane can be divided into identical non-convex heptagons.

2. If the plane is divided into convex heptagons, then among them there is either an arbitrarily small one or an arbitrarily large one. 3. There is an example of partitioning space into equal convex 38-hedrons, but no one knows whether the number 38 can be replaced by a larger one. 4. There is a set of three convex polygons that can be used to tile a plane in an infinite number of ways, but each of these ways is non-periodic. However, no one knows whether the number 3 can be reduced - this is an open problem. In space, the problem is solved - there is a polyhedron that can be used to tile the entire space, but only non-periodically. 5. It is unknown what tetrahedrons can be used to cover the entire space. 6. There are 3 types of convex hexagons and 14 types of convex pentagons that can be used to cover the entire plane.
