KVANT 2009/№2

Erdős–Szekeres problem on convex polygons

V. KOSHELEV, A. RAIGORODSKY

Parting words

The problem that we want to talk about in this article is rightfully considered one of the pearls of combinatorial geometry. The exceptional simplicity of its formulation makes a story about it accessible to a schoolchild, and the non-trivial nature of its solution and its numerous connections with other areas of mathematics immediately bring the interested listener to the forefront of modern combinatorics. This problem arose in 1934, and although the ability to count quickly is not, contrary to popular belief, a distinctive feature of a mathematician, each of us will instantly realize that this year marks exactly 75 years since her birth. Isn't it a round date? Well, let's celebrate it.

First steps

Statement of the problem

Let's consider some finite set of points X on the plane and ask, for example, the following question: is it true that in the set X there will always be three points that are the vertices of a triangle? At first glance, the question seems simply ridiculous. It is clear that if there are one or two points in X, then there is nothing to talk about; if X ³ 3 (the cardinality of X is not less than three), then it seems like any three elements in the set serve as vertices of a triangle. That’s how it is, but not quite: what if some three points from the set X lie on the same line? Then it is hardly appropriate to talk about a triangle with vertices in them. And a situation in which all the points of our set turn out to be “strung” on one straight line is absolutely no good. In short, the answer to the question is unexpectedly negative. However, if we assume from the very beginning that in the set X no three points lie on the same line, then natural logic will work and, under the condition X ³ 3, we can guarantee the presence of triangles in X. In theory, we would like to get an answer in the same form as in the case of triangles, namely: for each n O ¥, n ³ 3, it is desirable to find such a “boundary” value $g ( n ) О^{¥}$ such that in every set X that does not contain triplets of points on the same line and has at least g ( n ) elements, there are n vertices of a convex n-gon and so that for g ( n ) - 1 the described the property was not satisfied. Here g ( 3 ) = 3, and what are g ( 4 ), g ( 5 ), etc. equal to? In general, do these quantities exist (or rather, are they finite)? After all, it may well happen that, say, for any m there is a set X of size m in which there are no vertices of convex decagons. Who knows? The Erdős–Szekeres problem, to which we now turn to study, consists precisely in finding the values ​​of g ( n ). Below we will tell you a lot of interesting things about it, but now we will make a small note. To formulate the problem, it turned out to be very important to assume that the sets X do not contain triplets of points on the same line. In fact, such a property works very often in geometry, and therefore a special term was invented for it: a set X with this property is said to be in general position. The origin of the term is due to the fact that in some natural sense “almost all” sets on the plane are in a general position - hence the generality. However, we will not give more detailed comments here, otherwise we will go too far away from our main route. The main thing is that from now on, when considering sets of points on the plane, we will assume that they are in a general position: we simply do not care about other sets.
