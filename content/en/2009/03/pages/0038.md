Finally, your idea. You mean that we women will not be unfaithful to our husbands for the simple reason that those with whom we could possibly do this do not love us: they did not send us letters, they have someone better. And if no woman is unfaithful, then there will be no unfaithful husbands. - Excellent reasoning! – Can I ask? - said Diana. – Does this mean that I will be married to the only person I love if he has already written to me? – You can be sure of this! You will still refuse everyone else. - Why should I wait then? “Only so that everyone else gets their letter too.” – But can I tell my future life partner now that I chose him for the rest of my days? “I think you should tell others this too: then they won’t woo you and will find their mate faster.” “Then I don’t want to write any more letters!” – Diana said and tenderly kissed the dumbfounded captain.

I would like to believe that the reader understood the mathematical problem that lies at the heart of this tale. We hope the reader will agree that this is a beautiful solution and ask himself: how can it be used? And how to solve similar problems? For example, what happens if there are more women than men, or if there are men who would rather die than get married? And if women write first, will the solution be the same? If the reader thinks: “Is this true? Can I, for example, prove that everyone will find a spouse?” - this means he began to think like a mathematician. And then several tasks should be a pleasant addition to the fairy tale. 1. Formulate the problem strictly mathematically. This may turn out to be more difficult than expected. Don't retreat! Start like this: given two square tables of size $n \times n.$ Each row of each table contains all the numbers from 1 to n in some order... 2. Write a clear algorithm that corresponds to the solution in the fairy tale, and show that it always works. 3. Show that there are times when a problem has multiple solutions. 4. Let us now assume that the tables are infinite, numbered with natural numbers, and that each row contains all the natural numbers. Is it then possible to prove the existence of a solution if we assume that in each row the candidates are arranged in descending order of preference (in particular, the number of the best candidate is in the first place of each row)? 5. What if the order is the opposite? 6. Consider also such an algorithm for the initial (final) problem. Let's resolve divorces. Let's line up all the men. The first in line wooes all the women in turn in order of his preference (regardless of whether the next candidate is married or not) until someone agrees. They get married, and if the woman was married, she gets divorced first, and her ex-husband goes to the back of the line. Is it true that: a) such a woman will always exist; b) the process will end; c) will the resulting marriages be stable? Is this approach suitable for infinite cases?
