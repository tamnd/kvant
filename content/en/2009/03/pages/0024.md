– Would you advise our young readers to go into this profession? “I strongly advise them not to go there.” And those who want to, they will still go. They won't listen to my advice.

– You often give lectures. At one of them you had a juggler perform. – Yes, there was such a case five years ago. At that time, I was in charge of the French-Russian mathematical laboratory, and two young French mathematicians, specialists in combinatorics and representation theory, worked in this laboratory at my invitation. And it turned out that one of them is a very good juggler. Together with their colleague - they were friends and worked together for us - they studied the theory of juggling from the point of view of mathematics. And they gave an absolutely amazing lecture. One of them presented the theory, I translated it into Russian, and the other demonstrated the methods of juggling that were presented theoretically. There they proved a certain theorem for the classification of juggling of different types with different numbers of objects. I also want to say that the very famous specialist in combinatorics, the great American mathematician Ron Graham, was also a very strong juggler. He could juggle six balls. This is close to the limit of human capabilities. (Interview with Ron Graham published in «Kvant» No. 4, 1988.)

– You worked with A.N. Kolmogorov at the Physics and Mathematics School. – Yes, Andrei Nikolaevich invited me to the Physics and Mechanics School from the second year of its existence, and I worked there for about six or seven years very intensively. At first I taught exercises during Andrei Nikolaevich’s lectures, and then at some point I began to lead the next stream. There were two-year and one-year streams, and I had great pleasure working at the boarding school in the first years.

– Tell me something interesting about that time. – The initial selection of people teaching mathematics was carried out by Andrei Nikolaevich. And he selected only those mathematicians who, in addition to mathematical knowledge, the ability to teach and an interest in working with schoolchildren, had some other talent. We all shared our talent with our schoolchildren. There was, say, a teacher like Dima Gordeev, who was a serious artist. Zhenya Gaidukov played the violin beautifully. Andrei Nikolaevich held wonderful musical evenings, where he played classical music records. There was a wonderful writer Nikolai Ivanovich Gerasimov, who taught such interesting classes in literature that I always agreed with the dispatcher to arrange a schedule for me in such a way that somewhere I had a window during which I could go to his classes. The main glory of the FMS in those days was Yuliy Kim, the famous bard. He organized wonderful theatrical performances, now we would say musicals, with the help of schoolchildren, extremely successfully. They were a little risky. It was very difficult for the then director of the boarding school. The director was a wonderful woman, Raisa Arkadyevna Ostraya, a front-line soldier who graduated from IFLI, a person of deep humanitarian culture. And she had a very difficult position, because everything had to be orderly from the point of view of the Soviet government, while young mathematicians and other teachers

As for my own teaching at the boarding school, it ended with the party bureau of the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics prohibiting me from doing this. The same fate befell several other teachers, and about ten years after the opening of the boarding school, for one reason or another, almost all the teachers brought by Andrei Nikolaevich no longer worked there. – You wrote the book “How to Write a Mathematical Article in English” – This happened at the very beginning of perestroika. The fact is that I am fluent in English and have read a lot of mathematical texts in English in my life. I understood that at the beginning of perestroika, when the borders opened, our mathematicians found themselves at an extremely disadvantageous position. Almost none of the leading international mathematicians could give a report on mathematics, much less write an article. Very often, articles were returned with reviews of the following content: perhaps this is a very interesting work, but it is so poorly written in English that it is impossible to understand anything in it and that this work needs to be edited by a natural native speaker. And I felt that my role was to help people, i.e. my colleagues, Russian-speaking mathematicians, to overcome this barrier. And I wrote this book. This turned out to be a very successful experiment, I still meet people who say: “Oh, you are the same Sosinsky who wrote this book!” People who do not know me from my mathematical works know me as the author of this book.

– By the way, why are so many fewer books on mathematics being translated now? – In Russian? It’s clear why: firstly, professional mathematicians now read everything in English, they don’t care what’s in Russian or what’s in English. This time. Second. During Soviet times, we were completely isolated. Nobody came to us, after the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics stopped accepting applicants of Jewish nationality, the world community was outraged, and Western mathematicians almost never came to Russia. But we were not allowed to go abroad. That's why people didn't know English. But when the borders opened, it still made sense to translate books available to students, but almost no one translates monographs anymore. Although some publishing houses translate, for example, the publishing house MCNMO. In fact, it is Western publishing houses that should translate our popular science books; in this regard, as the song says, we are ahead of the rest...

– Are there really no books on the planet that need to be translated? - No, there is as much as you want, and it’s done little by little. But I will note that now there are wonderful popularizers of science in America: Sergei Tabachnikov, Dmitry Fuks, Grigory Galperin, Alexander Soifer... There are also their own, but there are fewer of them.

- Well, after all, there is still Gardner. – Gardner is a great man, there is no doubt about it, but almost all of his books have been translated.

– Maybe we are just closed in our own circle: we know the same Fuchs, Halperin and therefore we think that we are the best? - No no. Now, after all, Russia is a very open country, and in any case, when it comes to mathematics - both science and its popular science part - we are generally quite well informed.
