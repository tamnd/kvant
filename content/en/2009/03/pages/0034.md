Of course, if you limit yourself to superficially leafing through school textbooks, you will not immediately discover where the concept of flow appears. Yes, there is a flux of magnetic induction, and there is also a flux density of electromagnetic energy. That's all? Of course not. The concept of flow permeates almost all branches of physics, in most cases modestly remaining in the shadows. For example, when considering the movement of liquids and gases, the transfer of matter is what first catches the eye, but associated with it, although not so obvious, is the flow of momentum and energy. When an electric current flows, we first monitor the charge transfer, not always guessing why the conductor heats up. How does energy spread during the process of thermal conduction, how is it distributed in wave flows of various natures? Continuing a more careful journey through the chapters of the physics course, we will find in it many more interesting manifestations of the universal concept of flow. In addition, one has to deal with it many times and far beyond the scope of textbooks: mass and heat transfer in chemical reactors; streams of jet streams escaping from engines; pumping oil and gas through pipelines; streams of charged particles artificially created in accelerators or flying towards us from space; streams of light from the Sun, stars and lighting fixtures; ocean currents and the circulation of the atmospheres of the Earth and other planets... However, enough examples. It’s time for the concept of flow to come to the forefront of our “Kaleidoscope” - we hope that you will be interested not only in recognizing the participation of the concept of flow in a variety of problem situations, but also in solving them with its help.
