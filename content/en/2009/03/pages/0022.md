And I, modestly looking down, said that - yes, I was a little interested in some questions of complex projective geometry - and was going to say what I was doing. Bers spread his hands and said: “Don’t you understand, projective geometry died a long time ago. After Veblen’s work, there is nothing left there, there are no unsolved problems, this is a dead science, you can’t do this!” I became very confused and did not study projective geometry anymore. I realized how wrong I was when, as a topologist and graduate student at Moscow State University, I met Semyon Gindikin and Gennady Henkin, who, following Roger Penrose, did brilliant work on complex projective geometry, which was pointed out to me as a dead science.

– Could you briefly explain to schoolchildren what topology is? – Topology can be characterized in many ways. For example, this way. In Euclidean geometry, the main thing is the distance between points. In affine geometry, the main thing is the ratio of distances between points. In projective geometry, the main thing is the so-called double ratio of four points on a line. In Euclidean geometry, movement does not change the distance. But in topology there is no such simple invariant as distance. You can imagine that you are studying a geometric figure made of rubber. You can stretch it, you can compress it, you can deform it. And topology studies the deepest properties of geometric figures, namely those that do not change under such deformations. What kind are there?

– Are there dead sciences? – It is very difficult to pronounce a death sentence on one or another branch of science, but sometimes this is done. In my generation, we topologists pronounced the death sentence on general, or set-theoretic, topology. We all unanimously declared that this is a dead science. But there is no guarantee that she will not be resurrected.

– Is the death sentence still in effect? – Now former general topologists have switched to a more algebraic study of the foundations of topology, related not to specific geometric objects, but to very general abstract spaces. The classical abstract topology of the school of Pavel Sergeevich Alexandrov in Moscow, which flourished in the forties, has now gone out of fashion. There are only a small handful of researchers in the world who continue to work in this direction. But I'd be careful. Because you never know what might be useful in mathematics. It may suddenly turn out that a general topology will be in demand.

– What about set theory? – Set theory now continues to be at the center of the foundations of mathematics, of course. It plays a very important role, oddly enough, in issues related to computers, because the facts from the so-called descriptive set theory have gradually been developed into wonderful theorems of the theory of algorithms. Thus, Gödel’s theorem has deep applications to computer science: it follows from it that no computer can solve a number of problems in principle. In addition, a number of set theory constructs derived in the first half of the 20th century have now found unexpected applications in the theory of dynamical systems, in particular in connection with so-called strange attractors. So set theory can hardly be considered a dead science.

– Do you think mathematics should have applications? How do applied and so-called pure mathematics relate? – On this matter, I just want to give the point of view of Andrei Nikolaevich Kolmogorov. When I asked him this question, he told me the following. There is neither pure mathematics nor applied mathematics. Mathematics can be good and bad. Good mathematics can come straight from the tip of the pen, from the head of a mathematician who develops mathematics for the sake of mathematics itself, simply out of pure curiosity. But if what he does is meaningful, interesting and good, sooner or later it will find serious application. And if it’s bad math, everyone will forget about it in a generation. As for the results that arise when solving a practical applied problem, here too it is necessary to distinguish a strong result from an ordinary one. If, while solving some applied problem, a mathematician dug very deeply and came up with a fundamentally new solution method, then a theory will then emerge from it. And it will be as significant a mathematical theory as the best abstract theories that came from the speculative reasoning of mathematicians.
