William Gilbert breaks a permanent magnet into two parts, you get two new magnets with two poles - north and south - each. Neither Peregrine nor anyone after him succeeded in obtaining any one pole. This is the answer to the second riddle. Why this happens will be discussed in the second part of the article. And now - the answer to the third riddle: it is possible to magnetize a ball, and this was discovered by the English physicist and doctor William Hilbert. In 1600, he published the book “On the Magnet, Magnetic Bodies and the Great Magnet – the Earth.” In it he talked about his experiments with magnets. This is what he discovered: The magnet has different attractive powers in different parts; at the poles this force is most noticeable. A magnet has two poles: north and south, they differ in their properties; unlike poles attract, like poles repel. A magnet suspended on a thread is positioned in a certain way in space, indicating north and south. It is impossible to obtain a magnet with one pole: no matter how much you break the magnet, each newly obtained piece will always have two poles. The globe is a big magnet. When strongly heated, the magnetic properties of natural and artificial magnets disappear. Magnets exert their effect through glass, skin and water. But Gilbert conducted the most remarkable experiment with a magnetized ball carved from the mineral magnetite. It also turned out to have two poles - north and south, and, being near this ball, the compass needle always pointed to these poles, i.e. behaved the same way as in relation to the Earth. Therefore, Hilbert called his ball “terella”, i.e. small land. This word comes from the Latin terra - earth; in the Russian language there are many words with this root: territory, terrace (ledge on the side of a mountain), terrarium (room for amphibians), waste heap (heap of earth and waste rock), terracotta (baked clay), terrenkur (marked paths for therapeutic walking, usually in mountainous areas), as well as terra incognita - unknown land. Gilbert was the first to guess that the globe is also a magnet, which is why the magnetized compass needle points to the poles of this giant magnet. Does a compass needle always point north at one end and south at the other? It turns out - and this is the answer to the first riddle - not always. the traveler is far from him; usually no surprises happen with the compass needle. But as it approaches the pole, the needle begins to gradually “go crazy.” And the sailors noticed this long ago. At the beginning of the 16th century, when America was discovered and ships began to sail far to the north, a strange phenomenon was discovered. The sailors knew that on a clear night they did not need any compass: the North Star shows the direction to the north. But when they compared this direction with the compass reading, an amazing phenomenon was discovered: the further they sailed to the north, the more the blue compass needle deviated from the north! The angle between the axis of the compass needle and the exact direction to the pole (it coincides with the geographic meridian) is called magnetic declination. This declination is different in different parts of the world. What happens to the compass at the North Pole? It turns out that the blue part of the arrow points towards the northern part of Canada. There, at some depth, one of the Earth’s magnetic poles is currently located; the other magnetic pole is located in Antarctica. Why this happens is not known exactly. Moreover, it has been established that the Earth's magnetic poles move slowly. Thus, in Paris in 1580, the maximum magnetic declination was observed east of the meridian; it was $9,5 ^\circ$. And 230 years later, in 1810, the maximum declination in the other direction was recorded - to the west of the meridian, and it amounted to as much as $22,3 ^\circ$. This means that the Earth’s magnetic poles “walk”, moving from place to place. Moreover, very rarely - once every thousand years - they change places. Scientists do not rule out that this could happen again in the next hundred to two hundred years. And it is difficult to predict how this will affect living beings. Magnetic declination often has local deviations - sometimes very strong; they are called deviation (from the Latin deviatio - deviation from the path). For example, the famous Kursk magnetic anomaly is caused by deposits of magnetic ore, which acts on the needle. Small “magnetic anomalies” can be easily detected at home if there are iron objects near the compass. One can only imagine how many troubles and even misfortunes such “local anomalies” brought to sailors. After all, even on wooden sailing ships there were always a lot of iron objects: cannons, cannonballs, anchors. The situation worsened after iron steamships replaced sailing ships. In 1854, a terrible disaster shocked Great Britain: the Taylor, a vessel with a displacement of 2,000 tons, carrying several hundred emigrants heading to America, crashed on the east coast of Ireland. Most of the passengers and crew died. But this was a new ship, which easily withstood a two-day storm at the beginning of the voyage. The investigation showed that the culprit was an incorrect compass reading caused by deviation. It must be said that modern compasses have special devices for eliminating deviation. And here is the last riddle. If the blue pole of a magnet is called north, and the red pole is called south, then the blue end of the compass needle should point not to the north, but to the south: after all, only opposite poles attract! Why does the blue half of the compass needle point not to the south, but to the north? If the blue end of the arrow points to the north, therefore, there is the south magnetic pole of the Earth, and in the southern hemisphere there is the north magnetic pole!? Riddle four.
