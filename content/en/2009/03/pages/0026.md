KVANT 2009/№3

– Mathematics somehow expands all the time, different theories appear, millions of articles are published a year. How will it all end? Will mathematicians stop understanding each other completely? – Nowadays mathematicians do not understand each other well, and there are almost no generalist mathematicians who would be aware of everything. But surprisingly, despite the enormous expansion, ramification, and number of articles in mathematics, the best, outstanding mathematicians of our time are not narrow specialists. They simultaneously use many different pieces of mathematics in their works and seem to demonstrate that mathematics is a single science. If we take the last four Fields laureates, then Tao and Okunkov are absolutely versatile mathematicians; it is not clear what branch of mathematics their work belongs to. As for Perelman, he solves a topological problem, and it would seem that he is a topologist, but the main idea of ​​his proof comes from the theory of differential equations. He uses analysis, topology, geometry, and the wonderful works of A.D. Aleksandrov on geometry. And finally, Wendolin Werner is the first specialist in probability theory to receive the Fields Medal - but he is not a narrow specialist, he uses complex analysis very much, and combinatorics, i.e. his work cannot be attributed only to the theory of probability. But of course, the number of articles that are published on mathematics is becoming monstrous. For example, I am considered an expert on knot theory. Now I don’t have time to read even a tenth of the articles that are published on this topic. There is a specialized journal published every two months, which contains twenty articles on knot theory. And besides this, leading journals on topology and other mathematical journals also publish articles on the theory of knots and its applications, on the connection of this theory with physics. Even specialists do not have time to keep up with what is happening in their own field. This leads to various incidents when theorems are rediscovered many times, and, of course, this creates great practical difficulties. Yet, surprisingly, mathematics at its best demonstrates its unity intensely.

– Could you give me a list of unsolved problems - like what Hilbert did a little over a hundred years ago? Is there a similar list now? – Now there is a charitable mathematical institute in America, it is called the Clay Institute. It was created by a billionaire who studied mathematics in his youth and then moved into business. At the end of his life, he wanted to nostalgically return to his first studies. This institute gathered the best mathematicians in the world and invited them to select seven main modern problems in mathematics. This was done. The Clay Institute promises to pay a million dollars to solve each of these problems. Among these problems is the Poincaré conjecture, which we just discussed.

– But for some reason the Clay Institute has not yet paid this million to Perelman. – The fact is that it is impossible to pay this money now, because according to the rules, two years must pass after publication, and there is a problem with publication, because a text posted on the Internet is not considered publication. It turns out that Perelman has no journal publications on this topic at all.

– Doesn’t he want to be published? – He is not going to publish his work in magazines. Perelman was tortured by unscrupulous journalists who, for the sake of sensation or out of incompetence, confused the Fields Medal with the Clay Prize; they wrote that Perelman refused a million dollars. He didn’t refuse - no one gave him a million

– When I asked the question, I had something else in mind. I would like to tell readers «Kvant» about unsolved problems from the list of the Clay Institute. – Among these tasks, most cannot be formulated for readers «Kvant», because they require too much preparation. There is, however, one that, strictly speaking, relates not so much to mathematics as to theoretical computer science, and is formulated very briefly: N №NP, and the meaning of this formula can be explained to readers «Kvant». In addition, Clay's list of problems did not include perhaps the most famous problem from number theory - the Goldbach conjecture (that any even number greater than two is the sum of two prime numbers), which is easy to explain to any schoolchild, not just to readers of «Kvant».

– What other unsolved problems, not necessarily old ones, could you name? – I’ll try to formulate a couple more of these problems. The first is this: let there be a mechanism in space consisting of rods connected by hinges. For example, the frame of a tetrahedron or cube. It is necessary to find necessary and sufficient conditions for the rigidity of such a mechanism. (So, a tetrahedron is rigid, a cube is not, it bends into a parallelepiped.) This problem is very difficult, and therefore even partial progress towards its solution is of interest. Here's the second: come up with a "normal shape" for the nodes, along with continuous bending, bringing each node to its normal shape. Here, too, partial solutions are of interest. The special case of trivial knots (the untangling problem) is already very difficult; perhaps some particular results can be obtained using the so-called “knot energy” (we could also write about it for «Kvant»).

– Please give examples of one or two beautiful elementary problems that you like. - Please, as much as you like. First, I will name the problem for first grade. This is the task. Masha has eight more apples than Sasha. Masha gave Sasha one apple. How many more apples does she have now? One of my favorite tasks is that there is a river or lake with a straight bank, the rider is at point A. He must get from point A to point B and water the horse along the way. What should his shortest path be? Another example: from six matches, create a graph with six edges, which contains four triangles.

– Do you have a favorite quantum article? – My favorite article in the magazine «Kvant», oddly enough, is tiny. This is an article by Volodya Dubrovsky on how to build surfaces of negative curvature using a constructor. An absolutely wonderful article (see «Kvant» No. 7, 1979). This time. I really love Nikolai Borisovich Vasiliev’s article on algebraic curves, “Pascal’s Hexagrams and Cubic Curves” («Kvant» No. 8, 1987).
