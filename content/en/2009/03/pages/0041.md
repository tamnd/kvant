Mysteries of the magnetic needle

I.LEENSON

IT IS DIFFICULT TO FIND A PERSON WHO HAS NEVER SEEN A COMPASS. This simple device is necessary for a traveler, a hunter, and even a mushroom picker going for mushrooms in an unfamiliar forest. What secrets can such a device hide? The blue end of the compass needle points to the north, the red end points to the south - it would seem that this is the whole secret. It turns out that not everything is so simple, and this is just the beginning of our series of riddles about the magnetic needle. The first riddle. Does the blue end of the compass needle always point north? Next. Many people have seen horseshoe or bar magnets. If there is no strip magnet, but there is a strong horseshoe-shaped one, it can be used to easily magnetize a steel strip (or just a long needle), turning it into a strip magnet. The ends of the magnet where the strongest magnetic effects are found are called the poles of the magnet. The word “pole” comes from the Greek pólos – axis, so in what follows, instead of “end of an arrow” or “end of a magnet” we will say “pole”. Each magnet, like the globe, has two poles: north and south, only these poles are called magnetic. According to tradition, the half of the magnetic needle that points north is painted blue and its end is called the north pole of the magnet; it is designated by the letter N. The same half of the magnetic needle, which points to the south, is painted red and its end is called the south pole of the magnet; it is designated by the letter S. Any permanent magnets are colored and designated in the same way. This designation came into the Russian language from Dutch during the reign of Peter I. What does the Dutch have to do with it? The fact is that Holland was once the mistress of the seas and competed with England. So, on August 21, 1673, a naval battle took place near the Dutch island of Texel between the Dutch and the combined Anglo-French fleets; at this time the third Anglo-Dutch war was already underway (1672–1674). In a battle that lasted all day, the Dutch won, although the numerical superiority was on the enemy's side. Holland captured vast colonies at that time, especially in Southeast Asia. To this day, in the state of Suriname in South America, Dutch is the official language. If you have two magnets or one magnet and a compass (its needle is the same magnet, only small and light), then it is easy to see that the blue pole of one magnet attracts the red pole of the other, and identically colored poles repel each other. If we hang a strip magnet exactly by its middle on a thread away from iron objects, tie the thread to something and wait until the magnet stops swinging, then we will see that the strip has turned in the same way as the compass needle. Indeed, how does a compass needle know where the Earth is north and where south? It turns out that the Earth is the same magnet, only spherical. People did not immediately come to this understanding (just as they did not immediately realize that our Earth is not flat, but spherical). Here's how it happened. The ancient Greek philosopher Thales in the 6th century BC described an amazing phenomenon that was probably known to people long before that. This phenomenon is magnetism. According to one legend, it received its name from the Greek city of Magnesia (now called Manissa and located in Turkey), where a mineral capable of attracting iron was found. Now this mineral is called magnetite, or magnetic iron ore; it is a compound of iron and oxygen. But who and when invented the compass is unknown, although this invention is one of man’s greatest discoveries. Perhaps someone accidentally discovered that if an elongated piece of magnetite is hung on a thread or allowed to float on a wooden plank, then one end will always point to the north and the other to the south, no matter how we twist the thread or plank. It was previously believed that the compass was invented by the Chinese almost five thousand years ago, but the first Chinese document that mentions a compass dates back to the 11th century AD. In any case, back in the Middle Ages, this ancient discovery was widely used by sailors, determining their path on the open sea using a primitive compass. Over time, it was discovered that a piece of magnetite could transfer its “magical” property to steel objects - for example, a needle, and it would make a more convenient compass. In 1269, the Frenchman Pierre de Mericourt, nicknamed Peregrine, wrote the book “Letters on the Magnet,” in which he collected various information about the magnet, both previously known and discovered by him personally. It was Peregrine, experimenting with magnets, who discovered that they have two special ends - two poles. Since they pointed north and south, they were called the north and south poles of the magnet. When Peregrine hung two magnetic needles close to each other on threads, it turned out that their like poles repel, and their opposite poles attract.
