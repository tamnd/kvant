– But why then does China get first place at the international Olympiad, and all the time? Or does this phenomenon have completely different reasons? – Firstly, not all the time, sometimes we do too. Last summer, for example. But I want to say the following: the results at the Olympiads do not correlate at all with the level of popularization of mathematics in a given country. And in general, from my point of view, the international Olympiads have deteriorated, and for a very long time. This is why it happened. Leading countries, i.e. those countries that have traditionally taken high places at international olympiads have developed an extremely effective system for training schoolchildren to perform in olympiads. The Olympics have become a political event. When the Americans won the Olympics, they were invited to the White House and the president shook their hand. It's the same with us. This became a very important political act. And leading countries have learned to train Olympiad athletes. They have been especially successful in this in China. The choice is huge, the Chinese are very hardworking, very smart, very capable of mathematics. But at the same time, the leaders of these delegations – i.e. those who select tasks for the Olympiad began to act based on expediency, i.e. select those problems for which they were confident that their students would be able to solve them. And therefore, preference is now given to technically complex problems that use already known tricky mathematical techniques and about which leaders know that their students will solve them accurately. And when suddenly a seemingly simple task appears, the solution of which requires new ideas, they are afraid: what if our people don’t figure it out?

– Is combinatorics a science? – Israel Moiseevich Gelfand, speaking at his seminar about 25 years ago, said that mathematical science consists of the following parts: analysis, algebra, geometry and combinatorics, and the most important of these sciences is combinatorics, but it does not exist yet.

– And this remains fair? – No, a lot of people in the world study combinatorics, including leading mathematicians. For example, if you take the last Congress in Madrid, two of the four Fields Medals were won by mathematicians who rely heavily on combinatorics or actually do combinatorics. These are the outstanding American mathematician of Chinese origin Terence Tao and our Andrei Okunkov. In their work there is an amazing symbiosis between combinatorics and other sciences. For example, Tao may have combinatorics in the first place, but he considers himself a specialist in harmonic analysis and heavily uses probability theory to prove elementary theorems from number theory! Its main result - the existence of arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions made up of prime numbers - is clear to any reader «Kvant», and the proof uses an extremely diverse and complex arsenal of mathematical concepts and, in particular, combinatorics plays an almost central role in it. By the way, here is another topic for publication in «Kvant».

Poincaré's conjecture is related to the characterization of a sphere. How can you characterize a two-dimensional sphere? If we draw any closed path on a two-dimensional sphere, then it can be contracted continuously to a point. Poincaré conjectured that a connected compact space, locally structured as three-dimensional Euclidean space, will be a three-dimensional sphere if and only if it has the same property - that any closed curve contracts to a point. This hypothesis is actually not that significant, but it has gained great interest among mathematicians because many great mathematicians tried to prove it and for a long time they could not do it. The situation is similar to the situation with Fermat's theorem. Fermat's theorem, strictly speaking, is a completely unimportant small statement from number theory, it does not have any great mathematical significance, but it acquired enormous historical significance because a bunch of brilliant people tried to prove it, but no one succeeded before the English mathematician Andrew Wiles. Returning to the Poincaré conjecture, I note that the American mathematician William Thurston came up with some other hypothesis, which is called the geometrization hypothesis. At the same time, Thurston proved that the Poincaré conjecture follows from the geometrization hypothesis. And the geometrization hypothesis has enormous conceptual significance for all mathematics. It states that a certain collection of three-dimensional objects has a geometric structure. These objects are defined topologically and yet they have a geometric structure. It was the geometrization hypothesis that Grigory Perelman proved. This is truly an exceptional work, a brilliant work that uses analysis, topology, geometry. Perelman published three articles in succession, but on the Internet, not in magazines. The texts of these works were quite short. At first, there was not a single specialist in the world who could check everything. And some said that not everything had been proven there, that it had not been fully done, but when they began to look into it, they gradually came to the conclusion that this was not so. Only the details are missing. Parts in each specific location can be restored by a specialist in the field. By the way, I know that Grisha Perelman solved problems from “Problem Book «Kvant».

– Is it true that mathematicians are divided into two types: those who like to solve specific problems, and those who like to develop theories, or is this very conditional? – To some extent this is true, i.e. There are both solvers and theory builders, but if you look at major mathematicians, they most often manifest themselves in both forms. It would be more accurate to say that most mathematicians gravitate towards one or the other, but the example of the greatest mathematicians shows that there are both sides. The only striking exception is Erdős, who did not create a single theory and spent his entire life solving problems. In this regard, in my opinion, he stands apart.
