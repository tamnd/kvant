Mysteries of the magnetic needle

I.LEENSON

IT IS DIFFICULT TO FIND A PERSON WHO HAS NEVER SEEN A COMPASS. This simple device is necessary for a traveler, a hunter, and even a mushroom picker going for mushrooms in an unfamiliar forest. What secrets can such a device hide? The blue end of the compass needle points to the north, the red end points to the south - it would seem that this is the whole secret. It turns out that not everything is so simple, and this is just the beginning of our series of riddles about the magnetic needle. The first riddle. Does the blue end of the compass needle always point north? Next. Many people have seen horseshoe or bar magnets. If there is no strip magnet, but there is a strong horseshoe-shaped one, it can be used to easily magnetize a steel strip (or just a long needle), turning it into a strip magnet. The ends of the magnet where the strongest magnetic effects are found are called the poles of the magnet. The word “pole” comes from the Greek pólos – axis, so in what follows, instead of “end of an arrow” or “end of a magnet” we will say “pole”. Each magnet, like the globe, has two poles: north and south, only these poles are called magnetic. According to tradition, the half of the magnetic needle that points north is painted blue and its end is called the north pole of the magnet; it is designated by the letter N. The same half of the magnetic needle, which points to the south, is painted red and its end is called the south pole of the magnet; it is designated by the letter S. Any permanent magnets are colored and designated in the same way. This designation came into the Russian language from Dutch during the reign of Peter I. What does the Dutch have to do with it? The fact is that Holland was once the mistress of the seas and competed with England. So, on August 21, 1673, a naval battle took place near the Dutch island of Texel between the Dutch and the combined Anglo-French fleets; at this time the third Anglo-Dutch war was already underway (1672–1674). In a battle that lasted all day, the Dutch won, although the numerical superiority was on the enemy's side. Holland captured vast colonies at that time, especially in Southeast Asia. To this day, in the state of Suriname in South America, Dutch is the official language. If you have two magnets or one magnet and a compass (its needle is the same magnet, only small and light), then it is easy to see that the blue pole of one magnet attracts the red pole of the other, and identically colored poles repel each other. If we hang a strip magnet exactly by its middle on a thread away from iron objects, tie the thread to something and wait until the magnet stops swinging, then we will see that the strip has turned in the same way as the compass needle. What happens if a magnetized strip is cut in half exactly in the middle? And one more mystery. Riddle three. Is it possible to magnetize a ball? If there are riddles, there must be solutions. Indeed, how does a compass needle know where the Earth is north and where south? It turns out that the Earth is the same magnet, only spherical. People did not immediately come to this understanding (just as they did not immediately realize that our Earth is not flat, but spherical). Here's how it happened. The ancient Greek philosopher Thales in the 6th century BC described an amazing phenomenon that was probably known to people long before that. This phenomenon is magnetism. According to one legend, it received its name from the Greek city of Magnesia (now called Manissa and located in Turkey), where a mineral capable of attracting iron was found. Now this mineral is called magnetite, or magnetic iron ore; it is a compound of iron and oxygen. But who and when invented the compass is unknown, although this invention is one of man’s greatest discoveries. Perhaps someone accidentally discovered that if an elongated piece of magnetite is hung on a thread or allowed to float on a wooden plank, then one end will always point to the north and the other to the south, no matter how we twist the thread or plank. It was previously believed that the compass was invented by the Chinese almost five thousand years ago, but the first Chinese document that mentions a compass dates back to the 11th century AD. In any case, back in the Middle Ages, this ancient discovery was widely used by sailors, determining their path on the open sea using a primitive compass. Over time, it was discovered that a piece of magnetite could transfer its “magical” property to steel objects - for example, a needle, and it would make a more convenient compass. In 1269, the Frenchman Pierre de Mericourt, nicknamed Peregrine, wrote the book “Letters on the Magnet,” in which he collected various information about the magnet, both previously known and discovered by him personally. It was Peregrine, experimenting with magnets, who discovered that they have two special ends - two poles. Since they pointed north and south, they were called the north and south poles of the magnet. When Peregrine hung two magnetic needles close to each other on threads, it turned out that their like poles repel, and their opposite poles attract. William Gilbert breaks a permanent magnet into two parts, you get two new magnets with two poles - north and south - each. Neither Peregrine nor anyone after him succeeded in obtaining any one pole. This is the answer to the second riddle. Why this happens will be discussed in the second part of the article. And now - the answer to the third riddle: it is possible to magnetize a ball, and this was discovered by the English physicist and doctor William Hilbert. In 1600, he published the book “On the Magnet, Magnetic Bodies and the Great Magnet – the Earth.” In it he talked about his experiments with magnets. This is what he discovered: The magnet has different attractive powers in different parts; at the poles this force is most noticeable. A magnet has two poles: north and south, they differ in their properties; unlike poles attract, like poles repel. A magnet suspended on a thread is positioned in a certain way in space, indicating north and south. It is impossible to obtain a magnet with one pole: no matter how much you break the magnet, each newly obtained piece will always have two poles. The globe is a big magnet. When strongly heated, the magnetic properties of natural and artificial magnets disappear. Magnets exert their effect through glass, skin and water. But Gilbert conducted the most remarkable experiment with a magnetized ball carved from the mineral magnetite. It also turned out to have two poles - north and south, and, being near this ball, the compass needle always pointed to these poles, i.e. behaved the same way as in relation to the Earth. Therefore, Hilbert called his ball “terella”, i.e. small land. This word comes from the Latin terra - earth; in the Russian language there are many words with this root: territory, terrace (ledge on the side of a mountain), terrarium (room for amphibians), waste heap (heap of earth and waste rock), terracotta (baked clay), terrenkur (marked paths for therapeutic walking, usually in mountainous areas), as well as terra incognita - unknown land. Gilbert was the first to guess that the globe is also a magnet, which is why the magnetized compass needle points to the poles of this giant magnet. Does a compass needle always point north at one end and south at the other? It turns out - and this is the answer to the first riddle - not always. the traveler is far from him; usually no surprises happen with the compass needle. But as it approaches the pole, the needle begins to gradually “go crazy.” And the sailors noticed this long ago. At the beginning of the 16th century, when America was discovered and ships began to sail far to the north, a strange phenomenon was discovered. The sailors knew that on a clear night they did not need any compass: the North Star shows the direction to the north. But when they compared this direction with the compass reading, an amazing phenomenon was discovered: the further they sailed to the north, the more the blue compass needle deviated from the north! The angle between the axis of the compass needle and the exact direction to the pole (it coincides with the geographic meridian) is called magnetic declination. This declination is different in different parts of the world. What happens to the compass at the North Pole? It turns out that the blue part of the arrow points towards the northern part of Canada. There, at some depth, one of the Earth’s magnetic poles is currently located; the other magnetic pole is located in Antarctica. Why this happens is not known exactly. Moreover, it has been established that the Earth's magnetic poles move slowly. Thus, in Paris in 1580, the maximum magnetic declination was observed east of the meridian; it was $9,5 ^\circ$. And 230 years later, in 1810, the maximum declination in the other direction was recorded - to the west of the meridian, and it amounted to as much as $22,3 ^\circ$. This means that the Earth’s magnetic poles “walk”, moving from place to place. Moreover, very rarely - once every thousand years - they change places. Scientists do not rule out that this could happen again in the next hundred to two hundred years. And it is difficult to predict how this will affect living beings. Magnetic declination often has local deviations - sometimes very strong; they are called deviation (from the Latin deviatio - deviation from the path). For example, the famous Kursk magnetic anomaly is caused by deposits of magnetic ore, which acts on the needle. Small “magnetic anomalies” can be easily detected at home if there are iron objects near the compass. One can only imagine how many troubles and even misfortunes such “local anomalies” brought to sailors. After all, even on wooden sailing ships there were always a lot of iron objects: cannons, cannonballs, anchors. The situation worsened after iron steamships replaced sailing ships. In 1854, a terrible disaster shocked Great Britain: the Taylor, a vessel with a displacement of 2,000 tons, carrying several hundred emigrants heading to America, crashed on the east coast of Ireland. Most of the passengers and crew died. But this was a new ship, which easily withstood a two-day storm at the beginning of the voyage. The investigation showed that the culprit was an incorrect compass reading caused by deviation. It must be said that modern compasses have special devices for eliminating deviation. And here is the last riddle. If the blue pole of a magnet is called north, and the red pole is called south, then the blue end of the compass needle should point not to the north, but to the south: after all, only opposite poles attract! Why does the blue half of the compass needle point not to the south, but to the north? If the blue end of the arrow points to the north, therefore, there is the south magnetic pole of the Earth, and in the southern hemisphere there is the north magnetic pole!? Riddle four.
