Limitations on heat dissipation limits

In living organisms living on Earth, the level of intensity of circulating heat flows is set by the Sun. A heat flux of about 1 kW $м^{2}$ reaches the Earth's surface from the Sun, of which about 300 W $м^{2}$ is absorbed. In the case of tall trees, this heat flow triggers the main mechanism of water rising up the tree trunk (with the evaporation of water through the stomata). It turns out that, although the area occupied by the stomata is only about 1% of the leaf area, evaporation through them occurs at approximately the same rate as from the open surface. (The nature of this paradox is described in the article by A. Mineev “Leaves are smiling” in «Kvant» No. 4, 2006 - Ed.) The magnitude of the specific heat flux removed through the skin of animals during evaporation is generally of the same order. So, for a person in a calm state it is approximately 50 W $м^{2}$. With maximum physical exertion, for example, a record-breaking sprinter, the ratio of the thermal power released during running to the surface area of ​​the runner's body can reach 2 kW $м^{2}$. Interview with A.B. Sosinsky

Alexey Bronislavovich Sosinsky is a famous mathematician, author of scientific and popular science books and articles, including the monograph (joint with V.V. Prasolov) “Knots, links, braids and three-dimensional manifolds”, the popular science book (translated into 6 languages) “Knots. Chronology of a mathematical theory." For many years, Alexey Bronislavovich was the editor of the mathematics department «Kvant». He is one of the regular contributors to our magazine, an outstanding lecturer and teacher. On the website mathnet.ru you can watch his film-lecture “Gödel’s Theorem”. We also advise you to get acquainted with the report “The Mathematics of Juggling”, read by Jean-Christophe Novelli and Florent Iver, in which A.B. Sosinsky acted as a translator (see website mccme.ru). This is no coincidence: Alexey Bronislavovich has spoken French and English since childhood. Many Russian mathematicians learned to write scientific articles in English from A.B. Sosinsky’s book “How to Write a Mathematical Article in English.” Alexey Bronislavovich is a man of interesting destiny, and we are pleased to present to our readers his story about himself, about mathematicians and mathematics.

– Maybe you can tell us first about your family, who your parents are...

– Just briefly about my pedigree. My father was one of the landless nobles (his father, my grandfather, was a very famous railway engineer), fought against the Bolsheviks, emigrated after the end of the civil war and settled in Paris for many years. There he met my mother, Ariadna Chernova, the daughter of Viktor Mikhailovich Chernov, the leader and chief theoretician of the Socialist Revolutionary Party, chairman of the Constituent Assembly before it was dispersed by the Bolsheviks. I think there is no point in telling readers «Kvant» about the life of my parents and my childhood. Let me start right away from the moment when I ended up at the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics of Moscow State University. I was admitted to the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics at the age of nineteen straight into the second year. Before that, I lived and studied abroad. I was born in France, and received my first university degree in the States in 1957 - a bachelor's degree from New York University. At the same time, I was offered to enter graduate school at the Courant Institute of Mathematics, but I refused, realizing that at that moment, i.e. in the mid-50s, Moscow (and in particular Moscow State University) was one of the leading - if not the strongest - mathematical centers in the world.

- Is this the case now? - Unfortunately no. Everyone knows that a large number of very strong Russian mathematicians have gone abroad. We can say that almost all stellar Russian mathematicians either

I was a year younger than my classmates, having skipped one grade in high school.

– How old were you when you started school? - Apparently, from six; but during the war years I was in occupied French territory, where schools sometimes functioned and sometimes did not. At times I went to elementary preparatory classes.

– So there was no home schooling? – No, there was home schooling, of course. Moreover, it is very serious. But my parents did not teach me the exact sciences or arithmetic, and my education was humanitarian. Somewhere from the age of four I already knew how to write in Russian, and I learned to write in French a little later, already at school. My parents, very educated people, managed to interest me in Russian literature and poetry.

– Could you briefly explain to schoolchildren what topology is? – Topology can be characterized in many ways. For example, this way. In Euclidean geometry, the main thing is the distance between points. In affine geometry, the main thing is the ratio of distances between points. In projective geometry, the main thing is the so-called double ratio of four points on a line. In Euclidean geometry, movement does not change the distance. But in topology there is no such simple invariant as distance. You can imagine that you are studying a geometric figure made of rubber. You can stretch it, you can compress it, you can deform it. And topology studies the deepest properties of geometric figures, namely those that do not change under such deformations. What kind are there?

The classical abstract topology of the school of Pavel Sergeevich Alexandrov in Moscow, which flourished in the forties, has now gone out of fashion. – What about set theory? – Set theory now continues to be at the center of the foundations of mathematics, of course. It plays a very important role, oddly enough, in issues related to computers, because the facts from the so-called descriptive set theory have gradually been developed into wonderful theorems of the theory of algorithms. Thus, Gödel’s theorem has deep applications to computer science: it follows from it that no computer can solve a number of problems in principle. In addition, a number of set theory constructs derived in the first half of the 20th century have now found unexpected applications in the theory of dynamical systems, in particular in connection with so-called strange attractors. So set theory can hardly be considered a dead science.

– Do you think mathematics should have applications? How do applied and so-called pure mathematics relate? – On this matter, I just want to give the point of view of Andrei Nikolaevich Kolmogorov. When I asked him this question, he told me the following. There is neither pure mathematics nor applied mathematics. Mathematics can be good and bad. Good mathematics can come straight from the tip of the pen, from the head of a mathematician who develops mathematics for the sake of mathematics itself, simply out of pure curiosity. But if what he does is meaningful, interesting and good, sooner or later it will find serious application. And if it’s bad math, everyone will forget about it in a generation. As for the results that arise when solving a practical applied problem, here too it is necessary to distinguish a strong result from an ordinary one. If, while solving some applied problem, a mathematician dug very deeply and came up with a fundamentally new solution method, then a theory will then emerge from it. And it will be as significant a mathematical theory as the best abstract theories that came from the speculative reasoning of mathematicians. So it seems to me that it is very important not to separate applied and practical mathematics from the so-called pure mathematics. And this, by the way, is a very good feature of the Russian

However, this is a completely wrong point of view, because mathematics, like theoretical physics, continues to develop intensively. And it is developed by people who are called research mathematicians. How is their life? Some research mathematicians work at universities, and then they teach. They are paid money specifically for teaching. But they view this as by no means the main part of their work, and they consider their main work to be proving new theorems, inventing new theories, solving old but still unsolved problems. In addition, there are now several institutes in the world where mathematicians are paid money simply to develop mathematics.
