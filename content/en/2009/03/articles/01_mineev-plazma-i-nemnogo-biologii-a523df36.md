Plasma and... a little biology

A. MINEEV

IT WOULD SEEM, WHAT IS COMMON BETWEEN plasma physics (namely, the professional activity of the author of the article was connected with it – editor’s note) and biology? However, if you take a closer look, you are struck by the many similar ideas and analogies that arise in both sciences.

Plasma

The word "plasma" is often found in our lives. Plasma TV panels and lighting devices - in everyday life, lightning strikes and auroras - in nature. The radiation from the Sun, which provides us with life, is also due to reactions occurring in the plasma. If you look a little wider, beyond the Earth, more than 90% of the matter in the Universe is in the plasma state. It's a natural state there. Any substance turns into a plasma state if it is heated to an ionized state, when atoms break down into their components - electrons and ions. The temperature at which this occurs can be thousands of degrees, in which case we are talking about low-temperature plasma, or many millions of degrees, in the case of high-temperature plasma. Thus, the characteristic temperature in the center of stars (such as the Sun) is tens of millions of degrees, while intense fusion reactions of light nuclei occur. And here are a few terms from biology that are close to plasma in spelling: g blood plasma (yellowish translucent component of blood, without red blood cells, leukocytes and platelets), g protoplasm (liquid contents of a living cell), g plasma membrane (cell membrane surrounding protoplasm), g plasmodia (sorry, these are parasites that live in the red blood cells of mammals). As you can see, the meaning of biological “plasma” word formations is completely different. In addition, the temperature ranges of existence of biological objects and plasma are completely different. However, let us consider in more detail a number of more general analogies between biology and plasma physics.

From figure it follows that during a phase transition the temperature of the body remains constant (all supplied energy is spent on transferring the substance to a new phase state), and between phase transitions a monotonic dependence of temperature on the supplied energy is maintained. Let us give an example of such a dependence from plasma physics. As is known, it is impossible to retain plasma for a long time with material walls (made of ordinary matter). Outside the Earth (in stars), plasma is retained by gravitational forces, and on Earth it is possible to reduce the energy loss of hot plasma to the walls only by using a magnetic field. The greatest successes in heating and confining hot plasma have been achieved using toroidal magnetic installations of the TOKAMAK type, the idea of ​​which was proposed in the early 1950s by I.E. Tamm and A.D. Sakharov. (More details about the features of such systems can be read in G.S. Voronov’s book “Storm of the Thermonuclear Fortress” - M.: Nauka, series “Library «Kvant», issue 37.) The first tokamaks were built in our country, after impressive results on which installations of this type began to be built all over the world. And recently, in 2006, a decision was made to build the first international experimental tokamak-type reactor - ITER (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor) with the participation of Russia, Europe, the USA, China, India and South Korea. It would seem that about 60 years have passed since the idea of ​​tokamaks appeared, and research on confining and heating hot plasma is moving very slowly.
