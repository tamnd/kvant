Does a tailwind interfere with birds?

N. KONSTANTINOV

WHEN I HAD ALREADY LEARNED TO READ, I CAUGHT A BOOK about travelers - I forgot which book exactly, but I remembered only one episode from it. The travelers set up camp in the forest next to a lake, on which a flock of wild geese landed to rest during the migration. Evening came and it was no longer possible to hunt. “Tomorrow there will be a glorious hunt,” said one. – Don’t you think that the birds will fly away at dawn? - said another. “They won’t fly away, because the north wind is favorable for the geese.” Birds do not fly with a tailwind, as it blows under the flight feathers of their wings.

This conversation seemed strange to me. If I were the birds, I would rejoice at the tailwind - you can fly fast on it, faster than the wind. But I was little, and everything that was written in the books was true for me. So all I remembered was that I didn't understand something. Twenty-five years have passed. I studied in graduate school, my supervisor was Alexey Andreevich Lyapunov, a famous mathematician who at that time was deeply interested in cybernetics and mathematical approaches to biology. He was connected by scientific interests and personal friendship with our major biophysicist, one of the founders of the science of biophysics - Nikolai Vladimirovich Timofeev-Resovsky. By

On the advice of Alexey Andreevich, I attended a seminar on biophysics conducted by Nikolai Vladimirovich at the Miassovo biological station in the Urals. The story told by Nikolai Vladimirovich made me remember a book I read as a child. It turns out that there was a time (and I just found it) when the same idea was repeated from article to article, from book to book - that a fair wind blows under the wings of birds. And a group of biologists, including Timofeev-Resovsky, in several publications explained to biologists the fallacy of this idea and talked about Galileo’s principle of relativity. Another episode reminded me that some high school students in our time have not yet matured to understand the picture of the world that arose after the discoveries of Galileo. A construction team of students and schoolchildren worked in the summer at the White Sea Biological Station of Moscow State University (I was already a teacher). We were returning from one sea excursion on the ship “Nauchny”. A group of guys asked the captain for permission to sit not on the ship, but in a boat that was towed behind the ship (it was much more pleasant there). And behind the boat, at a distance of about three meters from it, was another small skiff, in which no one was sitting. And so the youngest of us, Lesha Kuvshinov, who then entered the tenth grade, wanted to transfer to a skiff. And to do this on the go was, in my opinion, impossible. At least, it was very difficult: even if you pulled the skiff up to the boat, it was unthinkable to transfer to it without capsizing. But Lesha came up with another way: “I’ll jump high, and while I’m going down, the skiff will already be under me.” And then all the high school students (and these were all math school students, and they are not to be trifled with) began vying with each other to explain Galileo’s principle of relativity to Lesha. The earth, they said, can be considered an inertial frame of reference. This means that if no external forces act on the body (or, more precisely, if all the forces acting on the body compensate each other), then it maintains a state of rest or uniform

rectilinear motion relative to the Earth. Of course, there are situations in which the reference frame associated with the Earth cannot be considered inertial. A clear example of this is the Foucault pendulum. In an inertial reference frame, the plane in which the pendulum oscillates remains constant, but in reality, say, if the experiment was carried out in Moscow, the plane of oscillations slowly rotates. Another example is that rivers flowing in the northern hemisphere wash away the right bank. And if the system associated with the Earth were inertial, both banks would have equal rights. But these are all rather subtle effects, observed either with very precise measurements or over very long periods of time. In our case, the reference system associated with the Earth can be considered inertial. This means that any other frame of reference that moves uniformly and rectilinearly relative to the Earth can also be considered inertial. Our boat is just such a frame of reference. And then, according to Galileo’s principle of relativity, all the laws of physics in the lifeboat system look the same as in the system associated with the Earth. And having jumped in the boat, you will land at the same point on the boat from which you started, just as it would be on the shore. Here one of our guys objected that if on solid ground, i.e. on the shore, shoot vertically upward, then the projectile will not fall at the same point from which it started, even if the air is completely motionless relative to the Earth. This is indeed true, but this is another case demonstrating the non-inertiality of the earth's reference frame. Since the effect is insignificant, it can be ignored in our experiment. However, Lesha was not convinced by all these explanations. They also cited to him the reasoning of Galileo, who explained to his contemporaries that if a duel with pistols takes place in the hold of a moving ship, then none of the duelists, whether he looks from the stern to the bow of the ship or vice versa, has an advantage. And they reminded how he, Lesha, traveling on the train to the Biostation, played a ball in the carriage and the ball moved in relation to the carriage in the same way as it would move on a stationary earth with the same blows on it. But everything was useless. Lesha definitely wanted to jump, but we objected, since the boat could leak as a result of the impact. But in the end they gave in, and Lesha jumped. He, as it should have been, sank to the starting point (and the boat swayed so much that it took on a certain amount of water). Lesha thought for a long time. And he probably remembered Galileo’s principle of relativity forever. I will take this opportunity to show with examples how superficially schoolchildren often learn their lessons. But... let's get back to the birds. I was always taught that Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks were replete with ingenious insights. And recently I finally decided to read these books, which, of course, have long been published by reputable academic publishing houses. In “Selected Works” of Leonardo da Vinci (M.: Publishing House of the USSR Academy of Sciences, 1955) there is a chapter “On the flight and movement of bodies in the air.” This is a detailed study with many interesting observations and wonderful figure. But understanding it is very difficult, sometimes impossible. After all, Leonardo wrote for himself, not caring about explaining the meaning of the terms used. This chapter includes a section on “Why do migratory birds fly against the current of the wind?” I couldn’t figure it out in this text, but the conclusion is obviously not correct. The pictures show how birds take off - indeed always against the wind. And when they are already high, you can’t see which way the wind is blowing. It can be assumed that this led to the error. It should be noted that Leonardo constantly refers to the laws of statics discovered by Archimedes (he studied Archimedes from the complete works, which, as is known from his notebooks, was available to him). But statics are not enough to study flight. But dynamics as a science did not yet exist, and Leonardo, where there was a lack of knowledge, constantly used intuitive guesses. So, all his life he tried to create an aircraft, but he failed. It took four centuries of development of science and technology for man to finally take to the air. So, I think I have guessed where biologists got the erroneous idea that tailwinds prevent birds from flying. It originated from the works of Leonardo da Vinci. And this idea persisted in the minds of some people because in their knowledge of physics these people had not passed the era of Galileo. But let’s not blame Leonardo for not being ahead of the genius who followed him...

How to make milk clear?

(See the beginning on the 4th page of the cover)

... It turns out that after some time, when the concentration of sugar in a solution of milk in water reaches the required value, the solution will again become transparent. The enlightening effect of sugar is explained quite simply. Bodies cease to be transparent when interfaces between media with different refractive indices of light appear inside them. In milk, these are the boundaries between droplets of milk fat (its refractive index n = 1.433 – 1.500) and water (n = 1.333). At these boundaries, light is reflected in a variety of directions, i.e. dissipates. This is why milk is opaque. If you increase the refractive index of water, bringing it to the refractive index of milk fat, for example by dissolving sugar in it, then the milk can be made transparent. K. Bogdanov
