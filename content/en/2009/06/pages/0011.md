27. Now it suffices to consider the case when all points of the set M belong to the union of lines AB, BC, AC. Since M cannot be contained in the union of two lines, there are points $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}$ on lines BC, AC, AB, respectively, that do not coincide with the vertices of triangle ABC. Let us assume that the points $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}$ do not lie on the same line. Then, according to what was proven above, all points of the set M lie in the union of the lines $A_{1} B_{1}, B_{1} C_{1}, C_{1} A_{1}$ and some conic circumscribed around the triangle $A_{1} B_{1} C_{1}$. Show, however, that no conic circumscribed around triangle $A_{1} B_{1} C_{1}$ can contain points A, B and C simultaneously. Contradiction. 28. Thus, points $A_{1}, B_{1}$ and $C_{1}$ are collinear. Moreover, the set M consists of only six points $A, B, C, A_{1}, B_{1}$ and $C_{1}$. (In fact, if there were one more point in the set M, then one could replace $A_{1}, B_{1}$ or $C_{1}$ with this point and get a non-degenerate triangle.) But in this case, the five points A, B, $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}$ define a degenerate conic that does not contain any other points of the set M.

We have thus completed the proof of the Wiseman–Wilson theorem.
