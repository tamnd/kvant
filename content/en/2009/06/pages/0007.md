From nano to mega

These are the interesting consequences that can be observed by simply stretching polymer films with thin nanometer coatings. But is it really only in them that the described phenomena can occur? It's hard to imagine that this is the case. There are many physical objects built on the principle of “hard surface on a flexible foundation.” It is possible that the deformation (compression and stretching) of polymer films with thin coatings models many processes in the world around us. In nature, situations very often occur when such systems are subject to various kinds of deformations. As a consequence, numerous regular structures arise. Loss of stability under conditions of plane compression leads to the appearance of amazingly beautiful reliefs - such, for example, as those formed when a drop of paint dries. Systems “hard coating on a yielding base” are also subject to in-plane tensile deformation. Its results were seen by everyone who noticed dry crusts in the soil. When wet soil dries out, the hard crust formed on its surface tends to shrink, but this is prevented by the underlying soft, almost incompressible base - a layer of mud. As a result, the crust finds itself in conditions of plane tension. Due to the evaporation of liquid from the soil, tensile stresses increase, and a network of cracks appears on the rigid surface. Cracks form and propagate according to strict laws inherent in the same “hard coating on a flexible base” systems. Similar pictures arise during the cooling of magmatic melts - the so-called volcanic bombs. As the melt cools slowly, the boundary between the hard layer and the liquid core that has not yet cooled moves deeper. The solid phase, continuously coexisting with the liquid, is constantly subjected to planar tensile deformation.
