Even if quadratic terms are present, the conic may turn out to be degenerate, i.e. the union of two lines (possibly coinciding) or a point. There are quadratic equations that are not satisfied by any point of the plane (for example, $x^{2} + y^{2} + 1 = 0 ),$, but we will not consider such equations. Non-degenerate conics are ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas. Note that if the equation of a conic is multiplied by a number other than zero, then the conic itself will not change. In total, 6 coefficients appear in the equation, but, as we have just seen, only the ratios of the coefficients, but not the coefficients themselves, can be significant parameters coefficients. It is not difficult to show that the conic actually depends significantly on 5 parameters. For example, a conic passes through any 5 points, and, as a rule, only one.

Exercises 18. Prove that a certain conic passes through any 5 points on the plane. 19. Let points A and B lie on the conic K. Then the intersection of the conic K with the line AB consists of either two points A and B, or the entire line AB.

Wiseman and Wilson [10] proved such a theorem in 1988. Theorem 8. Consider a finite set of points M on the plane. Let us assume that not all points of the set M belong to the same conic. Then there is a conic containing exactly 5 points from M and defined by these points (i.e. there is no other conic passing through the same 5 points). We will present a new proof of the Wiseman–Wilson theorem in the form of a series of exercises. It is more elementary than the original proof. Most of the exercises are tests of understanding rather than substantive problems - the reader is simply advised to read the terms of all exercises carefully and understand why they are obvious. Individual exercises cover individual steps in the proof.

Exercises 20. Consider three points A, B and C on the plane that do not lie on the same line. In other words, we are dealing with the vertices of triangle ABC. Let's place masses 1, u, v at points A, B, C, respectively. Let us denote by X (u, v) the center of mass of the three points under consideration. A pair of numbers (u, v) can be considered the coordinates of a point X (u, v). Thus, we introduced another coordinate system on the plane. (Note that the center of mass can be determined even when u and/or v are zero or even less than zero; the problem only arises when the sum of all three masses is zero, i.e. 1 + and + v = 0.) Prove that in the new coordinate system, any conic can also be represented by a quadratic equation.

21. Consider a conic passing through all three vertices of triangle ABC. In this case, the conic equation has a special form. Namely, some coefficients turn to zero. Let us first write the conic equation in general form:

$b_{0} + b_{1} u + b_{2} v + b_{3} u^{2} + b_{4} uv + b_{5} v^{2} = 0$.

Now let's try to understand what limitation is imposed on the coefficients by the fact that our conic passes through point A. Point A has coordinates (0, 0) (i.e. u = = v = 0). This means that when u = v = 0 the equation must be true. This will only happen if $b_{0} = 0$. Points B and C impose the following relations on the coefficients: $b_{3} = b_{5} = 0$. Prove this (the reasoning is more complicated than for point A, since points B and C do not correspond to any finite values ​​of the coordinates (u, v) - to consider these points you will have to place zero mass at point A). Let us now consider some finite set of points on the plane that do not belong to lines AB, BC and AC and satisfy the following condition: on a conic circumscribed around triangle ABC and containing two points of our set, there lies at least one more point of our set. In this case, all points of our set lie on the same conic circumscribed around triangle ABC. Note. Consider the coordinate system (u, v) associated with triangle ABC described above. The equation of any conic circumscribed around triangle ABC is au + bv + cuv = 0. Divide this equation by uv, we get $av^{-} 1 + bu^{-} 1 + c = 0$. This means that if instead of the coordinates u and v we introduce the coordinates 1/v and 1/u, then in the new coordinates the equations of the conics described around the triangle ABC will be linear. This means that on a plane with coordinates 1/v and 1/u such conics will be represented as straight lines. Apply the classical Sylvester–Gallay theorem to these lines.
