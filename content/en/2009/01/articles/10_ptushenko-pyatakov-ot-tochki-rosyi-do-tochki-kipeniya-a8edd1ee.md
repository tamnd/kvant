From the dew point to the boiling point, as any reader can probably easily answer, it is fraught with, if not mysteries, then certainly some pitfalls. So we will dedicate our article to him. So, where does the story of dewdrop begin? With condensation, of course, everyone knows this. Therefore, let us clarify the question: when and under what conditions does condensation begin? Let's discuss everything in order. Water, like any substance, has three states of aggregation - solid, liquid and gas. It is logical to expect that there are two temperature boundaries between the three states: the melting point and the boiling point. Now let’s think: at what temperature does gaseous water turn into liquid? If hot water vapor, taken at a temperature above $100 ^\circ$ C, begins to cool, then the first dewdrops (liquid) will appear in it..., of course, at the dew point - it’s not for nothing that this temperature is called that! Wait, what is boiling point then? Why is it not in it - at this border point between the liquid and gaseous states - that the transformation of gas into liquid occurs? In what sense then is it a boundary? Before answering these questions, let’s think: at what temperature does water as a liquid turn into steam? Yes for any! Steam rises from a cup of hot tea, and from a saucer of cold water that you left for the kitten, the water will sooner or later evaporate. Even a wet sheet in the frosty winter wind manages to dry faster than freezing and “turning to stone.” What then is the peculiarity of the boiling point? The answer is well known: at the boiling temperature, not just evaporation occurs, but boiling, i.e. intense evaporation throughout the entire volume of liquid.

This short formal definition, like a fairytale casket, contains much more inside than one might assume from the outside. Hidden inside this “casket” is one of the basic concepts of physics – the concept of equilibrium. In order for a liquid to exist for a long time, it must be in equilibrium with its surroundings. But there can be many different types of equilibrium - as many as there are different types of “movement”, i.e. any change in the liquid-gas system. For example, there is a mechanical equilibrium between all forces acting on a fluid from the inside and outside. There is thermal equilibrium, towards which hot tea cools to room temperature. And there is also a physicochemical equilibrium with oxygen, which strives to penetrate from the air into the water and dissolve in it, giving life to your aquarium fish... So what types of equilibrium are important in this case? Let's look at the liquid under a microscope: we will see tiny air bubbles in it. Water vapor strives to fill these bubbles and “explode” the liquid from the inside. Water vapor along with air, located outside, above the surface of the liquid, does not allow him to do this: they “squeeze” the liquid, this pressure is transmitted inward from one layer to another, is “felt” in every corner of the volume filled with liquid and restrains the growth of bubbles. In addition to the danger of being exploded from the inside, the liquid faces another danger - evaporation, molecule by molecule, through the open surface into the surrounding space. But this can be prevented by water vapor, which already fills this space above the surface of the liquid. Molecule by molecule, it can return back to the liquid, i.e. condense, compensating for evaporation. Such phase equilibrium occurs, for example, in a jar covered with a lid, in which there is water, and above it there is moist air with saturated water vapor. The role of a lid can also be played by stationary air masses under appropriate atmospheric conditions, and then puddles on the ground do not dry out for a long time, and the laundry on the lines remains wet, disrupting all the housewives’ plans. What happens when it boils? At the boiling point, the saturated vapor pressure becomes too high - it rises to atmospheric pressure, bubbles grow uncontrollably, and steam escapes from under the lid. Both types of fluid equilibrium are disrupted. The lid bounces and clinks, reminding you of this. You can, of course, “strengthen” the lid so that it does not jump - then you will get a pressure cooker$^{2}$, both equilibria will be restored, but only the pressure under the lid will be greater than atmospheric. Please note: to maintain the phase equilibrium of a liquid with an open surface, only its own vapor and its partial pressure are important, and what other gases are in the atmosphere and in what quantities is completely unimportant. But in order to maintain the equilibrium of the bubbles, in order to prevent boiling from occurring, it is necessary to have some other gas above the surface of the liquid, besides the water vapor itself. If you manage to pump out all the dry air from under the closed lid, leaving only water vapor there, the liquid will boil. No wonder: you have reduced the external pressure to the value created by only one saturated water vapor, i.e. boiling conditions have just been created.

This is where the catch lies, which often interferes with the understanding of the processes of boiling and evaporation. In their explanation, the main role is played by the partial pressure of water vapor and total (atmospheric) pressure. The total external pressure is generally considered to be a constant value, while the partial pressure of water vapor, on the contrary, can change as the water evaporates. Indeed, in a kitchen where the kettle has been boiling for a long time, you can quite clearly feel the increase in humidity, while the barometer in the kitchen still shows the same atmospheric pressure. Why doesn't this pressure increase? Under normal conditions, because the remaining terms decrease: water vapor, filling the kitchen, partially displaces nitrogen and oxygen from it, their partial pressures fall, and the total pressure remains unchanged. And in unusual conditions - for example, in the pressure cooker we have already mentioned, in the underground chambers of geysers, etc. – an increase in the partial pressure of water vapor may well lead to an increase in the total pressure, so that it ceases to be constant and changes, for example, with temperature. Now we can return to the questions with which we began the article. Let’s just take the last step - in order to more clearly compare the definitions of dew points and boiling points, let’s slightly reformulate one of them. (Reformulating classical definitions is a thankless task. But we hope the reader will appreciate the methodological meaning of this technique and forgive us this “familiarity” in relation to the classics.) As you know, the dew point is the temperature at which the water vapor contained in the atmosphere (along with nitrogen, oxygen, etc.) will be saturated. And we define the boiling point as the temperature at which water vapor would be saturated, completely “single-handedly” forming the atmosphere. From here it is immediately clear that the boiling point is the upper limit for the dew point and the limit for the existence of a liquid (at a given external pressure, usually atmospheric). Therefore, when cooling hot, humid air, you can detect the first drops of liquid at any temperature, but only below the boiling point: if there is little moisture in the air, then you can force the water molecules to “gather together” only by greatly lowering the temperature. If almost the entire volume of air is filled with steam, then the dew point may be quite close to the boiling point (remember how the steam escaping from a boiling kettle covers even a lid or spout that is very hot to the touch with droplets). Let's summarize what has been said. The processes of evaporation and boiling of a liquid are determined by two different factors: the interaction of the liquid with its own vapor and with the atmosphere as a whole. The total atmospheric pressure of the gas above the liquid determines the equilibrium between the liquid and the bubbles formed inside it, i.e. the possibility of its boiling. And the partial vapor pressure determines the equilibrium of the liquid with the surrounding vapor, i.e., in particular, the possibility of its complete evaporation or, conversely, the beginning of condensation at the dew point. Let's hope that our article will help the reader more confidently navigate these, although very familiar, but still not always easy to understand issues. But if it raises new questions, it will be even better!

About logical and illogical tournaments

We looked at a simplified competition model. The outcome of a game between “ideal” players is entirely determined by their ratings. There cannot be non-transitive triplets in the tournament table of such players. The real world is more complex and interesting. The ratings of real players only affect the probability of the outcome of the game and determine the statistics of the results in a fairly large series of meetings. And this is not the only difference. A real player is a complex “object” whose play is influenced by several independent factors (in chess this is, for example, opening preparation, the ability to conduct positional battles, the ability to combine, knowledge of endgames, the ability to play under time pressure, etc.). Therefore, even the statistics of games between two individual players do not always correspond to their ratings, since the rating reflects the average strength of the player in the totality of all his opponents, and due to the characteristics of the “multifactorial” qualities of the player, he has “convenient” and “inconvenient” opponents. In 1997, an article by A. Zaslavsky with the same title was published in the magazine «Kvant» (No. 5). The article examines some properties of competition tables held in a round-robin system, when each participant in the competition meets all other participants. A value is introduced - the degree of illogicality of the tournament, equal to the number of non-transitive triplets in the results table, and formulas are derived for calculating this value through the results of the participants. At the end of the article it is indicated that the value of the degree of illogicality is used in the theory of expert assessments to determine the competence of experts assessing the quality of any objects. The expert is asked to evaluate a group of similar objects by means of their complete pairwise comparison. The greater the degree of illogicality of the rating table (i.e., the more intransitive triples in the table), the less competent the expert is considered. The following thought suggests itself: with such an assessment of the expert’s competence, it is believed that the correct assessment of objects of any type must be transitive, and any non-transitive triple in the table is a consequence of the expert’s error. In reality, everything is not so simple. Let's assume that objects have several independent characteristics (for example, taste, color, smell, etc.). When comparing a pair of objects, we will consider the one that has the largest number of best characteristics to be the best. Thus, we reduce the comparison of a pair of objects to a type I team competition, where the objects become teams, and the players are the characteristics of these objects. We have seen that in such competitions transitivity is not respected, and therefore such an evaluation of objects is not transitive. The situation will not change if we assume that the characteristics are not equivalent and add up the “victories” with some weights. In this case, in our model we need to assume that the “team” has several “players” of each type (of equal “strength”), and their number is proportional to the weight of the corresponding characteristic. When studying Type I competitions, we always ordered the players on the team by rating. This is not required here, and, moreover, it is impossible.
