"Electrons, phonons, magnons"

(unpublished preface to the second edition of the book)

M.KAGANOV

RE-READING $КНИГУ^{1}$, WRITTEN BY ME approximately 30 years ago, I experienced a somewhat strange feeling. On the one hand, I undoubtedly wrote the book. I find out my thoughts, my attitude towards this or that phenomenon, discovery. This is how you unmistakably recognize yourself when looking at an old photograph, although in the mirror you see a completely different face. On the other hand, thanks to the passing years, a certain alienation arises, which allows one to evaluate what one reads objectively or almost objectively. This allows me to say: “Electrons, Phonons, Magnons” is a good book, I liked it when I re-read it. If this were not so, I would not have agreed to its republication. Of course, much has happened in the quantum theory of solids, the popular exposition of which this book is devoted to, over the years. But it seems to me that what happened is correctly called development. The fundamentals of quantum theory of solids have not been revised. Without knowing what the book says, it is impossible to understand, in particular, the changes that have occurred in the years that have passed between the first and second editions. The quantum properties of various solids are increasingly being used in technology. If the book were written now, there would most likely be references to nanostructures, high-temperature superconductors and many interesting discoveries made relatively recently. I admit that I wondered whether I should include inserts or footnotes with such references, and came to the conclusion that it was not necessary. It is impossible to talk seriously about what was not known in the 70s of the last century within the framework of this book, and the knowledge that can be gleaned by reading “Electrons, Phonons, Magnons” will be enough to use the information that fills publications in popular science magazines, on the Internet and in other available sources.

* * * It's not just solid state physics that has changed over the years. The idea of ​​the World, of its structural units - elementary particles, has deepened and changed; even the idea of ​​the structure of the Universe, its birth and fate has undergone a change. Here is one example from an area far from solid state physics - from elementary particle physics: the book never mentions quarks. In the years when the book was written, quarks were a fashionable topic; There was a lot of debate about whether quarks are real or whether they are just a convenient way to describe many particles and, most importantly, the discovered non-standard symmetry properties. The controversy is long over. Quarks are universally recognized. Quarks are elementary particles that make up nucleons and some mesons. At the same time, an understanding arose that quarks do not exist as free particles in a vacuum, outside baryons. Undoubtedly, this is an event of great significance. It may change our worldview. If further fragmentation of particles is impossible and it is quarks that are truly elementary particles, then humanity has finally discovered what everything consists of. And yet, I repeat, quarks are not found in the book, although nucleons (and protons and neutrons) are mentioned many times in the book. But what does solid state physics need to know about these particles? Their mass, charge and magnetic moment. Perhaps that's all. Their internal structure, which is important not only for studying interactions between particles, but also for understanding the structure of the universe, does not play a direct role in solid state physics (perhaps not yet). Solid state physicists need to obtain background information about the strength and elastic characteristics of building materials under different conditions. Electrical engineering - electrical characteristics of the conductors and insulators used. When studying condensed matter, physicists (theorists and experimentalists) use the results of atomic physics and cannot do without them. It is not enough to know that the body is made of atoms or molecules. To understand the nature of the properties of macroscopic bodies and the phenomena occurring in them, it is necessary to know the structure of the “bricks” from which they are built. But about particles - the building material of molecules and atoms, about electrons, protons and neutrons - it is enough to know what can be gleaned from any reference book. Moreover, I think that even to explain the phenomenon of life, this knowledge is quite sufficient. Clearly, this does not mean that we can already answer all the questions posed by molecular biology. Many years of intensive research lie ahead. And, most importantly, you need to wait for new ideas to appear. In recent decades, molecular biology, which has absorbed much of solid state physics, has made brilliant achievements. Physical knowledge about the structure of the molecules from which all living organisms are built is still completely sufficient. I think, although not everyone has this opinion, that any special deepening into the physics of elementary particles will never be required for the development of molecular biology. As before, molecular biologists, like solid-state physicists, will only need to know about electrons, protons and neutrons what is well known today.
