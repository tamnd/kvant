INTERVIEW WITH A. KUZNETSOV

On July 14, 2008 in Amsterdam at the European Mathematical Congress, our compatriot, an employee of the Mathematical Institute. V.A. Steklova RAS Alexander Gennadievich Kuznetsov received the European Mathematical Society (EMS) Prize for young mathematicians. We bring to your attention a conversation between Alexander Gennadievich and a member of the editorial board of our magazine, Nikolai Petrovich Dolbilin.

1. Tell us about the history of the EMO Prize for young scientists. Which of our scientists has received it? The European Mathematical Society Prize is an analogue of the most prestigious international mathematical prize, the Fields Medal (as is known, the Nobel Prize is not awarded to mathematicians), so it would be natural to first say a few words about it. Since 1897, the International Congress of Mathematicians has been held every four years, at which a specially selected commission consisting of the most famous mathematicians selects four laureates (sometimes there may be fewer), to whom medals are awarded. The selection criterion is very simple - the award goes to scientists whose work has made the most significant contribution to the development of mathematics. At the same time, there is an age limit - only mathematicians under 40 years of age are considered. Sixteen years ago, in the image and likeness of the International Mathematical Union, the European Mathematical Society was created, which, naturally, established its own analogue of the Fields Medal. However, there are significant differences between the two awards. Firstly, the EMO Prize is awarded to mathematicians no older than 35 years old. Secondly, applicants must be Europeans (in a rather mild sense - either by birth, or by citizenship, or by place of work). Finally, there are significantly more EMO prizes - at each congress (which also takes place every four years, exactly in the middle between international congresses) 10 prizes are awarded. Since the EMO Prize has been awarded relatively recently, and the age limit is quite strict, the names of the laureates are not very well known. Perhaps the most famous is Maxim Kontsevich, who received the prize in 1992.

2. Which of your works were awarded this prize? Is it possible to tell readers «Kvant» about them and, in general, about your scientific interests? I am studying algebraic geometry. This is an area of ​​mathematics located at the intersection of algebra and geometry (as the name suggests), but at the same time has very close connections with other mathematical disciplines and even with physics. Algebraic geometry studies the properties of algebraic varieties, i.e. bodies (geometry!), which can be specified by polynomial equations (algebra!). The simplest algebraic varieties are studied in school - these are a point, a line, a plane, three-dimensional space. One can also consider spaces of higher dimensions. The circle, ellipse, hyperbola and parabola are also algebraic varieties (since they are defined on the plane by quadratic equations). More complex manifolds are obtained by considering more equations of higher degree in higher dimensional spaces (for example, the intersection of an ellipsoid and a hyperbolic paraboloid in three-dimensional space). 3. How did you become a mathematician? What is the role of family, school, perhaps math competitions, additional literature, «Kvant»? Which professional mathematician had a decisive influence on you? As a rule, one becomes a mathematician as a result of studying at the mathematics department of some university or institute (it happens, however, that people who have received, say, a physics education, become mathematicians). In my case, this was the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics of Moscow State University. But, of course, it all started at school. For the first seven years I studied at a regular school (5 minutes from home), then moved to mathematics school No. 57, where I spent three wonderful years (at that time the school lasted 10 years, not 11, as it is now). I realized quite early that I would become a mathematician. I may not have felt particularly interested in mathematics at first (it’s hard to be interested in science if you don’t really understand what it is), but at least mathematics has always been very easy for me. For example, in the last year before school, I was sick a lot (I constantly caught a cold) and therefore sat at home for a long time. I came across a mathematics textbook for the first grade, and I worked through almost all of it. I treated the problems as interesting puzzles. A couple of years after this (while studying in the second grade), I found a problem book in physics, which really surprised me because I couldn’t solve any problems there. There were a lot of incomprehensible words (“parallel”, “perpendicular”, etc.). I remember being very upset by this, and over the next few years I would regularly take it out and see if anything became clearer to me. When I began to understand (I think it was already in the 6th grade), I really liked it, and I probably re-read the entire school physics course within a year. Of course, the 9th and 10th grade textbooks were incomprehensible (they contained concepts of derivatives that were completely mysterious to me at that time, as well as differential equations), but by that time I had learned, while skipping the incomprehensible, to nevertheless enjoy such reading.
