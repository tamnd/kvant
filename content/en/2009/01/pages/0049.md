LABORATORY "QUANTA"

Reflection from thin cylindrical mirrors

A.ANDREEV, A.PANOV

IN EVERYDAY LIFE, WE MOST OFTEN DEAL WITH flat mirrors; spherical and parabolic mirrors are used in optical instruments, but we rarely encounter cylindrical mirrors. Perhaps in an attraction called the “laugh room” or in anamorphoscopes - devices for viewing specially distorted (anamorphic) images (Fig.1). At the same time, a large number of objects around us have a cylindrical shape - these are all kinds of cylindrical containers, pipes, tree branches, trolleybus wires and much more. Some of them are good reflectors. Thin cylindrical mirrors. The easiest way to study reflection is from a thin cylindrical mirror, the radius of the cross section of which is quite small compared to the distance to the observer. A good example of such mirrors are trolleybus wires illuminated by car headlights or street lamps. Let us consider the simplest case, when a thin beam of parallel rays falls on a thin cylindrical mirror, completely covering the mirror in diameter. It turns out that the reflected light rays form a cone, the axis of which is the mirror, and one of the generatrices is the continuation of the incident light beam. To prove this statement, it is enough to use Fermat's principle. And here is the experimental confirmation - it is presented in figure 2. A thin cylindrical mirror here is an ordinary needle onto which a laser beam falls.

The intersection of the cone of reflected rays with a flat screen can be any conical section - an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola, it all depends on the location of the screen. If the incident ray is perpendicular to the mirror, then the reflected cone turns into a plane (Fig.3).

Let us describe several simple situations where the statement we made is used. It is easy to imagine what happens when the entire extended thin cylindrical mirror is illuminated by a beam of parallel rays. In this case, the entire space will be filled with identical cones consisting of reflected rays, and the cylindrical mirror will be the common axis of these cones. If the mirror is illuminated by a point source, then we will also get a system of coaxial cones, only they will no longer be the same. At the point of the mirror closest to the source, the cone will be a plane, i.e. have an opening angle of $180^\circ.$ As the apex of the cone moves away from the source, the opening angle of the cone will decrease to $0^\circ.$ If a cylindrical mirror is a piece of thin wire, then the wire can be bent, and when reflected from such a curved cylindrical mirror, we will again obtain a system of cones. But they will no longer be coaxial, and the axis of each reflected cone will serve as a tangent to the mirror - a straight line, which, together with the reflection point, also contains a small piece of wire.
