It was created using a point contact on a semiconductor substrate and had dimensions on the order of a centimeter. Soon such transistors began to be actively used in electronics as amplifiers of electrical signals or interrupters controlled by electrical signals and almost everywhere replaced vacuum tubes. In 1958, Jack Kirby creates the first semiconductor chip (microcircuit), consisting of only two transistors. Very little time passes, and microcircuits appear that combine tens and hundreds of transistors on a common substrate. Along with progress in the creation of processors based on semiconductor electronics for processing data, methods for storing data are also developing. Magnetic cores and drums, distant ancestors of modern hard drives, are beginning to be used as such storage devices. Along with the rapid development of the material elements of computers, logical solutions when carrying out calculations, as well as the architecture of the programs used, change significantly. The outstanding mathematician John von Neumann in 1946 presents the work “Preliminary consideration of the logical design of an electronic computing device,” in which he not only describes the electronic computing machine itself, but also discusses in detail the logic used in its work, and justifies the use of a binary system for representing data in a computer. From this point on, computers became the object of scientific research, and even today some scientists continue to call the computer a “von Neumann machine.” All these fundamental discoveries and inventions mark the beginning of the second period of the computer age (1955–1964). Third generation computers are created based on integrated circuits (1965–1974). Massive magnetic storage devices are being replaced by smaller and at the same time more capacious semiconductor storage devices. They are still used today in personal computers as random access storage devices. The successes of theoretical cybernetics, physics and technology achieved in the last third of the 20th century contributed to the rapid development of computer technology, its miniaturization and sharp reduction in cost. All this made the computer available to the general public. Since the mid-70s, computers have become an important element of everyday life. Unprecedented advances in technology now allow tens of millions of transistors to be placed in large integrated circuits in the same 3 square centimeter area that Kirby used. Thus, modern memory elements make it possible to store the contents of the entire collection of one of the largest libraries in the world - the US Library of Congress (which is 150 million books and documents) - in one laptop. More transistors per square centimeter, a higher clock frequency (characterizing the speed of the computer) - these requirements have become an obsession with manufacturers. In 2004, Intel's Prescott processor, made using 90-nanometer technology (this means, in particular, that several million transistors were located on one square centimeter of its area), contained 150 million transistors and operated with a clock frequency of 3.4 GHz. In 2007–2008, manufacturers have already switched to 45-nanometer technology, and by 2013 the transition to 25-nanometer is expected. However, this direct way to increase the speed and memory capacity of computers is sooner or later exhausted. For example, one of the obvious limitations on the path to further miniaturization is the finite speed of propagation of the electromagnetic signal in the circuit: its specific value depends on the specifics of the technical implementation of the processor, but it definitely cannot exceed the speed of light c = 300,000 km/s.
