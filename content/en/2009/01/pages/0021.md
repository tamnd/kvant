SCIENCE NEWS

PREMIUM FOR VIOLATIONS

The Nobel Prize in Physics in 2008 was awarded for fundamental theoretical research on the breaking of global symmetries in the world of elementary particles. The premium itself is also asymmetrical. Half of the prize was received by 87-year-old American physicist of Japanese origin Yoichiro Nambu (Enrico Fermi Institute of the University of Chicago, USA). He completed his main work more than 50 years ago (together with the Italian physicist Giovanni Jona-Lazigno) at the University of Osaka (Japan). The wording of the award is “for pioneering ideas about the possibility of spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry.” The second half of the prize was shared by two Japanese physicists, Makoto Kobayashi (High Energy Accelerator Research Center, Tsukuba, Japan) and Toshihide Maskawa (Institute of Theoretical Physics, Kyoto University, Japan) “for their discovery of the nature of broken symmetry, which predicts the existence of at least three families of quarks in nature.” It is not easy to understand these formulations. It would seem that in the problem of symmetry breaking the very concept of “symmetry” is not so complicated. However, we are not talking about trivial spatial symmetries, for example in crystals. Back in 1927, the creator of geochemistry, natural scientist and philosopher V.I. Vernadsky wrote: “What was new in science was not the identification of the principle of symmetry, but the identification of its universality.” The modern concept of symmetry includes: a) an object or phenomenon whose symmetry is being considered; b) transformations in relation to which symmetry is considered; c) invariance (immutability, preservation) of some properties of the object, expressing the symmetry in question. This concept of symmetry is broader than the concept of “laws of nature.” We can say that symmetry creates laws and underlies them. For example, the symmetry of parallel transport in space (in other words, homogeneity of space) corresponds to the law of conservation of momentum; the symmetry of transfer of the beginning of time (uniformity of time) corresponds to the law of conservation of energy. Much more complex and not entirely obvious symmetries can be discovered in the world of elementary particles. Chiral (in chemistry this is right-handed or left-handed) symmetry in the case of subatomic particles is the invariance of the equations that describe their behavior with respect to changes in the so-called hidden helicity of the particles.

According to the Nambu hypothesis, due to the interaction of particles with the physical vacuum, this internal symmetry can spontaneously break with a finite probability. The objects of transformed properties in the Nambu model were hypothetical very small particles, from which, in the event of symmetry breaking, mesons, baryons and hyperons could be “formed”. By that time, dozens of the latter had already been discovered at accelerators, and soon their number exceeded three hundred - a whole “zoo” appeared, as physicists joked then. The work of Nambu and Jon-Lazinho was the first attempt to restore order in this “zoo”, to identify the most fundamental “building blocks of existence”. She prepared the appearance 10 years later (1964) of the quark model of hadrons. The work of Kobayashi and Maskawa is related to the violation of two other symmetries in the world of elementary particles - spatial and charge. Spatial symmetry in this case is associated with mirror image. In everyday life, we are accustomed to the fact that in the mirror, left changes to right and vice versa. About micro-objects of modern physics, which, after transforming the reflection, turn into their “antipodes”, they are said to be odd (their “parity” is –1). In the world of elementary particles there are both even and odd particles. The rules associated with the preservation of parity in microworld processes are considered as a manifestation of P-symmetry (from the English parity). Charge symmetry is understood as the conservation of total charges, each separately - electric, baryon and lepton, in the processes of interaction of objects with each other. For particles and antiparticles these charges have opposite signs. Charge symmetry is designated as C-symmetry (charge). In 1956, a parity violation was discovered - it turned out that the rule of preserving parity was not a law. Then, in 1957, the hypothesis of the conservation of “combined parity”, the so-called CP invariance, was formulated - if parity changes, then the signs of the charges must change along with it, i.e. particles must be replaced by antiparticles (L. Landau, T. Lee, A. Salam and C. Yang). Experimental confirmation immediately followed (the talented experimental physicist Ts. Wu). The hypothesis became a law. When the 3-quark hypothesis of Hellmann and Zweig appeared in 1964, it took this law into account. And suddenly, at the same time, a case of violation of CP invariance was discovered (in experiments studying the decays of neutral Kmeson). It seemed that the quark hypothesis was refuted. But it was then that Kobayashi and Maskawa put forward their hypothesis about the possibility of spontaneous breaking of this symmetry. To do this, they had to assume that in nature there are not three quarks, but six - three pairs of quarks. Today, physicists talk about three “families” of quarks and their corresponding three “families” of leptons. This is the essence of the modern so-called standard model of the microworld. So, three physicists, two works, both are predecessors of future discoveries and both are related to symmetry violations. The Nobel “reward for violations,” although many years later, found its heroes.

Yoichiro Nambu
