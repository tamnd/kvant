21st century: in search of a new paradigm

In view of these difficulties that arose in the way of directly increasing the clock frequency of a traditional processor, since 2005 the two leading manufacturers Intel and AMD were actually forced to move to the implementation of a fundamentally new architecture of the “computer brain” - a multi-core processor. At first these were dual-core processors, and now processors with four or more cores are on the way. Such devices combine two or more independent processors that have a common memory and its controller. This architecture allows you to significantly increase the speed of your computer without increasing the clock frequency and, therefore, avoid the problems listed above. However, you should not think that with two cores the performance simply doubles. The situation turns out to be much more complicated: the performance of a multi-core processor depends significantly on the type of problem being solved, and on software based on new (taking advantage of parallelism) algorithms. In some cases, performance actually almost doubles, in other tasks this is not the case. Although the development of multi-core technologies is an effective engineering solution, it remains a half-measure and is not a qualitatively new direction for the further development of computer technology. The first microscopic length that should be kept in mind when miniaturizing elementary processor blocks is the mean free path of electrons $l_{e}$ when they move inside metal layers. Let us recall that Ohm's law is based on the assumption of a diffuse nature of motion, during which an electron, moving along a conductor, experiences multiple scatterings on impurities or other defects. In this case, the specific location of the defects becomes unimportant - the transport properties are determined by the electron mean free path $l_{e}$, averaged over the position of the impurities. Therefore, metal bridges between elementary transistors in a microcircuit can be considered ordinary resistors, obeying Ohm’s law only as long as their dimensions L remain much larger than the mean free path of electrons: $L_{?} l_{e}$ (in sputtered metal films used in microcircuits $l_{e}$ turns out to be on the order of nanometers). In the opposite limit, $L_{=} l_{e}$, the electron motion has a ballistic character, i.e. the electrons, instead of diffusion at a constant average speed, are simply accelerated by the electric field throughout the conductor.
