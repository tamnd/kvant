particles and fields

Almost every time, fragments from the works of outstanding scientists, preceding the next issue of our column, are built into a kind of “short course” in the history of one or another physical direction. The current topic turned out to be so vast and vast that almost all branches of modern physics are strung together on it, the chronological boundary of which is called the very end of the 19th century, when brilliant achievements followed one after another - such as the discoveries of the electron and radioactivity. We can say that the discovery of elementary charged particles - the tiny structural components of matter - seemed to spur researchers to further searches that promised a completely new understanding of the world around us. But not only that: studying and controlling microscopic “invisibles” required such cunning combinations of fields, such equipment, such technologies that literally before our eyes began to change production and everyday life, energy and communications - in a word, led to the industrial and information revolution, the fruits of which we are still reaping. Today, on the eve of the launch in Switzerland of the largest and most powerful particle accelerator ever created by the human mind, we expect unprecedented discoveries that will develop, and perhaps even revolutionize, our knowledge about the structure of matter and the structure of the Universe. In the expectation that you will still have many complex and interesting problems concerning particles and fields, let’s start with simpler, but no less exciting problems.

Questions and tasks 1. Why in room conditions, even with all precautions, is the electroscope bound to run out? 2. An uncharged metal disk is set into rapid rotation. An electrometer connected via contacts to the center and periphery of the disk shows that a potential difference has arisen between them. What is her sign?

3. What trajectory will an electron describe when flying between the plates of a flat capacitor, which are supplied with: a) constant voltage; b) alternating voltage of sufficiently high frequency? 4. Two intersecting planes are uniformly charged with a negative charge. A radioactive source is placed at a certain point between the planes. What are the trajectories of positively and negatively charged particles emitted by the source? 5. In the absence of an external electric field, electrons emitted from the cathode of a vacuum diode are not scattered in the surrounding space, but mostly return to the cathode surface. How can this be explained? 6. The speed of electron movement between the electrodes of a vacuum diode reaches thousands of kilometers per second, and in the metal conductors of the anode circuit - millimeters per second. Are the currents the same in the diode and in the conductors? 7. Collimated beam of charged particles, i.e. a beam in which all particles have the same direction of velocity gradually diverges under the influence of Coulomb forces. How does this effect depend on the particle speed? 8. On what characteristics of a particle does the shape of its trajectory depend when moving in a gravitational field, electric field, or magnetic field? 9. How will the radius of the trajectory of a charged particle moving in a circle in a uniform magnetic field change with increasing its specific charge? 10. Electric current flows through a long straight metal wire. Is it possible to get rid of its magnetic field by rushing along the wire at a speed equal to the average speed of the ordered movement of electrons in it? 11. Why does a particle rotating in a non-uniform magnetic field “reflect” from the region where the induction of this field increases? 12. Is it possible to consider the trajectory of charged particles flying into the chamber as an arc of a circle?
