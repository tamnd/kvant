We will give here another reasoning, based on the fact that each branch separately can be considered a thin cylindrical mirror. Computer model. Let's model the crown of a tree with a random set of thin cylindrical mirrors - segments of unit length located between the point source and the observer. Each point of such a segment, after reflection, emits a cone. We want to find a point on this segment such that one of the generatrices of the corresponding cone falls into the eye of the observer. In fact, due to the extent of the source, the reflection we need will occur not only from this point, but also from a small section of the branch containing this point. On figure 5 on the left there is a crown represented by a set of random single segments, the source-observer axis is perpendicular to the plane figure. On the right are small (1/10 in length) sections of segments that reflect the rays into the observer’s eye. In general, these sections actually form a pattern similar to that shown in figure 4. Let us also note that in the “crown” there are individual segments that do not fit along concentric circles. But they are all short in length, which means that they are almost parallel to the source-observer axis. Model made from pins. Here is another model, this time physical, which confirms this fact (Fig.6). Here, ordinary pins lying on the glass are used as reflectors. Scratches on the glass. By the way, pins are not at all necessary; you can get by with just one piece of glass - the scratches on it will be reflected (Fig.7). This example is also described in Minnaert’s book, and the author adds that the scratches on the glass are already thin enough for light to manifest its wave properties when interacting with them. Light diffracts onto them, and we see them colored in the colors of the spectrum. Looking through a coil of wire. Finally, the latest model. We have already said that a cylindrical mirror can be curved. This time, let's take a whole coil of tangled wire as a reflector and look through it at the light source (Fig.8). We see the same ring patterns and are convinced that the coil of wire successfully simulates the reflective properties of the tree crown in the same way. The wire we used in the experiment is quite thin - its diameter is only 0.2 mm. Therefore, a corresponding diffraction pattern appears here too.
