When metrology did not have the prefix “nano”

Since ancient times, most measures of length were built based on the convenience of their use. For example, the old Russian sazhen, or straight sazhen, was equal to the distance from the end of the fingers of one horizontally extended hand to the end of the fingers of the other outstretched hand. By decree of Nicholas I, in 1835 Russian and English measures of length were brought into conformity. He decided that one fathom should be equal to seven English feet (from the English foot - foot). When converted to meters, it turns out that 1 straight fathom is equal to 2.1336 meters. Oblique fathom is another old Russian unit of length, equal to 2.48 meters. Initially, an oblique fathom was defined as the distance from the toes of the left foot to the tips of the fingers of the diagonally extended right hand. These were easy-to-use measures. However, using them, we obtained... different lengths. The need and importance of accurate and accurate measurements was well understood by Dmitry Ivanovich Mendeleev, who was a pioneer not only in the creation of the periodic table of elements. It was obvious to him that in a society with growing industrial production, the issue of measurements and metrology takes on key importance. The rule of the inverse piezoelectric effect is very simple - the change in the size of the piezoelectric plate $\Delta D$ is directly proportional to the applied voltage $\Delta U : \Delta D = d_{33} \Delta U$, where the proportionality coefficient $d_{33}$ is the piezoelectric module. The use of two numbers in the module index shows that in the general case we can apply stress in one of three directions (along the coordinate axes X, Y or Z), and the change in dimensions can be observed in any other direction. In our case, the direction of the electric field coincides with the direction of movement, so two identical numbers 3 are used. It is easy to calculate that the piezoelectric module has 9 components in total: $d_{11}, d_{12}, d_{13}$, ... $, d_{33}$. (Such physical quantities are called second-order tensors.) Let's take ceramics with a piezoelectric module $d_{33} = = 200 \cdot 10^{-} 12$ Cl N $= 200 \cdot 10^{-} 12$ m V. If we apply a voltage of 5 V to the electrodes, then the change in the dimensions of the plate will be 1 nm. Note that the change in the size of the piezo plate does not depend on the thickness of the plate itself. And this is very good, because when creating a length standard, we do not have to think about the length of the standard itself. Thus, for our nanometer we can already formulate the main thesis: a nanometer is the distance by which the size of a plate made of piezoceramics with a piezoelectric module $d_{33} = 200 \cdot 10^{-} 12$ Cl N $= 200 \cdot 10^{-} 12$ m V changes when a voltage of 5 V is applied to its electrodes. In our standard, we used just such a piezoelectric plate and battery. If the battery has a voltage greater than 5 V, then we will need two electrical resistances, with the help of which we will make a voltage divider with an output of 5 V. And if we find piezoceramics with a different value of the piezoelectric module, then by selecting the required voltage on the divider, we will be able to fulfill the condition $\Delta D = 1$ nm for this case as well.

There are little tricks in every business. The manufacture of our nanometer has its own “tricks”. Here's the first one. If we use a piezoelectric plate made of piezoceramics with a piezoelectric module $d_{33} = 200 \cdot 10^{-} 12$ Cl N, then after each application of a voltage of 5 V, the size of the plate will change by 1 nm. Between voltage applications, it is very important not to forget to short-circuit the plate plates with a conductor. Piezoceramics is a very good insulator: once applied to the plates, the voltage will remain on them for a long time. This can last for hours or days, it all depends on the humidity of the air and the cleanliness of the surface of the record. By shorting the electrodes of the plate with a conductor, we allow the negative charge to flow down the conductor to the opposite plate so that the voltage on the plate becomes zero and the plate returns to its original dimensions. And one more important “trifle”. Electrical voltage can be applied so that the piezo plate becomes thicker, or so that the plate becomes thinner. In other words, the battery can be connected with a plus to the upper electrode, or it can be connected with a plus to the bottom electrode. In both cases, the plate will change its size by 1 nm. Is there a difference in how the voltage is applied? To answer this question, we need to tell you how piezoceramics are made. Piezoceramics are made from powder, which is sintered at high temperature and preferably at high pressure. This produces a solid material, which on a microscopic scale consists

from individual grains. In each grain, the positive charges are slightly shifted relative to the negative ones (in general, the grains are electrically neutral), and this charge shift causes the grain to have a dipole moment. When exposed to an electric field, such a grain will stretch or shrink. In freshly baked piezoceramics, the dipole moments of individual grains have random directions, the inverse piezoelectric effect is observed for each grain separately, and there is generally no directional movement of the plate. In a constant electric field, some of the grains will stretch, and some will compress in the direction of the field, but in general the size of the piezoceramic sample will not change. To cause directional movement, all dipole moments of individual grains must be oriented in the same way. To do this, piezoceramics are placed in an electric field of 1000 V/mm or more and heated to a temperature of about $300 ^\circ$ C. This process is called ceramic polarization. Ceramics cannot be overheated, because at a certain temperature (Curie point) the piezoelectric effect disappears. If polarization is successful, then all grains have dipole moments directed in one direction, while the polarizing field stretches the ceramics. If an electric field of the opposite direction is applied, a depolarization process will occur, compressing the ceramics. This is where the answer to our question lies: how to apply voltage in a nanometer standard - so that the ceramics stretches or contracts. The correct answer is this. If we want to avoid possible depolarization of the ceramic, then the voltage must be applied in such a way that only elongation of the plate always occurs.

We check the nanometer and become nanometrologists

Piezoceramics have a number of disadvantages - nonlinearity, hysteresis, creep. Nonlinearity manifests itself in deviations from the simple formula $\Delta D = d_{33} \Delta U$. Hysteresis is due to the “memory” of the material to the history of the applied voltage, as a result of which it may turn out that at the same applied voltage the elongations of the plate will be different. Due to creep, or creep, the elongation of piezoceramics may lag behind the applied voltage in time. Will these effects reduce the accuracy of our nanometer standard? Practically not, if only two values ​​of electrical voltage are strictly periodically applied to the standard electrodes: 5 V and 0 V. All the disadvantages of piezoceramics - nonlinearity, hysteresis, creep - will be present, but their contribution will always be the same. And if so, then by carefully choosing the voltage, we can achieve an elongation of the piezoelectric plate by exactly one nanometer. Concluding our story, we note that if you were not able to make your own nanometer standard, but understood everything in this article, then we can assume that you are already an accomplished nanometrologist. Economics of nanometer fabrication

Perhaps you still have an unresolved question about where to find a piezoceramic plate. In fact, such records literally surround us in our daily lives. Bells in watches, mobile phones and computers are often made from piezoceramic plates. True, they have one peculiarity. There, piezoelectric plates are glued to an elastic metal plate. As a result, when the dimensions of the piezoelectric plate change, such a glued structure undergoes additional bending and the center of the structure moves a much greater distance than the free plate. Therefore, if we want to achieve a displacement of 1 nm, then it is better to use just a piezoceramic plate - otherwise the applied voltage should be significantly less and the stability of such a system is much less. In addition, due to the difference in temperature coefficients of piezoceramics and metal, the glued structure will additionally bend when the ambient temperature changes. Where can you find the right piezo plate? It turns out that they are sold in electronic accessory stores. The cost of one record is about 10 rubles. Then you can calculate the budget for our development of a nanometer standard: battery - 40 rubles, wires - 8 rubles, piezoelectric plate - 10 rubles, searching and ordering a plate on the Internet (paying for traffic) - 35 rubles, delivery of a piezoelectric plate (if you don’t go yourself) - 150 rubles, your own work - evaluate it yourself. This is the economics of producing a one-nanometer standard.

We will consider two such expressions (K – L) and (M – N) to be equal as soon as K + N = M + L.
