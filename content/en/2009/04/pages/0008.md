In each grain, the positive charges are slightly shifted relative to the negative ones (in general, the grains are electrically neutral), and this charge shift causes the grain to have a dipole moment. When exposed to an electric field, such a grain will stretch or shrink. In freshly baked piezoceramics, the dipole moments of individual grains have random directions, the inverse piezoelectric effect is observed for each grain separately, and there is generally no directional movement of the plate. In a constant electric field, some of the grains will stretch, and some will compress in the direction of the field, but in general the size of the piezoceramic sample will not change. To cause directional movement, all dipole moments of individual grains must be oriented in the same way. To do this, piezoceramics are placed in an electric field of 1000 V/mm or more and heated to a temperature of about $300 ^\circ$ C. This process is called ceramic polarization. Ceramics cannot be overheated, because at a certain temperature (Curie point) the piezoelectric effect disappears. If polarization is successful, then all grains have dipole moments directed in one direction, while the polarizing field stretches the ceramics. If an electric field of the opposite direction is applied, a depolarization process will occur, compressing the ceramics. This is where the answer to our question lies: how to apply voltage in a nanometer standard - so that the ceramics stretches or contracts. The correct answer is this. If we want to avoid possible depolarization of the ceramic, then the voltage must be applied in such a way that only elongation of the plate always occurs.

We check the nanometer and become nanometrologists

Piezoceramics have a number of disadvantages - nonlinearity, hysteresis, creep. Nonlinearity manifests itself in deviations from the simple formula $\Delta D = d_{33} \Delta U$. Hysteresis is due to the “memory” of the material to the history of the applied voltage, as a result of which it may turn out that at the same applied voltage the elongations of the plate will be different. Due to creep, or creep, the elongation of piezoceramics may lag behind the applied voltage in time. Will these effects reduce the accuracy of our nanometer standard? Practically not, if only two values ​​of electrical voltage are strictly periodically applied to the standard electrodes: 5 V and 0 V. All the disadvantages of piezoceramics - nonlinearity, hysteresis, creep - will be present, but their contribution will always be the same. And if so, then by carefully choosing the voltage, we can achieve an elongation of the piezoelectric plate by exactly one nanometer. Concluding our story, we note that if you were not able to make your own nanometer standard, but understood everything in this article, then we can assume that you are already an accomplished nanometrologist. (After all, not every nanometrologist uses a self-made nanometrologist.) If you took the trouble, found a piezoelectric plate and built your own nanometer, then we can say that you are the most advanced nanometrologist. A very important question remains outside the scope of the article: how to make sure that our plate extends exactly one nanometer? In other words, how can the created nanometer be linked to the primary standard of the meter? Unfortunately, this procedure cannot be done at home. You need to contact the metrological service, and there, using an interferometer, they will be able to accurately determine the movement of the plate. How this is done is a topic for another article.

Economics of nanometer fabrication

Perhaps you still have an unresolved question about where to find a piezoceramic plate. In fact, such records literally surround us in our daily lives. Bells in watches, mobile phones and computers are often made from piezoceramic plates. True, they have one peculiarity. There, piezoelectric plates are glued to an elastic metal plate. As a result, when the dimensions of the piezoelectric plate change, such a glued structure undergoes additional bending and the center of the structure moves a much greater distance than the free plate. Therefore, if we want to achieve a displacement of 1 nm, then it is better to use just a piezoceramic plate - otherwise the applied voltage should be significantly less and the stability of such a system is much less. In addition, due to the difference in temperature coefficients of piezoceramics and metal, the glued structure will additionally bend when the ambient temperature changes. Where can you find the right piezo plate? It turns out that they are sold in electronic accessory stores. The cost of one record is about 10 rubles. Then you can calculate the budget for our development of a nanometer standard: battery - 40 rubles, wires - 8 rubles, piezoelectric plate - 10 rubles, searching and ordering a plate on the Internet (paying for traffic) - 35 rubles, delivery of a piezoelectric plate (if you don’t go yourself) - 150 rubles, your own work - evaluate it yourself. This is the economics of producing a one-nanometer standard.
