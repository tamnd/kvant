Competition named after A.P. Savin

"Math 6–8"

We are starting another competition to solve mathematical problems for students in grades 6–8. Send solutions to problems within a month after receiving this issue of the journal to the address: 119296 Moscow, Leninsky Prospekt, 64-A, «Kvant» or by email: math@kvant.info (marked “Competition “Mathematics 6–8”). Be sure to include your name, class, and home address. As before, we welcome participation in the competition not only by individual schoolchildren, but also by mathematical clubs. We ask circle leaders to provide an email address or contact phone number. According to tradition, the winning clubs of the correspondence competition are invited to the final in-person tournament.

1. A chess piece that can move both as a rook and as a knight is called a chancellor, and a piece that combines the capabilities of a queen and a knight is called a maharajah. Place four chancellors and four maharajas on the chessboard $8 \times 8$ so that none of the pieces beats any other. A. Gribalko

2. Numbers A and B are called friendly if the sum of all divisors of A, except A itself, is equal to B, and the sum of all divisors of B, except B itself, is equal to A. (For example, 220 and 284 are friendly.) Take two friendly numbers A and B. Then find the sum of the inverses of the divisors of A, subtract one and get the result $\alpha .$ Do the same for B, get the result $\beta .$ What is the product $\alpha \beta$? G. Galperin

3. There are 100 cities in the country. Some pairs of cities are connected by road, and there is an air connection between any two cities. It is known that every city has an odd number of roads. The traveler wants to travel along each road exactly once (in one of two directions). What is the least number of flights he will have to make for this? P. Kozhevnikov 4. We will call a natural number a cozy if one of the numbers a – 1 and a + 1 is simple, and the other is composite. Prove that there are infinitely many cozy numbers. D. Shvetsov 5. In the quadrilateral ABCD, the diagonal AC is the bisector of the angle BCD, while the angle BCD is equal to $120 ^\circ$ and the angle BAD is equal to $30 ^\circ$. Prove that the perimeter of triangle BCD is equal to the length of diagonal AC. V.Proizvolov

About one well-forgotten old problem

V. DOTSENKO, K. SHRAMOV

Bears rode on a bicycle, And behind them crayfish On a lame dog, And behind them a cat Back to front... K. Chukovsky Introduction In the spring of 2005, the authors of this article selected problems for entrance interviews to the 9th mathematical class of the 57th school in Moscow. For one of the last interviews, it was decided to select several little-known (at least to eighth-graders) quite meaningful problems, the solution of each of which would be - for one reason or another - a serious argument in favor of the fact that the student who solved it would be able to study in a mathematics class. Here is the condition of one of them: The distance between cities A and B is 30 km. Three tourists want to get from city A to city B. They have a motorcycle on which each of them can travel at a speed of 60 km/h, and a bicycle on which each of them can travel at a speed of 15 km/h. On foot, each of them can move at a speed of 6 km/h. Any of the means of transportation can be left on the road so that one of the remaining tourists can use it. a) Prove that tourists will not be able to organize their trip so that the last one to arrive will spend less than 2.5 hours on the journey.
