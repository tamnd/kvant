Subtracting polygons

“Let me go and meet the Queen,” said Alice. Of course, she was interested in chatting with the flowers, but how can they compare with the real Queen! - Towards? – Rose asked. - So you will never meet her! I would advise you to go in the opposite direction!

It is impossible to determine the Minkowski difference if you limit yourself to only convex polygons. Indeed, when added, the polygons do not become smaller (more precisely, the sum of K and L contains a parallel translation of each of the terms - this is clearly visible in figure 3). Therefore, it is impossible to subtract a large one from a small polygon: in this case, the small polygon would contain a parallel translation of the large one. How can we intelligently extend the class of convex polygons to make subtraction possible? If you take the definition of a sum and try to trick it by adapting it for subtraction, then nothing good will come of it (try it!). There is a universal algebraic technique: we will call the difference of two polygons K and L the formal expression (K – L), without giving the latter any geometric meaning. We will consider two such expressions (K – L) and (M – N) to be equal as soon as K + N = M + L. It is easy to check that such formal expressions can be added (according to the rule (K – L) + (M – N) = ((K + M) – (L + N)), and each (K – L) has an inverse element (L – K). You have already encountered this technique before in mathematics lessons - when moving from positive numbers to negative numbers or from integers to rational numbers fractions. However, we want to deal not with formal differences, but with geometric objects. Why is this so? The fact is that a polygon (or a polyhedron in space) has a rich geometric structure: it has edges, vertices, the concept of area, etc. We would like to have all this wealth for Minkowski differences.
