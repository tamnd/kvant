Where is the Moon looking?

Observations indicate that the lunar ball always faces the Earth with predominantly one side. But why, how did such a bright feature in the movement of the Moon arise? The famous mechanic and mathematician Lagrange was the first to come close to answering this difficult question. Back in 1764, he established that “the shape of the Moon should be an ellipsoid with the largest axis directed towards the Earth < we clarify that, strictly speaking, this axis is not directed towards the Earth, but makes slight oscillations relative to the point of the second focus >. And even if initially the period of rotation of the Moon did not coincide with the period of revolution around the Earth, still after some time these two periods should have become equal due to the influence of the Earth’s gravity and the associated tidal dissipation of the rotational energy of our satellite.” In his Principia, Newton writes: “...As a result of this... it happens that the same side of the Moon is observed from the Earth; in another position, the body of the Moon could not be at rest, but would constantly return to this position, oscillating. But these oscillations, due to the smallness of the acting forces, would occur very slowly, so that the side that should be constantly facing the Earth could also be facing the other focus of the lunar orbit... without immediately being pulled back and turned towards the Earth.” Definitely, Newton is talking here about a very subtle effect: due to optical libration, the slightly elongated figure of the Moon should also perform very small torsional vibrations, but no longer apparent, but actually occurring relative to its center of mass. This is the first prediction of the physical libration of the Moon. But at the same time, Newton describes the phenomenon of optical libration itself too vaguely and even ambiguously. Indeed, from his sentence, highlighted above in italics, it is impossible to understand how the side of the Moon, which should be constantly facing the Earth, could be facing another focus of the lunar orbit. After all, the distance between both foci of the Moon’s orbit reaches a value of 42207 km, which is 3.3 times the diameter of the Earth - not so small. Therefore, Newton's words are more puzzling than clarifying the essence of the matter. Elsewhere in the “Principles” we read: “Since for the Moon, with its uniform revolution around its axis, a day is equal to our month, then almost always the same side will always face the lower focus of its orbit...” “Towards the lower” means to the second focus of the orbit. The ellipse, as is known, has two foci: in one is the center of the Earth (more precisely, the Earth-Moon system), and on the other, according to Newton’s opinion expressed here (in italics), almost always the same side of the Moon will always face. However, this phrase of Newton does not add any certainty to the first statement. How can the selected radius of the Moon almost constantly be directed towards the same point in outer space? After all, the process of optical libration occurs continuously. And as we will now see, in fact, everything is the other way around: the selected radius of the Moon is almost always not directed (!) towards the point of the second focus, but oscillates relative to the direction towards it. While speaking about Newton's error, nevertheless, a misunderstanding of the essence of the matter regarding the actual direction of the selected radius of the Moon during optical libration along longitude is manifested in Newton quite clearly. There is no sense of the precise clarity characteristic of this great scientist, whose authority is still unusually high. It is not surprising that some ambiguity in the description of this phenomenon is also found in popular science literature. The remarkable popularizer of science Ya.I. Perelman in the book “Entertaining Astronomy” first writes: “... when the Moon is in E, the radius of the Moon facing the Earth at point A will describe an arc at $90^\circ$ and will be directed ... to a point ... not far from the other focus of the lunar orbit.” And below we read: “...The Moon invariably faces the same side not to the Earth, but to another focus of its orbit...”. Here the first phrase (true!) comes into conflict with the second. The famous Russian astronomer V.I. Bronshten, in his informative book “How the Moon Moves,” cites, without any comments, Newton’s above-mentioned view of the nature of the Moon’s motion. And even a prominent scientist, an expert on the rotation of the Moon, Moutsoulas, in the famous collective monograph “Physics and Astronomy of the Moon” (translated from English), speaking about Newton’s prediction of physical libration, quotes his words also, unfortunately, without any critical comments.

The secret role of the second focus

Any ellipse that is not degenerate into a circle has two foci. In astronomy, Kepler's first law states: at one of the foci there is an attracting center - for the planets it is the Sun, for the Moon the Earth, etc. The point of the second focus for the planets does not play any physical role and is simply a geometric point. A different situation arises when describing the synchronous motion of the Moon. Here the second focus is kinematically distinguished precisely by the fact that the main axis of the Moon makes slight oscillations around the direction towards it. The role of the second focus is the same for other satellites with synchronous rotation in the Solar System. However, this second focus does not have any special physical meaning for the four Galilean satellites of Jupiter, since it is located deep inside Jupiter itself. But the Earth-Moon system is unique in that only here the point of the second focus lies far beyond the Earth (more than three of its diameters), and it is no longer possible to ignore this fact in modern celestial mechanics.
