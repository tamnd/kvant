Quests

1. Is there a three-digit number divisible by 11 in which the first digit is greater than the second and the second is greater than the third?

2. The numbers 2.75 and 8 have the property that the product is equal to the sum of their constituent digits: 2.75 • 8=2+7+5+8=22. Find at least one more such pair of numbers.

3. It is not difficult to cut a large 4x6 rectangle by figure into two parts so that from them and the 1x1 square you can add a 5x5 square (see Fig.). But try cutting each of these two figures into two identical parts so that you can again form a 5x5 square from them.

4. Five terdes: blue, yellow, grey, brown and red lay in a pile in some order. They were laid out on the table. First the top one, then the one following it, etc. As a result, we got two stacks, shown in figure 1. Then we put them in a stack in the wrong order, and then laid them out again on the table, also removing the terdals from the top of the stack. This time we got two stacks, shown in figure 2. In what order were the terdals in the stack?

5. One day I was walking with two friends outside the city. We noticed the “humming” telephone wires. I asked others why the wires were humming. “Because they are the talk of the town,” said one. “No,” laughed another, “simply because the current flows through them. After all, the high-voltage transmission wires are also humming.” What do you think?

These tasks were offered to us by: D. G. Polyak, M. V. Varda, N. K. Antonovich, V. V. Proizvolov, A. P. Savin.

Today, a wide variety of components have been created, their number is growing every year, and this makes it possible to successfully solve problems that were previously considered insoluble. Therefore, when analyzing specific tasks, it is always useful to consider the option of using components. This is, perhaps, all the material science information we need.

What? Have you lost your mind? Do you think that if you make a composite from two components, one of which is low-melting and the other is refractory, then this material can operate at a temperature higher than the melting point of the low-melting component? But not higher than the melting point of the refractory component!

It must upset you. I meant the operation of materials precisely at temperatures higher than the melting point of the most refractory component. Well, are you ready to say that this is from the realm of science fiction?

No, this is completely real. Such materials exist and are successfully used in new technology.

What kind of materials are these? They are called pseudoalloys. Today, pseudo-alloys tungsten-copper, tungsten-serbro, molybdenum-copper and others have been widely used in space technology. Before we tell you how they are structured and work, let's remember something from school physics.

When studying the aggregative states of matter, you learned that the melting processes of pure crystalline substances occur at a constant (for a given pressure) temperature $T_{пп}$, called the melting point. Melting of a grated body can only occur if the body is continuously brought to it. The number of tellots that must be brought to melt to unite the mass of the solid body at a constant temperature $T_{пп}$ is called specific temperature $\lambda$. This is how the liquid boils. The temperature of the liquid and the vessel in which it boils does not increase, but all the liquid turns into steam. The amount of tellote $L$ required to convert a unit mass of liquid into vapor at a boiling point $T_{кпп}$ is called the specific boiling point ($3380^\circ\text{C}$). Has this already been done so that tungsten nozzles can operate reliably at such temperatures?

Imagine that we need to make a rocket engine nozzle. The temperature of the gases that form during the removal of solid fuel and flow from the nozzle reaches $4000^\circ\text{C}$. This is higher than the melting point of the hard metal itself, tungsten ($3380^\circ\text{C}$). What can be done so that tungsten nozzles can operate reliably at such temperatures?

Let's do it this way. We will make a nozzle not from solid, monolithic, but from porous tungsten. This can be done using powder metallurgy methods, where tungsten powder is pressed and baked. As a result of such operations, tungsten is obtained in the form of a porous sponge. By changing pressing and sintering, the number and size of pores can be adjusted. Next, this porous tungsten must be impregnated with a low-melting metal - copper or silver. What you get will be a pseudo-alloy of tungsten - copper or tungsten - silver.
