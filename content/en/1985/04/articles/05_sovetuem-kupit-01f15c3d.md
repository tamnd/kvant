Now let's see what mass of water will fall on the area Δx. Let the number of drops whose radii lie in the interval from r to r+Δr be equal to Δn(r). We will choose the value Δr so small that the masses of the drops can be considered identical and equal to m(r) = $\frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \varphi$. Then the mass of water that these drops will bring to the ground is equal to

$$\Delta M = m(r) \cdot \Delta n(r) = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \varphi \cdot \Delta n(r).$$

Finally, we formulate the requirement for droplet uniformity:

$$\frac{\Delta M}{\Delta x} = -\frac{4 \pi r^3 \varphi r^3}{6 uh} \frac{\Delta n(r)}{\Delta r} =$$

$$= -\frac{2 \pi \varphi}{3 uh} r^6 \frac{\Delta n(r)}{\Delta r} = C,$$

(5)

where C is a constant. But $\frac{\Delta n(r)}{\Delta r}$ in the limit ($\Delta r \rightarrow 0$) is the distribution function of drops over their radii that we are looking for!

So, $f(r) = \lim_{\Delta r \rightarrow 0} \frac{\Delta n(r)}{\Delta r} = K \frac{1}{r^6}$, that is $f(r) \sim r^{-6}$.

This is a very large addiction. Suppose we have chosen two identical intervals of the droplet radius Δr in the vicinity of two different values ​​corresponding to small ($r_1$) and large ($r_2$) droplets. Let their radii differ by a factor of two: $r_2 = 2r_1$. Then from

(6) the conclusion follows: within this interval Δr, the number of drops Δn(r) should be $(r_2/r_1)^6 = 2^6 = 64$ times greater than the number of large ones ($\Delta n(r_2)$).

Now, knowing the length of the irrigated area L = x_max - x_min (see figure 4), you can use formula (4) to find the smallest and largest radii of droplets r_min and r_max, their total number (formula (2)) and the total mass of water “issued” per unit time by the source (in part, the constant C in formula (5) also includes the width of the area - after all, it is clear that the total mass of water should be is proportional to the width of the area being irrigated, but in order to get rid of this trivial thought, we can assume that we carried out calculations per unit of its width, or per unit of pipe length). All these practical calculations can be done because we have found the nature of the droplet size distribution function. And not necessarily drops of water for irrigation: it can be dissolved to combat harmful insects or weeds, or a suspension of small seeds, or drops of paint to cover large areas, or...

So, we have found the function of droplet size distribution that should be “provided” by the designer using a set of nozzles.
