Rutherford's experiments and the phenomenon of radioactivity

Rutherford's experiments, described in § 93 of Physics 10, are doubly related to the phenomenon of radioactivity. On the one hand, in these experiments positively charged fast alpha particles emitted by radioactive substances were used to bombard atoms of heavy elements. Radioactivity, therefore, is for experiments. On the other hand, the results of Rutherford's experiments, which led to the creation of a planetary model of the atom, made it possible to approach the explanation of the very phenomenon of radioactivity, which had previously been extremely mysterious.

From Rutherford's experiments it was found that the atom of any element consists of a positively charged nucleus, the size of which is ten or even hundreds of thousands of times smaller than an atom, and negatively charged electrons that form the electron shell. It turned out that the electric charge of the nucleus, if expressed in terms of the charge of the electron, taken as one, is equal to the ordinal number of the element in the periodic table. In the symbolic notation of the nucleus, their charge is expressed by a serial number, which is written as a subscript when symbolizing the element. The superscript denotes the mass of the nucleus, expressed in atomic mass units. For example, the nuclei of iron and gold atoms are symbolically written like this:

$$\frac{56}{26}\text{Fe}; \frac{197}{79}\text{Au}.$$

Radioactivity is a nuclear phenomenon. In what part of the atom—the nucleus or the electron shell—does the process occur that leads to the emission of radioactive radiation (alpha, beta, and gamma radiation)?

It is not difficult to understand that it is all about the atomic core. This can be seen, firstly, from the fact that one of the types of radiation - alpha radiation - is a stream of fast, positively charged particles, and is simply not present in the electron shell. True, another type of radiation - beta radiation, consisting of electrons - in principle

could also come from the electron shell. However, and this is secondly, the energy of beta particles is so high (it reaches millions of electrons), that the electron shell clearly cannot provide such energy. It is known, for example, that in chemical reactions in which atoms participate with their electron shells, the energy per atom is approximately 1 eV. The energy of visible light quanta, which undoubtedly arise in the electron shell, is also about 1 eV. Only the energy of quanta penetrates rays (they are also emitted by the electron shell) can reach even ten thousand electrons. The electron shell cannot communicate its “own” electrons to the electrons.

Thus, both alpha and beta particles can only be emitted as a result of some processes in the atomic nucleus. The same can be said about Gamma radiation.

Radioactive transformations. So, radioactive radiation is emitted by the nuclei of atoms of radioactive elements. The charge of the nucleus is equal to the serial number of the element in the periodic table. Then it follows directly from the law of conservation of electric charge that when any nucleus emits a charged particle, the charge of the nucleus must change. This means that it turns into the atomic core of another element occupying another cell of the table.

As we found out, an alpha particle has a positive electrical charge, twice the charge of an electron, and an internal mass greater than an atomic mass unit. Therefore, the nucleus that emitted an alpha particle turns into the nucleus of an atomic element, whose atomic number is two units less than the original one, and its mass is four units less. If, for example, a complete radioactive element with serial number 84 and a relative atomic mass of 218 emits an alpha particle, then it turns into the nucleus of an atom of the element occupying cell number 82 of the table (this is lead) with a relative atomic mass of 214:

$$\text{Po} \rightarrow \text{Pb} + \alpha.$$

Since the α particle represents the nucleus of the gel atom, this process can be written differently:

$$\text{Po} \rightarrow \text{Pb} + \frac{1}{2} \text{He}.$$

What happens, as they say, *pasnads* the polonium core into two parts - the lead core and the gel core.
