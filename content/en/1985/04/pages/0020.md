It turns out that this rule contains the “secret” of the lever action. If, for example, a load is lifted with the help of a lever (Fig. 2), then the positive work of force $\vec{F}$ (work on the “input”) is equal to the negative work of gravity $m\vec{g}$ (work on the “output”). But from figure 2 it is clear that the movement of the lifted load is less than the movement of the end of the lever to which the force $\vec{F}$ is applied. According to formula (3), the model of gravity applied to the load is the same number of times greater than $F$. The ratio of the force “output” to the force “input” (according to the model) is called the gear ratio of the lever (sometimes it is also called the mechanical gain of the lever). The lever allows, as they say, to “win” in strength. But you have to pay for every win. And the price to pay is the “loss” in displacement: the displacement “at the input” is the same number of times greater than the displacement “at the output”, and the force “at the output” is so many times greater than the force “at the input”.

This rule, applied not only to the lever but to all other simple machines, is known as the “golden rule of mechanics.”

Another simple machine is the inclined plane. Formula (3) is valid not only for the lever. Another device, also called a simple machine, is a smooth inclined plane. If, for example, it is necessary to lift a load of mass $m$ to a height $h$, then with uniform vertical movement a force must be applied to the load equal to the force of gravity of the load $m\vec{g}$. But if we move this load uniformly along an inclined plane of length $l$, then for this there is enough force, so many times less than $m\vec{g}$, how many times $l$ is more than $h$. The gain in force (transfer number) is therefore equal to the ratio $l/h$. But $l/h=1/\sin \varphi$, so the gain in force for an inclined plane is $1/\sin \varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle of inclination of the plane to the horizon.

The inclined plane, like the lever, has been used by man since time immemorial. Varieties of the inclined plane have also been known for a long time - wedge and screw. The wedge is complex together

The bases are two inclined planes, the screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a rod. As an example of a screw device, a jack is shown at figure 3. From figure 3 it is clear that in order to replace the load at a height $h$ (screw pitch), the end of the handle to which the force is applied must travel a path equal to $2\pi R$, where $R$ is the distance from the screw axis to the end of the handle. It is clear that the gain in force in this case is equal to the ratio $2\pi R/h$. The “input” force is therefore $2\pi R/h$ times less than the “output” force.
