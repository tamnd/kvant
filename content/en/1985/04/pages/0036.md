Why is it called pseudoalloy? Because this is not a real alloy, whose crystal lattice consists of dissimilar atoms. Here, mixing of components at the atomic level does not occur. Tungsten and copper or silver do not dissolve in each other, they cannot be alloyed, they can only be mechanically displaced. In this case, we have obtained a pseudo-alloy with a structure consisting of a tungsten frame, the pores of which are filled with copper (silver).

Of course, this situation will not persist for any length of time. After all the melting copper has turned into steam, the temperature of the pseudo-alloy begins to rise again, and when it reaches a value $T_3$ equal to the melting point of tungsten $(3380\,^\circ\text{C})$, the material will melt. Strictly speaking, it is not the pseudo-alloy that will melt (it will actually no longer exist), but the tungsten frame, which cannot work at such a temperature without copper. Tungsten turns out to be strong as long as it has a weaker, but loyal friend - copper, capable of sacrificing itself, evaporating to the last atom in order to ease the fate of its friend.

However, other copper materials, like copper in humans, require compatibility. For example, in high-temperature pseudo-alloys, copper or silver turn out to be good friends with tungsten, but nickel or iron are poor friends. The fact is that copper and silver separate themselves as a sacrifice, not the cause of harm to tungsten, but nickel and iron do not agree to die alone. As the temperature rises, they begin to intensively corrode tungsten. As a result, it abruptly falls firmly from the frame, it becomes unequally capable. You need to be able to choose the other one.
