Princess Dido, isopermeters and soap films

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. V. TROFIMOV

This article deals with one class of elementary formulated problems known back in Ancient Greece. Tells how these problems arose, how they are solved mathematically, and how their solutions can be simulated using soap films. The article is accessible to eighth graders, but we hope it will also prohibit older readers.

The Legend of Princess Dido

A long time ago, the Physic princess Dido with a small detachment of people devoted to her left her hometown of Tire, fleeing the betrayal of her brother, the tyrant Pygmalion. Her ships sailed west across the Mediterranean Sea and dust, driven by a fair wind, until Dido found a convenient place for settlement on the African coast, in what is now the Gulf of Tunisia.

The Physicians who landed were not greeted very hospitably by the local residents, the Numidians. The King of the Numidians, Yarb, spoke belligerently and politely to the uninvited guest. He accepted the jewelry Dido offered for the purchase of land, but resolutely declared that in return he agreed to give her only a key of land, “which can be surrounded by a bull’s hide.” The princess meekly agreed. Yarb realized the cunning and cunning of the physicist too late: Dido made a thin, long belt from a bull’s skin and used it to fence off a significant area from the shore. The simple-minded but honest Yarb did not refuse this word. And Dido founded the city of Carthage on this site. In memory of this story, the Carthaginian citadel was named "Birsa", which in the language of the inhabitants of Carthage means "bull's skin".

Note that the size of the original territory could have been quite large. Let's imagine the bull skin as a rectangular piece 1x2 m and we will assume that Dido cut the skin into strips 1 mm wide according to the pattern shown in figure 1. Then it is easy to calculate the length of the resulting remon - it will be equal to approximately 2 km, so that Dido could in this case hatch from the shore (which we will consider straight), say, a rectangular section of 0.5 km² = 500 m×1000 m.

The legend dates the events described to 825 BC. e. and, of course, it is difficult for us to judge their reliability. But it is not the historical events that interest us here, but the mathematical problem that Dido apparently had to solve:

Problem 1 (Dido's problem).

How should a shaft of physical length L be positioned so that it separates a piece of land of maximum area from the straight bank?

Mouse films

For those readers who have begun to doubt the existence of a solution to isopermetric problems, as well as for all lovers of red physics experiments, we offer several very simple experiments with soap film that confirm the correctness of our solutions. Of course, physical experiment cannot replace a strict mathematical proof; it only makes the result more plausible.

To test the first - the simplest - experiments, you will need a saturated soap solution (it is better to use a good extinguishing soap, you can add a little glycerin), thin wire, ordinary thread; for the next one you will also need small rings (sliding freely along the wire) and several straight thin straws.

First experience Immerse the wire frame in the soap solution and carefully remove it from it. The frame will be covered with a thin film of soapy water. It floats on the film, having an irregular shape (photo 1). Then pierce the film inside the loop with a hot corner - the loop instantly and accurately takes the shape of a circle (photo 2).

The described phenomenon is explained as follows: from general physical principles it follows that a stable equilibrium position of a soap film corresponds to the minimum energy of its tension.
