Physicists to the front

Academician I. K. Kikoin

The Great Patriotic War shook up the entire Soviet people, including people involved in science, and of course, physicists. It is clear that technical science plays a significant role in the creation of modern weapons.

Of course, major military-scientific problems are solved in advance, and this requires a lot of time. However, some issues related to the improvement of military equipment needed and could have been resolved during the war, in a relatively short time.

Of the major developments that were prepared before the war and took several years, the first thing you should know is radar. The idea of ​​radar itself is very simple. It lies in the fact that electromagnetic waves, reaching metal objects, are reflected from them, and from the reflected waves it is possible, at least in principle, to determine the position of the object at any time. This very phenomenon was discovered by the founder of radio engineering A.S. Popov, who noticed that the passage of ships interfered with the transmission of radio signals. From the idea to the technical question there was a huge path that still had to be covered. The fact is that radio waves that reach the surface of a metal object are reflected to an insignificant degree, because some of them are absorbed, while the rest are scattered by the object in all directions. In the application of reflected radio waves, only a small part of the energy initially directed at the object being studied, say, an airplane, enters. And this insignificant part had to be registered.

The first attempt at a technical solution to this problem dates back to the early thirties. Then, over the course of several years, the technology developed and improved, and already in 1939, our army adopted the RUS - radio-controlled aircraft. Soon the problem of combining the source of electromagnetic waves and the application of reflected waves in one station was solved. It was called a radar - a radar station, and as far as I know, scientific priority, that is, primacy in its development belongs to Soviet physicists.

Today's radars are very different from those that were used at the beginning of the Great Patriotic War, for the simple reason that semiconductor technology had not yet been developed, everything was done with lamps - both generators and applications. They switched to the resulting systems during the war. Such radars could already be installed in reality, and not just on the ground. This made it possible to conduct a battle, that is, to “see” an enemy aircraft in the dark.

The successful use of radar to detect moving military objects - aircraft, etc. - played a huge role in military affairs and contributed to our victory.

Approximately the same fate befell the method of protecting warships from enemy magnetic mines. Let me briefly remind you of the very idea of ​​​​such mines and how one could defend against them.

On the one hand, this interferes with navigation using a compass as an indicator of the direction of the ship's movement. The ship distorts the true direction of the earth's magnetic field, comes to study the influence of the steel hull on the compass.

But, on the other hand, this magnetic field successful by the ship can be traversed in this way. It is capable of activating some mechanism that rotates under the influence of magnetic force and closes an electrical circuit. This circuit can include a detonator immersed in the mine's explosive material. Such magnetic mines differ from ordinary mines, which a ship directly bumps into and thereby causes an explosion, in that they lie on the bottom of the sea and explode at a distance - under the influence only of the ship’s magnetic field.

It was known that these mines were being diluted in many countries, and, it was believed, were at the disposal of the naval forces of Nazi Germany. The task of combating magnetic mines was set several years before the start of the war at the Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute. It was necessary to “demagnetize” the ships in order to eliminate the successful immi magnetic field. Which way?

On the ship, large coils of wires were placed in a special way through which electric current was passed. It generated a magnetic field that compensated for the ship's field, that is, a field in the direct opposite direction. By the beginning of the war, the gap was initially resolved, and it had to be transferred to a technical footing, that is, to create such devices on existing ships of the Soviet fleet.

It was organized very quickly. All warships were subjected to “antimagnetic” treatment in ports and went to sea demagnetic. Thus, many thousands of lives of military sailors were saved. It is clear that such work required the knowledge of physicists and physical laboratories, which predetermined its success.

Before the war, another problem was solved, seemingly not directly related to military operations, but its solution had a significant impact on technical work.

In wartime, a lot of aluminum is consumed for aircraft, copper for shells. These materials receive power by producing them in large quantities, and currents reaching tens of thousands of amperes pass through the power supply to the bath. So, at one of the aluminum plants that was launched before the war, it was supposed to use such high currents. The training method of changing currents with jesters was not suitable; the jesters were designed for a maximum of 10 thousand amperes. In order to be able to regulate, it was necessary to come up with a way to change it without having jesters.

Here it was impossible to do without physical knowledge. It is clear that when such a large current passes through a conductor, the magnetic field it creates is also quite large. By measuring the field, we learned how the current flows. Unfortunately, the shape of the wires was not cylindrical; it was a series of straight bars folded in parallel. And the connection between the magnetic field and the current flowing through such a conductor was very difficult to establish; there were no ready-made formulas.

Nevertheless, physicists managed to carry out calculations and develop a device that, first on a model and then directly at the factory, made it possible to measure the field and current. Few such devices were needed, only a few dozen copies, and they were manufactured directly in the laboratory within a relatively short time.

Just before the start of the war, the plant was regulated, commissioned, giant currents were used for the first time, and its products were sent to aviation factories. And here the help of physicists turned out to be very useful.

The capacity of the machine is, naturally, not fully utilized.

Ural physicists have come up with a good way to continuously change the diameter of the journal without stopping the grinding machine. To do this, a small device was placed near the obtitudinal neck, reacting to the magnetic field of the neck. It, of course, depends on the distance between the device and the surface of the neck. As grinding progressed and the diameter of the part decreased, the instrument readings also changed. It was only necessary to observe its arrow and, when its specific mark on the scale was reached, stop the machine and remove the part - all without intermediate changes.

Such continuous devices were installed in large workshops where crankshafts were manufactured. The productivity of grinding machines has increased dramatically. This means that the number of engines produced and, accordingly, tanks have increased. This primo contributed to the growth of military technology on the battlefields. So during the war, a very problem was solved within a few months.

Another example. Before the familiar Battle of Kursk in 1943, the Germans began to produce new types of tanks - “Panthers” and “Tigers”. These were tanks with sharply thickened armor, which shells penetrated with difficulty or did not penetrate at all. A few months before the battle, our troops managed to capture several of these tanks and determine how strong their armor was. To pierce it, it was necessary to come up with shells with better characters. This task was entrusted to our metallurgists at one of the Moscow institutes.

It is well known that to increase the workability of steel, tungsten should be added to it. However, it melts at a very high temperature; the general technology for smelting such steel is very difficult. It was impossible to organize the smelting of tungsten steel on a massive scale; the industry was not prepared for this. And so the institute’s staff proposed making projectile heads from metal powder with the addition of tungsten powder. Fine powder sinteres quite well at higher temperatures. The heads were also made using the powder metallurgy method, and they turned out to be uncut.

Tests carried out with the new shells showed that they easily penetrate the thickest armor of Tigers and Panthers. The mass production of projectiles with tungsten steel heads was established quite quickly. And when our artillery met German tanks on the Kursk Bulge, it became clear that the plans of the Hitlerite command related to the invulnerability of the

And, of course, physicists did not remain aloof from the task of managing the armor of our tanks. In the Soviet Union, solid state physics was widely developed, especially at the Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute. The director of the Leningrad Physics and Technology Institute, Academician A.F. Ioffe, and his staff were studying special stele, including those from which armor is made.

It is therefore not surprising that the knowledge and experience of these physicists was used during the war. The armor of our tanks was largely successful and met the highest requirements of wartime science and technology. Thus, specialists in the field of metal physics directly participated in the creation of formidable weapons for the Soviet Army, our armored forces.

I think a short story about land magnetic mines will be interesting. At the beginning of the war, representatives of the engineering troops approached scientists with a request to find out whether it was possible to develop such a mine not for ships, but for tanks. A tank, of course, weighs much less than a ship, ten tons. Perhaps its magnetic field is not very strong. I had to check.

This work was done in the Urals. The physicists were provided with several tanks. We measured the magnetic field underneath it at different depths. It turned out that the field was quite replaceable, and it was possible to try using a magnetic mechanism to detonate the tanks. However, an important additional requirement was set: the mine itself must contain as little metal as possible. After all, by that time mine launchers had already been developed.

It was necessary to come up with a special alloy for its different “compass” needle, which closes the target containing a small battery, an alloy that is easily magnetized under the influence of the tanks field. As a result of the work, the total amount of metal was limited to 2-3 grams per mine, and the alloy magnet was really good, which allows not only tank, but also automatically. It is impossible not to mention a physical problem of enormous importance, which was solved by the physicists who remained in Leningrad during the siege. As you know, the only way connecting the city with the country was the Road of Life, continued along the ice of Lake Ladoga. The question was whether and to what extent it was possible to carry cargo along the road in early winter or spring, when the lake had just frozen or was beginning to thaw. It was necessary to directly change the ice, indicate what load it could withstand.

Leningrad physicists, together with hydrologists and sailors, did a wonderful job, solving this problem brilliantly. They found a way to determine the strength of the ice pores. Primo on site, measurements were taken at different points of Lake Ladoga day and night. They were guided by practical principles, figuring out at what distance and with what load the machines should move. Without these instructions, there would have been many accidents, many people would have died, and the city would not have received enough food and ammunition. This is an example of good
