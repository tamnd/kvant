⟦folio 28⟧

Physics 8, 9, 10

The published book “Impulse and Kinetic Energy” is intended for the distribution of force over the duration of its action, that is, the impulse of force (“Physics 8”, § 50).

The relationship between the kinetic energy of a body and force looks like this:

$$E_{k2} - E_{k1} = Fs \cos \alpha = A,$$

where $s$ is the magnitude of the body displacement vector, $\alpha$ is the angle between vectors $\vec{F}$ and $\vec{s}$, and the product $Fs \cos \alpha = A$ is the work done by the force on the body. Thus, this entry means that the change in the kinetic energy of the body is equal to the work of the force applied to the body (“Physics 8”, § 55).

The role of impulse and kinetic energy in the description of motion. The difference between the quantities $\vec{P}$ and $E_k$ is not limited to the fact that one of them is vector and the other scalar.

From equality (1) it is clear that if the force $\vec{F}$ is given, the change in momentum is determined only by the time of action of the force and does not depend on which body it is applied to. Any body (a body of any mass) under the influence of a given force for a given time will change its momentum by the same amount. Further, if, for example, the heroes of Krylov’s fable “The Elephant and the Pug” collided with each other, then the impulse of each of them would change equally.

Equality (2) says that for a given force, the change in the kinetic energy of a body is determined only by the distance traveled by the body in the direction of the force (the product $s \cos \alpha$ is the projection of the displacement onto the direction of the force). Any body under the influence of a given force at a given distance will change its kinetic energy by the same amount.

Thus, the change in momentum is related to the amount of time it takes for a body to change its speed, and the change in kinetic energy is related to the distance that must complement the design of its velocity (for a given force, of course).

In other words, a change in momentum is a characteristic of the action of a force in time, and a change in kinetic energy is a characteristic of the action of a force in space.

⟦folio 29⟧

Another difference between momentum and kinetic energy. Perhaps the most substitutive property of the momentum of a body is that the total impulse of a closed system of bodies (that is, the vector sum of the impulses of all bodies of the system) remains unchanged under any interactions and any movements of the bodies of this system. This is the law of conservation of momentum (“Physics 8”, § 51).

Does it have the property of conservation of kinetic energy? Let's look at a simple example, from which it will immediately become clear that in this case momentum and kinetic energy differ from each other.

Let two identical steel balls of mass $m$ each move along a smooth (without friction) horizontal surface with equal absolute speeds $v$ (see figure). At some point, the balls will touch, then they will continue to approach each other for some time, then they will stop for a moment and after that they will begin to diverge until they begin to move in opposite directions with the same speeds.

Let's see what the total impulses and kinetic energies of both balls are equal to before, during and after the collision.

Before the collision, the momenta of the balls are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, therefore

$$\vec{P}_1 + \vec{P}_2 = (m\vec{v}) + (-m\vec{v}) = 0,$$

and the kinetic energies of the balls are the same and equal to $mv^2/2$ each, so

$$E_{k1} + E_{k2} = \frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{mv^2}{2} = mv^2.$$

During a collision, at that moment in time when both balls are at rest, their velocities are zero, therefore,

$$\vec{P}_1 + \vec{P}_2 = 0,$$

$$E_{k1} + E_{k2} = 0.$$

After the collision, the balls, as before the collision, move in opposite directions with the same absolute velocities, therefore

$$\vec{P}_1 + \vec{P}_2 = -m\vec{v} + m\vec{v} = 0,$$

$$E_{k1} + E_{k2} = \frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{mv^2}{2} = mv^2.$$

Thus, the total momentum of the system of two balls stays the same all the time. The kinetic energy before and after the collision is the same (it is this kind of collision that is called elastic), but during the collision it changes, so that at some point it even temporarily disappeared completely. This means that there is a law of conservation of momentum, but there is no law of conservation of kinetic energy.

Note that if the balls were not made of steel, but of soft clay or plasticine, then upon collision they were cut down, left and no longer moved (such a collision is called absolutely inelastic). Their total momentum would still be zero, and the kinetic energy during the collision would disappear not temporarily, but forever.

As is known, the conservation law also exists for energy, but not for kinetic, but for total energy, which includes other forms of energy. So, in our example, the temporary loss of kinetic energy during the elastic collision of balls only means the fact that at that moment it was completely converted into potential energy of elastic deformation of the balls. At the end of the collision, the potential energy was again completely converted into kinetic energy. During an inelastic impact, the kinetic energy of the balls is converted into internal energy (the balls heat up), which is no longer converted back into kinetic energy.
