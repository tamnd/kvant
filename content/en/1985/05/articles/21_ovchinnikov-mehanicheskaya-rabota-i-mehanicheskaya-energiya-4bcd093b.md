Mechanical work and mechanical energy

O. Yu. Ovchinnikov

§ 1. In mechanics, the work done by a constant force $\vec{F}$ when moving a body by an amount $\vec{s}$ is equal to the product of the force and displacement models multiplied by the cosine of the angle $\alpha$ between the force and displacement vectors:

$$A = Fs \cos \alpha.$$

This concept does not always correspond to the general idea of ​​work. For example, a weightlifter holding a load in his arms, or a porter carrying a heavy suitcase, do not perform mechanical work (explain why?).

When the force acting on a body is not constant (its model or direction changes), the work of such a force can be found as follows. Let us divide the entire displacement of the body into such small areas $\vec{s}_1, \vec{s}_2, \dots, \vec{s}_n$ that on each of them the force can be considered constant and equal to the corresponding $\vec{F}_1, \vec{F}_2, \dots, \vec{F}_n$. Then we will find work at each site:

$$A_1 = F_1s_1 \cos \alpha_1,$$

$$A_2 = F_2s_2 \cos \alpha_2, \dots,$$

$$A_n = F_ns_n \cos \alpha_n;$$

and the total work will be equal to the sum of the work in individual areas:

$$A = A_1 + A_2 + \dots + A_n.$$

In cases where it is known how the projection of force on the direction of movement $F_s = F$ cos $\alpha$ changes from point to point, the work can be found graphically (Fig. 1). The total work on section BC is numerically equal to the area of ​​the figure BDEC.

Problem 2. A homogeneous chain of length 2l and a system of two rods connected by a hinge, each of length l (Fig. 3, a), are suspended from points B and C, located on the same horizontal line. The mass of the chain is equal to the mass of the common rods. Which center of gravity - a chain or a system of rods - is located below?

By acting on the chain so that its position coincides with the position of the system of rods (Fig. 3, b). It is obvious that in this case the centers of gravity of the chain and rods also coincide. Since in order to move the chain to a new position it was necessary to do some work on it, it can be argued that its new position of the center of gravity is higher than the previous one.

Consequently, the initial center of gravity of the chain is the center of gravity of the rod system.

§2. In mechanics, it is customary to distinguish between kinetic energy, caused by movement, and potential energy, determined by the relative position of the body of a system or part of the same body.

The kinetic energy of a body of mass $m$ moving with speed $v$ is equal to

$$E_k = \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

The potential energy of a body located in a gravitational field at a height $h$ above the zero level is equal to

$$E_p = mgh,$$

and the potential energy of an elastically deformed body is equal to

$$E_p = \frac{kx^2}{2}.$$

There is a close connection between the concepts “mechanical work” and “mechanical energy”. The work done by the equal forces applied to the system is equal to the change in the kinetic energy of the system:

$$A = E_{k2} - E_{k1}.$$

The work done by the forces of gravity or elasticity acting in a system is equal to the change in the potential energy of the system taken with the opposite sign:

$$A = -(E_{p2} - E_{p1}).$$

Problem 4. A long (compared to the size of the rosette) rubber with a hardness of $k$ is attached to the rod. Find the maximum speed of a stone with mass $m$ released from the rosette, if it is first pulled back to a distance $x$ (Fig. 5).

Since the rubber is long, we can assume that two parallel elastic forces $\vec{F}$ act on the stone. When the stone is pulled back by a distance $x$, the total elongation of the spring will be $2x$, and the elastic force model will be $F = k(2x) = 2kx$.

When the rubber returns to its original undeformed state, the elastic forces acting on the stone will do work

$$A = 2\frac{(2kx)x}{2} = 2kx^2.$$

§ 3. Energy belongs to those few physical quantities for which conservation laws are satisfied.

In particular, if the system of bodies is closed and the bodies interact with each other only through gravity and elasticity, then the total mechanical energy of the system (that is, the sum of kinetic and potential energy) remains constant. The action of the friction force leads to the fact that part of the mechanical energy is converted into the internal energy of the system. But even in this case, the sum of all types of energy in the system remains unchanged.

Problem 7. A boy on skates will accelerate to speed v and roll onto a hill covered with ice. To what height, counting from the base of the slide, can it lift if the friction coefficient is μ and the slope of the slide to the horizon is α?

At the initial moment, the boy has kinetic energy $$mv^2/2$$, and at the final moment - potential energy $$mgh$$ (here m is the boy’s mass, h is the desired lift height). Due to the decrease in total mechanical energy, work is done against the friction force $$F_{tp} = \mu mg\cos \alpha$$:

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} - mgh = (\mu mg \cos \alpha) \frac{h}{\sin \alpha}.$$

From here we find the desired height h:

$$h = \frac{v^2}{2g(1 + \mu ctg \alpha)}.$$

Problem 8. A stone of mass m slides off the main slide of height H. This process is measured by distance in two different inertial reporting systems. From the point of view of an observer in a stationary system, the potential energy of the stone mgH transforms into kinetic energy $$mv^2/2$$, so that after sliding down the hill the stone has a speed v (Fig. 7). Observes, being in a reporting system moving with speed v to the right, will say that at the beginning the stone has potential energy mgH and kinetic energy $$mv^2/2$$, and at the end it has neither one nor the other. Where did the energy “disappear”?*)

Let's say right away that the law of conservation of energy is also true in this case. The reason was formed in a paradox that the reasoning was carried out for a non-freezing system - one stone, and the Earth, with which the stone interacts, was not taken into account. Let's fix this error.

a) In a stationary reporting system (associated with the center of mass of the stone - Earth system), at the initial moment the stone and the Earth are at rest, and all energy is equal to the potential energy of the system. At the final moment, the stone acquires speed $\vec{v}$, and the Earth acquires speed $\vec{u}$, which can be found from the law of conservation of momentum:

$$mv - Mu = 0,$$

where

$$u = \frac{m}{M} v,$$

where M is the mass of the Earth.

According to the law of conservation of energy,

$$mgH = \frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{Mu^2}{2} = \frac{mv^2}{2} \left(1 + \frac{m}{M}\right).$$

Since the mass of the stone is nothing small compared to the mass of the Earth, the values ​​of m/M can be exceeded compared to unity. Then we get

$$mgH = \frac{mv^2}{2},$$
