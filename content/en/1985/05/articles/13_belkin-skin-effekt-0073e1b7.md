Skin effect

From the school physics course (“Physics 9”, § 94) it is known that due to the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction in any conductor under the influence of a changing magnetic field, it is called eddy currents (Foucault currents). They are caused by an inductive electric field that occurs when the magnetic field changes. The power lines of the induction electric field are always closed, therefore the Foucault currents are also closed (eddy). Since conductors usually have electrical resistance, the energy of Foucault currents turns into heat (the conductor heats up), and the currents themselves attenuate.

It turns out that quickly alternating Foucault currents do not penetrate deep into the conductor and the text is practically only in its surface layer. This phenomenon is called the skin effect (from the English word skin - skin). It must be taken into account when using alternating currents (and fields) in technology.

The cause of the skin effect can be qualitatively understood using Lenz's rules for electromagnetic induction. According to this rule, the direction of induced currents is always such that they “interfere” with their magnetic field the change in magnetic flux that these currents caused. Therefore, when a conductor is placed in an external alternating field, Foucault currents arise, which weaken this field in the conductor and shield it. Moreover, the further into the conductor from the surface, the smaller the total magnetic field becomes. Accordingly, the magnitude of the current in the conductor with depth also kills. The decrease occurs according to such a rapid law that, starting from a certain depth, the field and current can be considered practically equal to zero. This depth is called the skin layer.

What determines the thickness of the skin layer? On the one hand, the faster the magnetic field changes, the greater the electromotive force of induction appears and the more Foucault currents flow in the conductor. On the other hand, the current strength also depends on

resistivity of the conductor - in conductors with lower resistivity, larger Foucault currents are induced and the magnetic field is less shielded. Therefore, as the frequency of the field changes increases and the conductor resistance decreases, the thickness of the skin layer decreases.

An accurate calculation shows that for copper, for example, at a frequency of alternating current v = 50 Hz, the thickness of the skin layer is 1 cm. This is not a very small value, therefore, in a conductor with a diameter of up to several centimeters, the alternating current is distributed over the entire cross-section of the wire, and the skin effect is fully manifested in much thicker wires. At the same time, in the range of medium radio waves with a frequency v~10⁷ Hz, the thickness of the skin layer in copper is ≈10⁻³ cm. This is already a fairly small value. Fast alternating currents of such frequency are practical only on the surface of the wires. You can make conductors completely fields, and this will have almost no effect on their resistance. Sometimes, to reduce losses, conductors in radio circuits are coated with a thin layer of silver - currents flow only in this layer, and silver has less resistivity than copper.

Interestingly, in superconductors, induction currents do not attenuate at all (resistance is zero), and therefore they are able to shield even a constant magnetic field. In the thickness of the superconductor, the total induction of the external magnetic field and the magnetic field of the screening currents is zero. This phenomenon is called the Meissner effect*).

$$\frac{M}{Z} X \rightarrow \frac{M}{Z+1} Y + \frac{0}{-1} e.$$

Here $X$ is the original nucleus, $Y$ is the decay product, $e$ is the electron (the superscript “0” shows that the mass of the electron is very small compared to the atomic unit of mass).

A careful study of beta decay has shown that this phenomenon contains two mysteries.

Riddle one: “loss” of energy. If the $X$ nucleus spontaneously transforms into the $Y$ nucleus, then this means that the $W_X$ energy of the $X$ nucleus is greater than the $W_Y$ energy of the $Y$ nucleus. And the energy emitted by the beta particles should be equal to the energy difference $W_X - W_Y$ (if we neglect the recoil energies).

Since all the original $X$ nuclei are the same, as well as all the $Y$ nuclei receiving from them are the same, all emitted beta particles must have the same energy. Experiments show that the energy of almost all beta particles is less than the energy difference $W_X - W_Y$. More precisely: β-particles have different energies, and they all range from zero to a maximum value of $W_X - W_Y$. For example, for beta particles emitted by $^{210}_{83}$Bi nuclei (half-life 5 days), the maximum energy value is about 1 MeV, and the average energy per particle is less than 0.4 MeV.
