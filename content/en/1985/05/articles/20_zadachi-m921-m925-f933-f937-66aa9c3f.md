M903. Is there a convex polyhedron such that any section of it by a plane not passing through the vertex is a polygon with a) even; b) an odd number of sides?

We denote by $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ the first terms of the progressions into which the natural series is divided, and by $d_1, d_2, \dots, d_n$ their differences. The product of all differences is included in one of the progressions, that is, for some $i, 1 \leq i \leq n$, and some natural $k$

$$d_1 d_2 \dots d_n = a_i + k d_i.$$

From this equality it follows that $a_i$ is divisible by $d_i$.

A. V. Kelarev

a) Answer: exists. Let us show that the conditions of the problem are satisfied by any polyhedron from each vertex of which there is an even number of edges, for example, octahedр (see figure).

Let us consider an arbitrary section of such a polyhedron that does not pass through the vertices. Obviously, it can be read to be moved so that it becomes non-parallel to any of the edges of the polyhedron, and the number of its sides remains the same. We will now move the section plane in parallel. Then the number of its side will change only when crossing a vertex, and only one vertex will be traversed each time. If an even number of $n$ edges converge in it and $k$ of them intersected the section plane before passing through this vertex (and therefore, $n - k$ - after the transition; see figure), then the number of vertices of the section will transition to $n - 2k$ and, therefore, its parity will be preserved. And since at the end the cutting plane will not intersect any of the edges, the side number must remain even all the time, except when passing the vertices.

b) Answer: does not exist. From the solution to problem a) it follows that if all sections of a polyhedron that do not pass through the vertices have the same parity (in particular, odd), then an even number of edges emerge from each vertex. But then, as was shown there, the side number of any such section must be even.

N. V. Vasiliev, V. N. Dubrovsky

F913. A lump of cheese line was thrown from a horizontal surface of the earth at an angle $\alpha = 60^\circ$ to the horizon with a speed $v_1 = 12$ m/s. However, a lump of twice the mass was thrown onto the top of the first perumu at an angle of $\beta = 30^\circ$ to the horizon, and the initial velocities of the lumps turned out to be light in one vertical plane. As a result of the collision, the lumps were cut down. Find the speed (modulo) of a sawed lump falling to the ground.

◆ Consider the movement of the center of mass (c.m.) of the lumps. Speed ​​c. m. immediately before hitting the ground is equal in magnitude to speed c. m. when throwing lumps, and
$$3m\vec{v} = m\vec{v}_1 + 2m\vec{v}_2,$$
(1)
where $\vec{v}$ is the speed c. m. when thrown, $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ are the speeds of the lumps at this moment, $m$ is the mass less than the lump.

Let us place the origin of coordinates at the point of throwing the lump of mass $m$, directing the axis $X$ horizontal so that it passes through the point of throwing the large lump, and the axis $Y$ – vertical upward. Let us write equality (1) in projections on the $X$ and $Y$ axes, having previously divided both sides of it by $m$:

$$3v_x = v_1 \cos \alpha - 2v_2 \cos \beta,$$
(2)
$$3v_y = v_1 \sin \alpha + 2v_2 \sin \beta$$

(here $v_x$ and $v_y$ are projections of $\vec{v}$ on the $X$ and $Y$ axes). We find the speed $v_2$ from the condition that at the point of collision, which will occur at time $t$, the coordinate along the $Y$ axis of the lumps is the same:

$$v_1 t \sin \alpha - \frac{gt^2}{2} = v_2 t \sin \beta - \frac{gt^2}{2},$$

where

$$v_2 = v_1 \frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = v_1 \sqrt{3}.$$

Substituting the found expression for $v_2$ into (2), we obtain:

$$v_x = -\frac{5}{6} v_1, \quad v_y = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} v_1.$$

F914. A closed sauce with a volume of $V = 33,6$ dm³ contains nitrogen $u = 1$ moles of water. At a temperature of $t = 100^\circ\text{C}$ the pressure in the sauce is $p = 2 \cdot 10^5$ Pa. Determine the amount of nitrogen in the sauce.

The pressure $p$ in the sauce consists of the partial pressure of nitrogen $p_a$ and water vapor $p_n$. The maximum possible value $p_n$ is the saturated water vapor pressure $p_H$ at a given temperature. At $T = 373$ K $p_H = 10^5$ Pa. Let's see what the amount of $v_1$ steam in the sauce should be to create pressure $p_H$:

$$p_H V = v_1 RT \Rightarrow v_1 = \frac{p_H V}{RT} = \frac{10^5 \cdot 33,6 \cdot 10^{-3}}{8,3 \cdot 373} \text{ моль} \approx 1,1 \text{ моль.}$$

This means that when $v = 1$ mole of water evaporates, the resulting vapor will not be saturated. The pressure of this steam is

$$p_n = v \frac{RT}{V} \approx 0,9 \cdot 10^5 \text{ Pa.}$$

Therefore, the partial pressure of nitrogen in the sauce is

$$p_a = p - p_n \approx 1,1 \cdot 10^5 \text{ Pa},$$

and the amount of nitrogen in the sauce -

$$v_a = \frac{p_a V}{RT} \approx 1,2 \text{ моль.}$$

S. M. Korshunov

F915. A liquid with dielectric permeability flows between the plates of an insulated flat capacitor with a speed of $v$. A magnetic field with induction $\vec{B}$ is directed perpendicular to the speed $\vec{v}$ and parallel to the plates. Determine the direction on the capacitor plates. Distance between plates $d$.

Lorentz forces directed in opposite directions act on opposite charges of moving diopoles of the dielectric from the side of the magnetic field (see figure). This leads to polarization of the dielectric, and as a result of the upper and lower plates of the capacitor, surface charges of opposite signs will exist in the dielectric, equal in absolute value. These polarized charges create an electric field.

Let the direction of this field be equal to $E$ (at figure $\vec{E}$ directed from top to bottom). Then the force acting on the positive charge of the moving diopole is equal to

$$F = q (vB - E).$$

According to the definition of dielectric permeability, this force should be ε times less than the force $F_0$ that would act on the charge $q$, moving at a speed $\vec{v}$, in the absence of a dielectric. But in the absence of a dielectric $E = 0$, and $F_0 = qvB$. So

$$F = \frac{F_0}{\varepsilon} \Rightarrow q (vB - E) = q \frac{vB}{\varepsilon},$$

where is $E$ from:

$$E = \frac{\varepsilon - 1}{\varepsilon} vB.$$

$$U = Ed = \frac{\varepsilon - 1}{\varepsilon} vBd.$$

F916. In the circuit shown in figure 1 (D is an ideal diode), switch K is closed for a time τ and then opened. At the moment of opening the layer, the point in the inductor is equal to $I_0$. After closing the key K in a very short period of time, the direction to the capacitor and to the inductor becomes the emf of the source $\mathcal{E}$ (see Fig. 1: $\mathcal{E} = \varphi_A - \varphi_B$, the current through the diode is not flow). During time τ, a constant charge $q = C\mathcal{E}$ will remain on the capacitor, and the current through the coil will increase according to a linear law:

$$L \frac{di}{dt} = \mathcal{E} \Rightarrow i = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{L} t \quad (0 < t \leq \tau).$$

At moment $t = \tau$, according to the conditions, $i = I_0$, that is

$$I_0 = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{L} \tau.$$

If at this moment the key K is opened, then the coil will continue to work over time due to the discharge of the capacitor, as happens in a conventional oscillatory circuit, that is, according to the law with a period of $T = 2\pi \sqrt{LC}$.

When the current reaches its maximum value, the direction to the inductor, diode, capacitor becomes zero, that is, the capacitor is completely discharged. Based on the law of conservation of energy, we have:

$$\frac{LI_0^2}{2} + \frac{C\mathcal{E}^2}{2} = \frac{LI_{\max}^2}{2}.$$

Given that $I_{\max} = 2I_0 = \frac{2\mathcal{E}}{L} \tau$, we find $LC = 3\tau^2$.

$$\Delta t = \frac{\pi}{3} \tau \sqrt{3} \approx 1,8\tau.$$

At time $t = \tau + \Delta t \approx 2,8\tau$ the potential of points A and B are compared, the resistance of the section containing the diode in the direction from B to A becomes zero. Thus, at subsequent moments of time there will be exactly a constant current $I = 2I_0$ through the inductor and diode, the charge of the capacitor will be zero.
