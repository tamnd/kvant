1. Selecting targets

So, we launch to Halley's Comet. First of all, why go to her? Obviously, expeditions need to be prepared in advance - this is many years of labor-intensive work; but you can only prepare in advance for a meeting with some familiar comet, the frequency of which has been studied. Naturally, these are short-period comets. On the other hand, from the point of view of physical interests, it is clear that the comet must be active - bright, with brown gases, a “lush” tail, a developed atmosphere, a “serbez” ionosphere, etc. Active, as a rule, are young comets that approach the Sun for the first time, but such “heavenly guests” appear in the field of view, rapidly sweep through the perihelions and again go into "non-existence". It is impossible to prepare a spacecraft and launch it towards such a comet. (In Lermontov’s “Demon” there are construction sites about how

"...running comet
Hello with a gentle smile
I loved being with him...”
This is exactly the same situation - neither the spacecraft nor the comet will have time to convey anything but “hello” to each other.)

Pozny still has to focus on short-period comets. But such comets, as a rule, are not very active - often the passage of the Sun during their “life”, they lose summer substances and become of little interest, meetings with them do not cause much enthusiasm.

Halley's Comet is exceptional in this sense. Despite the fact that its appearance near the Sun (or close to the current) orbit of humans may take more than 100 thousand years, this comet continues to remain active. It's hard to say what the secret of her youth is, but maybe we'll find out after the first date.

2. The roads we choose

The Vega project arose because its author, the famous Soviet physicist Academician R.Z. Sagdeev, drew attention to the favorable location of Venus during the passage of Halley’s comet to its prehelion. The position of Venus accidentally turned out to be such that it became possible to combine, figuratively speaking, “business with pleasure.” In December 1984, the USSR planned to launch automatic stations towards Venus. But it turned out that a small maneuver in the gravitational field of Venus could be enough to direct the station, which had completed its “Venusian” research tasks, to Halley’s comet. Calculations showed that the idea was feasible, and the Venus - Halley's Comet project came to life.

Let us now turn to “the roads we choose.” In the language of science, there is little talk about the strategy for choosing the flight trajectory of spacecraft to Halley's Comet. This strategy is determined by the current level of space technology capabilities. The fact is that the Earth flies on its own

4. Date with Venus

Just as “the theater begins with a hanger,” “Veta” begins with Venus. It is impossible to talk about the project scenario without at least a brief description of the flight to Venus and the proposed research of this planet.

By the time you read these lines, most of the “first act” of the script will already be behind you. A multi-stage carrier package launched the space station along with the so-called booster stage into orbit of an artificial Earth satellite. On the first orbit, the booster stage was switched on, and the station switched to the flight path to Venus.

Two days before the flight to the planet, as already mentioned, Veta is divided into a descent module and a protean vehicle. When moving in the atmosphere of Venus, an aerostat probe is separated from the descent vehicle. According to the scientific program, the probe must drift in the atmosphere and transmit to Earth information about the speed of Venusian atmospheric currents, temperature, pressure and other parameters along its trajectory. An international network of radio telecopes will be used to receive this information on Earth. Therefore, both the descent module and the aerostat probe must enter the atmosphere of Venus in such a way that both at the entrance and during the entire drift time (1-2 days) they remain on the part of the disk of Venus visible from the Earth.

Qia can transmit to Earth only through a repeater. The protein apparatus will serve as such a relay. Then it turns out that the demands placed on him by the complete nature of the Veta experiment are mutually contradictory. The protean apparatus should enter the atmosphere of Venus, the descent vehicle and the aerostat probe, which do not have their own flight control systems in space, in a very definite way, but at the same time it itself should not enter the atmosphere of the planet - after all, it still has to fly further. In addition, during the operation of the descent vehicle on Venus, the protean vehicle must be in the communication zone in order to relay the received information to Earth. It turns out that in this case the protean apparatus cannot enter the flight path to Halley's comet. This is where the need for a gravitational maneuver arises - having completed its functions, the protean apparatus must rotate through the retransmission thrust and change its trajectory in order to head towards the comet.

5. From Venus to a comet

In 2-4 weeks after the passage of Venus, it is planned to launch the first protean apparatus on a flight path to Halley's comet. This is where the special fix begins, with which the specialist does not yet have to deal. The fact is that for the first time in the world, a flight is being carried out to a celestial body whose motion parameters at the moment of launch will not be known with the required accuracy. Quite often, experts made mistakes in predicting the moment when a comet would pass its perhelion, even when using modern mathematical calculation methods. Pozny absolutely needs to clarify the elements of the comet’s orbit using ground-based observations already during the flight to it. For this reason, an accurate celestial-mechanical theory of the motion of Halley's comet has now been created. Based on the analysis of data on its movement in 1759, 1835, 1910, this theory showed very good agreement with observations.

During the Venus-comet flight segment, three corrections will be made. The first has already been discussed. The second will be carried out in the middle of the flight and the third - 2-4 weeks before the flight to the comet. The first scientific research session begins two days before the flight, when the distance to the comet will be 14 million km. The second session begins one day before the flight at a distance of 7 million km. The third session begins when meeting a comet.

The question that remains quite uncertain and, accordingly, is of concern to everyone is the distance from the nucleus of Halley's comet at which the spacecraft will pass. Here everything is determined by the accuracy with which the comet’s orbit is known, and this accuracy is not too great. After this, the circumstance is due to the fact that in the perihelion region the comet will experience significant non-gravitational accelerations. They are caused mainly by the action of reactive forces that arise during the powerful evaporation of the ices that make up the comet's nucleus.

Based on previous passages of Comet Halley, the prediction of the location of the node of its orbit can be made with unsatisfactory accuracy - about 10⁶ km. And the diameter of the head of Comet Halley is only 4·10⁵ km. It’s so easy to miss, to fly past a mismo comet. If, however, we include in the calculations the already ongoing observations of the movement of this comet, then the accuracy with which the specialists of the Vega program can hit it increases. It is now expected that the spacecraft will fly at a distance of approximately 10⁴ km from the comet's nucleus.

And one more very important question: will the device fly through the comet’s atmosphere without any problems? As it approaches the nucleus, the density of cometary dust increases noticeably, and, although the mass of dust grains is small, the speeds at which they will “fly” onto the apparatus are enormous - we can assume that they are equal to the relative speed of the apparatus and the comet upon meeting, that is, close to 80 km/s. So the kinetic energy of the collected dust particles will be great, and, of course, the size of possible damage is directly dependent on it.

These messages forced the ship's creator to think through reliable protective measures. According to calculations by Soviet and foreign experts, the probability that damage will occur is only a few percent. The probability is very small, but still not zero.

During flights to the planets of the Solar System, where the meteorological danger is immeasurably lower, scientific and service information is recorded on a storage device, from which it is transmitted to Earth in communication sessions. This practice had to be abandoned, since the best have “a bird in their hands than a pie in the sky”: if damage to scientific or service equipment occurs, this will jeopardize the success of the expedition. Therefore, the Vega project provides for direct transmission of information to Earth. Of course, this required abandoning existing technical solutions. For example, the information content of the service radio complex responsible for sending scientific change data to Earth has been increased by more than 20 times. The radio antenna installed on the protenome apparatus will be directed at the Earth all the time when carried through the atmosphere of Comet Halley.
