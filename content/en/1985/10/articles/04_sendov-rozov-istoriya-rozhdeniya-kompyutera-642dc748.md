The history of the birth of the computer

Academician of the Bulgarian Academy of Sciences
Vl. X. SENDOV,

N. X. ROZOV

The article “Prehistory of the Birth of the Computer” (see «Kvant», 1985, No. 9) talked about the centuries-old path of man from counting on his fingers to an arithmetic meter. Now we will continue our journey through history - from the emergence of the theoretical idea of ​​“counting machines” to the creation of the first real computers. This stage in the development of computer technology, directly preceding the era of modern computers, took approximately a century.

On the verge of realization and the fantastic

The Great Industrial Revolution, marked by a gradual but steady transition from small-scale manual repair work to large-scale machine factory production, over time affected all areas of technology and gave a powerful impetus to the further progress of science. It was an era of great discoveries, outstanding inventions, fruitful ideas. Progress could not but ultimately manifest itself in computational form, connected with commercial, industrial and scientific and technical problems.

Since the beginning of the 19th century, the main attention of students interested in facilitating and facilitating calculations was focused on the comprehensive improvement of counting devices, which were becoming more and more widespread. The main purpose of a counting device (the most successful example is the Odner arithmetmeter) is to independently carry out any arithmetic operation on numbers.

Is it possible to create a machine that would not just perform individual arithmetic operations, but would “be able” to automatically carry out the entire chain of calculations necessary to solve the problem as a whole? This formulation of the question seems absolutely natural and logical today, but a hundred and fifty years ago even this thought itself was non-trivial and was marked by the discovery of a new direction in scientific research. The mental idea of ​​a computing machine was the Englishman Charles Babage (1791-1871).

A prominent mathematician, a talented engineer, a versatile inventor, a famous natural scientist, an inquisitive researcher, C. Babage, with a wide variety of interests and accomplished activities, spent his whole life tirelessly and persistently working on the development and design of computers.

In 1822, he built a model of a machine, which he called a “difference machine,” and began to create a large difference machine designed for calculating scientific and astronomical tables. However, this work was never completed for various reasons.

In 1834, C. Babidge began work on his main product - a computer, which he called “analytical” and which was supposed to automatically solve all the computational problems faced by engineers and mathematicians. The grandiose project of such a machine, developed by the author with amazing detail, was so ahead of its time that its practical implementation could not but turn out to be unrealistic.

What exactly is the step forward taken by Ch. Babidge in the development of computer technology? First of all, Babage's machines, both analytical and difference, are designed not to perform each arithmetic operation according to individual human commands, received each time after the completion of the previous operation, but to carry out the entire computational exchange as a whole without human intervention at intermediate stages. Moreover, if the difference engine was focused on one fully defined, unchanging program of actions with given numbers, then the analytical engine allowed the implementation of different calculation programs depending on the problem under consideration. In modern language, a difference engine is a specialized computer with a fixed program, and an analytical engine is a universal automatic calculation

Charles Babidge is the author of the project for the first universal software computer.

It should be noted that Babage's analytical engine is a single set of specialized units: a device for entering initial information (initial data), a storage device (memo), an arithmetic device (performing operations on numbers), a control device (ensuring the execution of operations in the required sequence), a device for entering processed information (result). In other words, this machine, a precursor to the architecture of modern computers, had virtually all of their current basic structural components.

However, he is a child of his era! - the Analytical Engine, like all the calculating devices that preceded it, was (and could not help but be) a purely mechanical calculator. After all, centuries of research in the field of electricity have passed in the first half; work on its use has only just begun. That is why the brilliant ideas of Charles Babage regarding the purpose, logical structure and principles of operation of a computer turned out to be stuck in clothes, predetermining the insanity

First Lady of the Computer Kingdom

As subsequent history showed, one of Ch. Babidge’s other ideas was his proposed system for controlling a computer using a punched card. The idea of ​​using cards with answers punched into them (punched cards) became widespread after the French inventor Joseph Marie Jacquard (1752-1834) used them to automate the operation of a loom in 1804. However, for the first time, Ch. Babidge had to engage in detailed development of many issues related to the introduction of arrays of numbers and sequences of commands into the machine using punched cards.

Controlling a computer is very much impossible without developing a theory of rational description of the sets of operations that the machine should perform when solving various problems. Thus, the emergence of a new direction in science—programming—was inevitable. The credit for creating this trend goes to Augusta Ada Lovelace (1815-1852), daughter of the great English poet George Byron.

Lady Lovelace, who showed extraordinary mathematical abilities, was seriously interested in the work of Charles Babage and deeply understood his ideas. In her 1843 publication, which is rightfully considered the first work on programming theory, she expressed a number of thoughts and principles that retain their significance today, and some of his proposed terms have become entrenched in scientific language. She also owns, historically, the first quite complex concrete program - for calculating the so-called Bernoulli numbers using a specially developed algorithm (she, of course, could not practically try this program due to the lack of machines).

Speaking about programming, one cannot fail to note two more facts dating back to the 19th century and which played a particularly important role in the development of this science. The first is the “invention” of a special command by C. Babidge, now called the conditional transition command (they also say branching) and which has exceptional significance in modern programming. This command allows the machine itself to automatically determine the further sequence of operations depending on the result obtained at a certain step of the work. This opens up the possibility of instructing the machine to perform not only argumentative, but also logical operations.

However, in order to advance along this path, it is necessary to develop an apparatus for formulaic-mathematical analysis of logical statements. The theory proposed in 1847 by the English mathematician George Boole (1815-1864) and called Boolean algebra turned out to be exactly such an apparatus.

A time of hope, a time of searching

Ch. Babidge died, his analytical engine remained an unfinished project... But his main idea - to create an automatic computer with program control for performing scientific and technical calculations - could not. For it was a breakthrough foresight of the pressing needs of the continued development of civilization.

Already at the end of the century, the number of models and modifications of the arithmetic meter and other counting devices (keyboards, recorders, etc.) has rapidly increased, and their widespread production and use begins. The arithmetic meter significantly facilitated and reduced the most utilitarian and routine work - performing arithmetic operations. However, the actual solution of any serious specific mathematical problem, obtaining a realistic answer in the form of numerical data, primarily common and necessary in applications, remained a whole gap, not a simple and labor-intensive task that required long-term successful large teams of computers.

For example, one of the main places that connects mathematics with life is differential equations. Naturally, finding a solution (or, as mathematicians say, interlining) of such equations is a typical object of computational work. Here is what was said about this in the book “Numerical Interpretation of Differential Equations,” published only half a century ago, in 1932: “The reader should be sure to understand the main idea: solving these equations in the vast majority of cases is in practice an extremely difficult operation, a relatively large part of the time spent on calculations...”

These first of all explain that the increased interest in the design of computers, clearly emerging at the end of the 19th century, then began to grow sharply. Through the efforts of individual engineers, physicists, mathematicians, inventors, and most often entire teams, many remarkable results have been achieved. It is impossible not to support that, to a large extent, these results were impossible without the discoveries and research made by that time in physics, mechanics, technology, without an accessible level of industrial production and technical culture.

What kind of computers were designed and created in the first half of our century? Further, simply listing their model would take up too much space. Therefore, we will limit ourselves to only a brief listing of their main, fundamental features and a few examples.

A large group consisted of specialized electromechanical computers, represented by a mechanical calculator, which was driven by an electric drive and controlled by electrical impulses. Tabulators (first: counting and analytical, or counting and punching machines), which are used mainly to mechanize the solution of statistics and accounting problems, have become especially widespread. The first tabulator was developed back in 1884 by the American engineer Herman Hollerith (1860-1929).

Electromechanical computers were built that focused on other important practical tasks. It must be admitted that some of them are more naturally perfect design thoughts and ideal technical use. Among them, we should mention the first machine for integrating differential equations, invented by the famous Soviet mathematician and co-

slave academician A. N. Krylov (1863-1945). We further note that in 1915, the Ascania company (Werlin) manufactured a computer to determine the time of tides on the northern coast of Germany. The machine consists of an intricate lace of several hundred bronze gears and was not that bulky. In order to draw up a tide schedule for the year, he had to “move the gears” for eight hours - quite fast for those times. But it’s even more amazing that the car served faithfully and correctly for 60 years - until 1975!

The other group consisted of universal electromechanical computers with program control. These machines used a technical novelty - an electromagnetic relay. The historical first operating machine of this type was the Ts-3 machine, built in 1941 by the German engineer Konrad Zuse, but the end of Hitler’s Germany resulted in the fact that this achievement of German science did not receive timely fame and did not have an impact on the development of computer technology.

The most advanced of the machines of this group, called “Mark-1”, was created in 1937-1944 under the leadership of the American physicist Howard Aiken. She played a major role in solving a number of key issues in computer design. Here are some of its characteristics. “Mark-1” weighed 4.5 tons, occupied an area of ​​17x2.5 m and was a complex combination of a huge number of relay and mechanical elements connected by 800 km of electrical wiring. Entering the initial data - 23-digit numbers in general (decimal) form was carried out using a punched card, and the direction of the machine's operation was provided by paper punched tape. The addition (subtraction) operation took 0.3 seconds, while multiplication and division required 5.7 seconds and 15.3 seconds, respectively.

Finally, let us mention an even larger group of analog computers (or continuous-wave machines). The operation of these machines is based on the idea of ​​constructing - according to the initial data - a circuit model (mainly electrical), a change in one of the physical characteristics (for example, voltage) of which determines the desired wolf. These machines have proven themselves especially good at solving problems where it is primarily important to obtain a qualitative or graphical representation of the change in the unknown. However, they are practically unsuitable for special calculations.

So, the successes achieved in the creation of computers were undeniable. And yet these machines were in no way able to satisfy the enormous computing needs that had developed by the middle of the 20th century. Their design was prohibitively expensive and unacceptably cumbersome, the speed of operation remained too low, the amount of memory was clearly insufficient, and the control of the calculation process turned out to be inflexible in practice. Further intensive development of computer technology and its mass use could not objectively be ensured only by improving the implementation of existing ideas.
