First steps
Niels Bohr in science

Academician of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR
V. A. FABRIKANT

I regard Bohr's work as the greatest triumph of human thought.
Rutherford, 1936

This year, on October 7, humanity celebrated the centenary of the birth of one of the founders of modern physics, Niels Bohr. It is important to emphasize that Rutherford’s words, cited as an epigraph, were spoken by him after Bohr’s atoma theory had been replaced by sophisticated quantum mechanics. Without in any way detracting from the credit of Heisenberg, Schrödinger and Dirac for creating quantum mechanics, Rutherford rightly emphasizes the role of Bohr.

Dirac, who largely completed the construction of quantum mechanics and laid the foundations of quantum electrodynamics, assessed Bohr’s role in a lecture he read in 1975: “I believe that the emergence of Bohr’s idea was the most monumental step in the history of the development of quantum mechanics.”

It must be said that Bohr's role was unique. Bohr's so-called atoma model gained little popularity, although Bohr himself was quite skeptical about this model, realizing its inconsistency. Much more important were the fundamental ideas that Dirac had in mind.

Bohr's merits were also great as a teacher of a large group of prominent scientists, including the outstanding Soviet physicist Landdu. This is due not only to the magnitude of his scientific genius, but also to his amazing qualities. He was a man of exceptional kindness, purity and duty. Both young and established scientists came to Bohr. He conducted truly insightful discussions with them, leading to significant new results. The words spoken about Bor when he was still a child by his father came true: “People will come to Nils and listen to him.”

Student, Master, Doctor

In 1903, Niels Bohr entered the University of Copenhagen, where his father Christinan Bohr was a professor of physiology. The physics course at this university was taught by Professor Kristinen. The breadth of his interests and timely approach to the subject certainly played a very large role in the formation of Niels Bohr as a physicist.

In 1905, the Royal Danish Society of Sciences announced a competition for the best work on physics. The idea was to study the vibrations of a liquid in order to create a method for determining the coefficient of surface tension (we were talking about the development of the work of the famous English physicist Rayleigh). The oar bur successfully dealt with theoretical problems, but the experiment was successfully performed with only one liquid—water. The importance of Bohr's theoretical results was recognized with a gold medal, although his competitor, also awarded a gold medal, developed a simpler experimental method and determined the surface tension coefficient of a series of liquids.

After graduating from university in 1907, Bohr approached Kristinsen for a topic for his master's thesis. He advised me to take up the electronic theory of metals. Bohr listened and soon wrote to his brother Harald that Lorentz's work on electronic theory had fascinated him. But fascination did not stop Bohr from discovering the fundamental shortcomings of classical electronic theory. In 1909, Bohr received his master's degree and immediately began working on his doctoral dissertation, which was a continuation of his theoretical research.

The doctoral dissertation, presented on April 12, 1911, in part, contained proof of the fundamental impossibility of creating a theory of the magnetic properties of matter based on purely classical concepts. Since the dissertation was published only in children's language, its results were not widely disseminated, and eight years later the Dutch van Leuven re-conducted the same proof (the Bohr-van Leuven theorem). It is important to maintain that here Bohr for the first time personally reached the limit of application of classical physics.

The defense of the dissertation took place on May 13 of the same year. The weekly "Politics" published a note briefly describing Professor Kristinsen's speech at this defense: “He spoke in his generally pleasant manner, told several funny stories and expressed regret that Bohr's dissertation was not published in a foreign language. We in Denmark hardly have people with such competence in electronic theory, who could judge a dissertation on this topic.” Further it was written: “Dr. Bohr, a glib and shy young man, did not take an active part in the discussion, which broke all records due to its short duration.”

Kristinsen was right, and so Bohr, soon after his protection, happily went to Cambridge for a year-long internship.

Bohr gets to Rutherford

The “father of the electron” himself, J. J. Thomson, worked in Cambridge. There was no more competent person in the field that interested Bohr. However, for a number of reasons, Bohr was unable to establish business contacts with Thomson.

But in Cambridge, in October 1911, Bohr first met with Rutherford, who came here from Manchest for the so-called daily Cavednitsa lunch. Bohr recalled: “Although I was not able to meet Rutherford on this occasion, I was deeply impressed by his charm and energy - qualities with which he was able to achieve almost incredible substances wherever he worked.”

In November, Bohr went to Manchester to visit one of his father's colleagues. The colleague turned out to be a close friend of Rutherfort and introduced Bohr to him. Bohr later wrote: “During a conversation in which Rutherford spoke with genuine enthusiasm about many new prospects for the development of physics, he kindly agreed to my request that I join the group working in his laboratory after I was supposed to finish my studies at Cambridge in the early spring of 1912.” Rutherford only asked that the move be negotiated with G.G. (Thomson's nickname) so that this procedure would not look like the result of an employee changing from one manager to another. According to Thomson, it was obtained without much difficulty.

If in Cambridge Bohr was keen on Thomson's atoma model, according to which negatively charged electrons are included in a cloud of positive charge "like the raisins in a cupcake", then in Manchest he quickly freed himself with Rutherfor's nuclear atoma model, created only a year ago, where positive and negative charges seemed to be placed in places. In this model, a positively charged small nucleus is surrounded by a cloud of electrons moving in closed orbits around the nucleus (like planets around the Sun). Rutherfor's model has a server flaw - it could not explain the stability of the atoma structure, which manifests itself literally at every step.

Bohr enthusiastically plunged into the atmosphere of the Manchester laboratorium and began trying to resolve this contradiction. Rutherford quickly appreciated the depth of Bohr's approach and gave him his full support. Bohr had the idea to apply quantum concepts developed by Planck and Einstein to the solution of this problem. It is unknown, however, where and when Bohr first became acquainted with this idea (a characteristic detail is that in Christansen’s course of theoretical physics, published in German in 1910, there is not a word about quanta!).

Before this meeting, Bohr showed no interest in the structure of line spectra for the study and absorption of various elements. The introduction to the article contains a very important meaning regarding the role of Planck's quantum constant in atomic theory. This meaning is modern in nature and is associated with the so-called theoretical dimension. However, one of the pioneers in the application of methods of dimensional theory in physics was Rayleigh (with the development of whose works, while still a student, Bohr began his scientific activity).

If a certain system of basic quantities is chosen, for example in mechanics - length, time and mass, then the dimensions of the derivative quantities are written through the dimensions of the basic quantities. So, the dimension of length is denoted [l]=L, then the dimension of area [S]=L² and volume [V]=L³. The dimension of time [t]=T, corresponding to the dimension of speed [v]=LT⁻¹, and acceleration [a]=LT⁻². Since the dimension of mass [m]=M, then the dimension of force [F]=MLT⁻². And so on.

Bohr drew attention to the fact that in the Rutherford model of the atom there are only two parameters - riddle e and the electron mass m, from which it is impossible to form quantities that determine the structure of the atom and have the dimension of length. If we add the Planck constant h, then such a possibility appears.
