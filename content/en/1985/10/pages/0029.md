Trenitsa: harmful, useful, interesting...
A. V. SEMENOV

Have you ever seen your friend's sled catalogue? Of course - yes. And, of course, you have noticed that sliding a sled on ice is a pleasure, on snow it is more necessary, and if you come across bare, snow-free asphalt, then dragging the sled is oh so difficult. And why? Probably everyone will answer right away: there is more training when moving on asphalt. And he will be right. But let's look at training in a little more detail.

What do you think, if the surfaces in contact are motionless, can there be tension between them? Think carefully, refer to your everyday experience...

Of course it can. If it were not for the strength of the friction, we would not be able to stand even on a slightly flat hill; no body would be able to stay on an inclined plane - as a result of the action of gravity, it would slide down. It is the training that interferes with us when we try to move some object. This type of training is called resting training, in contrast to sliding friction, which occurs when surfaces move relative to each other.

So, when a body, say a box, is standing on the floor, and we are trying to meet it, we are overcome by the power of peace. When we succeeded and the box moved along the floor, the sliding friction force acts.

There is a significant difference between resting friction and sliding friction. The strength of resting friction depends on the force with which we apply the body, trying to move it from its place. We apply a small force - and the static friction force is small, just enough to balance the applied force. We increase the intensification - and the strength of the resting friction grows. But with some effort I managed to get the box out of the way; it slid across the floor. In this case, the sliding friction force remains practically constant and equal to the maximum static friction force. How hard did it take to see the body? In other words, what is the greatest strength of tranquility? What does it depend on?

Scientists have been thinking about these questions (and about what training is in general) many centuries ago. Reflections on the power of friction are found among the notes of Leonardo da Vinci. But structural answers, in the form of laws, were formed in 1699 by the French physicist Amonton. A hundred years after Amonton, the laws of tension were rediscovered by Coulomb, and now they are most often called the Amonton-Coulomb laws.

The main of these laws can be formulated as follows: the maximum static friction force ($f_{\max}$) between the surfaces of two bodies is directly proportional to the magnitude of the force ($N$) with which the bodies are pressed against each other:

$$f_{\max} = \mu N.$$

For a box standing on the floor, the force $N$ is equal to the force of gravity, and on an inclined plane the pressing force will become less, and the friction force will be correspondingly less. The proportionality coefficient $\mu$ depends on the materials from which the surfaces are made and on how they are processed. Surprisingly, the friction force does not depend on the surface area of ​​the friction process.

Speaking about friction, we have always emphasized the “harmfulness” of its action: because of friction, the air is damaged, the automatic tires are worn out, and machine parts wear out. They are fighting this harmful training and trying to reduce it. But the training is literally more effective, more effective and useful.

First of all, it is thanks to the force of friction that you and I can walk on the ground. Remember: how difficult it can be to walk down the street in hell.
