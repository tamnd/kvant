Niels Bohr in science

Academician of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR
V. A. FABRIKANT

I regard Bohr's work as the greatest triumph of human thought.
Rutherford, 1936

This year, on October 7, humanity celebrated the centenary of the birth of one of the founders of modern physics, Niels Bohr. It is important to emphasize that Rutherford’s words, cited as an epigraph, were spoken by him after Bohr’s atoma theory had been replaced by sophisticated quantum mechanics. Without in any way detracting from the credit of Heisenberg, Schrödinger and Dirac for creating quantum mechanics, Rutherford rightly emphasizes the role of Bohr.

Dirac, who largely completed the construction of quantum mechanics and laid the foundations of quantum electrodynamics, assessed Bohr’s role in a lecture he read in 1975: “I believe that the emergence of Bohr’s idea was the most monumental step in the history of the development of quantum mechanics.”

It must be said that Bohr's role was unique. Bohr's so-called atoma model gained little popularity, although Bohr himself was quite skeptical about this model, realizing its inconsistency. Much more important were the fundamental ideas that Dirac had in mind.

Bohr's merits were also great as a teacher of a large group of prominent scientists, including the outstanding Soviet physicist Landdu. This is due not only to the magnitude of his scientific genius, but also to his amazing qualities. He was a man of exceptional kindness, purity and duty. Both young and established scientists came to Bohr. He conducted truly insightful discussions with them, leading to significant new results. The words spoken about Bor when he was still a child by his father came true: “People will come to Nils and listen to him.”
