The judge turns out to be a quantity well known to us - temperature! And the judge is not impartial. Temperature acts as a co-awareness of the forces of disorder, forces that, in our “billiard” language, strive to transform a strict pattern into chaos.

THERE IS NOTHING ESPECIALLY SURPRISING ABOUT THIS. We know from the example of gases that as temperature increases, the movement of molecules becomes more and more intense. The same thing happens in other substances. At a certain temperature, this warm movement is capable of breaking the “energy path” and thereby disordering the system.

There are many examples of such transformations. One of them is melting. Let us remember what happens when heated to a solid body that is a regular crystal, that is, the same pattern that we have not talked about. As the temperature rises, the desire to break out of the crystal lattice becomes stronger and stronger, and finally they succeed - above the melting point, a liquid is formed from a regular crystal - the pattern turns into chaos.

Thus, at sufficiently high temperatures, the energy factor turns out to be decisive. Compliance at lower temperatures is dominated by the energy factor. What “sufficiently high” and “lower” temperatures mean is decided in each specific case in its own way. The main thing that needs to be understood and remembered is the temperature regulator regarding the importance of energy and energy factors.

Let us return, and now finally, to the world of alloys. At high temperatures, the distribution of atoms will be mainly determined by the energy factor - and the alloy will be disordered. However, as the temperature rises, the energy factor will play more and more of a role until, finally, at some temperatures it becomes more important than the energy factor. This temperature is called critical. At a critical temperature, the alloy becomes ordered and will remain so upon further cooling.

All information about order and disorder in alloys is usually contained in special diagrams, which are called state diagrams. On figure 6 such a diagram is given in a somewhat simplified form for the copper-gold alloys with which we began this story. The ordinate axis shows the temperature, and the abscissa axis shows the relative concentration of the components. Any point on the diagram corresponds to the definition of concentration and temperature values. The points lying on the curved lines indicate the values ​​of critical temperatures for various concentrations of components. Above the lines is the region of the disordered state of alloys, below the lines is the region of ordered alloys.
