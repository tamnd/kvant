Ping pong...
in the washbasin

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. V. BYALKO

The experiments that will be discussed are accessible to everyone and do not require any special equipment. All you need is a table tennis (ping pong) ball, a standard washbasin (or bathtub), a millimeter ruler, a three-liter jar and a clock with a second hand.

Experiment 1. Close the drain hole of the sink (bath) and fill it with water to a level of 3 - 5 cm. Place a ping-pong ball under the stream where it cuts through the surface of the water and release it. It seems to me that the jet should throw the ball aside. But that's not true! On the contrary, the jet captures the ball, and it remains in the place where it falls.

Take a closer look. It turns out that the ball is not motionless - under the influence of the jet it makes small oscillations. Cup your palms and move them under the ball so that it continues to float under the stream without touching your hands. Now lift the ball along the stream. You will notice that the vibration frequency decreases as the ball rises.

Experiment 2. Open the drain hole. In a standard sink (bath), it appears as a cylindrical hole with a lattice structure about 20 mm deep and about 40 mm in diameter. The ping pong ball is slightly smaller - its diameter is 37 mm, so the ball fits freely in the drain hole, and there is still a gap of 1 - 2 mm (Fig. 1).

Bring the ball under water to the drain hole and release. The flow of water will pull it into the hole, despite the fact that the ball does not float in the water. But what is it? You hear a low humming sound, very indo, caused by the vibrations of the ball. The first thought that comes to mind is that the ball is bouncing on the grate. However, not this. Touch the ball from above with your finger, and you will be convinced that it oscillates not vertically, but horizontally - as far as the gap allows it.

Moreover. Look at the factory mark on the ball, and you see that the ball also rotates, and quite slowly, so that it can be seen with the eye. Sometimes the rotation occurs only in one direction, and sometimes the direction of rotation of the ball randomly changes from time to time.

How can these experiments be carried out? Let’s say right away that both of them serve as a vivid illustration of Bernoulli’s law - one

many of the first laws of hydrodynamics. Qualitatively, it can be formulated as follows (“Physics 8”, § 65): in a moving liquid, the pressure is greater in those seconds where the speed of movement is lower, and vice versa, where the speed is higher, the pressure is lower. In other words, as the speed of a fluid increases, the pressure in it decreases. This decrease in pressure leads to the fact that in both experiments the flow of water seems to draw in a plastic ball inside itself.

Bernoulli's law will help explain both the oscillation of the ball in the stream and in the shell. But first, let's estimate the speed of water flowing from a water tap.

It is not difficult to select a flow of water into the sink (or bathtub) such that the water level in it remains almost unchanged. Using a three-liter jar and a stopwatch, you can measure the corresponding liquid consumption (that is, the mass of liquid consumed per unit time). It turns out to be approximately (80 - 100) g/s. Now, using a ruler, measure the diameter of the stream near the surface of the water in the sink. It is close to 6 mm. This corresponds to a jet speed of about 3 m/s (check this). Of course, the current speed can be made either greater or less than the obtained value, but in further estimates of pressure it can be considered exactly as such.

Explanation of experiment 1. Let's temporarily simplify the task for ourselves: the distance is not the interaction of the ball with the cylindrical stream, but the effect of the flat stream on the cylinder. This will not change the existing physical phenomenon, but it will be easier for us to reason.

First, we will discuss the case when the cylinder only slightly touches the stream and estimate the force acting on it (Fig. 2). According to Bernoulli's law, the pressure inside the bending jet drops by amounts

$$p_0 - p = \frac{\varrho}{2} (v^2 - v_0^2)$$

(the term $\varrho gR$ can be neglected, since in our case the values ​​$gR \approx 0,2 \text{ m}^2/\text{c}^2$ are noticeably smaller than $v^2/2 \approx 4,5 \text{ m}^2/\text{c}^2$). The trajectory of part of the fluid describing the cylinder is a circular arc, so they move with centripetal acceleration. This acceleration is due to the difference between atmospheric pressure $p_0$ (which is also the water pressure in a straight stream) and pressure $p$ in a curved stream:

$$m \frac{v^2}{R} = (p_0 - p)S,$$

or $$\frac{v^2}{R} = \frac{p_0 - p}{\varrho d},$$

where $S$ is the contact area of ​​the jet with the cylinder. For a stream whose thickness $d$ is much smaller than the radius of the cylinder $R$, the flow velocity is only slightly greater than the initial velocity $v_0$. Therefore, the force deflecting the stream is equal to

$$F = (p_0 - p)S = \frac{\varrho v_0^2 d}{R} S.$$

A force of the same magnitude, but in the opposite direction, acts on the cylinder.

This result, obtained for flow around a cylinder, remains valid for flow around a ball, possibly with other numerical coefficients. The contact area of ​​the jet with the ball is almost always of the order of the square of its radius $R$. Therefore, the force pulling the ball

Let us now consider the central flow around the ball when the jet falls exactly on its top (Fig. 3). In this case, the water flows around the ball symmetrically, and therefore $F=0$. For a central flow it is not difficult to find the layer thickness $d$. From the condition for constructing the flow it follows that

$$d \sim \frac{r_0^2}{R}$$

(show it yourself).

Finally, for the intermediate case, when the displacement $x$ of the ball from the symmetry axis of the jet is insignificant compared to its radius $R$ (Fig. 4), it turns out that the force $F$ linearly depends on $x$ and is directed so as to return the ball to the equilibrium position:

$$F \sim -\varrho v_0^2 d \cdot x \sim \frac{\varrho v_0^2 r_0^2}{R} x.$$

(Unfortunately, the exact value of the force $F$ for this case is difficult to obtain.)

Under the influence of this force the ball will oscillate. The expression for the frequency of oscillations can be written by analogy with the oscillations of a weight outward:

$$F_{\pi p} = -kx \Rightarrow \omega_{\pi p} = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},$$

$$F_{\pi} \sim -\frac{\varrho v_0^2}{R} x \Rightarrow \omega_{\pi} \sim v_0 r_0 \sqrt{\frac{\varrho}{RM}}.$$

With a ball mass $M \approx 3$ g we obtain $\omega_{\pi} \sim 40 \text{ c}^{-1}$, and $v_{\pi} \sim 6 \text{ Гц}$.
