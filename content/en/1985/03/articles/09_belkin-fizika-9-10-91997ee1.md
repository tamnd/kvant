Discovery of the electron

The word “electron” - the name of one of the loaded elementary particles - and its derivatives are perhaps most often found in scientific and technical literature these days. Relatively recently, the word “electronics” appeared, denoting, on the one hand, the study of the interactions of electrons with electromagnetic fields, and on the other, a new field of technology. Adjectives such as “electronic”, “electronic”, etc. have widely entered our language and our lives. It is enough to recall, for example, the existence of various electronic devices and electronic computers.

When, by whom and how was the electron discovered? When, who and how determined its basic properties and clarified its role in nature?

Rays or particles? The discovery of the electron represents the completion of several decades of research into gas discharge, that is, the process of passing an electric current through a gas (Physics 9, §70-72). In particular, around the middle of the last century, it was found that if a sufficiently high voltage is applied to electrodes soldered into a glass tube with gas, then an electric current passes through the gas, and the gas itself glows. The nature of the glow depends on the gas pressure and applied voltage, and the color of the light is determined by the nature of the gas. However, at a sufficiently low pressure (about one pakel, that is, one-half of an atmosphere), the glow of the gas almost disappears (although the current continues to flow), but the glass of the tube begins to glow green.

What happens in the discharge tube after the gas glow disappears? On this issue, a dispute arose between the physicists who most actively studied this phenomenon.

German physicists (G. Hertz, E. Goldstein) believed that special rays emanate from the cathode of the tube, which cause the glass to glow. They are therefore called cathode rays. Hertz, who discovered electromagnetic waves, was naturally inclined to believe that cathode rays are special electromagnetic waves, similar to light, but invisible light.

English physicists (W. Crookes, A. Schuster, then J. J. Thomson) believed that it was not rays coming out of the cathode, but some negatively charged particles, and that it was under their influence that the glass would glow. Crookes, for example, argued that these are gas molecules that, moving away from the cathode, acquire a negative charge and are then accelerated by the force of cauterization towards the anode. This was suggested by the fact that the cathode rays were deflected by a magnetic field. Of course, German physicists also knew about this important fact, but at that time it was not yet firmly established that electromagnetic waves do not interact with a magnetic field.

It was firmly established by both disputing parties that the properties of cathode rays do not depend on what material the cathode is made of. This dispute was fruitful, since each group of scientists tried to come up with and carry out experiments that would prove them right.

The decisive experiments were performed in 1897 by the English physicist Joseph John Thomson. These states were tested by observing the movement of charged particles in electric and magnetic fields.

In the thirties, a group of Dutch physicists led by V. Holst was working on the problem of improving the quality of photographic images obtained in low light conditions. The idea behind the proposed method was quite simple.

Object photographic film perceives visible light, the reflected object being photographed. However, the object itself always (at temperatures other than absolute zero) serves as a source of so-called thermal radiation, which is also electromagnetic in nature. It turns out that the wavelength, which corresponds to the maximum in the distribution of the energy of this radiation over wavelengths, depends on temperature (see the graph on figure 1, where the wavelength is plotted along the abscissa axis, and the radiation energy arriving at a unit wavelength interval is plotted along the ordinat axis). At very high temperatures, bodies emit mainly visible light. At room temperature, when photography is usually done at random, the maximum emission occurs in the invisible infrared region.

If a way could be found to convert this invisible infrared image into shorter wavelengths that film can sense, it would be possible to photograph in low light or even in the dark.

Such a converter was created and was called electron-optical conversion. Its main element is a photocathode - a thin processing film made of materials containing atoms of integral metals - cesium, rubidium or potassium. The red boundary of the photoelectric effect $\lambda_{\max}$ for such materials is in the infrared region, therefore, when infrared rays with wavelengths $\lambda < \lambda_{\max}$ fall on the film, the light pulls out electrons from it. To take advantage of the electrons emitted during photoefficiency, a layer of metal is deposited onto the film, so thin that the film remains transformative.

The principal conversion scheme is shown in figure 2. The image of object 1, illuminated by infrared light, is formed on the photocathode 3. As already mentioned, a thin layer of metal is not an obstacle to the rays, and in those places of the photocathode where the image of the object hits, photomission electrons appear on the opposite side of the film. In some cases, the anode 7 is located in the katom, the surface of which is covered with a layer of crushed material (this part of the device is filled with a common television tube). A high potential difference (on the order of tens of thousands of volts) is created between the anode and cathode, which accelerates photoelectrons. This will create a flow of electrons flying from the cathode to the anode, and, subject to certain conditions, the distribution of these electrons over the surface of the anode follows the shape of the object being photographed. Once on the anode, electrons cause it to glow.
