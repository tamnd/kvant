Paradox
Vavilova

Academician of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR V. A. FABRIKANT

In geometric optics, the concept of a beam of parallel light rays having a finite cross section is often and fruitfully used. Moreover, even in the theory of such a wave phenomenon as integration, in many cases it is permissible to use this concept.

In many cases, but not in all. In the book “Microstructure of Light” by the former Soviet physicist S.I. Vavilov, a very significant optical paradox in this sense is analyzed.

Let us briefly recall what the energy of the integration picture looks like. Integration, as we know, is the addition of oscillations. In electronic waves, the values ​​of the electric field strength and magnetic field induction fluctuate. These quantities at each point in space at each moment of time determine the energy of the electromagnetic field. An electromagnetic wave transfers energy, and the concept of energy flux density can be introduced. This is what we call a quantity equal to the amount of field energy flowing per unit time through a unit surface.

Each wave is also characterized by a phase. If, when adding two light beams, these beams, their phase difference remains the same all the time, we say that we are dealing with coherent beams.

In the integration picture that arose as a result of the addition of two coherent beams, a redistribution of light energy occurs. In the light bands the energy is greater than the sum of the energies of the stacked beams; in dark stripes, on the contrary, it is less. The excess of energy in the light stripes is just compensated by its lack in the dark ones. The total energy distributed over the entire integration pattern is exactly equal to the sum of the energies of the two integrating beams.

figure 1 shows the dependence of the energy flux density in the integration picture on the mixing across the screen on which it is observed. The picture was obtained by adding two coherent light beams of equal energy. The horizontal dotted line depicts the sum of the energy flux densities in the stacked beams. The parts of the curve above this line correspond to light integration stripes, and the parts of the curve lying under the dotted line correspond to dark ones. The total energy distributed in the integration picture is represented by the area under the curve. This area is exactly equal to the area under the dotted line. The requirement of nature's strict "accountant" - the law of conservation of energy - is strictly fulfilled.
