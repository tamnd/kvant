1. Pure metals and alloys

Let's take pure copper. Under normal conditions, it is a substance with a crystalline structure. The “bricks” from which the crystal is built can be depicted as in figure 1. This is the so-called elementary cell of copper. It is called a face-centered cubic (fcc) cell because the atoms are located both at the vertices of the cube and at the centers of its faces. The dimensions of the cube are small - the length of its edge is $a=3,6 \cdot 10^{-10}$ m. If we periodically “continue” such elements of the cube in all three dimensions, then we will obtain a crystalline fcc lattice of copper.

We will now add atoms of another element, for example gold, to the copper crystal. Of course, you can't add atoms one at a time. But you can take certain quantities of gold and copper, melt them together and let the liquid cool and freeze.

Such alloys are called substitution alloys because the atoms of one element simply replace the atoms of another. This is perhaps the most common group of alloys.

You can change the relative coefficients of alloyed gold and copper. Then the details of the microsopper picture will change, but in general, or, as physicists say, qualitatively, it will remain the same - the same fcc lattice, along the nodes (positions) of which atoms of gold and copper are distributed. This distribution can be either chaotic or somewhat orderly - for example, at the vertices of a cube there will be atoms of one type, and at the centers of the faces - of another. So how exactly will atoms of different varieties be “settled” in a crystal lattice? What determines the way they are distributed among positions?

Of course, to answer these questions, you can always set up an experiment that would allow you to observe with your own eyes “scenes from the life of atoms.” We have such an opportunity. By illuminating a sample of the alloy we are interested in with X-rays, we will obtain a diffraction pattern from which we can reconstruct the nature of the relative arrangement of atoms of different types in the lattice. For example, if at room temperature we look “through the eyes of X-rays” at an alloy consisting of 75% copper atoms and the remaining 25% gold atoms, then we will see an almost perfect picture, which has already been mentioned in passing - almost all the vertices of the cubes are occupied by gold atoms, and almost all the centers of the faces are occupied by copper atoms (figure 3). We say “almost all” because it’s not uncommon for some stray copper atom to flash at the top of the cube, or for an oblivious gold atom to appear in a copper position, but this is a very rare case. Basically, the picture is simply orderly in its correctness: at the top of the cube there is gold, in the center of the face there is copper. Alloys are also said to be ordered. What forces cause atoms in alloys to be ordered? Does this always happen? What other ordered structures could there be? Again a whole series of questions; questions that nature itself poses to the researcher. Alloys of all elements at different concentrations of components and at different temperatures did not move under X-rays, and therefore it is worth trying to develop some general ways to predict the “dispersal” of atoms in alloys. And to do this, you must first of all understand the basic laws of the “community” of atoms.

Atoms in a crystal are always “under surveillance”. They have two powerful bosses who are at odds with each other. You'll get there within a millisecond of another. We know the name of one leader well: it is energy. The second one appears to us incognito for now, and for the time being we will call her the Queen of Disorder.

It means that all atoms interact with each other in pairs. Paired forces are well known to us. These include the Coulomb forces, which act between charges, and the forces of mutual gravity between bodies. The characteristic feature of these forces is their decrease with distance. Bodies interact the weaker the greater the distance between them. Thus, the energy of the Coulomb interaction of charges kills in inverse proportion to the distance between them.

In a crystal consisting of metal atoms, the energy of interaction between atoms kills much faster. Therefore, the second proposal is logical: interactions between nearest neighbors in the lattice will be of primary importance.

Let us now return again to figure 1. It indicates some pairs of nearest neighbors in the fcc lattice. From the Pythagorean theorem it is easy to calculate the distance between nearest neighbors: it is equal to $a\sqrt{2/2}$ (recall that $a$ is the length of the edge of an elementary cube). Each atom in an fcc lattice has 12 nearest neighbors (try to show this yourself). The number of nearest neighbors, usually called the coordinate number, is denoted by $Z$.

So, each atom in the lattice interacts with its nearest neighbors. But, naturally, atoms of different types interact with each other in different ways. Since the lattice contains atoms of two types $A$ and $B$, their interaction is described by the theme of energy values, which we denote as follows: $v_{AA}, v_{BB}$ is the energy of interaction of neighboring atoms of the same type ($A$ or $B$), $v_{AB}$ is the energy of interaction of two neighboring atoms of the $A$ type and $B$. Now, through these parameters it is easy to describe the energy of any configuration of atoms in the lattice. It will be the sum of the interaction energy of all pairs of nearest neighbors - type $AA, AB, BB$:

$$E = P_{AA} v_{AA} + P_{BB} v_{BB} + P_{AB} v_{AB},$$

where $P_{AA}, P_{BB}, P_{AB}$ are the numbers of corresponding pairs of neighbors.

Let there be only $N$ atoms in the crystal we are considering, of which $n_A$ atoms of type $A$ and $n_B$ atoms of type $B$ (that is, $N=n_A+n_B$). In particular, the concentrations of components in the alloy are expressed through these quantities:

$$c_A = \frac{n_A}{N}, c_B = \frac{n_B}{N} = 1 - c_A.$$

We now have everything we need to evaluate the various energy contributions. Note that there are correspondences between the numbers $P_{AA}, P_{BB}$ and $P_{AB}$. Each atom of sort $A$ can have only $Z$ close neighbors. Accordingly, the total number of close neighbors for all atoms $A$ is $n_A Z$. Of these, the $P_{AB}$ position accounts for atoms of the $B$ variety. If the nearest neighbor of an atom of type $A$ is another atom of the same kind, then this neighborhood uses two of the $n_A Z$ positions at once, since for the second atom the first one will in turn be the closest neighbor. So for atoms of the sort $A$ should match:

$$n_A Z = 2 P_{AA} + P_{AB},$$

and for atoms of the sort $B$, similarly -

$$n_B Z = 2 P_{BB} + P_{AB}.$$

Now from these equalities we express the quantities $P_{AA}$ and $P_{BB}$ through $P_{AB}$ and substitute them into the expression for energy:

$$E = \frac{n_A Z - P_{AB}}{2} v_{AA} + \frac{n_B Z - P_{AB}}{2} v_{BB} +$$

$$+ P_{AB} v_{AB} = P_{AB} \left( v_{AB} - \frac{v_{AA} + v_{BB}}{2} \right) +$$

$$+ \left( \frac{n_A Z}{2} v_{AA} + \frac{n_B Z}{2} v_{BB} \right).$$

So, we presented the interaction energy of atoms in an alloy crystal as the sum of two adjacent ones. It can be seen that the second shift depends on the location of the atoms, since the redistribution of atoms in the lattice greatly affects the number $P_{AB}$. For example, let’s imagine a crystal in one part of which all the atoms $A$ are concentrated, and in the other - all the atoms $B$, as is schematically shown in figure 4. In such a crystal, the number $P_{AB}$ will be minimal, since the proximity of different atoms occurs only in a narrow zone of overlap between both parts of the crystal. Naturally, when “mixing” atoms, this number will increase greatly.

And now we understand what role the second ruler plays in the life of atoms, which we called the Queen of Disorder - she seeks to introduce chaos into the arrangement of atoms in the crystal lattice. And the real name of this ruler is entropy.

4. Order and disorder in alloys

Let's summarize some results. We know that the energy factor tends to order the arrangement of atoms in the nodes of the crystal lattice. This leads either to the formation of an ordered structure or to the decomposition of the alloy into pure elements. On the other hand, the entropy factor tends to disorder the system, to create some kind of chaotic arrangement of atoms. The struggle of these two tendencies determines those laws of the “community” of atoms, which we are talking about

The judge turns out to be a quantity well known to us - temperature! And the judge is not impartial. Temperature acts as a co-awareness of the forces of disorder, forces that, in our “billiard” language, strive to transform a strict pattern into chaos.

THERE IS NOTHING ESPECIALLY SURPRISING ABOUT THIS. We know from the example of gases that as temperature increases, the movement of molecules becomes more and more intense. The same thing happens in other substances. At a certain temperature, this warm movement is capable of breaking the “energy path” and thereby disordering the system.

There are many examples of such transformations. One of them is melting. Let us remember what happens when heated to a solid body that is a regular crystal, that is, the same pattern that we have not talked about. As the temperature rises, the desire to break out of the crystal lattice becomes stronger and stronger, and finally they succeed - above the melting point, a liquid is formed from a regular crystal - the pattern turns into chaos.

Thus, at sufficiently high temperatures, the energy factor turns out to be decisive. Compliance at lower temperatures is dominated by the energy factor. What “sufficiently high” and “lower” temperatures mean is decided in each specific case in its own way. The main thing that needs to be understood and remembered is the temperature regulator regarding the importance of energy and energy factors.

Let us return, and now finally, to the world of alloys. At high temperatures, the distribution of atoms will be mainly determined by the energy factor - and the alloy will be disordered. However, as the temperature rises, the energy factor will play more and more of a role until, finally, at some temperatures it becomes more important than the energy factor. This temperature is called critical. At a critical temperature, the alloy becomes ordered and will remain so upon further cooling.

5. Conclusion

Now let's give free rein to our imagination. Do we need an alloy with a certain strength, ductility, and electrical conductivity? Nothing could be simpler. We put all the requirements into the computer, and the machine produces an accurate alloy result.

He really wants everything to be exactly like this. But this is still a long way off. Today we are still contemplating the state of solid matter, and the problem of an ordering alloy is only a modest stage in a long journey.

This task is already 65 years old. Soviet physicists academicians L. D. Landdu and E. M. Lifshitz, American scientists H. Bethe and L. Onsager, and English physicist R. Peierls took part in its development. But there are still many blank spots: how to calculate the mixing energy values? how to take into account long-range interaction of atoms, that is, go beyond the approximation of close neighbors? how to generalize the results to alloys of three or more components? These and many other questions will spoil their solution.

Let us show that for the condition $\Delta L = \lambda/4$ to be satisfied, the angle $\alpha$ must be very small.

As can be seen from (3),

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\lambda}{a}.$$ (4)

Let's take $a=1$ cm, $\lambda=5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ cm; then $\alpha=2,5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ radian.

So, problems with the law of conservation of energy arise when the angle between the beams is of the order of the relative wavelength to the beam width.

The solution to the paradox is that at such small angles it is no longer possible to use the understandable ideal of a parallel beam of finite cross-section.

Any attempt to implement such bundles will fail. Due to the phenomenon of diffraction, limiting the size of the beam necessarily leads to its transformation into a diverging beam. The beam divergence angle $\varphi$ is determined precisely by formula (4). Naturally, the angle $\alpha$ at which the beams intersect can only be determined with an accuracy of the order of the angle $\varphi$. While $\alpha \gg \varphi$, the divergence of intersecting beams can be neglected. But when $\alpha$ becomes comparable in order of magnitude to $\varphi$, the concept of a bounded parallel sheaf loses its meaning.

If diffraction had not yet been discovered, we, on the basis of the law of conservation of energy and formula (4), would have to not only agree on its existence, but also point out the basic pattern governing the magnitude of the diffraction angle (divergence angle). This is a good example of how the conservation law in physics can always serve as a reliable guiding star.

Taking the properties of the beam, we will never, of course, get a contradiction with the law of conservation of energy. In interference experiments of this type there will always be evidence of a spatial redistribution of the energy flow.
