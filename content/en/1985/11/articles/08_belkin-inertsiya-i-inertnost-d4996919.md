Inertia and inertia

These two words, similar in sound, are used both in physics and in public life. Many people believe that “inertia” and “inertia” as physical concepts are equivalent to each other, that they are synonyms. Is this really true?

Synonic words can be replaced with one another (for example, “hurry” and “hurry”), if we do not talk about some stylistic shades. But it is precisely this property that the words and concepts “inertia” and “inertia” do not possess interchangeably. Newton's first law is called the law of inertia, but no one will call it the law of inertia. There is a term “inertial reporting system”, but it does not say “inertial reporting system”. You can say “movement by inertia,” but you cannot say “movement by inertia.” The number of such examples can be increased.

But if the words are unequal, this means that different concepts circle behind them. An attempt to interpret the words “inertia” and “inertia” as an expression of two different physical concepts was made in the textbook “Physics 8” (Chapter 4).

What is inertia? In 1632, Galileo’s book “Dialogue on the two most important systems of the world - Ptolemaic and Copernican” was published. In it, along with other important ideas related to mechanics, a position was formulated that later received the name of the law of inertia and became the main principle. Galileo came to it with the help of a very simple reasoning based on experiments and observations.

As the ball rolls down the inclined plane, the ball continuously increases its speed. The same ball, moving upward along an inclined plane, decreases its speed. “And... what would happen to the same moving body on a surface that does not rise or fall?” - says Galileo. The answer to this question is the law of inertia in its original Galilean formulation: “When a body moves along a horizontal plane, without encountering any resistance to motion, then... its movement is uniform and would continue constantly if the plane were in space without end.” Using a number of other examples, Galileo shows that motion by inertia should not only be uniform, but also rectilinear.

In 1687, Newton generalized this statement and gave it the following formulation: “Every body continues to maintain its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line, since it is not forced by applied forces to change this state.” Newton defines force as “an action performed on a body to change its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line.”

The Latin word “inertia” is translated into Russian as “inaction” and even... “laziness”! A body to which no forces are applied does not change its speed by itself - it seems to be too lazy to do this. Therefore, the Galileo-Newton law (Newton's first law) was called the law of inertia.

Mechanical movement is a natural phenomenon. This means that part of the movement - the movement during which the body maintains its speed - is also a phenomenon, the phenomenon of inertia. This is precisely the interpretation of the word “inertia” that is given in the textbook “Physics 8”. This corresponds to the common expression “movement by inertia.”
