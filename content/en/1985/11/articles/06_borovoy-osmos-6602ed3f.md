Ostmos

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. BOROVOY

The cut branch of the plant began to wither. But then you put it in water, and after a relatively short time the sheets straighten out, become elastic again, and are filled with moisture.

Why is the branch waiting? What forces cause moisture to penetrate into the plant and move inside it? What holds water in cells and prevents it from leaving? How does a plant cell become permeable to water in only one direction: from the outside to the inside?

Scientists have been trying to answer these questions for a very long time, but a certain clarity was achieved only at the end of the 19th century. Some more time passed, and laboratories managed to facilitate (albeit very roughly) these natural phenomena. And already in our country they were successfully forced to work in various fields of science and technology.

Who discovered space and how?

In 1748, the French experimental physicist J. A. Nollet, while studying the boiling of liquids, encountered a hitherto unknown phenomenon. In one of his wholesalers, he thermally closed a glass of alcohol with a film of bull bubble and immersed it in the bottom of a larger vessel with water. After a few hours, the bubble swelled greatly - water seeped into the glass and increased the pressure in it. Nolle did not misunderstand this amazing fact and explained it in the following way: “An animal bladder can be more permeable to water than to alcohol; in this case, the speed of passage of water turns out to be greater than the speed of passage of alcohol.”

Let's repeat Nolle's experiment, but use less scarce materials - replace the bubble of existence with a sheet of cellulophane, and replace the alcohol with an aqueous solution of sugar.

figure 1 shows a device that can be made several times. A wide glass tube, covered with blue cellulophane, is lowered into a jar of clean water. The cellophane is secured so that water does not flow inside the tube (sealing can be done using adhesive tape, a rubber ring, plasticine, etc.). A thin glass tube enters the thick tube through a rubber stopper from above.

At the beginning of the experiment, sugar solution is poured into the tubes until the liquid level in the tubes and in the jar coincide (this moment is shown on figure 1 dashed line). After some time, you will notice that the height of the raster column will increase - just as in Nolle’s experiment, water begins its movement through the film, in our case cellulophane.

The phenomenon in which one-way penetration of the solvent through a semi-permeable partition separating the solvent is observed is called osmosis. This word is of Greek origin and means only pressure.

Having repeated the experience of the French physicist, osmosis was discovered for the first time, let’s try to understand this phenomenon.

Ostmosis and one-way diffusion

Let us remember what happens when a substance is dissolved in a solvent. The molecules of the substance penetrate into the solvent, and the molecules of the solvent penetrate into the area occupied by the solvent. This mutual diffusion (penetration) leads to equalization of the concentrations of the dissolved substance and solvent throughout the entire volume.

Now let’s imagine a situation in which a solution and a pure solvent are separated by a semi-permeable partition - it allows molecules of the solvent to pass through, but does not allow molecules of the dissolved substance to pass through. Obviously, in this case, equalization of concentrations can occur only due to one-way diffusion of the solvent.

This is exactly what will happen in the experience we have delivered. Cellufane is “opaque” for sugar molecules, but “transparent” for water molecules, therefore, at the same height of liquid (the same pressure on the partition) in the jar and tubes, at the initial moment, more water molecules pass through the cellulophane upward than from top to bottom. As a result, the liquid level in the jar begins to fall into the water and penetrates into the solution under the influence of the forces of the so-called osmotic pressure. As soon as the hydrolysis pressure of the solution column balances the osmotic pressure, the process stops. The height of this column serves as a quantitative measure of osmotic pressure.

Semi-permeable partitions and “artificial cage”

After Nolle’s work, many scientists - botanists, chemists, physicists - began to conduct experiments and study the phenomenon of osmosis. For semi-permeable partitions, they used a variety of natural materials: egg skin, bull and pork bladders, membranes, etc.

Experiments with an osmoteter and the creation of the theory of osmosis

Thus, for the first time, quantitative data on osmosis was obtained, which helped the outstanding Dutch chemist J. H. Van't Hoff create theories of this phenomenon.

In 1887, the scientist completed a work in which he showed that the molecules of a dissolved substance behave in a solution in the same way as the molecules of an ideal gas in the state. And the dissolver in this case plays the role of... emptiness!

To understand Van't Hoff's reasoning, let's perform a thought experiment. Let a horizontal cylinder filled with water be divided into two equal parts of cellufane by a partition that can mix without breaking the seal (Fig. 3). If you dissolve sugar in one part of the cylinder, then due to the diffusion of pure water through the celluphan in the area of ​​the dissolver, the pressure there increases and the partition begins to mix. The volume of the solution will increase, the volume of clean water will decrease accordingly. But according to the results of this experimental assumption, the sugar molecules in the solution exert additional pressure on the partition. The dissolving substance tends to expand and dilates the cellufane - just as a gas tends to expand and dilates the piston separating it from the vacuum.

With the help of this assumption, Van't Hoff was able to provide all the features of osmosis discovered by Pfeffer. The scientist derived an equation completely similar to the equation of state of an ideal gas:

$$\pi V=nRT,$$

where π is the osmotic pressure, V is the volume of the solution, n is the number of molecules of the solution, R is the universal gas constant, T is temperature. It has been shown that the similarity between the rise of a dissolved substance and a gas is significant, such that the interaction of the molecules of the dissolved substance with each other can be claimed.
