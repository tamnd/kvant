Three significant works of Albert Einstein

(on the 80th anniversary of their publication)

In 1905, three articles were written in the German physics journal Apnalen der Physik, which, without exaggeration, can be said to have played a decisive role in the entire subsequent development of physical science. The author of these articles was a young (he was then twenty years old) and little-known Albert Einstein, who occupied the modest position of an expert at the Swiss Patent Office in the city of Bern.

Here we will talk very briefly about these works in the order in which they were published.

The first of them arrived at the editorial office of the magazine on March 18, 1905 and was introduced to “On a heuristic point of view” concerning the origin and transformation of light.” Note that the word “heuristic”, which has an ancient Greek origin, means an approximation of the following: “facilitating Japanese, helping to comprehend the truth” (remember the exclamation “heuristic” - found it! - in the legend of Archimedes). This work developed a new, quantum understanding of the nature of light.

In the second half of the century, the English physicist Jack. K. Maxwell created theories of electrical and magnetic phenomena, in which, in particular, he showed that light is electromagnetic waves. According to this theory, the field of an electromagnetic wave with a given frequency exists throughout space; the field energy is distributed throughout space continuously and can change in an equally continuous manner.

The ox theory of light received numerous experimental confirmations and was considered firmly established. However, she could not explain some physical phenomena, and primarily equilibrium tell radiation. In 1900, the German physicist M. Planck came up with a formula that perfectly describes the glow spectrum of a heated body. To substantiate the formula, Planck assumed that the radiation energy of atoms does not change continuously, but in leaps, each time decreasing or increasing by values ​​proportional to the frequency of the wave. This is how discrete (that is, jump-like, not continuous) changes in energy entered physics.

A. Einstein in his work argued that the pattern found by Planck is comprehensive, far beyond the scope of the problem of tell radiation. He pointed to a class of phenomena (tell radiation, photoliminescence, photoelectric effect) that are “better explained by the assumption that the energy of light is distributed discretely in space. According to this assumption made here, the energy of a beam of light emanating from a certain point is not distributed continuously throughout the entire volume of space, but adds up from a finite number of weekly energy quanta localized in space, absorbed or generated only as a whole.”

Einstein will present the possibility of physical experiments that can serve to test the statement of his point of view. However, from them there was an experience in knocking out electrons from metal surfaces with light (photo effect). Einstein, based on the ideas he expressed, formed a law that is now called Einstein’s law for the photoelectric effect.

Sixteen years later, in 1921, A. Einstein was awarded the Nobel Prize “for important physical and mathematical research, especially for the discovery of the laws of physical effect.” But the significance of the 1905 work goes far beyond the theoretical explanation of the physical effect, putting forward a perfect understanding of the nature of light and the electromagnetic field in general. This work was a major step in the development of quantum physics.

The next article by A. Einstein is called “On the movement of parts suspended in a resting life, required by the molecular-cynic theory of tellotes.” She joined the editors of the journal on May 11, 1905, less than two months after her work on light quanta. It essentially built a theory of Brownian motion (although Einstein himself noted in this work that the data available to him regarding this phenomenon were not so accurate as to form a definite opinion about it).

In 1827, the English botanist R. Brown discovered that small particles suspended in a liquid are in constant and chaotic incessant movement. Numerous further experimental studies have shown that this movement is not associated with any external influence and that its oxidation increases with increasing temperature and depends on the particle size and the viscosity of the liquid.

Einstein considered this issue theoretically.
