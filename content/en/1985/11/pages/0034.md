The great German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777-1855) was called by his contemporaries the “King of Mathematics.”
His mathematical talent awoke very early. Already a nineteen-year-old student, he solved a problem that had stood for 2000 years, since the time of the ancient Greek geometers. He indicated all the numbers n for which a regular n-gon can be constructed (using a compass and ruler). In particular, he indicated a method for constructing a correct seventeen-year-old mathematics. And at the age of 22, he proved a theorem called the “Fundamental Theorem of Algebra” (about the existence of a complex root of any polynomial).
Gauss made enormous contributions to many areas of mathematics. In addition, he worked extensively and successfully in geodesy and physics.
Read more about K. Gauss and his works in our magazine (No. 1, 1972; No. 1, 1973; No. 8, 1977).

A triangle is the simplest flat figure. At the tops and three sides. But the study of the triangle gave rise to a whole science - trigonometry, in which the metric properties of a triangle are expressed through the function of its angles. This science arose from practical needs in changing land plots, drawing up maps of the area, and designing machines and mechanisms.
We find the first mention of the triangle and its properties in Egyptian texts dating back more than 4,000 years ago. In particular, it mentions a method for finding the area of ​​an equilateral triangle, which gives a good approximation at small angles with the vertex opposite the base. This area is found as the product of half the base and the side.
After 2000 years in Ancient Greece, the study of the property of a triangle reaches a high level - just remember the Pythagorean theorem and Heron’s formula.
