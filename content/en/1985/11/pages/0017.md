⟦folio 15⟧

Ostmos

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. BOROVOY

The cut branch of the plant began to wither. But then you put it in water, and after a relatively short time the sheets straighten out, become elastic again, and are filled with moisture.

Why is the branch waiting? What forces cause moisture to penetrate into the plant and move inside it? What holds water in cells and prevents it from leaving? How does a plant cell become permeable to water in only one direction: from the outside to the inside?

Scientists have been trying to answer these questions for a very long time, but a certain clarity was achieved only at the end of the 19th century. Some more time passed, and laboratories managed to facilitate (albeit very roughly) these natural phenomena. And already in our country they were successfully forced to work in various fields of science and technology.

Who discovered space and how?

In 1748, the French experimental physicist J. A. Nollet, while studying the boiling of liquids, encountered a hitherto unknown phenomenon. In one of his wholesalers, he thermally closed a glass of alcohol with a film of bull bubble and immersed it in the bottom of a larger vessel with water. After a few hours, the bubble swelled greatly - water seeped into the glass and increased the pressure in it. Nolle did not misunderstand this amazing fact and explained it in the following way: “An animal bladder can be more permeable to water than to alcohol; in this case, the speed of passage of water turns out to be greater than the speed of passage of alcohol.”

Let's repeat Nolle's experiment, but use less scarce materials - replace the bubble of existence with a sheet of cellulophane, and replace the alcohol with an aqueous solution of sugar.

figure 1 shows a device that can be made several times. A wide glass tube, covered with blue cellulophane, is lowered into a jar of clean water. The cellophane is secured so that water does not flow inside the tube (sealing can be done using adhesive tape, a rubber ring, plasticine, etc.). A thin glass tube enters the thick tube through a rubber stopper from above.

At the beginning of the experiment, sugar solution is poured into the tubes until the liquid level in the tubes and in the jar coincide (this moment is shown on figure 1 dashed line). After some time, you will notice that the height of the raster column will increase - just as in Nolle’s experiment, water begins its movement through the film, in our case cellulophane.

The phenomenon in which one-way penetration of the solvent through a semi-permeable partition separating the solvent is observed is called osmosis. This word is of Greek origin and means only pressure.
