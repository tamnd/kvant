Converging lens. Let a plane light wave (Fig. 2) fall on a biconvex glass lens in air. From the moment when the wave surface (more precisely, its selected part) touches with its middle the first surface of the lens at point C, let all its points (except the middle) pass partly in the air and partly in the glass. As you move away from the middle of the wave front to its edges, the proportion of the “glass” part of the path decreases, therefore, when the middle of the wave reaches the second surface of the lens at point C', all other parts of the wave front will move a long distance (the extreme points will travel the longest distance). The result is that upon exiting the lens, the plane wavefront ACB turns into a spherical wavefront A'C'B'. The center of this spherical wave - point F - is called, as is known, the focus of the lens. Diffusive lens. Figure 3 or will construct the case when a plane wave is incident on a biconcave glass lens in air. After the wave surface touches the lens at points A and B, the outer sections will lag behind other parts of the wave front (as they pass through glass, where the speed of light is less than in air). As a result, the plane wave ACB turns into a spherical wave A'C'B'. But the center of this spherical wave (point F) is on the same side of the lens from where the plane wave came. Point F is also called the focus of the lens, but the minimum focus, since refracted light does not actually reach here. The corresponding ray path is also shown in blue.

The reader can easily construct wave pictures of the passage of light through lenses when not plane, but spherical waves fall on them. After passing through the lens, they are converted into spherical waves, but with different radii. From such pictures one can obtain the lens formula, the same, of course, as from geometric optics.
