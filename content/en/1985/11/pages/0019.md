Thus, for the first time, quantitative data on osmosis was obtained, which helped the outstanding Dutch chemist J. H. Van't Hoff create theories of this phenomenon.

In 1887, the scientist completed a work in which he showed that the molecules of a dissolved substance behave in a solution in the same way as the molecules of an ideal gas in the state. And the dissolver in this case plays the role of... emptiness!

To understand Van't Hoff's reasoning, let's perform a thought experiment. Let a horizontal cylinder filled with water be divided into two equal parts of cellufane by a partition that can mix without breaking the seal (Fig. 3). If you dissolve sugar in one part of the cylinder, then due to the diffusion of pure water through the celluphan in the area of ​​the dissolver, the pressure there increases and the partition begins to mix. The volume of the solution will increase, the volume of clean water will decrease accordingly. But according to the results of this experimental assumption, the sugar molecules in the solution exert additional pressure on the partition. The dissolving substance tends to expand and dilates the cellufane - just as a gas tends to expand and dilates the piston separating it from the vacuum.

With the help of this assumption, Van't Hoff was able to provide all the features of osmosis discovered by Pfeffer. The scientist derived an equation completely similar to the equation of state of an ideal gas:

$$\pi V=nRT,$$

where π is the osmotic pressure, V is the volume of the solution, n is the number of molecules of the solution, R is the universal gas constant, T is temperature. It has been shown that the similarity between the rise of a dissolved substance and a gas is significant, such that the interaction of the molecules of the dissolved substance with each other can be claimed.
