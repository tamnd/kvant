Dipole in an electric field. Let's see how a dipole behaves when placed in an external electric field. First, into a uniform field with direction $\vec{E}$ (Fig. 3). The dipole charges are acted upon by forces $+q\vec{E}$ and $-q\vec{E}$ equal in magnitude but opposite in direction, which tend to rotate the dipole. Relative to the axis passing through the center of the dipole (point $O$) and perpendicular to the plane of the drawing, each force creates a torque equal to the product of the force modulus on the corresponding area (see Fig. 3): $qE \cdot l/2 \sin \alpha$.

The total torque will be equal to

$$M = 2qE \frac{l}{2} \sin \alpha = qlE \sin \alpha = p \cdot E \sin \alpha.$$

Thus, for given values ​​of $E$ and $\alpha$, the rotating moment $M$ is determined by the magnitude of the dipole moment $p$.

Under the influence of the torque, the dipole will rotate until it takes the position shown in figure 3 dashed lines. In this position, both the sum of forces and the sum of moments of forces acting on the dipole are equal to zero. This means that the dipole is in equilibrium. In this case, the electric moment of the dipole is associated with the field direction vector.

Consequently, in a uniform external electric field, the dipole is rotated and positioned so that its dipole moment is oriented along the field. Note that this position is the position of its stable equilibrium.

Let now the dipole be in a nonuniform external field. Of course, here too a rotating moment will arise, turning the dipole along the field (Fig. 4). But in this case, the charges are acted upon by forces of unequal magnitude, the equivalent of which is different from zero. Therefore, the dipole will also move progressively, being drawn into the region of the strong field (see this for yourself).

Dipoles in nature. Multi-tailed molecules are similar to electric dipoles - positive and negative charges of equal magnitude are separated in space. Examples of such dipole molecules are, say, molecules of hydrochloric acid HCl, consisting of positive hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) and negative chlorine ions ($\text{Cl}^-$). The molecules of the most common substance on earth - water $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ consists of two positive hydrogen ions and one negative oxygen ion (Fig. 5). Although these are systems of not two, but three charges, they behave like electric dipoles - the center of a positive charge is at a certain distance from the center of a negative charge, and the total positive charge is equal in magnitude to the total negative charge.
