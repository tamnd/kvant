What is inertia? As for this concept, it is discussed in sufficient detail in § 22 of Physics 8.
When two bodies interact with each other, they both receive accelerations, but the accelerations, generally speaking, are different: one has less than the other. About a body whose acceleration is less, we can say that its motion is more similar to motion by inertia (at which the acceleration is zero). Therefore, they say about that body that it has more inertia than its interaction partner. The time of interaction (it is the same for both bodies) was “not enough” for him to change his speed by the same amount as the other body “did”. A more inert body requires more time to change speed by a given value than a less inert body. Thus, inertia is a property of bodies. Quantity is expressed by mass, a quantity first introduced into mechanics by Newton.

Electric dipole and its electric moment

For the most part, we are surrounded by electrical neutral bodies. However, it would be wrong to say that they do not take any part in electrical interactions. It is enough to remember, for example, that two charges placed in some kind of dielectric interact more weakly than in a vacuum. The reason for this is the dielectric molecules. Although the dielectric consists of neutral molecules, they are capable of creating their own electric field, which weakens the electrical interaction of charges.

Let's consider the simplest example of an electrical neutral system - an electric dipole. This is the name for a set of two equal in magnitude but opposite in sign exact electric charges ±q, located at a certain distance l from each other (Fig. 1).

Dipole field. The electric field of a dipole can be found at any point between us, based on the principles of superresistance (“Physics 9”, § 42). Let's do this, for example, for point A (Fig. 2). The field strength at this point is equal to the vector sum of the directions created by the exact charges +q and -q:

$$\vec{E} = \vec{E}_+ + \vec{E}_-$$

or

$$E = E_+ - E_- = \frac{kq}{(r-l/2)^2} - \frac{kq}{(r+l/2)^2} = \frac{2kql}{(r^2 - l^2/4)^2},$$

where r is the distance from the middle of the dipole to point A.

At large distances, when r >> l, we get

$$E = \frac{2kql}{r^3} = p \cdot \frac{2k}{r^3},$$

where p =ql is called the electric moment of the dipole. More precisely, ql is the modulus of the dipole electric moment $\vec{p}$, and this vector is directed from the impact charge to the positive one. Electric torque is the main characteristic of a dipole. In this case, it determines the electric field of the dipole at large distances from it.
