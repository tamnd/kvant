Mersenne problems and Pascal competition

This ends the first stage of the “life” of the cycloid. The first problems connected with it were solved very well, and the cycloid firmly took its place in the field of vision of major scientists. But there remained unsolved problems that were natural to pose within the framework of the developing analysis of infinitesimals. The promoter of new tasks (in the case of cycloids) was Mersenne, whom we have already mentioned. It is worth saying a few more words about him and his unique role in the history of science. The French monk Mersenne had his own achievements in both mathematics and physics (for example, he quite accurately measured the speed of sound), but his merits as an organizer of science were much higher. At a time when there were no scientific journals, almost all scientific communications were carried out through Mersenne: he was a kind of “mailbox”. Scientists from different countries informed him about their results, understanding that he would promptly let interested colleagues and competitors know about them (and Mersenne’s authority was a guarantee of maintaining priority). Mersenne kept track of current problems and transferred them to those scientists who were naturally occupied with them. In 1639, “Mersenne Thursdays” began, which were attended by Etten and Blaise Pascally (father and son), Desargues, Midorge, Roberval, in short, all the gray scientists who were in Paris that year. These "Thursdays" were representatives of the French Academy of Sciences. The example of Christien Huygens (1629-1695), with whom Mersenne corresponded since 1646, shows how Mersenne cared about the development of young talent. He begins with educational problems, moves on to unsolved ones, predicts that his young correspondent will become “Apollo and Archimedes ... of the coming century,” and finally poses a problem (promising a prize for the solution) about the reduced ligne of a physical matrix, which Heugens will be able to solve only after several decades, without stopping to think about it all this time.

As for the cycloid, Mersenne believed that we should not limit ourselves to calculating the area of a complete segment of the cycloid, but should consider the segments cut off by various horizontal ones, we need to find the centers of gravity of these segments, the volumes of the bodies of revolution formed by them, etc. Mersenne, it turns out, correctly assessed that in these problems, elementary manipulations with Cavalieri’s “indivisible layers” do not lead to the goal (you need to calculate the interval from sine in general form). Therefore, Mersenne aimed at these tasks the most promising and original of his colleagues, Blaise Pascal.

Pascal, however, took up this task only in the spring of 1658, when he was in the Port-Royal monastery after severe upheavals and when it seemed that he had completely become disillusioned with mathematics and would never return to it. The stories of his niece Gilbertie Père and his niece Margarita about Pascal’s last contact with the mathematician have been preserved. He remembered Mersenne's tasks when he could not sleep due to severe toothache. Reflections on the cycloid distracted him from the pain and gradually captured Pascal. By the morning, he knew the solution to a problem that had long eluded him and... was completely cured of his toothache. Pascal did not intend to write down the result, but his friend the Duke of Roanne persuaded him to do so, reassuring him that his return to mathematics was caused by “inspiration from above.” The Duke allocated 60 pistoles to organize a competition to solve Mersenne's problems.
