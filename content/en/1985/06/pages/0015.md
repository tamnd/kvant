The Pascal Competition played a big role in scientific life. For the advanced analysis of infinitesimals, it was fundamental to move from considering specific examples to general methods. Pascal's considerations practically did not use the specifics of the cycliod and could be easily transferred to the general situation. However, Pascal himself did not do this, and this was appreciated in hindsight when Leibniz (1646-1716), who, along with Newot, developed the general methods of differential and integral calculus, turned, on the recommendation of Heugens, to Pascal’s work. “I was illuminated with a new light,” Leibniz wrote and was surprised that Pascal did not formulate the promised reception, as if “there were veils over his eyes.” It's hard to guess why this happened. Scientists often do not see moves that, for some time, seem natural. But again, it can be assumed that Pascal was no longer worried about the fate of mathematics: he devoted his last energy to thinking about the meaning of life, about the place of man on Earth.

The Pascal competition began a new life for the cyclioda. The competition participants did not limit themselves to the tasks proposed by Pascal. For example,

Christopher Wren (1632-1723), a talented English mathematician, famous architect (creator of St. Paul's Cathedral in London), did not achieve much success in the competition, but reported a theorem that made a very strong impression on mathematicians. He proved that the length of one cycliod arc is 8r.

Clock with cyclondal dial

Another participant in the competition, 28-year-old Geugens, was distracted by solving the cyclonde problem from building a motherboard clock, which he enthusiastically devoted all his time to for the second year. The first watch appeared in mid-1658. Since then, Geugens has been busy arranging them. The idea underlying the matnik clock is that the period of oscillation of the matnik is a constantly reproducible unit of time, which, due to the ischronic property of the matnik discovered by Galileo (independence of the period from the amplitude of the swing), remains unchanged when the oscillations decay. Geugens wanted to create a marine chronometer with which it would be possible to change the longitude on board the ship (based on the difference in local time on board and at the port of departure; the time of departure is what the chronometer “remembers”). The problem of measuring longitude was the gap of the century. It is not surprising that leading maritime powers awarded fabulous prizes for a completely reliable way to measure longitude on board.

Soon after it became known about Heugens' invention, it was found out that Galileo had the idea of ​​a motherboard clock, but only came up with it a year before his death, when he no longer had the strength to implement it. His son Vincenzo, who completed the project, could not cope with this task either.

While creating the first clock, Geugens discovered that Galileo's statement about the timelessness of the oscillations of the mathematical mat was not accurate. It is valid only for small span angles. How can we ensure timelessness? Geugens realized that for this it is necessary to reduce the length of the mat as it deviates from the vertical.
