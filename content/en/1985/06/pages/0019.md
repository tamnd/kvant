Thousands of years ago, people knew that when certain substances rub against each other, they become, as we would say now, electrified. It is possible that the ancient Egyptian and ancient Greek priests amazed the public with the spectacle of a comb attracting pieces of papyrus. However, despite the ancient history, not everything is clear with electrifying training. It was once believed that there is a certain electrifying liquid that flows from body to body upon contact, and a body with its deficiency is attracted to a body with its excess.

When people discovered the electron, they realized that an electrified body must contain an excess or deficiency of electrons, and they began to believe that when electrons are transferred from one body to another. But such a conclusion was not entirely justified: in a dielectric—that’s why it’s a dielectric—electrons cannot wander freely. Significant progress in understanding how electrification by trene occurs has been achieved in the last decade. This is what we are talking about further. The experiments that will be discussed were carried out in 1969-1977 at the Physico-Technical Institute. A.F. Ioffe in Leningrad. Two cylinders made of good dielectrics (Teflon, amber, ruby, plexiglass), with a diameter of 10 mm and a height of 14 mm, were pressed against each other with their ends and rotated at a speed of 0.5 rps relative to each other (figure 1,a). After three revolutions, the charges of the cylinders were measured, for which they were separated and placed in turn in a chamber connected to an electrometer (figure 1,b); then they again placed one on top of the other, three more turns were made, the charges were measured again, and so on. It turned out that the initially neutral samples become charged during the process. The sum of their charges, however, remains equal to zero. Consequently, in the process of charge renaming there is no “third extra” involved.
