The term "cyclonde" (derived from a circle) belongs to Galileo (1564-1642), who, apparently, was the first to consider it. "Roulette" is a French term (from the verb rouler - to roll), "trochonda" is its Greek equivalent. The discrepancy in terminology reflects the vigorous Franco-Italian debate about priority in the discovery of the cyclonde: Pascal claims that Marin Mersin (1588–1648) considered it as early as 1615, before Galileo.

Epicyclondes in the Ptolemaic system

Pascal’s surprise at the fact that the cyclonde was not considered by the ancients is noteworthy. What is the reason for this? The fact is that for the ancient sources of the appearance of new curves there were various problems on geometric location, and the trajectories of curvilinear movements were beyond the interests of most mathematicians of antiquity.

Only the new mechanics, which began in the first years of the 17th century with Galileo’s works on motions, restructured the ideological origins, and it is famous that it was Galileo who, as a result of the first approach, became an epicyclonde. Epicyclondes (see page 2 of the cover, a, b) are obtained as the trajectories of points of a circle swinging without sliding along the outer side of another circle (when rolling on the inner side, curves called zypocyclondes are obtained; region, e). Epicyclondes are found in the geocentric system of the world*), Ptolemy (85?—165?). Even Plato (427-347 BC) and Aristotle (384-322 BC) believed that all planets, as well as the Sun and Moon, rotate uniformly in a circle with the center in the Earth. This theory not only contradicted the numerical data obtained from the compilation of astronomical tables, but did not explain a number of crude qualitative effects. Let's say Mars, which, as a rule, moves counterclockwise across the starry sky, in the vicinity of opposition begins to move clockwise (“retrograde movement”). This is easy to explain within the framework of the geocentric system, but tree scientists, who do not doubt the basic dogma of the geocentric system, decided to reconcile it with the data of astronomical observations. Apollonius, and after him Ptolemy, maintain the axiom that uniform circular motion reigns in the world, but believe that the motion of the planets is complex; the planet moves in a circle, the center of which in turn rotates in a large circle centered on the Earth. It turns out that it is possible to select the speeds of these two rotations in such a way that the trajectories of the planets will contain loops in which “retrograde motion” will occur. These trajectories will not be exactly epicyclones, but will be very close to curves.
