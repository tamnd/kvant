Introduction

There is a saying: “It is better to see once than to hear once.” The meaning of this saying is completely clear: you can say whatever you want, but in order to be convinced of what is said, you need to see everything with your own eyes. The eyes will not deceive you.

However, can you always trust your eyes? It turns out not always. Sometimes we see something that is not really there. Do not think that we are talking about hallucinations or visual defects. You observe, someone who is in good health and has good eyesight, under certain conditions, can see a very distorted picture of a phenomenon, very far from what will happen in reality. And the reason for this is not that feelings can change a person. Let’s put it together on an observation camera; its “readings” can already be considered objective. And yet, having watched the stained film, we see the same thing as the observer whom we expressed disbelief and whom we replaced with a film camera.

Both what we see on the screen and what the observer saw are not at all what will actually happen.

How can this be?

We will show how this can be with several examples. Otherwise, you won’t find anything mysterious in our examples. They only say that you need to think about every physical observation, try to understand what the instrument readings say, and based on these readings, reconstruct the real picture of the phenomenon.

Photographing light on the fly

About fifteen years ago, the American physicist Michel Duguay designed a photographic shutter that closed in an unusually short time. Why are such gates needed?

If we are photographing a still image, the amount of time the shutter is open—this period is called exposure time—does not affect the sharpness of the image. The subject is motionless, the image of the object on the film is also motionless, so increasing the exposure time does not improve the sharpness of the image. It's a different matter if the subject is moving. Then the movement and image of the object on the film. In this case, the shorter the exposure time, the sharper the image of the object in the photograph will be. And vice versa, the longer the exposure time, the more “smeared” the image of the object will be.

In some photographs (usually these are photographs of people running, quickly tormented by automatic machines), the blur of the image is reduced - it seems to expose rapid movement. But, as a rule, the faster the subject moves, the shorter the exposure time should be, the less time the shutter opens. (In our discussions, we do not take into account that photographic film has a certain magnification and that a shorter exposure time must correspond to a higher magnification. We will assume that the film magnification can be selected so that it corresponds to the desired exposure time.)

Let us now return to the beginning of our story. The howl was created by a photographic shutter with a fantastic exposure time - about $10^{-11}$ s. Of course, this time could be considered “fantastic” only before the creation of the shutter. When it is created, it is no longer a fantasy, but a real, remarkable achievement.
