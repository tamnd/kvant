A similar reasoning (perhaps somewhat equal) is adopted by the Frenchman Gilles Person, based on Robervala (1602-1675). Roberval's merit lies in the fact that he did not limit himself to considering the cyclode, but developed a general technique for drawing tangents by representing curves in the form of trajectories of complex movements. His method was at first completely competitive in comparison with other methods of drawing tangents available at that time.

Yet the future belonged to the more direct methods developed by Pierre Fermat (1601–1665) and René Descartes (1596–1650). These methods did not require an “individual approach” to each curve (for example, selection of component movements). Do these methods allow us to draw a tangent to the cyclode - the curve of mechanical origin?

We found out that Fermat’s tangent method can be constructed without any difficulties, but Descartes’ method, which is focused on considering curves defined by polynomials, failed. But Descartes is not such a person as to give reason to claim that he failed to cope with the task available to his colleagues.

He comes up with an amazingly beautiful mechanical theory. In the modeled version, we are talking about the fact that when processing a cyclode, we consider not the movement of an isolated material point, but the movement of a solid plate - a rolling circle. In this case, at each moment of time, the movement, as a rule, differs little from the rotation of the plate around a certain (instantaneous) center of rotation. Velocities at all points of redistribution of the radius of the instantaneous center of rotation. In the case of a cyclode, as is easy to understand, the instantaneous center of rotation is not the center of the rolling circle, but its point of contact with the directive line. From here the rule for drawing a tangent, formulated above, immediately follows (the written right angle rests on the diameter).

Area and “companion” of the cyclode

Let's finish with tangents and move on to areas. In the case of the cyclode, French mathematicians considered these problems even earlier than the tangent problem (perhaps the reason was greater confidence in area problems). First of all, we are talking about calculating the area of ​​the segment under the bottom arch of the cyclode. Vvinini and Toricelli argue that Galileo knew that the area of ​​this segment was equal to three times the area of ​​the generating circle. Roberval proved this theorem in 1634. A few words about Roberval’s very beautiful reasoning. At each moment of time, Roberval traces the observed point of the cyclode on the vertical diameter of the rolling circle and watches how this projection moves. A new curve is obtained, which Roberval called “the companion of the cyclode” (Fig. 2) And then they found out that this very symmetrical curve is ... a concatenated sinusoid! This is exactly how (and not as a sine graph) the sine wave first appeared in mathematics. The area under the sinusoid is easy to calculate: if you cut it with the middle horizontal and vertical lines, you get the parts that make up a rectangle on the sides 2lr and r (see Fig. 2, where r=1), that is, the area is 2lr².
