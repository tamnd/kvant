But we cannot yet impart a speed close to the speed of light to a material body of this size - this requires enormous amounts of energy. (For example, to accelerate a body weighing 1 g to a speed equal to half the speed of light, you need to expend energy on the order of $10^{11}$ J. Such energy is produced every day by ten modern nuclear power plants.) The maximum speeds of macroscopic bodies achieved under terrestrial conditions are on the order of tens of kilometers per second. We reduce electrons to speeds that are close to the speed of light. But you can’t photograph an electron without representation—it’s too small. We need an object that would have noticeable dimensions and move at the speed of light. And Duguay, the creator of the shutter, found such an object. It turned out to be... the light itself.

The light source was a laser, which produced radiation in the form of very short pulses—with a duration of approximately $10^{-11}$ s. A light pulse emitted by a laser can be represented as a certain volume filled with light waves and moving in space at the speed of light (figure 1). This volume is called a wave (or light) packet. The packet length is very high, $l=c\tau$, where $c$ is the speed of light, and $\tau$ is the emission time, that is, the pulse duration; substituting $c=3 \cdot 10^5$ km/s, $\tau \approx 10^{-11}$ s, we get $l \approx 3$ mm. A “body” of this size can be photographed.

It would seem that everything is simple - we place the camera on the side of the path of the light “body”, point it at some point along the path and at the right moment release the shutter. This setup of the experiment is depicted in figure 1. However, if we do this, we will not see anything on the film. Why does an image appear on film only if light from a moving body hits it? This can be “own” light if the body itself emits (as, for example, happens when photographing a hot light bulb). If the body itself does not glow, it must be illuminated, and then the light reflected or scattered by the body from an external source will fall on the film. From figure 1 it is clear that the light waves that make up the packet propagate along such a path that they cannot possibly get into the camera lens. Illuminating such a packet with an external source is also free - the packet does not reflect or scatter light, light waves from an external source pass through the wave packet as if through a free ghost, without changing or affecting the packet.

The solution was found like this. A glass vessel with water was placed in the path of the light clot. A few drops of milk were added to the water. This additive made the water a little cloudy. A turbid medium scatters the light falling on it. When a light clot enters such a vessel with moona water, its constituent light waves begin to dissipate and the clot becomes visible.
