"skin". The charge on the skin is exactly equal to the sample's own charge, but of course with the opposite sign (so overall the sample is neutral).

The rest is clear - different dielectrics, different self-charges and different charges of the skins. They rubbed and mixed the skins and got charged pieces.

Let, for example, each cylindrical sample have a length equal to its diameter. Then the end area will be 1/6 of the entire surface. Let us denote the charges of the skins on the samples (before friction) $q_A$ and $q_B$ and assume that the charge of each skin is distributed uniformly over the surface of the sample. Then, after friction, when the skins at the ends are mixed and their charges are divided equally, the total charges $q'_A$ and $q'_B$ of the new skins will be made up of 5/6 of their own old charges and half of the sum of $1/6 q_A$ and $1/6 q_B$:

$$q'_A = \frac{5}{6} q_A + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{6} q_A + \frac{1}{6} q_B \right) = q_A + \frac{1}{12} \left( q_B - q_A \right),$$

$$q'_B = \frac{5}{6} q_B + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{6} q_A + \frac{1}{6} q_B \right) = q_B + \frac{1}{12} \left( q_A - q_B \right).$$

It is clear that since the charge of the skin changes (and this will always happen when $q_A \neq q_B$), then the charge of the sample as a whole will change.

Can two pieces of the same dielectric be electrified?
If they have the same intrinsic charges, then no. But they can have different own charges, that is, contain different amounts of vacancies, if the conditions for the formation of the pieces differed (for example, one piece of an insulator was formed surrounded by vapors of some of the elements included in it, so that all the vacancies in the place of ins of this element were filled, and around another piece the vapor pressure was less, and the vacancies in the place of ins remained). That is why electrification was discovered during the friction of two cylinders of seemingly identical ruby.

Electrification occurs not only through friction. Faraday wrote in 1833 that electrification will occur when crystals are deformed, including when they split. However, the time apparently did not come then to study this process. Almost a hundred years later, in 1930, it was noticed that mica became electrified when split, but a detailed study of electrification when split began a quarter of a century later.

As already mentioned, a dielectric crystal “by itself,” that is, with a clean surface, without sandpaper, is not neutral. It contains vacancies, and, as a rule, they are distributed unevenly throughout the crystal. The charge of the skin growing on the sample can in many cases be considered distributed almost evenly over its surface. When we break a crystal in half, the halves “inherit” half the volume and surface, and with it half the skin. But the charge of the skin is divided purely, equally, while the charge of the volume, if it was distributed unevenly, is not divided equally. In this case, the charges of the parts resulting from heating will have opposite signs, since one part of the skin will have a greater charge than its own (volume), and the other will have less. But there is another reason for the uneven division of charge.
