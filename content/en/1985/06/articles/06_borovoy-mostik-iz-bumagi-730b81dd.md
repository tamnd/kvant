Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. VOROVOY

I. One of the most important properties of any engineering structures is their rigidity. It is clear that neither under the influence of its own gravity nor under the influence of external loads the structure should not change its shape. True, it is impossible to completely avoid deformation, but you need to choose the material and type of parts so that changes in shape occur to a certain, pre-calculated limit. This task facing engineers almost always complements another: to make the design as simple, cheap, light as possible, using as little materials as possible.

Navigating between these conflicting requirements, design engineers find the optimal solution for this. In a number of industries, such as aircraft manufacturing, the issue of combining maximum rigidity and at the same time minimum life mass is important.

The great designer is nature. It has long ago solved many problems that concern engineers.

In the course of natural selection, such masterpieces as bird feathers, bamboo stems, hollow bones of land animals, etc. arose.

It is not for nothing that we cited as an example natural structures based on a tube - a hollow cylinder. Why have they become so widespread?

Let's try to figure this out. We'll teach you how to use scales using a simple experiment.

II. The experiment that is proposed to be proven is known in the literature as the “Umov Experience” (or “Tube Strength”).

Nikolai Alekseevich Umov (1846-1915) was a prominent Russian physicist, for almost twenty years he was a professor at Moscow University. Nicholas, his important works were related to the issue of re-transfer of energy; he was the first to introduce the concept of speed and direction of energy movement, energy flow, energy density at a given point, etc. Umov was also known as an excellent lecturer, devoting a lot of attention to demonstration experiments.

At home, the experiment can be performed using very simple “equipment”. Cut from thick

Then, using two stacks of books as stands, as shown in figure 1, put one of the sheets on them and load it with light weights (and if there are none, then with coins). Under a small load, the leaf - a kind of bridge - will be strongly flexed (see Fig. 1,a).

It turns out that the rigidity of our structure can be increased tenfold, and in a very simple way. To do this, roll the second leaf into a tube and wrap it with thread so that it does not unravel. You will see that under the same load the tube will hardly bend. Only a significant large weight will cause it to noticeably deform (see Fig. 1.6).

How tough is, for example, a bird’s feather? Carry out similar tests. In our experiments, a quill feather about 10 cm long “held” a weight weighing 0.5 kg.

“Umov’s Experience” can be significantly changed. Make so-called profiles from identical sheets of paper: corners with equal and unequal sides, channels, tabs and I-beams, corrugated sheet (Fig. 2). Make sure that they all have much more rigidity than the original paper bridge. To do this, conduct a series of comparative experiments: determine the deflection values ​​with equal loads or achieve the same deflection by loading the structure with different weights.

III. Now it is necessary, at least at a qualitative level, to understand what the rigidity of a structure with respect to bending depends on.

Do one more experiment. For it you will need a rectangular block of rubber (or other elastic material) with a cross-section of approximately 1-2 cm² and a length of about 10 cm.

Apply a scale grid of longitudinal and transverse straight lines (Fig. 3,a) to the block. Then, according to the block, the mesh will be distorted (Fig. 3,b). In this case, the transverse lines will remain straight, but the surfaces relative to each other will no longer be parallel, and the longitudinal lines will be curved. It is easy to notice that the material of the block experiences tension on one side and rolling on the other. But among the longitudinal lines there is one, looking in the middle, which does not change its length. Obviously, all horizontal layers of materials lying behind this line do not experience deformation. Therefore, it is called a neutral layer (or neutral surface).

From this experiment it follows that the further a section of the block is located from the neutral surface, the greater the stretching (or rolling) it experiences. Then, according to Hooke's law, the force of elastic resistance to bending increases with distance from the neutral layer. In other words, the main contribution to the stiffness of the block comes from the layer farthest from the neutral one. And from here the conclusion is: in order to increase the rigidity of the structure, it is necessary to place its main material as far as possible from this layer. This is why the piece of bar in Umov’s Experiment offers almost no resistance to the load, while, for example, an I-beam made of the same material behaves much more rigidly.

However, removing the base material from the neutral surface does not increase too much. So, if an I-beam is made very thin in the middle, it becomes unstable and will twist. At the same time, the I-beam design, with the same rigidity as a solid square beam, turns out to be four and a half times lighter.

Now it is clear why nature uses tubular structures so widely. And they were born in the process of evolution. While life was concentrated in water, there was no particular need for a small mass of the skeleton - the force of the liquid that was pushed out helped. Therefore, sharks, for example, have preserved a massive cartilaginous skeleton from their ancestors. But then living creatures came out on land. Now the strength of the skeleton had to be combined with its possible lower mass. The process of transforming cartilaginous formations into tubular bones lasted for millions of years and ended with the creation of a strong, lightweight and economical structure in terms of materials consumption.

The principle of their action is the transformation of vertical loads into lateral pressures of the arch ring, which are transmitted to the base (heel) of the arch.
