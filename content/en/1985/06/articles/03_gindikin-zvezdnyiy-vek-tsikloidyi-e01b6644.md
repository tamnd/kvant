Star Age of Cyclopidae

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. G. Gindikin

Cyclops played an unprecedented role in the history of mathematics of the 17th century, the Geronic age of infinitesimal analysis. Perhaps the most eloquent traces of this have been preserved far from mathematics - in fiction. In the third part of Guliver's Travels, Jonan Swift tells how Guliver ends up on the flying island of Laputa and the king, patron of science and music, treats him to an exquisite dinner. A bar spatula cut in the shape of an equilateral triangle and a diamond-shaped piece of beef were followed by a pudding in the shape of a cyclopidae. Believing that at the beginning of the 18th century it was difficult to imagine a more sophisticated form!

Thirty years later, Cyclops appears on the pages of “The Life and Meninium of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman,” the immortal novel by Laurence Sterne. The eccentric good-natured Toby Shandy, intending to build a site that should become an example of fortification art, decides to give it the shape of a cyclopidae: “... he changed his mind on the site invented by the Marquis de L'Hôpital, which is so thoroughly and scientifically described by Bernoulli the Younger, as your dears can see in the Acta Erud. Lips, an. 1695; also, the places are kept in stable balance by a lead weight, which protects them no worse than two hours, if the places are drawn in the shape of a curved line, as close as possible to a cyclopid.”

We will return to L'Hopital, Bernoulli and the scientific journal Acta Eruditorium, but for now we will find out what Toby Shandy's contemporaries, who lived in the mid-18th century, could have known about Cyclopides.

An authoritative source of information is the "History of the roulette, also called the triode or cyclopida", dated as early as October 1658 and signed by Amos Dettonville; as we will see, this is a pseudo-mime of the French mathematician, physicist and thinker Blaise Pascal (1623-1662). He writes: “The roll is linear and columnar, that after the straight line and the circle there is no line more frequently encountered; it so often traces itself before the eyes of every one that one must be surprised at how its ancients have not been considered... or it is nothing other than the path of the wheel when it rolls with its own motion from the moment the nail began to rise from the ground, until the continuous quality of the wheel brings it back to the ground after the end of a whole revolution, considering that the wheel is a perfect circle, the nail is a point of its circumference, and the earth is perfectly flat.” This text contains the definition of cyclopidae: it is

the trajectory of a point on a circle that rolls without sliding in a straight line. The point, rising from its lower position, describes a convex arch symmetrically, and with each revolution a new arch is obtained; at the point of contact of the arches it is sharp (Fig. 1).

The term "cyclonde" (derived from a circle) belongs to Galileo (1564-1642), who, apparently, was the first to consider it. "Roulette" is a French term (from the verb rouler - to roll), "trochonda" is its Greek equivalent. The discrepancy in terminology reflects the vigorous Franco-Italian debate about priority in the discovery of the cyclonde: Pascal claims that Marin Mersin (1588–1648) considered it as early as 1615, before Galileo.

Epicyclondes in the Ptolemaic system

Pascal’s surprise at the fact that the cyclonde was not considered by the ancients is noteworthy. What is the reason for this? The fact is that for the ancient sources of the appearance of new curves there were various problems on geometric location, and the trajectories of curvilinear movements were beyond the interests of most mathematicians of antiquity.

Only the new mechanics, which began in the first years of the 17th century with Galileo’s works on motions, restructured the ideological origins, and it is famous that it was Galileo who, as a result of the first approach, became an epicyclonde. Epicyclondes (see page 2 of the cover, a, b) are obtained as the trajectories of points of a circle swinging without sliding along the outer side of another circle (when rolling on the inner side, curves called zypocyclondes are obtained; region, e). Epicyclondes are found in the geocentric system of the world*), Ptolemy (85?—165?). Even Plato (427-347 BC) and Aristotle (384-322 BC) believed that all planets, as well as the Sun and Moon, rotate uniformly in a circle with the center in the Earth. This theory not only contradicted the numerical data obtained from the compilation of astronomical tables, but did not explain a number of crude qualitative effects. Let's say Mars, which, as a rule, moves counterclockwise across the starry sky, in the vicinity of opposition begins to move clockwise (“retrograde movement”). This is easy to explain within the framework of the geocentric system, but tree scientists, who do not doubt the basic dogma of the geocentric system, decided to reconcile it with the data of astronomical observations. Apollonius, and after him Ptolemy, maintain the axiom that uniform circular motion reigns in the world, but believe that the motion of the planets is complex; the planet moves in a circle, the center of which in turn rotates in a large circle centered on the Earth. It turns out that it is possible to select the speeds of these two rotations in such a way that the trajectories of the planets will contain loops in which “retrograde motion” will occur. These trajectories will not be exactly epicyclones, but will be very close to curves. If we assume that it is not a circle but a circle that is rolling along a stationary circle, then its boundary points will describe epicyclondes,

Do these methods allow us to draw a tangent to the cyclode - the curve of mechanical origin?

We found out that Fermat’s tangent method can be constructed without any difficulties, but Descartes’ method, which is focused on considering curves defined by polynomials, failed. But Descartes is not such a person as to give reason to claim that he failed to cope with the task available to his colleagues.

He comes up with an amazingly beautiful mechanical theory. In the modeled version, we are talking about the fact that when processing a cyclode, we consider not the movement of an isolated material point, but the movement of a solid plate - a rolling circle. In this case, at each moment of time, the movement, as a rule, differs little from the rotation of the plate around a certain (instantaneous) center of rotation. Velocities at all points of redistribution of the radius of the instantaneous center of rotation. In the case of a cyclode, as is easy to understand, the instantaneous center of rotation is not the center of the rolling circle, but its point of contact with the directive line. From here the rule for drawing a tangent, formulated above, immediately follows (the written right angle rests on the diameter).

Area and “companion” of the cyclode

Let's finish with tangents and move on to areas. In the case of the cyclode, French mathematicians considered these problems even earlier than the tangent problem (perhaps the reason was greater confidence in area problems). First of all, we are talking about calculating the area of ​​the segment under the bottom arch of the cyclode. Vvinini and Toricelli argue that Galileo knew that the area of ​​this segment was equal to three times the area of ​​the generating circle. Roberval proved this theorem in 1634. A few words about Roberval’s very beautiful reasoning. At each moment of time, Roberval traces the observed point of the cyclode on the vertical diameter of the rolling circle and watches how this projection moves. A new curve is obtained, which Roberval called “the companion of the cyclode” (Fig. 2) And then they found out that this very symmetrical curve is ... a concatenated sinusoid! This is exactly how (and not as a sine graph) the sine wave first appeared in mathematics. The area under the sinusoid is easy to calculate: if you cut it with the middle horizontal and vertical lines, you get the parts that make up a rectangle on the sides 2lr and r (see Fig. 2, where r=1), that is, the area is 2lr². As for the two remaining figures (they were called Roberval's petals), they perfectly illustrated a very popular method of calculating areas in those years - the principle of Bonaventura Cavalieri (1598-1647). It consists in the fact that if two figures intersect any horizontal inch segments of equal length,

Mersenne problems and Pascal competition

This ends the first stage of the “life” of the cycloid. The first problems connected with it were solved very well, and the cycloid firmly took its place in the field of vision of major scientists. But there remained unsolved problems that were natural to pose within the framework of the developing analysis of infinitesimals. The promoter of new tasks (in the case of cycloids) was Mersenne, whom we have already mentioned. It is worth saying a few more words about him and his unique role in the history of science. The French monk Mersenne had his own achievements in both mathematics and physics (for example, he quite accurately measured the speed of sound), but his merits as an organizer of science were much higher. At a time when there were no scientific journals, almost all scientific communications were carried out through Mersenne: he was a kind of “mailbox”. Scientists from different countries informed him about their results, understanding that he would promptly let interested colleagues and competitors know about them (and Mersenne’s authority was a guarantee of maintaining priority). Mersenne kept track of current problems and transferred them to those scientists who were naturally occupied with them. In 1639, “Mersenne Thursdays” began, which were attended by Etten and Blaise Pascally (father and son), Desargues, Midorge, Roberval, in short, all the gray scientists who were in Paris that year. These "Thursdays" were representatives of the French Academy of Sciences. The example of Christien Huygens (1629-1695), with whom Mersenne corresponded since 1646, shows how Mersenne cared about the development of young talent. He begins with educational problems, moves on to unsolved ones, predicts that his young correspondent will become “Apollo and Archimedes ... of the coming century,” and finally poses a problem (promising a prize for the solution) about the reduced ligne of a physical matrix, which Heugens will be able to solve only after several decades, without stopping to think about it all this time.

As for the cycloid, Mersenne believed that we should not limit ourselves to calculating the area of a complete segment of the cycloid, but should consider the segments cut off by various horizontal ones, we need to find the centers of gravity of these segments, the volumes of the bodies of revolution formed by them, etc. Mersenne, it turns out, correctly assessed that in these problems, elementary manipulations with Cavalieri’s “indivisible layers” do not lead to the goal (you need to calculate the interval from sine in general form). Therefore, Mersenne aimed at these tasks the most promising and original of his colleagues, Blaise Pascal.

Pascal, however, took up this task only in the spring of 1658, when he was in the Port-Royal monastery after severe upheavals and when it seemed that he had completely become disillusioned with mathematics and would never return to it. The stories of his niece Gilbertie Père and his niece Margarita about Pascal’s last contact with the mathematician have been preserved. He remembered Mersenne's tasks when he could not sleep due to severe toothache. Reflections on the cycloid distracted him from the pain and gradually captured Pascal. By the morning, he knew the solution to a problem that had long eluded him and... was completely cured of his toothache. Pascal did not intend to write down the result, but his friend the Duke of Roanne persuaded him to do so, reassuring him that his return to mathematics was caused by “inspiration from above.” The Duke allocated 60 pistoles to organize a competition to solve Mersenne's problems.

In June 1658, a cyclical letter was sent to the largest geometers in Europe with a proposal to decide

The Pascal Competition played a big role in scientific life. For the advanced analysis of infinitesimals, it was fundamental to move from considering specific examples to general methods. Pascal's considerations practically did not use the specifics of the cycliod and could be easily transferred to the general situation. However, Pascal himself did not do this, and this was appreciated in hindsight when Leibniz (1646-1716), who, along with Newot, developed the general methods of differential and integral calculus, turned, on the recommendation of Heugens, to Pascal’s work. “I was illuminated with a new light,” Leibniz wrote and was surprised that Pascal did not formulate the promised reception, as if “there were veils over his eyes.” It's hard to guess why this happened. Scientists often do not see moves that, for some time, seem natural. But again, it can be assumed that Pascal was no longer worried about the fate of mathematics: he devoted his last energy to thinking about the meaning of life, about the place of man on Earth.

The Pascal competition began a new life for the cyclioda. The competition participants did not limit themselves to the tasks proposed by Pascal. For example,

Christopher Wren (1632-1723), a talented English mathematician, famous architect (creator of St. Paul's Cathedral in London), did not achieve much success in the competition, but reported a theorem that made a very strong impression on mathematicians. He proved that the length of one cycliod arc is 8r.

Clock with cyclondal dial

Another participant in the competition, 28-year-old Geugens, was distracted by solving the cyclonde problem from building a motherboard clock, which he enthusiastically devoted all his time to for the second year. The first watch appeared in mid-1658. Since then, Geugens has been busy arranging them. The idea underlying the matnik clock is that the period of oscillation of the matnik is a constantly reproducible unit of time, which, due to the ischronic property of the matnik discovered by Galileo (independence of the period from the amplitude of the swing), remains unchanged when the oscillations decay. Geugens wanted to create a marine chronometer with which it would be possible to change the longitude on board the ship (based on the difference in local time on board and at the port of departure; the time of departure is what the chronometer “remembers”). The problem of measuring longitude was the gap of the century. It is not surprising that leading maritime powers awarded fabulous prizes for a completely reliable way to measure longitude on board.

Soon after it became known about Heugens' invention, it was found out that Galileo had the idea of ​​a motherboard clock, but only came up with it a year before his death, when he no longer had the strength to implement it. His son Vincenzo, who completed the project, could not cope with this task either.

While creating the first clock, Geugens discovered that Galileo's statement about the timelessness of the oscillations of the mathematical mat was not accurate. It is valid only for small span angles. How can we ensure timelessness? Geugens realized that for this it is necessary to reduce the length of the mat as it deviates from the vertical. The last secret of the cyclone

Cyclonda had the opportunity to surprise mathematicians once again, to order in the analysis of infinitesimals. The need to move from considering individual problems to constructing a general method was first recognized by Isaac Newton (1643-1727), who during the “plague holidays” in Lincolnsper (1665-1667) developed his “fluoxy” method. However, he was in no hurry to publish it, concentrating on obtaining a variety of results with this method. The fireworks of these results sparkle across the country.

From the beginning of the 70s, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, distracted from his numerous government and scientific affairs, with increasing enthusiasm, began to develop the formulaism of differential and integral calculus (under the strong influence of the mentioned work of Pascal). He learned about Newton's work, and since 1676 he has been corresponding with him. Unlike Newton, Leibniz actively attracted attention to his method, both through personal contacts and through the pages of the Acta Eruditorium magazine (remember, Uncle Toby read it), published since 1682.

In 1696, a note “A new problem, which mathematicians are invited to solve” appeared on the pages of the Acta Eruditorium. Here is its formulation: “In the upper plane, two points A and B are given. Determine the path AMB, descending along this path under the influence of its own gravity, body M, starting to move from point A, will reach point B in the shortest time.” I liked the problem; Leibniz called her “so beautiful and completely unknown.” However, Galileo enthusiastically discussed in his Conversations that a heavy point descends along a straight line segment more slowly than along a broken line with the same ends, and quite confidently asserted that a point descends fastest along a quarter of a circle. The curve of steepest descent began to be called brachystrochone. But is it a different circle?

The timeliness of the problem was manifested in the fact that it was solved relatively quickly by both Bernoulli brothers - Jacob (1654-1705) and Johann (1667-1748), and Leibniz, and L'Hopital (1661-1704), and Newoton (he presented an anonymous solution, but according to Johann Bernoulli, “I recognized the lion by his horses”). The most popular was the solution of I. Bernoulli himself. The move he proposed was completely unexpected: he proposed replacing the mechanical problem with an optical one. It is easy to see that if we consider an optical medium in which the speed of light at point M is equal to $\sqrt{2gy}$, where $y$ is the difference in the heights of points A and M, then light from point A to B travels along the brachistochrone (by virtue of Fermat’s principle, light chooses the path that takes the least time). After this, using the law of refraction, on the one hand, and the then well-known property of the tangent to the cyclude (see above), it is easy to deduce that the brachistochrone is an overturned cyclude with the tip at point A!

One of the historians of mechanics wrote about it this way:

“Without any other method, with the help of his geometric imagination alone, Johann Bernoulli solves the problem with one glance, skillfully using what is already known by chance - the picture is a concept of a substitute and amazingly beautiful. We must recognize in Johann Bernoulli a truly artistic nature, working in the field of natural science. His brother, Jacob Bernoulli, was a scientist of a completely different kind. It had a stronger critical spirit, but much less creative imagination. And he solved the problem of the brachistochrone, but it is much more difficult to designate a method for solving a problem of this kind. Thus, we find in each of the brothers only one of those two sides of scientific talent, which in the greatest studies of nature, such as Newton, are combined with extraordinary force.”

The heronic history of the cycle ended with the end of the 17th century. It appeared so mysteriously when solving a variety of problems that no one doubted that it played an absolutely exceptional role. The reverence for the cyclude was maintained for a long time, but time passed, and it became clear that it was not connected with a functional law of nature, like, say, the conchex section. The problems that led to the cycle played a huge role in the development of mechanics and mathematical analysis, but when the magnificent data of these sciences were built, it turned out that these problems were particular, far from the most important. An instructive historical June took place. However, getting acquainted with the amazing history of the cycle, you can see many important facts from the history of science.
