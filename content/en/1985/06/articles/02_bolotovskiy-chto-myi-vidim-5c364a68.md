B. M. BOLOTOVSKY

Introduction

There is a saying: “It is better to see once than to hear once.” The meaning of this saying is completely clear: you can say whatever you want, but in order to be convinced of what is said, you need to see everything with your own eyes. The eyes will not deceive you.

However, can you always trust your eyes? It turns out not always. Sometimes we see something that is not really there. Do not think that we are talking about hallucinations or visual defects. You observe, someone who is in good health and has good eyesight, under certain conditions, can see a very distorted picture of a phenomenon, very far from what will happen in reality. And the reason for this is not that feelings can change a person. Let’s put it together on an observation camera; its “readings” can already be considered objective. And yet, having watched the stained film, we see the same thing as the observer whom we expressed disbelief and whom we replaced with a film camera.

Both what we see on the screen and what the observer saw are not at all what will actually happen.

How can this be?

We will show how this can be with several examples. Otherwise, you won’t find anything mysterious in our examples. They only say that you need to think about every physical observation, try to understand what the instrument readings say, and based on these readings, reconstruct the real picture of the phenomenon.

Photographing light on the fly

About fifteen years ago, the American physicist Michel Duguay designed a photographic shutter that closed in an unusually short time. Why are such gates needed?

If we are photographing a still image, the amount of time the shutter is open—this period is called exposure time—does not affect the sharpness of the image. The subject is motionless, the image of the object on the film is also motionless, so increasing the exposure time does not improve the sharpness of the image. It's a different matter if the subject is moving. Then the movement and image of the object on the film. In this case, the shorter the exposure time, the sharper the image of the object in the photograph will be. And vice versa, the longer the exposure time, the more “smeared” the image of the object will be.

In some photographs (usually these are photographs of people running, quickly tormented by automatic machines), the blur of the image is reduced - it seems to expose rapid movement. But, as a rule, the faster the subject moves, the shorter the exposure time should be, the less time the shutter opens. (In our discussions, we do not take into account that photographic film has a certain magnification and that a shorter exposure time must correspond to a higher magnification. We will assume that the film magnification can be selected so that it corresponds to the desired exposure time.)

Let us now return to the beginning of our story. The howl was created by a photographic shutter with a fantastic exposure time - about $10^{-11}$ s. Of course, this time could be considered “fantastic” only before the creation of the shutter. When it is created, it is no longer a fantasy, but a real, remarkable achievement. But... where to apply it?

This “lightning fast” shutter could be used to photograph objects moving at close to the speed of

But you can’t photograph an electron without representation—it’s too small. We need an object that would have noticeable dimensions and move at the speed of light. And Duguay, the creator of the shutter, found such an object. It turned out to be... the light itself.

The light source was a laser, which produced radiation in the form of very short pulses—with a duration of approximately $10^{-11}$ s. A light pulse emitted by a laser can be represented as a certain volume filled with light waves and moving in space at the speed of light (figure 1). This volume is called a wave (or light) packet. The packet length is very high, $l=c\tau$, where $c$ is the speed of light, and $\tau$ is the emission time, that is, the pulse duration; substituting $c=3 \cdot 10^5$ km/s, $\tau \approx 10^{-11}$ s, we get $l \approx 3$ mm. A “body” of this size can be photographed.

It would seem that everything is simple - we place the camera on the side of the path of the light “body”, point it at some point along the path and at the right moment release the shutter. This setup of the experiment is depicted in figure 1. However, if we do this, we will not see anything on the film. Why does an image appear on film only if light from a moving body hits it? This can be “own” light if the body itself emits (as, for example, happens when photographing a hot light bulb). If the body itself does not glow, it must be illuminated, and then the light reflected or scattered by the body from an external source will fall on the film. From figure 1 it is clear that the light waves that make up the packet propagate along such a path that they cannot possibly get into the camera lens. Illuminating such a packet with an external source is also free - the packet does not reflect or scatter light, light waves from an external source pass through the wave packet as if through a free ghost, without changing or affecting the packet.

The solution was found like this. A glass vessel with water was placed in the path of the light clot. A few drops of milk were added to the water. This additive made the water a little cloudy. A turbid medium scatters the light falling on it. When a light clot enters such a vessel with moona water, its constituent light waves begin to dissipate and the clot becomes visible.

Light "dumbbell"

Before describing the results of photographing the light “dumbbell”, a few words should be said about why such an experiment is needed.

According to the relative theory, the length of a body, measured in some reporting systems, depends on the speed at which the body moves relative to this reporting system. If, in particular, the body is at rest and its length is equal to l₀, then when this body moves with speed v, its length will be l = l₀√(1-v²/c²), where c is the speed of light. The factor √(1-v²/c²) is always less than one, so when a body moves, its dimensions in the direction of movement (this is the size we had in mind when we said “length”) are reduced by 1/√(1-v²/c²) times.* (For speeds achievable in terrestrial conditions at the present time, the set √(1-v²/c²) differs very little from unity. If the body has speed is 10 km/s (this is the order of magnitude of the speed of spacecraft - the package and satellites), then this set is equal to 0.9999999944, that is, it differs from one in the tenth decimal place. Therefore, when reaching low speeds, it is extremely difficult to detect a reduction in size during movement. But this statement of the theory of relative length during movement leads to a change in their shape. The moving ball, if the speed of its movement is close to the speed of light, should solidify and turn into. “pancake”, the plane of which is perpedicular to the direction of velocity; the cube should turn into a parallelepiped, etc.

*) Such a contraction is often called “Lorentzian” - after the Inderland physicist and theorist H. A. Lorentz, who was one of the first (in 1892) to predict this effect.

Since we do not have bodies moving at a sufficiently high speed, it was not possible to directly verify the change in shape. But the theoretical analysis of this phenomenon has been carried out in some detail. And the analysis showed that the matter is not as simple as it seems. Indeed, the dimensions of the ball in the direction of movement are reduced. But one will not be able to notice this contraction when looking at a moving ball. He is impressed by... the very method of observation.

To understand why this happens, let's look at a few examples related to the issues raised. On a summer day we admire some landscape. The sun is shining, the trees are moving, and so are the birds. We perceive it all at the same time.

But at the moment when we hear chirping, the bird may remain silent. Sound travels in air at a speed of more than 300 m/s. Therefore, there is a complete change in lag between the time the bird trills and the time it is “received”. We see the Sun in some places in the sky. But light from the Sun to the Earth “travels” for about eight minutes. This means that we cannot claim that now, “this minute,” the Sun is exactly where we see it.

Another example. We photograph the starry sky. What does the photograph we took say? Can we consider that it reflects the location of the stars at the exact moment when we took the picture? No. Light from distant stars travels to Earth for tens, hundreds and thousands of years. The star may have gone out a long time ago, but we won’t find out about it soon, the light is still coming from it, and we see this star in the photograph. A star was born, but the light from it has not yet reached us, and this star is not in the photograph.

All these discrepancies are caused by the same phenomenon - the finite speed of propagation of the signal that conveys to us information about the state of the object. While the signal is coming to us, the state of the object changes. If we simultaneously photograph several objects located at different distances from the camera, then we simultaneously receive information about the position of these bodies. But the images of all the bodies in this photograph reflect the positions of these bodies at different points in time. These moments in time are “shifted” into the past in relation to the moment of shooting.

Now let's return to the question of why the observed person will not be able to notice the Logentian contraction.

Everything we've said about shooting multiple subjects applies to one subject that has a specific size. If the object is motionless and the exposure time is sufficient for the signal (light) from the most distant point of the object to reach the object, then we can confidently say that the photograph accurately reflects both the position of the object and its dimensions. It's a different matter if the object is moving.

A photograph of a moving body gives us a complex picture; it depicts various points of the body in the positions they occupied at different points in time. Let, say, the body being photographed approaches the camera. Then the points of the body, which are more distant from the lens during shooting, will appear “lagging behind” compared to closer points, since the photograph produces an image of an earlier point in time. Thus, an object in a photograph that was moving appears stretched in the direction of movement. It is clear that such minimal “stretching” will be especially replaced if the speed of the body is comparable to the speed of light.

Calculations showed that minimal stretching will compensate for Logentian contraction. So the observer will not be able to notice a change in the shape of the rapidly moving body, and the ball will still seem like a ball to him.*)
