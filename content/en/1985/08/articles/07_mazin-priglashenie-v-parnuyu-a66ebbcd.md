Invitation to the steam room
(or Physics in the bathhouse)

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
I. I. MAZIN

“I saw marvelous things in the Slavic land on my way. I saw wooden bathhouses, and they would strip them until they were red, and they would undress, and they would nag, and they would get sick with kvass, and they would take the rods from their own hands, and they would achieve that they would come out still alive, and they would be doused with cold water, and they would live like that.”
This is how the bathhouse is described in Russian chronicles of the 11th century. And to this day, bathhouse lovers with zaart “muchayot” are on their own. For what? They say that it can be used with short-term exposure of the skin to high temperatures - hot air. However, in humid air it is difficult to withstand high temperatures even for a short time. So in a good bath it should be not only hot, but also dry. It’s not so easy to build a good bathhouse, and you also need to be able to “cook” steam, that is, in scientific terms, to create an optimal microclimate. Our ancestors knew how to do this back in the 11th century, but they, unfortunately, would not have been able to explain why in the bathhouse it is necessary to do this and not otherwise. But today's schoolchild knows enough to answer the questions asked by a physics lover in an ordinary steam room. This is what I suggest the reader do. Let’s start by looking at the bathhouse “session” from the outside and asking ourselves questions along the way. And then we will try to answer them.

So, off to the bathhouse! And to better navigate, we will use figure from page 19.

Now we are already in the steam room. Well, it’s hot... Well, post a little visa - it’s cooler here. If you’re used to it, you can go upstairs. Hot? It’s okay, today it’s not very hot yet, and it’s also possible that you won’t be able to stand on the hot wooden floor half-barefoot, and you won’t even be able to sit on the bench. And if the bench is nailed together with iron nails, then you should not sit on it even in a not very hot bathhouse: if you touch the head of a nail with your naked body, you will earn a fire.

After the foam we get used to the steam room. The air doesn't seem so hot anymore. However, it feels damp - there are wet spots on the bench and the floor. This can be fixed. You need, as they say, to “give steam”: take boiling water and, in small portions, with a ladle, throw it into the bee on hot stones. Immediately a hot wave smells from the oven to the top, it will become hotter on the shelf, hotter and drier; The wet stains will disappear, the floor and benches will dry.

Question 3. Why, when water is thrown onto the hot stones of the stove, does it become sushi in the steam room?

Question 4. Why do you need to throw water into the heater, and not immediately drink a bunch of water into the heater?

Question 5. Why do you need to throw boiling water and not cold water?

... But more than an hour has passed. There were a lot of people in the steam room, the air was no longer so fresh, there was dampness, there were leaves from brooms on the floor. It's time to “clean” the steam room. It's done like this. Everyone leaves for ten minutes. During this time, you need to sweep the floor and spray it with water from a hose; then open the door to the steam room wide, pour several glasses of cold water onto the floor from outside the door; and then start adding steam. Fresh steam will dislodge the old, stagnant air in the steam room. Now everything is ready, you can go in and start all over again.

Question 6. Why do you need cold water at the entrance to the steam room?

Question 7. Why does fresh steam displace old air?

So, we got acquainted with the rules of the bathhouse. Let's answer the questions that have arisen.

The first question was so simple that any reader probably answered it immediately. So let's start with the second question.

What happens when a person steps on or sits on a hot bench? The human body temperature is no more than 40 °C, while

⟦folio 19⟧

The temperature of the air, and therefore of the benches in a well-steamed room, is from 80 to 120 °C. Upon contact, the process of heat transfer from a hot body (skama) to a cold body (person) begins. How fast is the process? This is determined, in particular, by the thermal conductivity of the hot body. The greater the thermal conductivity of a material, the faster heat is transferred from its more heated areas to less heated ones. Iron, like other metals, has a significantly higher thermal conductivity than wood - about 300 times. When we come into contact with a hot wooden bench, the part that is in close contact cools down, and the heat “taken away” from the small volume of the bench is transferred to us. It’s a different matter if there is a nail in the contact zone: heat will quickly be “pulled” to the points of contact from the entire length of the nail. In addition, the volumetric heat capacity (that is, the heat capacity calculated per unit volume) of iron is approximately 40 times greater than that of wood; so, under the same conditions, we will receive tens of times more heat from a piece of iron than from a piece of wood of the same volume.

Now, I think, you yourself will be able to clearly formulate the answer to question 2. (Note: we did not say anything about the thermal conductivity and heat capacity of the human body. Think for yourself what role these characteristics play in our discussion of the process.)

To answer the following questions, let's remember some

information from molecular physics, in particular about water vapor.

Water, as is known, can be in three states of aggregation: solid - ice, liquid - actually what we call water, and gaseous - steam. As for ice, it has no spicy relation to the bath, but let’s talk about water and steam.

Water can turn into steam - this is evaporation, and steam can turn into water - this is condensation. When water evaporates, heat is absorbed, and when the same mass of steam condenses, the same amount of heat is released. There is always some amount of water vapor in the atmosphere. For example, in a liquid room, 1 m³ of air usually contains about 10 g of steam. The density of water vapor in water is already called absolute humidity.

Let's put a saucer of water in the room. The water will evaporate and turn into steam over time. At the same time, the absolute humidity in the room will increase, but not by much - the volume of the room is usually several ten cubic meters, and if there were 10 g of water in the saucer, then the humidity increased by no more than 1 g/m³. What if the same saucer is placed in a vessel with a volume of 1 liter (10⁻³ m³) and closed? The amount of water in the saucer will decrease until the steam into the vessel becomes saturated. And this will happen when the number of molecules leaving water per unit time and the number of molecules passing from steam to water per unit time become equal. And from this time on, the absolute humidity of the air in the vessel will not change. (Of course, we mean that the temperature of the vessel remains constant all the time.)

Thus, at each temperature there is a maximum absolute humidity equal to the density of saturated water vapor at that temperature. The higher the temperature, the higher the saturated vapor density value.

It is clear that the lower the absolute humidity of the air in relation to the maximum humidity of water possible at a given temperature, the more intense the evaporation of water occurs.
