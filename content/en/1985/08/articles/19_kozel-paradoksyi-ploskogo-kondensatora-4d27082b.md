Paradoxes of a parallel-plate capacitor

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. M. KOZEL

“What paradoxes can there be here,” the reader will say, “after all, this is such a simple electrical system.” And really, what could be the process of a flat capacitor? From a school physics textbook we know that this is the name of a system consisting of two parallel conducting plates located at a short distance, between which there is a dielectric layer.

If the plates (plates) of the capacitor are connected to the bateren strips, then a certain charge $+q$ will appear on one of them, and a charge $-q$ will appear on the other. The electric field of a parallel-plate capacitor contains valuable energy mainly in the space between the plates. This field is homogeneous, that is, the directional vector $\vec{E}$ is the same at all points. The capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor is expressed by the formula

$$C = \frac{q}{\Delta \varphi} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon S}{d},$$

where $\Delta \varphi$ is the potential difference between the capacitor plates, $S$ is the area of ​​each plate, $d$ is the distance between them. $\varepsilon$ is the dielectric constant of the medium, $\varepsilon_0$ is the electrical constant.

It would seem that everything is clear. Meanwhile, many applicants who “met” a flat-plate capacitor during the exam experienced certain difficulties. In a number of cases, they, relying on well-known facts, come to contradictory results, and a paradoxical situation arises. Of course, all these paradoxes can be easily resolved if you deeply and informally understand the laws of electrostatics. Let's give a few specific examples.

Paradox 1. Consider a charged flat air capacitor. The potential difference between its plates

$$\Delta \varphi = \frac{q}{C} = \frac{qd}{\varepsilon_0 S}.$$

Let us note that $\Delta \varphi$ is proportional to the distance $d$ between the capacitor plates. This means that by separating the plates of a charged capacitor, we can increase the potential difference between them. By charging, for example, a capacitor to a potential difference of 10 V and then increasing the gap $d$ by $10^3$, $10^4$, ... times, we get $\Delta \varphi$ equal to, corresponding to, $10^4$ V, $10^5$ V, etc. What a simple and good way to create a charged particle accelerator! Isn't it true?

We clearly encounter here a contradiction with common sense, which tells us that one more step, and we will come to a violation of one of the basic principles of physics - the law of conservation of energy.

And yet, are our reasoning correct or not? Obviously applicants immediately answer that this cannot be, but not all indicate the physical reason for this. But in fact, everything is very simple. The given formula for the capacitance of a flat capacitor is valid only for small values ​​of $d$, that is, when $d$ is significantly less than the linear dimensions of the plates. With large gaps between the plates, the field ceases to be uniform and the potential difference $\Delta \varphi$ ceases to linearly depend on $d$.

We have raised here a very important question about the limits of applicability of physical results. Any physical model always has limited applicability. It is impossible, for example, to apply the ideal gas model to real gases at high pressures and low temperatures. The formula for the capacitance of a flat-plate capacitor stops “working” when the distance between the plates becomes large enough and the electric field between them appears

heterogeneous. You cannot directly apply Coulomb's law to determine the strength of electrical interaction between the plates of a parallel-plate capacitor. Unfortunately, applicants in this case write the formula $F = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 d^2}$, while the correct expression for the force is $F = q \frac{E}{2} = \frac{q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 S}$ (in this case, the force does not depend on the distance $d$).

Now let’s create a problem for an independent solution.

Problem 1. Try to estimate how many times the potential difference between the plates of a charged flat capacitor can be increased if they are removed at a much greater distance from each other. The linear size of the plates $a = \sqrt{S}$ and the initial distance $d$ between them are considered known.

Note that estimation problems of this kind, where exact calculation is difficult, play a large role in physics. They require the selection of a simplified physical model and allow one to estimate the scale of the phenomenon in order of magnitude.

Paradox 2. Let's take a charged and insulated flat capacitor and drill two small branches in its plates - one directing the other. In this case, the field inside the capacitor will practically not change and will remain uniform. Let a charged frequency flow into the capacitor through one of the leads with a small initial speed so that the electric field of the capacitor accelerates it (Fig. 1). (For this, obviously, the frequency must influence the capacitor through a response in the same charged plate.)

Having passed through the capacitor, the part flies out from another branch, acquiring additional energy $\Delta W = q\Delta\varphi$. Now it is proposed to use a magnetic field to change the direction of motion of the particle so that it flies in again

through the first branch into the capacitor. Let us recall that the Lorentz force acting from the magnetic field on a moving charged particle does not do any work, since it is perpedicular to the velocity vector of the particle.

When flying through the capacitor again, the particle again acquires additional energy $\Delta W$, in the next cycle - another $\Delta W$, etc. We “invented” a cyclic accelerator that does not require an energy source. A little more imagination - and we will design a so-called engine that operates without consuming energy!

We arrived at this erroneous result because we did not take into account one of the fundamental properties of the electrostatic field - its potential nature. The potentiality of the electrostatic field means that if we transfer a charged body from one point in space to another, then the work of electric forces does not depend on the shape of the path, but is determined only by the positions of the starting and ending points. It follows that if a charged body is transferred along a closed trajectory, then when returning to the starting point, the total work done by the electric forces goes to zero. (Recall that the gravitational field also has the same property.) It is this property that allows us to introduce the concepts of potential energy and potential.

In our case, when a charged particle moves inside a capacitor, the electric field accelerates it, and outside the capacitor it slows it down, and when the particle approaches the response in the first plate, its energy takes on its previous value. This means that in the example under consideration we fundamentally cannot handle the field outside the flat-plate capacitor. Of course, there is a significant weak field between the plates, but in it the particle flies through the city to a greater distance; As a result, in the decelerating field outside the capacitor, it loses exactly the energy that was imparted to it by the accelerating field inside the capacitor.

The school textbook says that bodies, including conductive ones, located outside the capacitor have practically no effect on its capacitance. Most often this is the case. Now let's connect the outer uncharged plates with a conductor. Our capacitor appears to be placed inside a conducting box. What will happen?

Many applicants argue that in this case, the field between the plates of the capacitor will not change (since the outer plates were not charged) and, therefore, the capacitance of the capacitor will not change either. It turns out that the benefit of everything is the dissection field of the capacitor. Under its action, a redistribution of charges occurs on the outer plates, as a result of which the upper plate “a” turns out to be negatively charged, and the lower one “6” is positively charged. (By the way, it follows from figure 3 that if one of the outer plates is connected by a conductor to one of the plates of a given capacitor, then the emcapacity becomes equal to $2 C_0$.)

Paradox 4. Let us have two identical capacitors (further not necessarily flat) with the value $C$ of each. One of them is charged with charge $q_0$, and the other is not charged. The electrical energy stored in capacitors is equal to

$$i = \frac{1}{R} \left( \frac{q_0 - q}{C} - \frac{q}{C} \right) = \frac{1}{RC} (q_0 - 2q).$$

If we take into account that, by definition, current is equal to a change in charge of one

time (derivative of charge with respect to time):
$$i = \frac{dq}{dt},$$
then we get the equation
$$\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{2}{RC} q = \frac{1}{RC} q_0.$$

It is more convenient to write the equation not for the charge, but for the current, so we differentiate the above-written procedural-procedural correspondence in time:
$$\frac{di}{dt} + \frac{2}{RC} i = 0,$$
or $$\frac{di}{i} = -\frac{2}{RC} dt.$$

The last equation is easy to integrate. In this case, the left side of the required integration ranges from the first initial current value $i_0$ to the current value $i(t)$ at the considered moment in time, and the right side - from 0 to $t$:
$$\ln i(t) - \ln i_0 = -\frac{2}{RC} t,$$
or
$$i(t) = i_0 e^{-2t/(RC)}.$$

The value of the current $i_0$ at the first moment after closing the switch can be determined from the following physical messages: at the first moment the left capacitor is charged to a potential difference $q_0 v/C$, and the right one is not charged. Therefore, the voltage drop across the resistor is exactly $q_0/C$. It follows that $i_0 = q_0/(CR)$.

The graph of the function $i(t)$ is shown in figure 5. Such functions are called exponents. They are very common when solving physical problems. The time $t$ during which the current decreases by $e$ times is called the time constant of the system. In our case $t = CR/2$. The lower the resistance $R$, the faster the current decay will integrate.

Now we just have to calculate the Joule heat:
$$Q = \int_0^\infty i^2(t) R dt = \frac{q_0^2}{C^2 R} \int_0^\infty e^{-4t/(RC)} dt = \frac{q_0^2}{4C} = \frac{1}{2} W_1.$$

So, the paradox is resolved. Regardless of the value of $R$exactly the initial energy of the capacitor is converted into heat, and the second energy is stored in the form of electrical energy of the capacitors after they are recharged. Do not be confused by some of the complexity of the above calculation. Such calculations are not required from you during the exams. It was important for us to show that strict calculation completely resolves the contradiction.

It remains to say a few words about the limiting case $R \rightarrow 0$. What if we connect capacitors with perfect conductors (superconductors)? Formally, our result remains valid, since it does not depend at all on the value of $R$. But where is the “lost” energy released and in what form?

Of course, in this limiting case there is no Joule heat, but the radiation of electromagnetic energy begins to play a significant role. With rapid changes in current in the circuit, an alternating electromagnetic field will arise in the surrounding space, and half of the initial energy will be carried away by the electromagnetic wave.

It should be noted that in this case it is necessary to take into account the small inductance of the connected wires. Then the circuit becomes an oscillatory circuit with small (but not infinitely small!) attenuation. The process of recharging the capacitor becomes oscillatory in nature, and cannot be transformed in any way by the radiation of electromagnetic energy in the surrounding space.

Problem 2. There is an uncharged capacitor $C$ and a battery with emf $\mathcal{E}$. Let's connect the capacitor plates to the poles of the battery. Then a charge $q = C\mathcal{E}$ will appear on the plates, and the capacitor will acquire electrical energy $W = C\mathcal{E}^2/2$. In this case, the battery will perform work $A = q\mathcal{E} = C\mathcal{E}^2$. We see that $W = 1/2A$. Where did the second irrigation work, a perfect battery, go?

Paradox 5. A flat air capacitor of capacity $C$ was filled with a dielectric liquid with food $\varepsilon$ and charged to voltage $U$. At the same time, the capacitor acquired electrical energy $W_1 = \varepsilon CU^2/2$. Then the capacitor was disconnected from the source, the liquid was drained and then discharged. It is easy to report that during the discharge, energy $W_2 = \varepsilon^2 CU/2 = \varepsilon W_1$ is released (the charge of the capacitor $q = \varepsilon CU$ did not change when the liquid was drained, and the capacity became equal to $C$). Although the problem of discharging a capacitor from which the dielectric has been removed is well known in various versions, many applicants have experienced service difficulties when trying to resolve this paradox. And the explanation here is very simple.

When removing a dielectric from a charged capacitor, some external positive work must be done, since the dielectric is always drawn into the region of a stronger electric field (between the plates). Due to this positive work of the external force, the electrical energy of the capacitor will increase:

$$W_2 - W_1 = A_{\text{внеш}}.$$

In our case, the liquid dielectric flows out of the capacitor under the influence of gravity, so the energy of the capacitor will increase by the amount of work done by gravity. If the capacitor remained connected to the source all the time, then it would also be necessary to take into account the operation of the battery:

$$W_2 - W_1 = A_{\text{внеш}} + A_{\text{бат}}.$$

This corresponds to the law of conservation of energy for an isolated capacitor (and in general for any isolated electrostatic system) in the presence of external forces doing work.
