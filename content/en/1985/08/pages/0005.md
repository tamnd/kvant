Another important basis of linear optics is the principle of constant frequency of light when passing through various media. It was discovered by Newton during his substitutional optical experiments. In 1671, on their basis, Newton formulated the following proposition: “The type of color characteristic of each individual type of beam is not changed either by pre-fill, or by reflection from natural bodies, or by any other cause that I could observe” (figure 1).

It was fortunate for the development of physics that Newton's experimental capabilities, in terms of the intensity of light sources, were very limited (a sunbeam passing through a crack in a window shutter). If he had a laser in his hands, the observed picture could be greatly complicated, which would delay the development of optics. In connection with Newton’s experiments, S.I. Vavilov emphasized: “The imperfection of experience contributes to the development of science.” This statement sounds paradoxical, but the premature discovery of certain details in an experiment can delay the flow of experimental data and the development of theory for many years.

The limits of application of linear optics - where are they?

Almost all nonlinear effects are observed during the propagation of light in matter. In principle, the theory predicts the possibility of observing them in a vacuum, but so far they have not been discovered there.

In classical optics, when explaining the process of light propagation in a medium, they use the model of atoms as a system in which the electron is, as it were, fixed outside. A passing light wave causes its electric field to force the electron to oscillate. These oscillations, in turn, generate electromagnetic waves in the space surrounding the atom, that is, the atom itself begins to radiate. The resulting secondary wave coincides in frequency with the primary wave (with forced oscillations, the frequency of oscillations coincides with the frequency of the forced force). In this case, the phase difference between these waves remains constant (such waves are called coherent).
