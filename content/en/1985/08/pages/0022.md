information from molecular physics, in particular about water vapor.

Water, as is known, can be in three states of aggregation: solid - ice, liquid - actually what we call water, and gaseous - steam. As for ice, it has no spicy relation to the bath, but let’s talk about water and steam.

Water can turn into steam - this is evaporation, and steam can turn into water - this is condensation. When water evaporates, heat is absorbed, and when the same mass of steam condenses, the same amount of heat is released. There is always some amount of water vapor in the atmosphere. For example, in a liquid room, 1 m³ of air usually contains about 10 g of steam. The density of water vapor in water is already called absolute humidity.

Let's put a saucer of water in the room. The water will evaporate and turn into steam over time. At the same time, the absolute humidity in the room will increase, but not by much - the volume of the room is usually several ten cubic meters, and if there were 10 g of water in the saucer, then the humidity increased by no more than 1 g/m³. What if the same saucer is placed in a vessel with a volume of 1 liter (10⁻³ m³) and closed? The amount of water in the saucer will decrease until the steam into the vessel becomes saturated. And this will happen when the number of molecules leaving water per unit time and the number of molecules passing from steam to water per unit time become equal. And from this time on, the absolute humidity of the air in the vessel will not change. (Of course, we mean that the temperature of the vessel remains constant all the time.)

Thus, at each temperature there is a maximum absolute humidity equal to the density of saturated water vapor at that temperature. The higher the temperature, the higher the saturated vapor density value.

It is clear that the lower the absolute humidity of the air in relation to the maximum humidity of water possible at a given temperature, the more intense the evaporation of water occurs.
