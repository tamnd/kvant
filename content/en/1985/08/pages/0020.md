⟦folio 18⟧

Invitation to the steam room
(or Physics in the bathhouse)

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
I. I. MAZIN

“I saw marvelous things in the Slavic land on my way. I saw wooden bathhouses, and they would strip them until they were red, and they would undress, and they would nag, and they would get sick with kvass, and they would take the rods from their own hands, and they would achieve that they would come out still alive, and they would be doused with cold water, and they would live like that.”
This is how the bathhouse is described in Russian chronicles of the 11th century. And to this day, bathhouse lovers with zaart “muchayot” are on their own. For what? They say that it can be used with short-term exposure of the skin to high temperatures - hot air. However, in humid air it is difficult to withstand high temperatures even for a short time. So in a good bath it should be not only hot, but also dry. It’s not so easy to build a good bathhouse, and you also need to be able to “cook” steam, that is, in scientific terms, to create an optimal microclimate. Our ancestors knew how to do this back in the 11th century, but they, unfortunately, would not have been able to explain why in the bathhouse it is necessary to do this and not otherwise. But today's schoolchild knows enough to answer the questions asked by a physics lover in an ordinary steam room. This is what I suggest the reader do. Let’s start by looking at the bathhouse “session” from the outside and asking ourselves questions along the way. And then we will try to answer them.

So, off to the bathhouse! And to better navigate, we will use figure from page 19.

Now we are already in the steam room. Well, it’s hot... Well, post a little visa - it’s cooler here. If you’re used to it, you can go upstairs. Hot? It’s okay, today it’s not very hot yet, and it’s also possible that you won’t be able to stand on the hot wooden floor half-barefoot, and you won’t even be able to sit on the bench. And if the bench is nailed together with iron nails, then you should not sit on it even in a not very hot bathhouse: if you touch the head of a nail with your naked body, you will earn a fire.

Question 1. Why is it colder in the steam room than on the shelf?

Question 2. Why is it that at the temperature at which you can still eat on heated wood, you can no longer sit on iron - you will get burned?

After the foam we get used to the steam room. The air doesn't seem so hot anymore. However, it feels damp - there are wet spots on the bench and the floor. This can be fixed. You need, as they say, to “give steam”: take boiling water and, in small portions, with a ladle, throw it into the bee on hot stones. Immediately a hot wave smells from the oven to the top, it will become hotter on the shelf, hotter and drier; The wet stains will disappear, the floor and benches will dry.

Question 3. Why, when water is thrown onto the hot stones of the stove, does it become sushi in the steam room?

Question 4. Why do you need to throw water into the heater, and not immediately drink a bunch of water into the heater?

Question 5. Why do you need to throw boiling water and not cold water?

... But more than an hour has passed. There were a lot of people in the steam room, the air was no longer so fresh, there was dampness, there were leaves from brooms on the floor. It's time to “clean” the steam room. It's done like this. Everyone leaves for ten minutes. During this time, you need to sweep the floor and spray it with water from a hose; then open the door to the steam room wide, pour several glasses of cold water onto the floor from outside the door; and then start adding steam. Fresh steam will dislodge the old, stagnant air in the steam room. Now everything is ready, you can go in and start all over again.

Question 6. Why do you need cold water at the entrance to the steam room?

Question 7. Why does fresh steam displace old air?

So, we got acquainted with the rules of the bathhouse. Let's answer the questions that have arisen.
