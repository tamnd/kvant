Whiteout,
or
Don't believe it
to your eyes

Candidate of Technical Sciences
F. N. SKLOKIN

...Dense fog lies in a thin layer on the surface of the earth and is penetrated through and through by the bright spring Arctic sun. The illumination is so great that without dark glasses it is impossible to look either into the distance or in front of you. The white, dazzlingly bright snow cover of the ocean completely merges at a distance of several meters with the surrounding air space, as if you find a can of milk at the bottom. Objects do not cast shadows, there is almost no contrast in the surrounding landscape...

This is whiteout, one of the unusual natural phenomena that can sometimes be observed in the winter in the mountains and almost always in the spring in the Arctic. In a whiteout, visual sensations very often do not correspond to the actual, real picture of the space around you. And this sometimes leads to rather unpleasant consequences.

The main obstacle on the way of ice divers going to the North Pole are hummocks. The sizes of hummocks range from several tens of centimeters to several meters; you must either go around them or climb through them, overcoming them “head-on”. In the whiteout, the appearance of the hummocks is transformed. Often you can clearly see a dent in the snow ahead of you. But now you want to projectly lie over the dent and... you come across a piece of ice sprinkled with snow. As a result of this unexpected meeting, you lose your balance and fall; and behind his shoulders is a heavy backpack. Already lying down, you see that there is a real hummock in front of you, and no matter how much you look at it now, it will not turn into a dent.

I would like to cite one case from our polar expeditions. This was in early May during a false crossing through the Polyvon. Wrangel's attitude. There was a real whiteout itself. I peered intently at the line laid out by those searching for goods ahead, and suddenly noticed that it had become convex - the trace of the line was ten centimeters higher than the surface of the snow cover. Immediately I turned my gaze to the tips of my skis. No, the skis were pressed into the snow. But as soon as you respond to the lie, the lie immediately becomes convex. It was clear to me that this was just an optical effect, but it was not easy to get rid of this “obsession.”
