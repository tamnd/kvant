⟦folio 18⟧

These optical illusions are not difficult to explain. The size of objects can be judged if there is information about their distance from us and the ability to visually compare their sizes with the sizes of a known element - for example, with a human figure.

If an observer located at point H (figure 2) knows examples of the distance to a person and to the object being assessed and knows the approximate height of a person, he can find the approximate height of the object from comparing these data.

Knowing the realistic dimensions of an object, we can estimate the distance to it based on its apparent dimensions. The surrounding objects and landscape help us with this.

When assessing the distance and size of objects in the Arctic, you observe that there is nothing to compare with. The visual image of the torso is not an eton - after all, the torso can have any size: from centimeters to meters. The surrounding landscape, and in the whiteout there is a virtual absence of such, does not make it possible to estimate the distance. Hence the uncertainty in estimating the apparent size of objects in whiteout conditions.

These are the insidious surprises the Arctic has in store for travelers walking through the whiteout.
