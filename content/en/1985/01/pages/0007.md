⟦folio 5⟧

The number of jewels can also be seen on the dials of other watches with mechanical winding. What kind of stones are these? We are talking about ruby ​​stones used in watch movements as sliding bearings. To make such a bearing, it is necessary to align a small depression with a diameter of only 0.1 mm in ruby ​​(a very hard and at the same time brittle material). For many years this operation was performed mechanically using drills made of specially treated thin wire. The drill made more than 10,000 revolutions per minute and simultaneously made about 100 reciprocating movements. It took approximately 15 minutes to drill one stone.

Since 1964, low-productivity mechanical drilling of holes in watch stones has been widely replaced by laser drilling. Of course, the term “laser drilling” should not be taken literally. The laser beam does not drill a hole - it creates it due to the integrative evaporation of materials. A hole in a ruby ​​stone (with a workpiece thickness of 1 mm) is punched by a series of several laser pulses with an energy of 0.1 J and a duration of the order of $10^{-4}$ s (that is, having a maximum power of the order of $10^3$ W); when focusing a laser pulse into a light spot with a diameter of 0.01 cm, it provides a light intensity of the order of $10^7$ W/cm². The productivity of the laser system, operating in automatic mode, is one stone per second. This is approximately a thousand (!) times higher than the productivity of mechanical drilling.

In many changing instruments, in technical processes that require high precision, very thin wires made of copper, bronze, and tungsten are needed. To obtain such a wire, the metal is pulled through holes of the appropriate diameter, high in a superhard material, such as diamond (so-called diamond dies). Diamond dies allow wiring with a diameter of only 10 microns; This wire is ten times thinner than a person's entire wire. But how do you drill a pillar hole in a super-hard material like diamond? Mechanically this is quite difficult to do; It takes up to ten hours to drill one hole. It is not at all difficult to punch this hole with a series of several focused high-power laser pulses.

As you can see, laser drilling has a number of very important advantages: it allows you to make holes in materials that are characterized by high hardness (for example, diamond) or increased brittleness (for example, alumina ceramics); Very thin holes can be obtained, and at the same time the ratio of the depth of the holes drilled by the laser to their diameter reaches record-breaking values ​​(compared to other drilling methods) - the depth of the hole can exceed its diameter by 10-50 times. In all cases, laser drilling is highly productive. Finally, unlike conventional drills, “laser drills” do not break or wear out.

Development of microelectronic communication organization with improvement of microcircuit manufacturing technology. A laser beam can be of great help here. At first, lasers are used only for microwelding. Thus, with the help of a ruby ​​laser, inputs are tied to contacts on silicon plates, connecting thin wires to thin films. Then they began to use $\text{CO}_2$-lasers (carbon gas lasers) to increase the hole size in ceramic substrates for microcircuits. Today, the laser beam is increasingly used to adjust the parameters of thin-film circuits and to manufacture individual circuit elements. Using a laser, you can make an entire film circuit - with all the resistors, capacitors, and inductors included in it. Let us assume that a thin metal film is sprayed onto the dielectric substrate of the microcircuit. By moving a focused laser spot along the surface of the film, it is possible to evaporate certain areas of the film and thereby create the desired “figure” of the chip.
