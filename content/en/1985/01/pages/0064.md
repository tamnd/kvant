According to the law of conservation of energy, the amount of heat released by the resistor is equal to the difference between the work of the source and the energy of the capacitor:

$$Q = C_0^2 - C_0^2/2 = C_0^2/2.$$

Now consider the case where the right plate of the capacitor was initially charged. Let's denote this charge by $q$. In this case, even before the switch is closed into the capacitor, there is an electric field with a tense $E = q/(2\varepsilon_0 S)$, and this field has a reserve of electrical energy $W = \varepsilon_0 E^2 S/d/2 = q^2/(8C)$ (here $S$ is the area of ​​the capacitor plates, $d$ is the distance between the plates, $C = \varepsilon_0 S/d$ is the capacitance of the capacitor). What happens after the switch is closed, when the potential difference between the plates becomes equal to $\Phi$? Let us note two important circumstances. Firstly, the existing charge $q$ will not change: turning on the battery only leads to its redistribution. Therefore, the law of conservation of charge can be used. Secondly, even with the switch closed, the two plates do not constitute a capacitor in the usual sense of the word, since the total charge is not zero and the electric field is not only between the plates, but also outside. However, in this case, the electric field outside the plates, due to the constancy of the total charge, remains the same. Only the field energy changes in the space between the plates:

$$\Delta W = \frac{C_0^2}{2} - \frac{q^2}{8C}.$$

From equalities (1) and (2) we obtain

$$q_1 = \frac{q}{2} - C_0, \quad q_2 = \frac{q}{2} + C_0, \quad \Delta q = q_2 - q = C_0 - q/2.$$
