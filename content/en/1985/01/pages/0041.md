Now it's time to answer the main question of the article. (Haven't you hammered it yet?) In a few words, we'll tell you how cheese is made, or more precisely, how holes are made in cheese. First, prepare the “dough” for the cheese. Then the resulting mass is compacted under high pressure and filled into special forms. The formed cheese wheels are removed and placed in warm chambers for ripening. During this period, the cheese “ferments”.
Carbon dioxide is formed inside the compressed “dough”, which, accumulating,

is released in the form of bubbles.
The more carbon dioxide, the more the bubbles swell. (Don't forget that at this stage the inside of the future cheese is a solid soft mass.)
Then the cheese hardens, and a picture of the internal “breathing” of fermenting cheese is imprinted inside it in the form of carbon dioxide bubbles. As for the shape of the cavities formed, due to the fulfillment of Pascal’s law, the pressure in the bubbles is equally transmitted in all directions - this is firstly, and secondly, the “dough” at this moment is similar in its elastic properties. Therefore, the bubbles inflate into a strictly spherical shape. A deviation from this bubble will make you aware that in some place there is an internal or, conversely, void in the “test”. The harder the cheese, the less the inner bubble swells, the smaller the size.
Some types of cheese are not subjected to high pressure treatment before ripening (for example, Russian cheese), and in them the release of carbon dioxide during fermentation occurs in the voids already existing in the “dough”, which, as a rule, have an irregular shape - these are the spaces left between the grains of the semi-finished product after sintering the “dough” in the oven. Such cheeses, when cut open, do not reveal a picture of frozen bubbles, but a rather intricate pattern, the harmony of which is revealed only to an experienced cheese maker.
