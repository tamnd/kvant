In another case, a laser beam is used to “burn” the holes (ducts) in the radicular membrane and thereby restore the normal flow of intraocular fluid. Very soon, however, it became clear that “burning” the radicular membrane causes inflammation, which quite quickly eliminates the created ducts. Research by Soviet medicine led by Academician of the USSR AMH M. M. Krasov showed that it is necessary not to lose, but to “punch through” the ducts in the radicular membrane. This requires that the laser pulse be extremely short. In the Soviet laser installation “Yatagan” used for the treatment of glaucoma, using laser pulses lasting only 10⁻⁷s. The whole operation takes 10-15 minutes.

Starting from the second half of the 60s, the laser beam began to be used as a kind of scalpel in surgery. In the USSR, the practical use of lasers in surgery began in 1966 at the A. V. Vishnevsky Institute of Surgery (under the leadership of Academician A. A. Vishnevsky of the USSR AMH). The laser scalpel was used in operations on the internal organs of the chest and abdominal cavity. Currently, laser beams are used to perform skin plastic surgery, operating on the duct, stomach, intestines, kidneys, liver, spleen and other internal organs. They are taking the first steps to use lasers in heart surgery.

Typically a continuous CO₂ laser with a power of several tens of batts is used. The laser radiation enters a system of hinged connecting hollow expanding tubes, through which the light spreads, reflecting from the mirrors. At the exit from the system, the radiation bursts out in the form of a very intense light beam. During the operation, the surgeon holds the exit tube in his hand and can mix it, freely turning it in different directions and thereby sending the laser beam to the desired location.

The laser scalpel is used for both cutting and suturing tissue. Tissue dissection is performed with a focused beam. With a radiation power of 20 W and a focused laser spot diameter of 1 mm, an intensity of about 2.5 kW/cm² is achieved. The radiation penetrates into the tissue to a depth of approximately 50 microns. Consequently, the volumetric radiation power density for heating tissue reaches 500 kW/cm³. For biological tissue this is a huge value. Naturally, rapid heating and evaporation of the tissue occurs - the effective cutting of tissue by a laser beam is evident.

If the radiation intensity is reduced to 25 W/cm², then the tissue will not evaporate, but surface coagulation (thickening) of the tissue fluid will occur. This process is used to sew cut fabric together. Biological welding is carried out due to the coagulation of liquid contained in the dissected walls of the operated organ and specially squeezed into the gap between the connected sections of tissue.
