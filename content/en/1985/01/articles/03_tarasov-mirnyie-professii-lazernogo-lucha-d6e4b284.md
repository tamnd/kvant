The number of jewels can also be seen on the dials of other watches with mechanical winding. What kind of stones are these? We are talking about ruby ​​stones used in watch movements as sliding bearings. To make such a bearing, it is necessary to align a small depression with a diameter of only 0.1 mm in ruby ​​(a very hard and at the same time brittle material). For many years this operation was performed mechanically using drills made of specially treated thin wire. The drill made more than 10,000 revolutions per minute and simultaneously made about 100 reciprocating movements. It took approximately 15 minutes to drill one stone.

Since 1964, low-productivity mechanical drilling of holes in watch stones has been widely replaced by laser drilling. Of course, the term “laser drilling” should not be taken literally. The laser beam does not drill a hole - it creates it due to the integrative evaporation of materials. A hole in a ruby ​​stone (with a workpiece thickness of 1 mm) is punched by a series of several laser pulses with an energy of 0.1 J and a duration of the order of $10^{-4}$ s (that is, having a maximum power of the order of $10^3$ W); when focusing a laser pulse into a light spot with a diameter of 0.01 cm, it provides a light intensity of the order of $10^7$ W/cm². The productivity of the laser system, operating in automatic mode, is one stone per second. This is approximately a thousand (!) times higher than the productivity of mechanical drilling.

In many changing instruments, in technical processes that require high precision, very thin wires made of copper, bronze, and tungsten are needed. To obtain such a wire, the metal is pulled through holes of the appropriate diameter, high in a superhard material, such as diamond (so-called diamond dies). Diamond dies allow wiring with a diameter of only 10 microns; This wire is ten times thinner than a person's entire wire. But how do you drill a pillar hole in a super-hard material like diamond? Mechanically this is quite difficult to do; It takes up to ten hours to drill one hole. It is not at all difficult to punch this hole with a series of several focused high-power laser pulses.

As you can see, laser drilling has a number of very important advantages: it allows you to make holes in materials that are characterized by high hardness (for example, diamond) or increased brittleness (for example, alumina ceramics); Very thin holes can be obtained, and at the same time the ratio of the depth of the holes drilled by the laser to their diameter reaches record-breaking values ​​(compared to other drilling methods) - the depth of the hole can exceed its diameter by 10-50 times. In all cases, laser drilling is highly productive. Finally, unlike conventional drills, “laser drills” do not break or wear out.

Development of microelectronic communication organization with improvement of microcircuit manufacturing technology. A laser beam can be of great help here. At first, lasers are used only for microwelding. Thus, with the help of a ruby ​​laser, inputs are tied to contacts on silicon plates, connecting thin wires to thin films. Then they began to use $\text{CO}_2$-lasers (carbon gas lasers) to increase the hole size in ceramic substrates for microcircuits. Today, the laser beam is increasingly used to adjust the parameters of thin-film circuits and to manufacture individual circuit elements. Using a laser, you can make an entire film circuit - with all the resistors, capacitors, and inductors included in it. Let us assume that a thin metal film is sprayed onto the dielectric substrate of the microcircuit. By moving a focused laser spot along the surface of the film, it is possible to evaporate certain areas of the film and thereby create the desired “figure” of the chip.

Nowadays, laser beams are widely used for cutting materials. They can cut almost any material: fabric, paper, wood, plywood, rubber, plastic, ceramics, asbestos sheets, glass, metal sheets. With laser cutting, you can get neat cuts of complex profiles, with smooth, unfired edges. For cutting, continuously emitting lasers or lasers that generate a sequence of light pulses at a high repetition rate are used. The required radiation power depends on the material being cut and the thickness of the workpiece.

To demonstrate how widely laser cutting is used today, here are two examples. The first example is laser cutting and cutting of fabric in a modern textile factory. The device includes a continuously generating 100 W CO₂ laser, a system for focusing and moving a laser bow, a computer and a system for tensioning and moving tissue. During the cutting process, the laser beam moves along the surface of the fabric at a speed of up to 1 m/s. The diameter of the focused light spot is 0.2 mm. The movements of the laser bow and fabric are controlled by a computer. Such a device allows, for example, cutting out material for 50 suits within an hour. Cutting is carried out not only quickly, but also with very high accuracy. At the same time, it ensures good quality of cut edges.

Another example is the use of laser cutting in the aviation industry and, in particular, in the production of spacecraft. Using lasers, automated cutting of sheets of titanium, steel, and aluminum is carried out. A continuously generating 3 kW CO₂ laser cuts a 5 mm thick titanium sheet at a speed of 3.5 m/min.

Along with laser cutting, laser welding is also widely used today. It is remarkable that the strength of welded joints (the width of the seam is several millimeters) reaches the level of strength of the material being welded. Automatic laser welding of automatic bodies, ship hulls, and welding of gas pipelines is carried out. At the I. A. Likhacheva automobile plant in Moscow, for example, using a 5 kW CO₂ laser, they produce automatic laser welding of cardan shafts automatically. The service life is increased three times.

In another case, a laser beam is used to “burn” the holes (ducts) in the radicular membrane and thereby restore the normal flow of intraocular fluid. Very soon, however, it became clear that “burning” the radicular membrane causes inflammation, which quite quickly eliminates the created ducts. Research by Soviet medicine led by Academician of the USSR AMH M. M. Krasov showed that it is necessary not to lose, but to “punch through” the ducts in the radicular membrane. This requires that the laser pulse be extremely short. In the Soviet laser installation “Yatagan” used for the treatment of glaucoma, using laser pulses lasting only 10⁻⁷s. The whole operation takes 10-15 minutes.

Starting from the second half of the 60s, the laser beam began to be used as a kind of scalpel in surgery. In the USSR, the practical use of lasers in surgery began in 1966 at the A. V. Vishnevsky Institute of Surgery (under the leadership of Academician A. A. Vishnevsky of the USSR AMH). The laser scalpel was used in operations on the internal organs of the chest and abdominal cavity. Currently, laser beams are used to perform skin plastic surgery, operating on the duct, stomach, intestines, kidneys, liver, spleen and other internal organs. They are taking the first steps to use lasers in heart surgery.

Typically a continuous CO₂ laser with a power of several tens of batts is used. The laser radiation enters a system of hinged connecting hollow expanding tubes, through which the light spreads, reflecting from the mirrors. At the exit from the system, the radiation bursts out in the form of a very intense light beam. During the operation, the surgeon holds the exit tube in his hand and can mix it, freely turning it in different directions and thereby sending the laser beam to the desired location.

The laser scalpel is used for both cutting and suturing tissue. Tissue dissection is performed with a focused beam. With a radiation power of 20 W and a focused laser spot diameter of 1 mm, an intensity of about 2.5 kW/cm² is achieved. The radiation penetrates into the tissue to a depth of approximately 50 microns. Consequently, the volumetric radiation power density for heating tissue reaches 500 kW/cm³. For biological tissue this is a huge value. Naturally, rapid heating and evaporation of the tissue occurs - the effective cutting of tissue by a laser beam is evident.

If the radiation intensity is reduced to 25 W/cm², then the tissue will not evaporate, but surface coagulation (thickening) of the tissue fluid will occur. This process is used to sew cut fabric together. Biological welding is carried out due to the coagulation of liquid contained in the dissected walls of the operated organ and specially squeezed into the gap between the connected sections of tissue.
