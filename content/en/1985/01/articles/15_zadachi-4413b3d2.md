To make my words more clear, I will have to forgive your requests and remind you of not the best moments of your life. We've all been given injections at some point. Remember how, before giving an injection, the doctor presses on the syringe and releases shavings of healing liquid from a thin needle? Let’s imagine now that we managed to make small holes all over the surface of the syringe, and insert needles into them - we got something like a hedgehog.

If you now press on the piston of the hedgehog syringe, then all the needles at the same height will produce absolutely identical shavings. This happens because the liquid obeys Pascal's law and is squeezed out of the hole, located at the same height, with the same pressure force. For holes located at different heights, it is necessary to take into account the force of hydrostatic pressure.

To compare the elastic properties of a liquid and a solid, we will give another example. Let's imagine that we lowered a spring into one narrow beaker (such that the diameter of its coils coincides with the inner diameter of the beaker), and poured water into the other. Let us now imagine that the walls of the vessels were worn out. How will the spring and water behave? The spring remains in place as if nothing happened. The water will scatter in all directions like a burst bubble. Why do you think? The reason for the different behavior is due to the different ways in which pressure is transmitted by solids and liquids. The spring transmits practical pressure only along its length. In water, pressure is transmitted equally in all directions - up, down, and sideways - in accordance with Pascal's law. By the way, it was said that Pascal himself observed a similar picture when he established his law. Now it's time to answer the main question of the article. (Haven't you hammered it yet?) In a few words, we'll tell you how cheese is made, or more precisely, how holes are made in cheese. First, prepare the “dough” for the cheese. Then the resulting mass is compacted under high pressure and filled into special forms. The formed cheese wheels are removed and placed in warm chambers for ripening. During this period, the cheese “ferments”.
Carbon dioxide is formed inside the compressed “dough”, which, accumulating,

is released in the form of bubbles.
The more carbon dioxide, the more the bubbles swell. (Don't forget that at this stage the inside of the future cheese is a solid soft mass.)
Then the cheese hardens, and a picture of the internal “breathing” of fermenting cheese is imprinted inside it in the form of carbon dioxide bubbles. As for the shape of the cavities formed, due to the fulfillment of Pascal’s law, the pressure in the bubbles is equally transmitted in all directions - this is firstly, and secondly, the “dough” at this moment is similar in its elastic properties. Therefore, the bubbles inflate into a strictly spherical shape. A deviation from this bubble will make you aware that in some place there is an internal or, conversely, void in the “test”. The harder the cheese, the less the inner bubble swells, the smaller the size.
Some types of cheese are not subjected to high pressure treatment before ripening (for example, Russian cheese), and in them the release of carbon dioxide during fermentation occurs in the voids already existing in the “dough”, which, as a rule, have an irregular shape - these are the spaces left between the grains of the semi-finished product after sintering the “dough” in the oven. Such cheeses, when cut open, do not reveal a picture of frozen bubbles, but a rather intricate pattern, the harmony of which is revealed only to an experienced cheese maker.
