F949. Are the readings of thermometers the same, one of which is placed at the surface of a boiling liquid, and the other in its depth?

In order for a bubble filled with saturated steam to form in a liquid during boiling and not collapse, the pressure inside the bubble must be equal to the external pressure, that is, the pressure of the liquid surrounding the bubble. If the bubble is located deep in the liquid and the height of so much liquid above it is $h$, then the external pressure is

$$p = p_0 + \rho gh,$$

where $p_0$ is the pressure above the surface of the liquid. Therefore, the deeper the bubble is, the greater the pressure $p_H$ of saturated vapor in the bubble must be so that it does not collapse. But a higher pressure value $p_H$ corresponds to a higher temperature. This means that the existence of bubbles deep in a boiling liquid is possible at a higher temperature than above the surface.

Let us estimate how different the temperature $T_h$ at a depth of $h = 0,5$ m is from the temperature $T_0$ on the surface of boiling water. The additional pressure at this depth is $\Delta p = \rho gh \approx 5 \cdot 10^3$ Pa. Assuming that the pressure above the surface of the water is equal to normal atmospheric pressure, that is, $p_0 = 1,033 \cdot 10^5$ Pa, from the table of the dependence of saturated vapor pressure on temperature, we find how much higher the temperature of water vapor must be $100^\circ\text{C}$ for its pressure to be greater than $p_0$ by $5 \cdot 10^3$ Pa: $\Delta T \approx 1^\circ\text{C}$.

T. S. Petrova

F951. Determine the capacitance of the capacitor system shown in figure. Each capacitor is indicated with the value of its capacitance, measured in microfarads.

◆ It is impossible to imagine the original circuit as a system of parallel and series connected capacitors. In such cases, it is necessary, so to speak, to simplify the scheme. For example, what is meant by this.

Typically, based on messages of geometric or physical symmetry, some nodes of the circuit are disconnected or, conversely, connected, some capacitors are removed, etc., adding that the new circuit of series and parallel connected capacitors is equivalent to the original one, that is, so that when the same voltages are applied to the original and converted circuits, the circuits are charged equally - in this case, the capacitance of the circuits is the same, and the capacitance of the converted circuit is calculated.

The application of the method described above is based on the uniqueness theorem, the essence of which in our case coincides with the following. If a certain voltage is applied to the circuit, then the voltages (and, accordingly, charges) on all capacitors will take on the only possible values. Let us give a not very rigorous proof of this theorem by contradiction.

Let us assume that there are two possibilities for charges to “dissipate” on the capacitors of the circuit when a voltage $U_0$ is applied to it. This means that at least on one of the capacitors, for example, with capacitance $C_0$, the charges in the first and second cases will be different. Let's apply voltage sources $U_0$ and - $U_0$ connected to the circuit directly to the circuit (that is, the total voltage applied to the circuit is zero). Due to the principle, the circuit will independently “adjust” to voltages $U_0$ and - $U_0$; in the first case, a charge $q_0$ appears on the capacitor $C_0$, and in response to the voltage - $U_0$ the capacitor is charged with a charge $q'_0$ with the opposite polarity, and $|q_0 + q'_0| \neq 0$. Thus, zero voltage was applied to the circuit, the work of external sources was equal to zero, and the capacitor $C_0$ had a reserve of electrical energy $\frac{(q_0 + q'_0)^2}{2} \neq 0$. Contradiction.

F952. The observer examines a distant object and a photograph of this object taken from the point where the observer is located. What seems bigger to him - the object or its photographic image? How many times? The photograph was taken by Annaparat, whose objective lens has a focal length of $F=3$ cm; When printing the photo, a veillance $k=2,5$ was made.

The size of the image of an object on the retina is directly proportional to $tg\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the visual angle for a given object. Therefore, the apparent magnification when viewing an object in a photograph is equal to

$$\Gamma=\frac{tg\varphi}{tg\varphi},$$

where $\varphi_1$ is the visual angle at which the object is visible in the photograph, $\varphi$ is the visual angle when directly viewing the object. If $l$ is the size of the object in the picture, and $D$ is the distance from which the picture is viewed, then $tg\varphi_1=l/D$. Let's find $l$.

When photographing distant objects, a sharp image is obtained in the focal plane. Therefore, the distance from the lens to the film in this case should be equal to the focal length $F$. The image size on film is $l_1=F tg\varphi$. After printing, the image size in the photo becomes $l=kl_1=kF tg\varphi$.

Thus,

$$\Gamma=\frac{kF}{D}.$$

With normal vision, $D$ is no less than 25 cm. Substituting $D=25$ cm for the estimate, we get $\Gamma=0,3$ - when looking at a photograph of an object, each is approximately 3 times smaller than with direct observation.
