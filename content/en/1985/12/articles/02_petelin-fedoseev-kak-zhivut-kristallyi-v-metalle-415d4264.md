How do crystals live in metal?
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. L. PETELIN,
A. I. FEDOSEV

When it comes to crystals, we usually imagine beautiful polygons sparkling in the rays of light. However, crystalline materials found in nature most often do not have columnar forms. As a rule, these are polycrystals - substances consisting of many densely packed tiny crystals, differently oriented in space.

Metals usually have this structure. It is formed already during the process of crystallization, when the nuclei of crystals appear at various points in the liquid metal, the grains of the crystals grow, meet and press against each other. Gradually the molten metal turns into polycrystalline.

Many important properties of a metal depend not only on the stability of individual grains, but also on how they are packed in a polycrystal and what are the boundaries between grains. Physical processes that occur at these boundaries sometimes have a certain effect on the properties of the metal.

Order and disorder on the border

The geometric approach to the problem of contact between two crystal lattices, which began in the work of the English crystallographers M. Kronberg and F. Wilson in 1947, helped to bring relative clarity to the question of why the properties of GBs depend so strongly on the angle of rotation of grains. Let's take a closer look at this approach.

It will still be enough for us to consider flat square crystal lattices.

We will rotate two square grids relative to each other around an axis perpendicular to the plane of the grids, gradually increasing the angle of rotation. At certain, quite certain, angles of rotation along the line of contact of the grids, nodes will appear that are common to both grids - coinciding nodes (at figure 4 they are placed in red). The geometric model is the first step towards constructing a general theory of grain boundaries. It gives an idea of ​​their structure only with misorientations corresponding to special angles; the remaining boundaries remain not covered by this distance. Now, based on the DCS model, physical models have been developed that take into account distortions of crystal lattices at orientations that differ by several degrees from special ones. However, how GBs with rotation angles are arranged, far from special ones, remains unclear.

Structure and texture

Until now we have been talking about individual grains and how order emerges from chaos at the boundaries between grains. There are a lot of grains in the metal, and their location and shape, as a rule, are not completely random. Almost always there is some preferential orientation of crystal grains. In such cases, the material is said to have texture (from the Latin word textura - fabric, ordered structure).

The reasons for the appearance of textures and the textures themselves are different. On page 2 of the cover of this issue are two photographs of pronounced textures. However, they arose in the metal after significant deformations: a metal ingot was “rolled” between two solid cylinders-rolls so that a thin sheet was obtained (this method of processing metals by pressure is called rolling). As a result, the grains stretched along the rolling direction and solidified in the direction perpendicular to the plane of the sheet. This is an example of a warp texture.

The ingots themselves also have a definition of texture (top left photo on the 2nd cover page). It arises during the solidification of molten metal and is called the growth texture, crystallization texture. The process of crystallization of liquid metal located in a special bath, as described, begins in the coldest areas - near the walls. Initially, many small crystals are formed, randomly oriented. Growing in all directions, they seem to “bump into” their growing neighbors, after which their further free growth begins.
