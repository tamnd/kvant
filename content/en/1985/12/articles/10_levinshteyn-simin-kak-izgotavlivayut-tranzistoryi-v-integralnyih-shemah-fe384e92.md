IV. How to make transistors in integrated circuits

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences M. E. LEVINSHTEIN, Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences G. S. SIMIN

In previous issues of the magazine, we talked about the principles of operation of transistors. Today he talks about how modern transistors are made.

There is so much promise in the technological production of useful and bipolar transistors that it is enough to consider it using the example of only a bipolar transistor, the devices of which are somewhat more complicated.

figure 1 shows the design of a bipolar transistor, an integrated circuit (IC) element used in a computer. This design is so different from the schematic representation of the transistor shown in figure 2, which was discussed in the previous article, that at first glance it is difficult to believe that this is the same device. However, if you look carefully, it is not difficult to see on figure 1 the same $n—p—n$-areas as on figure 2.

The design shown in figure 1 is called planar, that is, flat (from the English word plane - flat): all operations for the manufacture of such a transistor are performed on one side of a silicon (or gallium arsenide) flat plate. Planar technology in the manufacture of integrated circuits is the only one possible. First, since IC manufacturing involves placing tens or hundreds of thousands of transistors several microns in size on a single wafer, it would be impossible to accurately align the individual elements of each transistor on both sides of the wafer. Secondly, planar technology allows the use of only such semiconductor wafers (one hundred or more microns thick). Such plates have sufficient mechanical strength and are convenient to use. With double-sided technology, there would be plates a fraction of a micron thick. It is practically impossible to work with plates of such thickness.

There are quite a few ways to make ICs. However, of the widespread technologies by which BTs were created, it is shown in a simplified form in figure 3.

1. The surface of the flint wafer is described. A layer of silicon dioxide SiO$_2$ is formed on it. Then a thin layer of light-sensitive polymer - phoresist - is applied on top. A phoresist has the ability to change its chemical properties under the influence of light.

A glass plate with figure applied on it, composed of arbitrary and opaque sections - phoresillon, is pressed against a semiconductor wafer coated with phoresist. After this, the phoresist is illuminated through the phoresillon, and those areas of the phoresist that are illuminated by light acquire the ability to dissolve in certain traps. This operation is called phoretography. It is one of the most important in the manufacturing process of all ICs.

When the wafer with the marked phoresist is immersed in the trap, the areas of the phoresist that were exposed to light, as well as the areas of silicon dioxide underneath, are dissolved. On the semiconductor wafer in these places, areas of the clean silicon surface - “windows” - are opened. Then, in another trap, which dissolves the unexposed phoresist, but does not dissolve the SiO$_2$, the remaining phoresist is removed. As a result, the surface of the wafer is a combination of “windows” with areas protected by a layer of durable, high-temperature resistant silicon dioxide - a “mask”.
