Interference of light

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
E. I. BUTIKOV

The wave properties of light reveal themselves most clearly in the phenomena of interference and diffraction. These phenomena are characteristic of waves of any nature. They are relatively easy to observe experimentally, for example, for sound waves or waves on the surface of water, but to observe the interference or diffraction of light, certain conditions must be met. They, in particular, will be discussed in this country.

The interference of light refers to the process of observing several light beams, in which the resulting light intensity*) at each point is not equal to the sum of the intensities of the individual beams. In some places the intensity turns out to be greater than the total intensity, in others it is less, that is, they form alternating light and dark areas - an interference pattern occurs.

The simplest example of interference is provided by the observation of two monochromatic waves of the same frequency and with the same direction of oscillation in the addition of waves. As a result, oscillations occur at each point with the same frequency and amplitude that is constant over time, depending, however, on the position of this point.

Let the amplitudes of the folding oscillations be the same, then for the effective oscillation $E(t)$ we obtain

$$E(t) = E_1(t) + E_2(t) = a \cos(\omega t + \varphi_1) + a \cos(\omega t + \varphi_2) = 2a \cos \frac{\varphi_1 - \varphi_2}{2} \cos \left( \omega t + \frac{\varphi_1 + \varphi_2}{2} \right),$$

that is, the amplitude $A = 2a \left| \cos \frac{\varphi_1 - \varphi_2}{2} \right|$ depends on the phase difference $\varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ of the resulting oscillations and takes values ​​from the double amplitude $2a$ of each wave (at points where the oscillations are in phase) to zero (at points where the oscillations occur in antiphase). The energy of vibrations, and therefore the intensity of light $I$, is proportional to the square of their amplitude: $I \sim A^2$. As can be seen from formula (1), the resulting intensity $I$, depending on the position of the observation point, takes values ​​from zero to quadruple the intensity of an individual wave $I_0 \sim a^2$:

$$I = 4I_0 \cos^2 \frac{\varphi_1 - \varphi_2}{2} = 2I_0 (1 + \cos (\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)).$$

Sets of points in space that correspond to the same intensity (in part, maximum or minimum), form surfaces, depending on the properties and location of light sources.

Question 1. Two identical exact sources are located at some distance from each other. What form does the maximum and minimum intensity have in this case?

The phase difference $\varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ at a given point depends on the path difference $l$ of waves from in-phase sources: $l = l_1 - l_2$. If the path difference is equal to an integer number of wavelengths: $l = k\lambda$ ($k = 0, 1, 2, ...$), then the waves arrive at the observation point in phase and, upon addition, oscillations with maximum amplitude. If the path difference is equal to an odd number of half-waves: $l = (2k + 1)\lambda/2$, then the waves arrive in antiphase and “cancel” each other. Thus, the differences between maxima and minima are determined by conditions $l_1 - l_2 = const$.

Problem 1. Find the distance between adjacent interference maxima on screen B, at a distance of L=1 m from the sources S₁ and S₂, if they are located at a distance of d=0.5 mm from each other (Fig. 2). Wavelength of light λ=5·10⁻⁷ m. According to what law does the illumination of the screen change when moving from maximum to minimum of the interference pattern?

Let us draw a straight line SP from the middle of the segment S₁S₂ to the observation point P and denote by θ the angle formed by this straight line with the direction SO to the center of the interference pattern. For small θ, the path difference l=l₁−l₂ can be found by lowering the redistributor from S₂ onto the straight line SP until it intersects with S₁P:l=d·θ. Setting the field here l equal to an integer wavelength kλ, we find the direction θ_k to the maximum of the kth order:

θ_k=kλ/d, k=0, ±1, ±2, … (3)

The angular distance Δθ=θ_k+1−θ_k between adjacent maximums is equal to λ/d, and the distance h between them on screen B is obtained by multiplying Δθ by L:

h=LΔθ=λL/π=10⁻³ m=1 mm.

From here it is clear that the distance L, and therefore the interference fringes, is wider, the closer the sources are located to each other.

To find the law of intensity changes along straight line OB, it is enough to express in formula (2) the phase difference of the folding oscillations φ₁−φ₂ through the position of the observation point P on the screen:

φ₁−φ₂=2π/λ(l₁−l₂)=θπl/λ=2π/λdθ.

Thus, it turns out that the screen illumination depends on the direction θ to the observation point according to a sinusoidal law

I(θ)=2I₀(1+cos(2πd/λθ)).

Until now, it was assumed that sources $S_1$ and $S_2$ create monochromatic waves of the same frequency. But for conventional (non-laser) sources, light is not strictly monochromatic. The wave emitted by an individual atom is not an infinite sine wave, but only a segment of it, or, as they say, a train of sine waves. The duration t of such a train does not exceed $10^{-8}$ s. The maximum length of a train $ct$ is about 3 m ($c=3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s is the speed of light at which trains move in space), and it contains several million “ridges” and “valleys” ($ct/\lambda \approx 10^7$ at $\lambda=5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m).

Even if we consider all the trains emitted by different atoms of the source to be identical and characterize them with the same wavelength $\lambda$, the phase correspondence between them is completely random, since acts of spontaneous emission of light by atoms will occur at random moments of time. Thus, the light of real sources can be considered as a chaotic sequence of sinusidal wave trains. When beams of light from different sources are superimposed, the phase correspondence between light oscillations at any point during the observation time will change randomly many times. As a result of the addition of a large number of oscillations with random phases, the energy of the resulting oscillation at each point will be equal to the sum of the energies of the added oscillations, that is, the redistribution of energy in space characteristic of the interface will not occur.

From here it is clear that special conditions are needed to observe the interface of light: light from the same source must be divided into two beams (or several beams) and then these beams must be brought together in some way. If the path difference from the separation point to the observation point does not exceed the length of an individual train, then random changes in the amplitude and phase of light oscillations in the two beams will occur in accordance. Such bundles are said to be coefferents. When the beam coefferences are superimposed, a stationary interface pattern is formed.

You can get an idea of ​​the characteristics of the change in phase of a wave upon reflection using the following mechanical analogue. If you send an impulse along a thick rope to the junction, then the impulses reflected back and passed into the thin rope will have the same polarity as the impulses sent.
