If the table $N \times N$ is colored in $N$ colors so that the cells of each color are $N$ and $N > (n-1)^2$, then there is a row or column in which there are $n$ cells of different colors (in our problem there were $N=10$, $n=4$).

We invite readers to prove that this estimate is accurate and to think about possible generalizations of this problem (for example, for rectangular tables, to a three-dimensional plane).

N. N. Konstantinov, L. D. Kurlyandchik

M940*. a) The square is divided into rectangles. Let us call “integer” such a set of these rectangles that their projections onto one side of the square completely cover this side without overlapping. Prove that any two rectangles belong to a certain chain.

b) Prove a similar statement for a cube divided into rectangular parallelends (in the definition of a chain, the side of the square must be replaced by an edge of the cube). b) Is it true that any two parallelends in the division of a cube belong to one “slice” - a set of parallelends whose projections onto a certain boundary fill it cohesively, without overlapping each other?

Fig. 1.

We will call the rectangles (in paragraph 6) - parallelpeds) into which the square (cube) is divided, "kirits", and the square itself (respectively, the cube) - "house". It is convenient to assume that the “house” is an arbitrary rectangle (primogol parallel).

Both statements a) and 6) can be proved in approximately the same way - by induction on the number of $n$ kiriches that make up the house. We will detail proof a) and then indicate the additional messages needed in point 6).

For $n \leq 2$ Kirichs the statement is obvious. Let us assume that for the number of kirichs less than $n$ it has been proven. Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be two of the $n$ kiriches that make up the house, which we want to connect with a chain. We will say that the chain goes “along the side $a$” if the projection of the components of the chain onto $a$ covers $a$ in one layer. Note that it is enough to construct part of the chain between $\alpha$ and $\beta$, running along $a$ (indicate the Kirichi whose projections on $a$ fill in one layer the segment between the projections of the Kirichi $\alpha$ and $\beta$), - after this it is easy to complete it to a chain: for this it is enough to find from $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in the direction of $a$ to the sides perpendicular to $a$ and included in the chain met at this kirichia (Fig. 1).

Listen, when kirichias $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can be intersected by one straight line parallel to the side of the house, it is obvious: all kirichias intersected by such a straight line form the desired chain. Therefore, we can assume that for each side of the house there is a straight line separating the kirichias $\alpha$ and $\beta$. In this case, there is a kirichia vertex $\alpha$ that is closest to $\beta$; we denote it $A$. Among the direct estimates of kirichi there is, in addition to $\alpha$, at least one more kirichi $\gamma$, for which $A$ serves as a vertex and which is adjacent to $\alpha$ on the side. It is separated from $\alpha$ by some straight line $l$ (going along the sides $\alpha$ and $\gamma$, Fig. 2).

By cutting the house together with the kirichi of this straight line $l$, we obtain a house containing kirichi $\gamma$ and $\beta$, but smaller than the original (in terms of the number of kirichi) house. In it, by the induction hypothesis, it is possible to connect $\gamma$ and $\beta$ by a chain; we are interested in the part $\gamma$, $\delta_1$, $\delta_2$, ..., $\delta$, $\beta$ from $\gamma$ to $\beta$.
