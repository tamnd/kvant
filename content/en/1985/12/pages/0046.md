Annular bands of equal thickness observed in the air gap between the convex surface of a lens of slight curvature in contact with the flat surface of the glass are called Newgon rings. In reflected light, their central spot, located in the area of ​​the point of contact, is dark. This is explained by the fact that with the thickness of the air traces,

much less than the wavelength, the phase difference of the interfering waves is due to the difference in reflection conditions: the phase of the wave reflecting from an optically denser medium changes to the opposite, and the phase reflecting from an optically less dense medium remains unchanged.

You can get an idea of ​​the characteristics of the change in phase of a wave upon reflection using the following mechanical analogue. Tie the end of a thin rope to a thick one and with a sharp movement of your hand up and down, send a short transverse impulse along the thin rope (Fig. 6). You will see that the pulse reflected from the junction of the ropes has the opposite polarity, while the pulse transmitted into the thick rope (analogous to a light wave penetrating into an optically denser medium) has the same polarity as the falling pulse. If you send an impulse along a thick rope to the junction, then the impulses reflected back and passed into the thin rope will have the same polarity as the impulses sent.

Question 4. Is it possible to observe Newgon's well in the transmission of light?

In principle, an interference pattern will arise in this case as well. In the transmission of light, the distribution of intensity, in accordance with the law of conservation of energy, will be complementary to the reflection of light: the center of the picture will be light, and then there will be alternating dark and light ring stripes.
