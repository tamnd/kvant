F949. Are the readings of thermometers the same, one of which is placed at the surface of a boiling liquid, and the other in its depth?

In order for a bubble filled with saturated steam to form in a liquid during boiling and not collapse, the pressure inside the bubble must be equal to the external pressure, that is, the pressure of the liquid surrounding the bubble. If the bubble is located deep in the liquid and the height of so much liquid above it is $h$, then the external pressure is

$$p = p_0 + \rho gh,$$

where $p_0$ is the pressure above the surface of the liquid. Therefore, the deeper the bubble is, the greater the pressure $p_H$ of saturated vapor in the bubble must be so that it does not collapse. But a higher pressure value $p_H$ corresponds to a higher temperature. This means that the existence of bubbles deep in a boiling liquid is possible at a higher temperature than above the surface.

Let us estimate how different the temperature $T_h$ at a depth of $h = 0,5$ m is from the temperature $T_0$ on the surface of boiling water. The additional pressure at this depth is $\Delta p = \rho gh \approx 5 \cdot 10^3$ Pa. Assuming that the pressure above the surface of the water is equal to normal atmospheric pressure, that is, $p_0 = 1,033 \cdot 10^5$ Pa, from the table of the dependence of saturated vapor pressure on temperature, we find how much higher the temperature of water vapor must be $100^\circ\text{C}$ for its pressure to be greater than $p_0$ by $5 \cdot 10^3$ Pa: $\Delta T \approx 1^\circ\text{C}$.

F950. In a sealed capillary tube there are two so many ruti, separated by a drop of an aqueous electrolyte solution $\text{HgI}_2$. The inner diameter of the tube is $d = 0,3$ mm. The tube is connected in series with a resistor with a resistance of $R = 390$ kOhm to a battery with an emf of $\mathcal{E} = 10$ V (see Fig.ok). How long will it take for the droplet to move one scale division?

◆ The flow of electric current in the electroplete is associated with mass transfer. In this case, at the cathode there is a reduction of metallic ru from the electrolyte solution, and at the end there is oxidation of ru, that is, its transition into the electrolyte solution. In accordance with Faraday's law of electrolysis, the mass of ruth that will be released at the cathode in time $t$ is equal to

$$m = \frac{1}{F} \frac{M}{n} I t,$$

where $F = 9,65 \cdot 10^4$ C/mol is the Faraday number, $M = 0,201$ kg/mol is the molar mass of ruti, $n = 2$ is the valency of ruti, $I$ is the flowing current. This current is practically determined by the resistance $R$, since in comparison with it the resistance of the metallic ruthe and electrolyte and the internal resistance of the source are negligible. Therefore $I = \mathcal{E}/R$, and

$$m = \frac{M \mathcal{E}}{FnR} t. \tag{1}$$

The release of ruthium at the cathode and its dissolution at ande leads to the mixing of an electrolyte droplet towards ande at dissolution $l$ such that

$$m = \rho \frac{\pi d^2}{4} l, \tag{2}$$

where $\rho = 13,6 \cdot 10^3$ kg/m$^3$ is the density of ruti.
