From symmetry it is clear that in space the set of points satisfying the condition l₁−l₂=const corresponds to the surface of a vertical hyperbola around a straight line passing through the sources - the so-called hyperbolon of a vertical hyperbola. The shape of interference fringes on any screen is given by the lines of intersection of such hyperbolons with the plane of the screen.

For example, on screen A, perpendicular to the straight line passing through the sources S₁ and S₂, the interference fringes have the form of concentric circles (see Fig. 1). On screen B, the bars are shaped like hyperbolics. When the distances from the sources to screen B are much greater than the distance between the sources, in a small vicinity of point O the interference fringes can practically be considered parallel straight lines, spaced at equal distances from each other.

Problem 1. Find the distance between adjacent interference maxima on screen B, at a distance of L=1 m from the sources S₁ and S₂, if they are located at a distance of d=0.5 mm from each other (Fig. 2). Wavelength of light λ=5·10⁻⁷ m. According to what law does the illumination of the screen change when moving from maximum to minimum of the interference pattern?

Let us draw a straight line SP from the middle of the segment S₁S₂ to the observation point P and denote by θ the angle formed by this straight line with the direction SO to the center of the interference pattern. For small θ, the path difference l=l₁−l₂ can be found by lowering the redistributor from S₂ onto the straight line SP until it intersects with S₁P:l=d·θ. Setting the field here l equal to an integer wavelength kλ, we find the direction θ_k to the maximum of the kth order:

θ_k=kλ/d, k=0, ±1, ±2, … (3)

The angular distance Δθ=θ_k+1−θ_k between adjacent maximums is equal to λ/d, and the distance h between them on screen B is obtained by multiplying Δθ by L:

h=LΔθ=λL/π=10⁻³ m=1 mm.

From here it is clear that the distance L, and therefore the interference fringes, is wider, the closer the sources are located to each other.

To find the law of intensity changes along straight line OB, it is enough to express in formula (2) the phase difference of the folding oscillations φ₁−φ₂ through the position of the observation point P on the screen:

φ₁−φ₂=2π/λ(l₁−l₂)=θπl/λ=2π/λdθ.

Thus, it turns out that the screen illumination depends on the direction θ to the observation point according to a sinusoidal law

I(θ)=2I₀(1+cos(2πd/λθ)).

The graph of this function is shown on the right side of figure 2.

The weakening of light vibrations in some places of the interference pattern and the suppression in others is not associated with any transformations of vibration energy. This is clearly seen from formula (4), according to which the average intensity value in all directions is equal to 2I₀, that is, the total intensity from two sources.
