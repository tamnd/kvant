Until now, it was assumed that sources $S_1$ and $S_2$ create monochromatic waves of the same frequency. But for conventional (non-laser) sources, light is not strictly monochromatic. The wave emitted by an individual atom is not an infinite sine wave, but only a segment of it, or, as they say, a train of sine waves. The duration t of such a train does not exceed $10^{-8}$ s. The maximum length of a train $ct$ is about 3 m ($c=3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s is the speed of light at which trains move in space), and it contains several million “ridges” and “valleys” ($ct/\lambda \approx 10^7$ at $\lambda=5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m).

Even if we consider all the trains emitted by different atoms of the source to be identical and characterize them with the same wavelength $\lambda$, the phase correspondence between them is completely random, since acts of spontaneous emission of light by atoms will occur at random moments of time. Thus, the light of real sources can be considered as a chaotic sequence of sinusidal wave trains. When beams of light from different sources are superimposed, the phase correspondence between light oscillations at any point during the observation time will change randomly many times. As a result of the addition of a large number of oscillations with random phases, the energy of the resulting oscillation at each point will be equal to the sum of the energies of the added oscillations, that is, the redistribution of energy in space characteristic of the interface will not occur.

From here it is clear that special conditions are needed to observe the interface of light: light from the same source must be divided into two beams (or several beams) and then these beams must be brought together in some way. If the path difference from the separation point to the observation point does not exceed the length of an individual train, then random changes in the amplitude and phase of light oscillations in the two beams will occur in accordance. Such bundles are said to be coefferents. When the beam coefferences are superimposed, a stationary interface pattern is formed.

The first proposal for the interface of light based on wave concepts was given by the English scientist Thomas Young at the very beginning of the 19th century. The experiment he performed with two rejections is described in detail in the textbook (“Physics 10”, § 69). The coreness of light beams from rejection in this experience ensures their origin from one primary beam.

Another method for obtaining coherent light beams is based on the use of reflection of the primary beam from two surfaces with a thin source from one primary beam.
