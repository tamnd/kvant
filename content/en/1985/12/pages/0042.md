Interference of light

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
E. I. BUTIKOV

The wave properties of light reveal themselves most clearly in the phenomena of interference and diffraction. These phenomena are characteristic of waves of any nature. They are relatively easy to observe experimentally, for example, for sound waves or waves on the surface of water, but to observe the interference or diffraction of light, certain conditions must be met. They, in particular, will be discussed in this country.

The interference of light refers to the process of observing several light beams, in which the resulting light intensity*) at each point is not equal to the sum of the intensities of the individual beams. In some places the intensity turns out to be greater than the total intensity, in others it is less, that is, they form alternating light and dark areas - an interference pattern occurs.

The simplest example of interference is provided by the observation of two monochromatic waves of the same frequency and with the same direction of oscillation in the addition of waves. As a result, oscillations occur at each point with the same frequency and amplitude that is constant over time, depending, however, on the position of this point.

Let the amplitudes of the folding oscillations be the same, then for the effective oscillation $E(t)$ we obtain

$$E(t) = E_1(t) + E_2(t) = a \cos(\omega t + \varphi_1) + a \cos(\omega t + \varphi_2) = 2a \cos \frac{\varphi_1 - \varphi_2}{2} \cos \left( \omega t + \frac{\varphi_1 + \varphi_2}{2} \right),$$

that is, the amplitude $A = 2a \left| \cos \frac{\varphi_1 - \varphi_2}{2} \right|$ depends on the phase difference $\varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ of the resulting oscillations and takes values ​​from the double amplitude $2a$ of each wave (at points where the oscillations are in phase) to zero (at points where the oscillations occur in antiphase). The energy of vibrations, and therefore the intensity of light $I$, is proportional to the square of their amplitude: $I \sim A^2$. As can be seen from formula (1), the resulting intensity $I$, depending on the position of the observation point, takes values ​​from zero to quadruple the intensity of an individual wave $I_0 \sim a^2$:

$$I = 4I_0 \cos^2 \frac{\varphi_1 - \varphi_2}{2} = 2I_0 (1 + \cos (\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)).$$

Sets of points in space that correspond to the same intensity (in part, maximum or minimum), form surfaces, depending on the properties and location of light sources.

Question 1. Two identical exact sources are located at some distance from each other. What form does the maximum and minimum intensity have in this case?

The phase difference $\varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ at a given point depends on the path difference $l$ of waves from in-phase sources: $l = l_1 - l_2$. If the path difference is equal to an integer number of wavelengths: $l = k\lambda$ ($k = 0, 1, 2, ...$), then the waves arrive at the observation point in phase and, upon addition, oscillations with maximum amplitude. If the path difference is equal to an odd number of half-waves: $l = (2k + 1)\lambda/2$, then the waves arrive in antiphase and “cancel” each other. Thus, the differences between maxima and minima are determined by conditions $l_1 - l_2 = const$.
