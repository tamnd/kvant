M937. Is there such a figure $F$ that it cannot be covered by a semicircle of radius 1, but two of its copies can be covered by a circular figure 1, if $F$ is a) an arbitrary figure; b) a convex figure?

The answer to both questions a) and b) is yes. Examples are presented at figure. To answer question a), in addition to various variants of the figure in the form of a “propeller” or the ancient Kist symbol “Yang and Yin,” the following degenerate example is also suitable: from a circle of radius 1 with center $O$ three points are drawn out - the vertices of an equilateral triangle with center $O$. To answer question 6) the following figure is suitable: a circle of radius 1 with center $O$ is divided into four arcs of size $\varepsilon, \varepsilon, \pi-\varepsilon, \pi-\varepsilon$ and a “convex hull” of two non-adjacent arcs is constructed. (The lower left figure corresponds to $\varepsilon=\pi/2$.) Note that for small $\varepsilon$ the constructed figure has an area that is angularly close to the area of ​​the semicircle.

The proof that the constructed figure does not cover a semicircle is in all cases based on the following message: the ends of the semicircle's diameter are at a distance of 2, therefore, they can only be covered by two points of the constructed figure that are diametrically opposite points of the circle of radius 1 from which we cut it out; Thus, our figure should contain the target semicircle with center $O$ radius 1.

The fact that two copies of a figure can cover a circle is very clear (in all examples it is enough to rotate the figure relative to the center of the circle by some angle or reflect from some straight line passing through the center).
