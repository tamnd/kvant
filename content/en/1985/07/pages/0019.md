⟦folio 17⟧

Robert Hooke was born on July 18, 1635 in the town of Freshuter on the English Isle of Wight into a true local church family. His father wanted Robert to also become a priest, but these plans had to be abandoned. The boy was in such poor health that he could not even go to primary school with his peers. (Hooke was persecuted throughout his life, and this affected his character: contemporaries often noted his temper, harshness and irritability.)

Having great access, young Hooke devoted a lot of time to designing and modeling various devices and mechanisms. We can say that his passion for invention appeared in early childhood. However, the serene life in the family was early transformed by a tragic event. When Roberty was 13 years old, his father died, and Hooke had to think about his future. In pursuit of the inheritance of 100 feet of sterling, Hooke becomes an apprentice to a London painter. However, he soon becomes convinced that he can draw quite well even without special training, and the smell of oil paint gives him a headache. Hence the decision to leave the painter and enter the school of the learned teacher R. Bashby. At school, Robert studied Latin and Greek, became acquainted with Euclid's geometry, in a word, received training sufficient to enter the university.

In 1653, Hooke became a student at the famous Oxford University. By that time, the money left to him by his father was exhausted, and the young man had to look for funds to continue his studies. Robert is installed as a choir boy in the church. However, he did not have to sing for long, because very soon he found a place that better suited his inclinations: the young man was taken on as an assistant by the chemist T. Willis. Apparently, Hooke worked conscientiously, and Willis recommended him in the same capacity to a young aristocrat, passionate about science, whose name was included in all physics textbooks - Roberty Boyle.

Boyle, a man as distinguished as he was rich, not wanting to participate in the complex political struggle that was tearing England apart in the middle of the 17th century, retired to Oxford, where he organized a chemical laboratorium at his own expense. Previously, he received an excellent education in France, Italy and Switzerland and was engaged in scientific research not as an amateur, but completely professionally. By the time Hooke became his assistant, Boyle was interested in the invention of O. Guerricke's air pump and the experiments carried out by the German natural scientist using this device.
