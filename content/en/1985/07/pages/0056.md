Phase transformations

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences A. I. BUZDIN,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences S. S. KROTOV

Many things are incomprehensible to us not because our concepts are weak; but because things are not included in the range of our concepts.
K. Prutkov. Fruit sections

In this article we will talk about processes in which there is a change from one state of aggregation of a substance to another (one phase to another). Such processes refer to phase transformations, or phase transitions. This is the melting of a solid (and the reverse process of solidification, or crystallization, of a liquid), condensation of steam (and the reverse process of evaporation of a liquid) and sublimation, or sublimation - the transition of a substance from a solid to a gaseous state, bypassing the liquid (and the reverse process, also called crystallization).

We often encounter phase transformations in everyday life - when we boil water for tea or cook soup, when we smother it on the glass of a bus or any icicles formed on the eaves of a house. We have already gotten used to it and don’t think about why the sidewalk is sprinkled with salt in winter (why not sugar?) or why aluminum is used when soldering radio circuits (why, say, not aluminum?). However, why go far for an example? The basis for the existence of all living things in nature—water—constantly undergoes various phase transitions. - drying whites in the cold;
— the formation of winter patterns on glass (by the way, on which side of the glass do these patterns form?);
- fogging of glasses in a person entering a warm room from frost;
- formation of frost on trees or dew on grass, etc. (Try to continue this list on your own.)

The experience of conducting university admissions exams shows that the attitude of applicants to this topic is different. Some people think that everything is simple here, others feel trepidation when faced with any question on this topic. And both points of view, in our opinion, are legitimate. On the one hand, to solve the vast majority of problems where phase transformations occur, it is enough to use only the heat balance equation, that is, a special case of the law of conservation of energy. On the other hand, in such problems there is not always a very clear answer to the question of what the final state of the system under consideration will be: for example, will ice thrown into water melt or will there be two phases - ice and water?

Now let's look at a few specific tasks and questions. Many of them were offered in entrance exams at various universities.

Let's start with problems in which the central place is occupied by the melting process (or its inverse process - solidification).
