$$c + \delta + m = \frac{2S_{AOB}}{AB} + \frac{2S_{BOC}}{BC} + \frac{2S_{COA}}{CA} = s$$

Press a point inside an equilateral triangle of height $s$, distant from its side at a distance $c, \delta, m$ corresponding to Fig. 1. In this case, each operation corresponds to shifting a point in the triangle to a distance 2 perpendicular to one of the sides. Divide the triangle into $s^2$ identical regular triangles of height 1 (Fig. 2); Obviously, their vertices correspond to all integer triples with a given sum $s$. Let's take one of these vertices, draw arrows from it to those points where it can be shifted using our operations, draw new arrows from the ends of the arrows, etc. All these arrows form another shifted triangle from side 2. Their vertices depict all the triplets of numbers that can be obtained from the original one by “repainting chameleons.” It is easy to verify that from any red point, with the exception of the vertices of the large triangle, you can go along the red arrows to any other. The remaining points will similarly be divided into two more classes of “connected” points (green and blue at figure 2; arrows are not shown for them). The value of the invariant $r$ for all points of the same class will, of course, be the same; Moreover, it is easy to check that for red points $r=0$, for green - $r=1$, for blue - $r=2$.

Thus, from the triple $(c, \delta, m)$ one can obtain another triple $(c', \delta', m')$ if and only if the first triple has at least two positive numbers and the values of the invariants $s$ and $r$ for these triples coincide (the values of any other invariant will already coincide automatically; prove directly that, for example, the remainder of division $m-6$ by 3 is expressed through $s$ and $r$). In particular, if $s$ is a multiple of trem, for all vertices of the large triangle $r=0$, that is, they are reached only from the point $c = 0$ (on figure 2 - from the red points); if $s$ is not divisible by 3, then in one of them there will be $r=0$, in the other - $r=1$, in the third - $r=2$, that is, from any point $(c, \delta, m)$ you can get to one (and only one) vertex.

V. G. Iltchev, N. V. Vasiliev
