The partnership with Willis and Boyle was of particular importance for the aspiring scientist also because they brought Hooke into the circle of naturalgonium lovers, regularly found in Oxford, many of whose members a few years later (in 1660) became the founders of the Royal Society of London (Academy of Sciences of England). The members of the circle were well aware of Hooke's talents, and when the question of candidacy for the post of demonstrator of the Royal Society arose in 1662, they did not hesitate to turn to Hooke. Hooke accepted this offer. His responsibilities included the preparation of three or four experiments, which were demonstrated at the weekly meetings of the Royal Society.

The middle of the 17th century is a time when experimental research is just entering the practice of scientists. Therefore, Hooke comes to work in the fall to satisfy the demands of human man, because he had to not only carry out experiments invented by himself, but also repeat experiments that were reported before England, carry out astroνομ observations, etc. And Hooke, with this goal, coped with these responsibilities for several decades.

Since 1665, Hooke became a professor of geometry at Gresham College and read lectures there. In 1666, the philanthropist Kutler offered Hooke, for a fairly large reward, to begin reading lectures for members of the Royal Society of London on various scientific problems. Hooke, in need of a medium, accepts this offer, and for many years he regularly gives lectures on various topics.
