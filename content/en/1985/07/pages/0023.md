The culmination of Hooke's work in this area was his lecture “On the Restorer of Ability, or on Control,” published as a separate brochure in 1678, in which the law of control was formulated.

In his lecture, Hooke described many experiments with controlled bodies, the generalization of the results of which became the sign law. At figure 4, an entry from the indicated brochure, shows diagrams of Hooke’s experiments on the study of various types of deformations. It is characteristic that Hooke approached these studies, on the one hand, as a practitioner, studying, for example, the deformation of watch springs or the bending of wooden beams used in construction practice, and on the other hand, as a natural scientist, conducting precise quantitative experiments and making broad generalizations. (We also note that the control law is one of the few scientific results, the priority for which is always given to Hooke.)

Finally, another problem in physics, including Hooke in the solution of which is very significant, is the theory of gravity. Like many other questions, Hooke became interested in gravity at the beginning of his scientific career. In the early years of the Royal Society of London, he conducted experiments in which he tried to find out whether the force of gravity acting on a body from the Earth changes when the distance from the center of the Earth changes. Hooke did not receive the expected results, but did not lose interest in this problem.

Studies in astronomy, in particular an attempt to discover parallel non-moving stars - a phenomenon that directly proves the movement of the Earth around the Sun, prompted Hooke to outline his views on the movement and interaction of bodies. In 1674, he published the work “An Attempt to Prove the Motion of the Earth by Observations,” where, in particular, he pointed out the existence of a universal force of gravity. However, Hooke was unable to find a function that determines the dependence of this force on the distance between interacting bodies. Some time later, he approached Newton with a question about the possible form of this function, and then suggested that the force of gravity obeys the law of squares. Newton, in his landmark book “Mathematical Principles of Natural Physics”, where he outlined the basic laws of dynamics, did not even mention Hooke’s merits in the study of gravity, which deeply offended him.
