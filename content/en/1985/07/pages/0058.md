Until cxx, we believed that the melting point of ice was $t_0 = 0^\circ\text{C}$ ($T_0 = 273 \text{ K}$). However, this is only true at normal external pressure $p_0 = 10^5 \text{ Pa}$. As the pressure changes, the melting temperature also changes, that is, the temperature at which two different phases—ice and water—are in equilibrium (coexist). Similarly, at a fixed external pressure, water and steam coexist only at a single temperature (we are talking about a closed vessel). The same applies to the case of coexistence of ice and steam. For variable values ​​of pressure $p$ and temperature $T$, three different phase coexistence curves can be obtained - steam, water - ice and ice - steam. At figure 3 they correspond to the evaporation curve $OK$, the melting curve $OB$ and the sublicini curve $AO$. All three curves have a common point $O$ - the so-called triple point. Only in it is the simultaneous coexistence of all three phases possible. For the triple point of water, for example, $T_{\text{кр}} = 273,16 \text{K}$ and $p_{\text{кр}} = 609 \text{Па}$ (4.6 mm pt. art.).

Note that the evaporation curve ends at a certain point (point $K$), called critical. At this point all distinction between the liquid and its vapor is lost. At a temperature above the critical temperature, the gas cannot be condensed, no matter how much we skim it. It is interesting that it was precisely due to this circumstance that it took so long to obtain oxygen, hydrogen and helium in a liquid state - the critical temperatures of these substances are very low, and to wait, the substance must first be cooled to a temperature below the critical one before being skimmed.

The specific melting temperature (as well as vaporization and sublicinization) as a quantitative characteristic of the phase transformation of a substance depends on temperature and external pressure. For the same substance, the specific heats of melting, vaporization and sublicinization are related to each other. Let us illustrate this with the following tasks.

Problem 4. How much will the specific melting temperature of a substance change when the melting temperature drops by $\Delta T$, if the specific heats of the substance in the liquid and solid phases are equal to the corresponding $c_1$ and $c_2$?

Suppose that initially it is in the liquid phase at a temperature of $T$, a substance with a mass of $m$ passes into the solid state and then cools to a temperature of $T' = T - \Delta T$. The amount of heat released in this case will be equal to $(\lambda + c_2 \Delta T) m$ (here $\lambda$ - specific temperature $T'$ can also be achieved in another way - first, the substance, remaining in the liquid phase, is cooled to a temperature $T'$, and then passes into the solid phase. During this transition, an amount of heat will be released equal to $(c_1 \Delta T + \lambda^2) m$ (where $\lambda'$ — specific melting temperature at temperature $T'$).

Let us further use the law of conservation of energy for thermal processes (the first law of thermodynamics). We will assume that within the temperature range $\Delta T$ the specific volume of a substance in both the liquid and solid states does not depend on temperature. Then the work associated with the specific volume rock at the point of phase transformation will be the same in general cases. Considering that the change in the internal energy of the system does not depend on the transition path, but is determined only by the initial and final state of the system, we launch the energy conservation law in the form $(\lambda + c_2 \Delta T) m = (c_1 \Delta T + \lambda') m$.

From here we find the change in the specific melting temperature:

$$\Delta \lambda = \lambda - \lambda' = (c_1 - c_2) \Delta T.$$
