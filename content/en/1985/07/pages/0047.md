♦ a) The required representation is easy to find in explicit form:

$$x^2 = \frac{1}{3} (x+1)^3 - \frac{1}{3} (x^3+3x+1).$$

b) We carry out the proof by induction on the degree of the polynomial. For polynomials of degree zero (constant functions), the statement of the problem is obvious. Let it be true for all polynomials of degree less than $n$. An arbitrary polynomial of the $n$th degree $P_n(x)$ can be written as

$$P_n(x) = ax^n + P_{n-1}(x),$$

where $P_{n-1}(x)$ is a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$, for which our statement is true by the induction hypothesis. Therefore, it is enough to prove it for one $ax^n$ (if the statement of the problem is true for two polynomials, then it is obviously true for their sum). It is clear that $ax^n$ is a monotonic function for odd $n$; for even $n$ there is a necessary representation follows from the formula

$$(n+1)x^n = (x+1)^{n+1} - x^{n+1} - Q_{n-1}(x),$$

in which $(x+1)^{n+1}$ and $x^{n+1}$ are the return of the function $(n+1)$ odd), and for a polynomial $Q_{n-1}(x)$ of degree $n-1$ the statement of the problem is satisfied by the induction hypothesis.

If $n=2k$ is even, one can reason differently. By the induction hypothesis $x^k = P(x)-Q(x)$, where $P$ and $Q$ are monotically increasing polynomials; hence,

$$x^{2k} = 2P^2(x) + 2Q^2(x) - (P(x)+Q(x))^2.$$

Substituting $P(x)$ together with $x$ into the formula of point a) we obtain a representation of $P^2(x)$ as the difference of two returning polynomials. The same can be done with $Q(x)$ and $P(x)+Q(x)$, which ultimately gives the required representation for $x^{2k}$.

A. Yu. Vayitrov, V. N. Dubrovsky
