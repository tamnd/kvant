There are periods in the history of science when there was a complete breakdown of the idea of ​​the nature and methods of its research. Such periods are usually called scientific revolutions. One of them dates back to the 17th century, during which the foundations of multi-characteristic physics were laid. The nature of the revolution in physics of the 17th century was determined, first of all, by the turn of scientists to experiment as a means of understanding the laws of nature. One of the greatest experimenters of the era was Galileo. His simple, poignant and very convincing experiments inspired many scientists to research and develop the field of physics. The 17th century greatly expanded the range of physical laws and phenomena studied. At the same time, scientific specialization had not yet gone very far at that time, and many outstanding scientists successfully worked in several areas of physics at once. Such universal scientists include Galileo, Kepler, Huygens, and Newoton. To this list should be added the name of the outstanding English natural scientist Roberta Hooke, who, in terms of the breadth of his scientific interests and the variety of his results, occupies one of the most prominent places in the galaxy of scientists of the 17th century.

We will discuss the life of this scientist, his scientific activities and the fate of his works in this article.

Robert Hooke was born on July 18, 1635 in the town of Freshuter on the English Isle of Wight into a true local church family. His father wanted Robert to also become a priest, but these plans had to be abandoned. The boy was in such poor health that he could not even go to primary school with his peers. (Hooke was persecuted throughout his life, and this affected his character: contemporaries often noted his temper, harshness and irritability.)

Having great access, young Hooke devoted a lot of time to designing and modeling various devices and mechanisms. We can say that his passion for invention appeared in early childhood. However, the serene life in the family was early transformed by a tragic event. When Roberty was 13 years old, his father died, and Hooke had to think about his future. In pursuit of the inheritance of 100 feet of sterling, Hooke becomes an apprentice to a London painter. However, he soon becomes convinced that he can draw quite well even without special training, and the smell of oil paint gives him a headache. Hence the decision to leave the painter and enter the school of the learned teacher R. Bashby. At school, Robert studied Latin and Greek, became acquainted with Euclid's geometry, in a word, received training sufficient to enter the university.

In 1653, Hooke became a student at the famous Oxford University. By that time, the money left to him by his father was exhausted, and the young man had to look for funds to continue his studies. Robert is installed as a choir boy in the church. However, he did not have to sing for long, because very soon he found a place that better suited his inclinations: the young man was taken on as an assistant by the chemist T. Willis. Apparently, Hooke worked conscientiously, and Willis recommended him in the same capacity to a young aristocrat, passionate about science, whose name was included in all physics textbooks - Roberty Boyle.

Boyle, a man as distinguished as he was rich, not wanting to participate in the complex political struggle that was tearing England apart in the middle of the 17th century, retired to Oxford, where he organized a chemical laboratorium at his own expense. Previously, he received an excellent education in France, Italy and Switzerland and was engaged in scientific research not as an amateur, but completely professionally. By the time Hooke became his assistant, Boyle was interested in the invention of O. Guerricke's air pump and the experiments carried out by the German natural scientist using this device. Finally, another problem in physics, including Hooke in the solution of which is very significant, is the theory of gravity. Like many other questions, Hooke became interested in gravity at the beginning of his scientific career. In the early years of the Royal Society of London, he conducted experiments in which he tried to find out whether the force of gravity acting on a body from the Earth changes when the distance from the center of the Earth changes. Hooke did not receive the expected results, but did not lose interest in this problem.

Studies in astronomy, in particular an attempt to discover parallel non-moving stars - a phenomenon that directly proves the movement of the Earth around the Sun, prompted Hooke to outline his views on the movement and interaction of bodies. In 1674, he published the work “An Attempt to Prove the Motion of the Earth by Observations,” where, in particular, he pointed out the existence of a universal force of gravity. However, Hooke was unable to find a function that determines the dependence of this force on the distance between interacting bodies. Some time later, he approached Newton with a question about the possible form of this function, and then suggested that the force of gravity obeys the law of squares. Newton, in his landmark book “Mathematical Principles of Natural Physics”, where he outlined the basic laws of dynamics, did not even mention Hooke’s merits in the study of gravity, which deeply offended him.
