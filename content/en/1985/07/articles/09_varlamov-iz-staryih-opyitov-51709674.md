Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. VARLAMOV

Under this heading, we publish articles about topics and, at the same time, scales in physics.*) Today, readers are invited to talk about how easy it is to “achieve” electric charges at home, how the Leyden jar was “discovered” and what experiments can be carried out with it.

The makki depicted on the upper figure are not at all the attributes of a priest of some pagan flame. In the past century, with their help, venerable physics professors demonstrated the phenomenon of electrical repulsion in their lectures at universities. To do this, a mask with a hair wig was mounted on a dielectric stand and charged from a power plant. Sometimes one of the students (from among the most inquisitive) was invited to demonstrate. Like the mask, it was installed on an insulating stand and connected to the conductor of the power plant machine. After some time, when a sufficiently large charge had accumulated on him, the hair on his head stood on end, and a faint crackling sound could be heard in the quiet hall - this was electrical charges flowing from the ends of his hair.

There are many different fun experiments with electricity that are quite easy to reproduce on your own. We have already talked about some experiments earlier (see «Kvant», 1983, No. 5, p. 29), and now we would like to introduce a few more.

In Gaston Tisandier’s book “Scientific Entertainment,” published more than a hundred years ago, there is a description of a very easy-to-make power plant machine, with the help of which you can easily “receive” the electrical charges necessary for experiments.

Take a lacquered metal tray 35-40 cm long and place it on the table on several glass glasses (Fig. 1). Cut a sheet of thick wrapping paper to the shape of the tray so that it fits well on the flat part of the tray. Using melted soup, glue two paper strips to the sheet to lift it up.

Bring the prepared sheet of paper to some heat source, for example, to a stove, so that the paper dries well and gets as hot as possible. After this, quickly place it on a wooden table and rub it vigorously with a dry and stiff clothes brush - thanks to the electrification of the surface, the sheet of paper will acquire a certain charge.

Now, holding the ribbons, transfer the sheet of paper to the tray, ground the latter by touching its edge with your finger, and immediately lift the paper. As a result of this, the tray will be charged (and with a charge of the opposite sign to the paper). Place a sheet of paper on the tray and, repeating the same technique, get another spark and so on, up to 7-8 times.

This electrostatic machine can be used to charge a capacitor called a Leyden jar.

However, the story of the discovery of the Leyden jar is worth telling about it separately.

In 1745, a professor at the University of Leiden (Holland) Muschenbrek decided to charge water using an electrostatic machine. To do this, he took a glass jar and placed a metal rod in it to supply the charge to the water. Having completed the charging process, he held the can with one hand and looked at the rod with the other. Subsequently, in a letter to the French physicist Reaumur, he wrote that his whole body was as if struck by lightning, and that even for the sake of the crown of France he would not repeat this terrible experiment.

Around the same time, a German keyist was conducting experiments to obtain electronic water, which was supposed to have healing properties. Having charged the water in the bottle, he touched the nail through which the charge was supplied to the water, and he suffered the same fate as Muschenbreck.

This is how the first electrostatic capacitor was discovered, which later became known as the Leyden jar.

News of these experiments upset the world, and they began to be repeated in many places.

An interesting experiment about the electrostatic naturalist Franklin. To begin with, he continued the promised experiment with the Leyden jar. Having dipped the stick of one hand into the water and touched the outer surface of the jar with the other hand, he received a significant blow. Assuming that the charge was contained in the water itself, in the next experiment he carefully poured it into another jar and tested its effect on himself - there was no shock. Therefore, it is not a matter of water. Having poured fresh water into the first jar and repeated his experiment, Frankin received a blow again and thus became convinced that “the force that gave it was contained in the glass itself.”

Quite a bit of time passed, and the Leyden jar took on a “modern” appearance - a glass jar covered with metal foil.

An excellent Leyden jar can be made, for example, like this (Fig. 2). Take a glass glass filled with hunting shot and place a metal teaspoon in it (of course, all these items must be completely dry). To charge the resulting capacitor (think, what serves as its plates?) Let us use the electrostatic machine just described. While you touch the tray with your finger and lift the sheet of paper, have your assistant, holding the jar by the bottom, bring it to the edge of the tray so that the spark hits the end of the spoon. After several such discharges, the Leyden jar will receive enough charge for the wave, and with its help it will be possible to continue many interesting experiments. Let's describe some of them.

The simplest experiment is to discharge a Leyden jar through thick paper, and you will see that the spark breaks through the paper with a curious shape.
