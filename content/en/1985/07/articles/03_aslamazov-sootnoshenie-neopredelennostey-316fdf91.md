1. Momentum and position

The German physicist Werner Heisenberg, who discovered the uncertainty relation in 1927, formulated it as follows: if, while studying a body, we were able to determine the projection of the body's implus with uncertainty Δp_x, then we will not be able to simultaneously determine the corresponding coordinate of the body with an accuracy greater than Δx≈h/Δp_x, where h=h/2π=1.054·10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant.

This ratio may seem puzzling at first. After all, Newton’s laws, which are studied in school, make it possible to find the law of motion of a body, that is, to calculate the dependence of coordinates on time. Knowing the law of motion, one can find the projections of the body’s velocity v (derivatives of the coordinates with respect to time) and the projections of its implus p=mv. It turns out that it is possible to simultaneously determine the coordinate and impulse, and there is no uncertainty relation. Indeed, in classical physics everything happens exactly this way, but in the microworld things are different (Fig. 1).

Imagine that you need to follow the movement of an electron. How to do this? The eye is not a suitable instrument for these purposes. Its resolving power is not enough to smooth out the electron. Well, let's look at the electron in the microsopp. The resolution of the microscope is determined by the wavelength of the light in which the observation is carried out. For total visible light, this value is on the order of 1000 angstroms (≈10⁻⁵ cm), and particles smaller in size were not smoothed out in the microscope. Atoms have dimensions of the order of an angstrom, so it is impossible to distinguish between them, much less individual electrons.

Let's just dream up.

Suppose we were able to construct a microcoppe that uses invisible light and electromagnetic waves with varying wavelengths, such as X-rays or even γ-rays. The shorter the wavelength, the smaller the objects can be observed. It would seem that such an imaginary γ-microcopp would be an ideal instrument for observing the electrome. With its help it would be possible to measure the coordinate of the electron as accurately as desired. What does the correspondence of uncertainties have to do with it?

Let's think deeper about this thought experiment. In order for us to obtain information about the position of the electron, at least one γ-quantum must be reflected from it - the carrier of the minimum portion of energy $\hbar \omega^*$). The shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency of radiation $\omega$ and the larger the portion of energy that the quantum has. And the momentum of a quantum is proportional to its energy. Colliding with an electron, a γ-quantum necessarily transfers part of its momentum to it. Thus, when measuring a coordinate, we always introduce uncertainty into the electron's momentum, and the more accurately we want to check the measurement, the greater the uncertainty will be. A detailed analysis of this process shows that the product of uncertainties $\Delta x \times \Delta p_x$ cannot be made less than Planck's constant.

It may seem that we considered only a special case, came up with a “bad” device for measuring coordinates, and it is possible to check the measurement of a city more finely without “pushing” the electron, without changing its state. But alas, this is not the case.

The best minds (among them A. Einstein) tried to come up with a device that could measure the position of a body and its momentum simultaneously with an accuracy greater than the correspondence of uncertainties allows. But there was no way to do this. This simply cannot be done. This is the law of the period.

2. Waves of confidence

In the simplest atom model, the Rutherfor model, electrons are scattered in orbits around the nucleus, similar to how planets move around the Sun. But electrons are loaded particles, and when they rotate, alternating electric and magnetic fields are necessarily created - radiation occurs, leading to energy loss. That is why, in the planetary model, electrons have an independent fate - they will definitely fall onto the nucleus, and the atom will collapse. But the stability of atom is a firmly established experimental fact.

It was necessary to “correct” Rutherfort’s model, and Niels Bohr did this in 1913. Electrons in the Bohr model are allowed to rotate only in certain orbits, where they have strictly specified energies. Electrons can change this energy only abruptly, emitting and absorbing quanta when moving from one orbit to another. This “quantum” behavior of electrons in an atom allows us to explain a lot - in particular, the stability of the atom and atomic spectra, but it contradicts the correspondence to uncertainty. After all, when moving even along a quantum orbit, momentum and coordinate can be determined simultaneously, but in the microworld, as we now know, this cannot happen.

I had to abandon this atom model as well. The picture describing the behavior of an electron in an atom turned out to be even more complex.

Without going into details about the design of the clouds, you can match their characteristic size. If the probability cloud has a size of the order of $\Delta x$, then it makes no sense to talk about greater than $\Delta x$ uncertainty in the particle coordinate. Therefore, the uncertainty of the particle momentum $\Delta p_x$ cannot be less than $\hbar/\Delta x$. In order of magnitude, the same expression also determines the minimum value of the particle momentum.

The smaller the size of the confidence cloud, the larger the momentum becomes and, therefore, the faster the movements within the localization cloud. It turns out that these general considerations are enough to correctly estimate the size of atoma.

An electron in an atome has kinetic energy of an electron - this is the energy of its movement. It is related to momentum according to the well-known formula $E_k = (mv^2/2) = (p^2/2m)$. Potential energy is the energy of the Coulomb interaction with the nucleus. There is also a formula for it: $E_\pi = (-Ke^2/r)$, where the minus sign corresponds to attraction, $e$ is the charge of the electron, $r$ is the distance from the electron to the nucleus, $K$ is the coefficient, depending on the choice of units of measurement.

In each state, the electron has a certain value of total energy $E$. The state with minimal energy is called the ground (unexcited) state. Let's estimate the size of atoma in the ground state.

Let the electron be spread out over a certain region of size $\sim r_0$. The attachment of an electron to the nucleus tends to reduce $r_0$, to “collapse” the cloud of belief. This corresponds to a decrease in the electron energy, which is of the order of magnitude equal to $-Ke^2/r_0$ (as $r_0$ decreases, the negative value model increases). If the electron did not have kinetic energy, it would fall onto the nucleus. But, as you already know, the kinetic energy of a localized particle is always a consequence of the correspondence of uncertainties. It “prevents” the electron from falling onto the nucleus!

As $r_0$ decreases, the minimum momentum of the particle $p_0 \sim \hbar/r_0$ increases, and, consequently, the kinetic energy $\sim \hbar^2/2mr^2$ increases. From the condition that the derivative of the total energy of the particle is equal to zero, we obtain that its minimum corresponds to the value $r_0 \sim \hbar^2/Kme^2$.
