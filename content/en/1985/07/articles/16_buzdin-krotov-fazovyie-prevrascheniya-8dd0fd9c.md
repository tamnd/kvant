Phase transformations

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences A. I. BUZDIN,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences S. S. KROTOV

Many things are incomprehensible to us not because our concepts are weak; but because things are not included in the range of our concepts.
K. Prutkov. Fruit sections

In this article we will talk about processes in which there is a change from one state of aggregation of a substance to another (one phase to another). Such processes refer to phase transformations, or phase transitions. This is the melting of a solid (and the reverse process of solidification, or crystallization, of a liquid), condensation of steam (and the reverse process of evaporation of a liquid) and sublimation, or sublimation - the transition of a substance from a solid to a gaseous state, bypassing the liquid (and the reverse process, also called crystallization).

We often encounter phase transformations in everyday life - when we boil water for tea or cook soup, when we smother it on the glass of a bus or any icicles formed on the eaves of a house. We have already gotten used to it and don’t think about why the sidewalk is sprinkled with salt in winter (why not sugar?) or why aluminum is used when soldering radio circuits (why, say, not aluminum?). However, why go far for an example? The basis for the existence of all living things in nature—water—constantly undergoes various phase transitions. - drying whites in the cold;
— the formation of winter patterns on glass (by the way, on which side of the glass do these patterns form?);
- fogging of glasses in a person entering a warm room from frost;
- formation of frost on trees or dew on grass, etc. (Try to continue this list on your own.)

The experience of conducting university admissions exams shows that the attitude of applicants to this topic is different. Some people think that everything is simple here, others feel trepidation when faced with any question on this topic. And both points of view, in our opinion, are legitimate. On the one hand, to solve the vast majority of problems where phase transformations occur, it is enough to use only the heat balance equation, that is, a special case of the law of conservation of energy. On the other hand, in such problems there is not always a very clear answer to the question of what the final state of the system under consideration will be: for example, will ice thrown into water melt or will there be two phases - ice and water?

Now let's look at a few specific tasks and questions. Many of them were offered in entrance exams at various universities.

Let's start with problems in which the central place is occupied by the melting process (or its inverse process - solidification).

1. In a thermally insulated state there is water, the mass of which is $m_1=1$ kg, and the temperature is $t_1=30^\circ C$. Ice is placed in water at a temperature of $t_2=-20^\circ C$. After some time, the vessel contained water with a temperature of $t_3=20^\circ C$. Find the mass of ice placed in the container. The specific temperature of water is $c_1=4,2$ kJ/(kg K), the specific temperature of ice is $c_2=2,1$ kJ/(kg K), the specific melting temperature of ice is $\lambda=0,33$ MJ/kg. The heat capacity of the vessel can be neglected.

Let us denote the mass of ice by $m_2$. Then the amount of heat received by ice when it is heated to the melting temperature $t_0=0^\circ C$ will be equal to

$$Q_1=c_2m_2(t_0-t_2).$$

Turning into water (at a constant temperature!), this ice will receive the amount of heat

$$Q_2=\lambda m_2.$$

The resulting water, upon subsequent heating to a temperature $t_3$, will receive the amount of heat generated

$$Q_3=c_1m_2(t_3-t_0).$$

The water initially found in the vessel, when cooled to a temperature $t_3$, will give up the amount of heat

$$Q_4=c_1m_1(t_1-t_3).$$

Using the heat balance equation $Q_1+Q_2+Q_3=Q_4$, we find the mass of ice lowered into the vessel:

$$m_2=m_1 \frac{c_1(t_1-t_3)}{c_1(t_3-t_0)+c_2(t_0-t_2)} \approx 0,09 \text{ кг.}$$

Until cxx, we believed that the melting point of ice was $t_0 = 0^\circ\text{C}$ ($T_0 = 273 \text{ K}$). However, this is only true at normal external pressure $p_0 = 10^5 \text{ Pa}$. As the pressure changes, the melting temperature also changes, that is, the temperature at which two different phases—ice and water—are in equilibrium (coexist). Similarly, at a fixed external pressure, water and steam coexist only at a single temperature (we are talking about a closed vessel). The same applies to the case of coexistence of ice and steam. For variable values ​​of pressure $p$ and temperature $T$, three different phase coexistence curves can be obtained - steam, water - ice and ice - steam. At figure 3 they correspond to the evaporation curve $OK$, the melting curve $OB$ and the sublicini curve $AO$. All three curves have a common point $O$ - the so-called triple point. Only in it is the simultaneous coexistence of all three phases possible. For the triple point of water, for example, $T_{\text{кр}} = 273,16 \text{K}$ and $p_{\text{кр}} = 609 \text{Па}$ (4.6 mm pt. art.).

Note that the evaporation curve ends at a certain point (point $K$), called critical. At this point all distinction between the liquid and its vapor is lost. At a temperature above the critical temperature, the gas cannot be condensed, no matter how much we skim it. It is interesting that it was precisely due to this circumstance that it took so long to obtain oxygen, hydrogen and helium in a liquid state - the critical temperatures of these substances are very low, and to wait, the substance must first be cooled to a temperature below the critical one before being skimmed.

The specific melting temperature (as well as vaporization and sublicinization) as a quantitative characteristic of the phase transformation of a substance depends on temperature and external pressure. For the same substance, the specific heats of melting, vaporization and sublicinization are related to each other. Let us illustrate this with the following tasks.

Problem 4. How much will the specific melting temperature of a substance change when the melting temperature drops by $\Delta T$, if the specific heats of the substance in the liquid and solid phases are equal to the corresponding $c_1$ and $c_2$?

Suppose that initially it is in the liquid phase at a temperature of $T$, a substance with a mass of $m$ passes into the solid state and then cools to a temperature of $T' = T - \Delta T$. The amount of heat released in this case will be equal to $(\lambda + c_2 \Delta T) m$ (here $\lambda$ - specific temperature $T'$ can also be achieved in another way - first, the substance, remaining in the liquid phase, is cooled to a temperature $T'$, and then passes into the solid phase. During this transition, an amount of heat will be released equal to $(c_1 \Delta T + \lambda^2) m$ (where $\lambda'$ — specific melting temperature at temperature $T'$).

Let us further use the law of conservation of energy for thermal processes (the first law of thermodynamics). We will assume that within the temperature range $\Delta T$ the specific volume of a substance in both the liquid and solid states does not depend on temperature. Then the work associated with the specific volume rock at the point of phase transformation will be the same in general cases. Considering that the change in the internal energy of the system does not depend on the transition path, but is determined only by the initial and final state of the system, we launch the energy conservation law in the form $(\lambda + c_2 \Delta T) m = (c_1 \Delta T + \lambda') m$.

From here we find the change in the specific melting temperature:

$$\Delta \lambda = \lambda - \lambda' = (c_1 - c_2) \Delta T.$$

Let us now remember that boiling of a liquid begins at a temperature when the saturated vapor pressure becomes equal to the external pressure. As can be seen from the liquid-vapor coexistence curve, the pressure of saturated vapor increases as its temperature increases, and, conversely, as the temperature decreases, the vapor pressure decreases.

Task 6. Cooking food in a pressure cooker occurs at high pressure. In this case, the temperature turns $t_k = 100^\circ\text{C}$. It was found that immediately after the pressure cooker was sized, 3% of the water contained in it evaporated. Determine the temperature the water had before sizing. Temperatures during the establishment of a new equilibrium state and the change in the specific heat of vaporization with the transformation temperature.

After the pressure cooker is sized, the pressure will become equal to atmospheric pressure. Since the temperature of water $t$ in a pressure cooker turns the temperature $t_k = 100^\circ\text{C}$ of boiling water at normal atmospheric pressure, intensive evaporation (boiling) of water begins. It will erupt until its temperature decreases to $t_k$. In this case, to evaporate 3% of water, that is, a mass of 0.03 mr, where $m$ is the initial mass of water in the pressure cooker, an amount of heat of 0.03 mr will be required. This heat is released when water cools from a temperature of $t$ to $t_k$. As a result, we can write

$$0,03mr = cm(t - t_k),$$

from which it follows that the temperature of the water in the pressure cooker was equal to

$$t = t_k + 0,03r/c \approx 116^\circ\text{C}.$$

Let us pay attention to the fact that when calculating the amount of heat released during cooling of water, we avoid reducing its mass. This is justified since only 3% of the water evaporates.

Question 1. Will water boil in a test tube placed in a pot of boiling water?

The boiling process, as is known, requires a continuous supply of heat. When the water in the test tube heats up to a temperature of 100°C, the supply of heat from the butter will stop. Therefore, naturally, the water in the test tube will not boil.

Question 2. What will happen if napittol (this is a lighter liquid not mixed with water), whose boiling point is 111°C, is placed on top of water in a test tube?

Here we are faced with an interesting phenomenon - the so-called boundary boiling. Its essence is that boiling begins at the boundary of two liquids, when the sum of the partial pressures of their saturated vapors is equal to the external atmospheric pressure. It is clear that in this case the pressure of saturated water vapor is less than atmospheric, and therefore the temperature is below 100°C. Thus, when poplar is added to a test tube with water, boiling at the heat-water boundary begins earlier than the boiling of water in kasrol.

Problem 8. In the upper cylinder under a weightless piston with an area of ​​$S=100$ cm$^2$ there is $m_1=18$ g of saturated steam. $m_2=18$ g of water is injected into the cylinder at a temperature of $t_0=0^\circ C$. How far will the piston go down? Atmospheric pressure $p_0=10^5$ Pa, specific temperature of water $c=4,2$ kJ/kg, specific vaporization temperature $r=2,3$ MJ/kg. The temperature of the cylinder and heat transfer to the environment can be neglected.

According to the conditions of the problem, the steam in the vessel is saturated. Since the piston is weightless, the steam pressure is equal to atmospheric pressure $p_0=10^5$ Pa, and the temperature of the steam before water injection was $t=100^\circ C$. This moment is the most important in this task. It is not difficult to further verify that the final temperature of the system after establishing equilibrium will also be equal to $t$ (show this yourself). Consequently, the water in the cylinder will be heated to a temperature of $t$, which can only occur due to the condensation of a certain mass of steam $m_{\text{n}}$:

$$cm_2 (t-t_0) = m_{\text{n}} r,$$

where

$$m_{\text{n}} = cm_2 (t-t_0)/r \approx 3,3 \text{ г.}$$

The volume $V$ occupied by this vapor before condensation can be found from the equation of the gas state:

$$V = \frac{m_{\text{n}} RT}{Mp_0} \approx 5,7 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ m}^3.$$

Considering the volume occupied by water condensed from steam to be negligible (check this yourself), we obtain that the displacement of the piston is equal to

$$h = \frac{V}{S} \approx 0,57 \text{ m} = 57 \text{ cm.}$$
