Convection and self-organizing structures

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
E. E. GORODETSKY.
V. S. ESIPOV

Winds and ocean currents, movement of materials and flows of solar matter... Each of these phenomena is very complex and diverse. And yet they (and much more) have a characteristic common feature: convection plays a significant role in them - one of the most beautiful phenomena in the physics of the macrocosm.

In recent years, interest in this “old” and seemingly familiar phenomenon has increased sharply. It turns out that convection is one of those processes as a result of which systems, initially disordered, come to an ordered state. The birth of order from chaos (or, in other words, self-organization) is a fundamental mechanism of evolution. And the concept of convection helps us advance our understanding of this fascinating and, to a large extent, still obscure problem.

Conditions for the existence of convection

In the narrow sense of the word, convection is the occurrence of ordered movement in unevenly heated liquids or gases.

Under what conditions will such a movement occur? Let's turn to a simple example: imagine that we heat up a flat horizontal layer of liquid. How can the process of heat transfer from the lower boundary of the liquid to the upper occur?

When the temperature difference ΔT of the lower and upper layers of liquid is small, the process of heat transfer occurs due to the heat-conducting mechanism: “hotter” molecules, when exposed to “colder” ones, transfer their excess energy to them, but there is no macroscopic movement of the liquid. However, when the temperature difference reaches a certain critical value ΔTcr, the heat-conducting mechanism is the transfer of thermal energy associated with the occurrence of macroscopic motion in the liquid.

What does “unstable” mean? In what sense is “higher”?

Let's look into these issues.

$$\tau = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta q \cdot R^2} = R^2 \frac{\rho c}{\chi}.$$

The combination $\chi/(\rho c)$ is called the thermal conductivity coefficient and is denoted $x$. So $\tau = R^2/\chi$.

Substituting the found value $\tau$ into the expression for $\Delta W$, we get:

$$\Delta W = R^3 \frac{\eta}{\chi} \left( 1 - \frac{\rho_1 \beta g L^3 \cdot \Delta T}{L \eta \chi} \right) v^2.$$

In accordance with the general principle, at $\Delta W > 0$ the randomly occurring motion fades out (analogy with figure 1, and at $\Delta W < 0$ the movement continues (analogue - figure 1, b) - the state of the resting fluid (conductive mode) becomes unstable. Condition $\Delta W < 0$ is satisfied when

$$\frac{\rho_1 \beta g R^4 \cdot \Delta T}{L \eta \chi} > 1.$$

Notice how this inequality includes the size of the ball $R$: the larger $R$, the more readily the ball rushes upward. This means that, first of all, the ball of the maximum possible size in a given situation begins to move. But this maximum possible size, very long, is equal to the thickness of the layer. Therefore, in the last inequality we can count $R \approx L$.

So, we have obtained the conditions that determine the onset of convective movement:

$$\frac{\rho_1 \beta g L^3 \cdot \Delta T}{\eta \chi} > 1.$$

You see that the critical development of temperature at which semiconductive bonds occur is determined by the parameters of the liquid (viscosity $\eta$, thermal diffusivity $\chi$, etc.), as well as the thickness of the layer $L$. The greater the thickness of the layer, the smaller the difference in temperature at its lower and upper boundaries, the heat-conducting regime is replaced by convective one.

Substituting the value $R \approx L$ into the expression for $\Delta W$, we find

$$\Delta W = L^3 \frac{\eta}{\chi} \left( 1 - \frac{\rho_1 \beta g L^3 \cdot \Delta T}{\eta \chi} \right) v^2.$$

Our task is completed; we have found a function whose minimum determines the equilibrium position of the system - this is $\Delta W(v)$. When the development of $\Delta T$ is small, the graph of $\Delta W(v)$ has a form similar to that shown in figure 1, and the minimum is realized at $v = 0$. Other types of instability

The reason for the instability in distance, serving us, is the presence of heat flow across the boundaries of life and the heterogeneity of density in height. We will obtain a completely similar result in the case when the non-hydrogen density is not caused by a temperature difference, but by distinguishing the composition of life and the upper layers of life. For example, when the water at the top is saltier (and therefore heavier) than the water at the bottom. Here we move on to a very interesting and varied range of issues related to convection in two-component liquids. An example of such a system is the ocean. In reality, in the ocean, along with non-hydrogen in composition, there is also non-hydrogen in temperature. The combination of these factors leads to many characteristic features of the ocean and, in particular, to the formation of stable layers in it with different salinity and temperature (the so-called ocean stratification).

To understand how such a structure is formed, consider water in the lower layers of which the temperature and salinity are higher than in the upper layers. At the same time, even if the density of water downwards is greater than at the top (that is, it would seem that the equilibrium is stable, and the transfer of energy should proceed in the usual heat-conducting way), it is nevertheless possible for a transition from the heat-conducting mode to the convective one.

Indeed, let us imagine that a small element of life began to move upward. Let us assume that its temperature and salinity do not change. Since the density of the element is greater than the density of its surroundings, it will soon be abandoned and begin to sink, moving back to its equilibrium position. To do this, let us cite: everyone has noticed that seagulls, when stirring sugar in a glass of tea, always gather towards the axis of the glass. Both are part of the manifestation of instability, which is called Taylor instability (named after the English scientist J. I. Taylor (1886-1975), who was involved in research on aerofundamics).

In its purest form, the Taylor instability occurs in a fluid located between two pine rotating cylinders. The cylinders carry the liquid along with them, as a result of which it begins to rotate. However, at a certain difference in the angular velocities of the inner and outer cylinders, the rotational motion of the fluid becomes unstable, and along with the rotation, fluid motion appears in the radial direction. The situation is very similar to the one we discussed above. The only difference is that in the horizontal layer “top” and “bottom” were allocated to the direction of the free fall acceleration g, but here “top” and “bottom” are specified by the direction of centripetal acceleration. In the first case, we saw a separated element of liquid in the float due to the fact that its density did not have time to equalize the density of the environment (the ball did not have time to cool down). Here, the speed of rotation of the liquid element, which accidentally moves along the radius, does not have time to equalize the speed of rotation of those layers of liquid in which it falls.

As a result of the development of such instability, the fluid rotating between the cylinders develops on the “steering wheel” system (figure 5; black arrows show the directions of the flowing fluid inside the rotating “steering wheel”). An ordered structure appears again!
