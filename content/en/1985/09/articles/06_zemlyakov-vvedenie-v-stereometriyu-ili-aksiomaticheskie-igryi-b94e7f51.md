Introduction
in stereometry,
or
"Aximatic Games"

Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences
A. N. ZEMLYAKOV

This article is addressed mainly to ninth-graders who began to study stereometry using the textbook by Academician A.V. Pogorelov. It can also be used by the teacher when consolidating the materials in § 14 of this textbook. The essence of the questions discussed in the article - about the meaning of the formulations of the axioms, about the role of various axioms, about the logical difficulties that arose in proving the theorems - will probably be understandable and interesting to all lovers of geometry and logic. You will kind of take a walk through § 14 of A. V. Pogorelov’s book “Geometry 6-10” (G., pp. 175-177) and see how many “differences” and “interesting things”, “tricks” and “all sorts of things” are contained in just three pages of the textbook. So, let's go...

1. From planimetry to stereometry

...Think about the meaning, and the words will come by themselves!
Lewis Carroll

You have already studied - passed - three spatial axioms C₁, C₂ and C₃ (Γ., p. 175), and probably also three theorems - 14.1, 14.2 and 14.3 (Γ., pp. 176-177). Now we will go through them with you, check whether you understood everything well on the first three stereometric pages. In the proof of the first theorem -

14.1: Through a straight line and a point not lying on it, you can check the plane, using irite only one

— not only spatial axioms were used—

C₂: If two different planes have a common point, then they intersect in a straight line

C₃: If two different planes have a common point, then through them you can check the plane, using only one

- but also two axioms, called “planimetric” in the textbook, or more precisely, “planimetric axioms” (Γ., p. 175) - I₁ and I₂:

I₁: Whatever a line is, there are points that belong to this line and points that do not belong to it

I₂: Through any points you can check a line, and only one

The question immediately arises: what is the meaning of these “planimetric axioms” in stereometry? Indeed, in planimetry, axioms I₁ and I₂ described the basic properties of “belonging of points and lines on a plane” (Γ., p. 4). Their full formulations in planimetry are more authentic than those given above:

(I₁): Whatever a line (in a plane), there are points (planes) belonging to this line and points (planes) not belonging to it

(I₂): Through any points (planes) you can check a straight line, and only one

(note that we changed figure to the axioms - “adapted” them to the plane). Planimetric leaves - the statement ($I_1$) and ($I_2$) is true in which plane - have the axioms of group I in stereometry? Or, perhaps, they are talking about points and lines in space, that is, in the just given formulations in all parentheses, along with the words “plane”, you need to put the word “space” (for example, $I_1$: Whatever the line in space, there are points in space... etc.)?

Take your time to read further. Try to answer the questions asked yourself, open the textbook and see how the axioms $I_1$ and $I_2$ are used in the proof of the first theorem - 14.1.

Have you looked? Let us follow the progress of the proof of Theorem 14.1 together. Here is the text of the textbook.

Let $a$ be a given line and $B$ a point not lying on it.

Here we have just introduced the notation for the conditions of the theorem.

Let us mark some point $A$ on the line $a$. Such a point exists by axiom $I_1$.

Stop! If the interpretation of the axiom $I_1$ is planimetric, then for its application it is necessary that the straight line $a$ lie in some plane. But such an axiom - like “For every straight line there is a plane passing through it” - alas, there is no such axiom in the textbook. This means that the axiom of planimetry $I_1$ in stereometry should be interpreted as spatial. Let's continue the "investigation". The following phrase from the proof:

Let us draw a straight line $b$ through the points $A$ and $B$ (axiom $I_2$).

Worth it again! The planimetric interpretation requires that the points $A$ and $B$ lie in the same plane for the axiom $I_2$ to apply. But we have no axioms guaranteeing this. This means that the axiom $I_2$ is also extensive, coinciding only in its brief verbal formulation with the planimetric axiom $I_2$ from § 1.

Let's summarize. The axims of planimetry $I_1$ and $I_2$ in stereometry have a broad meaning (“Think about the meaning!”), and their full formulation must include the corresponding words (“the words will come by themselves!”). Here are the full statements:

$I_1$: Whatever a line in space, there are points in space that belong to this line, and points in space that do not belong to it (the line);

$I_2$: Through any two points in space you can check a line (in space), and only one.

Planimetric axioms ($I_1$) and ($I_2$) in stereometry already become theorems:

$I_1'$. Whatever a line in a plane, there are points of this plane that belong to the line, and points of this plane that do not belong to it (the line);

$I_2'$. Through any two points in space, one can check a straight line lying in this plane using only one.

Theorem $I_2'$, of course, immediately follows from Theorem 14.2 and the extended axiom $I_2$. The first statement of Theorem $I_1'$ is even more obvious (think about it!). But we still need to tinker with the second statement. Let's formulate it as a task.

Problem 1. Given a plane $a$ and a line $a$ lying in it. Prove that there are points that belong to the plane $a$, but not to the line $a$.

3. Running in circles

When throwing pebbles into the water, look at the circles or formations; otherwise such throwing will be empty fun.

Kozma Prutkov

Let's look again at Theorems 14.1 - 14.3. Please note: the first of them in wording (see paragraph 1) is similar to the third -

14.3: Through three points that do not lie on the same line, a plane can be drawn, and only one

Both contain two statements: about the existence of a plane (“it is possible to draw a plane”) and about its.edintensity.

Pogorelov’s textbook, equivalent to Euclid’s fifth postulate) appeared, even by professional mathematicians. Only after a thorough “investigation” was it possible to discover a vicious circle in them. But that's another topic.

Perhaps some readers were left with a feeling of dissatisfaction - the mistake that “There is still something here!” Indeed, our vicious circle can be “broken” if we split Theorem 14.1 into two parts:

14.1.A. A plane can be drawn through a straight line and a point not lying on it, and
14.1.B. and only one.
In the proof of Theorem 14.2, only statement 14.1.A is used, so the full structure of the argument can be carried out according to the scheme depicted in figure 5.

4. Conclusion: final questions and tasks

To conclude our tour of §1, I will ask three more questions about the text of the textbook.

II. The conditions of Problem 7 (p. 177) discussed in the textbook speak of lines intersecting two given lines a and b, but not passing through their intersection point A. Is this last requirement significant? Is it used in the solution?
