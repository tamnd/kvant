Background to the creation of the computer

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
N. Kh. ROSOV,
Academician of the Bulgarian Academy of Sciences
Vl. H. SENDOV

Now, at the end of the 20th century, no one can be surprised by reports of high rates of progress in various scientific areas and technical reflections, about the rapidly growing number of scientific and technical innovations in various spheres of our life. The statement of this has become extensive, and there is a great variety of evidence of this, one development of another.

And yet one can think that the speed of its development, the intensity and breadth of penetration into literally all areas of activity, and computer technology was one of the most unique phenomena. Even people of the middle generation clearly remember the time when electronic computers were talked about as something abstract and distant, and the abbreviation COMPUTER itself remained unknown to many.

Today, without computers with their enormous versatile capabilities, many production processes, global economic planning, the finest fundamental research, meeting the diverse needs of millions of people, etc. simply could not exist.

Modern computing technology has dramatically changed and greatly expanded the capabilities of mathematical science. Not only do many tasks now turn out to be “accessible”, which, being solvable only “in principle” in theory, in practice remained insurmountable due to the fantastically large volume of required calculations. The most important thing is that, based on the use of computers, the role and importance of mathematical modeling is increasing more and more. Every day, more and more new experiments can be replaced by “calculation on a machine,” and this gives an amount of gain in terms of money and time. It is not for nothing that such a point of view has become widespread: mathematics, generated by the modern computer, has become an experimental science.

However, the discussion below will not be about the current state of computer technology and not about the prospects for its future development and use. This article is devoted to some aspects of the past history of the computer, its own past days.

This story seems to us an interesting, meaningful and instructive concrete example of the dialectical process of the formation of human knowledge. Each of the fundamental ideas that, in their totality, led to the creation of a computer, on the one hand, was the product of a sharp, original mind, and on the other, being a natural and inevitable product of the historical evolution of human conditions, his needs, and the development of science and technology.

"Machine" that is always with you

The need to count was, probably, one of the first intellectual needs experienced by nomo sapiens already at the beginning of his historical journey. It was predetermined and stimulated by the scientific problems of the very existence of primitive man and, above all, by the constant need to know and learn the changing quantities of food, livestock, grain and other animal objects. However, man mastered man very slowly, because the formation of the abstract concepts of “one”, “two”, etc. required the accumulation of thousands of years of practical, everyday experience.

In the long and difficult process of understanding the very idea of ​​counting, a person was especially helpful... his fingers. This is how nature works, that a person is given a natural “counting machine” - ten fingers on his hands - and constantly, throughout his life, carries it with him.

This simple, convenient and reliable “calculating machine” has all the capabilities of a computer in a miniature: you can enter numerical information into it, it helps you perform arithmetic operations, reports the resulting result and, if necessary, allows you to save it for some time. Of course, the duration of information storage and the memory capacity of such a “machine”, its computing capabilities and weight are very modest, although not as small as it seems.

Some books on mathematics describe very skillful techniques that allow you to “on your fingers” count to fairly large numbers and perform not only addition and subtraction, but also multiplication. Now this is just fun, tricks, but even in the 13th century, one of the creators of medieval mathematics in Europe, Leonardo of Pisa, better known as Fibonacci, recommended using fingers as an aid in counting.

Finger counting played a special role in the development of mathematics. It seems reliable that it was here that the servers’ fingers in practice gave birth to, and then psychologically reinforced, the habit of people counting in tens, which led to the emergence of the decimal (ten) number system. It gradually evolved from other number systems (say, duodecimal or sexagesimal) used by some peoples, and has long become generally accepted throughout the world.

Since it is naturally impossible to leave numerical information on one’s fingers for a long time, man was faced with the practical task of inventing ways to designate and store numbers. At the dawn of mankind, notches and notches on various objects, special counting sticks, pebbles, tags, etc. were used for this. But the most reliable and convenient way to designate and store numbers appeared with the advent of writing. By the way, some stories claim that people learned to write numbers even earlier than letters.

An outstanding historical event that determined all subsequent developments of mathematics was the discovery of positional numbering. Apparently she was born around 4000 years old

What a pity that time has not brought to us the name of the genius who first expressed the idea of ​​taking into account the place of each digit in a special way when writing numbers! The incomparable convenience of positional numbering ensured its victory over all other ways of recording numbers (for example, over the well-known Roman numbering system); It was she who later opened up the possibility of mechanizing counting and ultimately creating a computer.

We owe the creation of the decimal positional numbering (number system) instilled in us to the ancient Hindus - this happened approximately one and a half thousand years ago. From India, through the Arabs, in the early medieval period, this numbering system, together with the designations of numbers (which are still called “Arabic”) came to Europe, and then spread to all countries.

How to count faster and more reliably?

As the various practical needs of man grew, primarily with the expansion of domestic and international trade, the importance of various calculations in the life of society also increased, without which it is impossible to provide for these needs.

Today, such calculations seem quite simple - after all, they use only four arithmetic operations and are accessible to elementary school students! But let’s not forget that we are talking about other times, when convenient rules for writing counting in columns did not yet exist, when people did not know not only ballpoint pens, but even paper.

The great revolution in mathematics was the invention of the abacus, or, in other words, the abacus. Unfortunately, it is unknown who and when created this sheder, just as it is impossible to name the exact number of its historical and national varieties.

The abacus was a convenient and simple device that allowed a person to determine arithmetic operations on numbers by moving certain objects (counting stamps) according to special rules. It is widely known that ancient mathematicians attached special importance to geometry

solving a problem “with the help of numbers and a ruler.” But few people know that a similar situation should remain in arithmetic: the problem was considered solved only if it was indicated how the corresponding necessary calculations were carried out on the abacus.

In various countries, modifications of the abacus, despite the amazing simplicity of their design (or perhaps precisely because of this simplicity?), received wide recognition and were used for a century. A particularly significant contribution to the spread of abacus and methods of counting on it would be made by the investment French scientist Herbert (950-1003), who at the end of his life became Pope under the name Sylvester II.

Only in the late Middle Ages, after the advent of paper in Europe and with the spread and adoption of the decimal positional number system (in which written arithmetic operations are easy to perform), the abacus asserts its universal significance for arithmetic. Gradually it turns into an auxiliary device, for some time, so to speak, “technical” providing commercial calculations, etc. For example, Russian abacus still successfully helps store cashiers in their work (in recent years, abacuses have given way to their place, being automatically replaced by cash registers and microcalculators).

The abacus (abacus), of course, cannot yet be called a computing machine. This is just a calculating device that allows you to organize in a rotational form (alternative to the cissular “column” calculation) the execution of

However, with the advent of the abacus, the fundamental idea of ​​a special device was realized and consolidated, performing an unlimited number of operations of certain types without any intermediate records.

Counting instruments, in addition to the abacus and abacus, include many other devices that are quite original and interesting. We will not list them all here, but will mention only one more, which at first glance is quite unexpected in the series of counting instruments. These are... tables of logarithms. However, there is no need to be surprised! If you think about how, say, multiplication is carried out using tables of logarithms, it is not difficult to see that their role is, in principle, no different from the role of abacus.

Tables of logarithms were published in 1614 by the Scottish mathematician John Napier (1550-1617), although he created them much earlier - around the early 90s of the 16th century. Appearing initially as the basis of a free “calculating device,” the concept of logarithm played a special role in the history of the development of all mathematical science.

First attempts to entrust the bill to a machine

The rules of written counting in the decimal positional number system are undoubtedly very convenient, but this does not stop the calculations from being difficult and tedious, especially if these are long calculations with large numbers. Therefore, with the victory of the decimal positional number system, the idea of ​​​​creating devices that help counting has not lost its appeal. Moreover, the further intensive growth of the needs of trade and finance, the rapid development of technology during the formation of the capitalist mode of production, astronomical, surveying, navigation and other calculations, the need for a specific (“to the number”) solution of applied mathematical problems - all this made the task of liberating a person from the difficult time of counting scales important.

The next extremely important technology dates back to the 17th century - the appearance of a counting device.

It has long been believed that the first device was built in 1642 by the French mathematician, physicist and physicist Blaise Pascal (1623-1662). However, not so long ago it was established that for the first time, around 1623, a calculating machine was constructed by the German scientist Wilhelm Schickard (1592-1636), professor of mathematics and ... oriental languages ​​at the University of Tübingen.

The radical difference between a counting device and a calculating device is that it itself, without the participation of the human head, automatically carries out an arithmetic operation on the established initial numbers and reports the resulting result. However, that part of the work that is associated with drawing up a plan for the sequential implementation of various operations, therefore entering data before each operation and writing off intermediate results, remains with people.

It is also important to support the fact that the implementation of computational operations in a counting device is carried out on the basis of the mechanical movement of its parts (for example, the expression of swaddled wheels) with the automatic transformation of a ten in a certain digit into a unit of the following technical details).

Pascal's automaton gained wide popularity and served as the ancestor of a huge family of quoting devices created over the next three centuries. It is interesting to note that, although the creation of such devices in itself is rather an engineering, technical problem, the long list of names of their creator includes many outstanding mathematicians, and, as a rule, they were the ones who came up with the original design solutions. Here are just a few examples.

One of the founders of differential and integral calculus, the German mathematician, scientist and philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) was actively involved in the creation and improvement of counting machines for almost 40 years of his life. The counting device he built “skillfully” performed not only addition and subtraction, like Pascal’s automaton, but also multiplication and division. In other words, Leibniz was the author of the first arithmeter in history - a device that allows you to quickly and conveniently perform all four arithmetic operations.

The first slide rule was invented in the 20s of the 17th century by the famous English mathematician William Oughtred (1574-1660), who, by the way, proposed using the “cross” × as a multiplication sign. Having gained wide popularity, improved many times, and survived centuries, the slide rule turned out to be a faithful, convenient and indispensable assistant in a wide variety of practical calculations. Until relatively recently, it was precisely this that was, as it were, a symbol of engineering work: an engineer was always depicted in symbols and drawings with an invariable slide rule in his breast pocket.

The name of the Russian mathematician and mechanic, the founder of the St. Petersburg mathematical scale Pafnutya Lvovich Chebyshev (1821-1894) is well known throughout the world: his work had a decisive influence on the development of many branches of mathematics. P. L. Chebyshev also left a noticeable mark in the history of computer technology. He was the first to express (and implement in his arithmeter) the idea of ​​​​a continuous (rather than discrete) transformation of a ten into a high-order unit. The principle of continuous transmission of tens has become especially important in our time, after the advent of computers.

The 20th century was marked by a further sharp increase in the role of computing in a variety of areas of human activity. There was a lot to count and a lot to count. In the first half of this century, the most common calculating device in the world was the arithmeter. Its various modifications, produced in different countries, were not fundamentally different from the arithmeter designed in 1890 by the St. Petersburg inventor Vilg Teofilovich Oder (1845-1905). Lightweight and small in size, simple and easy to handle, reliable in operation, the Oderga arithmeter was the pinnacle of design art among numerous counting devices.

Did any of the older generation people, although somehow related to calculations or processing of numerical data, not have to deal with the classic Felix arithmeter? Until recently, it was the main mass computing tool; it could be found everywhere. And today “Felix” is just an exhibit of the museum of the history of science. Spurred by the needs of existence, computer technology moved on, and we had to say goodbye to this faithful and familiar assistant. Such calculations are always a little sad, but without them the progress of science is impossible, they are inevitable...
