F935. A current $I_0$ flows through a long straight conductor of constant cross-section. In section AB of the conductor (see figure), its resistivity decreases from $\rho_1$ in section A-A to $\rho_2$ in section B-B. What size and sign of the object breast is formed in area AB?

$$\Delta U = I_0 \rho \Delta l / S.$$

Here $\rho$ is the resistivity of the conductor in the selected volume, and $S$ is the cross-section of the conductor. Considering that the limit of the ratio $\Delta U / \Delta l$ at $\Delta l \rightarrow 0$ determines the magnitude of the electric field strength ($\lim_{\Delta l \rightarrow 0} (\Delta U / \Delta l) = E$), we can write the first equality in the form

$$E = \rho I_0 / S.$$

The resulting correspondence shows, for any section of the conductor, the relationship between the electric field strength $E$ and the resistivity of the conductor in the same section.

$$E_1 = |\vec{E}_1| = |\vec{E}_l| - \frac{\Delta Q}{2\varepsilon_0 S},$$

$$E_2 = |\vec{E}_2| = |\vec{E}_l| + \frac{\Delta Q}{2\varepsilon_0 S}.$$

F936. Along a long superconducting solenode with inductance $L_0 = 1$ H, containing $N = 200$ turns, the current flows $I_0 = 0,1$ A.*) From a distance, a closed conducting coil of the same radius as the solenode turns is brought to the solenode; turn inductance $L_1 = 1 \cdot 10^{-3}$ H. The coil is placed between the coils of the solenode coaxially with it. How will the current flowing through the solenoid change? How will the energy of the system change?

◆
Before introducing a turn, the magnetic flux through the solenoid was equal to

$$\Phi_0 = L_0 I_0.$$

This flow creates with all its turns; Assuming that the turns included in the total flux are the same (this can be considered if there are many turns and the solenoid is long), we will find the contribution of one turn (the flux created by one turn through the solenoid at current $I_0$):

$$\Delta \Phi_0 = \frac{L_0}{N} I_0.$$

On the other hand, the general magnetic field of the solenode creates a flux $\Delta \Phi_0^*$ in each of the turns; the sum of such flows is equal to $\Phi_0$; That's why

$$\Delta \Phi_0^* = \frac{\Phi_0}{N} = \Delta \Phi_0.$$

This means that the magnetic flux through one turn created by the solenide at current $I_0$ is the same as the flux through the entire solenide created by one turn at the same current. (This is a known fact.)

Let's add a coil to the solenoid. Let $I$ be the new current in the solenide, and $I_1$ be the induced current generated in the coil. Since the coil is superconducting, the magnetic flux through it must remain unchanged and equal to zero. This flux consists of the flux $\Phi$, created through the coil by the entire solenoid at current $I$, and its own flux $\Phi_1$, created by the coil with current $I_1$:

$$0 = \Phi + \Phi_1 = \frac{L_0}{N} I + L_1 I_1.$$

The magnetic flux through the solenoid (superconducting) must also remain unchanged and equal to $L_0 I_0$:

$$L_0 I_0 = L_0 I + \frac{L_0}{N} I_1.$$

Solving (1) and (2) together, we find the currents $I$ and $I_1$:

$$I = I_0 \left( 1 - \frac{L_0}{L_1 N^2} \right)^{-1} \approx 0,103 \text{ A},$$

$$I_1 = - I \frac{L_0}{L_1 N} \approx -0,510 \text{ A}.$$

Thus, the current flowing through the solenide has increased by $\Delta I = 3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ A. The energy of the system has increased:

$$\Delta W = L_0 \frac{I^2}{2} + L_1 \frac{I_1^2}{2} - L_0 \frac{I_0^2}{2} \approx 1,25 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ Дж}.$$

The energy increased due to the mechanical work that was done when introducing a turn into the solenoid (oppositely directed currents repel).
