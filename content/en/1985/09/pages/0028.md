About musical sounds and their sources

Musical sounds, or musical tones, are sounds that we hear when their sources perform harmonious vibrations. The amplitude of these vibrations determines the loudness of the sound, and the frequency determines its height (or ton height). A larger amplitude of oscillations corresponds to a greater volume, and a greater frequency corresponds to a higher tone.

Sounds of the same pitch can be returned in many ways - for example, using different musical instruments or human voices. But it is interesting that all these sounds (hereinafter identical in tone) are easily distinguishable by ear.

What's the matter here? Why do sounds corresponding to vibrations of the same frequency appear different to us? How do we manage to recognize the voice of a familiar person among the many other voices of people we do not know? How do we distinguish between the sound of a piano string or a violin string, the sound of an accordion or a flute? To understand this, you need to find out what and how fluctuates in the sources of musical sounds.

In most musical instruments, the vibrating elements are strings or air tables. Let's start with the strings.

String vibrations. What are the vibrations of the string as a whole? Let's imagine that we returned the string so that a transverse elastic wave ran along it. Having reached the fixed end of the string, the wave is reflected and runs back. Then, at any point on the string, two waves running in opposite directions meet. Since these waves are coherent, when they are added, a stable integration pattern is formed (“Physics 10”, § 36). At those points on the string where the oscillations caused by both waves are identical in phase, the displacements from the equilibrium position will vary to suit the amplitude.
