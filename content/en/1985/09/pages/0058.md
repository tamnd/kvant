⟦folio 56⟧

First acquaintance

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences M. E. LEVINSHTEIN, Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences G. S. SIMIN

Surely we will not be mistaken if we assume that the majority of our readers have a better idea of ​​what electronic embroidery machines can do than they know how these machines work. Many will not only tell you what problems can be solved using a computer, but also demonstrate what almost all high school students can do today using a microcalculator. But what is there, inside these devices? What is the basis of their work?

Electronic digital printing machines appeared 40 years ago, shortly after the end of the Second World War. The Eniak (USA), BESM-1, Ural-1 (USSR) and other machines contained several decimal vacuum tubes and performed 5-10 thousand operations per second.

Modern computers contain millions of semiconductor devices and perform ten million operations per second.

The colossal progress of computers is decisively connected with the development of semiconductor physics and technical semiconductors.

Semiconductor devices are the basis of modern radio electronics; A computer is the most complex radio-electronic device ever created by man. The circuit of a short-volver portable radio includes 10-15 semiconductor elements (transistors and diodes), a color TV on semiconductors - more than 100. A mainframe computer contains tens of millions of elements and is far superior to all other devices in the complexity of its internal structure.

And at the same time, this most complex radio-electronic device consists of simple language elements called logical circuits. In turn, their basis, and therefore the basis of the work of all machines, is one single element that has the main property: the ability to be in two well-different physical states, one of which can be conventionally called “zero” and the other “one”. Physicists are known for dozens of so-called “key” devices that have this property, the number of such devices is increasing every year, and on the basis of any of them it is possible, in principle, to build a computer. But only in principle! Real computers place a whole set of weighty requirements on their main element.

Firstly, the switching speed of the element, that is, the speed of its transition from state “0” to state “1” and back, must be very high, since it determines the speed of the entire machine; secondly, its dimensions must be very small - after all, there are millions of elements in a computer; thirdly, for the same reason, its power consumption and its cost should be very low; fourthly, it must be reliable; five, ..., eighth, ..., ten...

It turns out that the only type of device from the multitude of information about “key” elements that satisfies all the stringent and contradictory requirements of modern computers are transistors, combined into large and extra-large integrated circuits (VIS and SVIS).

After the advent of integrated circuits, transistors were created as follows. Several thousand transistors and all the necessary connections are made on a larger silicon wafer - the integrated circuit (IC) originated in 1952 and was implemented in 1959.
