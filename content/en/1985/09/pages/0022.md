Let's start with experiments in which the properties of fluorescent liquids are studied.

Experiment 1. We need a light source and a cuvette. At home, you can use a slide projector as a source, in which, together with the slide, you need to insert a piece of cardboard or foil with a hole cut in the center (2-3 mm in diameter). This allows you to obtain a weakly diverging beam of light. The cuvette can be a cologne bottle or the like. If the vessel is filled with clean water and looked at from the side (as shown in figure 1), then the beam of light in the water will practically not be visible (it is visible only at the water-glass boundaries). If we replace the water with kerosene, then inside the cuvette we will see a bluish-whitish stripe. The reason for this is the fluorescence of kerosene.

until, finally, the solution stops fluorescing completely. This phenomenon is called luminescence quenching. Quality can be explained this way. A molecule of a liquid, enveloped in light, is in an airy state for an initial, by volumetric standards, period of time - on the order of $10^{-8}-10^{-9}$ s. But even in this short time, it can interact so much with a molecule of the extinguishing substance (in our case, potassium iodide) and transfer to it part or all of the excitation energy, which will ultimately turn into heat. Thus, the re-emission process will not take place.

Similar events occur at high concentrations of the fluorescent substance. Its molecules collide intensely, and at the same time their excess energy (excitation energy) easily turns into heat. This is why solutions with a high concentration of shampoo emit light weaker.

It is clear that the higher the temperature of the solution, the higher the probability of collisions between molecules. It turns out that an increase in temperature also has an extinguishing effect. You can check this yourself.

Experiment 3. In the previous experiment we quenched fluorescence. Let's try to brighten the glow.

Experience shows that in aqueous solutions hydrogen ions have a rather strong extinguishing effect. Let's try to reduce their concentration by adding a few drops of alkali solution (KOH or NaOH) to the shampoo solution - the intensity of fluorescence will noticeably increase.

In 1852, the English physicist and mathematician J. G. Stokes established that during fluorescence, the wavelength of the emitted light is greater than the wavelength of the exciting light (Stokes' rule).
