We must prove that equation (*) has solutions in the interval ]0; 1 [ (the range of functions $y=\sin^2 x$ on the interval $0<x<\pi/2$) if and only if $(a-1)(b-1)<0$ or $a=b=1$.

In what follows we will assume that $a \geq b$ (otherwise, using the substitution $s=1-t$ we will move to the equation $s^b+(1-s)^a=1$ on the interval $0<s<1$).

Let us first show that if $(a-1)(b-1)>0$ or only one of the numbers $a$ and $b$ is equal to 1, equation (*) has no solutions. There are two possible cases: $a>b \geq 1$ or $1 \geq a>b$. In the first case, $t^a<t$, $(1-t)^b \leq 1-t$ and $t^a+(1-t)^b<t+(1-t)=1$.

The second is treated similarly.

For $a=b=1$ the function $f(t)$ is then effectively equal to 1.

Let, finally, $a>1>b$. Let us show that on the interval ]0; 1 [ there are two points, $t_1$ and $t_2$, such that $f(t_1)>1$, $f(t_2)<1$. From here, by the theorem on the intermediate value of a continuous function, it will follow that at some point $t$ on the interval ]$t_1$; $t_2$ [ $f(t)=1$. Consider the derivative function $f(t)$:

$$f'(t)=at^{a-1}-b(1-t)^{b-1}.$$

At $t \rightarrow 0$, the function $at^{a-1}$ tends to zero, and $b(1-t)^{b-1}$ tends to $b>0$ (the graphs of these two functions are Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). Therefore, for a sufficiently small $t_1$ on the interval $0<t<t_1$ the derivative $f'(t)<0$, therefore, the function $f(t)$ kills on the interval [0; $t_1$] and $f(t_1)<f(0)=1$. Similarly, at $t \rightarrow 1$ the function $at^{a-1}$ tends to $a$, and $b(1-t)^{b-1}$ grows without limit $(b-1<0)$. Therefore, $f'(t)<0$ at $t_2<t<1$, where $t_2$ is close enough to 1, therefore, the function $f(t)$ kills on the interval [$t_2$; 1] and $f(t_2)>f(1)=1$. Thus, our statement is completely proven.

A. M. Sedletsky
