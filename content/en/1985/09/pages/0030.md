Yes, unlike a string, not transverse, but continuous elastic waves arise in the impact column.

The pipe can be short or long, straight or curved. Its other end can be open or closed. Sometimes the performer vibrates the thin elastic tongue, which transmits the vibrating effect in the trumpet (clarinet); sometimes the performer’s lips vibrate, causing vibrations in the trumpet (cornet).

The pitch of the sound here, as in the case of a string, depends on the linear dimensions. In an open pipe, the fundamental tone is affected when 1/2 of the wavelength fits into the length of the pipe, and in a closed pipe - odd multiples. These features are due to the fact that at the opening ends of the pipe (and one of them is always opening) a thin antinode of a mixed standing wave acts.

The musician can change the effective length of the pipe by closing and opening holes made along the pipe using valves or simply pressing them with his fingers (flute, clarinet, pipe). In the trombone, for example, the length of the pipe, and at the same time the pitch of the sound, is changed using a sliding U-shaped attachment. In an organ, the lengths of the pipes are unchanged, but the number of pipes with very different lengths is extremely large - up to several thousand.

Problems 1–8 formulated in the article constitute a kind of “practikum for the application of axioms.” Their solutions, as solutions to multiple problems from the initial, “introductory” sections of a wide variety of axiomatic theories, can be considered as the definition of a “game by the rules.” The goal of the “game” is to prove everything, the “rules” are that you can only use pleasant axioms (and, of course, facts already deduced from them). It must be said that our tasks, partly deliberately, are “overpacked” so that a relatively difficult task can precede an easier one and one that helps solve the previous one.
