In all the experiments considered so far, we observed the fluorescence of the liquid while studying the fluorescence of crystals.

The external differences of these types of luminescence - in terms of the duration of the consequences - are due to differences in the course of the re-emission process itself. In the first case, the fluorescence “cell” is a single molecule. Inside it there is a process of excitation and return to a newly excited state, which is accompanied by the emission of light. In the second case, it is not a single molecule that participates in luminescence, but a larger number of them—practically the entire crystal lattice. An explanation of the fluorescence mechanism for this case can be found in the literature listed at the end of the article.

Crystal phosphors include non-toronic sulfides (chemical compounds of metals with sulfur), primarily sulfides of metals of group II of the periodic table of elements: CaS, ZnS, SrS, BaS. The most famous of them is sulfur cyclone (ZnS), which we will experiment with.

To prepare zinc sulfide, you need to take 1 part by weight of sulfur powder (sulfur-colored) and 2 parts by weight of zinc powder. These substances were mixed and heated. (Due to the pungent odor of the gases released, it is best to conduct the experiment either in the open air or in a fume hood in a school chemistry room.) The resulting sulfur cyclone is mixed with glue and applied to a sheet of cardboard - the phosphorescent screen is ready.*)

You can immediately prepare another phosphorescent screen by adding a little copper filings to the initial mixture of sulfur and zinc powders.

Then let's start experimenting.

Experiment 5. Let's shine a beam from an overhead projector onto a screen with zinc disulfide (we'll try not to look at the light ourselves) and turn off the source. In real darkness, the screen will glow with a faint green light for several tens of seconds. Let's do the same experiment with a copper-activated screen. Its glow will be different in hue, brighter and longer in time.

Experiment 6. It turns out that if phosphorus is heated, the rate of its luminescence will increase. Let's heat the coin and (after turning off the light) apply it to the back of the screen. At the point of contact with the coin, the screen will flash brightly, but then quickly go out - the place of the coin will subsequently be obscured by a dark spot in the fading light of the screen.

Experiment 7. So far we have irradiated phosphors only with visible light. I wonder how they will behave if they are exposed to rays from the invisible part of the spectrum, for example ultraviolet?

Let's use an ultraviolet lamp, continues in the store. In the dark, let's direct a beam of ultraviolet rays onto a screen made of sulfur cycn. We will see the sulfur cyclus flare brightly.

Many substances have the ability to luminesce in ultraviolet rays, even those that do not luminesce under direct illumination.

*) Zycn sulfide can be “broken” by scraping it from Christmas tree toys that glow in the dark.
