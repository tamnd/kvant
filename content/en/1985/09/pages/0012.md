The ratio inside the layer depends linearly on the height $h$:

$$T(h) = T_1 - \Delta T \frac{h}{L}.$$

Since the density of a liquid decreases when heated, in the lower, more heated, complex ones the density is less than in the upper ones - the liquid is inexpensive in density. The dependence of density on height has the form

$$\rho(h) = \rho_1 \left( 1 + \beta \cdot \Delta T \frac{h}{L} \right)$$

(here $\rho_1$ is the density at the lower boundary, $\beta$ is the volume distribution coefficient, equal to the change in units of body volume when the temperature changes by 1 degree). The larger $\Delta T$, the faster the liquid density in the layer increases with increasing $h$. In other words, as $\Delta T$ increases, the inexpensiveness of the density in the layer increases. And this, ultimately, leads to instability: the lighter liquid located below tends to rise, thereby exciting movement throughout the entire layer - convection occurs.

At first glance, it may seem to countries that such a movement does not begin immediately, no matter how small the overheating of the lower words. After all, if we immerse a body in a liquid, it floats up even when its density is at least slightly less than the density of the liquid.

To understand what is happening here, imagine that a small element of fluid (for example, a ball of radius $R$) began to move upward with a speed of $v$. Generally, we can assume that the volume, and therefore the density of the body, does not change as it rises. In the case under consideration, the released element of liquid, rising and falling into colder elephants, decreases in volume, becomes more dense and may lose its buoyancy. That's the whole point!

Before obtaining the conditions for the loss of stability of the heat-conducting regime in explicit form, we will make one very important value. Bearing in mind the purely qualitative nature of our estimates, we will not be at all interested in numerical coefficients in the formulas, or indeed any details. Thus, we will consider a liquid element in the shape of a ball, although in reality it could have a more complex shape. We will take the volume of this ball to be equal to $R^3$, and its surface area to be $R^2$ (instead of $4/3\pi R^3$ for volume and $4\pi R^2$ for surface area), etc. This method, called the method of dimensional estimates, is an important stage in physical research, a stage at which the very essence of the phenomenon of interest to us is clarified: on what parameters it depends, what are its spatial and temporal scales, etc. d.*)

So, let's be present. Let us introduce the time $t$, during which the temperature of the ball (and, accordingly, its density) remains approximately constant. During this time, the ball, moving at approximately constant speed $v$, manages to rise from level $h$ to level $h + \Delta h$, where $\Delta h = v t$. Its place at level $h$ is occupied by a denser element of liquid, descended from level $h + \Delta h$ (figure 3). The density of a ball rising from level $h$ is $\rho(h) = \rho_1 (1 + \beta \cdot \Delta T \cdot h/L)$, and the density of a ball descending at level $h$ is $\rho(h + \Delta h) = \rho_1 (1 + \beta \cdot \Delta T \cdot (h + \Delta h))/L$. The change in potential energy with such counter motion is equal to

$$(\rho(h) - \rho(h + \Delta h)) R^3 g \cdot \Delta h = - \rho_1 \beta \cdot \Delta T \frac{\Delta h}{L} R^3 g \cdot \Delta h = - \rho_1 \beta \cdot \Delta T \frac{v^2 \tau^2}{L} R^3 g.$$

The minus sign means that the energy decreases, which means that such movement in the liquid is burned out. It is necessary, however, to take into account the bodies that are still released due to friction, which is equal to the resistance forces $A = F_c \cdot \Delta h$. The resistance force is associated with the presence of internal friction (viscosity) in the liquid and in the case of a ball moving at a speed $v$, it is proportional to the radius of the ball and its speed, as well as the viscosity coefficient of the liquid $\eta$:

$$F = \eta Rv$$

(here we again discarded the numerical coefficient of proportionality, which is unimportant in our qualitative assessments).

Thus, the change in energy when motion occurs in a fluid is equal to

$$\Delta W = (-\rho_1 \beta \cdot \Delta T \frac{v \tau}{L} R^3 g + \eta Rv) v t.$$
