⟦folio 11⟧

The temperature development $\Delta T$ of the hot and cold body is proportional and inversely proportional to the thickness of the transition layer; the proportionality coefficient (characterizing the thermal conductivity properties of the body) is called the thermal conductivity coefficient and is designated $x$. In the case of our ball, the thickness of the transition layer is the radius of the ball, and $\Delta q = x \cdot \Delta T/R$. The time the ball is left is the time during which all the excess heat $\Delta Q$ will have time to move from the ball through its surface to the surrounding liquid. Consequently, the time $\tau$ is determined by the conditions $\Delta Q = \Delta q \cdot R^2 \tau$, whence

$$\tau = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta q \cdot R^2} = R^2 \frac{\rho c}{\chi}.$$

The combination $\chi/(\rho c)$ is called the thermal conductivity coefficient and is denoted $x$. So $\tau = R^2/\chi$.

Substituting the found value $\tau$ into the expression for $\Delta W$, we get:

$$\Delta W = R^3 \frac{\eta}{\chi} \left( 1 - \frac{\rho_1 \beta g L^3 \cdot \Delta T}{L \eta \chi} \right) v^2.$$

In accordance with the general principle, at $\Delta W > 0$ the randomly occurring motion fades out (analogy with figure 1, and at $\Delta W < 0$ the movement continues (analogue - figure 1, b) - the state of the resting fluid (conductive mode) becomes unstable. Condition $\Delta W < 0$ is satisfied when

$$\frac{\rho_1 \beta g R^4 \cdot \Delta T}{L \eta \chi} > 1.$$

Notice how this inequality includes the size of the ball $R$: the larger $R$, the more readily the ball rushes upward. This means that, first of all, the ball of the maximum possible size in a given situation begins to move. But this maximum possible size, very long, is equal to the thickness of the layer. Therefore, in the last inequality we can count $R \approx L$.

So, we have obtained the conditions that determine the onset of convective movement:

$$\frac{\rho_1 \beta g L^3 \cdot \Delta T}{\eta \chi} > 1.$$

You see that the critical development of temperature at which semiconductive bonds occur is determined by the parameters of the liquid (viscosity $\eta$, thermal diffusivity $\chi$, etc.), as well as the thickness of the layer $L$. The greater the thickness of the layer, the smaller the difference in temperature at its lower and upper boundaries, the heat-conducting regime is replaced by convective one.

Substituting the value $R \approx L$ into the expression for $\Delta W$, we find

$$\Delta W = L^3 \frac{\eta}{\chi} \left( 1 - \frac{\rho_1 \beta g L^3 \cdot \Delta T}{\eta \chi} \right) v^2.$$

Our task is completed; we have found a function whose minimum determines the equilibrium position of the system - this is $\Delta W(v)$. When the development of $\Delta T$ is small, the graph of $\Delta W(v)$ has a form similar to that shown in figure 1, and the minimum is realized at $v = 0$. When $\Delta T$ is so large that $\Delta W < 0$, we move to the situation depicted in figure 1, b, which means the spontaneous (spontaneous) transition of the liquid from a state with zero speed to a state with a speed different from zero.
