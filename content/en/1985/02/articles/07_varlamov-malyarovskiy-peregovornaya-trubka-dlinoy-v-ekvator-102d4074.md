We emphasize that the reflection of a wave propagating in a waveguide from its boundaries is a certain property of the waveguide - it is thanks to it that the wave energy does not propagate in all directions in space, but is transmitted in a given direction.

Relatively recently, about thirty years ago, scientists from the USSR and the USA discovered that nature itself had created giant acoustic waveguides, which in size were significantly larger than even the imaginary speaking tube between Moscow and Leningrad. Such waveguides, when determining conditions, arise in the oceans. Scientists called them underwater sound canals, or SSCs for short. Sound waves propagating through such channels can be received thousands of kilometers from their source. Thus, in one of the most successful experiments, the sound from an underwater explosion produced by scientists off the coast of Australia covered half the globe underwater and was recorded by another group of researchers near the Bermuda Islands, at a distance of 19,600 km from Australia (this is still a record for the range of propagation of pulsed sound signals.

What is the mechanism of formation of such a waveguide? What in it serves as reflected boundaries that cause sound waves to propagate great distances across the column? The upper boundary is immediately set to the ocean surface, which reflects sound quite well (see Appendix). However, she is rarely new due to constant excitement; and this leads to chaotic scattering of sound waves on it. The bottom reflects sound weakly and cannot serve as the lower reflected boundary of the waveguide. Therefore, the boundaries of the waveguide in the ocean should be sought somewhere between the house and the surface. And they were found. These boundaries turned out to be layers of water at certain ocean depths.

How does the reflection of sound waves from the “walls” of the shut-off valve occur? To answer this question, we will have to consider how sound propagates in the ocean in general.

Until now, we have been talking about waveguides, silently assuming that the speed of sound propagation in them is constant. Meanwhile, it is well known that the speed of sound in the ocean ranges from 1450 m/s to 1540 m/s. It is related to the temperature of the water, its salinity, the magnitude of hydroelectric pressure and other factors. An increase in hydroelectric pressure leads to the fact that when diving for every 100 m, the speed of sound increases by 1.6 m/s. As temperature increases, the speed of sound also increases. However, in the ocean, the water temperature, as a rule, drops quite sharply as it moves away from the upper, well-heated layers of water into the depths, where it reaches an almost constant value. The effectiveness of these two mechanisms leads to such a dependence of the speed of sound on depth, as shown in figure 1. Near the surface, the predominant influence is the rapid distribution of temperature - in these complex ones, the speed of sound decreases with increasing depth. As you dive, the temperature changes more and more slowly, and the hydroelectric pressure continues to increase. At some depths, these two factors “balance” - at this depth the speed of sound is minimal; and then it begins to increase with increasing depth due to an increase in hydroelectric pressure.

So, the speed of sound in the ocean depends on depth. And this affects the nature of sound propagation. In order to understand what determines the course of sound

Let us consider a stack of plane-parallel plates with different refractive indices $n_0, n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k, \dots$, and $n_0 < n_1 < n_2 < \dots < n_k < \dots$ (figure 2). A ray incident from the topmost plate onto plate 1 at an angle of $a_0$, after refraction at the boundary 0-1, will make an angle of $a_1$ with normal to this boundary; at this angle it will fall on the boundary 1-2; after refraction at this boundary, the ray, having passed plate 2, falls on the boundary 2-3 at an angle $a_2$, refracts again, etc. According to the law of refraction,

$$\frac{\sin a_0}{\sin a_1} = \frac{n_1}{n_0}, \frac{\sin a_1}{\sin a_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1},$$
$$\frac{\sin a_2}{\sin a_3} = \frac{n_3}{n_2}, \dots, \frac{\sin a_{k-1}}{\sin a_k} = \frac{n_k}{n_{k-1}}, \dots$$
($c_0 > c_1 > c_2 > \dots > c_k > \dots$). Variable sequentially these equalities to each other, we get the correspondence

$$\frac{\sin a_0}{\sin a_k} = \frac{c_0}{c_k}.$$

By directing the thickness of each plate to zero, and the number of plates to infinity, we arrive at a generalization of the law of refraction, which describes the path of a light ray in a medium with a smoothly varying refractive index (this law is called the generalized Snell’s law):

$$c(z) \cdot \sin \alpha(0) = c(0) \cdot \sin \alpha(z),$$

where $c(0)$ is the speed of light at the point where the beam enters the medium, and $c(z)$ is the speed of light at a distance $z$ from the boundaries of the medium. With such a limiting transition, the broken line showing the path of the ray turns into a smooth curve. The phenomenon of bending of light rays as light propagates in a medium with a continuously changing refractive index is called refraction.

Knowing how the speed of light changes in the medium, we can use the correspondence (*), which indicates what the path of a particular ray entering the medium will be. In the same way, according to the same law (*), curvature of the bite rays occurs when the bite spreads in an inexpensive environment, where the bite speed smoothly changes. A special case of such a medium is the ocean.

Now let's return to the question of how sound propagates in the PZK. Let's imagine that the sound source is at a depth of $z_m$ (figure 3). What will be the sound rays emerging from the source? The ray going along the horizontal $z = z_m$ will be rectilinear. And those rays that come out at odd angles to this horizontal will be bent. By analogy, this phenomenon is called refraction of sound. Since both above and below the level $z_m$ the bite speed returns, the back beams will bend in the direction of the horizontal $z = z_m$; at some point the ray becomes “parallel” to this horizontal line and, having been “reflected”, turns towards it (see figure 3).

The role of the “walls” of this waveguide is played by a layer of water at those depths where the “turn” of the sound beam occurs.

The depth level $z_m$, at which the speed of sound is minimal, is called the SCP axis. Typically the values ​​of $z_m$ lie in the range of 1000–1200 m; however, in tropical aquifers, where the water warms up to great depths, the PZK axis can drop to depths of 2000 m. On the contrary, in high arid regions, the influence of temperature on the distribution of sound speed is felt only in the near-surface layer, and the PTC axis rises to depths of 200–500 m, and in polar aquifers - even close to the surface.
