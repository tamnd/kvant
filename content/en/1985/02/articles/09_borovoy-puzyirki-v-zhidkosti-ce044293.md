How does a bubble chamber work?

In the early fifties of the 19th century, a young employee of the University of Michigan (USA), Donald Glaser, came up with a new type of device that registered elementary particles. It is called the bubble chamber.*)

figure 1 shows a modern bubble chamber. This content, occupying a large hall several floors high, is mainly complex technical and controlled by high-speed electrically conductive machines. Many of the installations even have their own names: “Lyudmila”, “Mirabelle”, “Gargamel”, etc.

*) You can read about this and other detectors of elementary particles, for example, in the book by A. A. Borovoy “How Particles are Registered” (M.: Nauka, 1981, series “Viblotechka «Kvant»)). (Editor's note)

However, the first camera models made by Glaser could not be called printing (Fig. 2). Their main part is a glass ampoule with ether with a volume of several cubic centimeters. The liquid was heated and was under pressure of about 20 atmospheres (1 atm≈10⁵ Pa). A special device (also simple) allows you to quickly relieve pressure, but the ether did not boil immediately. If during such a “waiting” a charged part sang through the liquid, leaving behind a trail of ions, then bubbles of vapor appeared along the trail. Having photographed the trace, it was possible to increase the pressure again, the bubbles disappeared (crushed), and the device was again ready for use.

Thus, bubbles in liquids turned out to be extremely useful for particle physics. figure 3 shows one of the images taken with the bubble camera.

Why did the bubbles appear exactly on the path of the particle?

Atmospheric pressure boils at 100 °C. It is at this temperature that the saturated vapor pressure becomes sufficient and the liquid boils. But does it always boil?

Let's take two test tubes. We will wash one of them especially thoroughly, make sure that there are no scratches or foreign particles on the walls, and fill it with distilled water (approximately 10 cm³). Pour the same amount of tap water into the second test tube and also throw in a piece of chalk. We will heat the test tubes under the same conditions and in the absence of direct contact with fire. It turns out that the water in them will boil differently (Fig. 4).

In a test tube with tap water, boiling begins earlier, and this process will occur quite calmly and continuously. Steam bubbles are formed mainly on a piece of chalk. As for a test tube with distilled water, the boiling process in it begins later (at a higher temperature) and will occur unevenly. Large bubbles form in the liquid; their appearance and collapse are accompanied by a loud crash.

In the laboratory, it is possible to clean the vessel and water (using double-distilled water) so well that boiling does not occur until a temperature of 140 °C. If you throw a grain into such water, which is called overheated, an explosion will occur - bubbles with steam will quickly form. Chemists are well aware of this property of boiling. The explanation for the properties described above is related to the presence of surface tension in the liquid. The surface of a liquid can be imagined as a stretched elastic film. The film tends to shrink—silicon tension tends to crush the resulting bubble. The additional pressure *) caused by these forces is greater, the smaller the radius of the bubble, so that the boiling process is suppressed in the embryo itself. This is why a homogeneous liquid can be overheated. At the same time, any defects both in the liquid itself and those associated with foreign inclusions have a boiling point. Such imperfections are formed, in part, in the path of a charged particle, which is why it becomes the site of formation of the first bubbles. Now it is clear that the most important condition for the operation of the Glaser camera is the homogeneity of the broadcast and the purity of the sample.

How to answer the questions asked?

Essentially, the entire explanation of the property is an answer to the first question - why sparkling water resembles a superheated liquid.

Probably many of you know how to make sparkling water. No, not in an industrial way, but in an ordinary home way - using a siphon and carbon dioxide (CO₂) canisters. Gas from the canister under pressure enters the space above the liquid. In order for it to quickly dissolve in water, a siphon was met. Dissolution of the gas occurs before equilibrium is reached.

If it does not react chemically with the liquid, then the equilibrium content (density, concentration) of the gas in the liquid is proportional to its pressure above the surface.*)

(Other home-made methods for preparing “spongie” are also known - by adding lemon juice, drinking juice, etc. to water.)

When we quickly open a bottle of sparkling water, the gas under pressure above the surface expands rapidly and cools at the same time. The water vapor released along with it condenses, forming a foggy cloud (smoke). After the pressure above the surface decreases significantly, carbon dioxide gas dissolved in water begins to leave it, both through the gas-liquid surface and through the formation of bubbles in the volume of the liquid. At first, after opening the bottle, when the balance is particularly disturbed and there is a greater excess of carbon dioxide in the water, bubbles form easily and there are many of them. Therefore, the liquid begins to foam. Gradually the foam subsides, although there is still “extra” gas in the water. But now the formation of bubbles is difficult - the concentration of gas molecules has become smaller and randomly works together, and it is difficult to immediately form a bubble that would not be crushed by the force of the surface of the tension surface. Bubbles begin to grow only on imperfections in the water, and this is the actual position on the surface of the surface.

A completely unexpected use for sparkling water

We started this article with a story about the bubble chamber. We would like to end it with a story about another application of bubbles, also in the physics of elementary particles.

Several years ago, Soviet physicists studying the properties of neutrinos were faced with the following problem. It was necessary to extract from a large volume of liquid (hundreds of liters) several atoms of gas that were formed in this volume under the influence of neutrino radiation. The gas was radioactive (neon-19), and its atoms decayed in less than one minute. Therefore, literally in seconds it was necessary to find 5-10 gas atoms, known to them, among the $10^{28}$ atoms of the liquid, transport them to the counter and count them! At first glance, the task seemed unsolvable.

The idea of ​​sparkling water came to the rescue. Before irradiation, the liquid was saturated with carbon dioxide. Immediately after irradiation, the volume was opened, and the liquid was vigorously mixed with special blades. Violent boiling occurred, thereby creating a surface through which neon atoms could escape, increasing ten thousand times. The escaping carbon dioxide carried them away and was absorbed in another vessel (at the distance of the lye). The neon was collected in a special test tube and quickly mixed into the counter. The entire process took less than twenty seconds.
