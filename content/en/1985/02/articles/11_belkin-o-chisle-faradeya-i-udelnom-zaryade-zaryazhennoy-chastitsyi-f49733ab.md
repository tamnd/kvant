How does a body “behave” when a couple of forces are applied to it? To answer this question, remember that the center of mass of a body (discussed in § 44 of Physics 8) moves as if the entire mass of the body is concentrated in it and all the forces acting on the body are applied to it (this statement is called in physics the theorem on the movement of the center of mass). But if the sum of the forces is zero, then the center of mass cannot move from its place (if, of course, it dug before applying forces). The axes of rotation passing through the centers of mass of bodies are remarkable because no forces act on them.

On the Faraday number and the specific charge of a charged particle

The phenomenon of the electric saw, known since the late 18th and early 19th centuries, was studied in particular detail by the eminent English physicist M. Faraday. In 1834, an article by Faraday was published, which described the results of experiments that led to the establishment of the electric saw law. In the same article, for the first time, Faradem and what have now become generally accepted. This is an electrode (as well as a cathode and anode), it (a cathode and anode), an electrolyte and the name of the process itself is an electric saw.

The power saw law is now (but not at Faraday's time) written as follows (Physics 9, § 69):

$$m = \frac{1}{F} \frac{M}{n} I \Delta t.$$

Here $m$ is the mass of the substance released on the electrode, $F$ is the Faraday number (constant), $M$ is the molar (or atomic) mass of the substance, $n$ is its valence, $I \Delta t$ is the charge passed through the electric saw.

Note that a quantity that is numerically equal relative to M/n is called *chemical equivalent* in chemistry (that’s what it was called under Farad, although the concept of valency did not yet exist). This value shows what mass of a given substance combines with a mass of hydrogen equal to its atomic mass (or replacing its mass in chemical reactions). For example, for atomic oxygen, the relative mass is 16 and the valence is 2, so its chemical equivalent is 8. To make water from oxygen and hydrogen, for every unit mass of hydrogen there must be a unit mass of oxygen: 1 kg of hydrogen is contained with 8 kg of oxygen, and the result is 9 kg of water.
