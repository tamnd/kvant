When parallel forces are applied to a body

It is well known how to find the equal force of two forces applied to a material point (Fig. 1). On the vectors representing these forces, a parallelgram is constructed as on the sides; the diagonal with an arrow at the end drawn from the point to which the forces are applied is the vector of the equal force.

When two forces are applied not to a material point, but to a body, but as shown in figure 2, then the equal force is determined in the same way. Finding that the point of application of the force can be transferred along the line of its action (“Physics 8”, § 46), they find point C at which the lines of action of both forces intersect.

Assuming that the forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ are applied precisely to this point, they construct a parallelgram and draw a diagonal. True, point C may be outside the body, but then the point is applied

The active one can be chosen anywhere along the line of its action. Indeed, no matter what point is chosen, the equal force $\vec{F}$ will impart the same acceleration to the body or cause the same torque as the forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ taken together.

If the forces are parallel and directed in one direction. Let two parallel coordinated forces (Fig. 3) be applied to the body. The actions of such forces never intersect with a line, and a parallelogram cannot be constructed on them. Nevertheless, it is possible to add up these forces and find their equivalent force.

It is not difficult to understand that the equal force is directed parallel to both forces and its model is equal to the arhythmic sum of the models of the added forces. And at what point is it applied? Or, in other words, to what point of the body must a force be applied, equal in the model, but opposite in the direction of the equal force, so that the body is in equilibrium?

To find the point of application of the equal force of two parallel and identically directed forces, you can use the rule of moments (“Physics 8”, § 48). Let's draw a straight line connecting points A and B (see Fig. 3). Somewhere on this straight line there must obviously be a point of application of the equal force. Let this be point O. Let us assume that a fixed axis passes through this point, perpendicular to the plane containing both added forces (that is, perpendicular to the plane figure). If O really is the point of application of the equilibrium action

veering, then the body will be in equilibrium - the equi-driven one is balanced by the reaction force from the axis. On the other hand, if a body with a fixed axis is in equilibrium, then the algebraic sum of the moments of forces relative to this axis must be equal to zero. From figure 3 it is clear that the force $\vec{F}_2$, if it were the only one, would rotate the body around $O$ clockwise, that is, the moment $F_2 d_2$ is positive, and the force $\vec{F}_1$, if it were the only one, would turn the body counterclockwise - its moment $F_1 d_1$ should be assigned a negative sign (here $d_1$ and $d_2$ — shoulders of forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$).

Hence,

$$F_2 d_2 - F_1 d_1 = 0,$$

or $$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{d_2}{d_1}.$$

From the similarity of triangles AOC and BOD it is found that $d_1/d_2 = r_1/r_2$. Therefore we finally get

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{r_2}{r_1}.$$

This means that the equal leader of two parallel, identically directed forces is applied to a point dividing the segment connecting the points of application of the added forces, in a ratio inverse to the ratio of the force models. It is clear that this point lies close to the larger of the forces.

If the directions of parallel forces are opposite. Parallel forces applied to the body can also be directed in opposite directions (Fig. 4). Now the point of application of the equal leader $\vec{F}$ cannot be located somewhere between the points of application of forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$. After all, around a point lying between them, each force rotates the body pro-
clockwise, the signs of the moments of these forces are the same, and their sum cannot be equal to zero, as required for equilibrium.

It is easy to understand that the point of application of the equi-driver lies behind the point of application of the larger force, as shown in figure 4. The modulus of the equi-driver is equal to the modulus of the difference between the force models $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$.

At what point exactly is the equal leader applied? At what distance $r_2$ from the point of application of the greater force? Let's use the rule of moments again:

$$\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{r_2}{r_1},$$

or $F_2 = F_1 \frac{r_1}{r_2}$.

Let us subtract the value $F_1$ from the right and left sides of the last equality:

$$F_2 - F_1 = F_1 \frac{r_1}{r_2} - F_1 =$$

$$= F_1 \left( \frac{r_1}{r_2} - 1 \right) = F_1 \frac{r_1 - r_2}{r_2},$$

where

$$r_2 = \frac{F_1 \left( r_1 - r_2 \right)}{F_2 - F_1} = \frac{F_1 r}{F_2 - F_1}.$$

Thus, the point of application of the equal leader of two oppositely directed parallel forces is located the further away from the point of application of the larger of them, the smaller the difference in the models of these forces.

A couple of forces. We have seen that if parallel forces, equally or oppositely directed, are applied to a body, then it is always possible to find the modulus and direction of the equileading force of these forces and determine the point of its application. If a force is applied to this point, equal to the equal leader in magnitude, but opposite to it in direction, then the body will be in equilibrium - it will not move translationally and will not rotate.
