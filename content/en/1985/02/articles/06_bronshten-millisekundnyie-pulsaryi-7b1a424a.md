Millisecond pulsars

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. A. BRONSHTEN

As already reported in «Kvant» (1983, no. 12, p. 12), relatively recently scientists discovered two of the fastest (to date) pulsars. One of them - located in the constellation Vulpecula - has a period of approximately 1.6 milliseconds, the other, visible in the direction of the constellation Cygnus, rotates with a period of about 6.1 milliseconds.

Why are these pulsars interesting, and why did they immediately attract the attention of scientists?

According to modern concepts, pulsars are neutron stars with a powerful magnetic field. As the star rotates, it is observed that periodic hits in the region of a directed beam of radio waves record radiation in the form of individual pulses with the period of rotation of the star.

Neutron stars form as a result of the burning of thermonuclear fuel (hydrogen, helium, carbon, etc.) within stars with a mass exceeding the mass of the Sun. First, hydrogen burns out, turning into gels (this process occurs in the depths of the Sun and most stars), then the gels begin to burn out, then carbon, etc. Having only the sources of energy that maintain an equilibrium state, the star experiences catastrophic skepticism (up to a size of the order of 10 km) and, accordingly, the speed of its rotation sharply increases.

But in this way it was possible to prove the existence of pulsars with rotation periods of about one second. The fact of the formation of millisecond pulsars required a special explanation.

This explanation was proposed simultaneously by two groups of researchers - four American scientists led by M. Alpar and, independently of them, individual astrophysicist J. Srinivasan and Dutch astrophysicist E. van den Heuvel. This is the hypothesis they were guided by.

Let's assume that a binary star system once formed in which one star (let's call it A) exceeded the other (component B) in mass. Several million years ago, star A, being more massive, was the first to disappear, become overgrown and began to skim. At the same time, part of the matter began to flow to star B. When star A was completely deprived of sources of thermonuclear energy, it exploded as a supernova, and its remainder formed a pulsar.

Meanwhile, the reserves of nuclear fuel in star B. Terrier have also come to an end, matter begins to flow from it to the pulsar (the remnant of star A), and the rotation of the pulsar can noticeably accelerate. It is possible that after this, star B will suffer the same fate as star A - it will explode and form a young pulsar.

While developing their hypothesis, these scientists did not yet know about the existence of millisecond pulsars. They wanted to explain the properties of other pulsars that are part of binary systems and have fairly rapid rotation - with a period of the order of tens of milliseconds. The discovery of the fastest pulsars reliably confirmed this hypothesis.

It turned out that the pulsar in the constellation Cygnus is indeed part of a binary system. It has been established that the period of formation of this pulsar around the center of mass of the system is 120 days, and its orbit is close to circular. The second component of the system is a white dwarf with a mass approximately one-half that of the Sun.

But how can we explain the existence of the first millisecond pulsar (in the constellation Vulpecula), which is not part of a binary system?

According to Srinivasan Ivan den Heuvel, such a system could be destroyed during the explosion of star B, so that both pulsars - the remnants of stars A and B - scattered in different directions. There are other explanations. It is certain that further research will lead to many interesting things in understanding the nature of these wonderful celestial bodies.

Observations of millisecond pulsars can provide necessary and very important results for studying our planetary system. The fact is that due to the movement of the Earth around the char, the frequencies of studying pulsars change with an annual period. Studies of these annual fluctuations make it possible, more accurately than the classical methods of celestial mechanics, to determine the slightest deviations in the movement of the Earth under the influence of the cauterization of other planets (planetary disturbances), and therefore the mass of the disturbing planets.

And last but not least for science, millisecond pulsars could become a new standard of time.
