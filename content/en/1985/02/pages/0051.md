Question 2. A glass with a small ball (Fig. 2) floats in a vessel with water. How will the water level change if a ball, one made of wood, and another glass, are transferred from a glass to a vessel?

The force of pressure on the bottom of the vessel is equal to that of water, a glass and a ball. If you put a vessel (which for simplicity can be considered weightless) on the scales, they will show the weight of the contents, and their readings will not change whether the ball is in a glass or in a vessel with water. On the other hand, the scale must show the force acting on the bottom of the vessel, which in the initial situation is determined only by the level of water in the vessel.

In the case when a wooden ball is transferred from a glass, it will float on the surface of the water, and the force acting on the bottom of the vessel will, as before, be determined only by the water level. And since this force does not change, the water level must also remain the same.

The result will be different if the ball is made of steel. Such a ball will sink to the bottom of the vessel, and the total pressure force on the bottom will be the sum of the water pressure force and the pressure force of the ball. The total force again should not change, which means the force of water pressure should decrease. Consequently, the water level in this case will decrease.

Question 3. A steel ball floats in the rue. A layer of water is poured over the rue to cover the ball (Fig. 3). How will the depth of the ball's insertion into the root change?

$$f = \left( \varrho_1 g h_1 + \varrho_2 g h_2 \right) \Delta S,$$
