Specific charge of a proton and other charged particles. Let us imagine that an experiment with the passage of current through the electrolyte is carried out in such a way that hydrogen is released at the electrolyte, and its mass is numerically equal to the chemical equivalent. Since hydrogen is monovalent, one mole of hydrogen ions will pass through the electrolyte. The number of these ions is, of course, equal to Avogadro's number, and the charge transferred by them is numerically equal to Faraday's constant. Thus, we can say that the mass of a hydrogen ion is numerically equal to the ratio of the chemical equivalent to the Avogadro number, and the charge of this ion is numerically equal to the ratio of the Faraday constant to the Avogadro number. From here, according to the law of electrolysis, we find that the ratio of the charge of a hydrogen ion to its mass, called the specific charge of the ion, is numerically equal to the Faraday number divided by the atomic mass.

But a hydrogen ion is the nucleus of an atomic hydrogen. It has a special name - proton (hydrogen nuclei are part of the atomic nuclei of all other chemical elements). In SI units, the specific charge of a proton is

$$\frac{q_p}{m_p} = \frac{F/N_A}{M/N_A} = \frac{9,65 \cdot 10^4 \text{ KJ/mоль}}{10^{-3} \text{ кг/mоль}} = 9,65 \cdot 10^7 \text{ KJ/kg}.$$

Why do we pay special attention to the specific charge of the proton here? The fact is that the specific charge of any electrically charged particle is one of its most important characteristics. For example, the speed and acceleration of a particle moving in an electric field depends on it. Let's give two examples.

Let some particle with charge $q$ and mass $m$ move in an electric field with direction $\vec{E}$, so that a force $q\vec{E}$ acts on it. Let's write an equation expressing Newton's second law:

$$m\vec{a} = q\vec{E},$$

from where we obtain for acceleration $\vec{a}$ particles

$$\vec{a} = \frac{q}{m}\vec{E}.$$

We see that the acceleration of a particle is determined not by its charge and not by its mass separately, but by the ratio $q/m$, that is, the specific charge of the particle.

Suppose that a particle having charge $q$ and mass $m$ moves in an electric field from one point to another, the direction between which is $U$. Then the work done by the field on the particle will be equal to $qU$. If at first the particle digs, then due to this work it will acquire kinetic energy $mv^2/2$:

$$qU = \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

From here we get for the particle speed

$$v = \sqrt{2\frac{q}{m}U}$$

— the speed of a particle is again determined by its specific charge.
