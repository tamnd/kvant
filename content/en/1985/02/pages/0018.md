The role of the “walls” of this waveguide is played by a layer of water at those depths where the “turn” of the sound beam occurs.

The depth level $z_m$, at which the speed of sound is minimal, is called the SCP axis. Typically the values ​​of $z_m$ lie in the range of 1000–1200 m; however, in tropical aquifers, where the water warms up to great depths, the PZK axis can drop to depths of 2000 m. On the contrary, in high arid regions, the influence of temperature on the distribution of sound speed is felt only in the near-surface layer, and the PTC axis rises to depths of 200–500 m, and in polar aquifers - even close to the surface.

In the ocean there can be two fundamentally different types of PZK. A channel of the first type is formed when the speed of sound at the water surface ($c_0$) is less than at the bottom

$(c_\pi)$. This case usually occurs in deep-sea areas, where the pressure at the bottom reaches hundreds of atmospheres. The sound coming from the water into the air is well reflected from the surface of their interface (see Appendix), and if the surface of the ocean is smooth (calm), then it serves as a sharp upper boundary of the waveguide and the channel occupies the entire layer of water from the surface to the bottom (figure 4).

Let's see what part of the sound rays is “captured” in the PZK. To do this, we rewrite the correspondence we obtained earlier (*) in this form:

$$c(z) \cdot \cos \varphi_1 = c_1 \cdot \cos \varphi(z),$$

where $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi(z)$ are the angles formed by the sound beam with the horizontal plane at depths $z_1$ and $z$, respectively. These angles are called sliding angles. (The concept that $\varphi_1 = \pi/2 - a_1$, $\varphi(z) = \pi/2 - a(z)$.) If the sound source is on the axis of the PZK, then $c_1 = c_m$; the channel occupies rays for which the grazing angle at the bottom is $\varphi(z) = 0$. So all rays that leave the source at grazing angles $\varphi_1$ satisfy the conditions (see figure 4)

$$\cos \varphi_1 \geq \frac{c_m}{c_\pi},$$

fall into the PZK.

When the surface of the water is new, sound rays will scatter on it; those rays that leave the surface at sufficiently large grazing angles must reach the bottom and be absorbed there.
