In conclusion, we outline two more proofs of the theorem, intended for readers familiar with vectors and geometric transformations.

1. For any point D of the segment $AB\cdot CD=λ\vec{A}+(1-λ)\vec{B}$, where λ=DB:AB; 1-λ=AD:AB. Indeed, $\vec{CD}=\vec{CB}+\vec{BD}=\vec{CB}+\lambda\vec{BA}=\vec{CB}+\lambda(\vec{CA}-\vec{CB})=\lambda\vec{CA}+(1-λ)\vec{CB}$.

In our case, the vector $\vec{CD}$ is parallel to the vector $(1/CA)\cdot\vec{CA}+(1/CB)\cdot\vec{CB}$ (see Fig. 7) and therefore $(1/CA):(1/CB)=λ:(1-λ)$, whence AD:DB=CA:CB.

About Lagrange's theorem

First of all, let us recall the formulation of Lagrange’s theorem.

Let the function f be differentiable at each point of some interval. Then between any two points a and b of this interval there is a point c such that

$$f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a).$$
