We emphasize that the reflection of a wave propagating in a waveguide from its boundaries is a certain property of the waveguide - it is thanks to it that the wave energy does not propagate in all directions in space, but is transmitted in a given direction.

Relatively recently, about thirty years ago, scientists from the USSR and the USA discovered that nature itself had created giant acoustic waveguides, which in size were significantly larger than even the imaginary speaking tube between Moscow and Leningrad. Such waveguides, when determining conditions, arise in the oceans. Scientists called them underwater sound canals, or SSCs for short. Sound waves propagating through such channels can be received thousands of kilometers from their source. Thus, in one of the most successful experiments, the sound from an underwater explosion produced by scientists off the coast of Australia covered half the globe underwater and was recorded by another group of researchers near the Bermuda Islands, at a distance of 19,600 km from Australia (this is still a record for the range of propagation of pulsed sound signals.

What is the mechanism of formation of such a waveguide? What in it serves as reflected boundaries that cause sound waves to propagate great distances across the column? The upper boundary is immediately set to the ocean surface, which reflects sound quite well (see Appendix). However, she is rarely new due to constant excitement; and this leads to chaotic scattering of sound waves on it. The bottom reflects sound weakly and cannot serve as the lower reflected boundary of the waveguide. Therefore, the boundaries of the waveguide in the ocean should be sought somewhere between the house and the surface. And they were found. These boundaries turned out to be layers of water at certain ocean depths.

How does the reflection of sound waves from the “walls” of the shut-off valve occur? To answer this question, we will have to consider how sound propagates in the ocean in general.

Until now, we have been talking about waveguides, silently assuming that the speed of sound propagation in them is constant. Meanwhile, it is well known that the speed of sound in the ocean ranges from 1450 m/s to 1540 m/s. It is related to the temperature of the water, its salinity, the magnitude of hydroelectric pressure and other factors. An increase in hydroelectric pressure leads to the fact that when diving for every 100 m, the speed of sound increases by 1.6 m/s. As temperature increases, the speed of sound also increases. However, in the ocean, the water temperature, as a rule, drops quite sharply as it moves away from the upper, well-heated layers of water into the depths, where it reaches an almost constant value. The effectiveness of these two mechanisms leads to such a dependence of the speed of sound on depth, as shown in figure 1. Near the surface, the predominant influence is the rapid distribution of temperature - in these complex ones, the speed of sound decreases with increasing depth. As you dive, the temperature changes more and more slowly, and the hydroelectric pressure continues to increase. At some depths, these two factors “balance” - at this depth the speed of sound is minimal; and then it begins to increase with increasing depth due to an increase in hydroelectric pressure.
