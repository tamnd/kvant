⟦folio 28⟧

does the body “give” by emitting light? What it has is light corresponding to its own frequencies of vibration. Therefore, there must be a close connection between the body's ability to emit light and its ability to absorb it. And this connection is simple: the more a body emits, the more it absorbs. In this case, naturally, the brightest emitter should be a black body, which absorbs vibrations of all frequencies. This connection was established mathematically in 1859 by the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff.

Let us call the emissivity of a body the energy emitted by a single area of ​​its surface per unit time, and denote it by $E_{\lambda,T}$. It is different for different lengths ($\lambda$) and different temperatures ($T$), hence the indices $\lambda$ and $T$. The absorption capacity of a body is the ratio of the light energy absorbed by the body per unit time to the incident energy. Let's denote it by $A_{\lambda,T}$ - it is also different for different $\lambda$ and $T$.

Kirchhoff's law states that the ratio of emissive and absorptive abilities is the same for all bodies:

$$\frac{E_{\lambda,T}}{A_{\lambda,T}} = C.$$

The value $C$ does not depend on the nature of the bodies, but depends on the wavelength of light and on temperature: $C=f(\lambda,T)$. According to Kirchhoff's law, a body that absorbs better at a given temperature should radiate more intensely.

Absolutely black body. Kirchhoff's law is valid for all bodies. This means that it can be applied to a body that absorbs all wavelengths without exception. Such a body is called completely black. For it, the absorption capacity is equal to unity, so Kirchhoff’s law takes the form

$$E_{\lambda,T} = C = f(\lambda,T).$$

Thus, the meaning of the function $f(\lambda,T)$ becomes clear: it is equal to the emissivity of an absolute black body. The problem of finding the function $C=f(\lambda,T)$ turned into the problem of finding the dependence of the radiation energy of an absolute black body on temperature and wavelength. Finally, after two decades of back and forth, it was solved. Its solution, given by the German physicist Max Planck, became the beginning of a new physics - quantum physics.

Note that absolutely black bodies do not exist in nature. Further, the blackest of all known substances - sakha - absorbs not 100, but 98% of the light falling on it. Therefore, an artificial device was used to experimentally study blackbody radiation.

It turned out that the properties of an absolute black body are possessed by... a closed cavity with a small response (see Fig.ok). In fact, when a ray of light enters the response, it experiences many successive reflections inside the cavity, so that it has very little chance of leaving the response in violation. (For the same reason, an open window in a house seems dark even on a bright sunny day.) If such a body is heated, then the radiation emanating from the response is practically no different from the radiation of an absolutely black body.

A good imitation of an absolute black body can also be a pipe, one end of which is closed. If the work is heated, its opening end shines like an absolutely black body. At the same temperature, it looks completely black, like the material in the cavity.
