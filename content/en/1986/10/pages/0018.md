Creating such high pressures to match the high temperatures proved difficult. Attempts by the American scientist P. Bridgman, the founder of high-pressure physics, to obtain diamonds in the laboratory ended in failure in 1940. Only in 1955 did a report appear in the press that a group of American scientists led by F. Bundy had obtained the first diamonds.

In the Soviet Union, the problems of obtaining artificial diamonds were solved in 1960 by a team of employees of the Institute of High Pressure Physics of the USSR Academy of Sciences, headed by Academician L.F. Vereshagin. In 1961, 2000 carats (1 carat = 2·10⁻⁴ kg) of diamonds were produced, and now our industry produces hundreds of thousands of carats of diamonds per year.

Special equipment has been developed for the industrial production of artificial diamonds. Before getting acquainted with the principles of its operation, let's talk about ways to create high pressure.

Let's mentally perform the following experiment. Pour water into a thick-walled steel glass with an internal diameter of 10 cm and insert a piston with a seal. By pushing the piston into the glass, we will skim the water. To reduce its volume by 18%, we will have to apply a huge force to the piston - 7.8 MH!

In this case, the pressure in the water will be equal to 1 GPa, which is 10,000 times more atmospheric. At this pressure and room temperature, the water will solidify. In the pressure range of 1-2 GPa, all liquids and steel pistons initially collapse, since the mechanical stresses in them exceed the tensile strength of steel.

Higher pressures can be obtained if solids.

Let us apply skimming forces to two opposite grams of a cube of solid matter. Then, inside the cube, in a flow perpendicular to the applied forces, a mechanical stress $\sigma = F/S$ will develop, where $F$ is the magnitude of the force, $S$ is the area of ​​the cube. When this stress exceeds the tensile strength of the cube's pitched materials, it collapses. But if each pair of opposite grams of the cube is simultaneously acted upon by strictly identical skimming forces, then identical stresses $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, $\sigma_3$ will arise in the cube in three mutually perpendicular sections, and even under the highest skimming conditions the cube will not collapse. This uniform state creates all-round pressure $p = \sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \sigma_3$ in the internal regions of the cube.

The cubic high-pressure apparatus shown in figure 4 operates on this principle. There are six specially shaped parts called poochsons, the properties of which are simultaneous to the center in those mutually perpendicular directions. The punches remove the container - a cube of plastic material placed in the center of the apparatus. The puchsonars are pressed using six pistons of a hydraulic press. The press is adjusted in such a way that the forces acting on different faces of the cube are always equal, and all-round pressure is created in the cube.

Often simpler devices are used, consisting of two puccsonaras with holes that skim a container shaped like a lentil (figure 5).

The most powerful hydraulic press for scientific research, developing up to 500 MH, has a high multi-story building. It is used to create high pressures in large volume devices.
