5. Let us assume the opposite: the numbers 1, 2, ..., 3n are divided into three groups $A, B, C$ such that the choice indicated is impossible. Let $a, b, c$ be the smallest elements in the groups, and let $a < b < c$ be the smallest elements. Then $a = 1, b \geq 2$. If $b = 2$ then $c \neq 3$ and therefore $c \geq 4$. If $b > 2$, then $c \geq b + 1 \geq 4$, that is, in any case $c \geq 4$. Let us denote by $s$ the smallest (in terms of abscylotic value) difference between neighboring members of the sequence formed from numbers of the group $C$, arranged in ascending order. Let's consider three cases.

a) $s \geq 3$. Then, of the numbers $3k - 2, 3k - 1, 3k$, where $k = 1, 2, ..., n$, no more than one is included in $C$, and none of the numbers 1, 2, 3 is included in $C$. Therefore, $C$ contains at most $n - 1$ numbers. Contradiction.

b) $s = 2$. Let $c'$ be an arbitrary number from $C$. Then $c' - 1 \notin C$. Since $1 \in A$ and $(c' - 1) + 1 = c'$, then $c' - 1 \notin B$. Therefore, $c' - 1 \in A$, that is, $A$ contains $n$ numbers of the form $c' - 1$, where $c' \in C$. Since $1 \in A$, and $c' - 1 \geq 3$ for any $c' \in C$, then $A$ contains at least $n + 1$ numbers. Contradiction.

b) $s = 1$. Let us choose the average of numbers $c$ belonging to $C$ and such that $c + 1$ also belongs to $C$, the smallest number $c$. Let's look at the (natural!) numbers $c - b$ and $c - b + 1$ and prove that they form $C$ (thereby contradicting the choice $c$). Obviously $b - 1 \in A$. Since $(c - b + 1) + (b - 1) = c$, then $c - b + 1 \notin B$. Since $(c - b + 1) + b = c + 1$, where $c + 1 \in C$ due to the choice of $c$, then $c - b + 1 \notin A$. Therefore, $c - b + 1 \in C$. It is proved in a similar way that $c - b \in C$.
