It is obvious that the American physicist “missed” the defectiveness of the foils used in his experiments. We do not know the exact conditions of the experiment in which he viewed them in the light, but some plausible assumptions can be made about them and try to estimate the size of the response that Hartig could see.

The ability to see a simple result is determined by the amount of light energy that passes into the pupil. Having made natural assumptions about the strength of the light source illuminating the response, and knowing the threshold color value of the eye, we will estimate the size of the response that can still be seen.

It has been experimentally established that under conditions of continuous illumination, the magnitude of the energy flux corresponding to the critical threshold of a good dark-adapted eye is approximately 5·10⁻¹⁷ J/s. An electric light bulb converts approximately 2% of the electrical energy supplied to it into light energy perceived by the eye. Therefore, a 40-watt lamp—the kind that was most common in the mid-1920s—emits 0.8 joules of light energy every second. Assuming that this energy is emitted evenly in all directions, he will calculate that part of it that passes through the tap in the screen (diameter of tap d) located at a distance l from the light bulb. The area of ​​such a response is π²/4, and the solid angle allocated by it from a sphere of radius l is π²/4l².
