D. Fahrenheit and his thermometers

This year marks the 300th anniversary of the birth of Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit (1686–1736), the creator of the first thermometers that could be used as standard and reliable laboratory instruments.

It is believed that the idea of ​​using the property of thermal expansion of air and liquid, known since ancient times, for temperature measurements belongs to G. Galily. At the end of the 17th century, he invented a simple device based on this principle - a thermopop, which for the first time made it possible to judge temperature changes objectively to some extent. Galileo's thermoscope is a small glass flask filled with air, with a very narrow and long pot lowered into a vessel with water. As the flask was heated or left, the air in it expanded or contracted and the water level decreased or increased accordingly.

During the next, 17th century, many scientists were engaged in mastering this device. So, in 1615 it was equipped with a measuring scale and from 1628 all parts were no longer called a thermoscope, but a thermometer. In 1630, air was replaced by more convenient liquids,

primarily water thermometers. A decade later, they began to be sealed so that the readings would not be affected by fluctuations in atmospheric pressure. Since the late 50s, students from the Florentine “Academy of Experience” have most often used wine alcohol as a thermometric liquid, which does not freeze in the most severe frosts and has a low viscosity and a relatively high coefficient of thermal expansion.

Since the 80s of the 17th century, the idea of ​​constructing a temperature scale based on two “constant” points became increasingly attractive (with this method, there was no need for the very difficult operation of determining the volumes of the reservoir and capillary for each thermometer).

So, in 1701, I. Newoton built a thermometer (with linseed oil as a liquid), on the scale of which he put 12 equal divisions, with the zero division corresponding to the freezing temperature of water, and the “12” division to the normal temperature of the human body. At the same time, he began to produce his own temperature scales; he took the freezing and boiling temperatures of water. In 1708, Römer visited Fahrenheit, which the Dane introduced in every detail to his thermometer calibrations. Apparently, Fahrenheit immediately realized that this method contained a solution to the long-standing problem of creating standard thermometers that gave “consistent” readings (under the same conditions they showed the same temperature).
