Optical instruments

L. P. BAKANINA

There are many different optical instruments, and each of them is recognized in one way or another to “help” our eye. For example, a telescope and binoculars allow you to view very distant objects, while a magnifying glass and microscope are used to view very small objects. A camera makes it possible to capture an image on film, and a projection device allows you to get an enlarged image of this film on a screen, which can be seen by more people at once. Glasses compensate for various vision deficiencies in people. Some of the listed devices can be found in this country.

The basic design of these devices is very simple - their optical system consists of one or two lenses. How many and what kind of lenses are used depends on the purpose of the specific device.

Glasses

Our eye has the ability to accommodate, that is, “tune in” to viewing various distant objects. For a normal eye, the area of ​​accommodation ranges from infinitely large distances to distances of the order of 10 cm (this distance increases with age). In the first case, the eye manages to obtain an image on the retina without any muscular conditions.

A person suffering from myopia cannot focus the image of distant objects on the retina, and a person suffering from farsightedness is unable to see objects close to the eye. Simple glasses with spherical lenses allow correction

test myopia or farsightedness of the eye by “bringing closer” or “moving away” objects relative to the eye. Obviously, in the first case, diverging lenses are used, and in the second, converging lenses.

It is common to distinguish between glasses “for distance” and glasses “for reading.” The first ones “transfer” objects located at an infinitely large distance to the far border of the accommodation region of a given eye. The latter “transfer” objects from the standard distance for a normal eye to the best vision of $d_0=25$ cm to the distance to the best vision of a myopic or farsighted eye.

Task 1. A myopic person best distinguishes small print located at a distance of $d=15$ cm from the eye. What kind of reading glasses does this person need?

We assume that the glasses are located close to the eye, and the person holds the book at a distance of $d_0=25$ cm from the eye for the best vision (Fig. 1). According to the lens formula

$$\frac{1}{d} + \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{F},$$

find the optical power of the glasses:

$$D = \frac{1}{F} = \frac{d-d_0}{d_0d} \approx -2,7 \text{ дитр.}$$

Problem 2. What glasses does a person need whose best vision distance is $d=75$ cm?

Just as in the previous problem, we assume that it is convenient for a person to hold a book at a distance of $d_0=25$ cm, and should receive an image with glasses at a distance of $d=75$ cm (Fig. 2). Using the lens formula, we find that the optical power of the glasses is

$$D = \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{d_0} - \frac{1}{d} = \frac{d-d_0}{d_0d} = +2,7 \text{ дитр.}$$

Microscope

The goal that is set in the design of this device is to obtain the greatest possible magnification of the image of small objects.

A microscope consists of two collecting lenses - an objective and an eyepiece. The lens facing the object gives a real magnification of the image of the object, and the eyepiece located directly in front of the eye is observed as

As can be seen from figure 5, the image in the microscope is enlarged and inverted. Let's find its angular magnification $\gamma$ - the ratio of the visual angle $\varphi$ on an object through a microsop to the visual angle $\varphi_0$ when observed with the naked eye: $\gamma = \varphi / \varphi_0$. If the size of an object is $h$ and it is viewed by the eye from a distance of best vision $d_0$, then

$$\varphi_0 = \frac{h}{d_0}.$$

Now let's find $\varphi$.

The magnification of a lens system is always equal to the product of the magnification of each of the lenses included in this system. In order for the lens magnification to be as close to the focus as possible, we can therefore assume that $d_1 = F_1$. The image produced by the lens is the subject of the eyepiece. If the eye is accommodated to infinity, then the “object” must be in the focal plane of the eyepiece, that is, $d_2 = F_2$. From figure 5 we get

$$\varphi = \frac{H}{F_2} = \frac{h(L - F_1 - F_2)}{F_1}.$$

Obviously, it makes sense to use short focal length lenses as both the objective and the eyepiece. Then

$$\varphi = \frac{hL}{F_1F_2},$$

and the increase in microsop in these approximations can be considered equal to

$$\gamma = \frac{\varphi}{\varphi_0} = \frac{Ld_0}{F_1F_2}.$$

Based on this formula, one might think that by increasing the distance between the lenses $L$ and bringing the object closer to the focus of the lens, we can obtain any angular value greater than the magnification of the microscope. However, this is not true. At sufficiently large distances between the lens and the image, it is no longer possible to use approximate constructions of geometric optics; it is necessary to take into account the wave properties of light and, in particular, the phenomenon of diffraction, which leads to distortion of the image and even to the fact that the image is not obtained at all. From a consideration of diffraction phenomena, it can be concluded that the minimum size of an object that can be viewed in a microscope without distortion must be of the order of the light wavelength. For visible light the magnitude is $l_{\min} \sim 0,5$ µm, and the maximum possible magnification microsop is $\sim 10^3$.

Problem 5. A microscope has objects with a focal length of $F_1 = 1$ cm and an eyepiece with a focal length of $F_2 = 3$ cm, the distance between $L = 20$ cm. At what distance from the focus of the lens should an object be so that its image is obtained at a distance of best vision of $d_0 = 25$ cm from the eyepiece? What is the magnification of the microsop?

We will assume that the image produced by the lens is close to the focus of the eyepiece. Then the distance from the lens to the image is $f_1 = L - F_2 = 17$ cm, and the required distance

$$x = d_1 - F_1 = \frac{f_1F_1}{f_1 - F_1} - F_1 = \frac{F_1^2}{L - F_1 - F_2} \approx 0,06 \text{ см.}$$

The magnification of the microscope is equal to the approximation

$$\gamma = \frac{(L - F_2)d_0}{F_1F_2} \approx 130.$$

Spotting scopes. Telecop

Telescopes designed for observing distant objects are divided into two groups: 1) a state of two collecting lenses - an “astronomical telescope”, or Kepler telescope; 2) a state of converging and diverging lenses - “earthly tube”, or Galileo’s tube. Both pipes can be used as a telescope. A lens system is called telescopic if a parallel beam of rays entering it also exits parallel, but at a different angle to the optical axis. The angular magnification of the telescope is the ratio of the angles at which the outgoing and incoming parallel beams of light are located: $\gamma = \varphi_2 / \varphi_1$.

As a rule, when viewing distant objects using a telescope, the eye is observed to be accommodated to infinity, but there are exceptions.
