Once again about a spoon in a stream of water

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. N. LUZIN

Let's take an ordinary deep plate, pour water into it and place it in the kitchen sink. Place a light aluminum spoon on the edge of the plate so that when empty it floats and when filled with water it sinks.

Hold the floating spoon by the handle and direct a stream of water at it. The stream should be such that only a thin layer of water remains in the spoon. Let's stop holding the spoon - but it doesn't sink, although a vertical stream of water strongly presses on it. The experiment is easily successful if the jet is even, smooth, and without visible hang-ups. (This is exactly the stream that usually flows from a kitchen faucet.)

The experiment looks more effective if a flat disk is taken together with the spoons, for example a metal lid from a tin can. The lid with sealing rubber floats even more stable. You can also use a carefully cut out hard tin can as a disk.

A floating disk will not “run away” to the side from under the flow of water if in its center there is a depression no larger in “height” (1-2 mm) and diameter (10-20 mm). Such indentations are almost always found on the lids and bottoms of cans. If there is no indentation, you can easily make one by placing the disc on a hockey puck and tapping the center with a hammer.

Why doesn't the disk (and spoon) sink?

Watch for a while and you will see that a water roller appears on the surface of the disk, which the water spreading in a thin layer drives further and further to the edges of the disk. As a result, something similar to an empty vessel is formed, the house of which is a disk, and the walls are a water roller. It is understood that the larger the volume of such a “vessel”, the more steadily it will float. Experience shows that the disc is especially stable in a stream of warm water. (Practical advice - if you lower the specified disk into a bathtub filled with water, the water will make much less noise.)

Now we suggest you turn your hard drive into a floating one and at the same time a rotating turbine. To do this, cut the edge of the disk into identical “petals” and bend each of them on one side. Is it possible to say in advance whether your turbine will rotate clockwise or counterclockwise? Try also making a toy boat with water sides, shown schematically at figure. With the help of a pump and inclined pipes, water is sucked out of the vessel in which the boat floats, under pressure it is supplied to the center of the disk and spreads over it in a thin layer. And then everything will happen exactly the same as with a regular disk.
