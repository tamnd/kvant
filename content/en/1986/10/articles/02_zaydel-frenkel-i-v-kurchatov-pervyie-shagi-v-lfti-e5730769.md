I. V. Kurchatov: first steps in LPTI

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. N. ZAIDEL,
Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. Y. FRANKEL

When, having read the biography of the outstanding Soviet scientist and organizer of science Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov (1903-1960), you ask yourself: what capacious word can be used to define his individuality? - seems most appropriate: rapid entry into a new, independent field of science - this is characteristic of both mature and modern Kurchatov. In 1923, he graduated from Simferopol University and in the same year, in less than five months, he completed a study of the radioactivity of snow, mastering both the fine experimental technical measurements of radioactivity and the set of geophysical information necessary to carry out and comprehend this work, which became the subject of his first scientific publication.

Six months pass, Kurchatov finds himself at a meteorological station in Fedosia, where he studies the physics of the sea. It took the young man (he was barely 21 years old) four months to conduct a complex study of fluctuations in the level of the Azov and Black Seas. Already in the early 30s, when Kurchatov’s name was known to a relatively narrow circle of colleagues, these works were included in the classic works on marine physics.

October 1, 1925. In the archives of the Physico-Technical Institute named after. A.F. Ioffe of the USSR Academy of Sciences (PTI) has preserved a record dated to this date: Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov (note: who by this time had managed to complete and publish five scientific works) is enrolled in the Institute as an employee of the first category (this value approximately corresponds to the theoretical position of a senior researcher). And on November 16 of the same year, Kurchatov, together with K. D. Sinelnikov (1901-1966), with whom they studied at Simferopol University, dated the completion of the first “On the question of the passage of electrins through thin metal foils.” The work is immediately translated into English, and both its versions - Russian and English - are sent to print: in the "Proceedings of the Leningrad Physics and Technology Laboratory" and in the American journal "Physical Review". Both articles were published the following year, 1926 - 60 years ago.

The research undertaken by young scientists from the Physicotechnical Institute was of both purely scientific and applied interest. Determining the degree of permeability of thin foils for electrins could contribute to the assimilation of Brown's works - as oscilloscopes were called at that time. The sensitivity of the phosphors that coated the screen of the Brown tube was insufficient for successful photographing of those traced by the electric beam. Therefore, photographic plastic or film was placed inside an evacuated tube, and its movement was carried out quite difficult, using a special device (the so-called vacuum sluice). An idea arose: to bring the electric beam through the end of the tube covered with thin metal foil directly outside, outside the vacuum volume, and place photoplastic there. To do this, it was necessary to find out how permeable the English version is with low-energy electrons of metal foil. The work of the American scientist G. Harttag, published in 1925 on the pages of the “Physical Review”, was related to this issue (this is why physical socialists needed to send the English version with low-energy electrons to metal foils). From the results obtained by Garttier, it followed that electrons, accelerated even in small fields of the order of several volts, freed aluminum foils up to 10 microns thick. It seemed to the countries.

Kurchatov and Sinelnikov decided to check Harttag's work. They repeated his experiment in a similar setup: foil was placed in the path of the electrins escaping from the cathode; if electrons “pierce” the foil, then they fall on the anode, and a current flows through the circuit. These results are a consequence of the imperfections of the foils - the beginning of invisible reactions in them. (Hartig was convinced of the perfection of his foils by holding them up to the light.) With the help of a simple setup, the Leningraders discovered that foils that were “defect-free by eye” could actually contain answers. The existence of such responses was proven by observing bubbles in liquids formed when air was released through the foil. This simple experiment was decisive proof of the fallacy of Harting's results. The diagram of the experiment carried out by the Leningrad authors is shown at figure. Using defect-free foils processed using such a setup, Kartatov and Sinelnikov made sure that slow electrons did not pierce the target foil; By making small responses in defect-free foil, they physically reproduced Harting’s results.

It is obvious that the American physicist “missed” the defectiveness of the foils used in his experiments. We do not know the exact conditions of the experiment in which he viewed them in the light, but some plausible assumptions can be made about them and try to estimate the size of the response that Hartig could see.

The ability to see a simple result is determined by the amount of light energy that passes into the pupil. Having made natural assumptions about the strength of the light source illuminating the response, and knowing the threshold color value of the eye, we will estimate the size of the response that can still be seen.

It has been experimentally established that under conditions of continuous illumination, the magnitude of the energy flux corresponding to the critical threshold of a good dark-adapted eye is approximately 5·10⁻¹⁷ J/s. An electric light bulb converts approximately 2% of the electrical energy supplied to it into light energy perceived by the eye. Therefore, a 40-watt lamp—the kind that was most common in the mid-1920s—emits 0.8 joules of light energy every second. Assuming that this energy is emitted evenly in all directions, he will calculate that part of it that passes through the tap in the screen (diameter of tap d) located at a distance l from the light bulb. The area of ​​such a response is π²/4, and the solid angle allocated by it from a sphere of radius l is π²/4l². From here, for the flow of light energy passing through the connection, we obtain the values

$$\Phi_d = \frac{0,8}{4\pi} \frac{\pi d^2}{4l^2} = 5 \cdot 10^{-2} \frac{d^2}{l^2} \text{ Вт.}$$

Taking l=0.1 m, we have:

$$\Phi_d = 5d^2 \text{ Вт},$$

where d is expressed in meters. We can assume that a response of a very small diameter in the screen (examined through the foil) radiates uniformly within a hemisphere, that is, a solid angle of 2π (this will result in

$$\Phi_{3p} = \frac{\Phi_d}{2\pi} \frac{\pi d_{3p}^2}{4r^2} = 0,625 \frac{d_{3p}^2}{r^2} d^2 \text{ Вт.}$$

Taking the diameter of the pupil of the adapted eye to be equal to 0.005 m, for the power entering it we find:

$$\Phi_{3p} = 0,625 \frac{25 \cdot 10^{-6}}{0,625 \cdot 10^{-1}} d^2 = 2,5 \cdot 10^{-4} d^2 \text{ Вт.}$$

Equating it to the threshold power $5 \cdot 10^{-17}$ W, we obtain a correspondence for determining $d: 2,5 \cdot 10^{-4} d^2 = 5 \cdot 10^{-17}$, from which $d \approx 0,5 \cdot 10^{-6}$ m = 0.5 µm.

So, the maximum size of the response that can be seen in the light is 0.5 microns.

It is interesting to calculate the pressure that would provide a “pushing” (blowing) effect in the foil, that is, it would give a picture of bubbles in the vessel observed by Kurchatov and Sinelnikov (see figure).

It is known that the surface of a liquid behaves like a stretched film (it has surface tension). Therefore, in order to produce, for example, a soap bubble, it is necessary to create excess pressure in it, determined by Laplace’s formula:

$$\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma'}{R},$$

where $\sigma' = 2\sigma$ is the doubled surface tension coefficient of the liquid (the film has two surfaces), $R$ is the radius of the bubble. If you place a tube in a liquid to a depth of $h$, then in order to blow out a bubble with its help, you need to create in it a pressure exceeding atmospheric pressure by an amount equal to the sum of Laplace pressure $\Delta p$ and hydrostatic pressure $\rho gh$. The size of the bubbles is of the order of the bubble diameter, and if it is small, then the Laplace pressure is significantly greater than the hydrostatic pressure, which in this case can be neglected. Considering the bubble to be semi-ferrous, the beatable pressure can then be found by the formula

$$\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma}{R} = \frac{4\sigma}{d} \quad (*)$$

(an air bubble in a liquid has one surface). The same formula can be used to estimate the excess pressure that needs to be created at the surface of the foil immersed in water so that the product is exposed to it through the surface; it is clear that this pressure will be less, the smaller the surface of the liquid tension.
