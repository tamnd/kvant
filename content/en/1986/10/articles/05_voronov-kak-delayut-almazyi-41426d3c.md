How are diamonds made?

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
F. F. VORONOV

Since time immemorial, diamonds have attracted people with the beauty of their intricate crystals, their bright brilliance and their seductive creativity. Large diamonds decorated the associated statuses, the scepters of kings, and the weapons of nobles. They were given names, they were bought for fabulous money, they lived for a long time, and they were used to resolve state conflicts.

Since ancient times, man has sought to obtain a diamond in a laboratorium. Achemists ground and calcined mixtures of sulfur, mercury and other substances in “double heat” - but to no avail.

Only after alchemy was replaced by practical chemistry with its quantitative research methods in the 16th and 17th centuries, the nature of diamond was discovered.

In 1797, the Englishman S. Tennant established that diamond, when burned in oxygen, completely transforms into carbon dioxide. From this it followed that diamond consists of carbon atoms. It became clear: in order to get a diamond, it is necessary to cut it from graphite or other carbon-containing substances.

But how do you get carbon atoms to form a diamond crystal? What conditions are needed for this?

Scientists knew that diamond, stable at ordinary temperatures, turns into graphite when heated without access to air. This suggested that the reverse transformation should also be sought at high temperatures. But how to cause this transformation, how to make the graphite turn into a diamond?

From a comparison of the densities of coal (1.3 × 10³ kg/m³), graphite (2.2 × 10³ kg/m³) and diamond (3.5 × 10³ kg/m³), it followed that to obtain a diamond, coal and graphite must be compacted, or high pressure must be applied.

Moissan decided to use his high-temperature furnace to produce diamonds. He made a hole in the bottom of the oven and placed a vessel with water under it. In the furnace, the iron was melted, saturated with carbon, and droplets of this molten iron saturated with carbon fell into cold water. The outer layers of the droplet quickly cooled and, compressing, exerted enormous pressure on the inner layer of the iron, which had not yet had time to cool. The resulting iron balls dissolved in acids. Small crystals of more or less regular shape up to 0.5-0.7 mm (figure 2) were found inside the balls. These crystals were very hard, and chemical analysis - digestion in oxygen - showed that they consisted of pure carbon.

Four crystals were black, two were yellowish, and the rest were colorless. The crystals with black dots were very full of Brazilian diamonds. One was exceptionally regular in shape. The crystal of the “pure water” of Moissan is called “regenote”.

The production of admases in the laboratory caused a peaceful sensation. However, numerous attempts by other scientists to repeat Moissan's experiments were unsuccessful. Only the German professor Ruff managed to obtain a small crystal measuring 0.05 mm, which was recognized as a diamond based on its specific gravity, glow in ultraviolet rays and combustion in oxygen.

There are other known scientists involved in “diamond creation”. N.V. Karazin in Russia (1823), Cagnard-Latour and Gannalem in France (1829) by “burning coal” obtained crystals similar to diamonds, but they turned out to be carbides - compounds of metals with carbon. In Russia, K. D. Khrushchev (1893) tried to obtain diamonds by crystallizing carbon from molten silver at atmospheric pressure, and also obtained carbide crystals.

All this was Japanese blind.

For the first time, the conditions for the formation of diamonds were determined in the works of the Soviet physicist O.I. Leypunsky, who in 1931 calculated the pressures and temperatures necessary for the transformation of graphite → diamond.

Commenting on his calculation, O.I. Leypunsky wrote: “Firstly, it is necessary to heat the graph no less than to 2000 degrees so that the carbon atoms can move from place to place. Secondly, it must be compressed with sufficient pressure, no less than 60 thousand atmospheres [≈6 GPa]. Then it will certainly turn into a diamond, just as a stone thrown by hand will certainly rise from the ground into the air.” The equilibrium curve between the grapher and the diamond, calculated by O.I. Only at a temperature of 3000 K and a pressure of 12 GPa was it possible to obtain original wireless diamond crystals from graphite.

Creating such high pressures to match the high temperatures proved difficult. Attempts by the American scientist P. Bridgman, the founder of high-pressure physics, to obtain diamonds in the laboratory ended in failure in 1940. Only in 1955 did a report appear in the press that a group of American scientists led by F. Bundy had obtained the first diamonds.

In the Soviet Union, the problems of obtaining artificial diamonds were solved in 1960 by a team of employees of the Institute of High Pressure Physics of the USSR Academy of Sciences, headed by Academician L.F. Vereshagin. In 1961, 2000 carats (1 carat = 2·10⁻⁴ kg) of diamonds were produced, and now our industry produces hundreds of thousands of carats of diamonds per year.

Special equipment has been developed for the industrial production of artificial diamonds. Before getting acquainted with the principles of its operation, let's talk about ways to create high pressure.

Let's mentally perform the following experiment. Pour water into a thick-walled steel glass with an internal diameter of 10 cm and insert a piston with a seal. By pushing the piston into the glass, we will skim the water. To reduce its volume by 18%, we will have to apply a huge force to the piston - 7.8 MH!

In this case, the pressure in the water will be equal to 1 GPa, which is 10,000 times more atmospheric. At this pressure and room temperature, the water will solidify. In the pressure range of 1-2 GPa, all liquids and steel pistons initially collapse, since the mechanical stresses in them exceed the tensile strength of steel.

Higher pressures can be obtained if solids.

Let us apply skimming forces to two opposite grams of a cube of solid matter. Then, inside the cube, in a flow perpendicular to the applied forces, a mechanical stress $\sigma = F/S$ will develop, where $F$ is the magnitude of the force, $S$ is the area of ​​the cube. When this stress exceeds the tensile strength of the cube's pitched materials, it collapses. But if each pair of opposite grams of the cube is simultaneously acted upon by strictly identical skimming forces, then identical stresses $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, $\sigma_3$ will arise in the cube in three mutually perpendicular sections, and even under the highest skimming conditions the cube will not collapse. This uniform state creates all-round pressure $p = \sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \sigma_3$ in the internal regions of the cube.

The cubic high-pressure apparatus shown in figure 4 operates on this principle. There are six specially shaped parts called poochsons, the properties of which are simultaneous to the center in those mutually perpendicular directions. The punches remove the container - a cube of plastic material placed in the center of the apparatus. The puchsonars are pressed using six pistons of a hydraulic press. The press is adjusted in such a way that the forces acting on different faces of the cube are always equal, and all-round pressure is created in the cube.

Often simpler devices are used, consisting of two puccsonaras with holes that skim a container shaped like a lentil (figure 5).

The material for the pooches, which must withstand enormous skimming stresses, is tungsten carbide cemented with cobalt. This super-hard alloy is much stronger than steel.

The container is made from the clay mineral pyrophyllite or other similar materials. Pyrophyllite is a flat conductor of tell and electricity. This allows you to get high temperatures in the container using electric current for heating.

Graphite and a metal catalyst (iron, nickel or their alloys) are placed in the container and scimitared between the poochsons. When the required pressure ($p \geq 6$ GPa) is available, an electric current is passed through the pooches, which heats the contents of the container to a high temperature. In this way, conditions are created that correspond to the p-T region of diamond formation.

The process is monitored by the strength of the current flowing through the apparatus. Diamond is not electrically conductive, and its formation causes an increase in the electrical resistance of the contents of the container and, accordingly, a decrease in the current through the apparatus. After the process is completed, turn off the heating, allow the container to cool, remove the pressure and remove the container from the apparatus.

The resulting diamonds - crystals ranging in size from fractions of a millimeter to 1-2 millimeters - are washed in acids to remove particles of metal and graphite, sorted and sent for the manufacture of diamond tools.

Scientists have developed other methods for producing diamonds and their products. If the process is in the presence of a catalyst (accelerator) at higher pressures, then a huge number of tiny diamond crystals are formed simultaneously, which grow together into a durable black coating - carbonado. Carbonado is stronger and more resistant to impact than regular diamonds.

Polycrystalline diamonds can be produced into any desired shape and thus avoid the labor-intensive processing of diamond blocks. To do this, a workpiece made of a carbohydrate-containing material is given the required shape, taking into account shrinkage, placed in a tube heater, covered with powdered catalyst, and the assembly is placed in a container. After the formation of polycrystalline diamonds, the finished diamond product of a given shape is removed from the container - for example, in the form of a cutter. All that remains is to sharpen the cutting edge, secure it in the holder and place it on the machine. Diamond cutters are hundreds of times more durable than regular ones. They form hard alloys, metals, and plastics. Diamond cutters operate at enormous speeds and provide high surface cleanliness.

Diamond crystals sintered with a metal bond are used to make dusts and grinding wheels for cutting and processing hard metals, graphite, and stones.

Diamond bits and bits are used when drilling wells in rocks. At the same time, the drilling speed returns more than twice, and the cost of work is reduced by 40-60%.

Did Moissan get the diamonds?

Let us estimate what pressures can be created by the internal cooling of iron droplets in the Myaccana experiments. Let's imagine such a cooling droplet in the form of a rigid spherical shell - a crust of solid iron, inside of which there is liquid iron saturated with carbon.

When the temperature changes from $T_1$ to $T_2 = T_1 + \Delta T$, the volume of the shell will change from $V_1$ to $V_2 = V_1(1 + \alpha \Delta T)$,

where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion. The relative change in volume is:

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V_1} = \frac{V_2 - V_1}{V_1} = \alpha \Delta T.$$

(1)

When cooling ($\Delta T < 0$), the crust-shell will decrease in volume and compress the liquid center of the drop. The relative change in the volume of the drop will be $\Delta V/V_1$. Let us determine what pressure will arise inside the liquid iron with such a slope.

Let us remember that the mechanical stress that arose when the image was stretched is directly proportional to the relative elongation:

$$\sigma = E \frac{\Delta l}{l_0},$$

where the coefficient of the proportional $E$ is the Young model.

A similar relationship between the pressure that arose during the rapid collapse of the image and the relative change in volume:

$$p = -B \frac{\Delta V}{V_1},$$

(2)

where the coefficient of proportional $B$ is called the volumetric elasticity model. It, like $\sigma$, has the dimension of pressure. Zakumnus shows that the decrease in volume with such a slope.

Leads to an increase in pressure inside the drop.

Substituting (1) into (2), we obtain an expression for the pressure inside the cooling drop:

$$p = -B \alpha \Delta T.$$

(3)

Let us carry out a numerical estimate of this quantity.

The crust-shell of solid iron, which was formed when a droplet fell into water, is cooled from the melting (solidification) temperature of iron 1811 K to the water temperature, which we use equal to 293 K (20 °C), that is

$$\Delta T = -1518 \text{ K} \approx -1,5 \cdot 10^3 \text{ K}.$$

The value of the coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion is used:

$$\alpha = 35 \cdot 10^{-6} \text{ K}^{-1}.$$

The modulus of bulk elasticity of liquid iron is equal to the modulus of bulk elasticity of solid iron:

$$B = 168 \text{ ГПа}.$$

Substituting these data into formula (3), we obtain the pressure values ​​inside the cooling drop:

$$p = -B \alpha \Delta T = (168 \cdot 35 \cdot 10^{-6} (-1,5 \cdot 10^3)) \Gamma \Pi a \approx 8,9 \text{ ГПа}.$$

This pressure is 1.5 times greater than that required to precipitate carbon from molten iron in the form of diamonds.

Our assessment, of course, is approximate. We do not take into account, for example, the difference in temperature between the internal and external properties of pure iron and a saturated solution of carbon in iron; we did not take into account the reduced exchange rate during the hardening of iron, as well as the strength and elasticity of the shell, which slightly increases its volume under the influence of the created pressure, etc.

These factors have different effects on the design pressure values. It can be expected, however, that taking them into account in total will not significantly change the result.

So, our assessment shows that Myaccana could have formed diamonds in the experiments.
