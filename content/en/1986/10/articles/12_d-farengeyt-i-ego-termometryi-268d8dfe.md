D. Fahrenheit and his thermometers

This year marks the 300th anniversary of the birth of Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit (1686–1736), the creator of the first thermometers that could be used as standard and reliable laboratory instruments.

It is believed that the idea of ​​using the property of thermal expansion of air and liquid, known since ancient times, for temperature measurements belongs to G. Galily. At the end of the 17th century, he invented a simple device based on this principle - a thermopop, which for the first time made it possible to judge temperature changes objectively to some extent. Galileo's thermoscope is a small glass flask filled with air, with a very narrow and long pot lowered into a vessel with water. As the flask was heated or left, the air in it expanded or contracted and the water level decreased or increased accordingly.

During the next, 17th century, many scientists were engaged in mastering this device. So, in 1615 it was equipped with a measuring scale and from 1628 all parts were no longer called a thermoscope, but a thermometer. In 1630, air was replaced by more convenient liquids,

primarily water thermometers. A decade later, they began to be sealed so that the readings would not be affected by fluctuations in atmospheric pressure. Since the late 50s, students from the Florentine “Academy of Experience” have most often used wine alcohol as a thermometric liquid, which does not freeze in the most severe frosts and has a low viscosity and a relatively high coefficient of thermal expansion.

But, despite the fact that the design principles in the basic definition of thermometers, manufactured at that time, were perfectly suitable for accurate and systematic temperature measurements. And above all because there is no correspondence

Since the 80s of the 17th century, the idea of ​​constructing a temperature scale based on two “constant” points became increasingly attractive (with this method, there was no need for the very difficult operation of determining the volumes of the reservoir and capillary for each thermometer).

So, in 1701, I. Newoton built a thermometer (with linseed oil as a liquid), on the scale of which he put 12 equal divisions, with the zero division corresponding to the freezing temperature of water, and the “12” division to the normal temperature of the human body. At the same time, he began to produce his own temperature scales; he took the freezing and boiling temperatures of water. In 1708, Römer visited Fahrenheit, which the Dane introduced in every detail to his thermometer calibrations. Apparently, Fahrenheit immediately realized that this method contained a solution to the long-standing problem of creating standard thermometers that gave “consistent” readings (under the same conditions they showed the same temperature). Within a couple of years he

managed to establish the production of such devices, and they quickly gained recognition in European laboratories. Scientists, familiar with the differences in the readings of old thermometers, perceived Fahrenheit thermometers as a kind of teacher. It must be said that Fahrenheit did not copy thermometers - he worked hard to master their designs, sought out the most suitable types of stack and, finally, modified the scale, making it much more convenient. The Fahrenheit temperature scale is still used in English countries. It is designed like this: the interval between the points of ice and boiling water is divided into 180 parts, with the first point assigned a value of 32, and the second 212 °F (degrees Fahrenheit).

Fahrenheit’s most important merit is also that he was the first (in 1717) to begin producing routine thermometers. It must be said that this idea was considered by Florentine academics half a century before him. But then ruthite, due to its low coefficient of thermal expansion (one sixth less than that of alcohol), was considered unpromising. Fahrenheit came to the conclusion that this deficiency of rutti is more than compensated for by the ease of obtaining and preserving it in its pure form, thanks to which the requirement for the accuracy of the working fluid filling the thermometer specimens is easily met.

(Alcohol is less convenient in this regard, since, intensively absorbing water, it significantly changes the coefficient of thermal expansion.)

Little is known about Fahrenheit’s life; his portrait has not reached us. He was born in Danzig (now Gdansk) into a merchant family and received a good primary education. At the age of 15, he suddenly lost his family. The “fathers” of the city decided that the boy should follow in his father’s footsteps, and in 1702 they sent him to Amsterdam to study as a merchant. He successfully mastered the profession, but his interests were increasingly inclined to the natural sciences and especially to the design of precision scientific instruments. On business, Fahrenheit often traveled from Amsterdam to other cities in Holland and abroad, including Russia, and each time he tried not to take advantage of the opportunity to expand his position in technology and science with the leading masters - instrument makers and scientists in Europe. This is how the above-mentioned meeting between Fahrenheit and Roemer took place, and scientific contacts were also established with the values ​​of the Dutch scientists of those times - the physician G. Boerhaave and the physicists W. Sgravesand and P. van Muschenbrouck, with the future Swedish natural scientist K. Linnaeus.

As a master professional, Fahrenheit in 1710-1712 began to manufacture pressed (very precise) equipment for physical, chemical and astronomical research - thermometers,

barometers, hydrometers, optical instruments, precision mechanics devices. The demand for Fahrenheit's products increased, and in 1717 he founded a special company in Amsterdam for the production of precision scientific instruments, and in part other thermometers for various purposes, which were beyond competition. It is significant that in 1721 a set of Fahrenheit thermometers was ordered by Peter I. In 1724, Fahrenheit was elected a member of the Royal Society of London, whose president at that time was I. Newton.

In addition to making instruments, Fahrenheit worked a lot as an experimental physicist. He discovered that the boiling point of a liquid is divided into 180 parts, with the first point assigned a value of 32, and the second 212 °F (degrees Fahrenheit).

Of course, Fahrenheit cannot be considered the inventor of the thermometer, and he himself did not hide how much

he is indebted to Roemer and his other predecessors. But the undeniable merit of Fahrenheit is the introduction into widespread use of a standard thermometer, which marked the beginning of thermometry, which for the first time made it possible to understand the most important development of precise scientific instruments of temperature and temperature (in relation to which complete confusion had previously reigned).
