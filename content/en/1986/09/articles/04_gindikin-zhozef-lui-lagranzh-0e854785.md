Joseph Louis Lagrange
To the 250th anniversary of his birth

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences S. G. GINDIKIN

Letter from Turin. In August 1755, the great Euler (1707-1783) received a letter from Turin from 19-year-old Lagrange, who had written to him before. Euler undoubtedly already had the opinion that his correspondent was a talented, mature mathematician, despite his youth. And yet the contents of the last letter amazed the scientist.

Since the end of the 17th century, the attention of mathematicians has been increasingly attracted to problems that are now commonly called *warshation*, but then were usually called *isopermetric*. It all started with the problem of the brachistochrone - the widest curve between two points. This problem was set by Johann Bernoulli (1667-1748). However, problems about curves that have certain maximum-minimum properties arose before: a circle with a given length limits a figure with the flattest area (isopermetric property, hence the name of the class of problems), a straight line is the shortest distance between points, etc. The number of such problems grew, mathematicians solved them with satisfaction, choosing their own “key and secret” for each of them.

However, the style of these heydays of differential and integral calculus required an attempt to find a general method, to form a calculus for solving isopermetric problems. This method was discovered in 1732 by Euler himself. His main observation is that the curves that are the solution to these problems correspond to the solution of certain differential equations. He saw the main task in deriving these equations. The difficulty here is that the discussion was about finding an extremal curve (and not an extreme point) and therefore the well-known examples of searching for an extremum were not used. Only in a roundabout way, replacing curves with broken lines, did Euler manage to overcome this difficulty.

But Lagrange, with the determination inherent in youth, dared to apply the scheme for finding an extremum from general analysis specifically to curves. This scheme, as any nine-classician now knows, consists of replacing the function $y=f(x)$ with its main part $dy=f'(x)dx$ (its differential) followed by solving the equation $dy=0$ (that is, $f'(x)=0$). Lagrange came up with the corresponding concept for the curve $l$, denoting it $\delta l$ (by analogy with $dy$); later $\delta l$ began to be called *varshation*. Equation $\delta l=0$ and bringing it straight to those differential equations - they are now called *Euler-Lagrange equations * - to which Euler went in a roundabout way.

Euler received short information to evaluate all the applications of Lagrange's improvement. Lagrange's letter revived Euler's own interest in extreme problems. Already in 1756, he made two reports at the Berlin Academy related to the Lagrange method. In the same year, Lagrange introduced

Euler is in no hurry to publish his new results, leaving his young colleague to slowly prepare his work for publication. He explains his position in a letter dated October 10, 1759:

“Your English solution of the isoperimetric problem contains, as far as I can see, everything that can be desired in this field, and I am extremely glad that this theory, which after my first attempts I worked on almost alone, has been brought to the greatest perfection. The importance of the question prompted me to, with the help of your illumination, come up with the English solution myself. I, however, decided to hide this until you publish your results, since I never want to deprive you of a part of your well-deserved word.”

A wonderful example of scientific ethics!

Euler’s letter added to Lagrange’s determination to publish what he had done, and in volume II of the “Turiin Notes” for 1761-1762 his topic “The practice of a new method for determining the maxima and minima of indefinite interval formulas” appears. In 1764, Euler also published his results, introducing the publication with the words:

“After I worked for a long time and for free on solving this issue, I was surprised to see that in the “Turiin Records” this problem was solved as easily as it was happy. This wonderful discovery aroused my admiration all the more because it differs significantly from the data of many methods and significantly surpasses them in simplicity.”

Note that Euler does not mention the previous correspondence. Euler proposes to call the new method “varnation calculus” by analogy with differential calculus.

This was Lagrange's scientific output. In one respect it is unique. There are other examples where great mathematicians obtained their first major results at the same age as Lagrange. However, she decided to solve specific problems. Interest in improving the method as such comes over the years. We see that already in Lagrange’s first work something was revealed that would always distinguish him in the future: complete clarification of the situation, improvement of the method, search for the first principle is valued above specific tasks.

Giuseppe Luigi. We have talked about Lagrange's first great work, but it is still worth saying a few words about the earlier events of his life. Joseph Louis Lagrange was born on January 25, 1736 in Turin, Italy. However, in his homeland they called him Giuseppe Luigi. His predecessor came from France and entered the service of the Duke of Savoy, and his grandfather and father continued to serve as treasurer of the factory and buildings. By the time the future was born, the family's mathematics was ruined.

“If I had been rich, I believe I would not have achieved my position in mathematics; And in what other activity would I add the same success? - the scientists subsequently said.

However, from the beginning, Joseph Louis’s family plans intended him to become a lawyer, and at the age of 14 he entered the University of Turin. However, he transferred to the Artillery School, which was due to his successful interest in mathematics. At the age of 19, he is a professor of mathematics at this school (according to some sources, even earlier).

A circle of young mathematicians and physicists formed around Lagrange, which later transformed into the Turi Academy of Sciences. Since 1759, “Philosophical and mathematical collections of the private Turi scientific society” began to be published, which were called simply “Turii records”. We have already said that in volume II of the notes the topic of Lagrange’s career in ovaration calculus appeared, and volume I contained two of his works, including the article “Research on the nature of the propagation of sound.”

Foundations of statics. Lagrange was the soul of the Turin circle. The articles of his comrades published in the Turin Records bear a clear trace of the strong influence of Lagrange. This especially applies to the article by Fonsena, who was, apparently, only an accomplice in Lagrange’s systematic thinking through the foundations of mechanics. Then his famous “Analytical Mechanics” will begin with the plot of this article. We are talking here about a comparison of two most important statics: the principle of leverage and the principle of the addition of forces applied to one point. Archimedes based this theory of the lever on the axiom about the equilibrium of a lever with equal arms and loads and about the double load on the fulcrum in this situation. Many authors tried to clarify and supplement Archimedes’ reasoning, but they, according to Lagrange, “violated simplicity ... gained almost nothing in terms of accuracy.” Lagrange notes that the first part of the axiom of the natural is considered very similar to the messages of symmetry: “it is impossible to establish the basis by virtue of which one load would pull another.” He, however, does not see any logical grounds for the fact that the load on the fulcrum must necessarily be equal to the sum of the weights of the loads:

“apparently, all mechanics consider this assumption as the result of everyday observation, which teaches us that the heaviness of a body depends only on its mass, but in no way depends on its shape.”

Lagrange proposes a derivation of the second half of Archimedes' axiom from the first. He considers a uniform triangular plate ABC, where the base AB of the equilateral triangle is horizontal. Vertices A and B are loaded with equal loads p, and vertex C with a load of 2p. The plate rests on the midline MN parallel to AB (Fig. 1). It will be in equilibrium, which follows from considering a pair of levers AC, CB with support points M, N due to the first part of Archimedes’ axiom. But then the lever CF will also be in equilibrium, where F is the middle of AB with the fulcrum E - the middle of CF (at which MN and CF intersect). Therefore, the load at point F must be equal to the load 2p at point C (strictly speaking, this applies the inversion of the first part of Archimedes' axiom, which is easily deduced), and this is exactly the load on the fulcrum in the lever AB. Lagrange carefully notes that he derived the technique of considering the equilibrium of a flat plate relative to a rod from Huygen.

Next, Lagrange considers the principle of the addition of forces applied to one point, which is easily justified by considering the addition of motion. A significant difference in the principles is that in one case the forces are applied to different points, and in the other - to one. Nevertheless, many statements of statics can be deduced from one principle as well as from another. There is a desire to generally refuse to accept the principle of leverage as an axiom, but Lagrange insists that all the known conclusions of Archimedes’ axiom from the law of addition of forces are very artificial:

“...although, strictly speaking, both principles of leverage and addition of motion always lead to the same results, it is interesting to note that the simplest force for one of these principles becomes the most complex for the other.”

Intuition allows Lagrange to accurately detect a subtle point, although he could not fully explain it. It is connected with the mutual relationship of mechanics and geometry. The fact is that the law of addition of forces applied to one point does not depend on the parallel axiom, while in Lobachevsky space the load on the fulcrum of the lever always exceeds the sum of the weights of the applied loads. The derivation of the second half of Archimedes' axiom uses the statement that the altitude of an equilateral triangle intersects the midline at its midpoint; and this is based on the parallel axiom and is not true in Lobachevsky geometry. Apparently, Lagrange did not yet know this, although it is known that he was thinking about the problem of the V postulate.

Principles of least action. In volume II of the “Turian Notes”, following the topic on the calculus of variations, Lagrange’s article “Application of the method outlined in the previous topic for solving various problems of dynamics” was placed. And here Lagrange follows in the footsteps of Euler. In 1744, Maupertion (1698-1759) formulated a very general and vague principle, according to which everything in nature, including mechanical movement, occurs in such a way that a certain quantity - action - reaches its minimum value. Visit to Paris. In 1766 Lagrange turned 30 years old. This was an important milestone in his life. He is recognized in the scientific world: in 1764 and 1766. he received prizes from the Paris Academy for his study of the motion of Lunyi and the moons of Jupiter. Provincial Turin becomes a dark place for Lagrange's scientific activities. In his personal life he was unpretentious, distinguished by his weak health, his modesty in communicating with people took the form of modesty and even incompetence. He maintained an extensive correspondence, but Lagrange had the opportunity to see how much direct communication with scientists gives during a trip to Paris in 1755. Lagrange accompanied his friend Karachili, who was appointed envoy to London. However, Lagrange did not make it to London.

“Having become dangerously ill after dinner at Abbot Nollet’s, at which Nollet treated him to dishes prepared in the Italian style, Lagrange could not go to London, but stayed for treatment in Paris and, out of anger, hastened to return to Turin,” recalled his friend and biographer Delambre.

The fact was that in northern Italy they use bonfire oil for cooking, pre-drained. In Nolle’s kitchen, where they decided to prepare dinner “the Italian way,” they used bonfire oil without the necessary preparation, and it fully demonstrated its well-known medicinal properties. However, scientifically it was more fruitful. Lagrange communicated a lot with the leading French mathematicians D'Alembert (1717-1783), Clairaut (1713-1765), Condorcet (1743-1794), but there were also less important scientists who remained his friends for life. Lagrange repeated more than once that these six months spent in Paris were the happiest period in his life.

Lagrange in Berlin. In 1766, Euler left Berlin for St. Petersburg, freeing up the position of director of the physics and mathematics class at the Berlin Academy of Sciences. He proposes Lagrange as his successor to Frederick II. This candidacy was energetically supported by D'Alembert, with whom the king considered him even more inferior. Already in November 1766 Lagrange was in Berlin, although the king of Sardinni reluctantly parted with the scientist. Lagrange ended up at the academy not in its best days. There was neither Euler, nor d'Alembert, nor Maupertion here. However, a very original mathematician Lambert (1728-1777) worked here, who proved, in particular, the irrationality of the number l. Lagrange and Lambert have many points of contact in mathematics; they fill each other with something in a human way. Their friendship lasted ten years until Lambert's death and was very important to them. It was not easy for the withdrawn Lagrange to adapt to the life of the Prussian court. But he, unlike Euler, was able to do this and avoid conflicts. Lagrange leads a measured life: external duties, meetings, correspondence take up most of the day, but all evening after the obligatory walk the department is busy with science in silence, behind closed doors.

"Analytical Mechanics". Lagrange spent a little over twenty years in Berlin. This was the time of his maturity, the most productive period of his life. There are several great scientists whose heritage includes one main book (Newton's Principia, Huygen's Pendulum Clock). Lagrange's book was Analytical Mechanics. It was published in 1788, when Lagrange was already in Paris. But she chose to incorporate the main thing that was done in Berlin and conceived in Turin. The intent of the book can best be understood from the words of the author himself:

“There are already several manuals on mechanics, but the plan of this social scientist is perfect. I had in mind to reduce the entire theory of this science and the art of solving problems related to it to general formulas, the simple development of which would provide everything necessary for solving any equation.”

Algebraic reflections. Problems of algebraic equations and their systems interested Lagrange in various aspects. In 1770-71 the memoir “Reflections on the Algebraic Solution of Equations” was published, undoubtedly conceived in Turin. Actually, this is a whole book, occupying more than 200 pages, which, according to Cauchy, marked the beginning of a new era in algebra. Along with Analytical Mechanics, this is the pinnacle of Lagrange’s work.

Lagrange seeks to solve the following problem: why it is not possible to obtain a formula for the roots of algebraic equations of degree greater than 4. He analyzes the expressions under the radicals in the formulas for quadratic and cubic equations, giving convincing arguments that for equations of the 5th degree similar expressions, and therefore formulas, cannot exist. This was later proven by Abel, and general deep ideas about permutations of roots served as Galois (1811-1832) the starting point for the construction of his great theory.

Crisis. Mathematics was Lagrange's only passion and it was enough to fill his entire life and bring him many happy moments. Everything else was subordinated to science. Delambre conveys Lagrange's attitude towards music:

“I love her because she isolates me; I hear the first three measures, on the fourth I also do not distinguish anything, I indulge in my thoughts, nothing interrupts me, and then I solve the most difficult of problems.”

It was characteristic of Lagrange that the great goals of meaning were true, world harmony was not intertwined with personal ambitions, with the desire to accomplish, to burn his contemporaries. If he learned that someone was successfully tackling a problem that he himself was thinking about, he immediately stopped thinking with a sincere feeling of being “freed from obligation.” Thanks to this Lagrange, he was always endowed with an extraordinary peace of mind, which gave him the strength to endure the hardships of life and not to stop strenuous activities.

Only one thing could shake Lagrange - loss of reference points, uncertainty in choosing the right goals. And this realization begins to appear soon after moving to Berlin. In 1772, he wrote to d’Alembert: “Don’t you think that the geometry of proximity to decline has emerged, only you and Euler support it.” This is a pshet scientist who is in the prime of his life (he is 36 years old), who is beginning to develop his “Analytical Mechanics” and who has just published an algebraic topic that determines the development of algebra for 100 years to come!

The feeling of the decline of mathematics does not leave Lagrange. On September 21, 1781, he again wrote to d'Alembert:

“I am beginning to feel the strength of my inertia, which is gradually increasing, and I cannot say with certainty that over the next decade I will continue to study mathematics. I also think that the mine is getting too deep and that sooner or later it will be abandoned if new Rudonian veins are not discovered.”

In Paris. The premonition does not deceive Lagrange. In 1787, shortly after the death of Frederick II, he moved to Paris and, in essence, stopped active studies in mathematics. Lagrange is 51 years old. In one year, 1783, the world lost both Euler and d'Alembert. Lagrange was enthusiastically greeted by French scientists; now he is undoubtedly the “first of Europe,” and only Lapaz can always compete with him. People at court are not indifferent to Lagrange. He is unusually easily distracted from geometry by being busy with philosophy, chemistry, history, medicine, and actively participates in the experiments of the great chemist Lavuzier (1743-1794). But soon the French scientists passed into the period of revolution, in which scientists adopted the most active teaching. Never before had they had the opportunity to directly influence the life of the country. They are members of the municipality, the Constituent and Legislative Assembly; the astronomer Bailly becomes the mayor of Paris, the mathematician Lazare Carnot heads the defense of France (he was called the “organizer of victories”), and Monge the minister of the sea. Scientists' activities aimed at solving practical problems have also intensified sharply.

Lagrange stays away from politics. The law of 1793 orders foreigners to leave France, but a special decree of the Committee of Public Safety makes an exception for Lagrange. In the most difficult days, he does not leave France, sharing the fate of his colleagues. Participation in the political life of the capital of Bailly, Condorcet. Lavouzier was a treasury tax farmer. Lagrange watches closely what is happening. Delambre preserved the words of Lagrange, spoken after the guillotine of Lavuzier:

“It only took one moment to blow this head off, and maybe it won’t be enough for a similar one to appear.”
