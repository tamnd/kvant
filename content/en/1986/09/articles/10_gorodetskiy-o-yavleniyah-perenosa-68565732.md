About the phenomena of sleepover

All bodies around us, even the smallest in size, consist of a huge number of molecules. The movement of each molecule is described by the laws of mechanics, but the behavior of a large number of molecules is subject to qualitatively different laws. Is it possible to establish these patterns, knowing the nature of the movement of individual molecules? In principle, yes, but in practice this is a very difficult task.

Fortunately, it turns out that in order to study some properties of macroscopic bodies it is not at all necessary to interact with each molecule separately. A general idea of ​​the internal structure of a body as a system of particles moving and colliding with each other is quite sufficient. In this case, the behavior of macroscopic bodies can be characterized by special physical quantities that relate to the entire set of molecules composing the bodies, that is, they are average characteristics. So, for example, the density of a substance is determined by the average number of molecules per unit volume, the temperature of a body is determined by their average kinetic energy, etc.

In a state of equilibrium, any average characteristics of the body are the same at all its points. But it is easy to imagine a situation where there is no balance. Let's look at an example.

If you place a vessel with water on a hot stove, then at first the temperature of the water near the day will be higher than on the surface. Only after a fairly long period of time has passed will the water warm up evenly throughout the entire volume. How does this heating happen?

But the proportional coefficient is determined solely by the internal structure of the substance (how the molecules move inside it and how they interact (collide) with each other). This coefficient is called the thermal conductivity coefficient (λ). It varies in different substances. Thus, the thermal conductivity of any metal is several orders of magnitude greater than the thermal conductivity of wood. That is why it is difficult to touch a metal spoon lowered into a glass of hot tea, but it costs nothing to hold a hot match in your hand.

Another example. Let's leave a mixture of two substances, for example water and a drop of ink in it. Obviously, the drop will spread, tending to spread evenly throughout the entire volume of water, that is, it will initiate the diffusion process. Unlike the first example, here there will be a flow of substance - ink, directed from an area where there is a lot of ink to an area where there is little of it. This flow is proportional to the difference in the concentration of ink molecules in different regions of space. The proportionality coefficient between the flow of a substance and the concentration difference is called the diffusion coefficient (D). The diffusion coefficient, just like the thermal conductivity coefficient, is different for different substances and is determined by their internal structure (the ability of molecules of one type to “resist” through the thickness of other molecules).

Finally, one last example. Imagine a solid body moving in a liquid. As experience shows, a layer of liquid is not present when

Due to the exchange of molecules between layers, this movement is transmitted to neighboring layers, from them to the next, etc. Thus, an impulse flow will arise from layers with a higher speed to layers with a lower speed. This is precisely the mechanism of liquid friction, or friction. Indeed, an increase in the implus of a liquid means that some force is acting on it from the side of the body (a change in the implus of the system is equal to the implus of external forces acting on it). Therefore, according to Newton's third law, a force acting on the body from the side of the liquid is directed in the opposite direction. This is the force of liquid friction.

The flow of impulse from layers moving quickly to layers moving at a lower speed is proportional to the difference in the velocities of these layers. The proportionality coefficient between the pulse flow and the speed difference is called the pulse velocity coefficient (η).

All the considered examples are characterized by a common property - the transfer of a certain attribute (energy in the first example, matter in the second, and momentum in the third) from one area of ​​the system to another. It is no coincidence that phenomena of this kind are called phenomena of transition. Each of them is characterized by its own transfer coefficient, and the task of the theory is to be able to calculate them.
