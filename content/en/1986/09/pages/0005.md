⟦folio 3⟧

Joseph Louis Lagrange
To the 250th anniversary of his birth

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences S. G. GINDIKIN

Letter from Turin. In August 1755, the great Euler (1707-1783) received a letter from Turin from 19-year-old Lagrange, who had written to him before. Euler undoubtedly already had the opinion that his correspondent was a talented, mature mathematician, despite his youth. And yet the contents of the last letter amazed the scientist.

Since the end of the 17th century, the attention of mathematicians has been increasingly attracted to problems that are now commonly called *warshation*, but then were usually called *isopermetric*. It all started with the problem of the brachistochrone - the widest curve between two points. This problem was set by Johann Bernoulli (1667-1748). However, problems about curves that have certain maximum-minimum properties arose before: a circle with a given length limits a figure with the flattest area (isopermetric property, hence the name of the class of problems), a straight line is the shortest distance between points, etc. The number of such problems grew, mathematicians solved them with satisfaction, choosing their own “key and secret” for each of them.

However, the style of these heydays of differential and integral calculus required an attempt to find a general method, to form a calculus for solving isopermetric problems. This method was discovered in 1732 by Euler himself. His main observation is that the curves that are the solution to these problems correspond to the solution of certain differential equations. He saw the main task in deriving these equations. The difficulty here is that the discussion was about finding an extremal curve (and not an extreme point) and therefore the well-known examples of searching for an extremum were not used. Only in a roundabout way, replacing curves with broken lines, did Euler manage to overcome this difficulty.

But Lagrange, with the determination inherent in youth, dared to apply the scheme for finding an extremum from general analysis specifically to curves. This scheme, as any nine-classician now knows, consists of replacing the function $y=f(x)$ with its main part $dy=f'(x)dx$ (its differential) followed by solving the equation $dy=0$ (that is, $f'(x)=0$). Lagrange came up with the corresponding concept for the curve $l$, denoting it $\delta l$ (by analogy with $dy$); later $\delta l$ began to be called *varshation*. Equation $\delta l=0$ and bringing it straight to those differential equations - they are now called *Euler-Lagrange equations * - to which Euler went in a roundabout way.
