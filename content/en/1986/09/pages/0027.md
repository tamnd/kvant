Let's talk about average speed

When we talk about the speed of movement of various bodies in the world around us, we most often mean average speed. It is this that allows you to estimate the distance traveled, knowing the time of movement, or, conversely, helps to find the time of movement along the traveled path. So, for example, when going to the station from the other end of the city, you calculate your time based on the average speed of city transport known from experience. In this case, it does not matter at all to you how the instantaneous speed of a bus or trolleybus changes from one stop to another.

To determine the average speed, we mentally replace true complex uneven motion with a certain simple uniform motion, in which the body travels the same path (or makes the same displacement) in the same time as in the process of true motion.

Let us note that in “Physics 8” (§11) two different concepts of average speed are introduced: vector average speed, calculated from the displacement vector s of a body over a certain time t, and scalar average speed, determined from the path l traveled by the body along the trajectory:

$$\vec{v}_{\text{cp}} = \frac{\vec{s}}{t}, \quad v_{\text{cp}} = \frac{l}{t}.$$

The average scalar velocity, generally speaking, does not coincide with the vector average velocity model. Thus, $v_{\text{cp}}$ for the Earth during its reverse motion around the Sun is approximately 30 km/s, while the average vector speed taken over intermediate ones, equal to one year, is very low, equal to zero. The equality of average speeds is satisfied only in the case of rectilinear motion of the body in one direction. Below we will talk only about scalar average speed. Let's start with this example.

Imagine that you are driving in a car along a deserted country highway, lined up next to the driver. You have a stopwatch in your hands, there are no visible kilometer markers outside the window, and you are conducting a small experiment. In the first experiment, the driver, at your command, “jumpily” changes speed every minute: $v_1 = 40$ km/h, $v_2 = 60$ km/h, $v_3 = 80$ km/h, $v_4 = 20$ km/h. Then the experiment is carried out differently: the driver sequentially goes through the same set of speeds, but you give him commands not using a stopwatch, but at those moments when the car passes the next kilometer mark. Are the average speeds the same in these two experiments?

In the first case, the movement was structured so that in each section where the speed was constant, the vehicle moved for the same period of time $\Delta t$ (Fig. 1). That's why

$$v_{\text{cp1}} = \frac{l}{t} = \frac{v_1 \Delta t + v_2 \Delta t + v_3 \Delta t + v_4 \Delta t}{4 \Delta t} = \frac{1}{4} (v_1 + v_2 + v_3 + v_4) = 50 \text{ km/ч}.$$
