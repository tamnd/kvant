About the phenomena of sleepover

All bodies around us, even the smallest in size, consist of a huge number of molecules. The movement of each molecule is described by the laws of mechanics, but the behavior of a large number of molecules is subject to qualitatively different laws. Is it possible to establish these patterns, knowing the nature of the movement of individual molecules? In principle, yes, but in practice this is a very difficult task.

Fortunately, it turns out that in order to study some properties of macroscopic bodies it is not at all necessary to interact with each molecule separately. A general idea of ​​the internal structure of a body as a system of particles moving and colliding with each other is quite sufficient. In this case, the behavior of macroscopic bodies can be characterized by special physical quantities that relate to the entire set of molecules composing the bodies, that is, they are average characteristics. So, for example, the density of a substance is determined by the average number of molecules per unit volume, the temperature of a body is determined by their average kinetic energy, etc.

In a state of equilibrium, any average characteristics of the body are the same at all its points. But it is easy to imagine a situation where there is no balance. Let's look at an example.

If you place a vessel with water on a hot stove, then at first the temperature of the water near the day will be higher than on the surface. Only after a fairly long period of time has passed will the water warm up evenly throughout the entire volume. How does this heating happen?
