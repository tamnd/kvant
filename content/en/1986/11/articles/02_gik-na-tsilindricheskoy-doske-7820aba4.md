ON A CYLINDRICAL BOARD
After the “Chess Page” entitled “Cylindrical Chess” appeared in «Kvant» No. 7 of 1985, the editor received many letters - the unusual chess-geometric plot aroused keen interest among readers. The materials of reader from Donetsk V. Popov deserve special attention. The reason was the following task.

F. Bondarenko. Checkmate in 1 move: a) on a regular board; 6) on a vertical cylinder (lines “a” and “h” are glued to each other); c) on a horizontal cylinder (lines “1” and “8” are glued together).

On a regular board the solution is 1.Qe2—e8×; on a vertical cylinder this move does not mate due to the answer 1...Ka8—h7!, and only 1.Qe2—g8× leads to the goal (the white queen goes along the route e2—a6—h7—g8); on a horizontal cylinder only 1.Fe2—a2× solves!

V. Popov sets a goal to improve the external design of the problem. Let's take another look at the original source position. The black king is constrained in a somewhat rude manner; he does not have the slightest freedom. The search for a more subtle initial construction led first to this position.

Checkmate in 1 move: a) on a regular board; 6) on a vertical cylinder; c) on a horizontal cylinder.

The advantages of the problem compared to the original are very interesting - the king of the black flowers is more powerful, and the b7 pawn is replaced by a more dynamic knight.

Further “Cylindrical” thoughts made it possible to discover that knight b7 is completely superfluous.

V. Popov, 1986 White: Cre3, Fs4, Cf4; black: Kra8. Checkmate in 1 move: a) on a regular board; 6) on a vertical cylinder; c) on a horizontal cylinder.

This is a position that seems to us an ideal questioning of Bondarenko’s theme. Indeed, there are only four pieces on the board, and the formation is very light and elegant - the white pieces are distant from the king, and at first it seems that a single-move mate is out of the question.

a) On a regular board, the only lead to the goal is 1.Q6×.

6) On a vertical cylinder, lines “a” and “h” are glued to each other and the math is 1.Fs4—a2—h1×! (1.Qa6(e4)+ Kra8—h8!, 1.Qs8(h7)+ Kra8—h7(:h7)). The queen immediately took control of four squares in the area of ​​the black king (including the one occupied by him) - b7, a8 along the h1-a8 diagonal and h7, h8 along the “h” vertical. It is curious that from a closer distance it is impossible to take away so many squares from the king without becoming a queen for battle - neither on a cylindrical, nor even more so on an ordinary board. Squares a7, b8 are held by the bishop along the diagonal c1-h6-a7-b8.

c) On a horizontal cylinder, the first one is glued to each other and the lines are restored and only 1.Fs4—f1—g8—h7× is solved! (1.Qa6+ Kra8—b1!). Once again, the queen took four squares from the king: a8, b1 along the h7-b1-a8 diagonal and a7, b7 along the seventh rank. Squares b8, a1 are held by the bishop along the diagonal h2-b8-a1. And here other checks do not checkmate with queens—the king goes to a7 or b7. If the white king is removed from the path of the bishop, then the checks 1.Qc8(e4, h1)+ become checkmates, since the a7 square turns out to be inaccessible to the king. Strange, but the white king, being in the rear of his pieces, prevents white from mate! In the same spice cylinder, it was immediately clear that the white king was useless, but the fact that it was harmful was unexpected...
