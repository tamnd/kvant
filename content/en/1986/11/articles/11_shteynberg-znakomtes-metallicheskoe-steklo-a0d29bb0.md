Two “dense” states of matter are well known: liquid and crystalline. Their external dissimilarity corresponds to an equally significant difference in their atomic structure. In a liquid, atoms are located relative to each other more or less randomly, but in a crystal their arrangement is strictly ordered. However, these atoms are located like nodes of a spatial lattice, which is called crystalline. Different substances have their own crystal lattice shape characteristics. figure 1 shows one of the possible options.

If a crystal is heated above its melting point, it will melt and turn into a liquid. If the melt is cooled below the melting point, it will crystallize again. At the same time, atoms seem to “feel” the approach of a critical moment when it is necessary to rebuild and organize “from the liquid of the free free” to build a crystal. Everything will happen as if the command “stand!” and the atoms-soldiers take their places one by one, lining up in a certain order. And no matter how “performing” atoms are, it still takes some time to “build”. The most famous example of crystallization is the formation of ice from water at 0 °C. All metals, their alloys and thousands of other substances behave in exactly the same way at their melting temperatures. But sometimes events develop according to an unusual scenario.

The “life” of atoms is constant movement. But their mobility quickly decreases as the temperature drops. This circumstance can be taken advantage of. Let's imagine that some kind of multi-group monster grabbed all the atoms of the liquid and held them in place until the melt cooled to a temperature below the melting point, and only then let go. Then the atoms will no longer be able to reorganize into a crystal. They remain in disarray. The liquid itself will thicken and become solid. This material is called glass.*)

Keeping atoms, of course, is unrealistic. But it can quickly cool the liquid. Then at high temperatures (melting point) the atoms will not have time to rearrange, and at low temperatures it will be too late. Remember one of the children's games. In it, all players must remain in the position in which they were caught by the “freeze” command. Sometimes it is unnatural. Sudden cooling is an order to “freeze” for atoms. Unlike people, atoms can be in “inconvenient” positions for years, scales, thousands of years. And if the players are revived by the command “die”, then the atoms are “awakened to life” by reheating.

But what does “cool quickly” mean? The answer is different for each substance. For a mixture of soda and sand, “fast” means a decrease in temperature by a fraction of a degree per second. This cooling rate is achieved in the natural absence of molten mass. That is why our ancestors were able to open common glass. But for metal alloys, “fast” is really fast. Estimates have shown that in order to prevent the liquid metal melt from crystallizing, it must be cooled at a speed of a million degrees. Of course, the temperature of the hot melt is not a million degrees. This rate of temperature decrease means that it is necessary to cool the melt, say, from 2000 °C to 1000 °C in 10⁻³ s, that is, in one millisecond. This is the price of “stopping” atoms.

The idea of ​​rapid cooling is not new. It has been known since antiquity under the name of hardening. The great Homer testifies: “As a blacksmith immerses a red-hot ax in cold water, and searches for iron with bubbling, stronger iron is made in fire and water.”

*) Glass belongs to a more general group of amorphous substances - this is the name given to solids that do not have a crystal lattice.

Let's leave the metal for a while and ask the question: how to quickly cool hot tea? The easiest way is to drink it from a cup in a saucer. The heat will be released mainly to the surrounding air. By increasing the air contact surface, we increase the cooling rate. The flatter we choose, the faster the tea will cool (of course, with its volume remaining the same!). Finally, you can simply pour the tea on the table. As a result of the spreading of the liquid, its surface area will increase even more. True, we won’t recommend this technique for drinking, then don’t doubt the remaining moments.

So, the first conclusion: to increase the cooling rate, you should maximize the surface area of ​​contact with the heat transfer medium, in this case - from the air.

But air removes heat relatively slowly. Metals are rightly considered to be good conductors of heat, and of them, first of all, copper. We are convinced of this every time we touch a metal coating - it always seems cold, as it quickly removes human heat.

This leads to another recommendation: tea should not be poured on the table, but on a wide metal (preferably copper) tray. Then it will cool down almost immediately.

This concludes our not-so-exquisite “tea ceremony.” She suggested a specific recipe. To quickly cool the liquid metal, it is necessary to create a very thin layer of it on the copper surface (usually called the substrate).

The first successful experiments in ultra-fast cooling of a metal liquid were carried out in 1960. A drop of molten metal appeared on the copper substrate. (In early work, the energy of the powder gases of a conventional pistol was used for this.) When hitting a copper screen, the drop is flattened, instantly cooled (the cooling rate reaches $10^8-10^{10}$ deg/s), and a tiny cake of solid amorphous metal soars into the air from the copper “springboard” (figure 2). In the thinnest places its thickness is equal to 0.0001 mm.

There are several other variations of the “drop squishing” method. Their main drawback is low productivity. And the amorphous metal is obtained in the form of a fine powder, and this is not always convenient for practical use. But applying the same cooling principles allows us to create ways to produce a continuous strip of amorphous metal. Here is one possible option. A thin stream of molten metal flows onto the rotating copper disk. In contact with the disk, it cools, and the already forcing amorphous ribbon (figure 3) is thrown off the disk.

Externally, metallic glass has nothing in common with ordinary glass production and is no different from ordinary (crystalline) metal. But this is only external. The disordered arrangement of atoms, unusual for room temperatures, also corresponds to unusual properties. Amorphous metal is extremely hard and durable. Magnetic heads made from it are many times longer than crystalline “assemblies” in terms of service life. And in terms of purity, magnetic characteristics are used on their side. Already today, different countries have begun producing magnetic heads made of metallic glass for video and audio magnetic tapes, and computer memory.

The high creativity of amorphous metals suggested another area of ​​their application. Extremely made from these mode tools.
