However, I considered it best, to the best of my ability, to eliminate in some way the mentioned difficulties and try to harness the incendiary power of these instruments, which are glorified by so many workers who move natural science forward, and which, I have no doubt, will come to the aid of chemical work requiring strong fire.〈...〉

And finally, I came up with the idea that the sun's rays, even after reflection from flat mirrors, still retain their calorific power, therefore, they must, when collected by a convex lens, generate heat. Immediately, triumphantly, I took a flat mirror and a lens two inches in diameter and, since the day was just clear, the sun’s rays reflected by the mirror were collected by the lens. When, after this, I substituted a piece of wood, a black spot formed on it and smoke came out from there. I looked at this with delight and, with the goal of going further, I connected the focus of the lens exposed to direct rays with the focus of the first and brought to them very white paper, which refused to light up from the direct rays of the sun collected by one or another of the named lenses; smoke immediately began to appear, a black spot formed on the paper, the paper flared up, and the fire, flaring up, began to burn it down. So, having repeated this experiment several times and STRAIN IN ANY DOUBT ABOUT ITS CORRECTNESS, I DO NOT HESITATE TO CONCLUSION THAT BY ARRANGEMENT AS I MENTIONED ABOVE, MORE MIRROR AND LENS, YOU CAN GET GREAT HEAT.
