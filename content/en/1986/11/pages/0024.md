This will mean that the focus of the lens is minimal, that is, that the lens is divergent. The locations of real and minimal objects and images are shown in figure 3.

Nomographs can also be used to determine the linear magnification of a lens - the ratio of the linear size $H$ of the image to the linear size $h$ of the object: $\Gamma = H/h = f/d$ (see § 60 of “Physics 10”). From figure 1 it is clear that

$$\Gamma = \frac{f}{d} = \text{tg} \beta.$$

Thus, the image can be enlarged, reduced, or the same size as the object itself.

You can also solve the inverse problem - using a given magnification and a known focal length of the lens, determine the distances $d$ and $f$. To do this, it is enough to construct the familiar point $K$ and draw through a straight line inclined to the horizontal axis at an angle $\beta$. The points of intersection of this line with the coordinate axes will give us the desired values ​​$d$ and $f$.

Number of divisions

(Research assignment)

An ordinary twenty-centimeter student ruler (with 19 divisions) has the following property of universality: with its help, in one dimension you can mark any segment with a length of 1, 2, 3, ..., 20 cm. This property can be preserved if some of the divisions are erased. What is the minimum number of divisions that must be left (and how to arrange them) in order for the property of universality to be preserved? This label is dedicated to this problem and its generalizations.

Let us call a ruler of length $n$ with $k$ divisions universal (or $(n, k)$-universal) if it can be used to plot any of the segments of length 1, 2, ..., $n$ in one dimension. Thus, the usual student ruler (20, 19) is universal, but there are also (20, 6) universal rulers (see below).
