Heat pump

We are all accustomed to electric heaters. It is enough to plug such a heater into the network, and after some time the room is warm - the energy of the electric current in it is converted into heat. An electric heater is very simple in design and is essentially a heating coil - a resistor. The conversion of electrical energy into heat is carried out almost completely, with the exception of that small portion of the energy that goes into light radiation if the spiral is sufficiently hot and glows. By the way, from this point of view, an electric light bulb is not as good a heater as, say, an electric fireplace, since several percent of its power is lost in the form of light radiation. But still, oddly enough, it is more of a heater than a light source, that is, it mainly heats and does not shine.

It would seem that an electric heater copes with its task perfectly, converting almost all the current energy into the necessary heat. Is it possible for us, by spending some energy, to obtain, say, twice as much heat and thereby save heating costs? At first glance, this is absolutely impossible and contradicts the law of conservation of energy. However, let’s not rush and try to look into this issue in more detail. And we’ll teach you from... a cold car, or simply a refrigerator.

The refrigerator takes heat from the internal container, where the temperature is kept low, and separates it into the room. Such a process cannot be identified by itself. Heat cannot transfer by itself from a cold body to a hot one (this is one of the formulations of the second law of thermodynamics).

“Reverse” heat transfer requires a constant supply of energy - the operation of the refrigerator compressor. We don’t know the details of the refrigerator, but it always requires energy to function.

Let, as a result of work A, the refrigerator take away the amount of heat $Q_2$ from the freezer. Then, according to the law of conservation of energy, an amount of heat $Q_1 = Q_2 + A$ was released in the room. It is therefore clear that when the refrigerator is running, even with the door open, the room is warm.

Let's try to find the efficiency of the refrigerator. But first of all, we remember about the thermal engine, the principle of design and operation of which is exactly the same as that of a refrigeration machine. Essentially, a refrigeration machine is a thermal engine operating in a reverse cycle.

As you know, the working fluid of the engine (Fig. 1) receives from the heater an amount of heat $Q_1$, does work $A'$ and obtains to the refrigerator (do not confuse it with a refrigeration machine!) an amount of heat $Q_2 < Q_1$. The maximum efficiency of an ideal thermal engine is

$$\eta_{\text{max}} = \frac{A'}{Q_1} = \frac{Q_1 - Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{T_1},$$

where $T_1$ is the temperature of the heater, and $T_2$ is the temperature of the refrigerator.
