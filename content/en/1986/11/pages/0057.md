The second task for ninth graders was more difficult. Participants were given a kopka - a switch with terminals at the crown. When you press a button, one circuit opens and another almost simultaneously closes. It was necessary to change the “dead” time - the time during which one circuit is already open, and the second is not yet closed. To do this, you could use a battery, a set of resistors with known resistances (0.1 kOhm, 1 kOhm, 10 kOhm and 100 kOhm), a high-capacity capacitor (500 μF), a semiconductor diode (type D9E), an atomer (“School”) and wires. The idea of ​​​​changing the state is that the capacitor is charged from the battery through a resistor only when it is already open from the switch circuits, and the second is not yet closed, that is, during the “dead” time - this is exactly what a diode is needed for (Fig. 10). To calibrate the device, the capacitor had to be charged to a certain known voltage (for this it was necessary to make a battery voltage divider using resistors) and measure its charge with a galvanometer. In this way, you can measure short periods of time quite accurately.

In the first task for 10th grade, it was necessary to use simple instruments (a test tube, a glass tube with a thin hole of a known diameter, a measuring beaker, graph paper, a ruler, plasticine, ice and water in a glass, a piece of stiff wire and a gauze swab for wiping equipment from moisture) to change the temperature and pressure in the room. To do this, it was enough to make a “gas thermometer” from a test tube, a glass tube and plasticine, separating the volume inside the system from the load volume using a drop of water pushed into the tube. Note that the resulting device is not only a thermometer, but also a barometer. By placing it on its side, and then placing it vertically and calculating the change in volume, you can find the pressure in the room. In this case, it is very important that the test tube is removed from the inside of the system from the load one using a drop of water pushed into the tube, and the experiment is carried out quite quickly - until the evaporation of the drop is noticeable. The temperature can be found by guessing the “thermometer” at 0 °C in a glass of water and ice, and then calculating the change in volume when warmed to room temperature.

It is interesting to note that the accuracy of the results obtained in this way turned out to be quite good - the practical results coincided with the readings of a “real” thermometer and barometer.

The second task for tenth graders was more traditional - determining the electrical circuit of a “black box” with terminals and changing the resistance value of the resistors placed inside. All equipment consisted of this “box” and an avometer. The difficulty lay in the fact that in addition to the usual diodes and resistors in such cases, there was also a high-capacity capacitor (500 μF) inside, which is why the ohmmeter readings changed during the charging of the capacitor. This capacitor also did not discharge immediately, so the method of change had to be carefully thought out.
