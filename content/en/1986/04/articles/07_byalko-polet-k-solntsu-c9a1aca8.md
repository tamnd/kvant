There is a saying: “the most interesting surface is a human face.” Let us ask ourselves: what surface in nature can a human face be compared to in terms of its content and unpredictable variability? There is a strong rival - the surface of the Sun.

Post on the photo of the Sun on p. 19. Unfortunately, it cannot give a complete idea of ​​the beauty and complexity of our star. Its surface is very inexpensive; no two identical areas can be found on it; and all this diversity is constantly changing. In addition, the solar surface is variable in spectrum - when observed in light with different wavelengths, it turns out to be different. This happens because the radiation of different wavelengths we receive from the Sun is formed at different altitudes in the solar atmosphere.

Phenomena on the surface of the Sun - the appearance and movement of spots, their disappearance, solar flares, powerful prominences, etc. - reflect complex processes occurring in the inner layers of our star. What happens on the Sun on an atomic scale is known. It is known, for example, how light interacts with individual atoms, how atoms interact with each other. Let us still calculate (this will be useful to us later) how long such a field from the Earth to the Sun would last.

The trajectory of the fall on the Sun - a straight line segment - is the limiting case of an elongated elliptical orbit with a major hemisphere equal to half the radius of the Earth's orbit. According to Kepler's third law, the squares of the periods of rotation of bodies in orbits are related to the cubes of large semi-sein orbits. This means that the time $t$ of falling on the Sun (that is, the shower of the period of revolution along an elongated orbit with a major hemisphere $R_\oplus/2$) can be determined from the condition $(2t)^2/T_\oplus^2 = (R_\oplus/2)^3/R_\oplus^3$, where $T_\oplus$, $R_\oplus$ are the period of revolution and the radius of the Earth’s orbit. Since $T_\oplus$ is 1 year, $t = 1/4\sqrt{2}$ years ≈ 0.177 years.

The direct field time turned out to be not long, but its initial speed, alas, has not yet been reached. How can you move the field towards the Sun with our own energy costs and with our same starting speed? The most economical field uses Jupiter's gravitational field to decelerate the station in its orbit around the Sun. But in order to carry out this maneuver, you need to fly up to Jupiter at a certain speed. And for this, in turn, it is necessary that, having overcome the earth’s gravity, the station enters orbit, having an initial speed relative to the Sun $v_0=40,5$ km/s; therefore, relative to the Earth, the speed of the station at this time will be 10.5 km/s. So, the field begins with the acceleration of the rocket in the same direction as the speed of the Earth’s movement in its orbit. What starting speed should be given to the rocket?

In order to assume earth's gravity, at launch it needs to be given kinetic energy corresponding to the second cosmic speed $v_{\text{II}}=\sqrt{2gR_\oplus} \approx 11,2$ km/s.*) But be careful - you don’t need to add the second cosmic speed to 10.5 km/s to get the starting speed! From

Once in the gravity field of Jupiter, the station will move along a hyperbolic trajectory that will reject this planet (Fig. 2). In this case, the isolation of the law of conservation of energy, the speed of approaching Jupiter and moving away from it at symmetrical points of the hyperbola will be the same in absolute value, but in direction they can differ. Therefore, the station must enter the trajectory encircling Jupiter with a speed (relative to Jupiter) $u_1$, equal in absolute value to the speed of the planet itself (relative to the Sun) $v_1 = 13,1$ km/s. The direction of the oblique must be such that, when moving away from Jupiter at a point symmetrical to the point of the beginning of the oblique, the direction of the speed of the station $\vec{u}_2$ ($|\vec{u}_2| = |\vec{u}_1| = |\vec{u}_4|$) is opposite to the direction of the speed of movement of Jupiter along its orbit. Then at this point the speed of the station relative to the Sun will become equal to zero - and under the influence of the small solar attraction at such a large distance, our successor will begin to very slowly approach the Sun. These conditions are met in the case when the speed of the station relative to the Sun at the beginning of the approach to Jupiter is equal to $v_1 = 14,3$ km/s (see Fig. 2). And this condition, in turn, determines the initial speed of the station in orbit - 40.5 km/s.

So, in the reporting system associated with Jupiter, the trajectory of the station near the planet is a hyperbola. And in the reporting system associated with the Sun, this section of the trajectory looks as shown in figure 3.

How long will this current towards the Sun continue? We have already calculated the time of falling on the Sun in a straight line. In this case, when the fall begins from the orbit of Jupter, it is necessary to take the period of revolution along the orbit of Jupter $T_u = 11,86$ year (earthly) together with the earthly year. As a result, it turns out that the fall will take $T_u / 4\sqrt{2} \approx 2,1$ years. Let us add to this the flight time along the elliptical section of the trajectory (before the “meeting” with Jupiter) - it turns out to be equal to 1.35 years. In total, the entire flight to the Sun will take almost 3.5 years.
