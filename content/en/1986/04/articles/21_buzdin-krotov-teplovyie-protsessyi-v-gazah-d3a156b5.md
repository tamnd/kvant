Thermal processes in gases

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. I. BUZDIN,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. S. KROTOV

When moving from the “Mechanics” section to the “Molecular Physics and Thermal Phenomena” section, students do not always clearly understand the principles of changes in the approach to describing the systems being studied.

In all bodies existing in nature, the movement of the smallest particles (atoms and molecules) that make up these bodies occurs. It is known to be called thermal motion, and its characteristic feature is disorder. In mechanics, we abstract from this internal motion of particles and study the motion of the body as a whole. Often one can completely neglect the dimensions of a body when describing its movement and consider the body of materials to be a point. A material point is the main object of mechanics.

In the physics of thermal phenomena, on the contrary, it is necessary to take into account the chaotic movement of a gigantic number of particles that make up gases, liquids, and solids (of the order of Avogadro’s number $N_A \sim 10^{23}$ 1/mol). In this case, the main interest is in finding average quantities that describe the state of the system as a whole - temperature, pressure, density, etc.

The greatest attention in school to ideal gases, subordinating Mendeleev - Clapeyron

$$pV = \frac{m}{M}RT,$$

where $p$ is pressure, $V$ is volume, $m$ is mass, $M$ is the molar mass of the gas, $R$ is the universal gas constant, $T$ is the gas temperature.

Any process in which at least one parameter characterizing the state of the gas changes can be considered a thermal gain. So, for example, during the manufacturing process over a given amount of gas, the temperature is maintained constant, and the pressure and volume vary (but $pV = \text{const}$, corresponding to the Boyle-Mariotte law).

Remember that the process with gas parameters is for isocoric and isobaric processes.

Problem 1. The thermal process that occurs with a gas in a closed state on the $V$ - $T$ diagram has the form of a circle (Fig. 1). At what points are the maximum and minimum gas temperatures, volumes and pressures?

Points where maximum and minimum volume and temperature are easily indicated. These are the points of contact of a given circle with the corresponding horizontal and vertical straight lines - isochores and isotherms. To answer the question about pressure, let's construct isobars on the same diagram.

In an isobaric process, the volume of a gas is proportional to its temperature:

$$V = \frac{mR}{Mp}T,$$

therefore, isobars are straight lines passing through the origin, and their slope is less, the greater the pressure. Now it is clear that we must draw tangents to the circle from the origin. In this case, the lower tangent will correspond to the maximum pressure, and the upper one will correspond to the minimum.

The most important characteristic of a gas is its internal energy $U$. From the point of view of molecular-kinetic theory, the internal energy of an ideal gas is the average kinetic energy of the xaotic movement of its molecules. It is important to note that internal energy is uniquely related to the state of the gas.

The change in internal energy during the transition of a gas from one state to another does not depend on the method of transition (on the type of thermal process), but is determined only by the values ​​of the parameters of the final and initial states of the system. Thus, for a monatomic ideal gas
$$U=\frac{3}{2}\frac{m}{M}RT, \quad \Delta U=\frac{3}{2}\frac{m}{M}R\Delta T.$$

For any thermal process, the change in internal energy $\Delta U$ is equal to the sum of the amount of heat $Q$ supplied to the system and the work done on the gas $A$:
$$\Delta U=Q+A.$$
This is the first law of thermodynamics, and essentially the law of conservation of energy during thermal processes. It can be formulated slightly differently:
$$Q=\Delta U+A',$$
where $A'$ is the work done to the system itself.

How the temperature of a gas changes when heat is supplied to it determines the specific heat of the gas. The heat capacity $C$ changes relative to the amount of heat $Q$ supplied to the gas, to the change in gas temperature $\Delta T:C=Q/\Delta T$. When carrying out specific calculations, it is convenient to use both total heat capacity and remote heat capacity (per unit mass) or molar heat capacity (per mole). Let us note that the heat capacity depends not only on the properties of the gas, but also on the conditions under which heat is imparted to it, that is, on the type of thermal process performed on the gas.*) So, for example, the heat capacity of a gas during an isobaric process $C_p$ is always greater than the heat capacity during an isochoric process $C_V$.

Problem 7. In an upper vessel with a volume V under a heavy piston there is a gas at a temperature T. The mass of the piston is M, its area is S. To increase the temperature of the gas by ΔT degrees, it was given the amount of heat Q. Find the change in the internal energy of the gas. Atmospheric pressure p₀, free pressure acceleration g. Ignore trenze.

Since the amount of heat imparted to the gas is known, in order to use the first law of thermodynamics and find out the change in the internal energy of the gas, we must find the work A performed on the gas. The gas is pressed by a piston with a force F, numerically equal to the sum of the gravitational forces of the piston Mg and the force of atomic pressure on the piston p₀S. When the gas is heated, it expands, the piston moves upward, that is, against the action of an external force, therefore the work A is negative and equal to

$$A = - F x = - (Mg + p_0 S) x,$$

where x is the displacement of the piston.

During heating, the force F does not change, therefore, the gas pressure remains constant, and then, in accordance with the Gay-Lussac law,

$$\frac{V'}{V} = \frac{T'}{T},$$

where V' = V + ΔV is the volume of gas after heating, and T' = T + ΔT is its temperature. From here we find the change in volume ΔV and the displacement of the piston x:

$$\Delta V = V \frac{\Delta T}{T}, x = \frac{\Delta V}{S} = \frac{V \Delta T}{TS}.$$

Now we can determine the work done on the gas:

$$A = - F x = - (Mg + p_0 S) \frac{V \Delta T}{TS}$$

and the change in its internal energy:

$$\Delta U = Q + A = Q - (Mg + p_0 S) \frac{V \Delta T}{TS}.$$

Problem 8*. A process is performed over an ideal diatomic gas in which the pressure and volume of the gas are related by the ratio p=αV. What is the molar temperature of the gas during expansion in such a process? Molar temperature at constant volume C_V = 5/2 R.

Consider a process with one mole of an ideal gas. If its volume does not change, then no work is done and Q = ΔU = C_V ΔT, that is, the temperature value at a constant volume immediately allows us to find the change in the internal energy of the gas:

$$\Delta U = \frac{5}{2} R \Delta T.$$

Now we determine the work done A'.

Let, as a result of the expansion of a gas from volume V to V + ΔV, its temperature changes from T to T + ΔT (we consider ΔV and ΔT small). The gas pressure increased from p to p + Δp, where Δp = αΔV. Since ΔV is small, the gas work can be considered equal to

$$A' = p \Delta V.$$

Then we write the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation for a mole of gas at temperature T and T + ΔT:

$$p V = RT, (p + \Delta p) (V + \Delta V) = R (T + \Delta T).$$

Subtracting the first from the second equation and running the product of small quantities ΔpΔV compared to Δp and ΔV, we obtain

$$p \Delta V + V \Delta p = R \Delta T.$$

Since V = p/α and Δp = αΔV, the product VΔp = (p/α) (αΔV) = pΔV and pΔV + VΔp = 2pΔV = RΔT.

Hence the work done by the gas

$$A' = p \Delta V = \frac{R \Delta T}{2}$$

and the amount of heat supplied to the gas.

Thus, the molar temperature of the gas in this process will be equal to

$$C = \frac{Q}{\Delta T} = 3R.$$
