Capillaries and wetting

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. BOROVOY

You decided to go out of town. Gather your things, see if anything is forgotten. And here... there are pots of flowers on the window. It is impossible to take them with you, and there is no one who could water the flowers in your absence. What to do? Fill flower pot trays with water? This method of watering is not suitable for all plants - many of them do not tolerate excess moisture. And most importantly, it is designed for a short period of time - a few days, a week.

This question is not new. Flower growers have long come up with a variety of designs for “auto-sawdust” that work reliably and for a long time and even dose water for watering flowers.

The simplest auto saw (Fig. 1) can be easily made by yourself. Water is poured into a container covered with a lid. One end of a tightly rolled gauze tourniquet is lowered into this vessel, and its other end is buried in the ground. Water flows to the plant through a rope, and the speed of its flow can be adjusted by changing the thickness of the rope, its slope, etc. The rope should be placed inside a rubber tube, then the water will spill less and evaporate on the way to color.

Both in the bundle, and in the soil filling the pot, and partly in the plants themselves, water moves and is retained due to a phenomenon called capillarity.

A capillary is a thin tube, its total internal diameter is less than one millimeter. If a capillary made of pure glass is lowered into a vessel with water, then the liquid level in it will be higher than in the vessel (Fig. 2). The thinner the tube, the greater this difference. This occurs due to wetting of clean glass with water. In a wide glass vessel, the surface of the water only bends at the very walls.

In soil, capillaries form between soil particles. Thanks to this, moisture can rise to a considerable height: up to 2 meters in sand, and much higher in pure clay. The process is slow and sometimes lasts several weeks or even months. (Try to come up with and conduct experiments yourself in which you could observe

If the inner surface of the tube is not wetted by liquid, then its level in the capillary is lower than in a wide vessel (Fig. 3). The shape of the surface of the liquid near the walls of the vessel will also change.

Interestingly, wetting (or non-wetting) determines how a drop of liquid will behave on the surface of another liquid. For example, a drop of sunflower oil or kerosene spreads over water in a thin layer, up to a monomolecular layer (that is, only one molecule thick!) This behavior of wetting liquids allows the English physicist Lord Rayleigh to estimate the size of one molecule.

He placed a drop of olive oil on a large surface of water and first made sure that the oil stain covered the surface with an even layer, without oil “plaques” and “windows” of water. For this, Rayleigh used small pieces of camphor. If such a piece hits the surface of the water, it begins to “throw around.” The fact is that camphor dissolves in water and at the same time the surface tension of the water decreases. But dissolution occurs unevenly: on one side the particles are faster, on the other - slower, and a difference in surface tension forces begins to act on a piece of camphor, causing it to move. You can easily observe this phenomenon, but in order for the experiment to be successful, you must not touch the water and pieces of camphor with your hands (it is best to scrape them off with a knife directly above the surface of the water). But camphor does not dissolve in oil and behaves calmly.

So, having made sure that the oil film was homogeneous, the scientist determined the radius of the spot $R$ and found its thickness $l$:

$$l = \frac{V}{\pi R^2},$$

where $V$ is the volume of an oil drop. If we assume that the drop has spread into a monomolecular layer, then the thickness of the spot is a characteristic size of the oil molecule. The resulting value is close to $10^{-9}$ m.

Wetting very often comes to our aid: we lubricate the parts of the machine that are falling apart, the liquid lubricant envelops the surface of the metal and holds it there; molten oloo wets pure copper, brass, aluminum during soldering; the glue spreads over the surface of the materials being glued.

Almost all ancient lighting devices, starting from ancient lamps in which the wick was immersed in petroleum, vegetable oil or fat, and ending with kerosene lamps, also used the wetting phenomenon: the flammable substance rose through the capillaries of the wick and gradually drained away.

Observing this unlearned behavior of a fluid as it moves against the forces of gravity inspired many inventors to create the Perpetuum mobile, a perpetual motion that would exploit the properties of capillaries. The projects were very diverse and appeared not only in antiquity or the Middle Ages, but even at the beginning of the 20th century. Most often, the idea was as follows: liquid (usually water) should rise through the wick from the lower vessel to the upper one, and then flow down through a tube or tray, leading to

Wetting plays a very special role under conditions of weightlessness.
This is what pilot-cosmonaut A. A. Serebrov* said:)

“Imagine a problem: drink liquid into a vessel without loss. On the spacecraft, water is supplied from the tap in portions of 25 cm³. Presented to themselves, these portions collect into spherical drops, as they should in weightlessness; These drops, as a result of various random influences, are then crushed into smaller ones...

If you act as on Earth and drink water from a tap into the neck of a vessel, then it will begin to push out air from the vessel, in which drops of different sizes are suspended - in other words, it will push itself out.

If a low-speed jet is directed directly at the wall of the vessel, then the water, wetting the wall, will adhere to it. Then there will be no suspended drops - at least until the vessel is shaken. In this way, you can drink liquid into a vessel without loss.
