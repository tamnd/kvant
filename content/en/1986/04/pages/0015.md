How does the Moon move?

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. A. BRONSHTEN

Probably, many readers «Kvant» will be surprised: what an easy question! Everyone knows that the Moon, the only natural satellite of the Earth, moves in an ellipse, making one revolution every 27.3 days. And this movement occurs in strict accordance with Kepler’s laws, which are a consequence of the law of universal gravitation discovered by Newton. All this is true, but...

The theory of the motion of the Moon turns out to be one of the most difficult problems of celestial mechanics, the solution of which requires two and a half centuries (counting only from Newton). Wonderful scientists like Euler, Clairaut, d'Alembert put a lot of effort into the development of this theory...

So what were the difficulties? How to overcome their daring?

Four lunar months

The movement of the Moon was studied by astronomers of Ancient Babylon. They carefully recorded the moments of solar and lunar eclipses. Based on these data, the ancient Greek astronomer Hiparchus (2nd century BC) determined the duration of the main periods characterizing the movement of the Moon. By tradition, we will call them “months.”

Four such months have been known since ancient times. The sidereal, or sidereal, month is the period of the Moon’s revolution around the Earth; it is determined by the movement of the Moon relative to the fixed stars. A synodic month is the period of time between two successive new moons (or half moons). An anomalous month is the period between two successive passages of the Moon through its orbital period. Finally, the draconic month is the period of time between two passages of the Moon through the ascending node of its orbit - the point of intersection of the orbit with the plane of the ecliptic (teler we know that this is nothing more than the plane of the earth's orbit, but in the time of Hiparchus the Earth was considered stationary, and the plane of the ecliptic was considered the plane of the orbit along which the Sun moved around the Earth).

Hyparchus calculated, based on the datos observed by astronomers of obscurations, that every 345 years the positions of the Sun, Moon, perigree and nodes of the lunar orbit are repeated; in other words, the 345-year cycle contains an integer number of all four months. The durations of the lunar months derived from the Hiparchus cycle differ only by a small fraction of a second from the values ​​that astronomers now obtain, having accumulated observations for another 21 century (these values ​​are given on p. 14).

Why the synodic month is longer than the sidereal month is not difficult to explain. The change of lunar phases depends on the position of the Moon relative to the Sun. The Moon, together with the Earth, revolves around the Sun. The direction of this movement coincides with the direction of the Moon's revolution around the Earth. As a result, the movement of the Moon along its “near-Earth” orbit occurs as if with a lag: the repetition of lunar phases occurs not through the sidereal month, but after a longer period, which is called the synodic month. It is not difficult to obtain the following correspondence between the durations of the sidereal (S) and synodic (P) months:

$$\frac{1}{S} = \frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{T},$$

where $T$ is the duration of the earthly year (in days).

The fact that the anomalistic month is the length of the sidereal one is essentially aware that the lunar orbit itself as a whole is expressed (in its own plane), and the line connecting the perigree and the apogee of the orbit (that is, the major axis of the lunar ellipse) is called the linear apse, turning in the same direction in which the Moon moves. So the Moon constantly comes to “catch up” with its perigree.
