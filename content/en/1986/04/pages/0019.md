In 1752, Clairaut presented to the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences a large thematic entitled “The Theory of the Moon, Derived from a Single Principle of Attraction, Inversely Proportional to the Squares of Distances.” This work was awarded a prize and published in St. Petersburg. In it, Clairo solves the problem of building up an ellipse, which is what the orbit of the Moon actually is. In his work, Clairaut showed for the first time that ray inequalities manifest themselves not only in the longitude and spiking of the Moon, but also in the distance from it to the Earth. In the formulas of Clairaut's theory, each of the quantities (longitude, distance and distance) is expressed by the sum of 20 terms of the series.

Clairaut's work serves as an impetus for new research. D'Alembert decided to check Clairaut's conclusions and came to the same results, albeit in a different way. A full member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, Leonhard Euler (1707-1783), improved Clairaut's theory, making it more convenient for compiling tables of the motion of the Moon. Such tables were soon compiled by the German astronomer Tobias Mayer. 20 years later, Euler again turned to the theory of the motion of the Moon. In 1772, he published a work entitled “The Theory of the Motion of the Moon, Explained in a New Way.” Indeed, in this work a fundamentally new way of constructing a lunar theory was proposed.

The ideas contained in Euler's second lunar theory make it possible, in principle, to achieve the most accurate description of the movement of the Moon. However, these ideas were ahead of their time - the development of science was not sufficient at that time to obtain a final solution to the problem on their basis. And lunar theories continued to be developed along the original, “prototype” path.

How to build theories?

The theory of Clairaut, d'Alembert and Euler's first lunar theory belonged to the analytical class. In these theories, expressions for the coordinates of a celestial body (or for the elements of its orbit) are derived as solutions to the equations of motion given by Newton's theory. The construction of such theories then required enormous work. The creator of one of the best analytical theories, French astronomer Charles Delaunay (1816-1872), spent 20 years of continuous work on it. But Delaunay’s theory can be applied not only to the Moon, but also to any other satellite of the planet, including the artificial satellite of the Earth.

In numerical theory, a whole series of orbital elements is taken from observation and then refined during calculations. The best numerical theory of the motion of the Moon was the theory of the German astronomer P. A. Hansen (1795-1874), on the basis of which tables of the motion of the Moon were compiled, which served astronomers for almost half a century - until the early twenties of our century.

Much greater success befell the numerical-analytical theories, on which Euler's second lunar theory was based. In this case, only a few quantities are taken from observation and substituted into the equation of motion before solving them.

In 1888, American astronomer George Hill (1838–1914) used Euler's ideas to construct his theory of the motion of the Moon. He managed to obtain the speed of movement of the perihelion of the lunar orbit analytically. All the inequalities of the Moon's motion were divided by him into classes, depending on what quantities were included in one or another inequality. Thus, it was always, as it were, “divided” into several separate tasks, each of which was solved separately. Hill's theory was completed by the American astronomer Ernest Brown (1866-1938). He managed to overcome, one by one, all the remaining difficulties of the theory and achieve a precision that reached to satisfy the observations of the naal and the mid-twentieth century.
