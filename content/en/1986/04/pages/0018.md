Yes, the theory of the motion of the Moon turned out to be a tough nut to crack both for Newton himself and for a long series of his followers. What is the main difficulty?

We know that the main force acting on the planets is the attraction of the Sun. Under the influence of this force, the planet must describe a Keplerian ellipse. The attraction of other planets, whose masses are thousands, hundreds of thousands and millions of times less than the mass of the Sun, leads to only small deviations from the Kepler ellipse, which we like to call perturbations. These disturbances are small and easy to calculate. (It is known that from disturbances in the motion of Uranus, astronomers J. Adams and W. Lever independently calculated the position and orbit of a previously unknown planet causing these disturbances - it turned out to be Neptune.)

In the case of the Moon, the situation is different. The Moon in its image around the Earth is constantly subject to disturbance from the most massive body of the solar system - the Sun. In addition, these disturbances change during the anomalous location (with a change in the distance from the Moon to the Earth) and throughout the year (with a change in the distance from the Earth to the Sun).

Competition of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences

Three quarters of a century have passed since the publication of Newton’s immortal work “The Mathematical Science of Natural Physiology.” And although Newton himself tried to develop theories of the movement of the Moon, his theory did not provide the required accuracy. But in those years, the theory of the movement of the Moon had practical significance: the astronomical longitude of the Moon was used to determine the geographic longitude of a place on Earth. Since the Moon moved across the sky by an average of 13° per day, its position in the sky at a given hour depended on the longitude of the place. Sailors and travelers used this to determine geographical longitudes.

And in 1750, the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences announced a competition for the best research on the following topic: “To show whether all the inequalities that are observed in the movement of the Moon agree with Newton’s theory, and what should be the true theory of all these inequalities, so that it can be used to determine with complete accuracy the position of the Moon at any time.”

This formulation was not chosen by chance. We remember that Newton failed to theoretically determine the rate of mixing of the lunar perigee. In 1745, two remarkable French mathematicians, Alexis Claude Clairaut (1713-1765) and Jean Leron d'Alembert (1717-1783), tried to solve the same problem. Both of them were members of the Paris Academy of Sciences, obvious rivals in science, working on the lunar problem in complete inequality. Solving the problem of the motion of the lunar perigee, they both came to the same conclusion as Newton: the period of rotation of the major axis of the lunar ellipse should theoretically be twice as long as it follows from observation. Both scientists even suggested that Newton's law was inaccurate and required amendments. It was this conclusion of a pillar of authoritative scientists that caused the announcement of a competition by the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences with the above formulation.

But already a few months before the announcement of the competition, in May 1749, Clairaut found the reason for the “discrepancy” between Newton’s theory and observations. The theory was not to blame. The fact is that the analytical (in the form of a formula) expression for the perigee mixture given by the theories was a power series of the form $k_0 + k_1 m + k_2 m^2 + \dots + k_n m^n + \dots$, where $m$ is the ratio of the daily mixtures of the Earth and the Moon along their orbits ($m \approx 1/13$), $k_n$ are numerical coefficients. The value of $m$ is small compared to unity, and each next member of the series is much smaller than the previous one. Both Newton, and d'Alembert, and Clairaut used to calculate the value of perigee mixing, limiting themselves to only the first term of the series. This, like the Clairaut agreement, led to the result of a discrepancy between the theoretically calculated and the actual rate of mixing of the lunar perigee.
