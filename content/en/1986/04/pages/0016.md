Lunar inequalities

With the name of the ancient Greek astronomer Claudius Ptolemy (c. 100 - c. 165) it usually represents the entire geocentric system of the world, which Copernicus, Giordano Bruno, Galileo, and Kepler fought against. But Ptolemy made a great contribution to the development of astronomy, in particular, in the theory of the movement of the Moon. True, at that time we could only talk about kinematic theory, or the causes and true laws of motion of celestial bodies were not known.

For a long period of development of astronomical science, it was believed that celestial bodies could only move uniformly in a circle. If the movement looked more complex, then it can be represented as a combination of movements in several circles. This is exactly what Ptolemy did with the Moon.

The Moon's movement across the sky was uneven. To represent it as a combination of uniform movements around a circle, it was necessary to first determine the magnitude of the deviations from the uniform movement, or the so-called inequalities. And in the time of Ptolemy, and even in the time of Kepler and much later, it was customary to call “inequalities” the individual components of the deviation of the position of the Moon from the position of an imaginary point moving uniformly along the ecliptic with a period equal to the middle month. Thus, the words “inequality” in astronomy mean something completely different from what it means in mathematics. However, in modern science one encounters similar examples literally at every step. Thus, the word “element” means one thing in chemistry, but something completely different in electrical engineering. No one will confuse the nucleus of a comet, the nucleus of a living cell and the nucleus of an atom. And now we will meet with another example of the use of terms accustomed to us in a different sense.

All positions in the sky of the Moon, Sun, stars and planets already during the time of Ptolemy changed in the coordinate system that filled geographic longitude and latitude. And these coordinates were called almost the same: astronomical longitude and astronomical latitude. Only the latitude of the celestial bodies without the axis of the ecliptic plane (recall that in those days this was the name of the plane in which the orbit of the Sun orbiting around the Earth lay), and longitude - from the point of vernal equivocality, at which the Sun crosses the celestial equator once a year. Having taken up the definition of lunar inequalities, Ptolemy considered mainly inequalities in longitude, deducing latitude from the inclination of the lunar orbit, equal to 5° - this is exactly how this angle was determined by Hipparchus, “a man of hard work and a worshiper of truth,” as Ptolemy called him. (According to modern data, Hipparchus was mistaken by only 8 minutes of arc).

Ptolemy presented the general inequality E of the motion of the Moon in longitude with the following formula (in modern notation):

$$E = 6^\circ 15' \sin l + 1^\circ 18' \sin (2D - l) + 19' \sin 2l.$$
