Traces in the sand and... the structure of matter

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. G. ASLAMAZOV

Of all the two hundred billion men, women and children who have ever walked on wet sand since the modern world until the meeting of the British Association at Aberdeen in 1885, how many are there who, when asked, “Did the sand shrink under your foot?” would answer differently than “yes”!
Lord Kelvin, Baltic Lectures, 1904

Have you ever thought about the answer to this question? At first glance, it seems that by compacting the sand, we always make it denser, darken the sands and press them closer to each other. But in reality the matter may be the circumstance itself. And the proof of this is the traces that remain on some occasions when you step on wet sand near the seashore or river. This is what O. Reynolds, a scientist known for his work on hydrodynamics, said about this in a report at a meeting of the British Association in 1885: “When the Hora presses on sand, dense after a gentle tide, the area around the foot immediately becomes dry... The pressure of the foot loosens the sand, and the stronger it is, the more water leaves... It makes the sand dry until the blue arrives sufficient amount of water."

So why does pressure increase the space between the sands, and the available water is no longer enough to fill it? It is no coincidence that this question worried scientists of the 19th century. The answer to this has a direct bearing on the atomic structure of matter. Yes-

Dense packing of balls

Is it possible to fill the entire space with solid balls? Of course not - there will always be free spaces between them. The fraction of space occupied by the balls is called their packing density. The closer the balls are located, the less free space between them, the greater the packing density. When is the maximum packing density of identical hard spheres achieved? The answer to this question provides the key to solving the “mystery” of footprints in the sand.

Let us first examine a simpler case—packing of identical circles on a plane. Dense packing of circles can be achieved by fitting them into mosaics composed of regular polygons that fill the entire plane. There are only three ways to construct such mosaics - from regular triangles, squares and regular hexagons. Packing circles using square and hexagonal motes are shown in figure 1. Further, “by eye” you can see that the second method is more economical. An accurate calculation (check it yourself) shows that in this case approximately 90.7% of the plane is filled with circles, while in the first case - only about 78%. The hexagonal method of packing on a plane is the densest. Apparently, because of this, bees use it when building honeycombs.

Dense packing of balls in space can be accomplished as follows. Let us place the first layer of balls on the plane in the most dense way already known to us. Then you can put a second layer of the same on them. If each ball of the top layer is located exactly above the bottom, so that all the balls are inscribed in a cubic honeycomb, then too much space is wasted. With this packaging method, only 52% of the space is filled.

It is clear how the balls can be packed more tightly. To do this, the upper ball must be placed in the hole formed by the tray of adjacent lower balls. But at the same time, the upper balls will not be able to fill all the holes - one of the two adjacent recesses is always free (Fig. 2). Therefore, when we lay the third layer of balls, this can be done in two ways: either placing the balls of the third layer above those recesses in the first layer that the balls of the second layer left free (the center of one of the balls on figure 2 will be at point A), or just above the balls of the first layer (in this case, the center of one of the balls of the third layer will be at point B). It is saved for subsequent layers. As a result, we obtain two ways of packing balls, shown in space in figure 3. In general cases, about 74% of the space is filled with balls.

It is easy to calculate that with this packaging method, each ball is in contact with 12 neighboring balls. The points of contact form the vertices of the 14-hedron. Its faces are alternating squares and equilateral triangles. Thus, the second method of close packing produces the Kepler cuboctahedron shown in figure 4.

Until now, we have considered only such methods of packing balls in which they fit into periodic “honeycombs”. Is it possible to achieve dense packing without this condition? One of the methods is shown in figure 5. The balls in each plane are located on the sides of regular pentagons (pentagons). In each pentagon, neighboring balls touch each other, but the balls, relative to different pentagons of the same plane, are separated in space. The sides of the pentagons of alternating layers contain alternately four and odd numbers of balls. The space filling factor in such a structure is 72% and is only slightly inferior to the case of dense packing shown in figure 2. It is possible to pack balls without forming a lattice from the centers more densely, reaching a filling factor of 74%, but whether even denser packings exist - this question remains open to this day.

Let's return to the next sand. We now know that there are special packings of balls that leave very little empty space between them. If you violate this arrangement by, for example, removing the balls of one layer from the holes of another layer, then the gaps between the balls will increase. It is clear, however, that grains of sand are never packaged in a special way. How to achieve dense packing of sand grains?

Let's remember everyday experience. You need to fill the vessel coarsely so that it contains the maximum amount of it. What do you do about it? Use the vessel or move through it, adding the desired effect. Then, after tightly packing the krula into the vessel, you can use this technique to reduce some additional amount of it.

A scientific study of this issue was undertaken in the 50s by the English scientist G. Scott. He filled spherical bottles of different sizes with ball bearings. If bottles are filled without shaking so that the balls are arranged randomly, the following dependence of the packing density on the number of balls is experimentally observed:

$$\varrho_1 = 0,6 - 0,37 / \sqrt[3]{N},$$

where $N$ is the total number of balls. If the number of balls is very large (and in experiments $N$ it reached several thousand), then it is clear that the packing density tends to become constant and corresponds to filling 60% of the space. But if you shake the bottle as it fills, the packing density increases:

$$\varrho_2 = 0,64 - 0,33 / \sqrt[3]{N}.$$

True, even in this case it turns out to be much less than 74%, corresponding to the regular arrangement of the balls.

It is worth thinking about the dependencies given here. Why is the correction inversely proportional to $\sqrt[3]{N}$? The balls located near the walls of the vessel are in a special position compared to the balls in the volume and affects the packing density. The magnitude of their contribution is proportional to the ratio of the surface area $\sim R^2$ to the volume of the vessel $\sim R^3$ and kills in inverse proportion to the size of the system $R$ (under the volume of the system we drink the full volume of space occupied by the balls along with free spaces). Size $R \sim \sqrt[3]{N}$, since the volume of the system is proportional to the total number of balls. Such dependencies often arise in physics when it is necessary to take into account effective surfaces.

Thus, precise experiments confirm everyday experience and show that by shaking a granular medium, a greater packing density can be achieved. But why does this still happen? The fact is that a stable equilibrium position always corresponds to a minimum of potential energy. The ball may lie steadily in the hollow, but it will definitely bounce from the top of the slide. Something similar is happening here. When shaken, the balls are rolled into free spaces, the packing density increases, and the total volume of the system decreases. As a result, the level of filling the vessel with balls decreases, and consequently, the center of gravity lowers and the potential energy of the system decreases.

Now, finally, we can imagine with sufficient clarity what is happening to the sand. The agitation of the water meets the sand, and as a result, a dense packing of the sand is achieved. By squeezing the cake with our foot, we break this packaging and increase the size of the pores. Water from the upper layers of sand goes into the depths, filling these enlarged gaps. As a result, the pitch “drops out”. When the foot is removed, the deformity disappears, the tight packing is restored, and the water flowing out of the new reduced spaces fills the trace left by the foot. It can also be used in such a way that after strong pressure the pitch does not recover. Then the trail will become wet again only when the water rises from the lower layers and fills the pores.

It is curious that this is a property of granular media found in Indian faqra. One of their trios consists of repeatedly sticking a narrow knife into a vessel with a narrow neck, filled to the top with rice. At some point, the knife got stuck in the rice, and the vessel could be lifted with the knife. It is clear that the trick was that the vessel, when filled with rice, met, achieving a tight pack. Sticking a knife in disrupted this packaging and increased the amount of space between the grains of rice. Since the volume of the vessel did not change, the forces squeezing the grains of rice increased, and therefore the tension between them and the knife increased. At some point it turned out to be enough to pull the knife out of the vessel.

There is, however, an equally important class of substances - amorphous bodies in which there is no long-range order. Liquid bodies are in an amorphous state. But a solid body can also be amorphous. Amorphous materials have recently found important applications in technology. Unique properties of amorphous metal alloys (metallic glasses). They are obtained by cooling liquid metal very quickly - at a speed of the order of several thousand degrees per second. This can be achieved, for example, by sprinkling small drops of metal on the surface of a fast-running cold disk. The drop is “smeared” over the disk in a very thin layer (several micrometers thick), and it is good for the warm-water metal to cool so quickly that its atoms simply do not have time to arrange themselves correctly. It turned out that amorphous alloys have increased hardness, high corrosion resistance, and an optimal combination of electrical and magnetic properties. The scope of such materials is expanding rapidly.

Sum power
Combinations of letters - angrams - naturally arise when changing two or more polynomials, and combinators - coefficients of angrams - when translating similar terms.

The formula for the square of the sum of two numbers is well known:
$(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$.

A similar formula can be obtained for the square of the sum of three or more terms. For example, let’s square the sum $a+b+c$:
$(a+b+c)(a+b+c) = aa + ab + ac + ba + bb + bc + ca + cb + cc = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ac$.

A similar formula is true for $(a+b+c)^2$ (on figure 3 each annual expresses the area of ​​its rectangle); In general, the square of the sum of $n$ numbers is calculated as follows: you need to add up all the squares of these $n$ numbers and add the doubled sum of all possible pairwise products of these numbers.

If we carefully change the variability of $a+b+c$ when folded into itself, we also get the formula:
$(a+b+c)^3 = a^3 + b^3 + c^3 + 3(a^2b + a^2c + b^2a + b^2c + c^2a + c^2b) + 6abc$.

Let us explain how you can get the coefficients 1, 3 and 6 here without tedious multiplication and matching of similar terms. This is the number of angrams already familiar to us. The term $a^3$ can only be obtained in one way - when from each parenthesis
$(a+b+c)(a+b+c)(a+b+c)$ is taken from the letter $a$. To obtain the coefficient for $a^2b$, you need to take $b$ from one bracket, and $a$ from the other two, that is, this term corresponds to the angram $aab, aba, baa$ from two letters $a$ and one letter $b$: their number is $(2+1)!(2!1!) = 3$. Finally, the coefficient of $abc$ is equal to the number $3!/(1!1!1!) = 6$ of angrams (permutations) of a word from three different letters $a, b, c$.

A similar formula will be obtained for the cube of the sum of a larger number of terms, for example
$(a+b+c+d+e)^3 = (a^3+...) + 3(a^2b+...) + 6(abc+...)$.
