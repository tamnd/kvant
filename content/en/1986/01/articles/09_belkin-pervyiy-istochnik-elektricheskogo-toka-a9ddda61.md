Electric current, even in the simplest electrical circuit, can seem like a somewhat mysterious phenomenon. In fact, current is the ordered movement of electrical charges, such as electrons in a metal conductor. Their ordered movement will be caused by an electric field. But, as is known, the work of an electrostatic field along a closed path is zero (and electrical circuits in which a constant electric current flows are always closed). Nevertheless, work is done in the circuit when current passes. Due to this work, for example, the conductors heat up.

Why is there an electric current in the circuit and why is work done when it passes?

Both are possible only because somewhere in the circuit there are some non-electrostatic forces that can create and maintain electrostatic fields in the circuit and whose work is not zero. These forces are called “external forces.” The place in the circuit where they operate is called (not entirely successfully) the “current source”.

What are these side forces and how do they work? As an example, consider the first chemical direct current source in history. It was invented at the very end of the 18th century by the Italian physicist Alexandro Volga (1745-1827). Now this source is called the Volga element. It consists of zinc and copper electrodes immersed in a sulfuric acid solution.

Note that copper and zinc do not consist of neutral atoms, but of positive ions of the corresponding metal and electrons that have been detached from the atoms and become, as they say, free. It should be borne in mind that the acid solution does not consist of

neutral molecules of water (H₂O) and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). A significant part of the H₂SO₄ molecules in water turns into three ions - two positively charged hydrogen ions H⁺ and a negatively charged SO₄²⁻ ion with a double charge:

H₂SO₄ → 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻.

Let's see what happens when electrodes are immersed in such a solution (see figure). Let's start with zinc.

When zinc is immersed in an acid solution, a chemical reaction between SO₄²⁻ and Zn²⁺ ions begins, as a result of which zinc ions are torn off from the electrode and transferred to the solution. In this case, an excess of electrons appears on the electrode, and it becomes negatively charged. As Zn²⁺ ions accumulate in the solution, some of them, pressed down by the electrode, return back. Eventually, dichamic equilibrium is established: the number of ions leaving the zinc is equal to the number of ions returning to it. But the electrode remains negatively charged, and the solution near the electrode (due to zinc ions) receives a positive charge.

The appearance of additional positive ions near the zinc electrode causes a redistribution of the ions already present inside the solution. Often negative ions from a neighboring layer move close to the electrode, and some positive ions are carried to a more distant layer. Similar movements occur in all layers of the solution, right down to the layer adjacent to the copper electrode. Here the following process occurs.

Unlike zinc, copper almost does not dissolve in acid, that is, it does not send its ions into the solution. On the contrary, positive hydrogen ions from the solution, falling on the copper

electrode, free electrodes are taken from it and neutralized. The copper becomes positively charged, and the solution around it acquires a negative charge.

Thus, the copper electrode in the Volta element forms a positive stripe, and the zinc electrode forms a negative stripe. The potential difference between them is approximately 1.1 V.

Let's now see what happens if the electrodes are connected with a metal conductor. Free electrodes in the outer part of the circuit (in the conductor) begin to move from zinc, where they are abundant, to copper, where they are deficient. This means that an electric current will arise in the outer part of the circuit, directed from the copper to the zinc.

But what is in the Volta element itself? Due to the loss of electrons from the zinc, the balance between the zinc electrode and the raster is disturbed, as a result of which an additional number of zinc ions will pass into the raster, thereby maintaining a negative charge of the electrode. And due to the arrival at the copper electrode, a larger number of positive hydrogen ions from the solution can be neutralized at this electrode. At the same time, the copper also maintains a positive charge.

We see, therefore, that while in the outer part of the circuit free electrodes move from the zinc electrode to the copper one, inside the source of the circuit movement: positive from zinc to copper and negative from copper to zinc. So in a closed circuit there is a continuous circular process of moving electric charges, that is, electric current.

What forces do the work to maintain a constant position of potentials between the copper and zinc electrodes? These are the so-called chemical forces operating within the Volta element. In other words, we can say that the source of electric current energy is the energy released during chemical reactions between the electrodes and the acid solution.
