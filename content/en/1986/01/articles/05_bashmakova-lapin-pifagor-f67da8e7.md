The father of Pythogoras is called Mnesarchus, a famous stone nester. According to the English biographer, being a young man thirsty for knowledge, Pythogor left his native island to travel around the world, and beat almost all Hellenic and many foreign countries. Pythogoras served to replace the Greek scientists and admired the wonders of the East. They said that he was in the hidden sanctuaries of Egypt, visited the Chaldean sages and Persian magicians, and descended into the tanish cave of Crete, with which the myth of the modernity of the gods was associated.

Returning to Samos, Pythogoras found his homeland under the tyranny of Polycrates. Seeing, as an English biographer says, that tyranny was too strong for “a free man to endure arbitrariness and de-despotism with dignity,” he decided to retire to Southern Italy, where the replaced Hellenic colonies were then located. He settled in the Italian city of Crotone, for whose citizens he drew up laws. They said that while going around foreign cities, Pythogors healed large ones, wild animals allowed him to stroke them, rivers greeted him with human voices, that he could be seen in two different places at the same time. This service was facilitated by both the philosopher’s extraordinary personality and his religious and mystical teaching.

Pythagorean Union. Soon a large circle of disciples devoted to him arose around Pythogoras, “for he so far surpassed the weakness of all the others that all youth would like to be his disciples.” Soon the Pythagorean Union or Brotherhood was organized from them, which was at the same time a scientific society, a religious union and a political club. Access to it was not open to everyone. The subject had to undergo a long test; they even said that he was ordered to remain silent for five years. Although a century after the death of Pythogoras the union had already disintegrated, almost all the great mathematicians of antiquity called themselves Pythogoreans.

Mathematical proof. We have already said that Pythogoras was the creator of mathematics as a science. But didn’t people before him know how to count, add and multiply fractions, and calculate the simplest areas and volumes? Of course they did. They could even solve quadratic equations! And yet, mathematics as a science did not yet exist. Mathematical knowledge, which was passed down almost unchanged from generation to generation over thousands of years, was not organized in a system - it was rather based on collected recipes, the correctness of which could be verified when solving specific problems.

Pythogoras realized that the basis of mathematics is proof. Where it is not there is no mathematics. What is evidence and why is it needed?

Let's say we want to establish that the sum of the angles of a triangle is 180°. How to do this? Let's do this: carefully draw a triangle (in ink in a notebook or with chalk on a blackboard) and change its angles using a protractor. Adding them up we get a number close to 180°. What did we reveal? Only that the triangle we drew has a sum of angles equal to, say, 180°01′. We can continue our changes by looking at other triangles being drawn, and we will each time get a result close to the one we want to establish. The results will be approximate, firstly, because the ruler we used will not be absolutely straight (which can be seen by looking at it through a magnifying glass). Secondly, the angles changed by the protractor will not be found close to the point. But even if we managed to get a perfectly accurate ruler and protractor, we still would not have established our proposal, because there are an infinite number of triangles and we will never be able to find the sum of the angles for all of them.

Pythogoras was apparently the first to come to the idea that mathematics considers abstract objects (that is, ideally straight lines without width, points without dimensions, etc.) and that their properties should be established not through changes, but through reasoning. For example, to prove the theorem on the sum of the angles of a triangle, let’s draw, as Pythogoras did, an arbitrary triangle (it can be drawn by hand, without using a ruler) and draw a straight line LM through its vertex B, parallel to the base AC (Fig. 1). Then $\angle LBA = \angle BAC = \alpha$, and $\angle MBC = \angle BCA = \gamma$ (as crossed angles). But the sum of $\angle LBA + \angle ABC + \angle MBC$ is a reverse angle, that is, equal to 180°. This means that the sum of the angles of any triangle will be equal to 180°.

However, in our proof we relied on the properties of parallel lines, which also need proof. Their proof will be based on some other properties and so on. So, having considered an infinite number of triangles, we “fell into the clutches” of another infinity - we came to the need to carry out an infinite number of proofs. We have reduced proposition A to proposition B, which will be reduced to proposition C, that is, $A \Leftarrow B \Leftarrow C \Leftarrow \dots$.

How can this be? It is clear that at some point we must stop, some proposals must be accepted without proof. But in this way we can get an infinite number of “unprovable” propositions, in other words, again “fall into the clutches” of infinity! And the next great thought of Pythogoras (or his contemporaries) is that in geometrics it is possible to select a finite number of unprovable propositions (later called axioms), from which it will be possible to deduce all the others.

So, mathematics, according to Pythogoras, studies the properties of abstract objects; The means of establishing the true ones is proof, which is carried out based on the finite number of displayed sentences accepted without proof.

One should not think that these ideas were accepted immediately. A torrent of ridicule and ridicule fell upon Pythogors and his followers; some scientists called him a stupid, multi-knowledgeable person and a charlatan; they said that he had never seen lines without width or circles touching a straight line only at one point. However, Pythogoras and his students continued to develop science on a new, solid foundation.

"Math." This is what the Pythogoreans called their system of knowledge. “Mathematics” consists of four parts: arithmetic, geometry, astronomy and the doctrine of harmony (music theory). The first two of them were purely theoretical - we still attribute them to mathematics, the other two were the application of arithmetic and geometry to the study of celestial bodies and the laws of consonances - we would attribute them to mathematical physics.

We know little about the arithmetic of the Pythogoreans. It is reliably known that it studied integers and their relations, that is, rational numbers. Plato in his dialogues calls arithmetic “the study of four and odd numbers.” We can reconstruct this teaching from Book IX of Euclid’s Elements, at the end of which (propositions 21-34) it is reproduced. Pythogoras divided all integers into four (divisible by 2) and odd (not divisible by 2) and proved the function theorem: the product of two numbers is even if and only if at least one of the components is four. This was the first theorem of divisibility theory, the subsequent model for the formulation and proof of a more general theorem: the product of two numbers of numbers is divisible by a prime number p if and only if at least one of the components is divisible by it. From this last theorem follows the law of uniqueness of the decomposition of an integer into prime factors. Similarly, from Pythogoras' theorem it follows that every integer can be uniquely represented as $N=2^kN_1$, where $N_1$ is an odd number and $k$ is a non-negative integer. The doctrine of four and odd allowed the Pythogoreans to prove a number of extremely important theorems, to which we will return.

In geometry, Pythogoras was attracted to regular figures and bodies. Thus, he knew that a plane could be “tiled” with regular triangles, squares and hexagons. He knew how to build a regular pentagon - both convex and star-shaped. The five-pointed star was said to be a symbol of health, and soon became an identification mark for the Pythogoreans scattered throughout the ancient world. Geometry was crowned by the Pythagorean theorem, which has fundamental significance both in mathematics itself and in its applications. How did Pythogoras prove it? Unfortunately, we don’t know this.

Pythogoras also developed the doctrine of similarity, and initially he apparently believed that all segments were commensurate, which means that their ratio can be expressed by a rational number, that is, his theory of similarity was based on the arithmetic of heteronomous numbers.

Discovery of incommensurability. Let us now turn to the greatest discovery of Pythagoras, which brought him unfading fame and introduced the triumph of mathematics. We are talking about the discovery of incommensurable segments, or, in other words, irrationality.

But what is it? Let us be given a square on side 1. Then, by the Pythagorean theorem, the square of its diagonal is equal to
$$c^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2.$$
What is the diagonal $c$ equal to? Let's try to find out by taking the root of 2.

If you apply for this job, you will first get $c_1 = 1$, then $c_2 = 1,4$, then $c_3 = 1,41$, etc. Finally, you have to reach the 10th digit, and you will get tired of this activity. You will say: “Enough! This series will never end." But we will object to you: “Continue! What if it ends on the 11th or 20th digit?” Alas, we again “fell into the clutches” of infinity!

But here too is the exit of Pythagoras. He reasoned this way: let $c$ be expressed as a rational fraction $p/q$. We assume that $p$ and $q$ cannot both be even (otherwise we would cancel the fraction by 2). By squaring the equality $\sqrt{2} = p/q$ we get $2p^2/q^2$ or
$$p^2 = 2q^2$$
(1)

This means that $p^2$ is divisible by 2. Hence, Pythagoras, based on the main theorem about even and odd numbers, concludes that the number $p$ is even. This means that $q$ is odd (otherwise we would reduce the fraction $p/q$ by 2). So, $p = 2p_1$. Substituting into equality (1), we get $q^2 = 2p_1^2$, that is, $q^2$ is divisible by 2, then, by the previous one, $q = 2q_1$, which means $q$ is even. We have arrived at a contradiction, since the number $q$ must be both even and odd, which is impossible.

The discovery of incommensurable magnitude deeply struck all the Hellenes. It was a theoretical proposal that seemed to contradict ever-changing practice! After all, in practice, all quantities are comparable: we lose them to the nearest centimeter, millimeter, micron, but we always get rational numbers as a result. The discovery of incommensurability can be compared with the discovery of Copernicus, which also contradicts our everyday observed sunrises and sunsets, or with the discovery of Lobachevsky's geometry. According to legend, the Pythogoreans kept this discovery secret, apparently wanting to find a worthy place for it in their “mathematics”, and the Pythogorean Hypas, who divulged the secret, was punished by the rich for this: he tired out and was shipwrecked. The famous ancient pythosophist Plato (late 5th - early 4th century BC) wrote that he learned about incommensurability late and that before that he was like an unreasonable animal. Another great philosopher, a student of Plato, ρισstele, discussing how science arose, wrote: “everyone begins with surprise (...), whether they are surprised, for example, at mysterious self-propelled toys, or solstices, or the incommensurability of the diagonal, or to everyone who has not yet understood the reason, it seems surprising if something cannot be measured with the smallest measure” (Metaphysics, book 1, ch. 2).

The students and followers of Pythagoras soon discovered that in addition to $\sqrt{2}$ there is also a whole series of irational numbers: $\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}, \sqrt{6}, ..., \sqrt{15}$, or, as they said, that the sides of squares with an area of ​​3, 5, 6, ..., 15 are incommensurable from the side of a unit square. In Plato’s dialogue “Theatetus” it is said that the Pythogorian Theodore of Cyrene gave a lecture to the Athenian youths, where he gave the corresponding proof for $\sqrt{17}$.

Let us use the Pythagorean method: let us need to prove the irrational $\sqrt{d}$, where $d$ is a non-square integer. If $d$ is even, then the proof is carried out last in the same way as for $\sqrt{2}$. Let $d$ be odd, and suppose that $\sqrt{d} = p/q$; Then
$$p^2 = dq^2.$$
(2)

It can be shown that in this case $p$ and $q$ will be odd, that is, they will have the form $2n+1$, then their squares will be of the form $4(n^2+n)+1$ or $\frac{n(n+1)}{2} + 1$, where $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ is an integer. Substituting these values ​​into formula (2) and grouping the terms, we get
$$8\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2} - d\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right) = d-1.$$
The left side of the equality is divisible by 8, which means that the rights must have the form $8k$, that is, they must be $d=8k+1$. If the number $d$ does not have the same form as, for example, 5, 7, 11, 13, 15, then this leads to a contradiction, which refutes the assumption made. If $d$ has the form $8k+1$, then this method of proof refuses to work. So the number 9 will be, but it is a perfect square, and for 17. That is why, in fact, Fedor settled on this number.

Harmony. The idea of ​​​​the possibility of constructing a numerical model of the world was used by Pythagoras as the basis for his theory of music. Pythagoras discovered that qualitative differences in the sound of strings are caused by purely quantitative differences, namely the length of the strings. The simultaneous sound of two horns will be pleasant to the ear if their lengths are in the ratio 1:2, or 2:3, or 3:4, which corresponds to musical intervals of an octave or quantum. Evaluating the discovery of Pythagoras, the greatest German physicist Sommerfeld wrote that the day when it was made can be called the birthday of mathematical physics. Indeed, harmonic oscillating systems have played a huge role in physics. In the 20s of our century, the analogy between an oscillating system emitted by the tone of a certain particle, and an atom emitting radiation of a certain particle, which were also determined by the ratios of integers, the latter the key to the creation of quantum mechanics. This fact was noted by A. Einstein:

“Thus,” he wrote, “we have discovered something similar between a vibrating string and an atom emitting particles from the elements: from the heavy ones that make up the nucleus, and from the light ones - electrons. Such a system of particles behaves like a small acoustic instrument in which standing waves are produced” (A. Einstein, L. Infeld. Evolutionary Physics. M.: Nauka, 1963, p. 224).

Astronomy. Pythagoras believed that the Earth was spherical and that it was at the center of the Universe. Around it there are eight concentric spheres - these are the spheres of the Moon, the Sun, the heel of the planets and the fixed stars. The outer sphere - the sphere of the fixed stars - makes a full revolution around the World Axis per day, increasing in its movement all seven other spheres. However, the Moon and the Sun are movements that occur in the direction opposite to the diurnal dilation of the sphere. Subsequently, the Pythagoreans came to the conclusion that the Earth does not remain motionless, but rotates along with other planets around the Central Fire. This idea predates Copernicus's idea that the Earth does not occupy a central position in the solar system by more than 2,000 years.

It is remarkable that Pythagoras immediately tried to create astronomical views from the theory of music. He believed that each planet, moving in its orbit, produces sound, and the tones are such that when all seven planets move, “music of the sphere” sounds. Pythagoras even called the solar system a seven-stringed lyre. He assured that he could hear this wonderful music, which people would hear only after death.

We have already said that the unexpected ideas of Pythagoras received new life in our century, but already in application to the world of atoms. Sommerfeld, whom we have already mentioned, wrote:

“What we manage to understand today in the language of spectra is the true music of the atomic sphere, the consonance of integer relations, the ever-increasing order and harmony with all their diversity.

...All integer laws of spectral lines and atomism ultimately follow from quantum theory. It represents that instrument full of subtlety on which nature uses spectral music and the irite of which it controls the structure of the atom and atomic nuclei" (A. Sommerfeld. Putti knowledge in physics. M., Nauka, 1973, p. 255).

They say that science differs from art in that while the creations of art are eternal, the great creations of science hopelessly fade away. Fortunately, this is not the case, and the work of Pythagoras is the best example of this. He was not only the greatest, but also the most frequent genius, since his ideas and theories did not leave the stage, continuing to excite minds until then. None of his scientific ideas died. With each new stage of science, they changed their appearance so that they would again excite the minds and hearts of scientists.
