Friction cone

If we consider the conditions of equilibrium of a body on an inclined plane, the angle of inclination of which can be changed, then it is easy to obtain (do it yourself) that the body begins to slide off the plane at an angle φ such that

$$tg\varphi = \mu,$$

where $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction of the body on the plane. Don't you find it surprising that this angle does not depend on body weight?

The same expression for the angle φ can be obtained in another, simpler way. But to do this, you must first be familiar with the concept of “friction cone”.

Let the body, which can be considered a material point, be located on a broad horizontal plane. The gravitational force $m\vec{g}$ presses the body to the surface, and the surface “responds” by acting on the body with a force of normal pressure $\vec{N}$. If some horizontal force is also applied to the body, then another friction force appears from the surface. As long as the magnitude of the horizontal force does not exceed the maximum value of the static friction force $F_{\text{тр.п.max}} = \mu N$, the body is at rest. When this value is reached, the body begins to move, and the surface acts on it due to the force of sliding friction.

$$F_{\text{тр.ск.}} = F_{\text{тр.п.max}} = \mu N.$$

Both the normal reaction force and the friction force are generated by the surface, so we can talk about the total reaction force of the surface. In the case when a body, under the influence of an external force (of course, including the force of gravity) moves along the surface (Fig. 1), the total reaction force is

$$\vec{R} = \vec{N} + \vec{F}_{\text{тр.ск.}}.$$

This force is directed at an angle φ to the normal, which is easy to determine:

$$tg\varphi = \frac{F_{\text{тр.ск.}}}{N} = \mu, \quad \varphi = \arctg\mu.$$

The angle f is called the friction angle.

We will now mentally rotate the vector $\vec{R}$ around the normal to the surface without changing the angle φ between them. In this case, the vector describes a cone (with an angle of 2φ at the apex), called the cone of friction. It has the following remarkable property. No matter how large the external force is applied to the body, if it lies inside the friction cone, the bodies remain at rest. If this force goes beyond the cone of friction, then no matter how small it is, the body begins to move.

The truth of this statement is not difficult. Indeed, let the external force $\vec{F}$ (see Fig. 1) be applied to the body so that its line of action makes an angle $\alpha$ with the normal to the surface. Then the force “shifting” the body along the surface is equal to $F_{\sin\alpha}$, and the normal reaction force is equal to $F_{\cos\alpha}$. Thus, the maximum possible static friction force holding the body in place is

$$F_{\text{тр.п.max}} = \mu N = \mu F_{\cos\alpha} = Ftg\varphi\cos\alpha.$$
