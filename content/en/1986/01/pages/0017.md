Fig. 4. Kepler's cuboctahedron.

It is easy to calculate that with this packaging method, each ball is in contact with 12 neighboring balls. The points of contact form the vertices of the 14-hedron. Its faces are alternating squares and equilateral triangles. Thus, the second method of close packing produces the Kepler cuboctahedron shown in figure 4.

Until now, we have considered only such methods of packing balls in which they fit into periodic “honeycombs”. Is it possible to achieve dense packing without this condition? One of the methods is shown in figure 5. The balls in each plane are located on the sides of regular pentagons (pentagons). In each pentagon, neighboring balls touch each other, but the balls, relative to different pentagons of the same plane, are separated in space. The sides of the pentagons of alternating layers contain alternately four and odd numbers of balls. The space filling factor in such a structure is 72% and is only slightly inferior to the case of dense packing shown in figure 2. It is possible to pack balls without forming a lattice from the centers more densely, reaching a filling factor of 74%, but whether even denser packings exist - this question remains open to this day.

Let's return to the next sand. We now know that there are special packings of balls that leave very little empty space between them. If you violate this arrangement by, for example, removing the balls of one layer from the holes of another layer, then the gaps between the balls will increase. It is clear, however, that grains of sand are never packaged in a special way. How to achieve dense packing of sand grains?

Let's remember everyday experience. You need to fill the vessel coarsely so that it contains the maximum amount of it. What do you do about it? Use the vessel or move through it, adding the desired effect. Then, after tightly packing the krula into the vessel, you can use this technique to reduce some additional amount of it.

A scientific study of this issue was undertaken in the 50s by the English scientist G. Scott. He filled spherical bottles of different sizes with ball bearings. If bottles are filled without shaking so that the balls are arranged randomly, the following dependence of the packing density on the number of balls is experimentally observed:

$$\varrho_1 = 0,6 - 0,37 / \sqrt[3]{N},$$

where $N$ is the total number of balls. If the number of balls is very large (and in experiments $N$ it reached several thousand), then it is clear that the packing density tends to become constant and corresponds to filling 60% of the space. But if you shake the bottle as it fills, the packing density increases:

$$\varrho_2 = 0,64 - 0,33 / \sqrt[3]{N}.$$

True, even in this case it turns out to be much less than 74%, corresponding to the regular arrangement of the balls.
