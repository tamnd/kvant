Dense packing of balls

Is it possible to fill the entire space with solid balls? Of course not - there will always be free spaces between them. The fraction of space occupied by the balls is called their packing density. The closer the balls are located, the less free space between them, the greater the packing density. When is the maximum packing density of identical hard spheres achieved? The answer to this question provides the key to solving the “mystery” of footprints in the sand.

Let us first examine a simpler case—packing of identical circles on a plane. Dense packing of circles can be achieved by fitting them into mosaics composed of regular polygons that fill the entire plane. There are only three ways to construct such mosaics - from regular triangles, squares and regular hexagons. Packing circles using square and hexagonal motes are shown in figure 1. Further, “by eye” you can see that the second method is more economical. An accurate calculation (check it yourself) shows that in this case approximately 90.7% of the plane is filled with circles, while in the first case - only about 78%. The hexagonal method of packing on a plane is the densest. Apparently, because of this, bees use it when building honeycombs.

Dense packing of balls in space can be accomplished as follows. Let us place the first layer of balls on the plane in the most dense way already known to us. Then you can put a second layer of the same on them. If each ball of the top layer is located exactly above the bottom, so that all the balls are inscribed in a cubic honeycomb, then too much space is wasted. With this packaging method, only 52% of the space is filled.

It is clear how the balls can be packed more tightly. To do this, the upper ball must be placed in the hole formed by the tray of adjacent lower balls. But at the same time, the upper balls will not be able to fill all the holes - one of the two adjacent recesses is always free (Fig. 2). Therefore, when we lay the third layer of balls, this can be done in two ways: either placing the balls of the third layer above those recesses in the first layer that the balls of the second layer left free (the center of one of the balls on figure 2 will be at point A), or just above the balls of the first layer (in this case, the center of one of the balls of the third layer will be at point B). It is saved for subsequent layers. As a result, we obtain two ways of packing balls, shown in space in figure 3. In general cases, about 74% of the space is filled with balls.
