Yes, half the wavelength (being in antiphase), when added they cancel each other out. For each point in one zone there is a corresponding point in the neighboring zone, from which the waves will arrive at point $D$ in antiphase, and the areas of all zones are almost the same. Therefore, the action of neighboring zones at point $D$ (for example, the first and second, second and third, etc.) practically destroy each other.

It is now easy to understand under what conditions the “Poisson paradox” can be observed. If there is an exact number of zones, then we will see a dark spot in the center of the screen. Behind it there will be a light ring, then a dark one, etc. (Fig. 2, a). On the screen we will observe a system of dark and light rings through the ears (but the central spot will be dark!). If the answer indicates an odd number of zones, then the result will be the opposite - a light spot will be observed in the center, surrounded by dark and light rings through the ears. Try to explain the diffraction pattern from a small ball (Fig. 2, b) yourself.
