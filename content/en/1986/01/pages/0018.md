It is worth thinking about the dependencies given here. Why is the correction inversely proportional to $\sqrt[3]{N}$? The balls located near the walls of the vessel are in a special position compared to the balls in the volume and affects the packing density. The magnitude of their contribution is proportional to the ratio of the surface area $\sim R^2$ to the volume of the vessel $\sim R^3$ and kills in inverse proportion to the size of the system $R$ (under the volume of the system we drink the full volume of space occupied by the balls along with free spaces). Size $R \sim \sqrt[3]{N}$, since the volume of the system is proportional to the total number of balls. Such dependencies often arise in physics when it is necessary to take into account effective surfaces.

Thus, precise experiments confirm everyday experience and show that by shaking a granular medium, a greater packing density can be achieved. But why does this still happen? The fact is that a stable equilibrium position always corresponds to a minimum of potential energy. The ball may lie steadily in the hollow, but it will definitely bounce from the top of the slide. Something similar is happening here. When shaken, the balls are rolled into free spaces, the packing density increases, and the total volume of the system decreases. As a result, the level of filling the vessel with balls decreases, and consequently, the center of gravity lowers and the potential energy of the system decreases.

Now, finally, we can imagine with sufficient clarity what is happening to the sand. The agitation of the water meets the sand, and as a result, a dense packing of the sand is achieved. By squeezing the cake with our foot, we break this packaging and increase the size of the pores. Water from the upper layers of sand goes into the depths, filling these enlarged gaps. As a result, the pitch “drops out”. When the foot is removed, the deformity disappears, the tight packing is restored, and the water flowing out of the new reduced spaces fills the trace left by the foot. It can also be used in such a way that after strong pressure the pitch does not recover. Then the trail will become wet again only when the water rises from the lower layers and fills the pores.

It is curious that this is a property of granular media found in Indian faqra. One of their trios consists of repeatedly sticking a narrow knife into a vessel with a narrow neck, filled to the top with rice. At some point, the knife got stuck in the rice, and the vessel could be lifted with the knife. It is clear that the trick was that the vessel, when filled with rice, met, achieving a tight pack. Sticking a knife in disrupted this packaging and increased the amount of space between the grains of rice. Since the volume of the vessel did not change, the forces squeezing the grains of rice increased, and therefore the tension between them and the knife increased. At some point it turned out to be enough to pull the knife out of the vessel.
