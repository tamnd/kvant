Combinations of letters - angrams - naturally arise when changing two or more polynomials, and combinators - coefficients of angrams - when translating similar terms.

The formula for the square of the sum of two numbers is well known:
$(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$.

A similar formula can be obtained for the square of the sum of three or more terms. For example, let’s square the sum $a+b+c$:
$(a+b+c)(a+b+c) = aa + ab + ac + ba + bb + bc + ca + cb + cc = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ac$.

A similar formula is true for $(a+b+c)^2$ (on figure 3 each annual expresses the area of ​​its rectangle); In general, the square of the sum of $n$ numbers is calculated as follows: you need to add up all the squares of these $n$ numbers and add the doubled sum of all possible pairwise products of these numbers.

If we carefully change the variability of $a+b+c$ when folded into itself, we also get the formula:
$(a+b+c)^3 = a^3 + b^3 + c^3 + 3(a^2b + a^2c + b^2a + b^2c + c^2a + c^2b) + 6abc$.

Let us explain how you can get the coefficients 1, 3 and 6 here without tedious multiplication and matching of similar terms. This is the number of angrams already familiar to us. The term $a^3$ can only be obtained in one way - when from each parenthesis
$(a+b+c)(a+b+c)(a+b+c)$ is taken from the letter $a$. To obtain the coefficient for $a^2b$, you need to take $b$ from one bracket, and $a$ from the other two, that is, this term corresponds to the angram $aab, aba, baa$ from two letters $a$ and one letter $b$: their number is $(2+1)!(2!1!) = 3$. Finally, the coefficient of $abc$ is equal to the number $3!/(1!1!1!) = 6$ of angrams (permutations) of a word from three different letters $a, b, c$.

A similar formula will be obtained for the cube of the sum of a larger number of terms, for example
$(a+b+c+d+e)^3 = (a^3+...) + 3(a^2b+...) + 6(abc+...)$.
