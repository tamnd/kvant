M967. Let us denote by σ(n) the sum of all natural divisors of n (including 1 and n) and by φ(n) the number of numbers less than n and coprime to it. Prove that for any natural number n ≥ 2
σ(n) + φ(n) ≥ 2n.

Let 1 = d₁ < ... < dₖ = n - all divisors of the number n.
Among the natural numbers not exceeding n, the number of multiples of dᵢ is equal to n/dᵢ, therefore the total number of numbers not coprime to n, equal to n - φ(n), is not more than the sum
$$\frac{n}{d_2} + \frac{n}{d_3} + ... + \frac{n}{d_k} = d_{k-1} + d_{k-2} + ... + d_1 = \sigma(n) - n$$
(for n/d₁ = dₖ, n/d₂ = dₖ-1, ...). Thus,
n - φ(n) ≤ σ(n) - n,
whence the inequality being proved follows.
Note that for prime p our estimate is exact:
$$\sigma(p) = p + 1, \quad \varphi(p) = p - 1, \quad \sigma(p) + \varphi(p) = 2p.$$
V. F. Lev

F978. Two identical weights B and C are attached to a rigid weightless rod ABC of length 3l as shown in figure 1: $|AB|=l, |BC|=2l$. The rod is suspended from point A to a very long weightless thread AO. At the initial moment, the rod is held in a horizontal position, and the thread is vertical; then the rod is released. What will be the speed of point A at the moment when the rod passes the lowest pole?

By the time the rod passes the lower pole, the OA thread will deviate very slightly from the vertical (since it is very long). Therefore, the tension force of the thread acting on the rod can be considered vertical all the time (its horizontal component can be claimed). Consequently, the horizontal component of the rod impulse remains unchanged, that is, equal to zero, and at a lower position

$$|mv_B|=|mv_C| \Rightarrow |v_B|=|v_C|=v.$$

The value of $v$ will be found from the law of conservation of energy:

$$2\left(\frac{mv^2}{2}\right)=mgl+3mgl=4mgl,$$

where

$$v=2\sqrt{gl}.$$

Thus, at the moment of passing the lowest pole, the rod rotates around the center K of segment BC (Fig. 2).

In this case $|AK|=2|OB|$, and the speed of point A

$$v_A=2v_B=2v=4\sqrt{gl}.$$

In order to determine the force $N$ acting on the overload $m$ from the side of the load $M$, we use Newton’s second law for the movement of the overload:

$$ma = mg - N.$$

After the weights are released, they perform harmonious oscillations (as one weight of mass $M + m$). This means that acceleration $a$ periodically changes its direction. Strength

$$N = m(g - a)$$

will be maximum at the moment when the acceleration has a maximum absolute value and is directed upward, that is, at the moment the arm is most stretched. To determine acceleration, Newton's second law is used for the movement of a load with an overload:

$$(M + m)a = (M + m)g - kx.$$

The maximum value $x_{\max}$ will be found using the law of conservation of energy (taking into account that at the moment of maximum stretching of the arm, the speed of the load is zero):

$$-(M+m)gx_0 + \frac{k}{2}x_0^2 = -(M+m)gx_{\max} + \frac{k}{2}x_{\max}^2,$$

where $x_0$ is the elongation of the spring without overload $m$. Considering that $kx_0 = Mg$, from (2) we obtain: $kx_{\max} = (M+2m)g$. Substituting this value into (1), we find the maximum acceleration value -

$$|a_{\max}| = \left| -\frac{m}{M+m}g \right| = \frac{m}{M+m}g,$$

and knowing $a_{\max}$, we find $N_{\max}$:

$$N_{\max} = mg \frac{M+2m}{M+m}.$$

F982. As is known, the maximum size of various image details on film is determined by the size of the crystals of the photosensitive layer. As the crystal size decreases, the image clarity increases. Estimate the maximum crystal size at which erithel ceases to replace the increase in image clarity on the cinema screen. It is assumed that you have a good understanding that you can set the quantities necessary for the solution yourself, select sufficiently correct numerical values ​​and obtain a numerical result.

In the case of the simplest projection film installation, the image on the screen is created as a result of the passage of a diverging light beam through the film film, providing the necessary magnification of the frame. It is important that there are no linear distortions on the screen. This means that the ratio of the size $\Delta h$ of a detail on the film to the size $h$ of the entire frame must be exactly the same as the ratio of the size $\Delta H$ of the image of this detail on the screen to the size $H$ of the entire image of the frame on the screen, that is

$$\frac{\Delta h}{h} = \frac{\Delta H}{H}.$$

Let $\Delta H$ be the maximum size of a detail, still visible to the viewer’s eye from a distance $l$, $H$ be the size of the screen, coinciding with the size of the frame image on it, $\Delta h$ be the minimum size of a detail on film, coinciding with the size of the photosensitive crystal (“grain”) of the photographic emulsion, $h$ be the size of the frame on the film (see Fig. figure).

The ratio $\Delta H/l$ is determined by the so-called “angular resolution of the eye” of the viewer, that is, the minimum angle $\theta$, at the boundaries of which the eye separates (sees not disjointed) two points or two lines. The magnitude of this angle is easy to estimate. Let us recall, for example, visiting an ophthalmologist: from a distance of several meters, looking at a table of letters in the same way as we watch a movie, we distinguish the details of letters that do not change a few millimeters; that is, in order of magnitude, the angle $\theta$ is equal to

$$\theta \sim \frac{\Delta H}{l} \sim 10^{-3}.$$

Thus, $\Delta H \sim l\theta$, and

$$\Delta h = h \frac{\Delta H}{H} \sim \frac{hl\theta}{H}.$$

At $h=1$ cm, $l=20$ m, $H=5$ m, $\theta \sim 10^{-3}$ for the minimum size of photoemulsion crystals, beyond which the viewer stops replacing the increase in image clarity on the screen, we obtain

$$\Delta h \sim 0,04$$ mm.

This assessment is performed with known parameters of film and photographic materials.

G. V. Meledin
