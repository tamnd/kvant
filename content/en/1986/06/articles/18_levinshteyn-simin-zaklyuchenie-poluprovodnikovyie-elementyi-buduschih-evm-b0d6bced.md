X. Conclusion. Semiconductor elements of future computers

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences M. E. LEVINSHTEIN, Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences G. S. SIMIN

The semiconductor elements on which modern computers operate have reached a high degree of perfection. Their switching time is a hundred-millionth of a second. Their dimensions are so small that hundreds of thousands of transistors - field-effect or bipolar - can be placed on a silicon wafer measuring 5x5 mm.

Modern computers are capable of controlling spacecraft and making large diagnoses, proving mathematical theories and managing enterprises, translating scientific texts and planning transportation to meet national goals.

However, there are tasks that are so far beyond the capabilities of even the most powerful of today's computers. For example, to make a satisfactory weather forecast for tomorrow, to carry out a very accurate calculation of the behavior of an airplane in an air flow or electrons in a solid body, you need a computer with a speed of about 1 trillion operations per second. And for such computers, the development of which is already underway, new, more advanced elements are being discovered.

Computer elements are being improved in several directions at once. The parameters of bipolar and field-effect transistors are improved, new switching elements are created and tested, new ways of combining switching elements into Ultra-large and Ultra-high-speed Intergal Circuits are proposed and thoroughly tested.

Improving existing computer elements

From the operating principles of bipolar and field-effect transistors (see notes II and III of this series in «Kvant» No. 10, 11, 1985) it follows that the switching speed of these elements is higher, the smaller their size and the greater the mobility of current carriers in the semiconductor from which they are made.

Therefore, the improvement of existing computer elements - bipolar and field-effect transistors - proceeds in two main ways. New technologies and processes are being created that make it possible to obtain transistors with a possibly smaller base thickness (bipolar transistor) or a possible shorter channel (field-effect transistor). A search is underway for new materials with high mobility of transistor carriers.

Modern technology processes make it possible, under computer control, to grow a layer of semiconductor material as thick as a single atomic layer. Practical creation and testing of devices with working element thicknesses of 10–15 Å (Fig. 2). Bipolar transistors with a base thickness of ~0.1 µm and field-effect transistors with a gate length of 0.15 µm have been created.

the creation of a new type of interconnected circuits - volumetric ICs, each of which should contain tens and hundreds of millions of semiconductor devices.

Volumetric IC fills a complex pie. Each layer of this pie is a familiar flat IC.

After the formation of a common flat IC on a semiconductor wafer is completed: all transistors, connections between them, external leads, etc. are manufactured, a dielectric layer is placed on top. A semiconductor layer is then placed on top of the dielectric. A second IC is created on this semiconductor layer, and so on.

The case now houses not a record, but a “cube” containing many ICs. In this case, the contents between flat ICs are made inside the “cube” immediately during the manufacturing process of a volumetric IC. As a result, the number of external contents decreases sharply. Reliability increases and the cost of the circuit decreases.

Semiconductor elements of optical computers.
In a computer, information, as we know, is stored and processed in the form of electrical signals. However, already in the mid-70s, work began on creating computers of a completely different type - optical computers.

In modern computers, “zero” and “one”—the two numbers with which all information stored and processed by the machine is recorded—conventionally corresponds to the closed and open states of transistors. In projected optical computers, “one” corresponds to the passage of light through an optical cell, and “zero” corresponds to the absorption of light in the cell.

It is assumed that a semiconductor laser beam introduced into the light guide (Fig. 6) will be directed to semiconductor optical cells, the output of which can change ten billion times per second with low energy consumption.

Amazing elements of a perfect unusual computer
Nature tells us that increasing speed reduction is not the only way to master the intellectual capabilities of a computer. The cells of the human brain—neurons—switch in a time that is approximately a million times longer than the “cells” of advanced computers. However, it was Man who invented the computer and many other wonderful things.

How the human brain works, why it works with such replacement efficiency - these questions are being intensively studied and are still very far from being resolved. However, the first steps have already been taken to create biomolecular ICs - the basis of future biological computers. A method for manufacturing ICs based on protein material, polyinizine, has been patented. Molecular switches, useful protein transistors, biosensors for signals, comparative toxic, biological or chemical influences have been created. It is assumed that the packing density of elements in such biopolymer structures will approach the packing density of structures responsible for photosynthesis in green plant leaves and will amount to more than a billion per 1 mm². There is reason to hope that biopolymer ICs will not be manufactured, but grown like living tissue.

However, if these reliable ones come true, such elements will not be related to semiconductors.

But in the next decade, it is the progress in the field of semiconductor electronics that will determine the level of achievement in the development of advanced computers.
