At the beginning of October 1594, King Henry IV of France received the envoy of the Republic of the United Netherlands to Fontable, who was most often called Holland after its largest province. Having emerged as a result of a long and persistent struggle against Spanish rule, the republic was very young - it was only in its second decade. The war with Spain did not stop - the Dutch government was currently looking for co-conspirators. And in France, the fires of many years of international support, supported by the Spaniards, had just been extinguished; he became the French king. Henry did not hide his interest in Holland as a co-conspirator in the fight against Spain, but most of all he was interested in the rapidly developing Dutch industry, trade and navigation. Therefore, walking through the park in Fontable, he listened attentively to the envoy’s story about the new silk manufactories of Rotterdam, the paper mills of Utrest, and the shipyards of Zaandam.

“There are now many talented engineers and scientists in Holland,” said the envoy. “The mathematician and mechanic Simon Stevin is developing new systems of locks and dams, fortresses are being built according to the designs of the mathematician Ludolf Zeilen, the mathematician Andrien Van Rowmen is famous for his puzzling calculations.” By the way,” the envoy continued, “not long ago Van Rowmen made a challenge to mathematicians around the world. He sent a letter to many countries in which he proposed solving the problem he had invented. But so far no one has succeeded.

Each number here is obtained by adding the numbers in front of it and above it. It is worth noting that Viet does not express sin $nx$ and cos $nx$ in terms of sin $x$ and cos $x$, as we do, but 2sin $nx$ and 2cos $nx$ in terms of 2sin $x$ and 2cos $x$. If in the expressions formed in this way we consider the quantities 2sin $nx$ or 2cos $nx$ as known, then we obtain an equation of the $n$th degree with respect to the unknowns 2sin $x$ and 2cos $x$.

The initial purpose for which Viet derived formulas for the sines of multiple arcs was to calculate the sines of arcs through the sines of minor arcs, that is, to compile a table of sines. Then these formulas found application in algebra and geometry. In particular, Viète connected the geometric problem of the trisculation of the angle $\alpha$ with the equation $3x-x^3=a$, which is satisfied by the values ​​$a=2\sin \alpha$, $x=2\sin(\alpha/3)$. Vieth interprets the two positive solutions of this equation as chords corresponding to the arcs $2\alpha/3$ and $(360^\circ-2\alpha)/3$. According to the general opinion of his time, he does not take the negative root into account at all. In the same way, Vieth associates the problem of dividing an angle into five equal parts with the equation $5x-5x^3+x^5=a$, which is satisfied by the values ​​$a=2\sin \alpha$, $x=2\sin(\alpha/5)$.

From what has been said it is clear to what extent Viet was prepared to instantly solve Van Routen's problem. He immediately saw that the proposed value $a$ was the long side of a regular decagon inscribed in the unit circle (check this!), or, what is the same thing, the chord corresponding to the arc $24^\circ$. The coefficients of the first and penultimate terms of the left-hand side allowed him to assume that the left-hand side is nothing more than the expression 2sin $45\alpha$ through 2sin $\alpha$. But since $a=2\sin 12^\circ$, then $\alpha=12^\circ/45=4^\circ/15$, and therefore $x=2\sin(4^\circ/15)$. It was this decision that Viet conveyed to the Dutch envoy.

After the audience, Viet tested his proposal. However, having continued the necessary calculations, he discovered that the left side of the proposed equation does not coincide with the expansion of 2sin $45\alpha$ by the power of 2sin $\alpha$!

He probably didn’t feel very comfortable at that moment, most likely just bad.

What happened? Maybe it's just an error in a very tedious calculation? Apparently, at this moment Viet managed to find another - geometric - way to express 2sin $45\alpha$ through 2sin $\alpha$: in order to divide the arc into 45 parts, you need to first divide it into five parts, then each part into three and again into three. In other words, the left side of Van Routen's equation can be obtained from the system
$$\begin{cases} 3z-z^3=a, \\ 3y-y^3=z, \\ 5x-5x^3+x^5=y. \end{cases}$$

Only after analyzing the answers that Van Routen reported for the other two values ​​of $a$, Vieth became convinced that he was being childish about dividing the arcs into forty-five parts and boldly corrected the error in the conditions. But Viet did not stop with this decision. Twenty-two other solutions communicated to him the next day were of the form
$$2\sin \frac{360^\circ k+12^\circ}{45}=2\sin \frac{120^\circ k+4^\circ}{15},$$
$k=1, 2, ..., 22$.

Thus, Viet set off to find all the positive roots (remember that only they were considered solutions in his time).

One could put an end to this here, but the mathematical perfection between Viet and Van Routen did not end there. After a short time, Vieth suggested that Routen construct a circle tangent to three given circles with a compass and ruler (Apollonius' problem). Soon Viet pointed out an elegant geometric construction that could only be done with a compass and a ruler.

It was said that, having suffered a second defeat, Van Routen became a real admirer of Viet and even came to study with him.
