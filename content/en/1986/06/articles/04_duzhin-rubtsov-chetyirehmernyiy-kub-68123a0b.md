Four dimensional cube

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. V. DUZHIN,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. N. RUVTSOV

“Let us consider a four-dimensional cube...”, “Let us identify a six-dimensional subspace in a ten-dimensional space...” The authors often encountered these phrases and many others in them in the scientific literature, heard them at various seminars, uttered them themselves, in a word, they got used to not being surprised by them. But one day we thought:
“But the times are not so far away when the words “four-dimensional cube”, “fourth dimension” were completely unfamiliar even to most mathematicians, and caused bewilderment and curiosity among non-specialists, amid interest in “Bigfoot”, “flying cockroaches” and other pseudoscientific things.” The following dialogue arose between the authors:

A₁: Four-dimensional cube? Of course it's trivial! It must be defined simply as a subset of four-dimensional space using formulas that generalize school formulas. Only formulas!

A₂: According to this, this will make it possible to formally define what a four-dimensional cube is, but it will be incomprehensible and unclear. A four-dimensional cube is easily described as a geometric figure, similar to a square on a plane or a cube in three-dimensional space. Projections, developments, sections - that’s the only way!

After arguing for a short time, the authors decided to try to unite their points of view, starting, however, with the visual side of the matter.

Well, if we weren’t able to see the four-dimensional cube in motion well, let’s try to unfold it.

How to deploy it
To imagine the development of a four-dimensional cube, first look at the development of a three-dimensional and two-dimensional cube (Fig. 3). We see that the development of a square is one-dimensional and consists of four segments; The development of the cube is two-dimensional and consists of six squares. Similarly, the development of a four-dimensional cube is three-dimensional and consists of eight three-dimensional cubes (Fig.4).

Just as a three-dimensional cube can be glued from a net (see Fig. 3.6) by identifying the vertices $G_1 \equiv G_2 \equiv G_3$, $H_1 \equiv H_2 \equiv H_3$, $E_1 \equiv E_2$ and $F_1 \equiv F_2$ (and the corresponding edges), a four-dimensional cube can be obtained from its net (see Fig. 4) by “gluing” vertices designated by the same letters, but with different numbers, for example $M_1 \equiv M_2 \equiv M_3 \equiv M_4$ or $I_1 \equiv I_2$; in this case, not only vertices (and corresponding edges) are glued, but also two-dimensional faces, for example $M_3O_3P_3Q_3 \equiv M_4O_4P_4Q_4$.

Figure 4 is worth a closer look. It can be considered that it shows a model of a kind of eight-room house. This house is described by the American science fiction writer R. Heinlein in the story “The House That Till Built.” During the “trip” undertaken by Heinlein’s heroes through the wonderful house, they were struck by the following circumstance. When they, moving in a certain direction, passed the room one after another, then the fifth room on

However, such a definition will not satisfy the picky reader. He asks: what is a coordinate system in four-dimensional space? And in general, what is four-dimensional space?

To answer the last question, we first remember - well, of course, you guessed it! - What is three-dimensional space? This is a set of all possible triples of numbers (x; y; z), where x, y and z are real numbers (coordinates of a point), for which the distance is determined by the formula
$$|MN| = \sqrt{(x_M - x_N)^2 + (y_M - y_N)^2 + (z_M - z_N)^2}$$
(see textbook by A.V. Pogorelov “Geometry 6-10”, p. 204). It turns out that the previous phrase can be taken as a definition of three-dimensional space: from this definition one can derive all the basic principles of stereometry using elemental algebra. Thus, defining a plane as a set of points satisfying a point satisfying a linear equation of the form
$$Ax + By + Cz + D = 0,$$
(1)
It is not possible to prove, for example, that through any triple of neocolinear points M, N, P there passes one and only one plane, namely, the plane with the equation
$$((y_P - y_M)(z_N - z_M) - (z_P - z_M)(y_N - y_M)) \times (x_M - x) + ((z_P - z_M)(x_N - x_M) - (x_P - x_M)(z_N - z_M))(y_M - y) + (x_P - x_M)(y_N - y_M) - (y_P - y_M)(x_N - x_M))(z_M - z) = 0$$
(2)
(see “Geometry 6-10”, p. 212).

Now the next definition should be arbitrarily natural. Four-dimensional space is the set of all four numbers (x; y; z; t), where x, y, z, t are real numbers for which the distance is determined by the formula
$$|MN|^2 = (x_N - x_M)^2 + (y_N - y_M)^2 + (z_N - z_M)^2$$
(3)

Defining a plane in four-dimensional space is not so simple. The fact is that there are both general two-dimensional planes and three-dimensional “planes” called zigerplanes. From the point of view of specifying planes by equations, it is the hyperplane that is an exact analogue of the general plane in three-dimensional space. In equation (1) of the declaration plane of the three variables x, y, z, you can assign arbitrary values, and then the value of the third variable will be determined uniquely from the equation. How to summarize it
A reader who understands the previous definition can easily determine the definition of a $n$-cube for any $n \geq 1$. To do this, he will first define a $n$-dimensional space as the set of sets of $n$ numbers $(x_1; x_2; ...; x_n)$, the distance between which is given by the formula

$$|AB| = \sqrt{(a_1-b_1)^2 + (a_2-b_2)^2 + ... + (a_n-b_n)^2},$$

and then the $n$-cube as the set of those points of $n$-dimensional space for which the $0 \leq x_1 \leq 1, 0 \leq x_2 \leq 1, ..., 0 \leq x_n \leq 1$ inequalities hold.

How to cut it
It is convenient to imagine four-dimensional functions using their sections by a family of hyperplanes parallel to each other (hyperplanes parallel to each other if their normal vectors are collinear). The meaning of this construction can be understood by considering sections of three-dimensional bodies by ordinary planes (Fig. 8, where a section of a cube of plane $y=h$ is shown). Sections of a four-dimensional cube of the plane $t=h$ look similar - remember figure 2!

For a better understanding of the geometry of a four-dimensional cube, consider the following difficult task: to depict a family of its hyperplane sections perederdicular to the main diagonal. If we take the diagonal connecting the vertices $(0; 0; 0; 0)$ and $(1; 1; 1; 1)$, then the equation of these hyperplanes will have the form $x+y+z+t=h$, where $h$ is some constant.

Let's look at the three-dimensional problem in a similar way. A three-dimensional cube can be represented as the intersection of two octants defined by systems of inequalities

$$\begin{cases} x \geq 0, \\ y \geq 0, \\ z \geq 0 \end{cases}$$

and $$\begin{cases} x \leq 1, \\ y \leq 1, \\ z \leq 1 \end{cases}$$

(Fig.9). Every plane of the form $x+y+z=h$ intersects each of these two octants along a regular triangle. Let's follow the change in this configuration as $h$ changes from 0 to $\sqrt{3}$. At the initial moment $(h=0)$ one of the triangles consists of only one point. Then its linear dimensions increase in proportion to $h$, at the same time the second triangle gradually decreases, remaining a ready-made apex with a negative coefficient. Starting from the moment when the vertices of the smaller triangle “pierce” the sides of the larger one, their intersection gives a hexagon. This hexagon becomes regular when the size of the triangles is compared. Next, the described process is repeated in reverse order.

Similarly, a four-dimensional cube represents the intersection of two parts of four-dimensional space, distinguished by the systems of inequalities $x \geq 0, y \geq 0, z \geq 0, t \geq 0$ and $x \leq 1, y \leq 1, z \leq 1, t \leq 1$. It is easy to show that the hyperplane $x+y+z+t=h$ intersects each of these parts along a regular tetrad. However, of these tetrads, $h$ increases with growth, while the other decreases. The desired set is the intersection of two such tetradra and can be a point, a tetradron, or a truncated tetradra (in particular, if the sizes of both tetradras coincide, we will obtain a regular octahedron at the intersection; it is depicted on figure 3 on the fourth page of the cover «Kvant» No. 6 for 1985).

We hope that readers have become so familiar with the concept of a four-dimensional cube that they can draw these sections on their own.
