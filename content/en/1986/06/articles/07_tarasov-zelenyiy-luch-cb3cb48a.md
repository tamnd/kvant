Green beam

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. V. TARASOV

Sometimes when the sun sets, you can observe an amazing phenomenon - the so-called green ray. When almost the entire solar disk has disappeared beyond the horizon, a bright green light suddenly flashes for a few seconds. The edge of the sun suddenly turns yellow-red into bright green, green rays bend in all directions from it. Seconds, two, three..., and the colorful ripening suddenly disappears. The green ray is most likely to be seen on an evening when the sun shines brightly until sunset and almost does not change its color, remaining yellow or, in extreme cases, yellowish-colored. Soviet astronomer G. A. Tikhov studied this amazing phenomenon for many years. Here's what he writes:
“If the sun at sunset is red and easy to look at, then you can say as a souvenir that there will be no green ray. On the contrary, if the sun has not changed its white-yellow color much and is setting bright, then we can assume that a green ray will appear. It is important that the horizon has a clear line, without any irregularities: nearby forests, buildings, etc. These conditions are easiest to achieve at sea, which is why the green ray is well known to the boats of the sea.”
The physics of the green beam is based on three effects: 1) the efficiency of blocking light rays in an optically inhomogeneous medium (in this case, the earth’s atmosphere), 2) the dependence of the degree of blocking of rays on the wavelength, 3) the dispersal of light in the atmosphere (more precisely, the weakening of the efficiency of light in a sufficiently clean and capable atmosphere). We teach these effects one by one - first the first one, and then the subsequent ones.

It is known that, when passing through the atmosphere, a light beam is bent, and so that its trajectory is always convex towards the less dense regions of the air. For this reason, we see the setting solar disk slightly flattened vertically; its vertical diameter is visible at an angle of 26′, which is 6′ less than the angular size of the horizontal diameter. This is due to the decrease in the density of atmospheric air with height, leading to a corresponding bending of the rays (the so-called phenomenon of light refraction in the atmosphere). When we, admiring the solar disk on the seashore, see how the lower edge of the star is touched by the horizontal lines, we usually do not realize that in reality at this moment this edge of the star is 2°35′ below the horizontal lines (which means that the entire solar disk is deep below the horizon). consists of two terms: 2° is explained by the fact that light from the Sun to the Earth travels a little more than eight minutes; but 35′ is associated with the refraction of light in the atmosphere. Note that due to refraction, the upper edge of the solar disk “rises” less - not by 35′, but only by 29′ (refraction decreases as the object’s height above the horizon increases). That is why the setting suns and each observed one are slightly flattened. The above appears figure 1, where A is the observer, AB is the horizon line. (In this regard, we advise you to read the article by A. A. Mikhailov “When day is equal to night”, published in «Kvant» No. 6 for 1980.) Mirages are also explained by the refraction of light in the atmosphere (see the article by G. I. Grineva and G. V. Rosenberg “The affairs and continuations of the Morgana fairies” in «Kvant» No. 8 for 1984).

Further, taking into account that the shorter the wavelength of light, the more it is refracted. This is what the spectral decomposition of light in features is based on. This effect causes blue-green rays to undergo stronger refraction in the atmosphere than red rays. For simplicity, let there be only two colors in the spectrum of sunlight - green and red; The “white” solar disk can be considered in this case in the form of green and red disks superimposed on each other. The refraction of light in the atmosphere raises the green disk above the horizon to a greater extent than the red one.

The resulting observables would be to see the setting sun as shown in the upper right corner at figure 2. The upper edge of the disk would be green and the lower edge red; in the central part of the disk, existing colors are observed (a “white” color would be observed).

The picture described is correct only if the atmosphere does not scatter light. In reality, however, it dissipates it. This leads to the fact that from the light beam coming from the sun, rays with a shorter wavelength are most effectively eliminated (the law of scattering was established by Rayleigh: the intensity of scattered light is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the light wavelength). Therefore, we do not see the rim at the top of the disk, and the entire solar disk will not look “white”, but reddish.

But let’s imagine that almost the entire solar disk has disappeared behind the horizon, so that only its very upper edge remains. And even though the weather is clear and calm, the air is clean, so the scattering of light in the atmosphere is relatively small. In this case, we can see the bright green edge of the sun along with the Russian green rays.
