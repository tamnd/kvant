Kamerlingh Onnes was able to begin studying the resistance of metals at “helium” temperatures only at the end of 1910. And he was immediately expected to be sorry: both forecasts - Kelvin and Dewar - turned out to be erroneous. Measurements on a wire from a clean plate showed that at the deepest cooling, the resistance ceases to depend on temperature (see Fig. 1). Corresponding the new result with his earlier data obtained in measurements at not so low temperatures, the Dutch physicist came to the conclusion: the ordinate of the horizontal portion of the dependence $R(T)$ should be smaller, the purer the metal. And thus, in the case of a metal of ideal purity, $R$ will vanish even before approaching $T=0$. From the point of view of the classical electronic theory of metals, such a drop in resistance to zero at a non-zero temperature seemed absolutely inconceivable, and Kamerlingh Onnes decided to lean towards the explanation of quantum theory, the widespread recognition of which was beginning just at this time. Thus, he was able to derive the dependences $R(T)$, which should have been expected for an ideal pure metal.

It is clear that experimental verification of this dependence required a metal of maximum purity. In those days, only rue, which could easily be effectively purified by distillation, could claim this role. But Kamerlingh Onnes was an experienced experimenter and understood perfectly well how inconvenient it was to deal with rue. Indeed, to increase the accuracy of resistance measurements, the value of this resistance should be as large as possible, that is, for experiments it was necessary to use long wiring. And the rue is liquid! Therefore, the scientist first, using his dependence formula $R(T)$, estimated what could be expected from such an experiment. The calculation showed that at a temperature of 4.3 K the path resistance will still be significant, and at 3 K it will drop to a constant low level. Thus, the magnitude of the opportunity opened up to trace in the experiment the entire picture of the disappearance of resistance, and having a long-term perspective in front of us, it was necessary to embark on a difficult struggle with experimental difficulties.

Only after a number of unsuccessful attempts, Kamerlingh Onnes and his help, the virtuoso glassmaker, managed to develop the necessary type of sample for measurement - an accumulative, eigzag-shaped structure of the finest glass capillaries, inside of which a channel with a diameter of about 50 microns was filled with liquid rue, forming a single “thread” about 1 m long (Fig. 2). It’s easy to imagine how much trouble such a sample caused. With the slightest careless movement, with strong walking speed or heating, the glass capillaries cracked and broke off; Sometimes when frozen, a difficult thread breaks.

And yet the true experimental skill won. In the spring of 1911, Kamerlingh Onnes triumphed: experience was in striking agreement with theoretical ideas! - showed that the resistance of pure routine, even in full measurement at a temperature of 4.3 K, at 3 K actually becomes immeasurable.

However, further precise measurements shook the scientist’s faith in his theories.

It turned out that the resistance disappears abruptly (Fig. 3), while the formula for $R(T)$ predicted, although circular, but still a smooth drop in resistance. As a result, it turned out that the coincidence of the Kamerlingh-Onnes theory with experience is generally purely accidental. Scientists discovered a fundamentally new phenomenon - superconductivity.

The task of explaining the physical nature of nature turned out to be extremely difficult, and it was not possible to solve it for about half a century. The theory of this phenomenon was created only in 1957 by the American scientist J. Bardeen, L. Cooper and J. Schriefer and the Soviet scientist N. N. Bogolyubov.

Our study of superconductivity has turned into one of the most profound studies of solid state physics. Tremendous successes have also been achieved in the practical use of superconductivity - ultra-sensitive devices for electromagnetic changes based on this phenomenon are already in operation; Superconducting solenoids are widely used, creating magnetic fields in such volumes and magnitudes that one cannot even dream of in the case of windings made of normal conductors.

In the near future, the industrial energy industry will receive superconducting electric motors and electric motors with incredibly high efficiency; Work is underway to create supercomputers that should surpass thermal imaging and memory capacity.
