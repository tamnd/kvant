Coulomb began a thorough study of electro- and magnetostatic phenomena immediately after completing experiments on torsion of metal threads (a torsion suspension was an ideal device for the quantitative study of weak interactions). The first measure on this topic was called “Design and application of electric scales based on the properties of metal wires immeted with silicone, proportional to the angle of torsion.” This work, in which “Coulomb’s law” was experimentally substantiated, celebrated its bicentenary in the process.

Since the mid-18th century, many researchers have tried to define the “law of electric force.” Some of them assumed, by analogy with the law of universal gravitation, that this should be the law of “inverse squares”; attempts to directly determine the dependence of force on distance were made difficult by the fact that the difference between the interaction of loaded bodies and the tension or repulsion of “elementary” (that is, precise) loads was not yet realized. As a result, some experimenters drew erroneous conclusions from measurement data.

The advantage of Coulomb's experiments on electricity is that they measured the interaction between contaminants, which, to a first approximation, could indeed be considered accurate. Coulomb used special torsional “electric” scales, with the help of which he determined the level of repulsion between two equal and one-dimensional contaminants.

According to the tradition of that time, Coulomb very carefully described the structure of the scales and the methods of conducting the experiment.
