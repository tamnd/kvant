Among the most significant works of Coulomb is his thematic book “Theory of simple machines, taking into account the friction of their parts and the rigidity of ropes.” It examines in detail the phenomenon of dry friction. In addition to Coulomb's work on this problem, a wide variety of statements regarding friction can be found in the scientific literature. Thus, Amonton’s law was already known, according to which the friction force is proportional to the force of normal pressure and does not depend on the area of ​​contact of the body. However, some researchers denounced its validity. A great discussion reigned in discussions about the dependence of the force of sliding friction on the relative speed of movement of bodies. Finally, it was possible to encounter contradictory experimental results. The reason for this situation is that training is a very complex phenomenon, depending on a large number of factors, including the condition of the surfaces. To obtain clear results, systematic quantitative research had to be carried out. From a practical point of view, it was especially important to stage full-scale experiments when the loads correspond to those used in practice.

Coulomb's research met all these requirements. In his experiments, the dependence of friction force on the nature of contact, pressure force, skimming surface (Coulomb used loads weighing up to 1000 kg), contact time, relative sliding speed, etc. was studied.

In general, Coulomb confirmed Amonton's law, but at the same time showed that it is an approximate law. It turned out that the sliding friction force depends very little on the contact area, and most often this dependence can be neglected.

Comparing the forces of static friction and the forces of sliding friction, Coulomb came to the conclusion about the existence of the phenomenon of stagnation and described this phenomenon.

When wood slides on wood without lubrication at a certain speed, the friction force is also proportional to normal pressure, but in magnitude it is much less than that required to break the connection between the surfaces after some time of their contact. In the 90s, Coulomb conducted a series of experiments to study brittle friction. In these experiments he used a large suspension. The friction effect was determined by the rate of decay of large oscillations of a cylinder placed in a vessel with liquid (this device is shown at figure). The scientist came to the conclusion that at very low speeds, resistance arose in proportion to the speed, and at high speeds - to its square.
