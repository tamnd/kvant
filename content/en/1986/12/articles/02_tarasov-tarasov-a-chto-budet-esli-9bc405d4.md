What will happen if...?

D. L. TARASOV,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. V. TARASOV

“What will happen if...?” Questions of this type can be very different; the word “if” can lead to a variety of sentences, including those regarding some physical processes or phenomena. Each time such questions allow us to consider some physical situation and thereby help us better understand the essence of certain phenomena, the role and scope of certain physical laws.

What will happen if the speed of light in all transparent media suddenly becomes equal to the speed of light in a vacuum? Perhaps today this question will seem far-fetched. But, as we will see, the answer to it, we will have to understand many familiar and seemingly very important things. And besides, years ago this question could have arisen, and quite seriously at that.

Let us remember: in the 17th-18th centuries, a purely mechanical approach to optical phenomena dominated. The Odinians adhered to Newton's “exhaustion theory,” according to which light is viewed as a stream of rapidly flying small “particles.” Others, following Huygens, considered light as the propagation of elastic waves in a special medium - ether - filling all microspace, including transverse bodies. To explain the observed phenomena, it was necessary to endow the ether with amazing and sometimes inexplicable properties. Thus, it was necessary to propose that the speed of “elastic” light waves in the ether depends on what kind of bodies “fill” the ether. This was not clear to the wave; at first glance it seemed that the speed of propagation of elastic disturbances in the floating ether should be the same here. It was then that the question could arise: isn’t the speed of light really the same in all bodies and in vacuum?

Let us assume that this is indeed the case, and let us see what “consequences” this proposal will lead to. Of course, we will use our knowledge of the actual nature of optical phenomena, a theory that is consistent with everyday experience. We know that the speed v of light in any medium is always the speed of light c in a vacuum. Always c>v. The c/v ratio is an indicator of the supply of light n for a given environment.

Let us assume that the speed of light in all transparent media has become equal to the speed of light in vacuum: v=c. Therefore, for all environments now n=1. This means, in particular, that the refraction of light rays at the boundary of media has disappeared. Passing from air to water, the light beam will no longer change its direction. A spoon in a glass of water will not seem “broken” to us, and the distance to a coin at the bottom of the pool will not appear to us.

So, in the new situation, the world has become noticeably poorer: the refraction of rays at the boundaries of media has disappeared, the refraction of light in the atmosphere has disappeared, and dispersion has disappeared. What will happen to the reflection? With the usual reflection of light from the surface of a transparent medium? It will disappear too! (This could have been tired already from figure 1, 6). The picture turns out impressive. Being on the shore of the lake, we now do not see trees, bushes, clouds reflected in the water, we do not see the night moonlit road on the water. We don’t see the rays, the rivers, or the sea at all - we will only see the bottom! Agree, this will be a very significant change in the landscape. But that's not all, even more impressive changes will occur.

Why is the sky blue? It's all about the scattering of light in the earth's atmosphere. The intensity of the scattered light is inversely proportional to $\lambda^4$, where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light. This means that in scattered light the part of the spectrum close to its philotine end has greater intensity. As a result, the spectrum of scattered light appears to be shifted towards shorter waves - instead of white light, blue light is obtained. Looking at the sky, we see light scattered by the atmosphere, and the sky is blue. When we look at the setting sun or simply in the direction of the sun, we perceive mainly not scattered light, but direct solar rays that have passed through only a layer of the atmosphere without scattering. The spectrum of such light is shifted towards longer wavelengths. Therefore, the setting sun is red, and the sky near it is “colored” in orange and red tones.

Now let’s think about what will happen to all this in that new situation,

which one we are discussing. Where exactly is light scattered? On changes in air density caused by random movements of molecules. These changes are useful: the chaotic density changes from point to point and from one moment in time to another. Light scattering is caused by random changes in the refractive index of air, which are associated with changes in its density. In other words, light is scattered by the optical inconsistencies of the atmosphere, caused by the thermal movement of its molecules. In our new situation, changes in air density do not lead to the appearance of optical inexpensiveness. This means that the scattering of light in the atmosphere will disappear - we will see a black starry sky, and on it the bright white disk of the Sun.

But in fact, will we see anything at all? After all, as soon as the speed of light in a medium becomes equal to the speed of light in a vacuum, the lens of our eye - this lens created by nature itself - will cease to perform its functions. The eye will turn into something like a simple pinhole camera. Will he be able to see? Wouldn't the resulting “farsightedness” be excessively large?

These are the non-obvious situations that just one question has led us to: what will happen if the speed of light becomes in a vacuum.
