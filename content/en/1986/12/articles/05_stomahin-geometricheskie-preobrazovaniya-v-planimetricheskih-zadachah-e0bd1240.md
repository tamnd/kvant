Geometric transformations in planimetric problems

Rotation, alignment and central symmetry, parallel translation, preparation - all these plane transformations often help to establish many interesting and important properties of geometric figures and more fully reveal the content of the subject.

In this article we will look at several problems in planimetrics, the solutions of which using plane transformations are simpler than other traditional methods, and we will touch a little on the issue of composition of transformations.

Task 1. Let $O$ be the center of the circle circumscribed about the triangle $ABC$, $M$ the intersection point of its medians, $H$ the intersection point of its altitudes. Show that the points $O$, $M$, $H$ lie on the same line.

Solution. On figure 1 the points $B_1$ and $C_1$ are the midpoints of the sides $AC$ and $AB$ of the triangle $ABC$, $BB_2$ and $CC_2$ are its altitudes.

Consider a ready-made one with center $M$ and coefficient $k=-2$. During this preparation, points $B_1$ and $C_1$ are mapped to points $B$ and $C$ respectively. Since the values of the angles are preserved during preparation, the segment $B_1O$ will go into a segment lying on the line $BB_2$, and the segment $C_1O$ will go into a segment lying on the line $CC_2$, so the point $O$ of the intersection of lines $B_1O$ and $C_1O$ will go to the point $H$ intersection of heights $BB_2$ and $CC_2$. It follows that the points $O$, $M$, $H$ lie on the same line.

Problem 4. Quadrilateral ABCD is inscribed in a circle. Prove that lines drawn through the midpoints of its side perpendicular to the corresponding opposite sides intersect at one point.

Solution. Let the points M, N, F, K be the midpoints of the side AB, BC, CD and AD, respectively (Fig. 4). The lines drawn through the points M, N, F, K perpendicular to the sides AB, BC, CD and AD, respectively, intersect at the center of the given circle - point O. It is clear that the quadrilateral MNFK is a parallelogram. Let P be its center of symmetry.

With central symmetry with the center at point P, the points F, M, K, N are symmetries, respectively, of the points M, F, N, K. Let a, b, c, d be lines passing through the points M, N, F, K, respectively, perpendicular to the sides CD, AD, AB, BC. Obviously, with central symmetry with center P, the lines a, b, c, d are images of the lines OF, OK, OM, ON, respectively (with central symmetry, the line turns into a line parallel to it). This implies the statement of the problem: straight lines a, b, c, d intersect at one point E - the image of point O with central symmetry with the center P.

Problem 6. Find a point whose sum of distances from the vertices of a given triangle would be minimal.

Solution. Let $ABC$ be a given triangle, $P$ an arbitrary point on the plane. Rotate the triangle $ABC$ around the point $A$ by $60^\circ$ (Fig. 6). Let the points $B_1$ and $P_1$ be the images of the points $B$ and $P$ under this rotation. Then $B_1P_1=BP$, $AP_1=AP$, and therefore,
$$l=AP+BP+CP=AP_1+B_1P_1+CP=B_1P_1+P_1P+CP.$$
It is clear that $l$ will be minimal if the points $P_1$ and $P$ lie on the line $B_1C$. It is easy to calculate that in this case $\angle APC=\angle APB=\angle BPC=120^\circ$.

Thus, the required point is the point from which the sides of a given triangle are visible at an angle $120^\circ$.

Problem 7. On the sides of triangle $ABC$, regular triangles $ABC_1$, $BCA_1$ and $ACB_1$ are constructed to the outside. Prove that lines $AA_1$, $BB_1$ and $CC_1$ intersect at one point.

Solution. Let $O$ be the intersection point of lines $AA_1$ and $BB_1$. Let's rotate the plane of the triangle $ABC$ around the point $C$ by $60^\circ$ counterclockwise. With this rotation (Fig. 7), the triangle $CBB_1$ is the image of the triangle $CAA_1$. To Pozny, the angles $A_1AC$ and $BB_1C$ are equal, hence the angles $AOB_1$ and $ACB_1$ are equal, that is, $\angle AOB_1=60^\circ$.

Let us now plot the segment $OO_1=AO$ on the line $BB_1$ from the point $O$ and rotate the plane around the point $A$ by $60^\circ$ so that the points $B$, $B_1$, $O_1$ go to the points $C_1$, $C$, $O$ respectively. We found that with this rotation the straight line $BB_1$ goes into the straight line $CC_1$ containing the point $O$.

Comment. Point $O$ lies on the circumcircle of triangle $AB_1C$, so $\angle AOC=120^\circ$. Same as $\angle AOB=\angle BOC=120^\circ$. So, all sides of the triangle $ABC$ are visible from point $O$ at an angle $120^\circ$ (compare this result with the solution to Problem 6).

Let us now prove three small but very important theorems about the compositions of various transformations.

Theorem 1. Successive symmetry about two intersecting axes is a rotation around the point $O$ of intersection of these axes by an angle twice the angle between them.

Proof. Let $M$ be an arbitrary point different from point $O$, $M_1$ be a point symmetrical to $M$ relative to the $l$ axis, $M_2$ be a point symmetrical to $M_1$ relative to the $k$ axis, $\alpha$ be the angle between the vector $\overrightarrow{OM}$ and axis $l$, $\varphi$ is the angle between axes $k$ and $l$. Suppose that the point $M_1$ lies inside the angle equal to $\varphi$ (other cases

Then (see Fig. 8) the angle between vectors $\overrightarrow{OM}$ and $\overrightarrow{OM_1}$ is $2\alpha$, the angle between vectors $\overrightarrow{OM_1}$ and $\overrightarrow{OM_2}$ is $2(\varphi - \alpha)$ and the angle between vectors $\overrightarrow{OM}$ and $\overrightarrow{OM_2}$ is $2\alpha + 2(\varphi - \alpha) = 2\varphi$. Since, obviously, $|\overrightarrow{OM}| = |\overrightarrow{OM_1}| = |\overrightarrow{OM_2}|$, our transformation is indeed a rotation through the angle $2\varphi$.

Theorem 2. Successive symmetry with respect to two different centers of symmetry is parallel transfer.

Proof. Let $O_1$ and $O_2$ be the centers of symmetry and $M$ an arbitrary point of the plane. Let, further, point $M_1$ be symmetrical to point $M$ relative to the center $O_1$ and point $M_2$ symmetrical to point $M_1$ relative to the center $O_2$. Then $O_1O_2$ is the middle line of triangle $MM_1M_2$ (Fig. 9), and therefore $\overrightarrow{MM_2} = 2\overrightarrow{O_1O_2}$. We see that our transformation will mix an arbitrary point onto the same vector $2\overrightarrow{O_1O_2}$; that means it is a parallel transfer.

Theorem 3. Consecutive execution of parallel transfer and geometry is pre-matter with the same coefficient.

Proof. Let the vector $\vec{a}$ define the parallel translation, $O$ the center of the geometry, $k$ the geometry coefficient. An arbitrary point $M$, under a composite transformation, goes into a point $M_1$ such that (see Fig. 10)

$$\overrightarrow{MM_1} = \vec{a} + (k - 1)(\overrightarrow{OM} + \vec{a}) = (k - 1)\overrightarrow{OM} + k\vec{a}.$$

Let us denote by $O_1$ such a point that

$$\overrightarrow{O_1O} = \frac{k\vec{a}}{k - 1}$$

(we have $k \neq 1$). Then

$$\overrightarrow{MM_1} = (k - 1)\overrightarrow{OM} + (k - 1)\frac{k\vec{a}}{k - 1} = (k - 1)\overrightarrow{OM} + (k - 1)\overrightarrow{O_1O} = (k - 1)\overrightarrow{O_1M},$$

$$\overrightarrow{O_1M} = \overrightarrow{O_1M} + \overrightarrow{MM_1} = k\overrightarrow{O_1M}.$$

That is, our transformation is a geometry with center $O_1$ and coefficient $k$.

Corollary 1. Consecutive application of parallel translation and central symmetry is central symmetry.

Corollary 2. An odd number of successive central symmetries is a central symmetry.
