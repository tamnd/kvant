If the centers of the squares coincide, the statement of the problem is obvious. This arrangement can always be obtained by cutting one of the squares in parallel, so it is enough to prove that with a parallel intersection of, say, a red square, the sum of the red sides of the intersection octagon does not change. In this case, we can limit ourselves to a shift along one of the sides of this square, since any parallel transfer can be replaced by two such shifts (along the peredicular sides).

So, let's move the red square along one of its sides (Fig. 1). Then the two sides of the octagon (parallel directions of the octagon) will not change; the third side will decrease by an amount equal to the hypotenuse of the right angle triangle shown in figure 1 by hatching, and the fourth side will increase by the same amount (the shaded triangles obviously have the same angles and the same heights, lowered to the hypotenuse, and therefore are equal). Thus, the sum of all 4 red sides does not change, which is what needed to be proven.

Let's give one more solution. Let us consider right triangles cut off from each square by the sides of another square, and draw in them the heights from the vertices of right angles. All these triangles are similar to each other, so it is enough to prove that the sum of the red heights (see Fig. 2) is equal to the sum of the blue heights. To prove it, you only need to note that the sum of the areas of 4 red triangles (Fig. 3, a) is equal to the sum of the areas of blue triangles (Fig. 3, b), since each of these sums is equal to the difference between the area of ​​a convex octagon, the vertices of which are the vertices of these squares, and the area of ​​the square, and that these sums are equal to the product of the half-perimeter of the square and the sums of the red and blue heights.

A similar reasoning about the areas of the right triangles mentioned above, cut off from each other by squares, shows that the sum of the squares of the lengths of the red sides of the octagon is equal to the sum of the squares of the lengths of the blue sides.

F1008. Rod AB is placed inside a cylinder. At point A the rod is hinged, at point C it rests on the top edge of the cylinder, points A and C lie in a vertical plane passing through the axes of the cylinder (Fig. 1); the angle that makes up the rod with the horizon is equal to α. The rod is moved along the upper edge so that it touches it at point C' and the angle C'OC is equal to φ. At what minimum coefficient will the triangle be in equilibrium in this position?

Let the rod be in equilibrium at the new position when it touches the cylinder at point C'. The rod is acted upon by the force of gravity, the reaction force of the hinge at point A, the force N of the normal reaction of the cylinder at point C' and the friction force f. The condition for the equilibrium of the rod is that the geometric sum of these forces is equal to zero and the sum of the moments of forces is equal to zero.

Of all the equations for the equilibrium of a rod, we consider one - the equality to zero of the sum of the moments of forces relative to the vertical axis passing point A (relative to this axis, the moment of the reaction force of the hinge and the moment of the gravitational force are equal to zero). It is easy to understand that this condition boils down to the fact that the total vector of forces N and f lies in the vertical plane passing point A (relative to this axis, the moment of the reaction force of the hinge and the moment of gravity are equal to zero). We direct the coordinate axes X, Y and Z from the point C' corresponding to the center of the circle O, tangent to the circle and vertical upward (Fig. 2).

Let us introduce three unit vectors n₁, n₂ and n₃, which

which are directed respectively along the rod, along the force $\vec{N}$ and along the force $\vec{f}$. Let the angle that the vector $\vec{n}_l$ makes with the horizon be equal to $\alpha'$. Since, as a result of rotation of the rod, the vertical plane in which it lies rotates through an angle $\varphi/2$, it is easy to show that

$$tg \alpha' = tg \alpha/\cos (\varphi/2).$$

Let us express each of the vectors $\vec{n}_l$, $\vec{n}_N$ and $\vec{n}_f$ in terms of the unit vectors $\vec{n}_x$, $\vec{n}_y$ and $\vec{n}_z$. Let's start with $\vec{n}_l$:

$$\vec{n}_l = \vec{n}_x \left( -\cos \alpha' \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} \right) + \vec{n}_y \cos \alpha' \sin \frac{\varphi}{2} + \vec{n}_z \sin \alpha'.$$

The force $\vec{N}$ is perpendicular to the rod and tangent to the circle at point $C'$ (the edge of the cylinder), which means that the vector $\vec{n}_N$ is perpendicular to the vector $\vec{n}_y$, that is

$$\vec{n}_N = \vec{n}_x \sin \gamma + \vec{n}_z \cos \gamma,$$

(*)

as well as the vector $\vec{n}_l$. From here we obtain the correspondence between angles $\gamma$ and $\alpha'$ in the form

$$tg \gamma = tg \alpha'/\cos (\varphi/2).$$

To determine the direction of vector $\vec{n}_f$, we take into account that it is perpendicular to both vector $\vec{n}_N$ and vector $\vec{n}_l$. After several calculations we get

$$\vec{n}_f = \vec{n}_x \left( -\cos \gamma \cos \alpha' \sin \frac{\varphi}{2} \right) + \vec{n}_y \left( -\sin \gamma \sin \alpha' - \cos \gamma \cos \alpha' \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} \right) + \vec{n}_z \sin \gamma \cos \alpha' \sin \frac{\varphi}{2}.$$

(**)

From equation (*) and (**) it follows that $N_x = N \sin \gamma, N_y = 0,$

$$f_x = f \left( -\cos \gamma \cos \alpha' \sin \frac{\varphi}{2} \right),$$

$$f_y = f \left( -\sin \gamma \sin \alpha' - \cos \gamma \cos \alpha' \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} \right).$$

As already noted, the equation of moments of forces properties means that, in particular, the horizontal projection of the sum of forces $\vec{N}$ and $\vec{f}$ must lie in the plane of the rod, that is

$$f_x \sin \frac{\varphi}{2} + N_x \sin \frac{\varphi}{2} + f_y \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} = 0.$$

The minimum value of the friction coefficient $k_{\min}$ is determined by the correspondence $k_{\min} N = f$. Then we finally get

$$k_{\min} = \frac{tg (\varphi/2)}{\sqrt{1 + ctg^2 \alpha \cos^2(\varphi/2)}}.$$

A balloon, the lifting force of which is created by hot air, is designed in such a way that the volume of its observable chamber is practically built up, and the pressure in it is equal to the external pressure, since the chamber in its lower part communicates with the atmosphere. ◆ The condition of a balloon floating at a certain height

$$mg + \rho V g = \rho_0 V g,$$

(1) where $m$ is the mass of the air shell of the balloon and the cargo compartment, $\rho$ is the density of hot air, $\rho_0$ is the density of atmospheric air at the floating altitude of the ball, $V$ is the volume of the chamber, $g$ is the acceleration of free pressure.

From (1) it follows that at the new height equilibrium will be possible if the values ​​$\rho_0$ change so that

$$\Delta \rho = \Delta \rho_0,$$

(2) where $\Delta \rho_0$ is the change in the density of atmospheric air, $\Delta \rho$ is the change in the density of hot air in the chamber.

For small changes in altitude, the pressure decreases with increasing altitude ($\Delta h$) by

$$\Delta p = \rho_0 g \cdot \Delta h.$$

Since the temperature of the atmosphere remains constant, for the density of atmospheric air at different altitudes we have:

$$\frac{\rho_0 - \Delta \rho_0}{\rho_0} = \frac{p_0 - \Delta p}{p_0},$$

where

$$\Delta \rho_0 = \rho_0 \frac{\Delta p}{p_0} = \rho_0^2 \frac{g}{p_0} \Delta h.$$

(3) The density of hot air in the chamber changes due to changes in temperature and pressure:

$$\Delta \rho = \frac{p_0 \mu}{RT} - \frac{(p_0 - \Delta p) \mu}{R (T + \Delta T)} =$$

$$\frac{\mu (p_0 \cdot \Delta T + \Delta p \cdot T)}{R (T + \Delta T)} = \frac{p_0 \mu \left( \frac{\Delta T}{T} + \frac{\Delta p}{p_0} \right)}{RT \left( 1 + \frac{\Delta T}{T} \right)},$$

$$\Delta \rho \approx \frac{p_0 \mu}{RT} \left( \frac{\Delta T}{T} + \frac{\Delta p}{p_0} \right).$$

(4)

Substituting (3) and (4) into (2), we find:
