Have you looked closely at how the evening fog appears over the river? It does not appear immediately, but first thickens over whirlpools and riffles. Individual light clouds in the moonlight fill the figures of women in white clothes. And you begin to understand our ancestors, who created so many legends about mermaids.

We also encounter descriptions of this sometimes amazingly red, and sometimes alarming and mysterious natural phenomenon on the pages of works of fiction. In the margins of this and the following pages are several works and poetic quotations that describe the fog. Try to guess who their authors are.

How often you can admire the fog depends on the terrain. For example, for residents of Newfoundland, an island located off the coast of Canada in a place where the warm Gulf Stream meets the cold Labrador Current, fogs are constant guests. For the third part of the year, including almost all summer days, the island is covered with thick haze. Probably, even the most red description of the fog is unlikely to cause admiration among the residents of Newfoundland. The real “land of fogs” is England, “foggy Albon”. In our country, the coasts of the northern seas - the Baltic, Okhotsk, and the Kola Peninsula - are especially rich in foggy days.

If natural fogs are rare where you live, don’t forget that at home you can see them literally at every step. A white cloud emerging from the spout of a boiling kettle is fog. In everyday life we ​​call it steam, but this is incorrect, since water steam is a non-sparkling gas. If you quickly open a bottle of sparkling water, smoke will appear above the neck at the first moment - this is also fog. On the street on a frosty day, fog swirls over an open water surface, over ice holes or full holes, rushes into the room through an open window, and appears when breathing (again, we are used to saying “steam from breathing”).

And they release excess moisture in the form of tiny droplets on condensation centers - dust particles, partial smoke, gas particles, etc. When droplets appear in the air, we talk about fog. And drops on the surface of the earth, on leaves and grass are called dew.

To imagine how much the water vapor content in the air changes with temperature changes, here are the following figures:

the mass of saturated water vapor in 1 m³ of air at 30 °C is 30 g;

when air is cooled to 10 °C, the mass of saturated steam decreases to 10 g/m³;

This means that in every cubic meter of air, 20 g of water should be released in the form of drops of fog or dew.

You can determine the size of the droplets that make up the fog as follows. Hold a piece of glass, smeared with seed, in the fog, and then examine the remaining drops of moisture on it under a microscope. A microsop is necessary because even the largest drops reach only ten microns in size, their diameter is almost a hundred times smaller than the thickness of a match.

Usually fogs do not last long. Small drops merge into larger ones and fall down - the fog should fall to the ground. But it may also happen that in the warm air flow droplets of fog evaporate and the fog dissipates.

All this can be observed in a simple experiment, which requires a coffee pot, a frying pan and a small piece of rubber tubing. We insert the tube tightly into the spout of the coffee pot, pour water into it and place it on the stove. When the water boils, a white stream of water mist begins to escape from the tube. Let's bring (using tweezers) the end of the tube to a cold frying pan standing on the adjacent burner. The fog will rise above it and partially settle in drops on the cold surface of the metal. But if you heat the pan, the fog will disappear. In a flow of heated air, the density of water vapor becomes lower than saturated, and water droplets do not form (or evaporate).

“Citizens, the Moscow-Vorkuta flight is delayed by two hours.” Unfortunately, announcements of this kind can often be heard at the airport. Most often, the delay occurs due to fog. It is he who prevents planes from landing and prevents them from taking off. How to deal with this?

At first, the engineers proposed an almost very prominent method, which “worked” so well in the experiment with the frying pan. It was during the war. English pilots must repel the Nazis. Huge torches blazed along the entire landing strip. They heated the air, drops of fog evaporated, and visibility increased many times - up to a kilometer.

But this method turned out to be expensive and ineffective. The fact is that when removed, oil itself produces a lot of water vapor, they continuously replenish the “reserves” of fog, and the flares basically had to fight with themselves. As a result, at each airfield, a whole barrel of oil was blown away in a second. While this was already quite expensive for small airfields, modern runways would require a whole river of oil. We had to give up heating the air.

More promising was the scattering of substances that absorb moisture over the airfield, for example, the finest powder of potassium chloride. Its particles collect fog droplets into larger ones and fall to the ground. Air humidity drops and the fog dissipates.

While we're on the topic of fog and airplanes, we would like to mention two more facts connecting them.

Each of you has probably replaced the white trail of a flying plane in the sky more than once. This is fog formed from water vapor, the supplier of which is stale fuel. Hot exhaust gas, saturated with water vapor, enters the cold atmosphere and forms fog.

A much more terrible “fog trail” can follow a plane that has crashed. This is a trace of fuel that is thrown into the air stream and sprayed into tiny droplets. The resulting fire can spread along such a trail at a speed of ten meters per second, so that no fire fighting equipment will be able to come to the rescue. In this regard, a problem arose in fuel substances that significantly reduce the flammability of such fog. And such work is underway.
