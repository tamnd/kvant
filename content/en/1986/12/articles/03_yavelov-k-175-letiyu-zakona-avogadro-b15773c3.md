To the 175th anniversary of Avogadro's law

The Italian physicist Amedeo Avogadro*) (1776-1856) came from a family whose men, starting from the 12th century, invariably became lawyers. It is even believed that the surname Avogadro itself originated from avvocato (in Italian - lawyer). But the future scientist, who received his doctorate in law at the age of twenty, did not remain faithful to the family tradition for too long. Even in his youth, he developed a keen interest in the exact sciences, and from 1800 he began to devote all his free time to the study of physics and mathematics.

A few years later, Avogadro is already the author of two scientific works and a corresponding member of the Turin Academy of Sciences; in 1806 he finally left the practice of law and became a teacher of physics and mathematics. The former core scientist continues to persistently study specialized literature, and the number of his own scientific works is growing. In scientific circles in Italy, the name of Avogadro is becoming widely known. In 1819, he was elected a full member of the Turin Academy, and a year later, by royal decree, he was appointed professor of the first department of higher (in modern - theoretical) physics in Italy at the University of Turin.

A man of a penetrating mind, encyclopedic knowledge and rare modesty, Avogadro enjoyed high scientific authority in his homeland. But outside of Italy his research finds no response. Wide recognition came only four years after the death of the scientist, when in 1860, at the first international chemical congress in Germany, his compatriot, chemist S. before the audience, the contents of one of Avogadro’s articles, written back in 1811. In this article, Avogadro's famous law was formed:

Equal volumes of different gases at the same temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules.

Avogadro came to this most important conclusion by analyzing the experimental results published in 1808 by the French physicist J. L. Gay-Lussac (1778-1850), who studied volumetric relationships in reactions between gases and established that the volumes of gases and gaseous reaction products that entered into the reaction are related as small integers.

Ten years before Avogadro, the founder of chemical atomism, the Englishman J. Dalton (1766-1844), considered the hypothesis of the equality of the number of parts in equal volumes of all gases. However, he was forced to respond to this, in his words, “foggy” idea as contradictory to the schemes of a number of chemical reactions he had developed.

The problem in which Dalton and Gay-Lussac, and with them many other prominent scientists, were entangled, was beautifully and simply solved by the then unknown Avogadro. He reasoned as follows. Dalton is undoubtedly right in asserting that the elementary act of a chemical reaction consists in the rearrangement of atoms, those reactively existing discrete portions of materials from which all substances are composed. And Kolbsky is so true, then Gay-Lussac’s discovery necessarily follows that there must be some kind of lifeless relationship between the volumes of gases and the number of parts forming them. The simplest thing is to assume that equal volumes of all gases under the same external conditions contain an equal number of parts. But in this case, a contradiction will arise between the promised diagrams of gases and their reactions and the measurement data. Indeed, the reaction of the formation of water vapor from hydrogen and oxygen represents hydrogen and one atoms of oxygen to form one molecule of water. This means that if we assume that equal numbers of parts occupy equal volumes, the ratio of the corresponding objects in this reaction should be 2:1:1, and not 2:1:2, as Gay-Lussac established.

Reflection on this and several similar discrepancies led Avogadro to the conclusion that the Khimki people’s ideas about gas reactions were inaccurate. So, in the reaction of the formation of water, it is not atoms, but diatomic molecules of hydrogen and oxygen that combine, that is, the reaction should proceed according to the scheme: $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 = 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$, exactly corresponding to the volume ratio of 2:1:2. In the same way, the scientist easily brought the rigor into correspondence with experimental data and a number of other gas reaction schemes.

It would seem that Avogadro's "simplest" assumption, as fully justified, should have quickly gained recognition as a new fundamental law of nature, especially since the scientist justified his position in a famous French scientific journal. But, as already mentioned, this will happen only half a century later. What is the reason for this protracted problem? It is probably partly explained by the fact that Avogadro, as a person devoid of pure binium, did almost nothing to promote his discovery and withdrew from the fight for its recognition by foreign collections. But most importantly, the spiritual world was not yet ready to accept Avogadro’s conclusions, because with regard to Avogadro’s conclusions, since with regard to the initial concepts of the atom-molecular theory, there was complete confusion. There is no basic mutual understanding: what one called an atom, another called a molecule, and a third called parts.
