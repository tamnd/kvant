Huge torches blazed along the entire landing strip. They heated the air, drops of fog evaporated, and visibility increased many times - up to a kilometer.

But this method turned out to be expensive and ineffective. The fact is that when removed, oil itself produces a lot of water vapor, they continuously replenish the “reserves” of fog, and the flares basically had to fight with themselves. As a result, at each airfield, a whole barrel of oil was blown away in a second. While this was already quite expensive for small airfields, modern runways would require a whole river of oil. We had to give up heating the air.

More promising was the scattering of substances that absorb moisture over the airfield, for example, the finest powder of potassium chloride. Its particles collect fog droplets into larger ones and fall to the ground. Air humidity drops and the fog dissipates.

While we're on the topic of fog and airplanes, we would like to mention two more facts connecting them.

Each of you has probably replaced the white trail of a flying plane in the sky more than once. This is fog formed from water vapor, the supplier of which is stale fuel. Hot exhaust gas, saturated with water vapor, enters the cold atmosphere and forms fog.

A much more terrible “fog trail” can follow a plane that has crashed. This is a trace of fuel that is thrown into the air stream and sprayed into tiny droplets. The resulting fire can spread along such a trail at a speed of ten meters per second, so that no fire fighting equipment will be able to come to the rescue. In this regard, a problem arose in fuel substances that significantly reduce the flammability of such fog. And such work is underway.
