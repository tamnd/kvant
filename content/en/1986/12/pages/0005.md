At this time, Evariste became acquainted with the works of Gauss and Abel and felt that he was capable of doing no less. Being just a preparatory class student, he, without outside help, prepares for exams at the Ecole Polytechnique, the best higher education institution in France at that time. Evarist believes that all the strength and energy of his mind and heart will be used in her.

An attempt to enter the Polytechnic School ended in failure. The failure greatly upset Galois and, according to the mathematical historian Dupioni, "was the first of the injustices that eventually poisoned his life." Evariste returns to the college he had been put in order and, having skipped the main one, enters a special mathematics class. Louis-Paul Richard, a wonderful teacher who passionately loved his science, worked there at that time. Among those who studied with him over the years were, in addition to Galois, the replaced astronomer Le Verrier and the outstanding mathematician Hermite.

Richard paid great attention to the equestrian student, whom he considered the most gifted of his students. Richard's comments about Galois concluded: “Galois works only in the highest areas of mathematics,” “He is significantly superior to all his products.” Under the guidance of Richard, Evariste completed his first scientific work, “Proof of a theorem on periodic continued fractions,” published in 1829 in Le Annal de Mathematician. At the same time, under the influence of the work of Lagrange, Galois began to intensively study one of the most difficult mathematical problems of that time - the problems of solvability of algebraic equations in radials.

This problem has a long history. They also discovered a way to solve the second degree equation $ax^2+bx+c=0$. Its roots in modern symbolism are given by the formula

$$x_{1,2} = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a},$$

which includes four arithmetic operations on coefficients, as well as the square radikal. At the beginning of the sixteenth century, Scippone del Ferro and Nicolo Fontana, better known as Tartaglia, obtained a formula for the roots of a cubic equation, which included four arithmetic operations and the square and cubic radiikal.
