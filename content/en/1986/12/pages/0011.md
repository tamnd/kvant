⟦folio 9⟧

What will happen if...?

D. L. TARASOV,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. V. TARASOV

“What will happen if...?” Questions of this type can be very different; the word “if” can lead to a variety of sentences, including those regarding some physical processes or phenomena. Each time such questions allow us to consider some physical situation and thereby help us better understand the essence of certain phenomena, the role and scope of certain physical laws.

What will happen if the speed of light in all transparent media suddenly becomes equal to the speed of light in a vacuum? Perhaps today this question will seem far-fetched. But, as we will see, the answer to it, we will have to understand many familiar and seemingly very important things. And besides, years ago this question could have arisen, and quite seriously at that.

Let us remember: in the 17th-18th centuries, a purely mechanical approach to optical phenomena dominated. The Odinians adhered to Newton's “exhaustion theory,” according to which light is viewed as a stream of rapidly flying small “particles.” Others, following Huygens, considered light as the propagation of elastic waves in a special medium - ether - filling all microspace, including transverse bodies. To explain the observed phenomena, it was necessary to endow the ether with amazing and sometimes inexplicable properties. Thus, it was necessary to propose that the speed of “elastic” light waves in the ether depends on what kind of bodies “fill” the ether. This was not clear to the wave; at first glance it seemed that the speed of propagation of elastic disturbances in the floating ether should be the same here. It was then that the question could arise: isn’t the speed of light really the same in all bodies and in vacuum?

Let us assume that this is indeed the case, and let us see what “consequences” this proposal will lead to. Of course, we will use our knowledge of the actual nature of optical phenomena, a theory that is consistent with everyday experience. We know that the speed v of light in any medium is always the speed of light c in a vacuum. Always c>v. The c/v ratio is an indicator of the supply of light n for a given environment.

Let us assume that the speed of light in all transparent media has become equal to the speed of light in vacuum: v=c. Therefore, for all environments now n=1. This means, in particular, that the refraction of light rays at the boundary of media has disappeared. Passing from air to water, the light beam will no longer change its direction. A spoon in a glass of water will not seem “broken” to us, and the distance to a coin at the bottom of the pool will not appear to us.
