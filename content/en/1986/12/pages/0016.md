And they release excess moisture in the form of tiny droplets on condensation centers - dust particles, partial smoke, gas particles, etc. When droplets appear in the air, we talk about fog. And drops on the surface of the earth, on leaves and grass are called dew.

To imagine how much the water vapor content in the air changes with temperature changes, here are the following figures:

the mass of saturated water vapor in 1 m³ of air at 30 °C is 30 g;

when air is cooled to 10 °C, the mass of saturated steam decreases to 10 g/m³;

This means that in every cubic meter of air, 20 g of water should be released in the form of drops of fog or dew.

You can determine the size of the droplets that make up the fog as follows. Hold a piece of glass, smeared with seed, in the fog, and then examine the remaining drops of moisture on it under a microscope. A microsop is necessary because even the largest drops reach only ten microns in size, their diameter is almost a hundred times smaller than the thickness of a match.

Usually fogs do not last long. Small drops merge into larger ones and fall down - the fog should fall to the ground. But it may also happen that in the warm air flow droplets of fog evaporate and the fog dissipates.

All this can be observed in a simple experiment, which requires a coffee pot, a frying pan and a small piece of rubber tubing. We insert the tube tightly into the spout of the coffee pot, pour water into it and place it on the stove. When the water boils, a white stream of water mist begins to escape from the tube. Let's bring (using tweezers) the end of the tube to a cold frying pan standing on the adjacent burner. The fog will rise above it and partially settle in drops on the cold surface of the metal. But if you heat the pan, the fog will disappear. In a flow of heated air, the density of water vapor becomes lower than saturated, and water droplets do not form (or evaporate).
