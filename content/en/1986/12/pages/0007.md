In June 1831, Galois was brought to trial on charges of "attempting to induce a purchase on the life and person of the King of the French by a statement made in a public place during a public meeting." The jury acquits Galois, but he will fall under the secret surveillance of the political police.

July 14, 1831 Galois takes part in a demonstration. Its participants will protest against the Louis-Philippe government's ban on freedom of manifestation. Many participants in this demonstration were arrested, and Evariste ended up in the Sainte-Pélagie prison. Here he celebrated his twentieth birthday, and here he edited his main mathematical work. On March 16, 1832, Galois was transferred to the prison hospital with suspected cholera. There is information that Galois remained in it for some time after his term of imprisonment ended on April 29. There are no traces of his life during May 1832. On the morning of May 30, a passer-by found him seriously wounded after being shot with pistols on the banks of the Glasbert pond in the Parisian suburb of Gentilly. The next day at 10 o'clock in the morning Galois died. The cause of his muzzle and his opponent are not known for certain. Before his death, he wrote a letter to his friend Auguste Chevalier asking him to show his manuscript to the German mathematicians Jacobi and Gauss. However, the manuscript saw the light only in 1846 and remained virtually unnoticed. Galois’s ideas were truly understood only in the 70s, after the publication of K. Jordan’s book “Algebraic Controls and the Theory of Substitutions.”

The manuscript left by Galois, from which this quotation is taken, was called “Metoire on the conditions for the solvability of control in radicals.” The idenes contained in this memoir were not understood by Galois's contemporaries and are considered difficult to study even now. At the same time, the formulation of Galois theory is not complicated. True, first you need to master several new concepts.

Let there be $n$ objects, which we will denote by the natural numbers 1, 2, ..., $n$. Their substitution is the transformation of the set of these objects, specified by the table

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & ... & n \\ i_1 & i_2 & ... & i_n \end{pmatrix},$$

where $i_1, i_2, ..., i_n$ are the same numbers 1, 2, ..., $n$, but written in a different order. Each substitution consists of replacing the number in the top line with the number signed underneath it in the bottom line.

From $n$ numbers you can make $n!$ different substitutions. For example, from three numbers 1, 2, 3 you can make the following substitutions:

$$p_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 123 \\ 123 \end{pmatrix}, p_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 123 \\ 312 \end{pmatrix}, p_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 123 \\ 231 \end{pmatrix},$$

$$p_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 123 \\ 132 \end{pmatrix}, p_4 = \begin{pmatrix} 123 \\ 213 \end{pmatrix}, p_5 = \begin{pmatrix} 123 \\ 321 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The set of substitutions of $n$ items is denoted by $S_n$.
