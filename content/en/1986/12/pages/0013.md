What will happen to the reflection? With the usual reflection of light from the surface of a transparent medium? It will disappear too! (This could have been tired already from figure 1, 6). We don’t see the rays, the rivers, or the sea at all - we will only see the bottom! Agree, this will be a very significant change in the landscape. But that's not all, even more impressive changes will occur.

Why is the sky blue? It's all about the scattering of light in the earth's atmosphere. The intensity of the scattered light is inversely proportional to $\lambda^4$, where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light. This means that in scattered light the part of the spectrum close to its philotine end has greater intensity. As a result, the spectrum of scattered light appears to be shifted towards shorter waves - instead of white light, blue light is obtained. Looking at the sky, we see light scattered by the atmosphere, and the sky is blue. When we look at the setting sun or simply in the direction of the sun, we perceive mainly not scattered light, but direct solar rays that have passed through only a layer of the atmosphere without scattering. The spectrum of such light is shifted towards longer wavelengths. Therefore, the setting sun is red, and the sky near it is “colored” in orange and red tones.

Now let’s think about what will happen to all this in that new situation,

which one we are discussing. Where exactly is light scattered? On changes in air density caused by random movements of molecules. These changes are useful: the chaotic density changes from point to point and from one moment in time to another. Light scattering is caused by random changes in the refractive index of air, which are associated with changes in its density. In other words, light is scattered by the optical inconsistencies of the atmosphere, caused by the thermal movement of its molecules. In our new situation, changes in air density do not lead to the appearance of optical inexpensiveness. This means that the scattering of light in the atmosphere will disappear - we will see a black starry sky, and on it the bright white disk of the Sun.

But in fact, will we see anything at all? After all, as soon as the speed of light in a medium becomes equal to the speed of light in a vacuum, the lens of our eye - this lens created by nature itself - will cease to perform its functions. The eye will turn into something like a simple pinhole camera. Will he be able to see? Wouldn't the resulting “farsightedness” be excessively large?

These are the non-obvious situations that just one question has led us to: what will happen if the speed of light becomes in a vacuum.
