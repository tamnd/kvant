M977. Is it possible to obtain $x$ using the operations of addition, subtraction and multiplication from polynomials $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ if: a) $f(x)=x^2+x$, $g(x)=x^2+2$; b) $f(x)=x^2+x$, $g(x)=x^2-2$; c) $f(x)=2x^2+x$, $g(x)=2x$; d) $f(x)=2x^3+x$, $g(x)=x^2$.

ANSWER: in problem 6) it is possible, in other problems it is impossible.

To solve problem 6) note that $f-g=x+2$, therefore $(f-g)^2-2(f-g)-f=(f-g)(f-g-2)-f=x(x+2)-x^2-x=x$.

In the remaining problems, we will use the fact that if the polynomial $x$ is expressed in terms of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ using the operations of addition, subtraction and multiplication, then any number $a$ is expressed in the same way through the numbers $f(a)$ and $g(a)$.

M978. Is it possible to place two regular triangles with sides larger than $\sqrt{2}/3$ in a square with side 1, without overlapping each other?

ANSWER: Not possible. It is enough to prove that any regular triangle with side $\sqrt{2}/3$, lying in a square with side 1, contains the center of the square ($\text{как внутреннюю точку}$).

Let us prove this statement by contradiction. If a regular triangle $ABC$ does not contain the center of the square $O$, then it has a side, say $AB$, such that the triangle and the point $O$ lie on opposite sides of the line $AB$ (see figure). Let us draw a line parallel to $AB$ through $O$ and drop a perpendicular $CK$ from point $C$ onto this line. Let $H$ be the point of its intersection with the sides $AB$, then

$$CH<CK\leq CO,$$

but the distance $CO$ obviously does not exceed half the diagonal of the square (since the point $C$ lies inside the square). And since $CH$ is the height of the triangle, for the length of its side we get the estimate

$$\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}CH<\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}CO\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}.$$

Contradiction.

F989. A model of a crawler-mounted vehicle was placed on an inclined plane with an inclination angle of $a=30^\circ$ and released. Find the acceleration of the model. The length of the model is $l=50$ cm, the height is $h=2$ cm. The tracks are made of rubber, their weight is 80% of the total weight of the model. The friction in the mechanism of the model is negligible.

At this angle of inclination, the rubber tracks move along the plane without puncturing. This means that no heat is generated during movement (there is no friction between the tracks and the surface and there is no friction in the mechanism of the model).

Let's use the law of conservation of energy. To calculate the kinetic energy, taking into account that the length of the track is greater than the height of the model, we will assume that when the model moves with a speed of $v$, the lower half of the track is motionless, and the upper half has a speed of $2v$. Then the kinetic energy of the model is
$$E_{\kappa}=0,4 m \frac{(2 v)^2}{2}+0,2 m \frac{v^2}{2}=0,9 m v^2$$
($m$ is the mass of the entire model; $0,4 m$ is the mass of half the tracks; $0,2 m$ is the mass of the model without tracks). In a short period of time $\Delta t$ the model will drop to $\Delta h=v \cdot \Delta t \cdot \sin \alpha$, and its kinetic energy will increase by $\Delta E_{\kappa}=mg v \cdot \Delta t \cdot \sin \alpha$. If during this time the speed of the model increased by $\Delta v$, then $\Delta E_{\kappa}=0,9 m (v+\Delta v)^2-0,9 m v^2 \approx 1,8 m v \cdot \Delta v$, that is
$$mg v \cdot \Delta t \cdot \sin \alpha=1,8 m v \cdot \Delta v,$$
where does the acceleration come from?
$$a=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}=\frac{g \sin \alpha}{1,8} \approx 2,7 \text{ m/c}^2.$$
A. I. Vuzdin

F992. When filming a frame from the film “Gulliver in the Land of the Lilliputians,” the actor playing the role of Gulliver was placed at a distance of 4 m from the film camera with a short-focus object, and the actor playing the role of the Lilliputian was placed at a distance of 40 m. At what distance should the camera object be pointed so that the images of both subjects on the film strip are equally clear?

Why is a short focal length subject used in this type of photography? Estimate the focal length of such a lens if you know that regular (non-combined) photography of people at a distance of 5 m is carried out with an object with a focal length of 50 cm and a depth of field of 0.5 m.

Let's consider the lens to be a thin lens. On figure (see p. 51) there is a diagram of how an image is constructed on film in a movie camera.

The images of both film characters will be equally clear if any two points of Lilliput and Gulliver, for example $A_2$ and $A_1$, are represented on the film strip by light spots of the same radius.

The actual images of points $A_1$ and $A_2$, located at distances $d_1$ and $d_2$ from the lens (objective), are located at points $B_1$ and $B_2$ at distances $f_1$ and $f_2$, respectively. If the screen (film film) is placed in the plane $BC$ at a distance $f$ from the lens, then the images of points $A_1$ and $A_2$ will be equally blurred - together with points $B_1$ and $B_2$ there will be spots of the same radius $r = |CB|$ on the screen. Consequently, the image of the movie camera follows the point $A$, located at a distance $d$ from the lens, such that its image hits the point $B$.

From the similarity of triangles $B_1CB$ and $B_1KO$, $B_2CB$ and $B_2KO$ it follows:

$$\frac{f-f_1}{f_1} = \frac{r}{R}, \quad \frac{f_2-f}{f_2} = \frac{r}{R},$$

where $R = |OK|$ is the radius of the object’s aperture (this value is mainly determined by the required depth of field and lighting conditions), and from here we get:

$$\frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} = \frac{2}{f}.$$

On the other hand, from the Lilliputian formula it follows that

$$\frac{1}{f_1} = \frac{1}{F} - \frac{1}{d_1}, \quad \frac{1}{f_2} = \frac{1}{F} - \frac{1}{d_2}, \quad \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{F} - \frac{1}{d},$$

where $F$ is the focal length of Lilliput. Substituting these expressions into (1), we get:

$$\frac{1}{d_1} + \frac{1}{d_2} = \frac{2}{d},$$

where

$$d = 2 \frac{d_1 d_2}{d_1 + d_2}.$$

Substituting the numerical values ​​$d_1=40$ m and $d_2=4$ m, we find that during combined shooting the lenses are located at a point located at a distance of $d=7,3$ m from the lens.

The final answer does not include the focal length of the lens. Why require it to be short-focus?

Let us express the quantities $r/R$ in terms of $d_1$, $d_2$ and $F$:

$$\frac{r}{R} = \frac{(d_1-d_2)F}{2d_1 d_2 - F(d_1 + d_2)};$$

since $F \ll d_1$, $d_2$, then to obtain an estimate we can assume that

$$\frac{r}{R} = \frac{d_1 - d_2}{2d_1 d_2} F,$$

and, therefore, the blur of the image of points located at a distance of $d_1$ and $d_2$ from the lens is directly proportional to the focal length.
