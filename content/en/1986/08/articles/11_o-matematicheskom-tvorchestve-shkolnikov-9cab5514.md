About mathematical creativity of SCHOOLCHILDREN

Our magazine rarely contains articles on mathematics written by schoolchildren. It's not because high school students aren't capable of producing interesting and original math work. The point here is that most of these works are narrowly specific, often rely on extra-grammatic material, and rarely contain general mathematical ideas useful for the majority of our readers. In addition, the text of the note in «Kvant» must not only be mathematically correct and formally correct, but be written in an understandable and clear, entertaining and methodological manner. If our experienced authors do not always cope with such a requirement, then what can we expect from scientific authors!

And yet, in this anniversary, two hundredth issue of the magazine, we decided to publish a retelling of several works by schoolchildren (sent to «Kvant» or told at various conferences of schoolchildren), which, it seems to us, should be of interest to many of our readers. These works also contain completed tasks for those who want to test their creative powers.

Equally self-intersecting polygon

In the work of Andrei Shann (Moscow, school No. 57), written in 1984, when Andrei was in high school, geometric problems about broken lines with an equal number of self-intersection points on each link are solved. Let us give a precise definition of the broken lines under consideration: broken lines of titanium (n, m), where n≥3, m≥1, are sequences of n different points A₁, A₂, ..., Aₙ (vertices) and n consecutively connecting these points of segments A₁A₂, A₂A₃, ..., Aₙ-1Aₙ, AₙA₁ (links) such that each link intersects exactly m other links, moreover, at m different internal points. For example, figure 1 shows the broken titas (6, 1), (5, 2) and (9, 2).

Problem 1. For what n does the titanium broken line (n, 1) exist?

ANSWER: for all four n (n≥6); to solve A. Shann presents the titanium polyline (6, 1), and then applies to it the operation of inserting a cross (Fig. 2, a) or inserting a harmonic (Fig. 2, b).

For odd n there are no broken lines (n, 1). A more general fact is true: if both numbers n and m are odd, then the titanium broken line (n, m) does not exist. In fact, the lines of the titanium line (n, m) intersect only in pairs, so the total number of intersection points is nm/2, which is impossible when both numbers n and m are odd.

Problem 2. Is it true that for any m there exists (for some n) a broken line (n, m)?

ANSWER: yes; A. Shann solves this problem, starting with the same example of the titanium polyline (6,1), and then applying the tracing operation to it (Fig. 2 b, 2).

Problem 3. For what pairs of numbers (n, m) is there a broken line (n, m)?

This problem has not been completely solved, although A. Schanne has made great progress in solving it, using different versions of the sum operation (Fig. 3). Andrey’s results can be summarized in the following table:

Generalized Markov equation

$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 3xyz$$

In natural numbers $(x, y, z)$, which I talk about in the article by M. G. Kerin («Kvant» 1985, No. 4, p. 13). At the end of this article, the author proposes to explore a generalization of the Markov equation; The tenth classicist P. Wolf opened up to this confession.

II. Wolfbein distinguishes three types of radical solutions: (I) $a_1 = a_2 = \dots = a_n$; this is, for $n = m$, solutions of the form (1, 1, ..., 1), for $n = 3$, $m = 1$ - a solution (3, 3, 3), for $n = 4$, $m = 1$ - (2, 2, 2, 2); there are no others.

(II) $a'_1 > a_1$; for example, for $n = 7$, $m = 5$ such a solution is (2, 1, ..., 1);

(III) $a'_1 = a_1$; for example, for $n = 11$, $m = 5$ such a solution is (4, 22, 1, ..., 1).

P. Wolfbein's reasoning is based on the following lemma proved by him: for a radical solution $a_1 \geq a_2 \geq \dots \geq a_n$ of type P or III, the inequality necessarily holds

$$ma_3 a_4 \dots a_n < n.$$

Equation (2) for $n = m$ (like equation (1), where $n = m = 3$) has only one root solution (1, 1, ..., 1), and from each of its solutions of the form $a_1 > a_2 > \dots > a_k > a_{k+1} = \dots = a_n = 1$ one goes down in a branching manner to $k$ different (not obtained from each other by rearranging coordinates) neighboring solutions. As for the general case $m \neq n$, P. Wolfbein obtained only some partial results here. The problem of a complete study of equation (2) in the general case still remains to be solved.

How to differentiate a function a fractional number of times

Marat Rovinsky, a 9th grade student at school No. 57 in Moscow, figured out how, for any real number $s$, to determine from the function $f(x)$ a new function $-f^{(s)}(x)$ - in such a way that:

1) If $s$ is a non-negative integer, then $f^{(s)}x$ is the $s$th arbitrary number at the point $x$.

2) $f^{-1}(x) = \int_0^x f(t)dt$.

3) If $f(x) = 0$ at $x \leq 0$, then $(f^{(s_1)})(s_2)(x) = f^{(s_1 + s_2)}$.

From properties 2 and 3 it follows that if $n$ is a natural number, then

$$f^{-(n)}(x) = \int_0^x \left( \int_0^x f(t)dt \right) dx_{n-1}.$$

For example,

$$f^{-(2)}(x) = \int_0^x \left( \int_0^x f(t)dt \right) dx_{n-1}.$$

— the expression in brackets is considered as a function of the variable $x_1$, which is then used for arbitrary interpretation.

As is known,

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x) - f(x-h)}{h}.$$

The starting point for Marat’s thinking was the formula

$$f^{(2)}(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x) - 2f(x-h) + f(x-2h)}{h^2}.$$

It is not difficult to show (by induction) that

$$f^{(n)}(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} h^{-n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k C_n^k f(x-kh).$$

Let us recall («Kvant», 1986, No. 1, p. 23) that $C_n^k$ is the number of combinations of $n$ elements.

The question of generalizing the concept of the $s$-th arbitrary to arbitrary values ​​of $s$ has long been studied by many outstanding mathematicians, including Leibniz, Johann Bernoulli, Euler, Fourier, Liouville and Riemann.

Definition (4) given by M. Rovinsky coincided with one of the ways that Liouville defined fractional arbitrary in the 30s of the 19th century. Formula (5) is known as the Riemann-Liouville formula.

Common fixed point of two functions

In the book of the famous user S. Ulam “Root Mathematical Problems” (M.: “Nauka”, 1964) there is the following hypothesis.

Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be continuous functions defined on the interval $[0; 1]$, taking values ​​adjacent to this interval and also that $f(g(x))=g(f(x))$ for all $x \in [0; 1]$; then the functions $f$ and $g$ have a common fixed point, that is, the point $x_0$ for which $f(x_0)=g(x_0)=x_0$.

This problem was very popular among mathematics students in the mid-60s and early 70s, but all attempts to solve it were unsuccessful. Telegr knows that Ulam’s hypothesis is not correct: there is a countermeasure.

A 9th grade student at Physics and Mathematics School boarding school No. 18 at Moscow State University, Andrei Slepukhin, on the recommendation of his teacher, candidate of physical and mathematical sciences A.P. Veselov (by the way, a graduate of Physics and Mathematics School at Moscow State University), took up the Ulam hypothesis in the class of polynomials. More precisely, he was solving the following problem.

Let $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ be two polynomials such that $p(q(x))=q(p(x))$ for all $x \in R$. Is it true that there is a point $x_0 \in R$ such that $p(x_0)=q(x_0)=x_0$, if it is known that the polynomials $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ themselves have fixed points (this, for example, means that the equations $p(x)=x$ and $q(x)=x$ have roots).

It turns out that if one of the polynomials $p(x)$ or $q(x)$ has an even degree, then a common fixed point exists.

For polynomials of odd degree, A. Slepukhin’s proof “does not work,” so for them the question remained open.
