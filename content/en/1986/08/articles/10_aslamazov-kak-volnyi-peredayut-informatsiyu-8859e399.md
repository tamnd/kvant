How do waves transmit information?

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. G. ASLAMAZOV

We are so accustomed to seeing events happening on the other side of the world on the TV screen that we are not even surprised by this. In the modern world, radio, television, and telephone make it quite easy to receive and transmit the necessary information. But until relatively recently everything was completely different...

In order to convey to St. Petersburg what was known about the coronation of the Empire of Elizabeth, which took place in Moscow, a whole group of soldiers with flags was deployed along the entire route between these cities. At the moment of coronation, the first soldier waved the flag, then the next, etc. Thus, the knowledge of the coronation reached St. Petersburg, where the cannons came out. This is how complex a method was used to quickly transfer information in a time-sensitive manner*).

*) Using this example in the article by L. G. Aslamazov and I. K. Kikon “What is a wave?” (*Kvant*, 1982, No. 6) the concept of a wave was discussed. Here we will continue the conversation we started and discuss the problem of information transfer.

What was spreading along the chain of soldiers? Each soldier remained standing in place, but at some point in time he changed his state (raised a flag). This change in state was propagated along the chain. In such cases, a wave is said to propagate.

Waves are different. In actic (sound) waves, the density of matter changes, in electromagnetic (light, radio waves, etc.) - by the direction of the electric and magnetic fields. There are terper waves, waves of concentration during chemical reactions, waves of epidemics, etc. Figuratively speaking, the waves cover the whole variety of phenomena in nature.

The simplest type of wave is a monochromatic wave, when in each place a change in state occurs over time according to a harmonic law with a certain frequency (according to the law of sine or cosine). Many of the waves found in nature can be considered monochromatic. These types of sound waves are called musical tones. They arise, for example, with the help of chamberts. Monochromatic light waves are produced using lasers. Waves close to monochromatic can be excited on the surface of water by periodically immersing an object into it. In our chain of soldiers you can also get similar waves.

Imagine that each soldier not only waved a flag, but oscillated it, periodically raising and lowering the flag. Each subsequent soldier repeats these oscillations, but with some delay (phase shift). A wave will run along the chain of soldiers. Something similar can be seen during sports festivals, when chains of people make periodic movements with a shift in phase.

The nature of the signal can be different - there are sound signals, light signals, etc.

The most interesting thing is that any signal can be represented as a sum of monochromatic waves with different frequencies (composed of such waves). This opportunity is given to us by the principle of interference: when waves propagate, oscillations in each place in space add up. Let's add, depending on the phase shift, oscillations of identical frequencies can strengthen each other (with a zero phase difference, the result is oscillations with a total amplitude - figure 1, or they can weaken (figure 1, b). It turns out that the amplitudes and frequencies of added monochromatic waves can be selected in such a way that the waves cancel each other out in almost the entire space, except for the opisthoses in place, and move at the speed of the wave, in other words, they spread signal. If the speed c of monochromatic waves of all frequencies is the same (as, for example, when electromagnetic waves propagate in vacuum), then the maximum moves with speed c. Its width is equal to $\Delta L = 2\pi c / \Delta \omega$. So the duration of the signal is $\Delta t = 2\pi / \Lambda \omega$.

This results in an amazingly simple fundamental relationship:
$$\Delta \omega \cdot \Delta t = 2\pi.$$
