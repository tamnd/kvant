Sixty-atomic carbon

About a year ago, a report appeared in scientific journals that chemists had discovered a molecule with an unusual geometry, “made” of sixty carbon atoms. The chemical formula of such a molecule is C₆₀. All sixty of its atoms are located at the vertices of a truncated icasadr - a polyhedron with 60 vertices, 32 edges and 90 edges.

What has been said apparently requires comment. First of all, you need to live what it means to “discover... a molecule.” The fact is that the C₆₀ molecule was not synthesized, just as, for example, the dodecadral molecule C₂0H₂0 was synthesized several years ago (see «Kvant», 1983, No. 9, p. 21), but rather discovered. When carbon was evaporated (by heating it) into a flow of inert gel, the formation of compact and fairly stable formations (clusters) of a large number of carbon atoms was discovered. Unexpectedly, it turned out that the classer consisting of 60 atoms is formed the most - in any case, 5-6 times more than classers consisting of 59 or 61 atoms.

It is possible that 60-atom molecules owe their existence to... geometry. And note that the more synthesized a molecule is, the more different it is (the greater its binding energy). This is why dodecadron molecules are stable and molecules of 60 carbon atoms are newly discovered, while classers of 59 or 61 atoms do not last long and quickly fall apart.

How is the new molecule “structured”? The truncated icasadr is probably almost familiar, for example, to an “official” soccer ball, sewn from 20 hexagons (usually light) and 12 pentagons (usually dark). Look at this ball carefully. His connection with the Ikasadr is not immediately visible - he burned too much! However, the connection is also very direct.

Imagine an icasadr - a polyhedron with 20 triangular edges and 12 vertices, in which the edges converge along the path. Let's cut a pyramid from each vertex so that a pentagon is formed in place of the vertex. The remaining parts of the faces together have 3 sides and become 6. If you form it “correctly”, then the hexagons will be regular and there will be 20 of them - the same number as there were faces and icasadras. The result is a polyhedron with 12+20=32 edges. It has 60 vertices, since flat faces have 5×12+6×20=180 vertices, and at each vertex of a polyhedron 3 faces are similar (180:3=60). The number of ribs can be counted in different ways. For example, like this. At each vertex 3 edges converge, so the ends of the edges will be 3x60=180, and the ends of the edges will be 180:2=90. Thus, the ikasadr “turns” into a truncated one.

And now the task. Carbon is conditionally 4-valent, and only 3 edges extend from each vertex of the truncated icasadr. This means that one bond must be double. In the structures of chemical formulas, a double bond is conventionally denoted by a double line. How should the double lines be positioned so that one and only one double line extends from each vertex of the truncated icasadr? Estimate how many ways this can be done. (Note that this task is important for determining the degree of stability of molecules - chemists know that the more ways to make double bonds, the more stable the molecule.)

It is interesting that the newly discovered molecule can be used as a “cage”, inside which some atom can be “locked”. In this case, the atom will be connected to the “cell” as a prisoner, but not “chained” to it by a chemical bond. This is how we managed to “lock” the lanthanum atoms. The “cage” is quite spacious - the size of C₆0 molecules is approximately 7·10⁻¹⁰ m, which is 14 times larger than the size of a hydrogen atom.

There is a hypothesis that the C₆0 molecule may participate in the formation of the organization of molecules in space.

Recently, it was suggested that there might be another non-teachable molecule, C₁20, whose “geometry” is even closer to a sphere. The atoms of such a molecule must be located at the vertices of a polyhedron having 62 faces, 180 edges and 120 vertices. It is not yet clear how realistic the existence of such an exotic compound is.
