The friction mechanism is much more complex. Due to the unevenness of the surfaces, they meet only at individual points on the tops of the protrusions. Here, the molecules of the colliding bodies converge at distances commensurate with the distance between the molecules in the bodies themselves, and “stick together”; a strong connection is formed. As the body moves, connections are constantly created and destroyed. In this case, molecular vibrations occur. Part of the energy of the moving body is spent on these vibrations. Eventually it turns into tello.

The actual contact area is a total of one to two thousand square microns. It practically does not depend on the size of the body and is determined by the natural surface, its treatment, temperature and the force of normal pressure. If you press on the body, the protrusions are crushed, and the area of ​​actual contact increases. The friction force also increases.

With significant surface roughness, mechanical substitution between the “hills” begins to play a major role in increasing the friction force. When they move, they become crushed, and at the same time molecular vibrations occur.

The experience with polished glass plates is now clear. While the surfaces were “rough”, the contact area was not large, but after good polishing it increased. Another example can be given of an increase in friction with “improvement” of the surface. If you take two metal bars with clean polished surfaces, they will fit together. Friction here becomes large, since the actual contact area is large. The forces of molecular cohesion, which are responsible for friction, turn two bars into a monolith!

You can do the following experiment at home. Place the glass on a glass plate and tie a string to the stem, potash the glass. If you look closely at the surface, you will even notice scratches. It turns out that tearing out pieces of glass is easier than breaking contact!

The friction model we considered is quite crude. We did not dwell here on the diffusion of molecules, that is, on the penetration of molecules of one body into another, on the role of electric charges on contacting surfaces, on the role and mechanism of action of a lubricant. These questions are largely unclear, and explanations are controversial. One can only be surprised that with such complexity trenne is described only by a simple law: $F=kN$. And although the friction coefficient $k$ is not very constant and varies somewhat from one point on the surface to another, for many surfaces that we often encounter in technology, it is possible to make fairly good estimates of the expected friction force.

Dry trenne has one essential feature: trenne of peace. If friction or gas friction occurs only when the body moves and the body can be moved by applying even a very small brute force to it, then with dry friction the body begins to move only when the projection of the force $F$ applied to it onto the plane tangent to the surface on which the body lies becomes greater than a certain value. Until the body begins to slide, the friction force acting on it is equal to the tangential component of the applied force and is directed in the opposite direction. As the applied force increases, the friction force also increases until it reaches a maximum value equal to $kN$, at which sliding begins

This is often forgotten when solving problems. To the question “what friction force acts on a table weighing 300 N standing on the floor if the coefficient of friction is 0.4”, the larger one answers: “120 N”, which is incorrect. The friction force is zero, otherwise the table would move in the direction of the friction force, since there are no other horizontal forces.

So, if the body ends up, then in order to move it from its place, a force must be applied to the body that is greater than the maximum possible force of static friction, which is determined by the strength of the molecular bonds. But what happens if the body is already moving? What force must be applied so that the body begins to move in another direction? It turns out how much the angle is small. This is due precisely to the fact that the friction force cannot be greater than the maximum static friction force.

Try a simple experiment. Take a book and place it with one edge on another thicker book. The result is an inclined plane. Now place a matchbox with a thread attached to it on this plane. If the box is slippery, then reduce the inclination of the plane by taking a thicker book stand. Pull the string of selection boxes. At the same time, it is also pushed down! Reduce the inclination of the plane and pull the thread again. Same picture. The box slips even at very small angles of inclination of the plane. For some reason, the frictional force that holds the boxes on the plane became very small.

Let's try to understand what's going on here. If the box moved only horizontally, then parallel to the edge of the inclined plane a frictional force equal to $kN$ would act on it. In order for the box not to slide down, a frictional force must act upward on it, equal in magnitude to the projection of the gravitational force on the inclined plane. The separating moving force of these two friction forces is greater than $kN$, but this cannot be. This means that the box must slide off the inclined plane.

Let's take a block, attach a thread to it and, placing the block on a horizontal plane, we will pull the thread at a constant speed $\vec{v}_1$. By applying a force perpedicular to $\vec{v}_1$ to the block, it can also be made to move in this direction with a constant speed $\vec{v}_2$. The friction force will be equal to $kN$ and directed opposite to the speed $\vec{v}$ of movement.

The phenomenon in question is not at all rare. For example, it is known that when the electric motor brakes sharply, the transmission belt often slips off the pulleys. This happens later that when the engine brakes, the belt begins to slip relative to the pulleys, and a small force is enough to slip the belt sideways. Since there is usually a slight distortion in the installation of the pulleys and belt, this force is a component of the belt tension force. Here are more examples. Let us now dwell on the last Amonton-Coulomb law: the friction force does not depend on the speed of the body. This is not entirely true.

The question of the dependence of friction force on speed is of very practical importance. And although the experiments here have many specific difficulties, they end with the use of the information obtained, for example, in the theory of cutting metals, in calculating the movement of bullets and shells in the barrel, etc.

Obviously they believe that in order to move a body from its place, a greater force must be applied to it than in order to drag the body. In most cases, this is due to contamination of the surfaces of corpses. For example, for pure metals such a jump in the friction force is not observed. Experiments with the movement of bullets in a barrel have shown that with increasing bullet speed, the magnitude of the friction force first quickly decreases, then it decreases more and more slowly, and at speeds greater than 100 m/s, it begins to increase.

Roughly this can be explained by the fact that a lot of tella is released at the point of contact. At a speed of about 100 m/s, the temperature at the point of contact can reach several thousand degrees, and a layer of molten metal forms between the surfaces. Friction becomes liquid. At high speeds, fluid friction is proportional to the square of the speed.

It is interesting that the friction force of the bow on the string has approximately the same dependence on speed. That is why we can listen to playing on bowed instruments - violin, cello, viola.
