α is the angle of inclination of the plane to the horizon). Pozotomu

$$P \sin \alpha = k P \cos \alpha \frac{v_2}{\sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}},$$

where

$$v_2 = v_1 \frac{tg \alpha}{\sqrt{k^2 - tg^2 \alpha}}.$$

(This is true, of course, only for tg α < k, since at larger angles of inclination of the plane to the horizon, the boxes are no longer held on the plane by the force of friction.)

At small angles of inclination of the plane to the horizon (such that tan α << k)

$$v_2 = v_1 \frac{tg \alpha}{k},$$

that is, the speed of the sliding box is proportional to the speed of its movement parallel to the edge of the inclined plane and the tangent of the angle of inclination of the plane to the horizon.

This conclusion can be easily verified experimentally. Since during uniform motion the path traversed by the body is proportional to the speed, the ratio of the speed v₂ to v₁ will be equal to the ratio of the segments that the boxes will travel in these directions.

The phenomenon in question is not at all rare. For example, it is known that when the electric motor brakes sharply, the transmission belt often slips off the pulleys. This happens later that when the engine brakes, the belt begins to slip relative to the pulleys, and a small force is enough to slip the belt sideways. Since there is usually a slight distortion in the installation of the pulleys and belt, this force is a component of the belt tension force. Here are more examples. When they want to pull a nail out of the wall without placing kleshes, they drag it, simultaneously turning it around its axis. For the same reason, when braking sharply, the car automatically loses control; the car skids. The wheels slip along the road, and the lateral force arises due to the unevenness of the road.

Let us now dwell on the last Amonton-Coulomb law: the friction force does not depend on the speed of the body. This is not entirely true.

The question of the dependence of friction force on speed is of very practical importance. And although the experiments here have many specific difficulties, they end with the use of the information obtained, for example, in the theory of cutting metals, in calculating the movement of bullets and shells in the barrel, etc.

Obviously they believe that in order to move a body from its place, a greater force must be applied to it than in order to drag the body. In most cases, this is due to contamination of the surfaces of corpses. For example, for pure metals such a jump in the friction force is not observed. Experiments with the movement of bullets in a barrel have shown that with increasing bullet speed, the magnitude of the friction force first quickly decreases, then it decreases more and more slowly, and at speeds greater than 100 m/s, it begins to increase.

Roughly this can be explained by the fact that a lot of tella is released at the point of contact. At a speed of about 100 m/s, the temperature at the point of contact can reach several thousand degrees, and a layer of molten metal forms between the surfaces. Friction becomes liquid. At high speeds, fluid friction is proportional to the square of the speed.

It is interesting that the friction force of the bow on the string has approximately the same dependence on speed. That is why we can listen to playing on bowed instruments - violin, cello, viola.
