About mathematical creativity of SCHOOLCHILDREN

Our magazine rarely contains articles on mathematics written by schoolchildren. It's not because high school students aren't capable of producing interesting and original math work. The point here is that most of these works are narrowly specific, often rely on extra-grammatic material, and rarely contain general mathematical ideas useful for the majority of our readers. In addition, the text of the note in «Kvant» must not only be mathematically correct and formally correct, but be written in an understandable and clear, entertaining and methodological manner. If our experienced authors do not always cope with such a requirement, then what can we expect from scientific authors!

And yet, in this anniversary, two hundredth issue of the magazine, we decided to publish a retelling of several works by schoolchildren (sent to «Kvant» or told at various conferences of schoolchildren), which, it seems to us, should be of interest to many of our readers. These works also contain completed tasks for those who want to test their creative powers.

Equally self-intersecting polygon

In the work of Andrei Shann (Moscow, school No. 57), written in 1984, when Andrei was in high school, geometric problems about broken lines with an equal number of self-intersection points on each link are solved. Let us give a precise definition of the broken lines under consideration: broken lines of titanium (n, m), where n≥3, m≥1, are sequences of n different points A₁, A₂, ..., Aₙ (vertices) and n consecutively connecting these points of segments A₁A₂, A₂A₃, ..., Aₙ-1Aₙ, AₙA₁ (links) such that each link intersects exactly m other links, moreover, at m different internal points. For example, figure 1 shows the broken titas (6, 1), (5, 2) and (9, 2).

Problem 1. For what n does the titanium broken line (n, 1) exist?

ANSWER: for all four n (n≥6); to solve A. Shann presents the titanium polyline (6, 1), and then applies to it the operation of inserting a cross (Fig. 2, a) or inserting a harmonic (Fig. 2, b).

For odd n there are no broken lines (n, 1). A more general fact is true: if both numbers n and m are odd, then the titanium broken line (n, m) does not exist. In fact, the lines of the titanium line (n, m) intersect only in pairs, so the total number of intersection points is nm/2, which is impossible when both numbers n and m are odd.

Problem 2. Is it true that for any m there exists (for some n) a broken line (n, m)?

ANSWER: yes; A. Shann solves this problem, starting with the same example of the titanium polyline (6,1), and then applying the tracing operation to it (Fig. 2 b, 2).

Problem 3. For what pairs of numbers (n, m) is there a broken line (n, m)?

This problem has not been completely solved, although A. Schanne has made great progress in solving it, using different versions of the sum operation (Fig. 3). Andrey’s results can be summarized in the following table:
