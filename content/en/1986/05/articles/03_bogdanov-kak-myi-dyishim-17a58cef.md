How do we breathe?

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
K. Y. BOGDANOV

What does the electrocardiogram tell you? How do bats catch midges in complete darkness? How do homing pigeons find their way home? Answers to these and many other questions are given by biophysics - a science that studies physical phenomena in living organisms, lying at the intersection of physics and biology, using the latest achievements of physics in biological research. Let’s try, armed with our school knowledge of physics, to answer a seemingly simple question: how do we breathe?*)

Our respiratory system

During normal breathing, our body consumes about 0.5 kg of oxygen per day and releases almost the same amount of carbon dioxide. Both the entry of oxygen and the exit of carbon dioxide occurs through the lungs.

The internal space of the lungs communicates with the atmosphere through airways. The airways consist of the nasal cavity, where the exhaled air is heated and moistened, the pharynx, larynx, trachea, and two main bronchi that supply air to the right and left lungs. Each bronchus expands into smaller bronchi (bronchioles) and ends with micropulmonary vesicles - alveoli, surrounded on all sides by a network of blood vessels. Alveoli, which number about 700 million in an adult, are air-filled bubbles connected to each other (Fig. 1). In 1929, Swiss scientist Carl von Ningrard discovered that the pressure required for lung development could be significantly reduced by filling the lungs with a physiological solution. This has nothing to do with the idea of ​​the alveoli as hollow elastic balls: if, when inhaling, we had to overcome only elastic forces, then our efforts would not depend on whether the lung was filled with solution or not - after all, elastic forces do not depend on this. The data obtained by Ningrard became clear when it turned out that each alveolus was covered from the inside with a thin layer of liquid. This circumstance significantly affects the mechanical properties of the alveoli. In particular, the pressure required for the development of the alveoli is greater than in the case of a hollow ball of lung tissue. And here's why.

It is known that the surface of a liquid behaves like a stretched film - it has surface tension. To evaluate the role of surface tension forces in the mechanics of the alveoli, consider a film of liquid in the shape of a sphere (the simplest example of such a film is a soap bubble).*) Due to the surface tension of the liquid, the air pressure inside the bubble is always greater than the external pressure. The magnitude of excess pressure inside a sphere of radius $R$ is determined by the Laplace correspondence $\Delta p=2\sigma/R$, where $\sigma$ is the coefficient of surface tension of the film. This excess pressure is greater, the smaller the radius of the sphere. In a soap bubble film, both the inner and outer surfaces are in contact with an impact $\sigma$ of the film equal to twice the surface tension coefficient of the liquid. In the alveoli, air-liquid contact occurs only on one side, from the inside.

How do we still breathe?

When considering the interaction between neighboring alveoli, we assumed in advance that the surface tension is the same for different alveoli and does not depend on the state in which they are - inflated or collapsed. In pure liquids, the actual surface tension does not depend on the size of the surface. But for liquids containing various impurities, σ depends on the size of the surface along which they border the gas.

Once again, Nature has provided for everything. The substance contained as an impurity in the liquid that wets the surface of the alveoli from the inside has such a regular surface tension value that σ is minimal at the beginning of inspiration (minimal surface) and maximum at the end (maximal surface). Therefore, despite the fact that the radius of the alveoli at the beginning of inhalation is very small, the inclusion structure is very close to general soap and, when dissolved, forms a thin film on the surface of the water. If the concentration of the impurity is high, so that the film can cover the entire surface of the water with a continuous layer, then σ of such a liquid is equal to the value determined for a given impurity. When the concentration of the impurity is not sufficient to cover the entire surface with a film, the surface tension of the liquid will be between the corresponding values ​​of σ for water and the impurity. In these cases, the surface of the liquid leads to a decrease in the surface concentration of the impurity and causes an increase in σ, bringing its value closer to σ for water. As the surface of the liquid decreases, σ will decrease.

Where do substances come from in the lungs that reduce surface tension and thereby make our breathing easier? Apparently they are synthesized by special cells located in the walls of the alveoli. The synthesis of these substances, called surfactants, occurs throughout a person’s life - from birth to death. Curve 2 at figure 4 corresponds to lung fluid with a reduced surfactant content. It can be seen that the minimum value of σ is almost eight times increased compared to the norm. In those rare cases when a newborn does not have cells in the lungs that produce surfactants, the child cannot independently take the first breath. Unfortunately, many newborns around the world still die without taking their first breath, due to the lack or absence of surfactants in their alveoli.

Exception to the Rule

However, many animals that breathe through their lungs do not suffer at all from the fact that there are no surfactants in their alveoli. First of all, this applies to cold-blooded animals - frogs, lizards, snakes, crocodiles, etc. Since these animals do not need to waste energy on heating their own body, their oxygen needs are on average ten times less than those of warm-blooded animals. Therefore, the surface of the lungs, through which gas exchange occurs between the blood and the air, is smaller in cold-blooded animals than in warm-blooded animals. The relative decrease in the surface area of ​​the lung per unit volume in cold-blooded animals is associated with an approximately tenfold increase in the diameter of their alveoli compared with the alveoli of warm-blooded animals. The relatively larger radius of the alveoli makes it easy to inflate them even in the absence of a surfactant on their inner surface (after all, Δp~1/R).

The second group of animals whose lungs lack surfactants are birds. Birds are warm-blooded and lead a rather lively lifestyle. The energy expenditure of birds and mammals of the same weight is close to each other. The birds' oxygen needs are also high. Moreover, the lungs of birds have a unique ability to saturate the blood with oxygen when flying at a higher altitude (about 6000 m), where its concentration in the air drops by half compared to the concentration at sea level. Any mammal (including humans) at such an altitude begins to experience a lack of oxygen, sharply limits its dietary activity, and sometimes even falls into a faint state. How does a bird’s lungs, which do not have surfactants, manage to breathe and saturate the blood with oxygen? Why doesn't this work in mammals?

Let's engage in self-criticism. What's wrong with the lungs? Firstly, not all of the visible air participates in gas exchange with the roof: the air that ends up in the trachea and bronchi at the end of inspiration will not be able to give oxygen to the roof and take carbon dioxide from there, since there are practically no blood vessels in these places of the lung. The part of the lung volume occupied by the trachea and bronchi is usually called “dead space”. The total “dead space” of the human lungs has a volume of about 150 cm³. You can artificially increase the amount of “dead space” by forcing yourself to breathe through a long tube. Anyone who tries to do this has probably noticed that it is necessary to increase the depth of breathing (the volume of air inhaled). Obviously, if the volume of “dead space” is made equal to the maximum possible inhalation (about 4.5 dm³), then the person begins to suffocate, since fresh air will stop flowing into the alveoli altogether. Thus, the existence of “dead space” in the respiratory system of mammals is a “mistake” of Nature.

In creating the lungs of mammals, Nature, in our opinion, made a second “mistake.” This disadvantage is due to the fact that the movement of air in the lungs changes its direction during the transition from inhalation to inhalation. There are some practical safety features almost half of the time - during inhalation, fresh air does not enter the alveoli.

But with birds, Nature once again reaches perfection. In addition to the normal lungs, birds have an additional system consisting of a spot and more pairs of air sacs associated with the lungs (Fig. 5). The cavities of these bags branch widely in the body and extend into neonate bones, sometimes even into the small bones of the phalanges of the fingers. As a result, the Utica's respiratory system occupies about 20% of the body's volume (2% - lungs and 18% - air sacs), while in humans - only 5%. The lung of a bird, in contrast to the lung of a mammal, is an improved parallel-connected thin tube open to the respiration, surrounded by blood vessels. The connection of the bags with the lungs is such that when air is inhaled through the bird's lung, the flow is in the same direction as when it exhales. During breathing in birds, only the volume of air in the sacs changes, while the volume of the lung remains practically constant. And if there is a need to inflate the lung, there is no need for surfactants in bird lungs.

That's how many interesting things can be said in response to a seemingly simple question - how we breathe.
