Auxiliary cube

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
M. R. LIVERSON

When solving stereometry problems, difficulties often arise at the very first step. You need to have a well-developed geometric imagination to imagine the corresponding space picture. If we are talking about geometric bodies that we encounter in everyday life (cube, ball, cylinder, parallelepid, etc.), then these objects are easy to imagine. You know how to draw them. It is much more difficult to depict, for example, intersecting lines or a set of lines and planes in space. And not just depict, but make a drawing that helps solve the problem.

There is a method that allows you to present these difficulties in some cases. It is based on the following consideration. If the spatial configuration is difficult to perceive and is not associated with a specific geometric body, then we will try to artificially associate it with some body. For example, with a cube.

The cube depicted on paper makes the work of our imagination easier. On figure 1, for example, it is not true that the lines $l$ and $m$ are skew. We removed the cube (Fig. 2), and the spatial clarity disappeared - we see the intersecting lines $l$ and $m$ on the plane.

Another example. When rays $l$, $m$ and $n$ emanating from one point $A_1$, for example, intersecting lines or a set of lines and planes in space. On figure 4 there is no cube, so the picture became flat. To see the same triangular angle on it, we must force our imagination to work.

 The use of an auxiliary cube not only “transforms” a flat picture into a three-dimensional one, but also indicates ways to solve the problem. Pro-

Problem 1. The plane angles of a trihedral angle are 45°, 45° and 60°. A straight line is drawn through its vertex, perpendicular to one of the faces, the plane angle of which is 45°. Find the angle between this straight line and an edge of a trihedral angle that does not lie in the mentioned face.

Solution. Consider the auxiliary cube $ABCDA_1B_1C_1D_1$ (Fig. 5). Let us draw the diagonals $AB_1$ and $AC$ of its two adjacent faces. We have $B_1\overset{\frown}{AB} = 45^\circ$, $B\overset{\frown}{AC} = 45^\circ$, $B_1\overset{\frown}{AC} = 60^\circ$ as the angle of the regular triangle $AB_1C$. Therefore, the trihedral angle $AB_1BC$ is the angle referred to in the problem statement. Edge $AA_1$ passes through its vertex $A$ and is perpendicular to face $BAC$. This means that we need to find the angle $A_1AB_1$. The answer is very clear: $A_1\overset{\frown}{AB_1} = 45^\circ$.

Solving this problem without using a cube is quite difficult and takes a lot of time**). Here we didn’t have to count at all: we were able to “read” the answer on the cube. But such cases are rare.

Problem 2. The angle between two edges of a trihedral angle is a straight line, and the magnitude of each plane angle of these edges is $\alpha$. Find the values ​​of the flat angle of the third face.

Solution. First, we will assume that the angle $\alpha$ is acute. Let the vertex $A$ of the auxiliary cube $ABCDA_1B_1C_1D_1$ (Fig. 6) be the vertex of a trihedral angle, and the segments $AM, AB$ and $AN$ be its edges (if $\alpha > 45^\circ$, then the points $M$ and $N$ lie on the extensions of the edges $BB_1$ and $BC$). You need to find the angle $MAN$.

Let us drop a perpendicular $MP$ from the point $M$ onto the segment $AN$ and connect the points $B$ and $P$. By the theorem of three perpendiculars, the segment $BP$ is perpendicular to the segment $AN$. From right-angled triangles $APB$ and $ABM$ we obtain

$$|AP| = |AB| \cdot \cos \alpha, \quad |AM| = \frac{|AB|}{\cos \alpha}.$$

The required angle $M\widehat{AN}$ is the acute angle of the right triangle $APM$; That's why

$$\cos M\widehat{AN} = \frac{|AP|}{|AM|} = \frac{|AB| \cdot \cos \alpha \cdot \cos \alpha}{|AB|} = \cos^2 \alpha,$$

$$M\widehat{AN} = \arccos(\cos^2 \alpha).$$

If the angle is $\alpha = 90^\circ$, then the desired angle is right, which can also be written by the resulting correspondence.

So, the answer in all cases is the same: $\arccos(\cos^2 \alpha)$.

We see that it is convenient to depict triangular angles on a cube, two of whose faces have been redistributed. This is due to the fact that the cube contains two right angles. But the cube also contains a linear dimension—the length of its edge. This makes it possible to depict distances in the drawing.

Problem 3. The base $MN$ and vertex $K$ of the isosceles triangle $MNK$ are located on different faces of a right dihedral angle with edge $l$. Points $M$ and $K$ are distant from $l$ by a distance $a$, and the projection of point $N$ onto edge $l$ is equidistant from the projections of points $M$ and $K$ onto edge $l$. Find the distance from point $N$ to $l$ if $MK$ forms an angle with $l$ equal to $60^\circ$.

Solution. Let the length of the edge of the auxiliary cube $ABCDA_1B_1C_1B_1$ be equal to $a$ and let the two angles from the problem statement be formed by grams $AA_1B_1B$ and $ABCD$ (Fig. 7). Then the point $M$ lies on the edge $A_1B_1$, and the point $K$ lies on the edge $CD$ (or on their extensions). Let's align point $M$ with the vertex of the cube $A_1$. Let point $K$ be on edge $CD$ (it can be on the continuation of edge $CD$ beyond vertex $D$, which does not change our distances).

Let us drop the redistribution $KP$ onto the edge $AB$ and mark the midpoint $Q$ of the segment $AP$. In the plane of the face $AMB_1B$ we reconstruct from the point $Q$ the redistribution to the edge $AB$. The vertex $N$ of the triangle $MNK$ lies on this redistribution, since its projection $Q$ is equidistant from the projections of the points $M$ and $K$. Note that point $N$ is located above edge $MB_1$; otherwise triangle $MNK$ will not be equilateral.

Let's draw the segments $MD$ and $KQ$. $|MD| = a\sqrt{2}$ is like the diagonal of a square from side $a$. By condition, the angle between the crossing lines $AB$ and $MK$ is equal to $60^\circ$. But line $CD$ is parallel to line $AB$, hence $MKD = 60^\circ$. From the right triangle $MDK$ there is

$$|MK| = \frac{|MD|}{\sin 60^\circ} = \frac{2a\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}}, |DK| = \frac{a\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}}.$$

Under conditions $|NK| = |MK|$ and $|AP| = |DK|$. Then $|QP| = \frac{1}{2}|AP| = \frac{1}{2}|DK| = \frac{a}{\sqrt{6}}$. From the right triangle $QPK$ there is $|QK|^2 = |QP|^2 + |KP|^2 = 7a^2/6$. Finally, from the right triangle $NQK$ we get

$$|NQ| = \sqrt{|NK|^2 - |QK|^2} = \sqrt{|MK|^2 - |QK|^2} = \frac{1}{2}a\sqrt{6}.$$

Problem 5. The angle between two crossing lines is 60°. Point M lies on one line, and point N lies on another, and the distances from each of these points to the common perpendicular of the crossing lines are the same and equal to the distance between the lines. What values ​​can the acute angle between the common perpendicular and the straight line MN take?

Solution. Let one of the crossing lines pass through vertices A and D of the auxiliary cube ABCDA₁B₁C₁D₁. We draw the second straight line through vertex A₁ at an angle of 60° to edge A₁D₁ in the plane of face A₁B₁C₁D₁ (Fig. 9). Then the edge AA₁ is a common perpendicular to the given lines. The distance between the lines is equal to the length of the edge of the cube a, which means that points M and N are removed from the segment AA₁ by a distance a. Let's align point M with vertex D. There are two possible locations for point N.

1. Point N lies inside the face A₁B₁C₁D₁.

Let's draw the segments ND₁ and MN. Triangle A₁ND₁ is equilateral, therefore |ND₁|=a. The required angle between straight lines MN and AA₁ is equal to angle NMD₁, since straight line AA₁ is parallel to MD₁. In triangle ND₁M

$$|ND₁|=|MD₁|=a, MD₁N=90°.$$

This means NMD₁=45°.

2. Point N lies outside the face A₁B₁C₁D₁ (at figure 9 of point N₁).

Let's draw the segments N₁D₁ and MN₁. Triangle N₁A₁D₁ is equilateral with a vertex angle of 120° and sides equal to a. Therefore, |N₁D₁|=a√3. The required angle between straight lines AA₁ and MN₁ is equal to angle N₁MD₁. From the triangle N₁D₁M we find N₁MD₁=60°.

We see that the introduction of an auxiliary cube simplifies the solution. In the next problem, as in problem 1, thanks to the cube, we will find the desired angle without calculations.

Let us place the application $HPQR$ inside the cube so that the points $P, Q$ and $R$ coincide with the points $D, B$ and $C$, respectively (Fig. 10). Point $H$ coincides with the middle of edge $RC_1$.

The last point is $H$ with the middle $S$ of edge $PR$. Let's draw a diagonal $RA$ of the bottom face of the cube. The point $T$ of intersection of the diagonal $RA$ and $PQ$ is the midpoint of the segment $PQ$. It is required to find the angle and distance between the crossing lines $HS$ and $RT$.

Let's draw the diagonals $C_1P, B_1A$ and $B_1R$ of the lateral faces of the cube. It is obvious that the segment $HS$ is parallel to the segment $B_1A$. Therefore, the angle we are looking for is the angle $B_1AR$, which is equal to $60^\circ$ (triangle $B_1AR$ equilateral).

Note now that the plane of the triangle $B_1AR$ contains the segment $RT$ and is parallel to the segment $HS$. Therefore, the required distance is equal to the distance between the straight line $HS$ and the plane $B_1AR$.

Consider a regular tetrahedron with base $B_1AR$ and vertex $D_1$. Let $D_1O$ be the height of the tetrahedron. From the point $F$ of intersection of the segments $D_1R$ and $HS$ we lower the perpendicular $FE$ to the segment $RO$. The segment $FE$ is perpendicular to the plane $B_1AR$, or the segment $FE$ is parallel to the segment $D_1O$. This means that the required distance is $|FE|$. From the similarity of triangles $D_1OR$ and $FER$

$$\frac{|FE|}{|D_1O|} = \frac{|FR|}{|D_1R|} = \frac{1}{4}.$$

It remains to find the height of the tetrahedron $D_1O$. In a right triangle $D_1OR$, the hypoten is $|D_1R| = 2\sqrt{2}$, and the leg $RO$ is 2/3 of the height of the right triangle $B_1AR$ from the side $2\sqrt{2}$, that is, $|RO| = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3}$. Then

$$|D_1O| = \sqrt{|D_1R|^2 - |RO|^2} = \frac{4\sqrt{3}}{3}.$$

Finally,

$$|FE| = \frac{1}{4}|D_1O| = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}.$$
