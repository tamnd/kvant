Satellite paradox

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
Y. G. PAVLENKO

When artificial Earth satellites move in the upper layers of the atmosphere, they experience the braking effect of air. Before the launches of satellites, information about the density of the atmosphere at altitudes up to 200 km was obtained through flights of geophysical rockets. Later, an analysis of the satellite’s movement made it possible to clarify these data (they are shown in the “table” on the spread).

The drag force acting on the satellite in the upper layers of the atmosphere is due to stolnova

with air molecules. This force is directed opposite to the speed and can be written as
$$\vec{F}_c = -k\vec{v},$$
where the coefficient $k$ is positive and, generally speaking, itself depends on the speed. The larger the cross-sectional area of ​​the body $S$ and the density of the atmosphere $\rho$, the larger it is.

Let us compare the magnitude of the acceleration imparted to the satellite by the force of resistance and the force of attraction. Let us assume that a satellite of mass $m=100$ kg has the shape of a sphere with a cross-section of $S=1$ m$^2$ and moves in a circular orbit at an altitude of $h=160$ km with a speed of $v=8$ km/s. For approximate calculations, we can assume that $k=S/v$. In this case, the absciliton value of the acceleration is determined by the resistance force,
$$a_c = \frac{kv}{m} \approx \frac{S_0v^2}{m} \approx 6.4 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/c}^2.$$
The gravitational force $F = G \frac{Mm}{(R+h)^2}$ acting on the satellite from the Earth ($M=5976 \cdot 10^{21}$ kg is the mass of the Earth, $R=6371$ km is its average radius), imparts to it an acceleration directed towards the center of the Earth and equal to
$$a_n = G \frac{M}{(R+h)^2} \approx 9.33 \text{ m/c}^2.$$
As you can see, the “braking” acceleration is approximately one fifteenth part of the centripetal one. And yet, the influence of the atmosphere at an altitude of 160 km turns out to be so significant that, having completed one or two revolutions, the satellite begins to spin quickly.

The first Soviet satellite had the shape of a ball with a diameter of 58 cm and a mass of 83.6 kg. The launch vehicle was significantly larger in size. It would seem that since the rocket experiences high resistance (it has more $S$), after entering orbit it should lag behind the satellite. However, it was observed that the launch vehicle was significantly ahead of the satellite. Let's try to understand this “paradox”.

The motion of satellites is currently calculated on a computer based on Newton's laws. To solve this problem in the case where the resistance force depends on the speed of the body, school knowledge of mathematics is not enough.
