However, the author did not give this concept a clear physical interpretation, and therefore controversy erupted around it. The essence of the problem was formulated very clearly by A. Various solutions to this problem have been proposed. G. Electric field energy

As you know (“Physics 9”, § 55), in order to charge a capacitor, it is necessary to do some work. This work is required to overcome the forces of electrical attraction that arise during the separation of positive and negative charges. Due to perfect work, potential power plant energy is stored in the capacitor

$$W = \frac{1}{2} U Q = \frac{1}{2} \frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2} CU^2,$$

where $U$ is the potential difference between the plates, $C$ is the capacitance of the capacitor, $Q$ is the charge on its plates.

It is clear that in order to charge any solitary body, that is, to create in it an excess of charges of the same sign, a certain amount of work must also be done. In this case, it is carried out against the forces of electrical repulsion, which act between the same charges accumulating on the body. Let's calculate this work.

Let's consider a solitary conductor. Suppose we have already managed to concentrate the charge $q$ on it. The potential of the electric field created by this charge at infinity is considered equal to zero, and the potential of the self-conductor is denoted by $\varphi(q)$. Now we will transfer an additional small charge $\Delta q$ to the conductor from infinity. To do this you will need to do work

$$\Delta A = \varphi(q) \Delta q.$$

It is clear that the potential of the electric field is always proportional to the magnitude of the charge created by this field. In this case, the potential of the conductor is proportional to the charge on it:

$$\varphi(q) \sim q,$$ or $$\varphi(q) = \frac{1}{C} q.$$

The quantity $C$ included in the proportionality coefficient is called the capacitance of a single conductor. It depends on the shape, size of the conductor and the dielectric constant of the environment.

The work $\Delta A$ performed when the charge $\Delta q$ is transferred from infinity to the conductor is represented on this graph by a flat shaded column. The total work $A$ on charging a single conductor to a charge $Q$ is determined by the sum of the areas of all similar columns, that is, the area of ​​the figure under the graph of the single conductor $\varphi$ from $q$. But where exactly is energy stored? There are two equal answers to this question. The first is based on the fact that the stored energy is the energy of interaction of charges located on the probe. According to the second point of view, the carrier of stored energy is the electric field, so that the energy is distributed in the space surrounding the conductor. In the case of a flat capacitor, the electric field created by it is uniform and concentrated in the area between the plates. Then it is easy to show (Physics 9, § 55) that the energy density - the energy per unit volume - is determined by the expression

$$w = \frac{\varepsilon_0 E^2}{2},$$

where $E$ is the field voltage, $\varepsilon$ is the dielectric capacity of the medium inside the capacitor.

It turns out that the same formula can be used to calculate the total energy of the electric field in the general case when the field is inhomogeneous. So, for example, in the space around a charging body, the electric field voltage decreases with distance from

A beautiful example showing the density of this approach to calculating energy is the problem of finding the repulsive force between two halves of a charged conducting sphere located in a vacuum.

First of all, let us remember that the field inside a conducting sphere, as well as inside any conductor (“Physics 9”, § 44), is equal to zero. Outside the sphere, the field voltage is the same as that of an exact charge equal to the charge of the sphere and located at its center:

$$E(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2},$$

where $Q$ is the charge of the sphere, $r$ is the distance from its center to the point in question. $Y$ damage to a sphere with radius $R$, thus the field voltage turns out to be equal to

$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{R^2}.$$
