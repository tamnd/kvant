⟦folio 35⟧

M964. Prove that in the sequence $(a_n)$ of different natural numbers satisfying the condition $a_n < 100n$ there is a number whose decimal notation
a) the number 1 appears;
b) 1986 units occur in a row.

M965. Given six numbers $a_1, a_2, ..., a_6$. To calculate head-on the sum of their pairwise products $a_1a_2+a_1a_3+...+a_5a_6$, you need to spend 15 multiplications and 14 additions. Show how you can immediately find: the sum of these numbers, the sum of their products by twos, threes, fours and heels, spending only 15 additions and 14 multiplications.

a) It is enough to show that if in the decimal notation of various natural numbers $a_1, a_2, ..., a_n$, the digit 1 does not appear, then the number $a_n/n$ can be arbitrarily large (in particular, $a_n/n > 100$ for some $n$).

Note that among the first $9^k$ numbers of our sequence there is a $a_n$ whose notation consists of at least $k+1$ digits, that is, $a_n \geq 10^k$. In fact, the number of natural numbers written in no more than $k$ digits other than 1 is $9^k-1$ (each of the $k$ digits of such a number contains one of the 9 digits 0, 2, 3, ..., 9, and the number 0 is excluded; we mean that numbers with fewer than $k$ digits are padded with leading zeros to $k$-digits). Since $n \leq 9^k$, the inequality $a_n/n \geq (10/9)^k$ is valid, and its right-hand side grows without limit with increasing $k$.

6) In exactly the same way as in point a), it is proved that if the numbers in the notation are $a_1, a_2, ..., a_n$, the numbers $S_n^k$ are the sum of their products of $k$ pieces. Then we can successively find

$$S_2^1 = x_1 + x_2, \quad S_2^2 = x_1x_2,$$

then

$$S_3^1 = S_2^1 + x_3, \quad S_3^2 = S_2^2 + S_1^2 \cdot x_3, \quad S_3^3 = S_2^2x_3,$$

then $S_n^k (1 \leq k \leq 4), \quad S_5^k (1 \leq k \leq 5)$ and finally $S_6^k (1 \leq k \leq 5)$, use the formulas

$$S_n^1 = S_n^{1-1} + x_n, \quad S_n^k = S_n^{k-1} + S_n^{k-1}x_n$$

($1 < k < n$), $S_n^n = S_n^{n-1}x_n$.

To calculate all $S_n^k (1 \leq k \leq n)$ it will take $n-1$ additions and $n-1$ multiplications. So to do this down to $n=6$, you need $1+2+3+4+5=15$ additions and the same number of multiplications. The last multiplication $S_6^k = S_5^kx_6$ can be saved by counting the product of all 6 numbers.

This sequence of operations is illustrated by a diagram in the margin (blue arrows are additions, red arrows are multiplications).

Briefly this decision can be explained as follows. We need to find the coefficients of the polynomial $(x+a_1)(x+a_2)...(x+a_6)$ for $x, x^2, ..., x^5$, and we successively find the product of $x+a_1$ by $x+a_2$, then by $x+a_3$, ..., by $x+a_6$.

Similarly, one can prove that all sums of products of $n$ numbers by $k (k=1, 2, ..., n)$ can be found in $1+2+...+(n-1)=n(n-1)/2$ additions and the same number of multiplications. It will be interesting to figure out whether it is possible to do this with fewer operations.
