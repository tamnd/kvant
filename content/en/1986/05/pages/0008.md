However, equilibrium is not established instantly. Therefore, for example, at the beginning of “inhalation”, a rapid increase in the surface of the liquid is resisted by a sharp increase in σ, since the dissolution in the volume of impurities does not have time to reach the surface; equilibrium between impurity molecules occurs only at the end of the “inhalation”. Where do substances come from in the lungs that reduce surface tension and thereby make our breathing easier? Apparently they are synthesized by special cells located in the walls of the alveoli. The synthesis of these substances, called surfactants, occurs throughout a person’s life - from birth to death. Curve 2 at figure 4 corresponds to lung fluid with a reduced surfactant content. It can be seen that the minimum value of σ is almost eight times increased compared to the norm. In those rare cases when a newborn does not have cells in the lungs that produce surfactants, the child cannot independently take the first breath. Unfortunately, many newborns around the world still die without taking their first breath, due to the lack or absence of surfactants in their alveoli.

Exception to the Rule

However, many animals that breathe through their lungs do not suffer at all from the fact that there are no surfactants in their alveoli. First of all, this applies to cold-blooded animals - frogs, lizards, snakes, crocodiles, etc. Since these animals do not need to waste energy on heating their own body, their oxygen needs are on average ten times less than those of warm-blooded animals. Therefore, the surface of the lungs, through which gas exchange occurs between the blood and the air, is smaller in cold-blooded animals than in warm-blooded animals. The relative decrease in the surface area of ​​the lung per unit volume in cold-blooded animals is associated with an approximately tenfold increase in the diameter of their alveoli compared with the alveoli of warm-blooded animals. The relatively larger radius of the alveoli makes it easy to inflate them even in the absence of a surfactant on their inner surface (after all, Δp~1/R).

The second group of animals whose lungs lack surfactants are birds. Birds are warm-blooded and lead a rather lively lifestyle. The energy expenditure of birds and mammals of the same weight is close to each other. The birds' oxygen needs are also high. Moreover, the lungs of birds have a unique ability to saturate the blood with oxygen when flying at a higher altitude (about 6000 m), where its concentration in the air drops by half compared to the concentration at sea level. Any mammal (including humans) at such an altitude begins to experience a lack of oxygen, sharply limits its dietary activity, and sometimes even falls into a faint state. How does a bird’s lungs, which do not have surfactants, manage to breathe and saturate the blood with oxygen? Why doesn't this work in mammals?
