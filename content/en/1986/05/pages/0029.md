⟦folio 27⟧

Ferdinand achieved great skill in the manufacture of various thermoscopes. Ferdinand sent one of his thermoscopes from Germany to Kircher, who was engaged in part-time scientific research. The device consists of a glass tube open at the top, almost completely filled with water (see figure). In the water there are several tiny pear-shaped vessels with necks pulled down. Each vessel contained an air bubble, the size of which was selected in such a way that at a certain temperature the vessels floated in water inside the tube. At a higher temperature, the air in the containers expanded, partially displacing the water; as a result of this, the buoyancy force increased, and the vessels rose to the surface of the water. From the description made by Ferdinand, it can be judged that the temperature at which the vessels “hung” inside the tube was about 15 °C. When the ambient air temperature dropped below 15 °C, the air inside the vessels decreased, the amount of water in them increased, and they sank to the bottom.

At the same time as this device and a detailed description of its operation, Ferdinand sent Kircher another thermoscope. It was very similar to the first one and differed only in that it was completely filled with water and sealed. In this device, however, on the contrary, the vessels sank when heated and rose when cooled. Ferdinand left Kircher to solve the riddle of the second thermoscope himself. We do not know whether Kircher coped with this task. But we hope that you, the reader, will do it without much difficulty. (You can check the correctness of your answer by looking at p. 58).

Ferdinand also built thermometers of a wide variety of types and designs. It is curious that he did a lot to “introduce” scientific discoveries into “production.” One of Ferdinad's projects was the large-scale breeding of chickens. He built one of the first incubators, where he used his thermometer to control the temperature while artificially incubating eggs.

However, even then the introduced achievement of science was not an easy task: out of 150 eggs, Ferdinand received only three chickens. Why did this happen? Perhaps due to the insufficient distance of the worker, who did not take the thermometer readings seriously and trusted more in his own feelings, or perhaps due to Ferdinad’s poor knowledge of biology. Now we can only guess why this experiment ended in failure.

With great success, Ferdinand used a thermometer to observe the weather, he carried out important meteorological measurements, and compiled materials from observing the temperature in deep wells and dungeons at different times of the year. It turned out that at depth the season occurs a little later than on the surface - just in time so that the earth has time to dry out or, conversely, cool down.

We will finish our little calculation about thermometers and thermoscopes with a question to the reader: why is a water thermometer inconvenient for measuring temperatures close to the freezing point of water?
