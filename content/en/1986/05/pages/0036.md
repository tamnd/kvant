$$S_{XAC} = S_{XAY} + S_{XCY} = \frac{1}{2} XY \cdot h,$$

where the letter S, as usual, denotes areas. Exactly the same

$$S_{XBD} = \frac{1}{2} XZ \cdot h.$$

Therefore, triangles XAC and XBD are equal in size if and only if X is the midpoint of the segment YZ. But the set of midpoints of all possible segments parallel to AB, with ends on lines AC and BD, is very large, and there are lines connecting segments AB and CD (and, by the way, passing through P).

Note that $S_{XAC} = S_{XBD}$ and for points X on a line parallel to AB and passing through P; for points not lying on the two lines indicated, these areas are different.

Let's move on to proving the statement of the problem. Let $A_1A_2...A_6$ be the given hexagon, $M_1, M_2, ..., M_6$ the midpoints of its sides, P, Q, R the intersection points of the (major) diagonals $A_1A_4$, $A_2A_5$, $A_3A_6$, O the intersection point of the “midlines” $M_1M_4$ and $M_2M_5$ (for notations, see figure 2). By the proven statement, $S_{OA_1A_4} = S_{OA_2A_5}$ (since O lies on $M_1M_4$) and $S_{OA_2A_5} = S_{OA_3A_6}$ (since O lies on $M_2M_5$), therefore $S_{OA_1A_4} = S_{OA_3A_6}$. Moreover, the point O belongs to the union of angles $A_1PA_2$ and $A_4PA_5$ and at the same time to the union of angles $A_2QA_3$ and $A_5QA_6$, and the intersection of these two pairs of angles is triangle PQR (Fig. 3), which is contained in one of the angles $A_3RA_4$ or $A_6RA_1$. Using our statement again, we conclude that the third “middle line” $M_3M_6$ also passes through O.

Let us mention three more solutions to this problem. The first of them is similar to what we presented and is based on the fact that the set of points X of the trapezoid ABCD with bases AB and CD, for which the triangles AXD and BXC are equal in length, is a segment connecting the bases (compare with the auxiliary statement proved above). In addition, we need to use one more fact, interesting in itself: if in the hexagon $A_1A_2...A_6$ the opposite sides are parallel, then the triangles $A_1A_3A_5$ and $A_2A_4A_6$ are equal in size. Another solution is based on specially selected parallel engineering. Namely, as can be shown, there is always such a projection at which the diagonal of a given hexagon becomes equal (it is enough to ensure that its two “midlines” become perpendicular to the sides that they connect). It is easy to see that in this case the “midlines” will be bisquetrices of a triangle bounded by large diagonals, and, therefore, intersect at one point. In this case, the hexagon turns into a circle with the center at the point of intersection of the “middle lines”. Finally, the statement of the problem can be deduced from the general condition for the intersection at one point of three straight lines drawn from the vertices of a triangle (in this case, the triangle PQR - see Fig. 2) - the so-called Ceva’s theorem (see, for example, problem 34 in the article by M. V. Balk and V. G. Boltyansky “The center of gravity of the solutions" in «Kvant» No. 4 for 1984 g.).
