the binding energy due to nuclear forces turns out to be proportional to the number of nucleons in the nucleus, that is, the mass number $A : E_\alpha \sim A$.

However, there are fewer nucleons on the surface of the “nuclear drop” nearby than inside the nucleus, so they make a slightly smaller contribution to the binding energy than we have already attributed to them. This can be taken into account by subtracting the surface energy $E_{\text{пов}}$ from $E_\alpha$, proportional to the number of nucleons on the surface of the nucleus, and therefore its surface area: $E_{\text{пов}} \sim S_{\text{пов}} \sim R^2_\alpha \sim A^{2/3}$ (here we took advantage of the fact that, as experience shows, the radii of nuclei are quite accurately proportional to the cube root of the mass number). As we see, here too there is a complete analogy with a drop of general liquid - the molecules located on the surface tend to go deeper and create surface tension forces, which are associated with the surface energy of the liquid.

To obtain the final expression for the binding energy of the nucleus, we need to take into account that some of the nucleons (protons) are charged. This means that, in addition to the nuclear attraction to their nearest neighbors, they also experience the action of the forces of general electrical repulsion, which obeys Coulomb’s law. The mutual repulsion of protons tends to break the nucleus apart and thus reduces its binding energy. Unlike nuclear forces, Coulomb forces have a long-range structure - each proton interacts with all protons of this nucleus. The interaction energy of two protons is $\sim e^2/R_\alpha$. If there are $Z$ protons in the nucleus, then each of them interacts with $(Z - 1)$ others and the interaction number.

So, the final result for the nuclear binding energy is $E_{\text{cb}} = E_\alpha - E_{\text{пов}} - E_{\text{эл}} = \alpha A - \beta A^{2/3} - \gamma Z^2/A^{1/3}$, where $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are some constants. This formula describes well the experimental curve of the dependence of the specific binding energy on the mass number (“Physics 10”, § 108). First, as $A$ increases, the binding energy increases. Somewhere in the middle part of the periodic table, $E_{\text{cb}}$ reaches a maximum and then begins to decrease.

Let us now consider the process of nuclear fission. Let the nuclear “drop”, having absorbed the neutron that got into it, begin to oscillate and at some point stretch out. A spherical drop has the minimum proton interaction at a given volume. In an elongated drop, the surface energy increases, and the binding energy of the nucleus corresponds to the average distance between nucleons, and the energy of their electrical repulsion decreases, as a result of which the binding energy should increase. If the power plant interaction wins in this fight, the core will be torn apart; if it is superficial, the core will return to its original state.

From the resulting expression for $E_{\text{cb}}$ it is clear that the fate of the nucleus largely depends on the number of protons ($Z$) and the total number of nucleons ($A$). As the atomic number of an element increases, the energy of electrostatic repulsion returns quickly to the surface of the energy, so only heavy nuclei can divide.

As already mentioned, during the process of nuclear fission, several more neutrons are always emitted, which can cause the fission of other nuclei (for uranium, usually 2-3). These neutrons are named and allow, by refuting the prediction of Rutherfort, which was considered, to implement in practice an entire nuclear fission reaction, which is accompanied by the release of a huge amount of energy.
