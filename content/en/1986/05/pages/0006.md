⟦folio 4⟧

In 1929, Swiss scientist Carl von Ningrard discovered that the pressure required for lung development could be significantly reduced by filling the lungs with a physiological solution. This has nothing to do with the idea of ​​the alveoli as hollow elastic balls: if, when inhaling, we had to overcome only elastic forces, then our efforts would not depend on whether the lung was filled with solution or not - after all, elastic forces do not depend on this. The data obtained by Ningrard became clear when it turned out that each alveolus was covered from the inside with a thin layer of liquid. This circumstance significantly affects the mechanical properties of the alveoli. In particular, the pressure required for the development of the alveoli is greater than in the case of a hollow ball of lung tissue. And here's why.

It is known that the surface of a liquid behaves like a stretched film - it has surface tension. To evaluate the role of surface tension forces in the mechanics of the alveoli, consider a film of liquid in the shape of a sphere (the simplest example of such a film is a soap bubble).*) Due to the surface tension of the liquid, the air pressure inside the bubble is always greater than the external pressure. The magnitude of excess pressure inside a sphere of radius $R$ is determined by the Laplace correspondence $\Delta p=2\sigma/R$, where $\sigma$ is the coefficient of surface tension of the film. This excess pressure is greater, the smaller the radius of the sphere. In a soap bubble film, both the inner and outer surfaces are in contact with an impact $\sigma$ of the film equal to twice the surface tension coefficient of the liquid. In the alveoli, air-liquid contact occurs only on one side, from the inside.

Let's estimate the values ​​of $\Delta p$. For intercellular fluid $\sigma = 5 \cdot 10^{-2}$ N/m; Let us also accept this value for the liquid lining the inner surface of the alveoli. By-
