The photograph placed next to the epigraph was not taken on Solaris, not from a spaceship plunging into the dark depths of Jupiter’s atmosphere, and not from the window of a bathyscaphe venturing close to an erupting underwater volcano. It depicts the work of the Rainbow lamp, which, although not always, can be bought in Gifts stores. He tant in himself many complex and red phenomena.

The design of the lamp is very simple. It is a transparent cylindrical flask, in the base of which, under a glass house, a regular electric lamp is built. The glass near the day is covered with a colored filter, and along its perimeter there is a metal spiral (Fig. 1). The flask is approximately 1/6 of its volume filled with a waxy substance (which we will further refer to as “substance A”), and then almost to the top is filled with a transparent liquid (which we will refer to as “substance B”). Observations are best made in the dark, when the Rainbow serves as the only source of light. Let's plug it into the network and be patient. As we will see, the origin of a lamp can be divided into several phases. We will call the first of these conditions the phase of rest and accumulation of strength.

Substance A is amorphous, that is, it does not have a strictly ordered structure. As the temperature rises, it softens and gradually turns into a liquid state. Note the important difference between the transition

For an amorphous substance, the solid and liquid states are assumed to be no different. As the temperature increases, the viscosity of the amorphous substance decreases, and it becomes more and more flowing.

The light bulb that is turned on, illuminating the inside of the cylinder with a reddish-green light through a light filter, also serves as a source of heat. There are two possibilities of “hot spot” (area of ​​increased temperature). In this area, substance A begins to demagnetize, while neither the upper crust, nor, especially, liquid B has yet had time to warm up and remains cold. As it heats up, more and more of substance A becomes liquid, its solid crust becomes thinner and thinner. Due to thermal distribution, the volume of the melted lower layers of substance A tends to increase, the pressure under the crust increases, and at some point liquid A penetrates the solid crust and bursts upward in bubbles. The volcano seemed to work for two. The phase of rest and accumulation of strength is completed - it is replaced by a phase of volcanic activity (Fig. 2).

Substances A and B are selected in such a way that the density of the heated liquid substance A, escaping from a crack in the crust, turns out to be somewhat less than the density of the still cold substance B. Therefore, portions of substance A flare up one after another. On the way, they are left in the cold liquid B and, upon reaching the surface, harden, taking on the most intricate shapes. When solidified, the density of substance A becomes slightly greater than the density of liquid B, and the “fragments” begin to slowly descend. However, some of them depend on the surface for a long time. The reason why small sedges float on the surface may be the force of the surface of liquid B, and the “shards” begin to slowly sink. The fact is that liquid B does not wet substance A, therefore the surface tension force acting on the resulting sedges is directed upward and tends to push them out of the liquid. Thanks to the same effect, water striders are held on the surface of the water, and a steel needle lubricated with fat floats.

Meanwhile, too much pressure in the lower part of the vessel, under the crust, has already been released, the edges of the crack have melted, and successive portions of molten substance A continue to flow through this crater at a low speed. However, now they do not break away from the day, but are slowly pulled out of the crater in the form of an upwardly extending scab. The surface of this scab, colliding with the skin

If you turn off the lamp at this moment, the “petrified forest” in it will remain unchanged - the lamp itself will not be able to return it to its original state. However, despite the fireworks of events that have taken place, we have not yet reached the operating mode, so we will leave the lamp on and continue observations.

Time passes, liquid B warms up, the shards lying at the bottom begin to melt, and the upward-going trunks gradually settle down. However, you won’t see any flattened drops among the existing shards—they all gradually take on a spherical shape. Under general conditions, the flattening of droplets on a non-wettable surface occurs due to gravity. It counteracts the forces of surface tension, which tend to give the drop the shape of a sphere - a body whose surface is minimal for a given volume. In the lamp, in addition to the force of gravity and surface tension, the drop is acted upon by the Archimedes force, which almost completely compensates for the force of gravity. Therefore, the drop appears to be able to prevent it from taking on a spherical shape.

Scientists have been pondering this question for quite some time. Moreover, it is not at all idle, but, as it turned out, is of great practical importance. Thus, it is directly related to the understanding of the physical processes occurring in powder metallurgy, where pressed metal grains are “sintered” into substances with unique properties. In 1944, the remarkable Soviet physicist Ya. I. Frenkel proposed the simplest model of such a process, which resulted in his pioneering work, filling the physical foundations of powder metallurgy. The idea behind this work allows us to estimate the service time.

Let two identical liquid drops begin to collide. Together with the contact, an isthmus is formed (Fig. 4),

which gradually grows as the droplets merge. To estimate the service time t, the easiest way is to use energy messages. In total, the “active” system of two drops has energy $\Delta E_{\pi}$, equal to the difference between the surface energies of the initial and final states (that is, two individual drops of radius $r_0$ and one “common” radius $r$): $\Delta E_{\pi}=8\pi r_0^2-4\pi r^2$. Since when drops merge, their total volume does not change, then $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3=2 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3$, which reveals the substances of the drops and the environment during their merger. We will evaluate this work by order of magnitude. To merge frictional friction, we used the Stoksa expression, valid for the case of a ball of radius $R$ moving with a speed $v$ in a liquid with velocity $\eta$: $F=6\pi \eta Rv$. We will assume that the temperature $\eta_A$ of the substance from which the drops are composed is much greater than the temperature of the surrounding environment, therefore we will substitute exactly $\eta_A$ into the Stoksa formula. Next, together with $R$ we substitute $r_0$. The same value characterizes the scale of movement of the liquid mass when droplets merge: $\Delta x \sim r_0$. Thus, to work with intense training there is:

$$\Delta A \sim 6\pi \eta_A r_0^3 / \tau_\Phi \sim 4\pi \sigma(2 - 2^{7/3}) r_0^2,$$

where

$$\tau_\Phi \sim (r_0 \eta / \sigma).$$

For water drops with $r_0 \sim 1$ cm, $\sigma \sim 0,1$ N/m and $\eta \sim 10^{-3}$ kg/($\text{m} \cdot \text{c}$) this time is only $10^{-4}$ s. However, for significantly higher liquid glycerol (at $20^\circ \text{C}$ $\sigma_{\pi \pi} \sim 0,01$ N/m, and $\eta \sim 1$ kg/($\text{m} \cdot \text{c}$)) the corresponding time $\tau_\Phi$ will already be ~1 s. For different liquids, depending on their thickness and surface area, $\tau_\Phi$ can vary widely.

Let us now return to the balls lying at the bottom of the lamp. While the temperature of liquid B is not high, the thickness of amorphous substance A is greater. Now it is clear that it is for this reason that the balls do not merge. In the same way, two wax balls will not merge if they are brought into contact or even squeezed at room temperature. However, as soon as you warm them up, the thickness of the wax will sharply decrease, and the liquid balls will merge quite quickly. Let us also note the important role of the state of the surface of the balls: if it is uneven and heavily polluted, then it is difficult to form a bridge between the balls.

The merging of drops is necessary for the further functioning of the lamp, and its design provides a special mechanism for the “overflow” of substance A from individual drops into the already melted main mass. This is the metal arm mentioned above, a Hindu around the perimeter of the lamp. It is well heated, and upon contact with it, drops of substance A warm up, their vitality drops, and they “willingly” flow into the main mass.

Let us now, having understood many of the details of the lamp’s behavior, take a look at this phenomenon as a whole. This begs the question: why do these continuously replacing each other, repeating processes of separation, collision and death of balls arise? It is clear that the entire “driving force” of the process lies in the temperature difference between the upper and lower ends of the lamp (“heater” and “refrigerator”). If we assume that the heat flow is realized due to the thermal conductivity of liquid B, then its temperature will simply change smoothly in height and nothing unusual will happen in the system. The appearance of balls, as well as convection, is a consequence of instability that arises under certain conditions in the system, in which heat flows propagate across the boundaries due to temperature differences.*) A new, rapidly developing science, synegretics, is searching for the general laws of such phenomena.
