Magnetized atomic hydrogen

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
I. P. KRYLOV

Hydrogen is the most abundant substance in the Universe. Blazing stars and cold interstellar gas are 90% hydrogen, the No. 1 element of the periodic system. The remaining 10% is almost entirely helium - element No. 2. The share of all the heavier elements that we have on Earth, on a cosmic scale, accounts for only 1/1000 of the total amount of matter. Hydrogen on Earth exists in molecular form, while in space hydrogen is mainly atomic. What's the matter? Is it possible to create and store atomic hydrogen on Earth? How to do this? You will get answers to these questions by reading this article. Interstellar gas is extremely rarefied: on average 1 atom of hydrogen per 1 cm³. What are the maximum densities of atomic hydrogen that can be obtained in the laboratory? The answer to this question is being asked every day, or now scientists in advanced physics laboratories are diligently working to increase this density.

All earthly gases become liquid when the temperature drops and then harden. Helium resisted the most stubborn transformation of gas into liquid. Only when the temperature dropped to several degrees near absolute zero (−273.15 °C) did helium become liquid. This was first done almost 80 years ago. But element No. 2 is not completely done: unlike other elements, it does not harden at normal pressure, even at zero absolute. Does atomic hydrogen become liquid? Scientists currently cannot give a confident answer to this question. There is reason to believe that element No. 1 in atomic form is even more capital than element No. 2, and not only does not harden, but does not even turn into liquid. But better let's take it in order.

“Earthly” and “cosmic” hydrogen: molecules and atoms

In the textbook we read: “hydrogen is a light wireless gas that turns into liquid at −253 °C and solidifies at −259 °C.” All this is said about ordinary “earthly” hydrogen, or, to put it in precise language, about molecular hydrogen H₂. The properties of “cosmic”, atomic hydrogen H are completely different. Let's start from the very beginning. Let's remember how to construct a hydrogen atom - the simplest atom in nature.

A hydrogen atom is formed by two elementary particles: a proton and an electron. Their electric charges are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. The proton is positively charged, 1836 times heavier than the electron and serves as the nucleus of an atom with a size of less than 10⁻¹² cm. The electron moves around the nucleus. However, its movement is subject to a special quantum law. According to modern concepts, one cannot assume that an electron moves in a specific orbit. It is, as it were, “smeared”: making independent attempts to determine the location of the electron in absolutely identical hydrogen atoms, we will detect the electron at very different points around the nucleus.* This is how an image of an electron cloud arises, the density of which indicates where it is more and where it is less likely to detect an electron (Fig. 1). The electron cloud in atoms does not have clear boundaries; however, beyond a certain distance, the density decreases very quickly. This distance can be taken as the radius of the atom. For a hydrogen atom, this value is called the Bohr radius and is equal to a=0.53·10⁻¹⁰ m=0.53 A (here the unit of length angstrom is used - 1 Å=10⁻¹⁰ m).

Hydrogen molecule

Let us now consider what happens when two hydrogen atoms come together. Atoms are generally neutral, so there are no electrical forces over large distances. As the distance decreases, the electron clouds begin to overlap (Fig. 3). From the laws of quantum mechanics it follows that the nature of the deformation of clouds is determined by the mutual orientation of the magnetic moments of the electrons. If the arrows showing the direction of magnetic moments look in the same direction (see Fig. 3), then the electron clouds between the nuclei are rarefied. In this case, repulsive forces prevail, acting between the nuclei more densely than at distant points. In this case, the forces of attraction prevail. Emphasizing that the forces of interaction between atoms are electrical forces; The magnetic interaction forces in this case are very small.

It is convenient to describe the interaction of atoms using a graph of the dependence of the potential interaction energy $U$ on the distance between atoms $r$. figure 4 shows these graphs for parallel ($\uparrow\uparrow$) and antiparallel ($\uparrow\downarrow$) orientations of the magnetic moments of hydrogen atoms. A positive sign of potential energy, as usual, corresponds to repulsion, a negative sign to attraction. As can be seen from figure, at small distances, even in the case of antiparallel orientation ($\uparrow\downarrow$), the atoms begin to repulse. This happens because the attraction of electron clouds to “foreign” nuclei can no longer compensate for the repulsion of positively charged nuclei. A stable equilibrium position corresponds to a minimum of potential energy. In this case, two hydrogen atoms in the $\uparrow\downarrow$ state will be located at a distance $r_0=0,7\AA$.

A stable system is formed - a hydrogen molecule. The energy of formation of a molecule—the “well depth” in the $U(r)$ graph—is $E_0 = 4,5$ eV. When atoms “merge” into a molecule, called recombination, this energy - the energy of recombination - must be given to surrounding bodies. In the reverse process, called dissociation, this energy—dissociation energy—must be supplied from an external source. Let us note again that in the $\uparrow \uparrow$ state, hydrogen atoms cannot form a molecule at all.

Potential energy graphs are very clear: the system tends to move as a sled would move on a slippery mountain, the slope of which is shown on the graph. For graph $\uparrow \uparrow$ the sled would move to the right, in the area of ​​large distances. This means that atoms repel and move away from each other. On graph $\uparrow \downarrow$ there is a slope along which the sled would move to the left, towards the origin of coordinates, which corresponds to the attraction of hydrogen atoms.

How do collisions occur?

Generally, the magnetic moments of atoms are randomly oriented. Half of the collision will originate in the $\uparrow \uparrow$ state, and the other half in the $\uparrow \downarrow$ state.

It is interesting that not every collision of two hydrogen atoms in the $\uparrow \downarrow$ state will lead to the formation of a molecule, that is, to recombination. Turning to our illustration with a sled, we can easily imagine that, having rolled onto a completely slippery mountain from somewhere on the right, the sled slides down into a hole, but by inertia it flies out onto the opposite slope and is left at the height from which it began sliding on the right slope. Then the sled will go down again, again reach the pit and go to the right with the same kinetic energy with which it arrived. It is clear that this imaginable movement will occur due to the law of conservation of energy, provided there is a complete absence of friction. To realize mountains, part of the kinetic energy always goes to work against the friction forces, that is, in the tello, and sooner or later the sled is left at the bottom of the pit. When hydrogen atoms collide, the law of conservation of energy is also valid. And there are no more difficulties here. If there are only two atoms colliding, then they have nothing to transfer their energy to. When they collide, the atoms will stick together, leak out into a stable position by inertia, come together until they are completely left, and then scatter again, like perfectly elastic balls. Elementary magnets of atoms are constantly subject to impacts from other atoms as a result of any non-stop telous motion. Therefore, even in the strongest magnets that create fields $B \approx 30$ T, at room temperature ($T = 300$ K) the magnetic moments of hydrogen atoms are oriented along the field and against the field with practically equal probability 1/2. Only by creating a field of thousands would we be able to orient atoms at room temperature. But we do not know how to create such magnetic fields. But we can easily lower the temperature! The technique for obtaining low temperatures $T \lesssim 1$K is now available to many laboratories. In addition, at these temperatures, a magnet can be made from superconducting wire. Small superconducting magnets produce magnetic fields of $B \approx 10$ T. Finally, we came to the correct technical solution to the problems of storing atomic hydrogen: a chamber in the field of a superconducting magnet at a temperature no higher than $T \approx 1$ K.

The easiest way to make a superconducting magnet is by winding superconducting wire around a cylindrical frame. Such a coil is also called a solenoid. By passing electric current through the wiring, we get a magnetic field that is maximum in the internal coils, where the hydrogen chamber should be placed. In addition to the orientation of atoms, solenodes will perform another important role: it will draw atomic magnets into the region of a strong field (just like a compass needle). Therefore, above the center of the solenoid, the concentration of oriented atoms will be much greater than far from the magnet (Fig. 6).
