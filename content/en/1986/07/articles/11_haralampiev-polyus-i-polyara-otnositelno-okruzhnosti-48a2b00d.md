Polis and polars relative to a circle

S. Ts. KHARALAMPIEV (NRB)

From the editor. In this note we talk about very beautiful problems that can be solved using the concept of polis and polars relative to a circle. Bulgarian schoolchildren are familiar with these witnesses, who, however, are not included in the school curriculum in the USSR. Therefore, the editors offered the note some information about the basic properties of the policy and polara.

Introduction: polis, polara and their properties

Two points $P$ and $Q$ are said to be conjugate to circle $(k)$ if the circle with diameter $PQ$ is orthogonal to circle $(k)$ (Fig. 1). The set of all points conjugate to the point $P$ relative to the circle $k(O; r)$ is called

lar point $P$ relative to $(k)$. Next we will fix the circle $k(O; n)$, and therefore we will omit the words “relation $(k)$”.

Property I (construction of a polar). The polar of the point $P$ is straight; it can be constructed as shown in figure 2. If the line $l$ serves as a polar for the point $P$, we also say that $P$ is the pole of the line $l$.

Property II (construction of a pole). Any straight line that does not pass through the center of the circle has a single pole; it can be constructed as shown in figure 3.

Property III (reciprocity). The polar $l_p$ of any point $P$ belonging to the polar $l_Q$ of the point $Q$ passes through $Q$.

Property IV (characteristic property). For the point $M$ polars $l_P$ points $P$ and only for them the correspondence is satisfied

$$OM^2 - PM^2 = 2r^2 - OP^2.$$ (1)

We leave the proofs of these properties to the reader.

Example 3 (Brucard's theorem). Let P, Q and R be the intersection points of the diagonals and opposite sides of the quadrilateral ABCD inscribed in the circle k(O; r), respectively. Then the orthocenter of triangle PQR coincides with the center O.

Proof. From the previous problem it follows that the points P=(AC)∩(BD), Q=(AB)∩(CD) and R=(AD)∩(BC) relative to the circle (k) have the lines QR, PR and PQ as polars, respectively. Then OP⊥QR, OQ⊥PR, OR⊥PQ, that is, O is the orthocenter.

Example 4. Let X₁X₂X₃X₄ be a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle (k), and Xᵢᵣ be the intersection point of the tangents (k) at the points Xᵢ and Xᵣ (i≠j, i, j=1, 2, 3, 4). Then the diagonals of the quadrilaterals X₁X₂X₃X₄, X₁₂X₂₃X₃₄X₁₄ have a common intersection point.

Proof. Let P=(X₁X₃)∩(X₂X₄), Q=(X₁X₂)∩(X₃X₄) and R=(X₁X₄)∩(X₂X₃) (Fig. 8). But X₁X₂ and X₃X₄ are secant circles (k) passing through point O, therefore the intersection points of X₁₂ and X₃₄ of pairs of tangents to (k) at points (X₁, X₂) and (X₃, X₄) will be located on the polar PR of point Q. By analogy, it is established that points X₂₃ and X₁₄ lie on the polar PQ of the point R, the consequence of which is (X₁₄X₂₃)∩(X₁₂X₃₄)=(PR)∩(PQ)=P.

Example 5. On a given circle there are paths of points: U, V, A, B, C. Three pairs of points are constructed: M=(UA)∩(VB) and M₁=(UB)∩(VA), N=(UB)∩(VC) and N₁=(UC)∩(VB), P=(UC)∩(VA) and P₁=(UA)∩(VC). Then the lines MM₁, MN₁ and PP₁ have a common point or are parallel (“Mathematics at school”, 1982, No. 2, problem 2480).

Proof. Let K=(MM₁)∩(NN₁) and K₁=(MM₁)∩(PP₁). Quadrilaterals ABVU and BCVU are inscribed in (k) (Fig. 9), so the polar of point K passes through the intersection points of the pairs of lines UV, AB and UV, BC, that is, the polar K is the line UV. By analogy, the quadrilaterals ABVU and ACVU are inscribed in (k), therefore the polar of the point K₁ passes through the intersection points of pairs of straight lines UV, AB and UV, AC, that is, the polar K₁ is the straight line UV. Points K and K₁ have the same polar, so K≡K₁. If the lines MM₁ and MN₁ are parallel (their intersection point K is the “at infinity” point), then UV is the diameter of the circle (k), so PP₁∩MN₁.
