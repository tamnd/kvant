Pear game PATIENCE

Each element α of group $G_1$ rearranges tokens. Let $\alpha(i)$ be the number of the chip, the place of which is replaced by the chip from cell $i$. The set of numbers $\alpha(1), \alpha(2), \dots, \alpha(8)$ is called a permutation of the numbers 1,2,...,8. So, each element of the group $G_1$ corresponds to some permutation.

The set $S_n$ of all permutations of a set of $n$ elements forms a group (the product $\alpha\beta$ of permutations $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is to perform them sequentially, that is, $(\alpha\beta)(i) = \beta(\alpha(i))$. This group is called a symmetric group. We have seen that the group $G_1$ can be considered part of the group $S_8$.

Let now $f = f(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_8)$ be some polynomial in all variables and $\alpha \in S_8$ be some permutation. Let us now replace in the polynomial $f$ the variable $x_1$ by $x_{a(1)}$, the variable $x_2$ by $x_{a(2)}$, ..., the variable $x_8$ by $x_{a(8)}$. We get a new polynomial $\alpha f = f(x_{a(1)}, x_{a(2)}, \dots, x_{a(8)})$.

It is clear that for two permutations $\alpha$ and $\beta$ there will be $(\alpha\beta)f = \beta(\alpha f)$.
Consider the polynomial $P_n(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = (x_1 - x_2)(x_1 - x_3) \dots (x_1 - x_n) \dots (x_2 - x_3) \dots (x_2 - x_n) \dots (x_{n-1} - x_n)$.

The permutation $\alpha \in S_n$ is called even if $\alpha P_n = P_n$, and odd if $\alpha P_n = -P_n$.

Figures similar to the playing fields of the considered puzzles are called graphs. Hence their common name - “travel through graphs”. More precisely, a graph is a finite set of points, called vertices, some of which are connected by arcs, called graph edges. Obviously, any graph can be associated with one or more iterations and groups of permutations that correspond to them. The reader may have already noticed that the groups of games considered are either signed variable (type $A_n$) or symmetric (type $S_n$). It turns out that this is not accidental: under some weights of general restrictions on the structure of graphs, they will always correspond to groups of either type $A_n$ or type $S_n$. To form a more accurate editary pattern, we will need the following definitions.

By a simple cycle $k$ ($k \geqslant 3$) we mean a sequence of vertices of the graph $P_1,...,P_k$ that satisfies the following conditions:

1) $P_1 = P_k$ (that is, the initial and last vertices of the cycle coincide);
2) $P_i$ and $P_{i+1}$ are connected by an edge ($i=1,...,k-1$),

⟦folio none⟧

3) $P_i \neq P_j$ at $i \neq j$ ($i, j=1, \dots, k-1$).

We will call a graph cyclic if any two vertices of this graph belong to some simple cycle.

Theorem. Let $\Gamma$ be an arbitrary cyclic graph with vertices $P_0, P_1, \dots, P_n$, and $G(\Gamma)$ be a cerra group “travel along $\Gamma$” with tokens 1,..., $n$. Then the following statement is true:

1) if all simple cycles $\Gamma$ are of even length, then $G(\Gamma)$ coincides with the group $A_n$;

2) if the graph $\Gamma$ is different from the graph $\Gamma_6$ depicted in figure 10, and contains at least one simple cycle of odd length, then $G(\Gamma)$ coincides with the group $S_n$.

We omit the proof of this statement. Note that partial cases of this theorem have already been proven earlier. An interesting exception is related to the graph $\Gamma_6$ (Fig. 10). All permutations of the group $G(\Gamma_6)$ were calculated on a computer and it was found that it is a proper subset of the group $S_6$, consisting of 120 elements. If we want the game “travel along the graph $\Gamma_6$” to always have a solution, then we must use at least three identical tiles. This is the only graph with this property.
