But as technical improvements are made, concentrations increase rapidly at first. The most successful addition and well-known prim is scatie. Everyone knows how to diagram air in a cylinder with a movable piston. The same can be done with atomic hydrogen. But when interacting with the surfaces of all substances except a liquid body, atoms very quickly recombine. Therefore, the inner surface of the chamber, including the piston, is covered with a film of a liquid body. It can be done without a piston; or rather, use the surface of a liquid body as a piston. At the beginning, hydrogen fills almost the entire volume of the chamber. By raising the level of a liquid body, gas can be made into a small bubble and the concentration of H can be increased hundreds and thousands of times (Fig. 7). In this way, a year ago we managed to reach a record value of $n = 5 \cdot 10^{18} \text{ cm}^{-3}$. This value is already comparable to the gas density under normal conditions. The same recombination prevents you from getting more.

However, is it correct to compare warm room air with very cold atomic hydrogen? Of course, in order to better evaluate what is available, it is necessary to compare the properties of various gases at low temperatures. Experiments with atomic hydrogen were carried out at $T=0,5$ K. We will choose this temperature for comparison. The pressure gauge directly changed the pressure $p$, and the concentration $n$ was calculated using the ideal gas formula. Thus, the above value for $n$ was obtained from the measured pressure value $p=35$ Pa. Let's see what pressures can be achieved with other gases.

As is known, if a gas is compressed at a constant, sufficiently low temperature, then the maximum pressure of the saturated vapor pressure is $p_{\text{н.п.}}$ of this substance. Further rolling will not increase the gas pressure, but will lead to a transition to the liquid or solid phase. At $T=0,5$ K, the saturated vapor pressure for all substances, with the exception of gases, is practically equal.
