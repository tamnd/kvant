The designations $A^{-1}$ and $B^{-1}$ show that the actions of these series are similar to the actions of the series $A$ and $B$.

The conditions $A, A^{-1}, B$ and $B^{-1}$ are called elementary series, and the transformations they produce are called elementary (admissible) transformations. figure 2 shows visual diagrams demonstrating the actions of these series on the playing field chips.

Elementary series have one field property: after they are completed, the central cell again becomes free, which makes it possible to perform them in any order one after another. A finite sequence of elementary series, for example $A, AB, A^{-1}BA$, will be called an admissible series or formula, and its action on the playing field pieces is an admissible transformation. When studying the effect of various formulas, the conditions used are chips on which numbers from 1 to 8 are written instead of letters. Then we will consider the standard configuration of chips to be the one in which each chip is in a cell with the corresponding number.

Let us describe the “algebra of admissible series”, which will help us with arbitrary algebraic transformations of admissible series in order to simplify them.

If two formulas $X$ and $Y$ produce the same transformations, then we will write $X=Y$. Let a series of moves that does not change the configuration be denoted by symbol 1. If $X$ and $Y$ are two admissible series, then the product $XY$ is also an admissible series, the action of which is to sequentially execute $X$ and $Y$. It is not difficult to see that $A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=1, B^{-1}B=BB^{-1}=1$. Similarly, for any admissible transformation $X$ there is an inverse, that is, such that $X^{-1}X=XX^{-1}=1$.

Let $G_1$ be the set of all admissible transformations. In this set the multiplication operation is defined, that is, for any $X$ and $Y$ from $G_1$ the product $XY$ is defined, which consists of sequentially performing the transformation $X$, and then the transformation $Y$.

This multiplication has the following properties: 1) $(XY)Z=X(YZ)$; 2) there is a transformation 1 such that $X \cdot 1=1 \cdot X=X$; 3) for any $X$ there is a $X^{-1}$ such that $X^{-1}X=XX^{-1}=1$.

In mathematics, an arbitrary set $G$, in which a multiplication operation having properties 1–3 is introduced, is called a group. We have shown that the set of admissible transformations $G_1$ that we have constructed is a group. We can also add that the group $G_1$ generates the transformations $A$ and $B$.
