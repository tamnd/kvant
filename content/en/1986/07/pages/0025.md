Now let's repeat one of Arrhenus' experiments on the electrical conductivity of rasters. To do this, we will need vinegar essence (Caution! Beware of the oko!) and the device with which we carried out the previous experiment. It’s better to take two batteries connected in series instead of one battery.

About test 2. Pour a little vinegar essence into a glass and close the target. The light is not on. Now we will pour water into a glass while watching the light bulb. We will see that at some point the light bulb will light up and then glow brighter and brighter. Observing this phenomenon, Arrenuus came to the conclusion that water molecules “tear” the molecules of the dissolved substance into ions, and the more of the former (that is, the diluted the solution), the more electrical carrier appears in the solution.

Speaking about pons, we should have a good idea of ​​their behavior in rasters. Let's try, for example, to set the speed of ion mixing in an experiment.

Test 3. Take a saucer or small plate. Place a blotter soaked in a solution of table salt at the bottom, and sprinkle crystals of potassium permanganate on top of the blotter. We will strengthen two graphite electrodes at opposite ends and connect them to the source (to two batteries connected in series). The crystals will begin to dissolve, and we will see that crimson tongues will stretch towards the positive electrode. By measuring the speed of their movement, which is easy to do with a watch and a ruler, you can estimate the speed of mixing of ions in the solution. It is clear that it will depend on many factors, and above all, on the source voltage. For comparison, we present several values ​​of the average speed of movement of ions under the influence of an electric field, for example, which is equal to unity (this speed is called mobility

The speed of ions also depends on temperature and the concentration of the solution. Try to see this for yourself.

The effect of electric current does not pass through the electrolyte without leaving a trace. It causes irreversible chemical changes in it.

Test 4. Prepare a solution of table salt, add a few drops of phenolphthalene to it and lower the ends of the electrodes connected to the poles of the battery. After some time, the solution at the negative electrode turns crimson. This indicates that alkali has appeared in the solution, that is, a chemical reaction has occurred.

Effectively, when the chain was closed, the positive sodium ions Na+ began to move towards the cathode, and the negative chlorine ions Cl− - towards the andus. Sodium reduction reaction occurs at the cathode

Na+ + e− = Na0,

which immediately reacts with water, resulting in the formation of an alkali:

2Na + 2H2O = 2NaOH + H2↑.

On the Andes, another process occurs at this time - the formation of chlorine gas:

2Cl− − 2e− = Cl2↑.

That is why this experiment must be carried out in a well-conducted room.
