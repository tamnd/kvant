⟦folio 13⟧

If you hit the compass lightly, it begins to fluctuate, and you will not be able to find out the direction of north until the needle calms down. Elementary magnets of atoms are constantly subject to impacts from other atoms as a result of any non-stop telous motion. Therefore, even in the strongest magnets that create fields $B \approx 30$ T, at room temperature ($T = 300$ K) the magnetic moments of hydrogen atoms are oriented along the field and against the field with practically equal probability 1/2. Only by creating a field of thousands would we be able to orient atoms at room temperature. But we do not know how to create such magnetic fields. But we can easily lower the temperature! The technique for obtaining low temperatures $T \lesssim 1$K is now available to many laboratories. In addition, at these temperatures, a magnet can be made from superconducting wire. Small superconducting magnets produce magnetic fields of $B \approx 10$ T. Finally, we came to the correct technical solution to the problems of storing atomic hydrogen: a chamber in the field of a superconducting magnet at a temperature no higher than $T \approx 1$ K.

The easiest way to make a superconducting magnet is by winding superconducting wire around a cylindrical frame. Such a coil is also called a solenoid. By passing electric current through the wiring, we get a magnetic field that is maximum in the internal coils, where the hydrogen chamber should be placed. In addition to the orientation of atoms, solenodes will perform another important role: it will draw atomic magnets into the region of a strong field (just like a compass needle). Therefore, above the center of the solenoid, the concentration of oriented atoms will be much greater than far from the magnet (Fig. 6).
