⟦folio 20⟧

They are trying to sow drops “between heaven and earth” for a single person, which cannot decide whether to rush after them or return them to their native element. And now there are up to ten liquid balls in the cylinder at the same time, one of which rises up, the others fall down (Fig. 6) - the phase of collision and catastrophe begins. It is this, the longest and most decisive phase, that is considered by those who create it as the operating mode of the lamp.

The balls in the lamp collide and change the direction of their movement, but you will not be able to observe their merging during such a collision. As we have already found out, it is more profitable (from an energy point of view) for the ball to merge together. But this takes time. It is clear that the time “allotted” to them is the collision time $t$. If $\tau_{\phi}$ is greater than $t$, the balls will not have time to merge and disperse. What determines the time of impact? In the lamp, there are mainly random impacts (Fig. 7), in which the softened balls are easily deformed

fussing over each other. In this case, the characteristic collision time is $t \sim (r_0/v)$. The speed of the balls in the lamp is only a few centimeters per second, the radii of the balls are several centimeters. So $t \sim 1$ s, and during this time the balls do not have time to merge. So they come to “wander” in the lamp, temporarily lying at the bottom, hanging at the top, colliding, but not merging.

The collision and catastrophe phase lasts a very long time, 5-7 hours. At this time, the instructions recommend turning off the lamp. However, at certain (sufficiently high) ambient temperatures, this phase may not be the last. After a stationary temperature distribution along the height is established in the lamp (all liquid B of final heating), the densities of substances A and B are practically comparative.

All substance A is collected into one large ball, which depends on the day, exposing the light filter. Over time, this ball, due to contact with the walls of the cylinder, cools down somewhat, its density increases slightly and it slowly sinks to the bottom. Having touched the bottom, the ball receives an additional portion of heat and returns to its original place. Here it freezes until it cools down again, after which the described process is repeated. This phase, not provided for in the instructions, can be called the large ball phase (Fig. 8).
