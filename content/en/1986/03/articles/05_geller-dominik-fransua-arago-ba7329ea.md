Dominique Francois Arago
Striving zealously for discoveries, he was cautious in his conclusions and most of all loved to pave new paths along which one could come to establish the identity of the causes of various phenomena.

Alexander Humboldt

February 26, 1986 marked the 200th anniversary of the birth of Dominique Arago, a remarkable French scientist, one of the most popular people in France of his time.

Arago was born in Estagel, a tiny town in the Eastern Pyrenees, on the border with Spain. He was not distinguished by any special abilities in early childhood. He diligently attended elementary school, dreaming of becoming a military man when he grew up. Then he continued his studies at the school of the neighboring town of Perignan. The rules at this school were not very different, and Arago spent almost all his time reading French classics. He preferred to read the surrender on the grassy old city rampart. It was here that a meeting took place that completely changed his life and led to the birth of Arago the scientist.

One day, Dominique Francois saw an officer of the engineering troops taking some complex geodetic measurements on the ground behind the old rampart. The young men were shocked by the youth of this officer (they were almost the same age). Since Aragoni had never been shy, he immediately approached the officer and asked how he managed to get epaulettes at such a young age. The officer replied that he received them after graduating from the Ecole Polytechnique in Paris.

On the same day, in the school library, Arago found the program of exams that had to be taken upon admission to the Polytechnic School. The big fashionable man was intimidated by mathematics - he didn’t know it at all. However, the fear of mathematics fought with the desire to gain office significance. The dream of becoming a military winner won, and Arago sat down to study mathematics.

Very soon he studied all the textbooks at his disposal, but this was clearly not enough. Then fifteen-year-old Arago began reading “growing up” mathematical books. As a result, in a year and a half, he marveled at mathematics so much that he brilliantly passed the two and a half hour entrance exam “for Monge himself”*). Arago's knowledge of certain issues in mathematics corresponded by that time to the level of a graduate course.

Student life began, which lasted three years.

After graduating from the School in 1806, Arago became secretary of the Bureau of Longitudes in Paris, and from 1809 to 1830 he was a professor at the Polytechnic School. In 1809, when Dominique François Arago was only 23 years old, he was elected a member of the Paris Academy of Sciences.

The next 20 years or so were the years of Arago's most creative activity. As a professor at the Ecole Polytechnique, and later as director of the Paris General School, he managed to do a lot of work. The breadth of his scientific interests gives rise to... Arago studied astronomy, optics, and electromagnetism. He carried out important works in the field of meteorology and physical geography. Finally, he made his mark as a historian of science. In a short article there is no way to even include a talk about all the problems to which Arago contributed. Therefore, we will provide only a short list here.

Arago tested the validity of the Boyle-Mariot law for various gases and air by testing a series of optical studies of elastic vapors at high temperatures.
