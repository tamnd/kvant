Second escape velocity

If a certain body is given a speed equal to the first cosmic velocity, then it will not fall to Earth, but will become an artificial satellite moving in a near-Earth circular orbit. Let us recall that this speed must be perpendicular towards the center of the Earth and equal in magnitude

$$v_1 = \sqrt{gR} = 7,9 \text{ km/c},$$

where $g = 9,8 \text{ m/c}^2$ is the root of free fall of a body at the surface of the Earth, $R = 6,4 \cdot 10^6 \text{ m}$ is the radius of the Earth (see “Physics 8”, § 40).

Can a body completely break the chains of gravity that “tie” it to the Earth? It turns out that it can, but to do this it needs to be “thrown” with even greater speed. The minimum initial speed that must be imparted to a body at the surface of the Earth in order for it to overcome the Earth's gravity is called the second cosmic speed $v_{II}$. Let's find its value.

When a body moves away from the Earth, the force of attraction does negative work, as a result of which the kinetic energy of the body decreases. However, the force of attraction gradually decreases with this. If the kinetic energy drops to zero before the gravity force becomes zero, the body will return back to Earth. In order for this to happen, it is necessary that the kinetic energy remains non-zero until the force of attraction becomes zero. And this can only happen at an infinitely large distance from the Earth.

According to the theorem on kinetic energy (see “Physics 8”, § 55), the change in the kinetic energy of a body is equal to the work done by a force acting on the body. For our service you can write:

$$0 - \frac{mv_II^2}{2} = A,$$

or

$$\frac{mv_II^2}{2} = -A,$$

(*)

where $m$ is the mass of the body thrown from the Earth, $A$ is the work done by the gravity force. Thus, to calculate the second cosmic velocity, it is necessary to find the work done by the force of attraction of a body on the Earth when the body moves away from the Earth’s surface to an infinitely large distance. Surprisingly, this work is not at all infinitely large, despite the fact that the movement of the body seems to be infinitely large. The reason for this is a decrease in the force of attraction as the body moves away from the Earth. What is the work done by the attractive force?

Let us take advantage of the fact that the work of the attractive force does not depend on the shape of the trajectory of the body (see “Physics 8”, § 56), and by distance the simplest case is that the body moves away from the Earth along a line passing through the center of the Earth. The figure shown here shows the Earth and a body of mass $m$ moving along the direction indicated by the arrow.

$$A_1 = -F(r_1-r) = F(r-r_1).$$
