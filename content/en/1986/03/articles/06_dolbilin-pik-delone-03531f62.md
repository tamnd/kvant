Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
N. P. DOLBILIN

One of the most beautiful peaks in the Belukha massif in Altai is called “Delone Peak”, named after its first climber. The life and work of this amazing man is described here.

Boris Nikolaevich Delaunay was, first of all, a great mathematician, and mathematical creativity was certainly the main thing in the life of this remarkable scientist. However, it is impossible to talk about Boris Nikolaevich only as a mathematician, just as it is impossible to single out its main, empty, brightest theme in a complex polyphonic work, since it is so inextricably linked with other themes.

The life of B. N. Delaunay, and he lived for 90 years, can be compared to a whole work of art. It was extremely rich and multifaceted. Further, the process of listing the most characteristic moments of his biography would require too much space for one article. In such a chronological listing, one of the first would be

1897 - a seven-year-old boy reads Goethe's Faust in the original, knows individual chapters of the poses by heart, and paints his landscapes in oils first.

Somewhere at the end of the list would have been canceled

1975, July 6 - Boris Nikolaevich, in the 86th year of his life, spends almost 25° below zero on a glacier under the peak of the Khan Tengri peak at an altitude of 4200 m. In the morning, he descends by helicopter to Lake Issyk-Kul and from there flies to Frunze, where the heat is 40 degrees. By the end of the day, he finds himself at the airport near Moscow, from where he has to get to the dacha located in the vicinity of Abramtsevo. Arriving at the station on the last train, Boris Nikolaevich with a heavy backpack walks through the forest late at night and loses his way. Having gotten lost in the forest at night, he throws off his robe in a secluded place and, lightly, only finds his home at dawn.

These biographical details show, on the one hand, that the versatile pressure of B. N. Delaunay manifested itself at a very early age. On the other hand, until his old age he retained his youthfully perky temperament, and his outstanding physical health allowed him to engage in both scientific work and tourism with full dedication at the end of his life. Geometry of numbers, mathematical crystallography, discrete geometry - this is the scope of scientific works written by Boris Nikolaevich when he was already over eighty. Altea and the Caucasus, the Carpathians and the Tien Shan - this is the geography of the journey made by Boris Nikolaevich at a wonderful age.

The studies of B. N. Delaunay related to equations of the 3rd degree, but, as we will see, for very effective and comprehensive results. First, he began to study the cubic analogue of Pell's equation:

$$ax^3 + y^3 = 1,$$

where the integer $a$ is not the cube of an integer. (In the case where $a$ is the cube of an integer, the left-hand side of the equation is expanded into a product of polynomials with integer coefficients, and the cubic Pell equation indicates the solution of diffactive equations of degrees 1 and 2.)

The answers to these questions are related to the study of a set of numbers of the form $A^3\sqrt{a^2}+B^3\sqrt{a}+C$ (A, B, C ∈ Z), where a is the coefficient of the Pell equation).

Among these numbers, units stand out, that is, those numbers whose figurative ones have the same form. For example, when a=2, the number $3^2-1$ is one, since its inverse is $\varepsilon^{-1}=3^2+3^2+1$. It is known that for each integer a ($3^2\notin Z$) among the units there will be such a (basic) unit $\varepsilon_0$, 0 < $\varepsilon_0<1$, from which all other (positive) units are obtained by raising to an integer power.

Showing exceptional ingenuity, B. N. Delaunay proved a difficult theorem: the equation $ax^3+y^3=1$, where a is an integer, but not a cube integer, can have, in addition to the solution (0, 1), no more than one solution in integers; the second solution exists if and only if the basic unit $\varepsilon_0$ is binomial, that is, of the form $\varepsilon_0=X_0^3\sqrt{a}+Y_0$, and then the second solution is $(X_0, Y_0)$.

As for finding the basic unit, twenty years earlier Voronoi gave an algorithm for calculating it.

For example, for the equation $4x^3+y^3=1$ the basic unit is three-term, therefore this equation has no other solutions except (0; 1). And the equation $37x^3+y^3=1$, for which the basic unit is binomial, $\varepsilon_0=-3^3\sqrt{37}+10$, has two solutions (0,1) and (-3, 10).

Then B. N. Delaunay moved on to the more general case of cubic equations with two unknowns of a negative discriminant

$ax^3+bx^2y+cxy^2+dy^3=1$, (A) where the coefficients a, b, c, d are integers, and the words “in the case of a negative discriminant” mean that the corresponding polynomial equation $at^3+bt^2+ct+d$ has a single real root $t_0$. Otherwise, the problem also reduces to the study of two-term units. After the “increase algorithm” he created, B. N. Delaunay obtained the following functional result: in the general case, an equation of the form (A) has no more than three solutions in

integers. In only two cases does an equation of form (A) have four solutions, and in one other case does the equation have five solutions. No equation of form (A) has more than five solutions. After the cycle of work on diophantine equations, B. N. Delaunay still had 60 years of creative activity, which added to the authority of a major algebraist the authority of a major geometer. The mixing of his research towards geometry occurs naturally. The role of definition here was played by the contact in the process of researching indefinite equations with the works of Voronoi. But the main reason for the geometrization of B. N. Delaunay’s research was his exceptional designation, artistic perception of the world.

It is characteristic that Boris Nikolaevich never wanted the energy or time to bring the understanding of a seemingly already completed mathematical result to a level at which the result would take shape in a conventional geometric image. “What does this simply mean?” - Boris Nikolaevich’s favorite question, which he asked himself and his colleagues when discussing his next job. It is no coincidence that in his work they “stretch divided parallelepids”, “stretch out their noses”, and “let balls fly” between points scattered in space.

Recognition of scientific merits did not take long to arrive: in 1929, V. N. Delaunay was elected a corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences. By this time, he had been working as a professional at Leningrad University for seven years. His extremely clear, well-thought-out lectures arouse great interest among students. V.N. Delone never missed the opportunity to tell them about a difficult, unsolved problem. In her teaching practice, V. N. Delaunay was guided by the well-known principle “The student is not a vessel that needs to be filled, but a torch that needs to be lit.” High scientific authority, a devoted and at the same time natural attitude towards science, personal charm, artistry - all this attracted young people to him. It is not surprising that Boris Nikolaevich had many students, including outstanding mathematicians: Academician A. D. Aleksadrov, corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences D. K. Faddeev and I. R. Shafarovich.

In the spring of 1934 in Leningrad, B. N. Delone organized the first mathematics Olympiad for schoolchildren, which actually began the Olympiad movement in our country. Participants in the Leningrad Olympiad still remember what a “stunning impression” their meetings with Boris Nikolaevich made on them (see «Kvant» 1984, No. 9, p. 52).

In 1934, the Mathematical Institute named after. V. A. Steklova, where B. N. Delaunay from the day of its foundation headed the algebra department (until 1960), and then the geometry department. In 1935, the Naiad Mathematical Institute, together with other leading institutions of the USSR Academy of Sciences, was transferred to Moscow. From the same year, B. N. Delone headed the department of geometry at Moscow University. While working at Moscow State University, he gave an original course of lectures on analytical geometry, which was distinguished by its exceptional wealth of geometric ideas. Students who had the pleasure of attending B. N. Delaunay’s lectures hardly suspected that their great descriptions hid careful preparation. So, in the first years, it took him two full days to prepare a two-hour lecture, later - a day. Boris Nikolayevich could not stand superficiality or negligence; he polished every idea, verified every word, despaired every figure. And, as a consequence of this, during the lecture, cute “affine cats”, elegant parabolids and hyperbolids, clear dodecaders and icoasdes quickly appeared on the blackboard.
