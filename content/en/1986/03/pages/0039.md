The square (red polyline at figure 1) contains $k \geqslant [(n-1)/2]$ sides (see Fig. 1), so $A$ must go along the path to $B$ at least $k$ sides, that is, make at least $k$ intersections. But during these crossings, the order of the relative positions of the remaining $n-1$ participants does not change and they need to make at least $t_{n-1}$ crossings among themselves in order to be placed in the opposite order, which is where (1) follows.

Since $t_3=1$ (Fig. 2), from estimate (1) we obtain that

$$t_{2k} \geqslant 1 + \left[ \frac{3}{2} \right] + \left[ \frac{4}{2} \right] + \dots + \left[ \frac{2k-1}{2} \right] = 2(1+2+\dots+(k-1)) = k^2-k,$$

(2)

$$t_{2k+1} \geqslant 1 + \left[ \frac{3}{2} \right] + \dots + \left[ \frac{2k-1}{2} \right] + \left[ \frac{2k}{2} \right] = k^2.$$

(3)

Let us show that the intersection number found here is sufficient, that is, the sign $\geqslant$ in (2) and (3) can be replaced by the sign $=$. Let's list the participants of the unit in order from 1 to $n$ and divide them into two groups - from 1st to $k$ and from $(k+1)$ to $n$. To change the order in the first group, it will sequentially match the 1st participant with the 2nd, 3rd, ..., $k$, then the 2nd with the 3rd, 4th, ..., $k$, etc. (Fig. 3). This will take $(k-1)+(k-2)+\dots+1 = (k^2-k)/2$ crossings. Similarly, with $(n-k)(n-k-1)/2$ intersections we get the reverse order in the second group. In total, at $n=2k$ or $n=2k+1$ (that is, $k=[(n+1)/2]$ it will be necessary to perform $k^2-k$ or $k^2$ intersections, respectively.

Let us present an idea for another derivation of estimates (2) and (3). Note that of any three participants of the part, at least two must be placed in places (otherwise their order is preserved). We connect two participants with a segment if they never have to intersect each other. We will get a set of segments with endpoints at $n$ points (or, as they say, a graph with $n$ vertices). Due to the replacement made, these segments (edges of the graph) do not form a single triangle. It is not difficult to prove by induction that a graph with $n$ vertices without triangles contains at most $[n^2/4]$ edges (this is a special case called Turán’s theorem; the proof for even $n$ was given in the solution to Problem M934a in «Kvant» No. 11 of 1985). Consequently, the number of pairs of participants that intersect each other at least once is at least $n(-1)/2 - [n^2/4]$, which, as can be easily verified, coincides with the right-hand side of (2) or (3) - depending on the parity of $n$.
