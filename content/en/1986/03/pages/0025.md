It is not difficult to show (do it yourself) that in any intermediate position, when the normal to the contra plane, an arbitrary angle β is formed with an indication of the magnetic field, the torque is equal to

$$M = BIS \sin \beta.$$

From this expression it is clear that for a given value of the field indication and when determining the position of the counter with current, the torque depends only on the product of the area of ​​the counter S and the current strength I in it. The quantity IS is called the magnetic moment of the current-carrying contraption. More precisely, IS is a model of the magnetic moment vector. And this vector is directed perpendicular to the contra plane and in such a way that if we rotate the gimlet in the direction of the current in the circuit, then the direction of the translational movement of the gimlet indicates the direction of the magnetic moment. For example, the magnetic moment of the contra shown in Figures 1 and 2 is directed away from us behind the plane of the pages. The magnetic moment is measured in A m².

Now we can say that a circuit with a current in a uniform magnetic field is installed so that its magnetic moment “looks” in the direction of the field that caused its rotation.

It is known that not only a circuit with current has the property of creating its own magnetic field and turning in an external field. The same properties are observed in a magnetized rod, for example, in a compass needle.

Back in 1820, the remarkable French physicist Ampere expressed the idea that the similarity of the behavior of a magnet and a contra with a current is explained by the fact that closed currents exist in parts of the magnet. It is now known that in atoms and molecules there are actually tiny electric currents associated with the movement of electrons in their orbits around nuclei. Because of this, atoms and molecules of multi-tailed substances, such as paramagnetic materials, have magnetic moments. The rotation of these moments in an external magnetic field leads to the magnetization of paramagnetic substances.

Others have also found out. All particles that make up an atom also have magnetic moments that are not at all associated with any movements of charges, that is, with currents. For them, the magnetic moment is the same “innate” quality as charge, mass, etc. Even a particle that has no electric charge—the neutron, an integral part of atomic nuclei—has a magnetic moment. Therefore, atomic nuclei also have a magnetic moment.

Thus, magnetic moment is one of the most important concepts in physics.
