$$\varrho_{\pi} = \frac{M_{\pi}}{V_{\pi}} \approx \frac{m_p A}{V_{\pi}} \approx 10^{17} \text{ кг/м}^3.$$

It turns out to be miraculously big. If it were possible to completely fill a cube with a volume of one cubic centimeter with kernels, then the mass of this cube would be approximately 100 million tons!

Such a high density of nuclear matter obviously indicates the existence of enormous attractive forces acting between the particles that make up the nucleus. (The basic properties of nuclear forces are described in § 107 of Physics 10.) In addition to ordinary nuclei, theoretical physicists suggest the possibility of the existence of “unusual” ones: purely neutron, superdense (in them the matter is compressed several times more strongly), etc. Experimental searches for them have not yet led to positive results.

Can nuclear matter exist in nature in the form of large formations? A positive answer to this question was received when astronomers discovered (in 1967) a neutron star.

Such a star can form from a common star during rapid decline. In a common star, there is a balance between the gravitational forces, which tend to compress the star, and the pressure forces of heated stellar matter, which oppose this compression. Compression begins when the thermonuclear energy sources inside the star exhaust their resources. The star cools down, the equilibrium is disturbed, and matter rushes towards the center of the star. The density of matter increases sharply, atomic electrons are “pressed” into nuclei and nuclear reactions begin, converting protons into neutrons. There comes a point when the matter inside the star will consist mainly of neutrons and have a nuclear density. In this case, a star with a mass equal to the Sun should shrink to a ball with a radius of ~10 km.

The possibility of the existence of neutron stars was predicted by the outstanding Soviet physicist L. D. Landdu.
