The answers to these questions are related to the study of a set of numbers of the form $A^3\sqrt{a^2}+B^3\sqrt{a}+C$ (A, B, C ∈ Z), where a is the coefficient of the Pell equation).

Among these numbers, units stand out, that is, those numbers whose figurative ones have the same form. For example, when a=2, the number $3^2-1$ is one, since its inverse is $\varepsilon^{-1}=3^2+3^2+1$. It is known that for each integer a ($3^2\notin Z$) among the units there will be such a (basic) unit $\varepsilon_0$, 0 < $\varepsilon_0<1$, from which all other (positive) units are obtained by raising to an integer power.

Showing exceptional ingenuity, B. N. Delaunay proved a difficult theorem: the equation $ax^3+y^3=1$, where a is an integer, but not a cube integer, can have, in addition to the solution (0, 1), no more than one solution in integers; the second solution exists if and only if the basic unit $\varepsilon_0$ is binomial, that is, of the form $\varepsilon_0=X_0^3\sqrt{a}+Y_0$, and then the second solution is $(X_0, Y_0)$.

As for finding the basic unit, twenty years earlier Voronoi gave an algorithm for calculating it.

For example, for the equation $4x^3+y^3=1$ the basic unit is three-term, therefore this equation has no other solutions except (0; 1). And the equation $37x^3+y^3=1$, for which the basic unit is binomial, $\varepsilon_0=-3^3\sqrt{37}+10$, has two solutions (0,1) and (-3, 10).

Then B. N. Delaunay moved on to the more general case of cubic equations with two unknowns of a negative discriminant

$ax^3+bx^2y+cxy^2+dy^3=1$, (A) where the coefficients a, b, c, d are integers, and the words “in the case of a negative discriminant” mean that the corresponding polynomial equation $at^3+bt^2+ct+d$ has a single real root $t_0$. Otherwise, the problem also reduces to the study of two-term units. After the “increase algorithm” he created, B. N. Delaunay obtained the following functional result: in the general case, an equation of the form (A) has no more than three solutions in

integers. In only two cases does an equation of form (A) have four solutions, and in one other case does the equation have five solutions. No equation of form (A) has more than five solutions. (Thus, the equation $x^3-xy^2+y^3=1$ has five solutions: (1; 1), (1; 0), (0; 1), (-1, 1), (4, -3), and the fact that there are no other solutions follows from Delaunay’s theorem.

Moreover, we come to work in exceptional conditions: Denikites and Petliurists, “Kaiserites” and White Poles, “Greens” and “Zhovtolakitnye” - whoever terrorized Kyiv during the civil war...

It can be said that in determining the meaning of the result, B.N. Delaunay remained continuous near-pulves until the end of the 60s. As corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences D.K. Faddeev wrote:

“In terms of concreteness of analysis, simplicity and clarity, the cycle of work by B. N. Delaunay, devoted to indefinite equations, is exceptional in the mathematics of the 20th century with its often cumbersome apparatus and abstract constructions. In its style, this cycle is close to the best examples of the classical works of Gauss and Chebyshev on number theory.”

After the cycle of work on diophantine equations, B. N. Delaunay still had 60 years of creative activity, which added to the authority of a major algebraist the authority of a major geometer. The mixing of his research towards geometry occurs naturally. The role of definition here was played by the contact in the process of researching indefinite equations with the works of Voronoi. But the main reason for the geometrization of B. N. Delaunay’s research was his exceptional designation, artistic perception of the world.
