The simplest diffactive equation is, perhaps, the equation of the first degree with two unknowns

$$ax + by = 1,$$

where $a$ and $b$ are integers. If $a$ and $b$ have common factors, then there are no solutions. If $a$ and $b$ are relatively prime, then there are infinitely many solutions (for more details, see «Kvant» 1973, no. 4, p. 38). The theory of such equations, according to historians of mathematics, was outlined by the Indian mathematician Arribhata in the 5th-6th centuries. n. e. (by the way, the first Indian artificial Earth satellite is named after him).

The next most difficult step after the natural linear equation is a similar quadratic equation

$$ax^2 + bxy + cy^2 = 1.$$

An important part of the case of a 2nd degree equation is the Pell equation

$$x^2 - dy^2 = 1$$

- was studied by Euler (for more details see «Kvant» 1985, No. 1, p. 20). If $c (>0)$ is the square of an integer, then, apart from the obvious solution (1; 0), there are no other integer solutions. If $c$ is not the square of an integer, then there are infinitely many solutions. The study of the general form of diffactive equations of the 2nd degree was carried out by the replaced French mathematician Lagrange (1736-1813).

Unfortunately, or perhaps not, Delaunay did not know about the work of the Norwegian mathematician A. Thue, published shortly before.

Thue considered differential equations of the form

$$a_0 x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} y + \dots + a_{n-1} x y^{n-1} + a_n y^n = b (1)$$

and proved that for $n \geq 3$ they can have only a finite number of solutions, if, of course,

the left side of the equation does not expand into a product of polynomials with rational coefficients containing components of degrees less than 3. (If the expansion contains a polynomial of the second or first degree, then there can be an infinite number of solutions.)

Thue's theorem is one of the most fundamental in the theory of differential equations. However, by asserting for each differential of an equation of the indicated type that the number of solutions is finite, Thue’s theorem cannot say anything about how to find these solutions. It says nothing about the upper bound for the values ​​of solutions to a particular equation. And this is important. Let's imagine that from the coefficients of a particular differential equation we can calculate CONSTANTу $c$, which depends on exceeding the absolute values ​​$|x_0|, |y_0|$ for any integer solution $(x_0, y_0)$ of this differential equation. Then, by substituting into the equation all possible pairs of integers $(x, y)$ with the condition $|x|, |y| < c$, we can find all solutions to a particular equation. Thue's theorem itself is not effective in this regard.

The studies of B. N. Delaunay related to equations of the 3rd degree, but, as we will see, for very effective and comprehensive results. First, he began to study the cubic analogue of Pell's equation:

$$ax^3 + y^3 = 1,$$

where the integer $a$ is not the cube of an integer. (In the case where $a$ is the cube of an integer, the left-hand side of the equation is expanded into a product of polynomials with integer coefficients, and the cubic Pell equation indicates the solution of diffactive equations of degrees 1 and 2.)
