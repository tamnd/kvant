Cities for electricians

Candidate of Technical Sciences
D. G. KRUTOGIN

Interval semiconductor circuits have become the main building block of solid-state electronics in recent years. Next, an experienced radio amateur now prefers to use a ready-made microcircuit instead of assembling an amplifier from parts. Moreover, this is what professional designers do. In a household radio equipment, for example in a TV, the number of microcircuits reaches ten, in a special one - hundreds and thousands of pieces per product. Multi-element interval circuits are called large - abbreviated as LSI. The use of such schemes turns out to be especially effective in computer technology.*)

How are microcircuits made? Let's follow the main stages of manufacturing LSIs, let's try to evaluate both the difficulties and achievements in their technology.

BIS under a microscope fills with its clear layout a map of a large city. So let’s compare the construction of an LSI with the construction of a city, a city for electricians.

300 microns. The method for producing wafers is not very economical: a diamond saw with a thickness of 300 microns, and then grinding, converts more than 60% of the volume of a single-crystalline silicon ingot into sawdust, obtained at the cost of considerable us or energy costs. And the thickness of the finished plates will be poorly used. The useful depth, that is, the thickness of those layers in which the points we need will flow, does not exceed 1 micron. But, unfortunately, it is impossible to make the plates thinner - they will be destroyed during processing.

As a construction site for VIS, you can use a dielectric plate - aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃), commonly called salfur. The salfir plate is polished as carefully as a flint plate, and then a monocrystalline layer of silicon of the required thickness is applied to it. Salfir was chosen as a substrate because the location of atoms on the surface of the salfir crystal, when determining the direction of the medium, is very close to the structure of the silicon crystal lattice. This circumstance makes it possible to grow a thin crystalline layer of silicon on the surface of the salfir—the working area of ​​the future transistor. The combination of layers “silicon on salfir” - it is designated KNS - is widely used in the technological production of VIS. It has an important application: salfir is an excellent dielectric; it provides complete electrical insulation of individual circuit elements. From the point of view of the topology of the microcircuit, these elements in a silicon circuit are similar to blocks dug into the ground, and in a SNS structure they are similar to houses (Fig. 1).

Within the framework of our analysis, a modern VIS is equivalent to a city of 100-150 thousand transistor houses with developed communication systems (roads, electricity, etc.). The dimensions of this city range from 2x3 mm to 6x8 mm. And on such a construction site we will build all the houses at the same time. Moreover, on one silicon wafer we will simultaneously build 100-150 VIS cities (depending on the size of the wafer and the area of ​​the chip). Our life-size “huge” construction site looks something like the one on figure 2.

Perhaps the reader has a question: why not make a microcircuit on a chip with a size of, say, 15x15 mm - this is also not much for a plate with a diameter of 150 mm, and the capacity of the VIC will increase by an order of magnitude. It turns out that on a crystal of increased size there will almost certainly be an atomic lattice defect that can completely disable the VIS. On a surface area of ​​1-2 cm², even the best plate today will have at least one defect. So it comes before the volition of “malm”.

Let's start building with... windows

So, we start construction. The houses in our city are not small. Look at figure 3,a: three “floors” in the working area of ​​the transistor, two floors of metal wiring and several floors of intermediate insulation; so the home store has at least 5-7 floors.

The total number of operations performed during the construction of an integrated circuit reaches hundreds, but some operations are repeated many times. We will look at the most important points in the VIS manufacturing process.

The working areas of the transistor - collector, base, emitter - are formed by doping silicon with appropriate impurities of elements of groups III and V of the periodic system. Alien atoms can be introduced into the creative body using the phenomenon of diffusion. If a wafer of silicon is heated in the vapor of a volatile compound of the alloying element - for example, in the vapor of BH₃ or AsH₃ - then some of the atoms in or As will penetrate into the silicon.

(This process is called diffusion doping.) In order to be used not everywhere, but only in the places provided for by the VIS topology, it is necessary to cover the surface of the plate with a screen that is impenetrable to the use of atoms. “Windows” must be left in the screen: through them “building materials” will flow to the next floor - atoms of applications (donors and acceptors) will be penetrated into the silicon, forming the working areas of the transistor (see figure 3, b-2).

Such a screen for silicon turned out to be its own oxide SiO₂. A fairly “thick” oxide layer easily forms on the silicon surface at a temperature of about 1000 °C; it is impenetrable to the use of atoms; stable at diffusion temperature; firmly adheres to silicon. Planting SiO₂ in acid (etching) allows you to make windows in it for subsequent diffusion. (Technologists do not always feel this way. For example, an excellent semiconductor material—GaAs—does not form such a power layer during oxidation, and its shielding becomes a separate problem.)

Is it easy to “cut through a window”?

Before etching windows in SiO₂, it is necessary to protect the rest of the oxide film from dissolution (at this stage, the protected layer itself needs protection). This is where the most important procedure in microscopic technology is included: photolithography. Its purpose is to “open” the powered layer of SiO₂ in accordance with the VIS topological diagram.

Photolithography is a set of operations involving the use of light-sensitive compounds similar to those applied to photographic film or photographic paper. These compositions are called photoresists. The verb “to resist” in English means “to resist.” Illuminated photoresist easily dissolves in etching solutions; unexposed photoresist “resists” etching. (You can also choose a composition that, on the contrary, becomes stable after exposure.)

In a special centrifuge, the liquid resist is washed out in a thin layer over the surface of the oxidized silicon wafer and dried.
