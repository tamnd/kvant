New meter

What will be discussed here is not a new discovery in any particular field of science. Nevertheless, we will talk about an event that, of course, will be written about in all textbooks and which will be reflected in the entire life and activity of a person.

More than two years ago, in October 1983, the International Committee of Weights and Measures (its official name is CIPM - la Comité Internationale des Poids et Mesures) published a recommendation to introduce a new meter. Here is the beginning of this document:

“The International Committee of Weights and Measures recommends that the meter be specified by one of two methods -

a) the length of the path l traversed in vacuum by a plane electromagnetic wave in time t; the path length is calculated by measuring time t, using the correspondence l=ct and the value of the speed of light in vacuum c=299,792,458 m/s;

b) the wavelength in vacuum λ of a plane electromagnetic wave of frequency v; the wavelength is calculated by measuring the frequency v, using the formula λ=c/v and the value of the speed of light c=299,792,458 m/s...”

Let's remember how the meter is defined now. Also, the official document adopted in 1960 by the 11th General Conference on Metas and Libras states: “A meter is a length equal to 1,650,763.73 wavelengths in vacuum of the radiation corresponding to the transition between the 2p10 and 5d5 levels of the atom of krypton-86.”

What would the meter be like before 1960?

According to the first definition, adopted in France during the French Revolution, the meter was equal to a ten-mile fraction of a four-second length of the wig meridian. In 1799, the first prototype of a meter in the form of a platinum ruler was manufactured and approved - the so-called archival meter. Then, in 1872, the International Metric Commission decided to abandon the “natural” standard of length and adopt the archival meter as the standard measure of length. The prototype of this meter in the form of a bar made of an alloy of platinum and a dove with strokes applied on one of its planes is kept in Sèvres (near Paris) in the International Biomeasures and Weights. The relative error in reproducing such a meter is about 10⁻⁷.

This definition of the meter continued for almost 80 years. Only by 1960 did optical measurement methods reach such perfection that they could compete with simply applying the sample being measured to a wig.

Lon. They allow you to reproduce the meter with an accuracy of about 10⁻⁸.

Why is now the time to introduce a new definition of meter? One reason is fundamental. The new method is based on the speed of light in a vacuum - a fundamental constant value that does not depend on the movement of the observable. In the new definition of the meter, the speed of light is considered given, it is exactly equal to 299,792,458 m/s (just as before 1960 a meter was exactly equal to the height of the corresponding meter).

Another reason, technical, is associated with the expansion of precise methods for measuring frequency up to the visible frequency of the spectrum, in which optics are able to measure wavelength with a greater degree of accuracy (using the interference method). Since 1982, it has become possible to measure the frequency and wavelength of the same spectral line with an error not exceeding 1-2 units in the tenth digit (!). One can jokingly say that only in recent years has the correspondence of c=λv for visible light been verified.

So, when the recommendation of the International Committee of Weights and Measures is adopted, physical tables will contain the exact value of the speed of light, and lengths can be measured with an error of no more than two units in the tenth digit.
