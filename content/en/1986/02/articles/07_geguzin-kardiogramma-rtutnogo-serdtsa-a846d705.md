Cardigram of mercury heart

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
Y. E. GEGUZIN

Of the many features and properties of a true mercury heart, only one has the ability to pulsate, periodically changing its shape. The mercurial heart knows no more, no interruptions, no worries; It has an unconditional and compelling advantage over a real heart - it can remain inactive for as long as desired, and then come back again.

In our laboratory, in the cabinet where chemical reagents are stored, there lives a mercury heart. Its design is quite simple: a porcelain cup with a concave house, at the bottom of the cup there is a root in the form of a flattened drop-puddle (≈ 4 cm in diameter). It is covered only with a layer of a weak (5 percent) aqueous solution of hydrochloric acid (≈50 ml), in which the salt of potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇; ≈60 mg) is dissolved. The porcelain cup is covered with a plastic glass plate, a nut is fixed in the plate, and in it there is a screw ending with an iron wire-needle. By rotating the screw, the wire can be lowered until it comes into contact with the surface of the mercury, and the mercury heart begins to work, that is, to pulsate rhythmically.*)

In order for such a heart to work reliably, the wire must touch its surface to the puddle drops either in the center or at one of the points on the contour.

The pulsation of the mercury heart is more impressive: on the clean surface of the drop, iridescent highlights of a curious shape appear, and the contour of the drop acquires a rapidly changing sequence, which is repeated in each next cycle of pulsations. The heart works without installation: we leave it for an hour, two, and once we left it overnight - and in the morning we found it pulsating.

Now about the mechanism of pulsation of the mercury heart - rays of mercury, which continuously quiver when it comes into contact with an iron wire.

First, about two effects that you need to familiarize yourself with in order to understand the reason for the periodic contractions and expansions of a mercury drop.

The first effect will be to reduce the surface tension of the metal if there is an electric charge on its surface. The easiest way to understand this is using the example of a free spherical liquid metal drop, say a drop of mercury. Let the radius of the drop be R. If it is not charged, the substance located in its volume will experience a skimming pressure p_czh=2σ/R. It is associated with surface curvature (R) and surface tension (σ). Let us now assume that a charge of magnitude q is distributed over the surfaces of the drop. It is obvious that charge carriers will repel each other with a force, the magnitude of which (in accordance with Coulomb's law) is proportional to the square of the charge. This means that with a constant volume of a spherical drop (and, therefore, with a constant radius), the presence of an electric charge on its surface will lead to a decrease in the skimming pressure. This circumstance can be presented as a consequence of a decrease in surface tension by values ​​Δσ~q².

We did not strive to ensure that the calculation was accurate; All that is important is the basis for asserting that the magnitude of the decrease in the surface tension does not depend on the sign of the charge located on the surface. The guarantee of this is the square dependence of Δσ on the charge value q.

We need to know all this, how mercury in a solution of hydrochloric acid receives a charge, and therefore the surface tension at the mercury-solution boundary is understood. (We will not intersect in detail -

The described effect of the influence of a charge on a surface on the value of surface tension, which is called the “electrocapillary effect,” was discovered and studied by the French physicist Gabriel Lippmann (1845–1921) and found many practical applications.

The second effect is that the moment the metal needle comes into contact with the surface of the mercury, the amount of charge present on the mercury changes, therefore the effective surface tension should also change. With the help of a simple experiment, it is easy to verify that at the moment of contact the magnitude of the charge on the mercury decreases, and at the same time the decrease in surface energy that occurred when the mercury was filled with hydrochloric acid solution also decreases. Two consecutive negations are equivalent to one statement: a decrease in lowering is familiarized by increasing. The moment the iron needle touches the surface of the mercury, its surface tension increases slightly. The consequence of this increase should be some rolling down of the drop-puddle, which at the bottom of the cup has flattened under its own weight, and a partial approximation of its shape to a spherical one. This is not observed if a metal needle is immersed in the center of a drop into its body: the drop-puddle will tremble, and its horizontal diameter will change.

Now you can understand the pulsation mechanism. Let's start with the case where the iron needle touches the mercury at a point on the contour of the puddle (Fig. 1). At the moment of contact of the needle with the surface of mercury - contact, not penetration! - the surface tension increases slightly, the mercury puddle will reduce its diameter, and the contact between it and the needle will be disrupted. After this, the surface tension should return to the value that was before the needle came into contact with the drop, that is, it should lower, and the radius of the mercury of the puddle should increase. This means that the puddle will come into contact with the needle and everything will start again: contraction of the puddle - loss of contact, expansion of the puddle - restoration of contact, etc.

An interesting mechanism for the occurrence of pulsation is in the case when the needle touches the surface of mercury not on the contour of the mercury, but in its center, at the top (Fig. 2). It would seem that the increase in surface tension should be accompanied by a raised crown and the formation of non-disturbing contact with the needle. In reality, however, something different happens. The top of the puddle slightly creeps onto the needle, and then under the influence of gravity from it. This first impulse gives rise to vibrations, the drop sways, the heart begins to pulsate.

An interesting detail: when moving the needle from the contour of the puddle to its crown, the pulsation frequency increases.

It must be emphasized that the root heart is by no means a perpetual motion machine. During its operation, energy is consumed, released during the chemical interaction between rue, iron, hydrochloric acid and calcium dichromate. In this reaction, the initial components diverge, and it will stop when, say, an iron needle is “eaten.” You can easily verify the “non-eternity” of a rut heart by taking a thinner wire. Pretty soon the contact between the drop and the wire will cease to be reproduced, since the tip of the wire will be “eaten”. In order for the heart to start working again, you need to move the wire closer to the beam: clearly not a perpetual motion machine!

We decided to take a cardiogram of the local heart. You can’t extract much information from it, except to determine the number of pulsations per second (and you can simply count them by observing a puddle, or, to be sure, use a movie graph). The fact that the rut heart is healthy is simply clearly visible to the eyes: it pulsates rhythmically, without interruption, and clearly does not get tired (the pulsation frequency of our laboratory heart is about 120 beats per minute). Still, it’s interesting to take a cardiogram.

At first, they decided to use the beating heart as a converter of the electrical circuit, recording the moments of turning on and off using a self-recording device. This idea, however, was abandoned, since any electrical injection into the rut heart would inevitably distort its pulsations. They acted differently. A thin beam of light was directed onto the mirror surface of the pulsating puddle, and the rushing beam reflected from it sent a signal to the Samos, which recorded the cardiogram (Fig. 3). The cardiogram shows a sequence of intersecting highs and lows - clear, strict, periodic, without interruptions, the envy of any human heart.
