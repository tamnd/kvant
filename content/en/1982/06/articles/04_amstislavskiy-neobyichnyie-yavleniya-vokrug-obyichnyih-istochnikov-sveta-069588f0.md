Unusual phenomena around ordinary light sources

Probably everyone has had the opportunity to apply so-called crowns - multi-colored rings surrounding a distant source of light, for example the Sun, Moon or street lamp. Their occurrence is associated with the phenomenon of light diffraction - the absorption of various obstacles by light waves.

A lot has been written about diffraction rings, and this is not surprising. They are very beautiful, they are quite easy to obtain, and with their help you can make very delicate measurements, and in a modest home laboratory. It can be expected that the reader «Kvant» will also find this topic of some interest.

What does the star image look like on your phone?

Imagine looking through your phone at the starry sky. It is known from geometric optics that a parallel beam of light coming from an infinite distant source, hitting the lens, must be focused in one of the side foci of the lens, creating an exact image of the source there. However, in reality this is not the case.

How to make a good drug?

To prepare the drug you will need two glass plates the size of four tetrad sheets and lycopodium. Lycopodium can be bought at the pharmacy, and it is convenient to use emulsified photographic plastic 9x12 cm in size as plates.

Apply a drop of machine or vegetable oil to the surface of a clean plate (you can use grains of fat, margarine, butter or semolina - whatever comes to hand) and spread it with a thin layer. Then, using a clean cloth, gently wipe the lubricated surface until thoroughly cleaned. The thin layer of fat that remains serves as an adhesive base to hold part of the coating. Small amounts of lycopodium (about the size of a thimble) are poured onto the treated surface, the plate is tilted and, tapping along the edge, powder is added to its base. A wide trace remains on the plate in the form of a fairly dense and uniform layer of lycopodium. Then the inclination of the plate is changed and powder references are added in a different direction. This procedure is repeated until all sheets of paper are removed and excess lycopodium is removed by tapping the bottom edge of the plate on the table. A properly filled plate should be neither too dense nor too transparent. In appearance it resembles frosted glass of medium density.

If the purchase turns out to be unsuccessful, it doesn’t matter. It is easy to sterilize and reproduce again. However, it must be handled carefully.

If you want to save the drug for repeated use, proceed as follows. The dusty plate is placed on the table with the cover facing up, a paper frame 0.5-1 cm wide, cut to the size of the plate, is placed on it, covered with a second (clean) plate of the same dimensions and pecked at the edges with black paper. The resulting device can be successfully used at any time when there is a desire to use a red diffraction pattern or show it to friends.

Application. What is the wavelength of light?

At the very beginning of the article it was said that very subtle changes can be made using the diffraction pattern. For example, the wavelengths of light. Let's show how this can be done.

As observations and calculations show, the overwhelming majority of light passing through the hole falls into the region of the central light circle (which is assumed to be the diffraction image of the source). Its dimensions are related to the hole diameter d and the light wavelength λ, respectively.

$$d \sin \varphi = 1,22 \lambda,$$

where φ is the angular radius of the first dark ring, and therefore the central light circle. From here, knowing d and φ, you can find λ.

The same relationship is also true for the diffraction pattern obtained using a glass plate dusted with lycopodem. The dynamism d of particles<linked can be easily estimated from a micrograph of a diffraction grating with a period of 0.02 mm. From figure 4 it is clear that d≈0.03 mm≈3·10⁻⁵ m.

To determine the angle φ, we proceed as follows. From black paper we will make a diffraction with a crown made by playing with punctures: a central one, with a diameter of 5-7 mm, and two side ones, located symmetrically relative to the central one and spaced from each other at a given and precise measured distance a, for example a = 3 cm.

Let's use a flashlight as a light source. Let's cover its glass with the diffract we made so that the central hole is exactly opposite the light bulb, and put a sedge of red glass on it. Looking at the light bulb through a dusty plate, it will move away from the source to such a distance L that the first dark spiky diffraction pattern visible to the eye will pass through the side punctures in the diffraction pattern. In this case

$$\sin \varphi \approx tg \varphi = \frac{a}{2} L = \frac{a}{2L}$$

(we took advantage of the fact that for small angles the sines of angles can be replaced by their tangents).

Thus, the wavelength λ can be calculated using the formula

$$\lambda = \frac{d \sin \varphi}{1,22} \approx \frac{da}{2,44L}.$$

The inverse problem can also be solved. If the wavelength is known (for red light λ = 0.65 μm), it is not difficult to determine the diffractive lycopodem. Do it yourself.
