What is a wave?

When throwing stones into the water, look at the circles they form; otherwise such throwing will be empty fun.
Kozma Prutkov. Fruit sections

The concept of a wave seems very interesting to us, and we intuitively associate it with some kind of movement. If we throw a stone into the water, a wave will run across the surface of the water. But if a branch is floating on the water at this time, then we will notice that it does not mix at all in the direction of propagation of the wave, but makes an oscillatory movement up and down. What moves when a wave propagates? Let's look at a few examples.

They say that Empress Elizabeth, daughter of Peter I, wished that the solemn moment of her coronation be marked by an artillery salvo from the Peter and Paul Fortress in the new capital - St. Petersburg. And according to the law, the coronation of Russian tsars took place in the Assumption Cathedral in Moscow. Nowadays, transmitting any information from Moscow to Leningrad is simple: just send a radio signal, and the cannon will fire on time. And then it was necessary to come up with another way of notifying the moment the patriarch placed the crown on the head of the empress.

And along the entire route (about 650 km) from the cathedral in Moscow to the fortress in St. Petersburg, soldiers were deployed at a direct visibility distance (about 100 m) from each other. For this, as is easy to calculate, she added approximately 6,500 soldiers. Each soldier had a flag in his hand. At the moment of coronation, the first soldier waved his flag, the next one repeated his movement, followed by everyone else. The human reaction time is ten seconds, and therefore, after 10-20 minutes, the known about the coronation should reach the artillery in the Peter and Paul Fortress.

What moved from Moscow to St. Petersburg? Each soldier remained standing in his place. The only thing he did was wave the flag. In scientific language, we can say that by raising and lowering his hand with a flag, he changed his state for some time. This change in state moved along the chain of soldiers.

Variables in space of change of state are called waves.

In 1905, strikes began in St. Petersburg, and then a letter was printed that the wave of strikes spread throughout Russia and reached the farthest borders. In this case, a condition spread in which workers quit their jobs in industrial plants and made political and economic demands.

Here's an example of how rumors spread. It is known that a rumor started by even one person can spread throughout the city within a short time. It is significantly less than the time required for this person to visit (or call) all the people in the city. It is clear that hearing carriers themselves may not mix. The lighting state will be mixed. That's exactly what they say - a wave of rumors is spreading throughout the city.

Let us finally look at a physical example. There is a chain of balls (Fig. 1, a) lined up on the billiard table. Another ball hits it so that its speed is directed along the chain. After the impact, the ball will be left, and the last ball will bounce off (Fig. 1, b). We impart momentum to the first ball, and the last one receives it. This happens because a deformation wave propagates along the chain of balls. Upon impact, the first ball is flattened and deforms the neighboring one, the next one, etc. Any intermediate ball on the left and right is acted upon by elastic forces equal in magnitude but opposite in direction (Fig. 1, ​​c), and it remains in place. The elastic force acting on only one side imparts an impulse to the last ball, and it rebounds.

Such deformation waves propagating in elastic media are called sound waves. Consequently, as a result of the impact, a sound wave ran through the chain of balls. It can spread in any elastic body. For example, if a fixed rod (Fig. 2, a) is hit from one end with a hammer, a deformation wave (sound wave) will run along the rod. When this wave reaches the opposite end of the rod, the highest ball there will bounce off (Fig. 2, b). Similarly, you can excite a sound wave in a liquid or gas, only it is more convenient, of course, to use a piston instead of a hammer.

Let's try to find out the mechanism of propagation of sound waves in elastic bodies. In particular, we will find out what the speed of wave propagation depends on. First, let's solve a simplified problem for a model of an elastic body. We will assume that we have a whole of balls with mass $m$, connected by springs with stiffness $k$ (Fig. 3). The size of the balls is small compared to

This model is close to implementation in a solid body. In a crystal lattice, atoms are arranged in such a way that in a state of equilibrium, the vector sum of the forces acting on each atom from all the others is equal to zero. When atoms deviate from the equilibrium position, tension and repulsion forces arise, which are similar to elastic forces*).

Let's give some ball, for example the first one on the left, an impulse directed along the chain (push it). Then, as in the example with biliary balls, a wave of elastic deformation will run along the chain, which after some time will reach the right end of the chain. Since the last ball is connected to the chain by a spring, it will not be able to bounce off at all. The stretched spring will force it to return back, and then, due to its inertia, the ball will retract the spring. Now the deformation wave will run from right to left. In this case, they say that the wave was reflected from the end of the chain and began to propagate in the opposite direction. For the same reasons, it will be reflected from the opposite end, etc. These reflected waves are the conditions for our consideration, and in order to get rid of them, we will consider an “infinite” (that is, without ends) chain. It can be implemented by closing a chain of a large number of balls into a ring (Fig. 4). Along such an “infinite” chain, the wave of elastic deformation will move in a circle without reflections until it fades.

*) At long distances between atoms, predominantly tension forces act, and at close distances, repulsion forces act (quantum mechanics prohibits atoms from piercing each other). Only at some equilibrium distance (approximately equal to the size of the atoms) the interaction forces between atoms become zero.

Let's deflect one of the balls from its equilibrium position (for example, sweep it clockwise) and release it. Then, under the action of the springs connected to it, the position of the ball in space will periodically change. This movement is called oscillatory.

Vibrations play an important role in nature and technology. The pendulum in the clock performs an oscillatory movement; In household electrical appliances, current and voltage fluctuate; the change of day and night, seasons is also an oscillatory process caused by the movement of the Earth. All-rotating mechanisms cause vibrations in the foundation, which must be taken into account during design.

The simplest type of oscillatory motion is harmonic oscillation, when the displacement of a body from the equilibrium position changes over time according to the law

$$\alpha = \alpha_m \sin(2\pi t/T) = \alpha_m \sin 2\pi vt = \alpha_m \sin \omega t,$$

where in the case of an oscillating chain $\alpha$ is the angular deviation of the ball from the equilibrium position. As you can see, the harmonic oscillation is characterized by two quantities (parameters): the maximum deviation (amplitude) $\alpha_m$ and the oscillation period $T$ (the time interval after which the oscillation is completely repeated). The frequency v is equal to the number of oscillations per unit time, the cyclic frequency $\omega = 2\pi v$ is introduced to simplify the mathematical recording of the oscillatory motion, and the value $\varphi = \omega t$, which determines the position of the ball at a given time $t$, is called the oscillation phase.

Let's give an example. Let the ball perform a complete oscillation in time $T = 4$ s; at the initial moment it is in the equilibrium position, and its maximum deviation is $\alpha_m = 0,1$ rad. Then, if it performs a harmonic oscillation, then the dependence of the deviation on time is given by the formula

$$\alpha = 0,1 \sin(\pi t/2).$$

At the moment $t_1 = 1$ s the phase of oscillations is equal to $\varphi_1 = \pi/2$, at the moment $t_2 = 2$ s the phase

The shorter the wavelength, the greater the shift in the phases of arrival on neighboring balls. The maximum “discrepancy” occurs when the phase shift between neighboring balls is equal to n. In this case, the balls oscillate in antiphase (Fig. 6), and correspond to the length $\lambda_{\min} = 2a$.

Let's calculate the frequency with which oscillations occur corresponding to the minimum wavelength (this allows us to estimate the speed of wave propagation in the chain). If we assume that the middle ball oscillates according to the law
$$\alpha_n = \alpha_m \sin \omega t$$
then the vibrations of the previous ball are described by the formula
$$\alpha_{n-1} = \alpha_m \sin (\omega t + \pi),$$
and the subsequent one -
$$\alpha_{n+1} = \alpha_m \sin (\omega t - \pi).$$

Knowing how the ends of the springs move, it is easy to find how their deformation depends on time, and using Hooke’s law $F = kx$ to calculate the elastic forces acting on the middle ball. Resultant force
$$F = kx_m (\sin (\omega t - \pi) - \sin \omega t) + \sin (\omega t + \pi) - \sin \omega t) = -4kx_m \sin \omega t$$
where $x_m = R\alpha_m$ is the maximum linear deviation of the ball ($R$ is the radius of the bulb in which the chain is closed). As you can see, the middle ball oscillates as if it were attached to a single spring, but with quadruple the stiffness. Substituting the value $k_0 = 4k$ into formula (*), we find that when a wave with a minimum length $\lambda_{\min} = 2a$ propagates along the chain, the oscillation frequency is $\omega = 2\sqrt{k/m}$. This is the maximum frequency of vibration of the balls in a closed chain.

As a result, a solid also has a maximum frequency at which atoms can vibrate.

What is the speed of propagation of the wave? If the frequency of oscillations is $\omega$, then their period is $T = 2\pi/\omega$. At propagation speed $v$, the wave travels $l = vT = 2\pi v/\omega$ in time $T$. This path is equal to the wavelength, since shifting in time by a period of $T$ the oscillations will occur in phase. Thus,
$$\lambda = vT = 2\pi v/\omega,$$
and therefore
$$v = \frac{\lambda \omega}{2\pi} = \frac{2}{\pi} a \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

What are the propagation speed and oscillation frequency for longer waves? They can be counted, for example, in the same way (although this is a slightly more difficult task). It turns out that as the wavelength increases, the oscillation frequency decreases, and the wave propagation speed increases, but only slightly. For long wavelengths ($\lambda \gg a$), the propagation speed becomes constant and equal to
$$v_0 = a \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

So the expression we derived with good accuracy gives the speed of propagation of waves in a chain of connected balls for other wavelengths.

Let's return to the solid body. What determines the speed of propagation of sound waves in it? Carrying out analysis with a chain of balls, one can understand that the speed depends on the elastic properties of the materials, the mass of the atoms that make up the substance, and the distance between them. Vibrations of other physical quantities can also propagate in space. If the values ​​of the electric and magnetic field strengths change periodically, then they say that an electromagnetic wave is propagating. Temperature fluctuations can propagate - temperature waves, fluctuations in the magnetic field induction in a substance - magnetization waves, etc. Figuratively speaking, the entire edifice of modern physics is permeated by different types of waves.

Application

Let's imagine that the gun is put on a rod AB located along the diameter of the ring (Fig. 7). One end of the spring is connected to the ball, and the second is fixed at the edge of the rod A. The ball can move freely along the rod, and in a state of equilibrium it is in the center of the ring O. Let us rotate the stake in a horizontal plane with an angular velocity $\omega_0$. Then the ball will disconnect from the center. If its displacement is equal to $r$, then from the side of the spring a force will act on the ball equal to $F = k_0 r$ according to Hooke’s law and directed towards the center of the ring. According to Newton's second law, this force must create a centripetal acceleration $a_ii = \omega_0^2 r$:

$$m\omega_0^2 r = k_0 r.$$

Therefore, in order for the ball to be in a stable state when the ring rotates, it is necessary that the rotation speed be equal to $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k_0/m}$.

In this case, the projection of the ball onto the fixed axis performs a harmonic oscillation with a cyclic frequency equal to the angular velocity of rotation. For example, $x = r \sin \omega_0 t$. Thus, the frequency of harmonic vibrations of a ball of mass $m$ external stiffness $k_0$ is determined by the formula

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k_0}{m}}.$$
