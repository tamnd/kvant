Since the sum of the first $b_m$ numbers of the $m$th packet is equal to
$(2b_m + 1) + (4b_m + 1) + \dots + (2b_m^2 + 1) = b_m^3 + b_m^2 + b_m > b_m^3 + 1,$
that is, greater than the largest number of the $m$th packet, and therefore greater than $a$, we get that $k < b_m$.
That's why
$$r = k + (d_1 + \dots + d_l) < b_m + (d_1 + \dots + d_l) < 2b_m.$$
But $a - r = (c_1 + \dots + c_k) - k = (c_1 - 1) + \dots + (c_k - 1)$. Since each number in the $m$th packet leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by $2b_m$, the numbers $c_1 - 1, c_2 - 1, \dots, c_k - 1$ are divisible by $2b_m$, so $a - r$ is divisible by $2b_m$. Since in this case $r < 2b_m$, $r$ is equal to the remainder of division of $a$ by $2b_m$, that is, $r = 1$; means $k + d_1 + \dots + d_l = 1$, which is impossible. From here we conclude that not a single member of the constructed sequence $(a_n)$ is equal to the sum of several others.

Let us now check that $a_n \leq 100n^{7/2}$ for any $n$.
At $n = 1$ this is obvious. If $a_n$ is in the second packet, then $a_n \leq 1001 < 100 \cdot 2^{7/2} \leq 100n^{7/2}$. Let $a_n$ lie in the $m$th packet and $m \geq 3$. Let's represent $a_m$ as $2kb_m + 1$ ($1 \leq k \leq b_m^2/2$) and the distance is two cases.
$1^\circ$. $k \leq b_m^2 - 1$.
Then
$$a_n \leq 2b_m^2 - 1b_m + 1 < 3b_m^2 - 1b_m = 3b_m - 1 = 3 \cdot 2^{7/2} \cdot (b_m^2 - 1/2)^{7/2}.$$
But $b_m^2 - 1/2 < n$, since $b_m^2 - 1/2$ is the number of terms in the $(m - 1)$-th packet. Therefore $a_n \leq 3 \cdot 2^{7/2} \cdot n^{7/2} < 100n^{7/2}$.
$2^\circ$. $k > b_m^2 - 1$.
It is clear that the number $a_n$ in the $m$-th packet does not exceed its number in the sequence, that is, $k \leq n$. That's why
$$a_n = 2kb_m + 1 < 3kb_m = 3k(b_m^2 - 1)^{5/2} < 3k \cdot k^{5/2} \leq 3n^{7/2}.$$
Thus, the inequality $a_n \leq 100n^{7/2}$ is proven for all $n$. The inequality $a_n \leq n^{10}$ for $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ can be verified directly, and for $n > 2$ it follows from the fact that
$$a_n \leq 100n^{7/2} < 3^{13/2} \cdot n^{7/2} \leq n^{13/2} \cdot n^{7/2}.$$

Let us prove that at least one term of the sequence $(a_n)$ is such that $a_n < 100n^{3/2}$ for all $n = 1, 2, \dots$ is equal to the sum of several others. (Of course, the number 100 here can be replaced by any constant; the exponent 3/2 in our proof requires that it be less than $(\sqrt{5} + 1)/2$).

Consider a straight head table with $10^{30}$ rows and $10^{18}$ columns. Let's assign numbers from $10^{30} + 1$ to $2 \cdot 10^{30}$ to its rows, and numbers from 1 to $10^{18}$ to its columns. At the intersection of the $n$th row and the $k$th column we write the number $c_{n,k} = (a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_k) + a_n$. It does not exceed $a_1 + \dots + a_{10^18} + a_2 \cdot 10^{30} \leq 100 + 100 \cdot 2^{3/2} + \dots + 100 \cdot (10^{18})^{3/2} + 100 \cdot (2 \cdot 10^{30})^{3/2} < 100 \cdot (10^{18})^{3/2} \cdot 10^{18} + 100 \cdot (10^{30})^{3/2} \cdot 2^{3/2} = 10^{47} + 10^{47} \times 2^{3/2} < 4 \cdot 10^{47}$.

Since the table contains $10^{48}$ numbers, each of which can take less than $4 \cdot 10^{47}$ values, any two of these numbers coincide. Let, for example, $c_{n,k} = c_{m,l}$. Since in the $n$th line all numbers are distinct, $n \neq m$. We can assume that $n > m$ (the case $m > n$ is similar). The equality $c_{n,k} = c_{m,l}$ means that
$$(a_1 + \dots + a_k) + a_n = (a_1 + \dots + a_l) + a_m,$$
whence $k < l$ and $a_n = (a_{k+1} + \dots + a_l) + a_m$. Since in this case $l \leq 10^{18} < m$, we obtain that $a_n$ is represented as the sum of several other terms of the sequence.
