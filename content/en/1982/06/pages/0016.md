Ask - we answer

You are right: according to the definition of an integral given in the school textbook (“Algebra and the beginnings of analysis 9-10”, paragraph 60), for the existence of the integral $\int_a^b f(x) dx$ it is required that the subinternal function $f$ be continuous on the segment with endpoints $a, b$ (that is, on $[a; b]$ for $a < b$ and on $[b; a]$ for $a \geq b$).

The answer indicated in «Kvant» is obtained with an overly formatted interpolation that does not take into account the conditions under which the Newton-Leibniz formula is true

$$\int_a^b f(x) dx = F(x) \bigg|_a^b.$$

This formula is true if the function $f$ is continuous on $[a; b]$ and $F'(x) = f(x)$ everywhere on $[a; b]$.

Pozny

$$\int_0^x \frac{dy}{(y+1)^2} = -\frac{1}{y+1} \bigg|_0^x$$

only for $x > -1$ and this inequality is equivalent to the system

$$\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \frac{9x-15}{19} < -\frac{1}{y+1} \bigg|_0^x -1 \\ x > -1. \end{array} \right.$$

The correct answer in the problem is: $\emptyset$.

By the way, from (*) it follows

$$\int_0^x \frac{dy}{(y+1)^2} = \frac{1}{x+1} -1.$$

For $x < -1$ we obtain a paradoxical result: the integral of a positive function, that is, it seems to be the area, is negative (recall that $\int_a^b f(x) dx$ is the area for $a < b$ and $f(x) \geq 0$ on $[a; b]$).

For those familiar with the concept of an improper integral, note that for $x_0 < -1$ the integral $\int_0^x \frac{dy}{(y+1)^2}$ does not exist.
