In 1877, Calte reported the results of his experiments on the combustion of oxygen at the Paris Academy of Sciences. Soon Kalta managed to obtain nitrogen in liquid form.

It is not difficult to understand why the gas cools in Kalthe's experiments. When the gas expands quickly enough, there is no noticeable heat exchange between the system and the environment, so the expansion process is very close to adiabatic. According to the first law of thermodynamics, during an adiabatic process $\Delta U = A$, that is, the change in the internal energy of the gas is equal to the external forces on the gas. When the gas expands, it does positive work ($A' > 0$), and the work of external forces is negative. Therefore, $\Delta U < 0$, which means cooling of the gas (since the internal energy of the gas is proportional to its temperature).

A very important parameter determining gas cooling is pressure. At the end of the 18th century, the Dutch physicist Van Marum, conducting experiments with ammonia in order to test the validity of the Boyle-Mariotte law, noticed the following. Initially, as the volume occupied by the gas decreased, the gas pressure increased proportionally. When the pressure reached 7 atm, something unexpected happened - with further decline, the pressure did not increase. Liquid ammonia appeared in the cylinder, and the amount of liquid increased as it rolled down.

It was along the path of stingative that researchers followed in their attempts to cool gases. However, hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen could not be cooled, no matter how hard they were compressed. Scientists believed that these were “permanent gases,” that is, hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen could not exist in the form of a liquid. It remained a mystery why they were “permanent” gases and others were not. The theory of the Dutch physicist Van der Waals helped solve this problem.

Van der Waals in the second half of the 19th century proposed a fairly simple equation that describes the state of real gases. As is known, the “ideal gas” model corresponds more accurately to real gases, the higher the temperature of the gas and the lower its density. At low temperatures and high densities, the forces of interaction between gas molecules and the fact that the molecules have their own volume play a role. Suggested. The van der Waals equation of state of real gases takes these features into account.
