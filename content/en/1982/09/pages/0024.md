In 1907, Kamerlingh-Onnes collected the results of measurements of gel isotherms in the temperature range from +100°C to -216°C. Soon after this, measurements followed at temperatures of liquid hydrogen (-259°C).

Research at such temperatures required unique equipment and a powerful technological base for burning gases. And here we will tell you about some of the outstanding qualities of Kamerlingh Onnes, which largely determined the conditions of the Leiden laboratory in low-temperature research.

Kamerlingh Onnes realized that the complexity of research technology required skillful and well-trained support staff. He felt that the days of amateur professors conducting experiments using unassuming homemade instruments were irrevocably gone. Penetration into the ever-deeper secrets of nature required the creation of an “industry” of scientific instrument making. Onnes founded a school of instrumentation and glassblowing at his laboratory in 1901. He constantly emphasized that physical observations must be carried out with astronomical precision; and this required a radical change in the technical preparation of the experiment. Essentially, Onnes's laboratory became the prototype and model for research institutes of the 20th century.

Realizing the importance of quickly acquainting scientists with scientific achievements, Kamerlingh Onnes founded the journal “Messages from the Physical Laboratory of Leiden University,” which published reports about the work performed in his laboratory. The doors of the laboratory were widely opened to everyone who wanted to work in the field of cryogenic technology. It was a completely new style, a new type of interaction between physicists from different countries and scientific schools.

Gel liquefaction

Kamerlingh Onnes was primarily interested in the values ​​of the critical parameters of the gel, mainly the temperature $T_k$. By measuring isotherms of the gel at increasingly lower temperatures, he concluded that the value of $T_k$ lay between 5K and 6K. It was decided that the gel could be cooled to a temperature below the critical temperature by rapidly cutting the gel, previously rolled up to a pressure of 100 atm and cooled to the temperature of liquid hydrogen. During an experimental check, Kamerlingh Onnes noticed a dense gray cloud of fog. This would seem to be evidence of gel condensation.

However, the continuation of these experiments showed that the reason for the appearance of the cloud was the presence of an insignificant admixture of hydrogen in the gelyan, which remained despite thorough cleaning. When the experiment was repeated with the onion gel even more thoroughly, no fog formation was observed. Increasing the speed of distribution, Kamerlingh Onnes saw a light cloud of fog. But the fog was very thin, and the cloud disappeared within a second; so the question about the true value of the critical temperature of the gel remained open.
