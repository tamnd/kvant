In an elliptical orbit (Fig. 2,a), the comet moves periodically with variable speed, reaching a maximum each time it passes through perihelion and a minimum when passing through apheia (the point of the elliptical orbit furthest from the Sun).

When moving along a hyperbolic orbit (Fig. 2,b), the comet is adjacent from infinity along an asymptote, when approaching the Sun it turns, hugs it and moves away to infinity along another asymptote. In this case, at a very large distance from the Sun, the speed can be considered constant; when approaching the Sun, its magnitude increases, reaching a maximum at perihelion, then it kills and the comet goes to infinity with the same speed. The role of the Sun, thus, coincides with turning the comet's velocity vector.

A parabolic orbit (Fig. 2,c) has no asymptote and, importantly, when moving away to infinity, the comet’s speed tends to zero.

Hyperbolic and parabolic orbits are related to each other: the comet is adjacent to the Sun from infinity and moves away to infinity. Elliptical orbits are qualitatively different from others: the comet always remains at a limited distance from the Sun.

Thus, the picture of the movement of the captured body is purely asymmetrical in time in relation to the moment of capture.

What if there are more bodies?

Three body problem

It would seem that with the appearance of a third participant in the ensemble of moving bodies, calculation of their motion should not exist. However, this is not so: the complexity of the problem increases sharply when moving from the case of two bodies to the case of a body. The motion of two bodies will be difficult to calculate (although not without difficulty) by anyone who has well mastered the methods of differential and integral calculus. The three-body problem is a significant problem that has been worked on by more than one generation of mathematicians and astronomers.
