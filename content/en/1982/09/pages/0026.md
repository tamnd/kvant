One thing is that even at such a record low temperature, helium remained liquid.

Kamerlingh Onnes in the first experiment showed a very low density of the liquid gel. It turned out to be 8 times lighter than water; this suggests that the light atoms of the gel are at a relatively large distance from each other; It is more difficult to convert such a liquid into a solid city state than ordinary liquids.

For this reason, helium remains liquid down to the lowest temperatures. But even near absolute zero it would remain liquid, since here the laws of quantum mechanics turn it into solidification. Let us recall that from the point of view of classical physics, as the temperature decreases, the thermal vibrations of a part of the substance become weaker and weaker. The presence of mechanical forces must ultimately lead to the solidification of the substance. According to the laws of quantum mechanics, the usual idea of ​​atoms completely stopping at absolute zero turned out to be incorrect - atoms perform so-called “zero” oscillations, which are not associated with thermal motion.

Thus, from the point of view of classical physics, the unique behavior of a liquid gel at low temperatures does not solidify at all down to absolute zero. Thus, at $T = 1,78$ K, solid helium exists at a pressure of $p = 30,000$ atm.

Another very strange property of the liquid gel was noted by Onnes in his first burning experiment. Measuring the density of the liquid gel, he discovered that as the temperature decreases from the boiling point, it increases, that is, the helium is skimmed off. At a temperature of 2.2 K, a sharp maximum occurs, and with further cooling, helium increases in volume and its density decreases. Research carried out by Onnes in the last years of his life revealed anomalies in the physical properties of the gel at temperatures below 2.2 K.

Later, after Onnes's death in 1926, the results of research on the liquid gel carried out by his students were published. Thus, in 1932, Keesom and Clausis discovered a sharp increase in the heat capacity of liquid gel near $T = 2,2$ K. A few years later it became clear that at $T = 2,2$ K a phase transition occurs in liquid helium, characterized by a change in physical properties. At $T > 2,2$ K up to the boiling point, liquid helium appears as a solid liquid (called HeI), but at $T < 2,2$ K it has completely unknown properties. In 1938, Soviet physicist P. L. Kapitsa discovered that at $T < 2,2$ K, liquid helium (named HeII) is characterized by a complete absence of viscosity: it flows through a narrow capillary without resistance, that is, friction forces are strictly equal to zero. This property of HeII was called superfluidity. The theory of this phenomenon was created by L. D. Landuu.

The work of outstanding Soviet physicists was highly appreciated - both of them were awarded the Nobel Prize.

In his experiments with liquid gel, Kamerlingh Onnes actually observed the transition of a liquid gel into a completely new, superfluidity, but was unable to estimate the magnitude of the discovery he made. Let's not forget, however, quantum physics was just taking its first steps; To explain the behavior of a liquid gel at low temperatures, a radical new approach was required, a rejection of classical concepts. Only physicists of the next generation could and did do this. In Onnes's time, the very expectation of a gel was a huge scientific victory and seemed to be the last step on the path to absolute zero.
