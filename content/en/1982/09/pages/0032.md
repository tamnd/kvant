Most recently, on August 8 of this year, the eightieth birthday of the outstanding physicist of our time, Nobel Prize winner Paul A. M. Dirac was celebrated. Many striking achievements of quantum theory are associated with his name, and perhaps the most impressive among them is the prediction of the first antiparticle - the “antielectron”, or, as it is called, the positron. Let us briefly recall how things stood*).

Dirac composed an equation describing the behavior of electrons taking into account the effects of the theory of relativity. This equation perfectly covered a number of experimental facts, but a certain “strangeness” was also discovered: part of its solution corresponded to negative values ​​of the electron energy. What was to be done with them? Should we brush them aside or try to reveal the physical meaning hidden in them? For Dirac the answer was clear. Let us cite his words, spoken, however, in a lecture dedicated to A. Einstein and the theory of relativity, but fully applicable to him: “Anyone who understands the deep harmony connecting natural phenomena and general mathematical principles must feel that if a theory is as beautiful and elegant as Einstein’s theory, then it is, in essence, certainly true.” And he expresses an original and exceptionally bold, especially for that time, hypothesis: the solutions to his equation, seemingly devoid of physical meaning, actually describe an as yet unknown elementary particle, almost indistinguishable from an electron, but with an opposite (positive) electrical contamination of the same magnitude. So positrons were stopped. And the very next year, 1932, they were detected in cosmic rays.
