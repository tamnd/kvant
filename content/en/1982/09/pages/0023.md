As is easy to understand, the presence of a section of the isotherm $I$, corresponding to pressure pressure with decreasing volume, indicates gas instability.

Indeed, let us consider a gas in a cylinder closed by a piston: in a certain section of the isotherm $I$ (between points $a$ and $b$) a decrease in the volume of gas would lead to a decrease in pressure on the piston and, therefore, as stated below. In this area, the van der Waals theory is not applicable - gas condensation into liquid occurs here. During condensation, the pressure remains constant - equal to the saturated vapor pressure - and a horizontal section ($V_a-V_b$) appears on the real isotherm. After all the gas has condensed, we are already dealing with a liquid. It is possible to determine exactly at what value $V$ the condensation process begins only experimentally.

As the temperature increases, the maximum and minimum in the section of the isotherm corresponding to the unstable state come closer, and at some temperatures they coincide (isotherm $II$ at figure 1). This temperature $T_k$ - the critical temperature - is a characteristic value for each gas. At temperatures above $T_k$ the gas cannot condense at any pressure. In other words, $T_k$ is the maximum temperature at which a substance can exist in a liquid state. The critical pressure value $p_k$ corresponding to this temperature is the maximum pressure of saturated vapor (gas), and the value $V_k$ determines the maximum gas density. As the critical point is approached, the difference in the densities of gas and liquid decreases, and in the critical state it becomes zero. Although Kalthe managed to burn oxygen, he continued to be faced with the task of retaining a noticeable amount of oxygen in the liquid phase - after all, the oxygen presented to itself evaporated very quickly. He failed to solve this problem. Further progress in the combustion of gases is associated with the names of Polish physicists Olszewski and Wroblewski, who improved the low-performance Kalte device and obtained liquid oxygen “calmly boiling in a tested tube.” However, despite real attempts, Wroblewski and Olszewski were unable to complete the list of burnt gases—hydrogen was not supplied to the burner. The first to obtain hydrogen in the liquid state was the English physicist Dewar in 1898.

In Holland, the leader in conducting gas combustion research was taken over by Van Der Waals' friend and follower Kamerlingh Onnes, who headed the cryogenic laboratory at Leiden University in 1882. The first step in his scientific activity along this path was the experimental verification of Van Der Waals' theoretical predictions regarding the critical values ​​of temperature and pressure at which a gas can turn into a liquid state.

Kamerlingh Onnes used gases as objects of research, cooling them to lower and lower temperatures and changing methods.
