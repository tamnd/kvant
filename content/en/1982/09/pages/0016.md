From here, taking into account the reservations made earlier, after some transformations we obtain

$$\frac{\Delta v}{u} = \frac{\Delta m_r}{m} - \frac{\Delta m_B}{m} \left( \frac{v}{u} - 1 \right).$$

Let just enough fuel be supplied so that it all reacts with the oxygen in the air. The mass ratio $\Delta m_r / \Delta m_B$, of course, will depend on the chemical state of the fuel, but for a given substance it will be a constant value, which we denote by $\beta$. Then the previous equality takes the form

$$\frac{\Delta v}{u} = \frac{\Delta m_r}{m} \frac{1 + \beta - v / u}{\beta},$$

or

$$\frac{\Delta v}{u} = - \frac{\Delta m}{m} \frac{1 + \beta - v / u}{\beta}.$$

Look externally at equation (1) and (2). They have something in common, namely: in order for the speed of an aircraft to increase ($\Delta v > 0$), its mass must decrease ($\Delta m < 0$). But there is a very important difference: the right side of equation (2) includes the set $1 + \beta - v / u$, which, upon reaching the speed $v = u (1 + \beta)$, becomes equal to zero. This means that an impact-reactive digator, which has “swallowed” a resting impact, cannot accelerate to a speed that is greater than the relative speed of the outflow of the product of the type. The speed of such a digator has a limit that suggests it is impossible. But for a rocket there is no similar limit.

In fact, this has already said everything, but in principle it is possible to obtain exact expressions for the speeds of the rocket and the propeller.

The one who has the summed small increments, that is, integrate, is easy to obtain from expression (1) (considering that at the moment $t_0 = 0$ $v = 0$ and $m = m_0$)

$$\frac{v - 0}{u} = - (\ln m - \ln m_0),$$

or

$$\frac{v}{u} = \ln \frac{m_0}{m}.$$

This is the famous Tsiolkovsky formula. Historians of science say that he spent a lot of time on it: it is always difficult for a pioneer. Today, a literate schoolchild will receive it in a few minutes, and a student at the Faculty of Aeromechanics and Flight Technology of the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology (specially trained for this) will receive it in a few seconds.

Let's analyze the resulting formula. If the loss of rocket mass per unit time is a constant value (we denote it by $\mu = - \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}$), then over time the rocket mass changes according to the law (see Fig. 1)

$$m(t) = m_0 - \mu t.$$

After time $t$, when all the fuel has been ejected, the mass of the rocket will become $m_\tau = m_0 - \mu t$, and its speed will be

$$\frac{v_\tau}{u} = \ln \frac{m_0}{m_\tau}.$$

Obviously, the rocket reaches a higher speed, the smaller the remaining mass. This is sad or uneconomical, but what can you do - that’s nature. The good thing is that a loophole has been found, using which one can achieve “escape velocity” from the planet, albeit at a high cost. But there is another possibility - increasing the relative rate of release of spent fuel.

Here is what Tsiolkovsky himself wrote about this: “In order to receive the most powerful rockets from the outside, it is necessary that particles of combustion products or other waste receive the most powerful combustion waste. It is constant for certain waste substances. Saving energy should not take place here: it is impossible and unprofitable.”

So, we need to build up to increase the ejection speed $u$.
