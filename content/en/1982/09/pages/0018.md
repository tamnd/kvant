If the density $\rho$ is constant, for example, the flow reflects a very simple idea: where the channel is wider, the flow is slower, and where it is narrower, it is faster. Unlike an incompressible liquid, a gas can expand greatly, while its density decreases. Consequently, for the same cross-sectional area of ​​the channel, the gas velocity will be much greater than the liquid velocity. Which is what is required for our purposes. Now it’s clear why gas should be ejected from the rocket.

For simplicity of discussion, we will assume that the gas is ideal and single-phase. This means that it obeys the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation $pV = \frac{m}{M} RT$, or

$$p = \rho \frac{RT}{M}$$

(4)

(here $p$ is pressure, $\rho = m/V$ is density, $T$ is temperature, $M$ is the molar mass of gas, $a$ $R$ is the universal gas constant), and that its internal (thermal) energy is equal to

$$\frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{M} RT.$$

Let us further assume that no chemical reactions occur in the gas, heat does not flow to it anywhere and nothing is obtained from it. Then the flow of total energy of the gas, as well as the flow of mass, must be conserved. How to write this down?

What unit volume of gas has during movement kinetic energy $\rho u^2/2$ and thermal energy $^3/2\rho RT/M$, so the flow of these energies is equal to $(\rho u^2/2)uS$ and $(^3/2\rho RT/M)uS$. In addition, the pressure force $pS$ performs work equal to $pSu$ per unit time, which causes a corresponding increase in energy flow. Thus, the total energy flow is equal to

$$\left( \frac{\rho u^2}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \rho \frac{RT}{M} \right) uS + pSu = \text{const},$$

or, after dividing by $\rho uS$,

$$\frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \frac{RT}{M} + \frac{p}{\rho} = \text{const}.$$

Let us also transform this equation by substituting the expression for pressure from the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation. Then we get

$$\frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \frac{RT}{M} + \frac{RT}{M} = \frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{5}{2} \frac{RT}{M} = \text{const} = \frac{5}{2} \frac{RT_0}{M}$$

(5)

Here we have specified the expression for the constant temperature $T_0$ at which the gas will end ($u=0$).

Now, good job, opa get the “harvest”. From the last equation it is clear that if the gas could be cooled to $T=0$ during movement, then it would acquire the highest speed

$$u_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{5RT_0}{M}}.$$

In this case, all the thermal energy of the gas would be converted into the kinetic energy of its directed motion.

Let's make a numerical estimate. Let's say we managed to heat the lightest monatomic gas helium ($M=4$ kg/kmol) to $T_0=3000$ K. Then it can be accelerated to the speed

$$u_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{5RT_0}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{5 \cdot 8,31 \cdot 10^3 \text{Дж}/(\text{кмоль} \cdot \text{К}) \cdot 3 \cdot 10^3 \text{К}}{4 \text{ кг/кмоль}}} \approx 5,6 \cdot 10^3 \text{м/c} \approx 5,6 \text{ км/c}.$$

This is approximately one and a half times less than the first escape velocity, but the beauty is that the package can pick up speed that is much higher than the rate of gas outflow.
