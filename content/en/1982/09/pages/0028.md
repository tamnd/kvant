A coil of lead wire (Fig. 3) can be connected to the battery by short-circuiting switch 1, and can be short-circuited using switch 2. At the beginning of the experiment, switch 1 was closed and switch 2 was open. The coil, cooled by liquid gel in a Dewar flask, was in a superconducting state. In this case, the current from the battery passing through the coil created a magnetic field around it, which was easily detected by the deviation of the compass needle located outside the Dewar sauce. Then kuchch 2 closed, and kuchch 1 opened. The superconducting winding turned out to be short-circuited. The compass needle, however, remained deflected, indicating the presence of current in the coil, already disconnected from the battery. Watching the needle for several hours (until all the helium from the vessel had evaporated), Onnes did not notice the slightest change in the deviation of the needle. This means the absence of winding resistance - after all, if there was resistance, the energy would be dissipated in the coil, the current would decrease, and this could be noticed by a change in the deflection of the compass needle.

Based on the results of the experiment, Kamerlingh Onnes came to the conclusion that the resistance of a superconducting lead coil is at least 10¹¹ times less than its resistance in the normal (non-superconducting) state.

The longest recorded existence of an undamped current so far is about two years. (This current would have circulated even if it had not been for a break in the weak liquid gel caused by a strike of transport workers.) Then, two years later, there was no noticeable weakening of the magnitude of the circulating current, which allows us to rightfully consider the resistance of the superconductor to be zero.

The practical application of superconductivity was a bright prospect. After all, a superconducting electromagnet does not consume electricity at all, and with its help it would be easy to obtain strong magnetic fields. In a common electromagnet, obtaining a strong magnetic field requires large currents, which leads to the release of a colossal amount of heat in the windings. The latter circumstance limits the maximum magnitude of the magnetic field created by the electromagnet. The use of superconductors in transformers, electric motors, and generators also offered enormous advantages that more than compensated for the need to operate at glial temperatures.

Onnes was the first to create a superconducting magnet. However, disappointment awaited him here. Experiments in Leiden showed that in a magnetic field exceeding a certain threshold value, superconductivity disappears. The magnitudes of these threshold fields turned out to be very small - several hundred ekrasted, which is significantly lower than the fields even in small electric machines. The passage of a strong electric current also destroyed superconductivity - the resulting magnetic fields turned out to be above the threshold even at relatively weak currents.
