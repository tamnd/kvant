Discovery of superconductivity

Having the possibility of obtaining a temperature only one degree above absolute zero, Kamerlingh Onnes began to study the properties of various substances at “helium temperatures”. Determining the electrical resistivity of a sample is relatively easy at any temperature. It is understandable, therefore, that Onnes began by studying the behavior of resistance at low temperatures. Moreover, at the beginning of the century this issue was very important.

There were theories that gave completely opposite predictions regarding the behavior of electrical conductivity at very low temperatures.

According to one, at absolute nULE, electrical resistance should disappear. Indeed, the electrical conductivity of a metal is proportional to the time between two successive collisions of an electron with a vibrating atom. As the temperature decreases, the amplitude of atomic vibrations decreases. Consequently, collisions between electrons and atoms become more rare, and conductivity increases; so at $T=0$ the resistance becomes zero.

On the other hand, a hypothesis was put forward according to which electrical conductivities at low temperatures are associated with atoms, which leads to infinite resistance at $T=0$.

Kamerlingh Onnes began measuring the resistance of platinum at low temperatures. The results he obtained did not fit within the framework of existing theories - as the temperature decreased, the resistance of platinum remained constant. However, Onnes drew attention to the fact that the value of the resistance of various samples, other things being equal, was the lower, the higher the metal turned out to be. From this he will conclude that the existence of resistance at $T \rightarrow 0$ - the so-called residual resistance - is due to impurities, and a pure metal at zero temperature must have infinite conductivity. (Onnes, however, mistakenly believed that the resistance of pure metals should decrease to zero already at helium temperatures.) The task, therefore, was to study the possibility of processing the best of most metals, in particular the best platinum. The values ​​of the specific residual resistance of gold obtained from measurements were much lower than those of platinum, and increased with increasing processing frequency. Kamerlingh Onnes also turned to the study of the behavior of the resistance of mercury. The fact is that this substance is in the liquid phase at room temperature, and through successive distillation it is possible to obtain more and more pure mercury.

The results of experiments with rttuto were unexpected. The resistance of mercury changed with decreasing temperature, but at a temperature slightly below the boiling point of the liquid gel it dropped sharply and became immeasurably small.

On April 28, 1911, Kamerlingh Onnes reported the results of his experiments to the Royal Netherlands Academy. What he discovered is what he called superconductivity.

In 1913, in his lecture upon receiving the Nobel Prize, Onnes suggested that the phenomenon of superconductivity may be associated with the quantization of energy, discovered by Planck at the end of the last century. However, about half a century had to pass before a complete theory of superconductivity was constructed.

Having discovered the transition to a previously perfect state, Kamerlingh Onnes set out to thoroughly study its properties. The first question that came up was: how low does the resistance of the sample become in the superconducting phase? Onnes needed to learn how to measure extremely low resistivities.
