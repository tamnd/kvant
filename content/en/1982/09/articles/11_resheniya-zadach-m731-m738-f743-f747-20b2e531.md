F744. The spacecraft is a rigid thin-walled sphere of radius $R=2$ m, filled with gas. Inside the apparatus there is a ball of radius $r=R/2$, filled with the same gas as the eighth apparatus, but at higher pressure. The ball touches the inner surface of the apparatus. Consider the mass of the shells; temperatures are considered unchanged.

When changes occur inside the apparatus, the position of the center of mass (centre of mass) of the apparatus does not change.

After the balloon burst, c. m. of the apparatus is determined by how. m. sphere of radius $R$, filled with gas with density $\varrho$, where $\varrho$ is the gas density in the sphere after damage to the ball.

The fact that after the damage to the ball the entire apparatus was swept away to a distance $a$ means that... m. apparatus with a solid ball is located at a distance $a$ from the center of the sphere (see figure). From this condition we find a correspondence between $\varrho_1$ and $\varrho_2$:

$$\frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \varrho_1 a g = \frac{4}{3} \pi \frac{R^3}{8} \left( \frac{R}{2} - a \right) g \Rightarrow \frac{\varrho_2}{\varrho_1} = \frac{R+14a}{R-2a}.$$

The gas density in the apparatus after the balloon burst is determined by the condition

$$\frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \varrho_1 + \frac{4}{3} \pi \frac{R^3}{8} \left( \varrho_2 - \varrho_1 \right) = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \varrho \Rightarrow \varrho = \frac{7}{8} \varrho_1 + \frac{1}{8} \varrho_2.$$

The gas pressure $p$ in the apparatus after damage to the ball can be found from the condition $\frac{p}{p_1} = \frac{\varrho}{\varrho_1}$, where $p_1$ is the initial gas pressure inside the thin-walled sphere.

Thus,

$$\frac{p}{p_1} = \frac{\varrho}{\varrho_1} = \frac{7}{8} + \frac{1}{8} \frac{\varrho_2}{\varrho_1} = \frac{R}{R-2a} = 2,$$

that is, the gas pressure inside the apparatus increased by 2 times.

F745. The telloisulated vessel is divided into two parts by a telloisulated piston, which can move into the vessel without friction. The left side of the container contains 1 mol of an ideal monatomic gas, and the right side contains a vacuum. The piston contains a spring with a right stack, the length of which in the free state is equal to the length of the spring. Neglecting the capacity of the fluid, piston and spring, determine the capacity of the system.

Let $T_1$ be the initial temperature of the gas under the piston, $T_2$ be the temperature of the gas after the system is informed by the body ΔQ. Since there is no friction and the vessel is telloisulated, the entire tello ΔQ goes towards changing ΔU within the energy of the system, that is, ΔQ = ΔU. The change within the energy of the system consists of a change within the energy of the gas and a change in the scalar potential energy (we neglect the capacitance of the vessel, piston and spring).

The internal energy of one mole of a monatomic ideal gas when heated from temperature $T_1$ to temperature $T_2$ increases by

$$\Delta U_1 = \frac{3}{2} R \left( T_2 - T_1 \right).$$

Potential energy of scalars changes by quantities

$$\Delta U_2 = \frac{k}{2} \left( x_2^2 - x_1^2 \right),$$

From the piston equilibrium condition it follows that

$$p = \frac{F}{s} = \frac{kx}{s} \Rightarrow x = \frac{ps}{k}$$

(p - gas pressure, s - piston area, k - spring elasticity). According to the Mendeleev-Clapeyron law, for 1 mol of a monatomic gas there is a correspondence to the corresponding system of co.

Let the battery terminals be shorted to the load and the voltage across the terminals be $U$. Let's find the current flowing through the load, that is, we'll establish the current-voltage characteristic of the battery.

Do spheres must have electrons with energies $W \geq eU$. In the Stationary mode (at constant $U$), the charge of the sphere remains unchanged. Consequently, the number of electrons hitting the sphere every minute is equal to the number of electrons returning during the same time through the load to a piece of radioactive substance.

At $eU \leq W_{\min}$ all electrons must be from the sphere, the charge transferred by them per unit time is the maximum current that such a battery can produce, that is

$$I_{\max} = ev.$$

At a higher voltage, when $W_{\min} < eU < W_{\max}$, only those electrons whose energy is $W \geq eU$ fall on the sphere; these electrons determine the current flowing through the load (the remaining electrons return to the center of the sphere, minus the load). The fraction of these electrons from the total number of electrons emitted from a piece of radioactive substance is equal to

$$n = \frac{W_{\max} - eU}{W_{\max} - W_{\min}}$$

(due to the uniform energy distribution of electrons). The current flowing through the load is equal to

$$I = evn = ev \frac{W_{\max} - eU}{W_{\max} - W_{\min}}.$$

The lowest voltage $U_{\max}$ at the open battery terminals (battery emf) is determined by the condition

$$eU_{\max} = W_{\max} \Rightarrow U_{\max} = W_{\max}/e.$$

As soon as this voltage is reached, the charge of the sphere and the piece of radioactive substance stops changing.
