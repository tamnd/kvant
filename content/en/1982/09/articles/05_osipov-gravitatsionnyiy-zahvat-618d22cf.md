Gravity Capture

How was the solar system formed? This question has captured the minds of scientists, probably from the very moment it became clear in what order the Sun, Earth, and other planets are located in cosmic space. The ancients believed that the central figure of this cosmic community was the Earth. However, the true picture turned out to be different: planets, including ours, are formed around the Sun in orbits that are close to circular and located approximately in the same plane. How did this round dance come about?

In 1796, the French mathematician and mechanic Pierre Simon Laplace put forward his famous cosmogonical hypothesis, which offered an answer to this question. Laplace believed that the solar system was formed from a swirling gaseous nebula. Under the influence of attractive forces between gas particles, the nebula, with the law of conservation of angular momentum, rotated faster and faster. At some point, for the particles located at the equator of the rotating structure, the force of attraction to its center turned out to be equal to the centrifugal force of inertia, so the orbits of these particles no longer narrowed; other particles continued to approach the center. The nebula solidified to a plane perpendicular to its rotation axis. Then, according to Laplace's assumption, it divided into a central clot, from which the Sun was subsequently formed, and peripheral rings, which later condensed into planets.

Laplace's hypothesis well explained the concentric nature of planetary orbits. But not only planets participate in the circumsolar round dance. Together with them, comets also form around our world. Many of them have orbits that are very different from circular ones; they are elongated ellipses and, what is especially noteworthy, do not lie in the plane of planetary orbits, but are oriented rather chaotically. How to explain such a disgram?

In 1770, astronomers observed a comet called Lexel's Comet. At the expected date of her next appearance, she was not detected at the pre-calculated point in the sky. As it turned out, Jupiter was to blame for this: the comet was abruptly knocked out of its orbit by the powerful attraction of this giant planet at the moment when it passed its environs.

And so, faced with the need to include comets in his picture of the origin of the Solar System, Laplace boldly put forward a proposal: among the bodies that compose it, there are not only “natural” children of the Sun, but also “adopted” ones; comets are aliens from outer space; those of them that, singing through the solar system, passed close to some circular planet, sharply, like Lexel’s comet, changed their orbit and turned from their previous path directed to infinity into elliptical circumsolar orbits.

True, Laplace did not prove his proposal, apparently relying on its self-evident plausibility. This combination of plausibility and lack of evidence predetermined the hypothesis of gravitational capture a difficult fate in the history of science.

Hyperbolic and parabolic orbits are related to each other: the comet is adjacent to the Sun from infinity and moves away to infinity. Elliptical orbits are qualitatively different from others: the comet always remains at a limited distance from the Sun.

What if there are more bodies?

Three body problem

It would seem that with the appearance of a third participant in the ensemble of moving bodies, calculation of their motion should not exist. However, this is not so: the complexity of the problem increases sharply when moving from the case of two bodies to the case of a body. The motion of two bodies will be difficult to calculate (although not without difficulty) by anyone who has well mastered the methods of differential and integral calculus. The three-body problem is a significant problem that has been worked on by more than one generation of mathematicians and astronomers.

It is easy to write the equation of motion for each of the bodies. But in the general case, it was not possible to solve the resulting equation, to find the trajectories of bodies given their positions and speeds at a certain moment in time. Moreover, it has now been strictly proven that it is impossible to find a general solution to the problem! It also seems extremely difficult to give a geometric description of the solution.

Well, what if you approach the three-body problem without the desire to find its exact solution and try to determine at least the types of their motion? With this approach, some results can be achieved.

For the sake of clarity, there are several existing questions similar to what we did when analyzing the two-body problem. Let one of the three bodies significantly exceed the others in mass. And let at some period of time both light bodies (comets) benefit from the heavy one (Sun). Let us consider moments of time infinitely further from this period, past or future in relation to it, and we will ask: where is this or that comet located at such moments - at an infinite distance from the Sun or in its limited vicinity? Depending on the answer to this question, classify the types of movement.

A similar classification for the three-body problem was proposed by the French astronomer J. Chazy (1882-1955). He divided all mental movements into four types:

tin hyperbolic (Fig. 3,a), when in infinite further time and future both comets have passed infinitely far from the Sun;

type hyperbolic-elliptical (Fig. 3,b), when one of the comets is at a limited distance from the Sun, and the second comes from infinity and goes to infinity;

type of orangic movements (Fig. 3,c), when all three bodies are at limited distances from each other for an infinite time;

The type of orangic movements (Fig. 3,e), is very exotic, when there is a certain limited region of space where all three bodies from time to time find themselves simultaneously, but there is no such limited region where they would remain forever.

Based on this classification, for the case of three bodies the concepts of partial and complete capture are defined. We speak of partial capture if in the past the movement belongs to the hyperbolic type, and in the future it belongs to the hyperbolic-elliptic type.

K. A. Sitnikov: partial capture is possible!

Several years have passed. Schmidt's example has been moved. The results showed that the scientist’s main idea was correct - a celestial body flying towards the Sun at great speed can “push” another body flying nearby from a hyperbolic orbit to an elliptical one, and then retire to infinity. Moreover, they found that this idea can be used to construct trajectories by purely theoretical means, without numerical integration.

In 1952, K. A. Sitnikov constructed an analytical primer of partial capture, and in 1956, V. M. Alekseev - examples of exchange. It turned out that such examples do not at all represent isolated exceptions - small changes in the parameters of celestial bodies did not lead to a change in the qualitative picture of the phenomenon.

But the possibility of combining other types of movements remained in doubt. It was unknown whether oscillating movements existed at all, or whether complete capture was possible. The matter was complicated by the fact that such examples, even if it were possible to discover them, would represent an exceptional case.

Isosceles three body problem

Already using the example of the two-body problem, we could see how much a characteristic special case sometimes means for understanding a general problem. When analyzing the two-body problem, we assumed that one of them was significantly heavier than the other. This made it much easier to classify their movements. The classification remains valid for the general case, but if we immediately accepted it, the explanation would require more conditions.

In the analysis of oscillating movements, success was also due to the model example. Proposed by A. N. Kolmogorov, it was later called the equilateral three-body problem. The word “equilateral” expresses a characteristic feature of the example: at each moment of time, the triangle forming the triangle turns out to be equilateral. The two bodies that make up the base of this triangle have the same, solid mass. Let's call them "stars"; the third, much lighter, is naturally called a “speck of dust.”

The stars in Kolmogorov’s example move in a plane in elliptical orbits around their center of mass, while a speck of dust moves along a perpendicular to the plane, restoring at this center (Fig. 5).

In 1960, Sitnikov showed that the movement of a speck of dust can be of an oscillating nature, that is, it can represent oscillations with a changing range, and this range at times reaches remarkably large values.

Such fluctuations can also occur with a constant scope - if the stars move in a circle, as if chasing each other. But if the orbits of the stars are elliptical and due to this the stars either come together or diverge, at least slightly, an effective swing occurs in the movement of the dust particle.

This example proved the existence of oscillating motions in the three-body problem.

V. M. Alekseev: full capture of opportunity!

The starting position for the last bakton's maneuver was the same equilateral three-body problem. Investigating it, V. M. Alekseev proved in 1968: the initial position and speed of a speck of dust can be chosen so that its subsequent movement will belong to the type of limited or oscillating movements, and full movement and speed of a speck of dust can be acquired even when it arrives from infinity.

In general, the picture of the movement looks like this: a speck of dust approaches from the end to the plane of elliptical motion of two massive bodies, produces this plane, and during this time the massive bodies come closer together, the forces of mutual attraction between all three participants in the movement increase so much that they tighten the speck of dust, turn it back and force it to subsequently oscillate with a limited or unlimited scope. The first of these two possibilities corresponds to complete capture, the second to oscillation capture, as the researchers say.

As usual, the very last steps are the most difficult. All those examples of motion that had a different character in the past and future, which were built before 1968, seemed to survive from a huge ocean of uncertainty, which is the entire set of possible motions in the three-body problem. Alekseev’s research shed light on how this totality is structured.

Alekseev’s result ended many years of debate about Shazi’s statement: it turned out to be completely wrong! The most controversial combinations of heterogeneous types from the Shazi classification, which describe the movement of three bodies in the past and future, turned out to be feasible and received a structural analogical justification.

Comet Kirnca-Qui: captured by Jupiter?

It cannot be said that with the refutation of Shazi’s statement in the problem of gravitational capture, no ambiguities remained. An attentive reader could notice some of them even with the help of this short article.

Well, for example: the possibility of capturing a comet, as Laplace saw it - due to its passage mismo of Jupiter or another giant planet? There is reason to think that the answer is yes.

Here's what that grip might look like. A comet, approaching Jupiter at a hyperbolic (relative to the Sun) speed, is slowed down by it and transferred to an elliptical orbit around the Sun so that at the next meeting with Jupiter it will move to another elliptical orbit, etc.
