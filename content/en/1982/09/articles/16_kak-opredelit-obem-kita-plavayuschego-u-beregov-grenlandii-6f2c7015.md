How to determine the volume of a whale swimming off the coast of Greenland?
This problem was proposed to teams of schoolchildren at the IV Moscow Tournament of Young Physicists. Team No. 91 presented 91 solutions. Some of them represent the attention of readers.

In all solutions, the density of the whale is assumed to be equal to the density of water, from which
$$V_{\text{кита}} = M_{\text{кита}} / \rho_{\text{H}_2}\text{O}.$$

"Spring"
We take a spring of a given stiffness $k$. We attach the WHALE to it. We measure the oscillation period $T$. Then the mass of the WHALE is equal to
$$M_{\text{кита}} = \frac{T^2 k}{4\pi^2}.$$
This is where its volume is found.

"Decoy"
An inflatable toy WHALE is purchased from the Toy House. It is pressed, and its volume changes. Then he is delivered to the shores of Greenland and released to swim. Since the conditions of the problem do not specify which specific CIT is meant, this CIT satisfies the conditions. Its volume is known in advance.

"Scale"
In task No. 1 (see p. 57) it was necessary to change the parameters of the galaxy. After this, it is easy to see that the volume of CIT compared to the volume of the galaxy can be neglected.

"Moon"
The whale, like any body with mass, acts on the Moon with force
$$F = \gamma \frac{M_{\text{кита}} \cdot M_{\text{Луны}}}{R^2},$$
where $R$ is the distance from WHALE to the Moon (known). It remains to change the force $F$ by changing the orbit of the Moon under the influence of CHAPE.

"Genually simple method"
KEITH needs to be cocked. Put it on the scale and WEIGH IT!
Then we query to find its volume.

"Very simple method"
We need to change the WHALE with a centimeter!

Our Readers' Assignment
(see «Kvant» No. 7, p. 60)

In the $k$th column, tables are considered that construct the numbers $k + 9n$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$), that is, numbers that give a remainder $k$ when divided by 9. Since $(k + 9n)^2 = k^2 + 9(2nk + 9n^2)$, the squares of all these numbers also give the same remainder when divided by 9 and therefore appear in the same column.

Let an arbitrary number $a = a_1 + 10a_2 + 100a_3 + \dots$ be given. Let us denote the sum of its digits by $\Sigma(a)$, then $\Sigma(a) = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \dots = a - 9(a_2 + 11a_3 + \dots)$, therefore the numbers $a$ and $\Sigma(a)$ give the same remainders when divided by 9 and therefore stand in the same column.

After the first squaring of an arbitrary number $a$ from the $k$th column and adding the digits of the result, we get the number $\Sigma(a^2)$, the remainder of which when divided by 9 is:

$$k = 1 \ 2 \ 3 \ 4 \ 5 \ 6 \ 7 \ 8 \ 9$$

$$\Sigma(a^2) = 1 \ 4 \ 9 \ 7 \ 7 \ 9 \ 13 \ 10 \ 9.$$

When these operations are repeated, a number from the 1st, 3rd, 6th, 8th or 9th column remains in the same column; numbers from the 4th go to the 7th and back; from 2nd and 5th to 7th, then to 4th, again 7th, etc.

We will show that after enough numbers are repeated, the result will become less than 27. If $a - m$ is a digit number, then $a \geqslant 10^{m-1}$. On the other hand, the number $a^2$ has in this case at most 2$m$ marks; hence $\Sigma(a^2) \leqslant 9 \cdot 2m = 18m$. But $10^{m-1} > 18m$ with $m \geqslant 3$ (prover), therefore, if $a$ is written with at least a third sign, $\Sigma(a^2) < a$. This means that by repeating our operations, we can reduce the number until it becomes two values. Further, for any two numbers $a$ we have $\Sigma(a^2) \leqslant 18 \cdot 2 = 36$, and the square of any number $a \leqslant 36$ does not exceed $36^2 = 1296$, therefore for the sum of its digits we have $\Sigma(a^2) < \Sigma(999) = 27$ (999 is not a square).

Now it remains to consider numbers less than 27. For the 1st and 8th columns these are 19, 10 and 1: $\Sigma(19^2) = 10$, $\Sigma(10^2) = 1$, and 1 generates the sequence 1, 1, 1... For the 3rd, 6th and 9th columns these are 18 and 9: $\Sigma(18^2) = 9$, $\Sigma(9^2) = 9$, which generate the sequence is 9, 81, 9, ... For the 4th column it is 22 and 13: $\Sigma(22^2) = 16$, $\Sigma(16^2) = 13$, $\Sigma(13^2) = 16$, which generate the sequence 13, 169, 16, 256, 13, ... For the 2nd, 5th and 7th columns it is also the same sequence. For comparison, he advises the reader to solve problem No. 2 from G. Steinghuzz’s book “One Hundred Problems” (M., “Nuka”, 1976).
