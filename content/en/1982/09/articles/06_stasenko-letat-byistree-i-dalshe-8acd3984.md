For thousands of years, man dreamed of flying and achieved this, “relying on the power of his mind,” using the amount of science and technology. The aircraft, in fact, is the crown of technical civilization. It is no coincidence that its development and construction are not available to all countries today. We can rightfully be proud of the contribution of Russian scientists to the realization of this universal dream. One of them was Konstantin Eduardovich Tsiolkovsky, who first substantiated the possibility of using packet art for interplanetary communications.

Let's consider some aspects of the physics of an aircraft, in particular the rocket engine developed by Tsiolkovsky and described in his scientific and popular articles and books.

Why exactly the package?

In a certain sense, any movement is reactive. “The steamship pushes away the body, the airship and the aeroplane - the air, the man and the horse - the globe... We usually observe such reactive phenomena on earth. That’s why they could never encourage dreams and research... The rocket will contain within itself substances for waste” (K. E. Tsiolkovsky).

They started making rockets a long time ago, but at first it was “now a party, then a shooting,” as in the song. Only Tsiolkovsky realized that the package is the only means to achieve any speed in principle, and therefore necessary to overcome the force of gravity. “Only reason and science could indicate the transformation of these phenomena into gradual, almost incomprehensible feelings,” wrote Tsiolkovsky.

So why the package? Let’s try to understand this by comparing it with another aircraft that would seem to be closest to it “in spirit”—direct-flow impact propulsion. But first, let’s talk about the package itself (Fig. 1). Let us imagine that at some arbitrary moment in time $t$ the packet had a speed $v$. We will carefully divide the package into two parts - the one that, after a short period of time $\Delta t$, is “going” to fly back (spent fuel packages), denoting its mass as $\Delta M$, and the “remainder” with a mass $m$, which will fly further, but at a different speed, equal to $v + \Delta v$. The speed of the flying part relative to the “remnant” will be denoted by $u$, then its speed relative to the earth will be observed to be equal to $(v + \Delta v) - u$. Since the separation occurred under the influence of internal forces, the total impulse of the package did not change *):

$$(m + \Delta M)v = m(v + \Delta v) + \Delta M(v + \Delta v - u),$$

or

$$mv + \Delta M \cdot v = mv + m\Delta v + \Delta M \cdot v + \Delta M \cdot \Delta v - \Delta M \cdot u.$$

The terms underlined by straight lines (one or two) will cancel each other out, and the term underlined

Here and further we will consider all increments - $\Delta t$, $\Delta v$, $\Delta M$ and others - to be very small, so that their product of type $\Delta M \cdot \Delta v$ will be very small, or, as mathematicians say, of the second order of smallness. So, what remains of the law of conservation of momentum? Here's the equality:

$$m\Delta v = u\Delta M.$$

If we take into account that the discarded mass $\Delta M$ is exactly equal to the lost mass of the packets: $\Delta M = -\Delta m$, then the resulting equality can be rewritten in the form

$$\frac{\Delta v}{u} = -\frac{\Delta m}{m}.$$

Now let's carry out analytical calculations for the impact of the motor divigator (Fig. 2). Here he is “preparing” at the moment of time $t$ to “swallow” a portion of air with a mass $\Delta m_B$ and, having consumed a mass of fuel $\Delta m_r$, throw out the resulting mixture with a relative speed $u$. In this case, of course, the change in the mass of the aircraft itself will be equal to $\Delta m = -\Delta m_r$. Let us write down the law of conservation of momentum for the engine dividing system - fuel - air:

$$\Delta m_B \cdot 0 + (m + \Delta m_r)v = (\Delta m_B + \Delta m_r)(v + \Delta v - u) + m(v + \Delta v)$$

(here $v$ is the speed of the vehicle at time $t$, $v + \Delta v$ is its new speed at time $t + \Delta t$, the first term on the left reflects the fact that the absorbed divi-returning motor has ended).

From here, taking into account the reservations made earlier, after some transformations we obtain

$$\frac{\Delta v}{u} = \frac{\Delta m_r}{m} - \frac{\Delta m_B}{m} \left( \frac{v}{u} - 1 \right).$$

Let just enough fuel be supplied so that it all reacts with the oxygen in the air. The mass ratio $\Delta m_r / \Delta m_B$, of course, will depend on the chemical state of the fuel, but for a given substance it will be a constant value, which we denote by $\beta$. Then the previous equality takes the form

$$\frac{\Delta v}{u} = \frac{\Delta m_r}{m} \frac{1 + \beta - v / u}{\beta},$$

or

$$\frac{\Delta v}{u} = - \frac{\Delta m}{m} \frac{1 + \beta - v / u}{\beta}.$$

Look externally at equation (1) and (2). They have something in common, namely: in order for the speed of an aircraft to increase ($\Delta v > 0$), its mass must decrease ($\Delta m < 0$). But there is a very important difference: the right side of equation (2) includes the set $1 + \beta - v / u$, which, upon reaching the speed $v = u (1 + \beta)$, becomes equal to zero. This means that an impact-reactive digator, which has “swallowed” a resting impact, cannot accelerate to a speed that is greater than the relative speed of the outflow of the product of the type. The speed of such a digator has a limit that suggests it is impossible. But for a rocket there is no similar limit.

In fact, this has already said everything, but in principle it is possible to obtain exact expressions for the speeds of the rocket and the propeller.

The one who has the summed small increments, that is, integrate, is easy to obtain from expression (1) (considering that at the moment $t_0 = 0$ $v = 0$ and $m = m_0$)

$$\frac{v - 0}{u} = - (\ln m - \ln m_0),$$

or

$$\frac{v}{u} = \ln \frac{m_0}{m}.$$

This is the famous Tsiolkovsky formula. Historians of science say that he spent a lot of time on it: it is always difficult for a pioneer. Today, a literate schoolchild will receive it in a few minutes, and a student at the Faculty of Aeromechanics and Flight Technology of the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology (specially trained for this) will receive it in a few seconds.

Let's analyze the resulting formula. If the loss of rocket mass per unit time is a constant value (we denote it by $\mu = - \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}$), then over time the rocket mass changes according to the law (see Fig. 1)

$$m(t) = m_0 - \mu t.$$

After time $t$, when all the fuel has been ejected, the mass of the rocket will become $m_\tau = m_0 - \mu t$, and its speed will be

$$\frac{v_\tau}{u} = \ln \frac{m_0}{m_\tau}.$$

Obviously, the rocket reaches a higher speed, the smaller the remaining mass. This is sad or uneconomical, but what can you do - that’s nature. The good thing is that a loophole has been found, using which one can achieve “escape velocity” from the planet, albeit at a high cost. But there is another possibility - increasing the relative rate of release of spent fuel.

Here is what Tsiolkovsky himself wrote about this: “In order to receive the most powerful rockets from the outside, it is necessary that particles of combustion products or other waste receive the most powerful combustion waste. It is constant for certain waste substances. Saving energy should not take place here: it is impossible and unprofitable.”

So, we need to build up to increase the ejection speed $u$.

If the density $\rho$ is constant, for example, the flow reflects a very simple idea: where the channel is wider, the flow is slower, and where it is narrower, it is faster. Unlike an incompressible liquid, a gas can expand greatly, while its density decreases. Consequently, for the same cross-sectional area of ​​the channel, the gas velocity will be much greater than the liquid velocity. Which is what is required for our purposes. Now it’s clear why gas should be ejected from the rocket.

For simplicity of discussion, we will assume that the gas is ideal and single-phase. This means that it obeys the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation $pV = \frac{m}{M} RT$, or

$$p = \rho \frac{RT}{M}$$

(4)

(here $p$ is pressure, $\rho = m/V$ is density, $T$ is temperature, $M$ is the molar mass of gas, $a$ $R$ is the universal gas constant), and that its internal (thermal) energy is equal to

$$\frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{M} RT.$$

Let us further assume that no chemical reactions occur in the gas, heat does not flow to it anywhere and nothing is obtained from it. Then the flow of total energy of the gas, as well as the flow of mass, must be conserved. How to write this down?

What unit volume of gas has during movement kinetic energy $\rho u^2/2$ and thermal energy $^3/2\rho RT/M$, so the flow of these energies is equal to $(\rho u^2/2)uS$ and $(^3/2\rho RT/M)uS$. In addition, the pressure force $pS$ performs work equal to $pSu$ per unit time, which causes a corresponding increase in energy flow. Thus, the total energy flow is equal to

$$\left( \frac{\rho u^2}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \rho \frac{RT}{M} \right) uS + pSu = \text{const},$$

or, after dividing by $\rho uS$,

$$\frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \frac{RT}{M} + \frac{p}{\rho} = \text{const}.$$

Let us also transform this equation by substituting the expression for pressure from the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation. Then we get

$$\frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \frac{RT}{M} + \frac{RT}{M} = \frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{5}{2} \frac{RT}{M} = \text{const} = \frac{5}{2} \frac{RT_0}{M}$$

(5)

Here we have specified the expression for the constant temperature $T_0$ at which the gas will end ($u=0$).

Now, good job, opa get the “harvest”. From the last equation it is clear that if the gas could be cooled to $T=0$ during movement, then it would acquire the highest speed

$$u_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{5RT_0}{M}}.$$

In this case, all the thermal energy of the gas would be converted into the kinetic energy of its directed motion.

Let's make a numerical estimate. Let's say we managed to heat the lightest monatomic gas helium ($M=4$ kg/kmol) to $T_0=3000$ K. Then it can be accelerated to the speed

$$u_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{5RT_0}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{5 \cdot 8,31 \cdot 10^3 \text{Дж}/(\text{кмоль} \cdot \text{К}) \cdot 3 \cdot 10^3 \text{К}}{4 \text{ кг/кмоль}}} \approx 5,6 \cdot 10^3 \text{м/c} \approx 5,6 \text{ км/c}.$$

This is approximately one and a half times less than the first escape velocity, but the beauty is that the package can pick up speed that is much higher than the rate of gas outflow.
