The most important molecule

Consuming things are produced biologically. I think Jim Watson made a discovery comparable to what Rutherford made in 1911.

Max Delbrück
(from a letter to Niels Bohr dated April 14, 1953)

Of all that surrounds us, life seems to be the most necessary. We are accustomed to the fact that it is always around us and in ourselves, and we have lost the ability to be surprised. But almost into the forest, look at the trees, grass, flowers, birds and ants as if you were seeing them for the first time, and you will be overcome by a feeling of helplessness in the face of the great mystery of life. Is there really something in common in all this, something that unites all living beings, be it a person or a microbe invisible to the eye? What is the reason for the continuity of life, its revival again and again from generation to generation? These questions are as old as the hills, but only we, living in the second half of the 20th century, were lucky enough to learn the answers for the first time.

The revolutionary transformations that took place in physics in the first third of our century had a profound impact on other areas of science, primarily on chemistry and biology. However, the most important thing on the path to creating new physics was Rutherford's discovery of the atomic nucleus in 1911. The very existence of Rutherford's atoms is in blatant contradiction with classical theory. The period of creation of a new physics, now known as quantum mechanics, began.

This new theory, the development of which was begun by Planck, Einstein and Bohr, found its final and clear formulation in the famous Schrödinger equation. This equation not only allows physicists to solve all the “puzzles” that have arisen in the field of atomic spectra, but, when supplemented by the Pauli principle, it has placed the whole of chemistry on a solid theoretical foundation. Finally, the meaning of the atomic number in the periodic table has become clear, the true meaning of valency is clear, and the nature of the chemical bond has been clarified.

The physicists had a feeling of omnipotence. And their gaze turned to the connection of connections - to life itself. Will new physics help unravel the mystery of life? Or, perhaps, on the contrary, we will be able to prove that life contradicts quantum mechanics, and then we will have to “invent” some new laws. This would be especially interesting.

...In August 1932, the International Congress on Light Therapy was held in Copenhagen. Niels Bohr presented at one of the meetings. His lecture was called “Light and Life.” In it, Bohr shared his thoughts on the problem of life in connection with the latest achievements of quantum mechanics. The lecture was attended by a young German theoretical physicist, an intern at the Bohr Institute in Copenhagen, Max Delbrück. Until now he had never been interested in biology. He studied quantum chemistry, then nuclear physics, but could not find something he liked. Bohr's lecture determined everything: Delbrück firmly decided that he would devote himself to biology.

Returning to Berlin, Delbrück began to seek contacts with biologists. He was lucky. At this time, the famous geneticist Nikolai Vladimirovich Timofeev-Resovsky was working in Berlin.

The friends and collaboration between the physicist Delbrück and the geneticist Timofeev, which lasted only a few years,

Respovsky were the beginning from which twenty years later the solution to the mystery of heredity was born.

Delbrück began to gather physicist friends at his home and invite Timofeev-Respovsky, who spent hours teaching them his science - genetics. While telling the story, Timofeev-Respovsky, as was his habit, ran from corner to corner, like a tiger in a cage. He spoke of Mendel's strict mathematical laws governing heredity. About genes and the replacement work of Morgan, who established the patterns of gene arrangement in chromosomes. About the fruit fly Drosophila and about the mutations that can be induced in it with X-ray radiation. He was working on this last question together with the experimental physicist Zimmer.

Delbrück was extremely interested in their work. In general, there was so much in genetics that was in tune with quantum mechanics! After all, quantum mechanics brought discreteness and discontinuity to physics. She also made us take randomness seriously. And now it turns out that biologists have also discovered a discrete, indivisible particle of heredity - a gene - that can randomly transition from one state to another (this transition is called mutation).

What is a gene? How is it built? This was often discussed at Delbrück's evenings. Timofeev-Respovsky said that in general this question is of little interest to geneticists. For them, a gene is the same as an electron for physicists. A gene is an elementary part of heredity.

- What will you answer if I ask what an electron consists of? - Timofeev-Respovsky once said. Everyone laughed.

- This is how geneticists get confused when they are asked what a gene consists of. The question of what a gene is goes beyond the scope of genetics, and it makes no sense to address it to geneticists,” he continued. - You, physicists, must look for the answer to it.

“And yet,” insisted Delbrück, “are there really no hypotheses, empty speculative ones?”

“Yes,” answered Timofeev-Respovsky, after thinking a little. — My teacher Nikolai Konstantinovich Koltsov believes that a gene is a polymer molecule, most likely a protein molecule.

- Well, what does this explain? - the skinny, long Delbrück directly shouted at the broad-shouldered, powerful Timofeev-Respovsky. - Because we call a gene a protein, we will understand how genes are made? After all, this is precisely the main mystery! Didn't you tell us how the characteristic shape of the lip was passed down from generation to generation in the Habsburg family? What mechanism ensures the exact copying of genes over centuries? Does chemistry give us such examples? In any case, I have never heard anything like it. No, there needs to be a complete mystery here. Great mystery. Perhaps a new law of the period. Now the main question is how to achieve this experimentally.

The great secret hidden behind the short word “gene” haunted Delbrück. How does the doubling, or reduplication, of genes occur during cell division?

Delbrück became especially excited when he learned about the existence of bacteriophages. These amazing particles, which cannot even be called alive, outside the cell behave simply like large molecules - crystals are even grown from them. But if such a particle enters a cell, then after about twenty minutes the cell membrane bursts, and a hundred exact copies of the original particle fall out. “Here is the key to the solution,” thought Delbrück. - This is a very simple phenomenon, much simpler than the division of a whole cell. It won't be difficult to figure it out here. In fact, we need to look at how external conditions will affect the reproduction of virus particles. We need to conduct experiments at different temperatures, in different environments, with different viruses.”

This is how a theoretical physicist turned into an experimental biologist. But the thought - the thought remained purely physical. And the main thing is the goal. There was no other person in the whole world who would do virtual research with the sole purpose of finding out how a gene works.

... In 1937, Delbrück was invited to work in the USA, where he could devote himself entirely to the problem of reduplication of bacteriophages. He is in a hurry to take advantage of this offer, since life in national Germany is becoming miserable.

In America, Delbrück gathered around him a handful of enthusiasts who became infected with his idea of ​​​​studying the nature of heredity using bacteriophages. This is how the “phage group” arose.

As the years passed, students of the phage group learned more and more about how phage infection occurs, how the process of reproduction of phage offspring depends on external conditions, etc. Many remarkable studies have been carried out, especially in the field of studying mutations in bacteria and bacteriophages. But all this did not seem to even bring us closer to solving the main problem.

As often happens in science, people united to solve a larger and more important problem gradually began to scrupulously study specific issues, became master specialists in their specific fields, but ceased to see the original goals in front of them. So the travelers see mountain peaks from afar, but then, approaching them, they find themselves in the forest foothills, from where these peaks are not visible. These forests abound in mushrooms and berries... If you walk along the foothills for a long time, the snowy peaks seen from afar begin to seem like a mirage. Perhaps it was just clouds that looked like snowy mountains? But even if these were indeed mountains, why rush there? It’s so good here, in these unburnt forests. It takes a loud voice of leadership to get people to remember the purpose again.

And he produced such a voice. It was the voice of the remarkable physical theorist, one of the creators of quantum mechanics, Erwin Schrödingar.

In 1944, Schrödingar’s small book was published under the catchy title “What is Life? From a physicist's point of view." She attracted almost no attention then. There was a war going on, and most of those to whom the book was addressed were immersed in scientific and technical problems, the solution of which largely determines the outcome of physical Germany.

But when the war ended, many specialists appeared, especially among physicists, who had to start all over again, again look for a place in peaceful science - for them Schrödingar’s book came in handy.

In his book (it was published in Russian for the first time in 1947), Schrödinger, first of all, gave a very clear and succinct presentation of the basic genetics. For physicists, this was a unique opportunity to find out, in a lectured and, most importantly, accessible presentation of their illustrious colleague, what this mysterious, attractive science consists of. But that's not all. Schrödinger outlined and further developed the idea of ​​Delbrück and Timofeev-Resovsky about the connection between genetics and quantum mechanics. While these ideas were expressed by leaders unknown to physicists, they did not pay much attention. But when Schrödinger himself started talking about it...
According to everyone who studied the problem of the gene in subsequent years, it was Schrödingar’s book that served as the main impetus for them. Schrödinger was the one who seemed to shout: “Here they are, above the peaks, look, they are very close. Why are you delaying?

Of the many who responded to Schrödingar’s confession, two were lucky enough to be the first to see the distances that opened from the very top. These were a very young student of the phage group, Jim Watson, and an employee of the famous Cavendish Laboratory, Francis Crick.

We will not trace their path to the top in detail - Watson himself spoke about this in his book “The Double Helix”. Let's try the shortest route to reach this peak, one of the greatest ever conquered by man, and at least feel a little of the feelings experienced by the pioneers and those who followed them.

Obsessed with the idea of ​​finding out how a gene works, and believing that the phage group was not up to the task, Watson went to work in Europe in 1951. He soon “settled” in the Cavendish Laboratory, as he met Crick there, who was as militant as himself. Watson by that time was already sure that the key to solving the mystery of the gene lay in elucidating the structure of DNA.

The fact is that in the same 1944, when Schrödinger’s book was published, an article by three American bacteriologists led by sixty-year-old Avery was published in one of the medical journals, which in subsequent years attracted much more attention from geneticists, chemists and physicists than from physicians. In this meticulously completed work, it was absolutely unequivocally proven that when non-torious genetic characteristics are transferred from a killed bacterium to another, living bacterium (this phenomenon had long been known by that time and was called transformation), then only the molecule socribonuclinic acid (DNA) is transferred. Neither proteins nor any other components of the killed cell are involved in the transformation. In fact, Avery’s work is now considered the first evidence that genes are exactly a DNA molecule. So, was it Avery and his two assistants, and not Watson and Crick, who were promoted to the top for the first time?

Undoubtedly, Avery took a very important step in the right direction, but he did not reach the top. Einstein once said words that were insightful in their depth: “Only theory decides what we manage to observe.” Avery had nothing in store that could be called a theory, and he limited himself to a dry presentation of facts. This, however, did not make the problem any less serious. Geneticists were faced with a choice - either they must refuse to believe Avery’s data, or they must admit that the substance of heredity is DNA, and not protein, as was commonly believed. It was difficult not to believe Avery - there was simply nothing to be appreciated in his work. And yet, geneticists never wanted a defended idea of ​​the protein nature of the gene. Avery's experiments were given the following explanation: DNA, of course, does not and cannot contain any genes, but it can cause gene mutations, that is, change genes, which, as they should be, consist of protein. This explanation saved the protein theory of the gene. True, the effect of DNA turned out to be unteachable: the mutations it caused were repeated from experiment to experiment. And this, of course, could not but interest geneticists, who had long been looking for ways to purposefully change heredity. Both geneticists and everyone who dealt with the problem of the chemical (or physical) nature of heredity were finally forced to accept that DNA should be given serious attention.

The fact is that, although DNA was discovered by Miescher back in 1869, it remained a “stepchild” even among biochemists, not to mention geneticists or physicists involved in biology. It was generally accepted that DNA is a regulatory polymer consisting of an array of repeating four-dimensional units (adenine, guanine, thymine and cytosine), and this molecule most likely performs some purely structural function in chromosomes, but is in no way associated with the storage and transmission of genetic information. Avery's work has cast doubt on these views.

The theory that decided what Avery and his sequences actually managed to observe was the model of the structure of the DNA molecule, invented by Watson and Crick in 1953.

Details aside, the essence of the Watson-Crick model is extremely simple. The DNA molecule consists of two polymer chains. Each of the chains is a certain sequence of four types of links - A (adenine), G (guanine), T (thymine) and C (cytozinine). The sequence in one of the two circuits can be completely arbitrary. Then the sequence in the opposite, or, as they say, complementary chain is uniquely specified using the following complementation principle (Fig. 1):
against A must be T
against T must be A
against G there must be C
against C there should be G
The principle of complementation was derived from the spatial structure of monomeric DNA units known at that time. In this, Watson and Crick followed the general method of constructing molecular models developed by Linus Pauling.

If inside each of the mutually complementary polymer chains the atoms are held together by very powerful covalent bonds, then between the chains there are relatively weak bonds, similar to those that hold molecules near each other in crystals.

The most remarkable feature of the Watson-Crick model was that it solved the main problem—the problem of gene reduplication—with extraordinary elegance.

After all, if we separate two strands, and then on each one we grow, according to the principle of complementation, a new strand, then we will get two DNA molecule from one, and both will be identical to the original one (Fig. 2).

One can imagine how excited Delbrück was when he received a letter from Watson containing the solution to the riddle of gene duplication. He immediately and unconditionally believed in the Watson-Crick model. Under the influence of Watson's letter, he wrote the words that we have given as an epigraph. Of course, he then felt special gratitude to Bohr for the fact that he had inspired him to study biology at one time. No, no new laws of physics have been discovered in biology. But the central problem, the problem of the physical structure of the gene, was solved.
