Light, air and water

Refraction when passing from water to air

In almost all books on physics, in the place where they talk about the refraction of light, there is figure, which depicts water, the exact source of light inside it and the rays emerging from the source and refracting at the water-air boundary. For example, as shown in figure 158 from a physics textbook for grade 10 (“Physics 10”, M., “Prosveshcheniye”, 1981). But, of course, it is much more interesting to experiment and observe the phenomenon itself, and not figure.

First of all, make the appropriate illuminator. Take an opaque plastic jar with a diameter of 40-50 mm and a height of 20-30 mm. In the wall of the jar, use a hacksaw blade to make a series of identical slits, equihistoric from each other, approximately 1-1.5 mm wide. You will receive a housing for the light.

In the lower part of the rectangular plate (made of aluminum or plastic), drill a hole the diameter of the light bulb socket for a flashlight. One surface of the plate is coated white. (The easiest way to do this is to spray the surface with white nitro enamel, which is sold in an aerosol can.) Or simply stick a sheet of white paper onto the record.

Solder the conductors to the light bulb, place it in the hole and

Lubricate the light bulb socket from the back surface of the plate with plasticine. This coating will fix the light bulb in the plate and at the same time insulate the junction of the light bulb with the conductors.

Connect the leads of the light bulb to the flashlight battery. Place the manufactured casing on the light bulb so that the light bulb is on its axis, and attach the casing to the plate. On the white surface of the plate, you should observe light rays emanating from the illuminator and shining within an angle not exceeding l. You may have to slightly adjust the position of the casing or correct the cracks in its wall. It is most convenient to secure the pose casing to the plate with plasticine.

Of course, if you want to make a more substantial device (say, for a school physics classroom), you will have to come up with and implement a more reliable way of attaching the casing. But before you do that, try the simplest option described here.

After the device is made, proceed to the experiment. Lower the plate with the illuminator into a vessel with water so that the plate is positioned vertically. It is best to use a vessel with a flat front wall, such as an aquarium. Through the transparent wall of the vessel you will observe approximately the same path of light beams as shown in figure 1 (here 1 is a vessel with water, 2 is an illuminator, 3 is a plate with a white surface).

Reflection from water heating

Place a tin can, such as a coffee can, into a large vessel of cold water (this could be a glass jar placed on a sheet of white paper, or a basin with a white house) and somehow secure it in a vertical position (Fig. 2). The larger the jar, the better (we used a jar with a diameter of 80 mm and a height of 120 mm, and these are, please, the minimum dimensions suitable for the experiment).

Boil water, quickly pour boiling water into a jar and look from above through the water in a large vessel along the side surface of the jar - you will see it tin like a mirror.

The extraction experience is especially effective when the surface of the can is black. If you were unable to find such a jar, take any one and wrap it in black paper from a photo bag (the paper can be secured, for example, with adhesive tape). When filling the jar with hot water, the paper will shine brightly.

It is obvious that in this experience there is a complete reflection of light. The question is why this happens.

Perhaps the light is being reflected from a layer of air on the surface of the can? It turns out not. The liquid and the paper are well moistened with water, so that no airy molding is formed.

pour boiling water into a jar, dip your finger into cold water near the wall of a large vessel. After pouring boiling water into a jar, move your finger closer to the jar - you will feel a gradual increase in the temperature of the water. Continue observing, and you will be convinced that the total reflection of light will disappear when everything only in the radial direction of the wide vessel warms up to approximately the same temperature.

So that's the answer! Complete reflection under the conditions of this experiment occurs because the water in the outer vessel is heated unevenly. Near a jar of boiling water, the water temperature is most directional and the density is lowest. As you move away from the jar, the temperature of the water decreases and its density increases. At the same time, the optical density of water also increases, or, in other words, its refractive index increases. Light, saturated with light, gradually moves from an area with a higher optical density to an area with a lower optical density, which is why its complete reflection is observed.

Unlike many experiments with total reflection, in this case there is no sharp boundary between media with different optical densities, therefore, there is no reflection of light in the strict sense of the word. Light in an optically infertile medium with a floating change in optical density propagates curvilinearly (as shown in figure 2), bending in the region of high refractive index values.
