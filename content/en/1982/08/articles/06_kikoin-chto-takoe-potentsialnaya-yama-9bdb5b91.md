What is a potential hole

A potential hole is a hole that has not yet been dug...
(from scientific folklore)

In physics, the phrase is often found: “The particle (or system) is in a potential well.” What is a potential hole and why does any physical object end up in it? Let's try to figure this out. The most convenient way to do this is with specific examples. On it from the simplest.

Yashik and ball

Let's take a box with nerve dynamium, in which there are bulges and dents (holes), and throw a metal ball into it. After rolling along the bottom, the ball will eventually remain at the bottom of one of the holes. Let us ask ourselves: why did the ball remain in the hole, and precisely at its bottom, and not on the slope? The obvious answer is this. The ball located on the wall of the hole (or convexity) is acted upon by two forces: the force of gravity $\vec{F}_\tau$ and the reaction force of the wall $\vec{F}_p$ (Fig. 1). The resultant force $\vec{F}$ is directed down the slope, so the ball, in accordance with Newton’s second law, acquires acceleration and rolls down to the bottom of the hole. At the bottom, the action of two forces ($\vec{F}_\tau$ and $\vec{F}_p$) is balanced, and the equivalent force vanishes, so here the ball will be in an equilibrium position.

Let's consider the same situation from an energy point of view. A ball resting at the bottom of a hole has zero speed and its kinetic energy is zero. It follows that all the energy he possesses is potential. Let's try to lift the ball from its equilibrium position. It is clear that any movement of the ball up the slope must require work against the force of gravity. Due to this work done on the body, its potential energy returns. But if this energy ($E_\pi$) returns for any

displacement ($r$) relative to the equilibrium position, it means that at the equilibrium point itself ($r=0$) it is minimal (Fig. 2).

Now let's follow the transformation of the energy of a ball thrown to the bottom of the box. At the first moment it possessed a certain kinetic and potential energy. When moving up and down along the depressions and convexities of the bottom, a mutual transformation of kinetic and potential energies occurred, but at the same time part of the energy was lost to work against friction. Eventually, the total supply of mechanical energy became so small that it was not enough for the ball to get out of the next hole, and it remained at its bottom. In this position, all the energy remaining in the ball is potential and equal to the minimum value possible in this hole. For example, figure 3 shows two possible equilibrium positions of a ball in two adjacent holes of different depths. Each of the equilibrium positions corresponds to its own minimum energy, and $E_{n\min 1} < E_{n\min 2}$ (to move the ball from position 1 to position 2, positive work must be expended).

So, we have come to another definition of equilibrium (more precisely, stable equilibrium):

The stable equilibrium position of a body always corresponds to the minimum value of its potential energy.

Or, as we say in this case, in a position of stable equilibrium the body is in a potential hole.

The example we analyzed is simple and clear: a potential hole for a ball turns out to be a hole in the literal, everyday sense. Now let's move on to an object for which no real well can be imagined, but a potential well, nevertheless, exists and determines its equilibrium state.

Soap bubble

Any child knows that an ordinary soap bubble is always spherical in shape. Why is this so?

In order to answer this question, we need to remember surface tension. A soap bubble, from a physical point of view, is a liquid film bounding a cavity filled with gas. If you look at this film on a larger scale, you can see that it consists of two surface layers (outer and inner) and a liquid enclosed between them. Molecules on the surface are in special conditions compared to bulk molecules. Each of the molecules inside the liquid is surrounded by its own kind, and the resultant of all attractive forces is zero. On the surface of the molecules, on one side the action is of a non-liquid molecule, but a gas, which has a significantly lower density. Therefore, the equilibrium force of attraction on the surface is always directed inward, into the volume. Consequently, in order to reach the surface, molecules must do work against

these gravitational forces. This means that the molecules on the surface of the liquid have more energy than those inside. This additional potential energy is called surface energy. The larger the surface area S, the greater the surface energy Eₙ₀ₑ:

$$E_{пов} \sim S, \text{ или } E_{пов} = \sigma S.$$

The proportionality coefficient is the surface tension. This coefficient is changed by relating the work A required to increase the surface area by ΔS to the value of ΔS. It is different for each liquid.

When we collapse a bubble, the size of the cavity (and therefore the surface of the film) increases, but the amount of liquid in the film naturally remains constant. It is clear that an increase in the surface is possible only due to the release of new molecules from the volume into the surface layer, that is, due to the performance of work and an increase in the surface energy of the liquid.

Now we can answer the question of why a soap bubble has the shape of a sphere, and not, for example, an egg, an ellipson or a polyhedron. The fact is that the sphere has a remarkable property: of all the geometric figures that limit a given volume, it has the smallest surface area. Therefore, if we try to give the bubble a non-spherical shape, then we will be forced to increase the surface area of ​​the layer, that is, to do additional work to bring molecules to the surface, and therefore to further increase the surface energy of the liquid.

In other words,
the spherical shape of the bubble removes the minimum potential energy of the film that forms its shell.

The question is, who is in the potential hole here? It is clear that this is not a separate part, since the very nature of the surface of tension is not associated with individual molecules, but with their interaction. It cannot even be said that there are molecules of the layer surface in the potential well, since when the radius of the bubble changes, a transition of molecules occurs from the volume to the surface or vice versa (and even with constant bubble sizes, due to the tempo movement, there is a continuous volume of molecules between the volume and the surface).

Thus,
The potential well contains the entire surface energy of the liquid.

And we only managed to introduce the concept of a potential well to obtain a potential well corresponding to the spherical shape of the bubble (Fig. 4). Reduce the area of ​​the molecule by compressing the bubble, that is, perform additional work on rolling the gas located inside the cavity. The corresponding increase in the energy of the system in this case is shown in figure 4 by the dashed line. So, in reality, in the potential well there is a complex system of “surface liquid molecules + bulk liquid molecules + gas molecules inside the cavity.”

In a crystal, the situation is, of course, more complicated. In addition to the nearest neighbors with charges of the opposite sign, each one has next, identically charged neighbors, behind them - more, and so on. Although the size of the crystal is limited, the number of neighbors can be considered infinitely large. Indeed, let’s take as an example the very finely ground “Extra” salt. Its crystals have dimensions of ~0.1 mm, and the lattice constant is $a \sim 10^{-8}$ cm, so that on the edge of such a crystal there is a decrease of ~10^6, on the face - 10^12, and in the volume of ~10^18 identical ones.

Calculating the total interaction energy of a columnar number of ions, at first glance, is an incredibly difficult task. However, with the help of an ingenious trick based on functional physics messages, the calculation can actually be performed in a few lines. This will be discussed in detail in Appendix II at the end of the article, but here we will only present the final result. It turns out that the total interaction energy of all ions in a crystal has the form

$$W_{\text{кр}}(a) = -\frac{Ae^2}{a} + \frac{B}{a^n},$$

where $A$ and $B$ are constants. Graphically, it represents a curve with a minimum that describes the potential well in which the crystal as a whole is located. This means that in a state of equilibrium, the lattice takes on a value $a_0$ that corresponds to the minimum potential energy.

We examined three, at first glance, completely different objects and made sure that for each of them the potential energy is depicted by a curve with a minimum. In the general case, the type of potential well can be more complex, in particular, it depends not on one parameter, but on several, but the general principle is always the same:

in a state of stable equilibrium, any system has minimal potential energy, or, in other words, remains at the level of a potential well.

Appendix I

Let's calculate the potential $\varphi_1$ created at point 1 with coordinate $r_1$ by the electron charge + $e$ located at the origin (Fig. 7). As is known, this potential is equal to the work of the electron field to move a unit positive charge from a given point to infinity.

Let us place a test positive charge $q$ at point 1. According to Coulomb's law, the charge $e$ acts on it by a force $F = qe/r_1^2$ (in the abscissa system of units). Let's find the work $A_{1-2}$ to move the charge $q$ from point 1 to the nearest point 2. In this case, we will choose the distance $r_2 - r_1$ so small that throughout the entire section 1-2 the force can be considered constant and equal to $F_1 = qe/r^2 = qe/(r_1r_2)$. Then

$$A_{1-2} = F_1(r_2 - r_1) = \frac{qe}{r_1r_2}(r_2 - r_1) = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right).$$

Similarly, you can calculate the work $A_{2-3}$ to move the charge $q$ from point 2 to the nearest point 3:

$$A_{2-3} = F_2(r_3 - r_2) = \frac{qe}{r_2r_3}(r_3 - r_2) = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_3}\right).$$

Obviously, the work of the field in section 1-3 is equal to the sum of work 1-2 and 2-3:

$$A_{1-3} = A_{1-2} + A_{2-3} = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) + qe \left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_3}\right).$$

Continuing the reasoning, we can make sure that the intermediate points of the lower fields do not play, and the expression for the work includes only the coordinates of the starting and ending points. Therefore, the work of the field to move the test charge $q$ from point 1 to infinity ($1/r_\infty = 0$) is equal to

$$A_1 = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_\infty}\right) = qe/r_1.$$

Then finally for the potential at point 1 we obtain

$$\varphi_1 = \frac{A_1}{q} = \frac{e}{r_1}.$$

Those who are familiar with integration can easily get this result in one line...
