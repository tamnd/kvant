Archimedean force and knits

On land, the goose gives the impression of a sedentary, clumsy bird. “The goose is heavy on red paws...” - this is what A.S. Pushkin wrote, using the very expressive word “heavy” to describe the bird. But then the goose entered the water and swam... Now we see a light, graceful bird, moving quickly and freely. Even a breath of wind is enough to change the speed of its movement. Why such a change?

The peculiarities of the body's behavior in water are associated with low friction and the presence of buoyant - Archimedean - force.

Place a cork or plastic jar lid on the table and serve it on the side. She won't budge. Place the cork on the surface of the water - when you blow it, it begins to move easily. You will see that the friction force in water is much less than the friction force between solid bodies.

And the cork floats on the surface of the water because two forces acting on it in opposite directions are equal to each other: gravity force and Archimedean force.

The body of the largest animal on Earth, the whale, is perfectly adapted for life in water. The largest representatives of the cetacean order are blue knits. The mass of the blue whale reaches 130 tons, but it is capable of reaching speeds of up to 20 knots in the water (a knot is a speed equal to one nautical mile per hour, and since a nautical mile is 1.852 km, a knot is a speed equal to 1.852 km/h). For comparison, we point out that the MKM motor boat can reach speeds of up to 30 km/h, that is, about 16 knots.

A sperm whale, weighing about 60 tons, jumps out of the water and rises several meters above its surface.

(Think and explain why such legs would be necessary for a land animal.) A body floats in water if the Archimedean force and the force of gravity acting on it are equal. Let's calculate the Archimedean force acting on the whale and compare it with the force of gravity.
The Archimedean force is equal to the weight of the liquid displaced by a body immersed in it, that is
$$F_A = g \rho_{\text{ж}} V$$
where $g \approx 10$ N/kg, $\rho_{\text{ж}}$ is the density of the liquid, $V$ is the volume of the body. How do we calculate the volume of a whale? Let's do it this way: let's assume that the whale's body has the shape of a cylinder. Then the volume is equal to $V = \pi d^2 h / 4$, where $d$ is the diameter of the cylinder, $h$ is its height. In our case, the height $h$ is the length of the whale. What is the diameter of our whale-cylinder? Let's assume that this is the average diameter of the whale's body. And let’s estimate the average diameter using figure 1. To do this, let’s change to figure the diameter of the whale in several, for example, ten, different places: at the head, at the tail, at the middle. We will take the arithmetic mean of these changes as the diameter of the whale-cylinder. But just take into account that figure 1 is made on a certain scale: on figure the length of the whale is ~7.5 cm, but in fact its length is 30 m, so the scale figure is 1:400.

Do all the calculations further, and you will see that the Archimedean force that supports the whale in the water is calculated in millions of newtons. (Of course, your calculations are approximate, and it is impossible to say exactly how many newtons; but the fact that this is a number between one and ten million is certain.) It is clear that such a force keeps in balance a body with a mass of hundreds of tons, and this is precisely the mass that the blue whale has. A whale in water is weightless, because the force of gravity acting on it is also calculated in millions of newtons (to calculate the force of gravity, multiply 9.8 N/kg by the mass of the whale).

Of course, the whale will not be able to stay on land. There are known cases when a whale, for unknown reasons, washes up on the ocean shore. At figure 2 you see a photograph of a whale sleeping in the water. It is not completely submerged in water. This means that the buoyant force acting on it should be less than in the case of complete immersion (after all, this force is equal to the weight of the liquid displaced by the whale). But the force of gravity remained the same. It would seem that the balance should be upset. But the whale sleeps peacefully on the water, it does not drown. Therefore, the buoyant force and the gravitational force are still equal to each other. How to explain this apparent contradiction?

Now is the time to talk about how a whale dives and how it emerges.

The whale's tail has horizontal blades and develops up to 500 horsepower (one horsepower is a unit of power equal to approximately 736 watts). For comparison, let's say that this power is only two times less than the power of the AN-2 aircraft engine and seven times more than the power of the DT-75 tractor engine. When a scuba diver is pushed by the body of a swimming whale, “the impression is as if pushed by a speeding steam locomotive.”

With a powerful movement of its tail, the whale directs its body into the depths of the ocean - it dives. The diving depth is several ten meters, and the sperm whale reaches a depth of 1000-1200 meters. At such a depth, the water pressure is high (calculate it yourself, taking into account that the density of sea water is 1030 kg/m³). Under this pressure, the whale's lungs shrink to the so-called residual volume. In a person at a depth of 60 m, where the pressure is four times greater than atmospheric pressure, the volume of the lungs decreases four times - from 6 liters on the surface to 1.5 liters; therefore, for human lungs at a depth of 60 m, the residual volume is 1.5 liters.
