Solar eclipse July 31, 1981
(see «Kvant» No. 7)

1. Due to the slight ellipticity of the Earth's orbit, the distance of the Earth from the Sun changes throughout the year: the Earth is further from the Sun during summer in the northern hemisphere and close to it in winter. Therefore, the angular size of the Sun changes slightly in summer than in winter. And since the distance of the Moon from the Earth, and therefore its angular size, also fluctuates, in summer the Moon can completely cover the solar disk more often than in winter.

2. During an eclipse, the lunar shadow moves across the Earth's surface approximately from west to east at a speed of about 1 km/s (roughly speaking, this is the speed of the Moon's orbit). The Earth rotates in the same direction, to the east, but at a lower speed around its axis. The speed of rotation of the earth's surface varies from a maximum value of about 0.5 km/s at the equator to 0 at the poles. Therefore, the one observed in the equator region may be in the lunar shadow longer than others, since it “lags behind” it at the lowest speed. The second, less important, reason is that the size of the lunar shadow at the equator is slightly larger than in the polar regions (after all, it is observed at the equator a little closer to the Moon than at the pole).

3. A solar eclipse can be observed if the length $P$ of the shadow from the satellite is greater than the distance $H$ from the satellite to the surface of the planet (see Fig.): $P > H$. The latter magnitudes are simple with the satellite's orbital radius ($R_c$) and the equatorial radius of the planet ($r_\pi$): $H = R_c - r_\pi$. And the length of the satellite’s shadow is easy to find, knowing its radius ($r_c$), the distance of the planet from the Sun ($R_\pi$) and the radius of the Sun ($R_\odot$): $P = r_c R_\pi / R_\odot$. On Jupiter, total solar eclipses will occur with all of its satellites listed in the table, and on Mars there were no solar eclipses at all.

4. It is solved in the same way as the previous problem, only the planet itself now plays the role of a satellite. There are eclipses of satellites on Mars and Jupiter.

5. If the plane flies at a speed of about 1 km/s, then it will “catch up” with the lunar shadow. Such planes exist, and with their help, astronomy has already observed total solar eclipses that lasted an hour on board the plane!

6. If obstructions are favorable, you can observe a solar eclipse from the Salyut orbital station or another satellite. But the duration of the total eclipse will not be long: since the speed of the satellite is about 8 km/s, and the diameter of the shadow does not exceed 270 km, the eclipse will last less than 270/8≈34 s.

7. It is possible if observed on Earth is located so that the Moon and Sun are not close to the horizon. The fact is that, firstly, at the moment of a total lunar eclipse, the Moon can deviate from the “Sun-Earth-Moon” line by 0.5° (this is the difference between the angular radius of the earth’s shadow and the radius of the Moon) and, secondly, the curvature of light rays in the earth’s atmosphere - refraction - allows us to “look under the horizon”. Also by about 0.5°.

8. The angular diameter of the Moon is greater when it is in the plane of the Earth's equator and is formerly visible at the North Pole relatively high above the horizon.
