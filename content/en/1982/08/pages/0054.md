Jacques-Yves Cousteau writes: “... on land, the giants faced insoluble problems... breathing required enormous conditions... on land, the whale’s skeleton cannot withstand the weight of a mouse and a fat layer, while in a dense water environment it serves the whale perfectly.”

During the expedition, Cousteau and his comrade tried to save a stranded whale, whose mass was “only” two tons. To lift it on board the ship, it was necessary to use a special ramak, since even a newborn whale can “break” under the influence of its own gravity if there is no uniform support under it.

At figure 2 you see a photograph of a whale sleeping in the water. It is not completely submerged in water. This means that the buoyant force acting on it should be less than in the case of complete immersion (after all, this force is equal to the weight of the liquid displaced by the whale). But the force of gravity remained the same. It would seem that the balance should be upset. But the whale sleeps peacefully on the water, it does not drown. Therefore, the buoyant force and the gravitational force are still equal to each other. How to explain this apparent contradiction?

Now is the time to talk about how a whale dives and how it emerges.

The whale's tail has horizontal blades and develops up to 500 horsepower (one horsepower is a unit of power equal to approximately 736 watts). For comparison, let's say that this power is only two times less than the power of the AN-2 aircraft engine and seven times more than the power of the DT-75 tractor engine. When a scuba diver is pushed by the body of a swimming whale, “the impression is as if pushed by a speeding steam locomotive.”

With a powerful movement of its tail, the whale directs its body into the depths of the ocean - it dives. The diving depth is several ten meters, and the sperm whale reaches a depth of 1000-1200 meters. At such a depth, the water pressure is high (calculate it yourself, taking into account that the density of sea water is 1030 kg/m³). Under this pressure, the whale's lungs shrink to the so-called residual volume. In a person at a depth of 60 m, where the pressure is four times greater than atmospheric pressure, the volume of the lungs decreases four times - from 6 liters on the surface to 1.5 liters; therefore, for human lungs at a depth of 60 m, the residual volume is 1.5 liters.
