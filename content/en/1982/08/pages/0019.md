Now let's follow the transformation of the energy of a ball thrown to the bottom of the box. At the first moment it possessed a certain kinetic and potential energy. When moving up and down along the depressions and convexities of the bottom, a mutual transformation of kinetic and potential energies occurred, but at the same time part of the energy was lost to work against friction. Eventually, the total supply of mechanical energy became so small that it was not enough for the ball to get out of the next hole, and it remained at its bottom. In this position, all the energy remaining in the ball is potential and equal to the minimum value possible in this hole. For example, figure 3 shows two possible equilibrium positions of a ball in two adjacent holes of different depths. Each of the equilibrium positions corresponds to its own minimum energy, and $E_{n\min 1} < E_{n\min 2}$ (to move the ball from position 1 to position 2, positive work must be expended).

So, we have come to another definition of equilibrium (more precisely, stable equilibrium):

The stable equilibrium position of a body always corresponds to the minimum value of its potential energy.

Or, as we say in this case, in a position of stable equilibrium the body is in a potential hole.

The example we analyzed is simple and clear: a potential hole for a ball turns out to be a hole in the literal, everyday sense. Now let's move on to an object for which no real well can be imagined, but a potential well, nevertheless, exists and determines its equilibrium state.

Soap bubble

Any child knows that an ordinary soap bubble is always spherical in shape. Why is this so?

In order to answer this question, we need to remember surface tension. A soap bubble, from a physical point of view, is a liquid film bounding a cavity filled with gas. If you look at this film on a larger scale, you can see that it consists of two surface layers (outer and inner) and a liquid enclosed between them. Molecules on the surface are in special conditions compared to bulk molecules. Each of the molecules inside the liquid is surrounded by its own kind, and the resultant of all attractive forces is zero. On the surface of the molecules, on one side the action is of a non-liquid molecule, but a gas, which has a significantly lower density.
