⟦folio 20⟧

In a crystal, the situation is, of course, more complicated. In addition to the nearest neighbors with charges of the opposite sign, each one has next, identically charged neighbors, behind them - more, and so on. Although the size of the crystal is limited, the number of neighbors can be considered infinitely large. Indeed, let’s take as an example the very finely ground “Extra” salt. Its crystals have dimensions of ~0.1 mm, and the lattice constant is $a \sim 10^{-8}$ cm, so that on the edge of such a crystal there is a decrease of ~10^6, on the face - 10^12, and in the volume of ~10^18 identical ones.

Calculating the total interaction energy of a columnar number of ions, at first glance, is an incredibly difficult task. However, with the help of an ingenious trick based on functional physics messages, the calculation can actually be performed in a few lines. This will be discussed in detail in Appendix II at the end of the article, but here we will only present the final result. It turns out that the total interaction energy of all ions in a crystal has the form

$$W_{\text{кр}}(a) = -\frac{Ae^2}{a} + \frac{B}{a^n},$$

where $A$ and $B$ are constants. Graphically, it represents a curve with a minimum that describes the potential well in which the crystal as a whole is located. This means that in a state of equilibrium, the lattice takes on a value $a_0$ that corresponds to the minimum potential energy.

We examined three, at first glance, completely different objects and made sure that for each of them the potential energy is depicted by a curve with a minimum. In the general case, the type of potential well can be more complex, in particular, it depends not on one parameter, but on several, but the general principle is always the same:

in a state of stable equilibrium, any system has minimal potential energy, or, in other words, remains at the level of a potential well.

Appendix I

Let's calculate the potential $\varphi_1$ created at point 1 with coordinate $r_1$ by the electron charge + $e$ located at the origin (Fig. 7). As is known, this potential is equal to the work of the electron field to move a unit positive charge from a given point to infinity.

Let us place a test positive charge $q$ at point 1. According to Coulomb's law, the charge $e$ acts on it by a force $F = qe/r_1^2$ (in the abscissa system of units). Let's find the work $A_{1-2}$ to move the charge $q$ from point 1 to the nearest point 2. In this case, we will choose the distance $r_2 - r_1$ so small that throughout the entire section 1-2 the force can be considered constant and equal to $F_1 = qe/r^2 = qe/(r_1r_2)$. Then

$$A_{1-2} = F_1(r_2 - r_1) = \frac{qe}{r_1r_2}(r_2 - r_1) = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right).$$

Similarly, you can calculate the work $A_{2-3}$ to move the charge $q$ from point 2 to the nearest point 3:

$$A_{2-3} = F_2(r_3 - r_2) = \frac{qe}{r_2r_3}(r_3 - r_2) = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_3}\right).$$

Obviously, the work of the field in section 1-3 is equal to the sum of work 1-2 and 2-3:

$$A_{1-3} = A_{1-2} + A_{2-3} = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) + qe \left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_3}\right).$$

Continuing the reasoning, we can make sure that the intermediate points of the lower fields do not play, and the expression for the work includes only the coordinates of the starting and ending points. Therefore, the work of the field to move the test charge $q$ from point 1 to infinity ($1/r_\infty = 0$) is equal to

$$A_1 = qe \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_\infty}\right) = qe/r_1.$$

Then finally for the potential at point 1 we obtain

$$\varphi_1 = \frac{A_1}{q} = \frac{e}{r_1}.$$
