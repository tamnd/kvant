Fundamental theorem of algebra

Here we will prove the fundamental theorem of algebra, which states that every polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root.

In this case, real numbers are considered a special case of complex numbers. This theorem was first proven by Gauss in 1799 for the special case of polynomials with real coefficients. Gauss showed that every polynomial has at least one real or complex root. From the point of view of modern abstract algebra, this theorem shows that the field of algebraic complex numbers is closed: this means that by considering the roots of algebraic equations (that is, the roots of polynomials) in this field, we cannot obtain new numbers.

In this sense, the field of complex numbers is radically different from the field of real numbers, which is not algebraically closed.

It is worth noting that the field of complex numbers is obtained from the field of real numbers by adding only the root of one equation $z^2+1=0$.

The proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra is based on messages from abstract algebra, and on a concrete consideration of the field of complex numbers. Its rigorous proof must be based on the exact definition of a real number and on the exact definition of the continuity of functions. What I am presenting here is not a rigorous, but geometrically convincing proof based on consideration of paths in the plane of a complex variable and their deformations. Proving this not only proves the theorem, but to some extent obliges why it is true.

As a consequence of the fundamental theorem of algebra, we will show how a polynomial with complex (in particular, real) coefficients is considered on a polybody.
