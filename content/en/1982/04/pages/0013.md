Now the researchers' focus was all on the next step - understanding what the core is made of. And again α-particles came to the rescue. Rutherford and his colleagues exposed them to nitrogen and other light gases and observed the fission of nuclei. At the same time, hydrogen nuclei - protons, as Rutherford later called them - flew out of them. The first works on nuclear fission were published in 1919, and already in them scientists concluded that protons are a structural part of heavier nuclei.

A year later, Rutherford gave a lecture in which he described how he imagined the structure of the core. According to Rutherford, nuclei more complex than hydrogen should consist of positively charged protons and electrons. There were good reasons for this assumption. The presence of protons was confirmed by Rutherford's own experiments. The existence of electrons seemed natural since they were emitted from the nucleus during beta decay. Finally, this hypothesis helped to explain the data that had accumulated by that time on the correspondence between the masses and charges of nuclei. Thus, the charge of the helium nucleus was twice that of a proton, and the mass was four times greater than the mass of the hydrogen nucleus. For carbon, these correspondences were 6 and 12, respectively, etc.
