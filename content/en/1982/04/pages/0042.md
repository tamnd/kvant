In general, any equation of the form $\sqrt{f(x)} = \sqrt{g(x)}$ is equivalent to “choice” of each of the systems
$$\begin{cases} f(x) = g(x) \\ f(x) \geqslant 0 \end{cases}$$

By choosing the “easier” one, you can usually get by without searching for DZ.

The equation $\sqrt{x+4} - \sqrt{x-1} = 1$ is equivalent to the equation
$$\sqrt{x+4} = 1 + \sqrt{x-1},$$
(5)

which, in turn, is equivalent to the equation
$$x+4 = (1 + \sqrt{x-1})^2.$$

Indeed, if $x_0$ is the root of equation (5), then it is, of course, also the root of equation (6): the equation $[f(x)]^2 = [g(x)]^2$ in serdda, that is, for any $f$ and $g$, is derived from the equation $f(x) = g(x)$. If, conversely, $x_0$ is the root of equation (6), that is, the numerical equality $x_0 + 4 = (1 + \sqrt{x_0-1})^2$ is true, then, first of all, $x_0 + 4 \geqslant 0$; means $\sqrt{x_0+4} = \sqrt{(1 + \sqrt{x_0-1})^2} = |1 + \sqrt{x_0-1}|$; since $1 + \sqrt{x_0-1} \geqslant 0$, $|1 + \sqrt{x_0-1}| = 1 + \sqrt{x_0-1}$; therefore, $x_0$ is the root of equation (5).

Equation (6) is equivalent to each of the equations
$$x+4 = 1 + 2\sqrt{x-1} + x-1,$$
$$\sqrt{x-1} = 2,$$
$$x-1 = 4.$$

Equation $\sqrt{x+3} + \sqrt{3x-2} = 7$ is equivalent, of course, to equation $\sqrt{x+3} = 7 - \sqrt{3x-2},$ (7)
but, unlike the previous case, equation (7) is not equivalent to equation $x+3 = (7 - \sqrt{3x-2})^2$; it is equivalent, in view of the equivalence of (3) and (4) proven above, to the system
$$\begin{cases} x+3 = (7 - \sqrt{3x-2})^2 \\ 7 - \sqrt{3x-2} \geqslant 0 \end{cases}$$
and, further, to each of the systems
$$\begin{cases} x+3 = 49 - 14\sqrt{3x-2} + 3x-2 \\ \sqrt{3x-2} \leqslant 7, \end{cases}$$
$$\begin{cases} 7\sqrt{3x-2} = x+22 \\ \sqrt{3x-2} \leqslant 7, \end{cases}$$
$$\begin{cases} 49(3x-2) = (x+22)^2 \\ 3x-2 \leqslant 49. \\ x+22 \geqslant 0. \end{cases}$$

The equation $\sqrt{x-2} + \sqrt{1-x} = 3$ can be solved in a similar way, but in this case it is easier to immediately say that its ODZ is an empty set and, therefore, it has roots.

Let's move on to inequalities
$$3\sqrt{6+x-x^2} > 4x-2.$$

Inequality (8) is equivalent to the totality
$$\begin{cases} 4x-2 \geqslant 0 \\ 9(6+x-x^2) > (4x-2)^2 \end{cases}$$

$$\begin{cases} 4x-2 < 0 \\ 6+x-x^2 \geqslant 0. \end{cases}$$

(Solving the second from system (9), we at the same time find the ODZ of inequality (8).)

In fact, let first $x_0$ be a solution to inequality (8), that is, the numerical inequality $3\sqrt{6+x-x^2} > 4x-2$ is valid. Let us prove that then $x_0$ is a solution to one of the systems (9). Let's consider two cases: $4x_0-2 \geqslant 0$ and $4x_0-2 < 0$. If $4x_0-2 \geqslant 0$, then by one of the properties of numerical inequalities (if $a > b \geqslant 0$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$, then $a^n > b^n)$ $9(6+x-x^2) > (4x_0-2)^2$; also, in this case, $x_0$ is a solution to the first from system (9). If $4x_0-2 < 0$, then $x_0$ is a solution to the second from system (9), since it is true and, naturally, $\sqrt{6+x-x^2} \geqslant 0$; from $4x_0-2 < 0$ follows $\sqrt{6+x-x^2} > 4x_0-2$, that is, $x_0$ is again a solution to inequality (8).

It is easy to prove that any inequality of the form $\sqrt{f(x)} > g(x)$ is equivalent to the totality of the system
$$\begin{cases} g(x) \geqslant 0 \\ f(x) > [g(x)]^2 \end{cases}$$

(The inequality $\sqrt{f(x)} \geqslant g(x)$ can be solved in a similar way.)
