⟦folio 15⟧

The liquid (water, alcohol, etc.) increases sharply with the help of a piston or diaphragm. Steam, thoroughly cleaned of dust, remains in a supersaturated state for some time (0.1÷1 s) and does not condense. If at this moment a particle sweeps through the volume, ionizing the vapor molecules, then drops of water will initially appear on the formed particles. The particle trail becomes visible and can be formed. To do this, the wall of the chamber is made pronunciation. Due to the thermal conductivity of the walls, the temperature of the steam rises quite quickly, the trace disappears, and sometimes the entire chamber is filled with fog. We need to prepare the device for operation again, and preparation already takes ten seconds. This is the main drawback of the device - it has, as physicists say, “a large dead time.” (The outwardly simple principle of operation of the fog chamber (its description is even in school textbooks) does not give the slightest idea of ​​the physical and technical difficulties that arose during its manufacture and adjustment.) But ten years passed before more advanced “fog chamber” computing installations were developed, and with its help, outstanding discoveries were made. Therefore, Charles Thomson Rhys Wilson “for his method of making visible the paths of charged particles by means of vapor condensation” received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1927.

So, physicists have the opportunity to observe particles and determine many of their characteristics. Here in the picture (Fig. 1) there is a “fat trace” of an α-particle, and on the left is the first trace of rare drops formed by a fast electron. The difference is easy to explain if we use an approximate formula for the number of ions N formed on a path 1 cm long:

$$N \sim Z^2/v^2,$$

(1)

where Z is the charge of the particle, v is its speed ($[v] = cm/c$). An electron with an energy of 5 MeV moves almost at the speed of light, and an alpha particle with the same energy moves 20 times slower. In addition, it has 2 times the charge.

By measuring the curvature of the path of electrons in photographs, one can find their momentum. We will not dwell now on studies of the electron effect, which in themselves are extremely interesting.
