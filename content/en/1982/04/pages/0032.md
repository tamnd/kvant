M700. Is it possible to divide the set of all finite decimal fractions into a) two, b) three classes so that two numbers with a difference of $10^m$ do not fall into one class (for any integer m=0, ±1, ±2...)?

F708. A series of experiments were carried out to study the properties of the non-plinoio resistor. First, the dependence of the resistor resistance on temperature was investigated. When the temperature increased to a value of $t_1 = 100^\circ C$, the resistance changed from a value of $R_1 = 50$ Ohm to a value of $R_2 = 100$ Ohm; during cooling, the reverse rolling pin passed at a temperature of $t_2 = 99^\circ C$ (Fig. 1).

In the second experiment, a constant voltage $U_1 = 60$ V was applied to the resistor, at which its temperature turned out to be equal to $t_3 = 80^\circ C$.

Finally, when a constant voltage $U_2 = 80$ V was applied to the resistor, self-resistance current oscillations appeared in the circuit. Determine the period of these oscillations, as well as the maximum current value. The air temperature in the laboratory is constant and equal to $t_0 = 20^\circ C$. The heat transfer from the resistor is proportional to the difference in temperature and the surrounding air. Temperature resistor $c = 3\Delta J/K$.

6) Answer: you can. Let us divide the set of all finite decimal fractions into 3 classes, combining into one class those fractions whose sums of digits give the same remainders when divided by 3. It is easy to see that the difference of any two fractions belonging to the same class has a sum of digits divisible by 3, and therefore is not equal to $10^k$ for any $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

The tellan power released when current passes through a resistor, part of it goes to heating the resistor, part of the dissipation in the surrounding space. In a state of thermal equilibrium (when the temperature of the resistor remains constant), all power generated is dissipated.

Let us denote by α the coefficient of proportionality between the power dissipated by the resistor and the temperature difference between the resistor and the surrounding air.

At temperature $t_3 = 80^\circ C$ the resistor resistance is $R_1 = 50$ Ohm (see Fig. 1), and $U_1^2/R_1 = \alpha(t_3 - t_0)$ (where $U_1 = 60$ V), whence

$$\alpha = U_1^2/R_1(t_3 - t_0) = 1,2 \ B^2/(\Omega \cdot \text{град}).$$

Self-resistance fluctuation of current at a voltage across the resistor $U_2 = 80$ V will occur as a result of fluctuations in the resistance value of the resistor. When the temperature of the resistor becomes $t_1 = 100^\circ C$, its rolling pin resistance increases from $R_1 = 50$ Ohm to $R_2 = 100$ Ohm. As a result, the thermal power released on the resistor decreases; the resistor begins to cool than the tap. At a temperature of $t_1 = 100^\circ C$ rolling pin resistance will occur again and the process repeats.

The period of current oscillations in the circuit is equal to $T = t_1 + t_2$, where $t_1$ is the heating time of the resistor from $99^\circ C$ to $100^\circ C$, $t_2$ is the cooling time from $100^\circ C$ to $99^\circ C$.

Let's write the heat balance equation:

$$\frac{U_2^2}{R_1} \tau_1 = \tau_1 \alpha(t_1 - t_0) + c(t_1 - t_2),$$

$$\frac{U_2^2}{R_2} \tau_2 = \tau_2 \alpha(t_1 - t_0) - c'(t_1 - t_2)$$
