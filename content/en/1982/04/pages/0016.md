⟦folio 14⟧

At the beginning of our century, after the discovery of radioactivity, the situation seemed to be getting better. A significant amount of radioactive elements is always present in the soil, in the walls of a building, in materials, and finally, in the experimenters themselves. Radioactive gases enter the atmosphere (although they quickly decay). This radiation coming from everywhere “breaks” molecules and leads to air poisoning.

Experimental technology has improved; relatively simple instruments have been replaced by complex ones - electron meters, which can consume even several tens of ions per cubic centimeter of air. To further increase the sensitivity of the electronmeters and get rid of the external background, they were surrounded by a layer of lead that absorbed external radiation, and kept for several days so that the radioactive gases inside the device decayed. And yet the current discharged. It was hypothesized that energetic radiation from the earth's rocks penetrates through lead. And since the beginning of our century was marked by amazing successes in the effects of radiation, why not raise it above the surface of the Earth and thereby move away from its radiation? And so it was done. First we try to use the Eiffel Tower (300 m), then, in 1909-10, K. Haeckel climbed to a height of about 4 km using a balloon. The most accurate measurements at that time were carried out by V. Hess. According to his data, the discharge current initially decreased and then quickly increased; at an altitude of 5 km it was already three times greater than at the surface of the Earth. Hex proposed that there is penetrating radiation, otherwise from above and attenuated by the atmosphere.

In 1922-26, work carried out by a group of American physicists under the leadership of R. Millikan on the influence of balloons (they rose up to 5 km), in mountainous areas, under water, made it possible to accurately establish that unknown radiation falls on the Earth from outer space. Millikan called it cosmic. In order to understand the nature of this radiation, the onization method was no longer suitable - it registered only a consequence (onization), which could be caused by a variety of reasons. To advance further in the study of cosmic rays, the physicist needed “the most original and amazing instrument” (these are Rutherford’s words) that existed at that time for recording particles - a cloud chamber.

Wilson chamber
(1911—1912)

A stream of moist air, heated by the earth, rises. In higher layers of the atmosphere, air masses expand and simultaneously cool. Now the water vapor is in a supersaturated state and begins to condense, collecting into droplets of moisture. We say: “the sky is covered with captive fog.” The process of droplet growth in supersaturated vapor is not simple. Thus, scientists have found that for fog to form, condensation centers are necessary, otherwise the steam may remain supersaturated for a considerable time. Most often, small dust particles become centers of condensation.

English physicist Charles Wilson also deals with these issues. It was he who discovered that centers of vapor condensation can become nones formed when supersaturated vapor is irradiated with RENTen rays or other types of radiation (α, β, γ). With his characteristic tenacity and enormous experimental skill (and Wilson was weakened by the fact that he made almost all the equipment himself), having worked on this topic for many years, scientists are creating a new device for detecting particles. He called it a “fog chamber,” but very soon the experimenters changed it to a “Cloud chamber.”
