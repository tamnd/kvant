calculating the change in current in any branch of the circuit when the EMF of one of the sources changes - the superposition method is very convenient. A change in EMF by magnitude ΔΦ can be represented as adding a series battery without internal resistance with an EMF equal to ΔΦ to a given source. It is clear that in this case there is no need to calculate all the simple circuits; it is enough to consider only one - with EMF ΔΦ. The resulting values ​​of currents in the branches will be the desired changes in currents.

Let us find, for example, the change in current through battery $\delta_2$ (in the previous problem) when the emf of the first battery ($\delta_1$) changes by ΔΦ. Let us use a circuit similar to 2,a, in which a battery with emf ΔΦ is connected in place $\delta_1$. In such a circuit, the current flowing through $r_2$ is equal to

$$\Delta I = \frac{\Delta \delta}{\rho_{06\Sigma}} \frac{R}{(r_2 + R)} = \frac{\Delta \delta \cdot R}{r_1 r_2 + R (r_1 + r_2)}$$

This is the change in current through the battery $\delta_2$ when the emf $\delta_1$ changes by ΔΦ.

Let's solve by the superposition method problem F709. Let us recall its conditions.

F709. The circuit shown in figure 3 was assembled. The emf of battery $\delta_1$ was reduced by 1.5 V, after which the current in various parts of the circuit changed. How do you need to change the emf of the battery $\delta_2$ so that

1) Has the current through the battery $\delta_2$ become neglected?
2) Has the current through the battery $\delta_1$ become neglected?

Neglect the internal resistance of the batteries.

Fig. 3.

figure 4 presents simple circuits for a circuit in which $\delta_1$ is replaced by ΔΦ, and $\delta_2$ by ΔΦ, where ΔΦ is the desired change in EMF $\delta_2$. In order for the current through the second battery to be neglected, the current flowing through this battery (that is, through the branches from which this battery is “thrown out”) in circuits 4, a and 4, b, must be equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

In circuit 4, and the current flowing from the battery $\delta_1$ develops, and a current ΔI flows into the diagonal of the resulting space, equal to the difference in the branching currents:

$$\Delta I = \frac{\Delta \delta}{R_{AB}} \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{\Delta \delta}{5R/4} \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{\Delta \delta}{5R}$$

In diagram 4, b

$$\Delta I = \frac{\Delta \delta}{R_{CD}} = \frac{\Delta \delta}{5R/4} = \frac{4\Delta \delta}{5R}$$

Equating the resulting expressions for ΔI, we find $\Delta \delta_2$:

$$\Delta \delta_2 = \Delta \delta_1/4$$

We will find the signs of changes, compare the polarity of the battery in the original circuit (Fig. 3) and in circuits 4, a and 4, b. In our case, battery $\delta_1$ is connected against battery $\delta_1$ (the total emf is less than $\delta_1$), and battery $\delta_2$ is conditionally directed against battery $\delta_2$, while we received $\Delta \delta_2 < 0$ - the emf of battery $\delta_2$ should be reduced. If it turned out that $\Delta \delta_2 > 0$, this would mean that the emf $\delta_2$ should be increased.
