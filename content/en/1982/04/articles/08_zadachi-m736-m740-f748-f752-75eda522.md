F710.*) When observing in the region beyond the limit of a drop, which increases in size, placing the smallest droplets encountered on its path, it was found that the drop moves all the time with constant acceleration. Determine this acceleration, assuming the initial drop size is small. Neglect the drag of air as the drop moves.

The change in momentum of a falling drop of variable mass in a short time $\Delta t$ is equal to
$$\Delta(mv) = mg \cdot \Delta t.$$ (1)

According to the condition, the change in mass of the drop over time $\Delta t$ is equal to
$$\Delta m = a\varrho v_{\text{cp}} s \cdot \Delta t,$$ (2)

where $\varrho$ is the density of water, $v_{\text{cp}}$ is the average speed of the drop over time $\Delta t$, $s = 4\pi r^2$ is the surface area of ​​the drop, $\alpha$ is the dimensionless proportionality coefficient. On the other hand, since $m = 4/3\pi r^3 \varrho$ ($r$ is the radius of the drop),
$$\Delta m = 4\pi r^2 \varrho \cdot \Delta r = s\varrho \cdot \Delta r.$$

If in time $\Delta t$ the drop travels a distance $\Delta y$, then $\Delta t = \Delta y/v_{\text{cp}}$. Substituting the expressions for $\Delta m$ and $\Delta t$ into (2), we obtain
$$\Delta r = \alpha \cdot \Delta y \sim \Delta y.$$

Consequently, the radius of the drop increases in proportion to the distance traveled, that is, $r \sim y$.

Since the drop moves with constant acceleration $a$, $y = at^2/2 \sim t^2$. So, $r \sim t^2$, $m \sim r^3 \sim t^6$. Taking these relations into account, from (1) we obtain
$$\Delta(t^6 at) \sim t^6 g \cdot \Delta t.$$

Carrying out the following differentiation, we find $a$:
$$\Delta(at^7) = 7at^6 \cdot \Delta t = t^6 g \cdot \Delta t \Rightarrow a = g/7.$$
