Do I need to look for ODZ?

The traditional type of problems on university entrance exams are equations, inequalities, systems (equations or inequalities).

Numerous manuals for schoolchildren and applicants, when solving problems, often give an unconditional recommendation: “First we look for ODZ!” Apparently, an echo of this recommendation is the artistic recognition “Remember ODZ!”, which hung in one of the Leningrad vocational schools.

First of all, we note that the abbreviation ODZ is not in current school textbooks. The phrase “Areas of Acceptable Values” means “areas of definition.”

The OD of an equation or inequality is, by definition, the intersection of the OD of its left and right sides.

So, is it necessary to start solving an equation (inequality, system) with the Japanese ODZ? Is it necessary, when solving an equation (inequality, system), to look for the ODZ, especially starting the solution with the Japanese ODZ? as a rule, that is, in most of the equations (inequalities, systems) usually proposed in entrance exams, there is no need to start the solution with the Japanese ODZ; Sometimes, in some specially selected or more difficult equations (inequalities, systems), it is useful to start the solution with the Japanese ODZ.

As a rule, there is no need

$$\sqrt{x^2-3x+4}=1-3x. \tag{1}$$

Equation (1) is equivalent to the system

$$\begin{cases} x^2-3x+4=(1-3x)^2 \\ 1-3x\geq 0, \end{cases}$$

which is no longer difficult to solve. (By the way, the inequality $x^2-3x+4\geq 0$, which determines the ODZ of equation (1), follows from the equation included in system (2).) You can also, if desired, move from equation (1) to the derived*) equation $x^2-3x+4=(1-3x)^2$ and check the found roots. (Having solved together equations (1) and system (2) that is equivalent to it, there is, of course, no need to do a check.) This example: goes not to the equivalent, but to the inferential one, and to do the check must be kept in mind in the other examples under consideration.

It is easy to prove that any equation of the form

$$\sqrt{f(x)}=g(x) \tag{3}$$

equivalent to the system

$$\begin{cases} f(x)=[g(x)]^2 \\ g(x)\geq 0. \end{cases}$$

Indeed. If $x_0$ is the root of equation (3), that is, the numerical equation $\sqrt{f(x_0)}=g(x_0)$ is valid, then $f(x_0)=[g(x_0)]^2$ and $g(x_0)\geq 0$; This means that $x_0$ is a solution to system (4). If, conversely, $x_0$ is a solution to system (4), that is, the numerical equation $f(x_0)=[g(x_0)]^2$ and $g(x_0)\geq 0$ are valid, then $\sqrt{f(x_0)}=\sqrt{[g(x_0)]^2}=|g(x_0)|$; since $g(x_0)\geq 0$, $|g(x_0)|=g(x_0)$; hence, $x_0$ is the root of equation (3).

In equation (1), the search for ODZ leads to an unnecessary, but easily performed calculation. However, sometimes insurmountable difficulties arise for applicants who are accustomed to always looking for educational qualifications. Thus, establishing the ODZ equation $\sqrt{x^3-x+1}=\sqrt{1-x}$, he, conversely, fails to cope with the inequality $x^3-x+1\geq 0$, while the equation in question is equal to

In general, any equation of the form $\sqrt{f(x)} = \sqrt{g(x)}$ is equivalent to “choice” of each of the systems
$$\begin{cases} f(x) = g(x) \\ f(x) \geqslant 0 \end{cases}$$

By choosing the “easier” one, you can usually get by without searching for DZ.

The equation $\sqrt{x+4} - \sqrt{x-1} = 1$ is equivalent to the equation
$$\sqrt{x+4} = 1 + \sqrt{x-1},$$
(5)

which, in turn, is equivalent to the equation
$$x+4 = (1 + \sqrt{x-1})^2.$$

Indeed, if $x_0$ is the root of equation (5), then it is, of course, also the root of equation (6): the equation $[f(x)]^2 = [g(x)]^2$ in serdda, that is, for any $f$ and $g$, is derived from the equation $f(x) = g(x)$. If, conversely, $x_0$ is the root of equation (6), that is, the numerical equality $x_0 + 4 = (1 + \sqrt{x_0-1})^2$ is true, then, first of all, $x_0 + 4 \geqslant 0$; means $\sqrt{x_0+4} = \sqrt{(1 + \sqrt{x_0-1})^2} = |1 + \sqrt{x_0-1}|$; since $1 + \sqrt{x_0-1} \geqslant 0$, $|1 + \sqrt{x_0-1}| = 1 + \sqrt{x_0-1}$; therefore, $x_0$ is the root of equation (5).

Equation (6) is equivalent to each of the equations
$$x+4 = 1 + 2\sqrt{x-1} + x-1,$$
$$\sqrt{x-1} = 2,$$
$$x-1 = 4.$$

Equation $\sqrt{x+3} + \sqrt{3x-2} = 7$ is equivalent, of course, to equation $\sqrt{x+3} = 7 - \sqrt{3x-2},$ (7)
but, unlike the previous case, equation (7) is not equivalent to equation $x+3 = (7 - \sqrt{3x-2})^2$; it is equivalent, in view of the equivalence of (3) and (4) proven above, to the system
$$\begin{cases} x+3 = (7 - \sqrt{3x-2})^2 \\ 7 - \sqrt{3x-2} \geqslant 0 \end{cases}$$
and, further, to each of the systems
$$\begin{cases} x+3 = 49 - 14\sqrt{3x-2} + 3x-2 \\ \sqrt{3x-2} \leqslant 7, \end{cases}$$
$$\begin{cases} 7\sqrt{3x-2} = x+22 \\ \sqrt{3x-2} \leqslant 7, \end{cases}$$
$$\begin{cases} 49(3x-2) = (x+22)^2 \\ 3x-2 \leqslant 49. \\ x+22 \geqslant 0. \end{cases}$$

The equation $\sqrt{x-2} + \sqrt{1-x} = 3$ can be solved in a similar way, but in this case it is easier to immediately say that its ODZ is an empty set and, therefore, it has roots.

Let's move on to inequalities
$$3\sqrt{6+x-x^2} > 4x-2.$$

Inequality (8) is equivalent to the totality
$$\begin{cases} 4x-2 \geqslant 0 \\ 9(6+x-x^2) > (4x-2)^2 \end{cases}$$

$$\begin{cases} 4x-2 < 0 \\ 6+x-x^2 \geqslant 0. \end{cases}$$

(Solving the second from system (9), we at the same time find the ODZ of inequality (8).)

In fact, let first $x_0$ be a solution to inequality (8), that is, the numerical inequality $3\sqrt{6+x-x^2} > 4x-2$ is valid. Let us prove that then $x_0$ is a solution to one of the systems (9). Let's consider two cases: $4x_0-2 \geqslant 0$ and $4x_0-2 < 0$. If $4x_0-2 \geqslant 0$, then by one of the properties of numerical inequalities (if $a > b \geqslant 0$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$, then $a^n > b^n)$ $9(6+x-x^2) > (4x_0-2)^2$; also, in this case, $x_0$ is a solution to the first from system (9). If $4x_0-2 < 0$, then $x_0$ is a solution to the second from system (9), since it is true and, naturally, $\sqrt{6+x-x^2} \geqslant 0$; from $4x_0-2 < 0$ follows $\sqrt{6+x-x^2} > 4x_0-2$, that is, $x_0$ is again a solution to inequality (8).

It is easy to prove that any inequality of the form $\sqrt{f(x)} > g(x)$ is equivalent to the totality of the system
$$\begin{cases} g(x) \geqslant 0 \\ f(x) > [g(x)]^2 \end{cases}$$

(The inequality $\sqrt{f(x)} \geqslant g(x)$ can be solved in a similar way.)
