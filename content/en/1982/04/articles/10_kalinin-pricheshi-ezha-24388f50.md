Comb the hedgehog

Did you notice the figure on page four of the cover?
The polyhedron with bizarrely protruding prickly edges is the very “hedgehog” that we have to “comb.” The hedgehog puzzle consists of 8 wooden pentagons, fastened with an elastic band: by turning the halves of the hedgehog relative to each other, you need to give it the correct, “combed” shape (Fig. 1).

But before you comb the hedgehog, you need to make it.

Let's make a hedgehog

The easiest way to make our puzzle is from children's wooden cubes. To do this, you will need eight cubes (for those who are good at geometry, three is enough). You also need scraps of thin elastic and eight rings that can be cut from a plastic tube. Instead of rings, small buttons are also suitable. We recommend doing the puzzle in the following sequence:

1. Cut out 8 pentagons from the cubes (Fig. 2).
2. Using a knife and file, round off the edges of mutually perpendicular edges so that they do not touch each other when turning.
3. Drill a channel inside each pentagon, extending from the apex where the three straight lines meet.

from the corner, to a point on the opposite edge (Fig. 3). The channels can be drilled with a nail or a thin drill.

4. Pass an elastic band through the channels of two pentagons and tie it with rings or buttons as shown in figure 3. Continue the same with the remaining three in pairs. Please note that in figure 3 the elastic is shown stretched. It needs to press the pentahedrons against each other.

5. Paint the edges of the puzzle, sand the rubbing parts, cover them with varnish, and sand them again.

6. Assemble the puzzle by hooking four pairs of pentagons connected by rubber bands to each other, so that you get a “combed” hedgehog (Fig. 1, $\theta$).

Now you can play: rotate one half of the puzzle relative to the other several times. Do this in different planes. After turning, the edges of the puzzle begin to “stick out” in different directions. This can be seen on the cover of the magazine and on figure 1, $a$. Now try to return the puzzle to its original position. You will be surprised that this turns out to be a difficult task. Difficult, but also very interesting.

When playing, keep in mind that the rubber hinge can withstand about 100 turns, after which you need to spend a few minutes untangling the rubber bands and the puzzle will be ready to play again.

Where did the hedgehog come from?

In science and technology, it happens that one discovery only leads to the rapid emergence of many new interesting ideas and images. Something similar happened with the Magic Cube puzzle. We can assume that our hedgehog is a relative of the banner cube.

In the Magic Cube, 26 small cups in groups of nine are rotated in six planes. This results in a very difficult puzzle, since the number of possible arrangements of small cups in a large one exceeds 43 quintillion, that is, $43 \cdot 10^{18}$ (it is necessary to devote several pages of the journal to finding the only correct state of the cube).

If you reduce the number of cups along the edge of the puzzle from three to two, you get an eight cup puzzle (Fig. 4). She didn’t bother herself either - another article in our magazine is dedicated to her: “Turning the cubes” («Kvant», 1981, No. 12)

Let's look at a few more ideas that can help you get new original puzzles. Our goal is to learn how to invent and make magic puzzles of varying complexity ourselves.

Idea one: change the shape

If you set yourself the goal of inventing a new puzzle, it is not difficult to come to the conclusion that the moving elements of the puzzle do not necessarily have to be cubes. And the puzzle itself may not look like a cube, but, for example, a sphere (Fig. 5). The difficulty of solving the puzzles in Figures 4 and 5 is the same. But if we take other polyhedra as the basis for a cube puzzle, then new puzzles of varying complexity will appear (Fig. 6). The transition from a colored cube to other colored polyhedrons is the first idea for obtaining new puzzles.

To understand how and which polyhedra can be used to create puzzles, let us note four of their properties:

1. All sections that bisect a colored polyhedron and allow it to be rotated are congruent regular polyhedra whose centers coincide.

2. There must be at least three such sections.

3. Sections can pass along the edges of the polyhedron, as on figure 6 for the octahedron, cuboctahedron and icoododecahedron, or intersect faces, as at the same figure for the icoododecahedron.

4. Each movable element of a colored polyhedron is itself a polyhedron, some of the faces of which are always located outside the puzzle, and the remaining faces are always entirely inside.

Based on the properties described and the examples shown in figure 6, you can come up with your own versions of colored polyhedron puzzles. If you have a penchant for construction, consider making a hinge mechanism that will make the puzzle work more securely than our rubber bands. In this regard, I advise you to look at the article by M. Evgrafov “Mechanics of the Magic Cube” in the previous issue «Kvant».

Idea two: change the coloring

If in one of our colored polyhedra all the faces are painted the same color, then there will be no puzzle. The two-color puzzle on figure 7 can be in seven different states. This is of course a very simple puzzle. But it becomes dramatically more complicated if, simply, we move the color boundary (Fig. 8). Why is this happening?

The fact is that the red-blue cubes of the puzzle from figure 8 may, after a series of rotations, end up in their places, but differently oriented (for example, having a horizontal blue edge). If the same series of rotations is performed with the puzzle from figure 7, then the corresponding blue cubes will look the same as in the original position. In other words, the same state of the puzzle from figure 7 corresponds to different states of the puzzle with shifted coloring. The puzzle has become more difficult!

By combining the ideas of changing colors and shapes, using the idea of ​​“protruding edges,” you can come up with many different puzzles.
