Proton radioactivity
This phenomenon was discovered by scientists relatively recently, but already has its own history.
In 1962, the so-called delayed proton radioactivity was discovered at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna, when a proton escaped from an excited nucleus formed after the nucleus emitted a positron.
In 1970, the next step was taken - the proton radioactivity of nuclear reactors, that is, nuclei with a long lifetime in an excited state, was recorded.
Finally, in 1981, physicists from Germany observed the emission of a proton from a nucleus in a state with the lowest possible energy, or, as they say, in the ground state.
Note that proton decay is “allowed” only for nuclei whose number of neutrons is significantly less than that of the lightest stable isotope of a given element. Such nuclei are called neutron-deficient. For example, $^{118}$Cs, a delayed proton emitter, has 15 fewer neutrons than the stable isotope $^{133}$Cs.
Proton decay from the ground state produced nuclei with an even greater deficiency of neutrons, and they are not easy to obtain. Scientists have followed this path. A beam of nickel, accelerated in a powerful accelerator, was sent to a mine made of ruthenium or molybdanum. Nuclear nickel, merging with minsch nuclei, turned into neutron-deficient isotopes of lutetium or thulium:
$$\begin{align*}
58Ni+96Ru\rightarrow^{151}Lu+p+2n, \\
58Ni+92Mo\rightarrow^{147}Tm+p+2n,
\end{align*}
$$
which then experienced proton decay.
Measurements have shown that when lutetium is converted into interesting:
$$\begin{align*}
151Lu\rightarrow^{150}Yb+p \\
\end{align*}
$$
the proton energy is 1.22 MeV, and its lures period is 85±10 ms. For the thulium isotope these values ​​are 1.04 MeV and 420±100 ms, respectively.
Now the physicist remains to discover the last type of proton decay predicted by the theories - two-proton radioactivity, that is, the simultaneous emission of two protons by a nucleus.
