M748. a) Is it possible to have a finite number of parabolas on a plane so that their interior areas cover the entire plane? (We call the inner region of a parabola the convex figure whose boundary is this parabola.)

6)* There are several non-intersecting ends in space. Prove that they cannot be moved so that they cover the entire space. (We call here a cone an unlimited convex figure obtained as a result of the treatment of neotorodo uel around its bissertis.)

a) Let's draw an arbitrary straight line that is not parallel to any of the axes of the parabolas. Such a straight line can intersect with the interior region of any of the parabolas only along an interval. In fact, the equation of parabolas in a certain coordinate system has the form $y = ax^2$ ($a > 0$), and the equation of a straight line has the form $y = kx + l$; The abscissas of the points of the line belonging to the interior of the parabola satisfy the inequality $kx + l > ax^2$, the solution set of which is either empty. Or is the interval $x_1 < x < x_2$, where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the roots of the equation $ax^2 - kx - l = 0$.

It follows from this that on the drawn line he will find points that do not belong to any of the interior regions of the parabolas.

6) Let $O$ be an arbitrary point in space and $K'$ be a cone obtained from the cone $K$ with vertex $Q$ by parallel translation taking point $Q$ to point $O$. Let us draw an arbitrary ray $l$ with its origin at point $O$. Let us consider the sections of the ends $K$ and $K'$ by a plane passing through the ray $l$ and the point $Q$ (Fig. 1). It intersects cones at two angles with parallel sides; Using this, it is easy to verify that the ray $l$ intersects the cone $K$ along the ray if and only if it is entirely contained in the cone $K'$. Otherwise, the ray $l$ intersects with the cone $K$ along a segment or at one point $Q$ or does not intersect at all.

We now intersect the vertices of all ends at the point $O$ and prove that no two cones $K'_1$ and $K'_2$ have a common point $A \neq O$. Then the ray $l = [OA)$ is contained in each of them. As was shown, in this case the ray $l$ intersects with the cone $K_1$ along some ray $l_1$, and with the cone $K_2$ along the ray $l_2$. But then the rays $l_1 \cap l_2$ belong to the common cone $K_1$ and $K_2$. Contradiction.

Let us now consider a sphere with center at point $O$. It intersects with each end of the “bouquet” along a certain “spherical cap” (Fig. 2). Since all the “caps” do not intersect in pairs, their total area is less than the area of ​​the sphere. Therefore, no matter how we move them around the sphere, it is impossible to cover the name of our sphere. This means that for any location of the ends in space on the sphere, it will find a point that is not covered by “caps”. If $A$ is such a point, then the ray $[OA)$ can intersect with cones only along a finite number of segments. Therefore, on this ray it will completely find points that do not belong to any of the ends.
