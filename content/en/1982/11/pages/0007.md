⟦folio 5⟧

Atoms grow larger throughout the crystal

About a hundred years ago, in 1896, the English metallurgist William Roberts-Austen performed such an experiment. He pressed the thin gold disk firmly against the polished end of a pure lead cylinder, made sure that the contact was sufficiently tight, and placed the pair in an oven at 200°C for ten days. When the annealing ended, it turned out that it was no longer possible to separate the metals. Then the experimenter cut the composite cylinder along the axis and, having measured it under a microscope, was convinced that the gold and lead had penetrated into each other and mixing had occurred.

THE INTRODUCTION OF ONE SUBSTANCE INTO ANOTHER IS CALLED DIFFUSION. It is diffusion that leads to the creation of a carburized zone in steel, which ensures the joining of metals during welding, soldering, chrome plating, nickel plating, sintering of powders and many other processes.

Why do atoms (or molecules) of one substance penetrate inside another? How to calculate the depth of this penetration? What does it depend on? In this article we will try to answer these questions.

Why will this be prescribed?

Diffusion in gases and liquids has been known for a long time. Everyone knows well that if successive portions of different gases are introduced into a vessel, then after some time all the gases will mix homogeneously: the number of particles of each type per unit volume of the vessel will become constant, and the concentrations will equalize. If we carefully pour two mixing liquids one on top of the other into a narrow test tube (for example, water and a solution of copper sulfate), we will see that the interface (between light water and dark blue sulfate), very clear at the first moment, begins to gradually melt. A transition zone will appear, which will increase in size until the entire liquid becomes homogeneous in composition (we will see this by its uniform color). The process proceeds very slowly, and if you take so much liquid several centimeters high, the difference in color (and therefore concentration) in the upper and lower ends of the test tube will be noticeable even a day after the start of the experiment. But eventually the liquid will become homogeneous.

Thus, we come to a new definition of diffusion: diffusion is the spontaneous equalization of concentrations. Atoms (or molecules) of type A will be mixed where there are fewer of them or none at all and where there are more atoms of type B, and vice versa. As a result, stirring will be prescribed.

However, diffusion in solids up to Roberts-Austen was not observed.
