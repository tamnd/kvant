Attempts to prove the fifth applicant have continued since then for 2000 years. They have been undertaken by many scientists. Here is an incomplete list: Greeks Ptolemy (2nd century AD, the same Ptolemy, “whose system”) and Proclus (5th century), Arabal-Khaysam (10th century), Persian (or Tajik) Omar Khayyam (11th century - early 12th century, the same Khayyam who is known as the Great Pozt), Azerbaijani-Tusi (13th century), German Clavius-Chlusel (1514; hereinafter data works) ial Cataldi (1603), Borrelli (1658) and Vitale (1680), English Valisse (1663), Italian Saccheri (1733), German Lambert (1766), French Bertrand (1778) and Legendre (1794, 1823), Russian Guryev (1798). All their attempts boiled down to the fact that the fifth applicant was removed from some other position. At the same time, they did not replace this much, believing that they succeeded in the proof. Others, more insightful and critical, clearly formulated the position from which the fifth applicant was derived, as, for example, Omar Khayyam did.

The tension of Ponk proofs with the rapid development of mathematics in the 17th and 18th centuries. increased.

Significant conditions were made by the Italian monk, teacher of mathematics and grammar Girolamo Saccheri, whose work with an attempt to prove the fifth applicant appeared in 1733 - the year of his death. It is called "Euclid, cleared of all stains, or a geometric attempt to establish the first principles of all geometry." Starting from the work of his predecessors, Saccheri tries to prove the fifth proposition by contradiction - having accepted an assumption that is equivalent to the negation of the fifth proposition, he deduced consequences from it, trying to arrive at a contradiction. But since the negation of the fifth applicant is Lobachevsky’s axiom, the conclusions that Saccheri received were nothing more and no less than Lobachevsky’s theories of geometry. In other words, Saccheri developed a new geometry without, however, understanding what he was doing.

He did not come to a contradiction, but still concluded that he was able to prove the fifth entry, although, apparently, he was not in this matter. He seemed to convince himself when he wrote about the hypothesis that was tantamount to denying the fifth arrival, that he had “pulled out this harmful hypothesis by the roots.”

Of the fairly numerous (55) that appeared in the 18th century. works on the theory of parallels, the “Theory of Parallels” written in 1766 by I. G. Lamberti, a German mathematician, physicist and astronomer, especially stands out. Conducting the proof of the fifth argument by contradiction, Lamberti drew many consequences from its negation. He, one might say, largely entered into the basics of Lobachevsky's geometry. There was no contradiction in his conclusions, and he did not think he had found it, as almost all his predecessors had done. Lamberti even expressed the idea that he “almost must conclude” that the hypothesis he was refuting “takes place on some minimal sphere.” But still he remained confident that geometry based on the negation of the fifth arrival was impossible. His work did not, however, provide evidence for this belief. Therefore, one must think that he was still dissatisfied and did not publish it. It was published only in 1786 - 9 years after his death and 20 years after it was written. In general, Lamberti came very close to the discovery of a new geometry, but did not make it.

The German mathematicians Schweickart (1818) and Taurinus (1825) came close to understanding the possibility of non-Euclidean geometry, but they still did not express a clearly expressed idea that their theory would be stolbologically legitimate, like Euclidean geometry.
