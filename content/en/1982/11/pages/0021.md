⟦folio 19⟧

“Who did he communicate with?”, “When and where did he die?”—the questions, the answers to which constitute a person’s biography, are unlikely to be answered by many readers of his books, people skilled in giving answers to the intricate questions of nature. Neither in the first, nor in the tenth, nor in the twenty editions of Perelman’s books was there room for a brief biographical note about their author.

Fortunately, information about Yakov Isidorovich Perelman is available in the funds of the Leningrad branch of the Archive of the USSR Academy of Sciences. The archive is located on the Universitetskaya embankment in Leningrad, next to the important Kunstkamera. It stores papers issued by reporting and foreign scientists, in particular those who were found in Perelman’s books. If there are many such documents - letters, manuscripts, etc. - they form a special fund. The Yakov Isidorovich Foundation is numbered 796; it is quite general, and its description takes up four large pages. The information below is taken from this fund.

Ya. I. Perelman was born on November 22, 1882 in Bialystok into the family of an employee. In 1901, he moved to St. Petersburg and entered the Forestry Institute (now the Forestry Academy named after S. M. Kirov), where his older brother Osip, later the famous writer Osip Dymov, was already studying. (From a letter from O. I. Dymov to his younger brother, one can learn about some details of the biography of Yakov Isidorovich.) However, it is not 1901, the year of moving from the provinces to the capital, that Ya. I. Perelman identifies as a turning point in his life, but 1899 - from him he traces the absence of the life of a writer who chose exact sciences as the heroes of his works. This year in Russia, and in other countries too, a lot was written about the comet, which was supposed to pass near the Earth, and about the stream of meteorological bodies generated by it. There were rumors, as often happened, almost about the end of the world, but on September 23, 1899, an article appeared in the Grodno Provincial Gazette newspaper, refuting these rumors. It was called “Concerning the expected meteorological rain”, its author was the hypnasist Yakov Perelmen, but he hid his last name under a pseudonym. (He often spoke, published articles and notes, under various pseudonyms, one of which sounded characteristic: Tsyfirkin.) The fact is that, according to the charter of hypnasia, their students did not have the right to publish their works in print until they graduated from the educational institution.

When a catalog of his works was published in Leningrad in 1924 to mark the 25th anniversary of Ya. I. Perelman’s scientific popularization activities, it opened with reviews about him written by the patriarch of Russian physics, Orest Daniilovich Khvolson, the author of the most famous course in general physics in the 1920s. Khvolson wrote: “The author has gained well-deserved fame for his popular works, among which “Entertaining Physics” takes first place. This is truly an entertaining book, interesting even for a physics specialist. It contains erroneous and varied material; the presentation is easy and correct.” Then the catalog provided a list of 17 books written by Perelman over 25 years of work, and the total circulation of their publication was more than a million copies - a figure that was gigantic at that time. (For comparison, we indicate more figures: seven editions of the first part of “Entertaining Physics” amounted to a total of 90,000 copies, and the 17th edition (1965) available to the author of these lines was published in a circulation of 100,000 copies; after that, the book was published three more times. In 1981, another yearbook published by UNESCO, “Index Translationum,” was published. In it collected information about all the books in the world translated from one language to any other during 1977. Perelman’s books were published in translation 17 times in the USSR, as well as in the German Democratic Republic, Czechoslovakia, Spain and Japan.)
