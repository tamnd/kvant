Mathematics in the USSR

December 30 marks sixty years since the First All-Union Congress of Soviets solemnly passed the decree on the creation of the world's first multinational socialist state - the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. This year, the gift turned out to be the united peoples and nationalities inhabiting our country.

Initially, in 1922, the Soviet Union included only four union republics - the RSFSR, the Ukrainian SSR, the BSSR, and the ZSFSR. Two years later, the Uzbek and Turkmen Union Republics became part of the USSR. In 1929 they were joined by the Tajik Republic. After the Kazakh and Kyrgyz autonomous republics of the RSFSR achieved certain successes in economic and cultural development, in 1936 they were transformed into union ones. In the same year, the Transcaucasian federation was divided along national lines into three union republics - Azerbaijan, Armenian and Georgian. The Republics of Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia and Moldova joined the Union in 1940. Fifteen equal republics went through a long and difficult path of economic and cultural development, together they withstood all the tests and won the Great Patriotic War.

The Soviet Union began to exist in very difficult conditions. Three long years of destructive imperialist war, which led to the revolutionary explosion of 1917, ruined the country. The next five years of the Civil War, intervention, famine and devastation deprived it of a huge number of producers - peasants, workers, engineers, teachers, doctors. It was necessary to immediately begin to restore the economy destroyed by the war, LINK to see the mass homelessness of children.

But tsarism also left other difficult legacies - almost 75% of the population of expensive Russia was illiterate. On the national screens the situation was many times worse. Thus, among the Kyrgyz, literacy reached only 0.6%, among the Turkmens - 0.7%, among the Tajiks - 0.5%, among the Uzbeks - 1.5%. At best, there were only a few people with higher education and belonging to the elite nationalities of the royal outskirts. In the vast expanses of Belarus, Transcaucasia, Central Asia, and Transkaikalia there was not a single higher educational institution. The peoples inhabiting these territories were virtually deprived of the opportunity to grow and flourish.

Representatives of the 2nd generation remember well how immediately after the Great Patriotic War they reached out for knowledge. Then schools were opened in the deepest corners of our homeland.

But Soviet power was not limited to the development of only the primary stage of education. The Civil War was still raging and famine had its terrible harvest, and the Soviet government began to create universities on the former outskirts of Russia, cut off from education for centuries (Irkutsk, Baku, Yerevan, Tbilisi, Tashkent). Over the years of their existence, these universities have trained decimal specialists.
