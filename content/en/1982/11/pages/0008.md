Let's try to figure this out.

Why does mixing happen? What causes particles of different types to mix? The answer to these questions seems very clear: mixing is the result of thermal motion of particles. But behind this “obvious” answer a legitimate question arises: why, as a result of thermal movement, particles are not separated by type? Let's take an empty vessel and let in a portion of nitrogen and a portion of oxygen. The gases will mix. Let's wait: maybe after some time they will separate - in one half of the vessel there will be nitrogen, in the other - oxygen? Not so. In the same way, it is easy to mix salt and sugar (the tell movement has nothing to do with it, they need to be shaken), but it is hopeless to wait until they separate again.

The reasons why particles mix are associated with the tendency of any system consisting of a large number of particles towards disorder, a chaotic arrangement of particles. These principles operate equally in gases, liquids, and solids, leading to random movements, random wanderings of particles that create disorder. In this sense, it would seem that there is nothing surprising in the fact that solid gold and lead mutually penetrate each other. Nevertheless, this experimental fact is extremely surprising. Why?

Let us remember what movements particles make in various states of matter. In gases, a particle, before colliding with another particle, moves rectilinearly, making a free run. After a collision, the particle flies in a new direction and with a new speed, a segment of a new straight line until the next collision. It is clear that such movement leads to mixing and diffusion.

In a mixing fluid, particles during thermal motion are comparable to their sizes. However, the positions of the particles, as in a gas, are not fixed; the particle mixes all the time and, as a result, moves far from the initial position over a sufficiently long time. This movement also leads to mixing.

This is not the case in crystalline solids. For a crystal, the character is an ordered arrangement of atoms in an order of defined places - at the nodes of the crystal lattice. The crystal lattice is a collection of repeating completely identical geometric bodies, the so-called elementary cells. No matter how much we “walk” around the crystal, everything in any place is arranged exactly the same.

The main type of thermal movements that atoms perform in solids is a small one near the equilibrium - a lattice node. Underlining - small. The amplitude of these oscillations is much less than the distance between the nodes. An atom performing such oscillations has no chance of getting into a neighboring node.

In the light of all that has been said, the mutual penetration of atoms of gold and lead is an amazing substance. How do they do it? What is the mechanism for shuffling atoms?

How does this happen?

No matter how different the mechanisms of a larger particle in gases, liquids and solids are, it is important that these larger ones are random. It is impossible to predict in advance where a gas molecule will go after the next collision. She can sing with equal probability in any direction.

An interesting problem arises. Suppose that at an arbitrary point in space, which we take as the origin of coordinates, there is a group of particles capable of making only random large particles. Suppose that at some point in time, which we take as the beginning of the cutoff, they all begin to move. Let us ask ourselves: where will the parts be located?
