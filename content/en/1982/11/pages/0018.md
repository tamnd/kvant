He wrote to Bers: “I am increasingly coming to the conviction that the necessity of our geometry cannot be proven, at least by human reason and for human reason.” Since he writes “I am coming more and more,” it means that he has not yet arrived completely. He continues: “Perhaps in another life we ​​will come to other views on the nature of space that are now inaccessible to us. Until then, geometry has to be placed not on the same rank with argmetic, which exists purely a priori, but rather with mechanics...” At that time he far developed non-Euclidean geometry, but only in 1824, in a letter to Taurinus, he wrote definitely that non-Euclidean geometry, “in which the sum of the angles of a triangle is less than 180°, is completely consistent” and that he “developed it for himself completely satisfactorily.” However, it was only in 1831 that he undertook to present, at least briefly, his conclusions, but during his entire life he never published anything about non-Euclidean geometry. In 1829, in a letter to Bessel, he wrote: “I am afraid of a cry if I express my views...”). He is rich in undermining his scientific authority.

But when Gauss wrote all this, a man had already been found who had not only completely satisfactorily developed geometry, negatively the fifth postulate, and not only had come to the conviction that this geometry was completely consistent, but, without fear of shouting anything, owed it all to the scientific community. It was Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky, who came to the conclusion about the possibility of non-Euclidean geometry back in 1824 and presented a report outlining its principles to the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Kazan University on February 23 (11), 1826; He published it in an expanded form in the work “On the Elements of Geometry” in a number of issues of the Kazan Bulletin, a scientific publication of Kazan University, from February 1829 to August 1830.

In 1835-38, Lobachevsky published a more developed presentation of his theory, “New principles of geometry from the complete theory of parallels,” in the preface to which he writes:

“The vain aging since the time of Euclid, for two thousand years, made me suspect that the most understandable still does not contain the truth that I wanted to prove and which, like other physical laws, can only be verified by experiments, such as, for example, astronomical observations.” For Lobachevsky, the question of the truth of this or that geometry would therefore be a question of experience; he considered his geometry as a possibility for a theory of the properties of real space, that is, the properties of the structure of the corresponding relationships of mathematical bodies and phenomena.

Almost simultaneously with Lobachevsky - in 1825 - the young General mathematician Janos Bolyai* came to the same geometry. Janos Bolyai presented his conclusions in 1832 as an appendix (Appendix) to the geometry textbook of his father Farkas Bolyai. Farkas Bolyai sent the textbook to Gauss. He responded approvingly to Janos’s results and wrote that he had known all this for a long time. Janos, who understood the significance of his discoveries, decided that Gauss simply attributed them to himself. He stopped his studies in non-Euclidean geometry for a long time. But Lobachevsky continued to develop his geometry and publish works outlining it until his death.
