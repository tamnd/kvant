⟦folio 7⟧

After time $t$, how far will they go from the starting point?

To answer this question, a number of assumptions follow that will simplify our task: we will assume that all parts of the movement are only along the $X$ axis; the particle takes each next step at equal intervals of time $\tau$ ($\tau$ is the time of “settled” life), and the length of each step is the same and equal to $\delta$; the probability of a step to the left and the probability of a step to the right are the same and equal to 1/2.

On figure 1 there is a kind of instant photograph of ten particles that began to move simultaneously, after a time $t$ from the beginning of the movement. (We placed the particles at the initial moment on the line $x=0$.) It is clear that it is impossible to predict the location of an individual particle after time $t$: during this time, the particles almost did not move from their place, others went quite far. We can only talk about mixing, averaging over all particles.

Since for each particle the probability of stepping to the right or to the left is the same, it is obvious that the average mixing of a particle from the line $x=0$ (that is, the sum of the mixings of all particles divided by their number) is equal to zero, since the number of particles to the left and to the right of this straight line will be the same and with equal probability the mixing can have both positive and negative values. But in general, the situation in the group changes: at first all the particles were together, and then they still diverge. How to characterize this situation?

The mixing particles after $n$ steps and $n+1$ steps are related very closely by the relation

$$x_{n+1} = x_n \pm \delta.$$

Let's square both sides of this equation:

$$x_{n+1}^2 = x_n^2 + \delta^2 \pm 2x_n\delta.$$

This equality is valid for any particle, therefore it is also valid for the average values ​​of the quantities included in it (remember that when speaking about the average value, we mean the conditions for the entire number of particles):

$$\sqrt{x_n^2} = \sqrt{\delta^2} \sqrt{t} = \sqrt{\delta^2\Gamma} \sqrt{t}.$$

(The mean value of the particle's mixing is zero - $x_n = 0$, $x_{n+1} = 0$, but the mean value of the squared mixing is not zero, since $x^2$ is positive regardless of the sign of $x$.) Since we assume that the step length is constant, the value of $\delta^2$ is simply $\delta^2$, and $x_n\delta = x_n\delta = 0$. Hence,

$$\sqrt{x_{n+1}^2} = \sqrt{x_n^2} + \delta^2.$$

This correspondence is valid for any $n$; meaning $\sqrt{x_1^2} = \delta^2$, $\sqrt{x_2^2} = 2\delta^2$, $\sqrt{x_3^2} = 3\delta^2$, etc. Thus,

$$\sqrt{x_n^2} = n\delta^2.$$

Now let us remember that the particle takes each next step at equal intervals of time $\tau$. Consequently, in time $t$ it will take $n = t/\tau$ steps, and the average square of particle mixing in time $t$ is equal to

$$\sqrt{x_n^2} = \frac{\delta^2}{\tau}t.$$

You can enter your step frequency $\Gamma = \tau^{-1}$. Then

$$\sqrt{x_n^2} = \delta^2\Gamma t.$$

The value equal to $\sqrt{x_n^2}$ characterizes the mixing of the particle performing random walks from the initial position in time $t$. From the correspondences we obtained, it follows that the diffusion path is proportional to the square root of time:

$$x_{\text{дифф}} = \sqrt{x_n^2} = \sqrt{\frac{\delta^2}{\tau}} \sqrt{t} = \sqrt{\delta^2\Gamma} \sqrt{t}.$$
