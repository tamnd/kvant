M746. A paper square is folded in half along a certain straight line $l$ passing through its center into a (non-convex) nine-part system. How to draw straight line $l$ in order to:
a) did the resulting nine-parter have the largest area?
b)* did it contain a circle of the largest possible radius?

Fig. 2.

Let the straight line $l$ along which the square is folded form an angle $\alpha (0 \leqslant \alpha \leqslant 45^\circ)$ on its horizontal side, the length of the side of the square is $2q$.

a) If the figure obtained by folding the square along the straight line $l$ is supplemented with a symmetrical one (relative to the center of the square $O$ or relative to the straight line $l$ - it doesn’t matter!), then you get the polygon shown at figure 1 - the union of the square with the surface at the angle $2\alpha$. It has four axes of symmetry. To find out when its area is maximum, we then note that it can be obtained by an additional square $2q \times 2q$ with four identical pink right-angled triangles. The hypotenuse $c$ and the legs $a, b$ of each of them are easy to find, then note that $a + b + c = 2q$, $a = c \sin 2\alpha$, $b = c \cos 2\alpha$, whence $c = 2q / (1 + \sin 2\alpha + \cos 2\alpha) = 2q / (1 + \sqrt{2} \cos(45^\circ - 2\alpha))$; the area of ​​the triangle is $q(q - c)$ and is maximum at $\alpha = 22,5^\circ$ when the hypotenuse $c$ is minimum. Thus, a 16-gon at figure 1 has a maximum area of ​​$8q^2(2 - \sqrt{2})$ when it is in a "star" shape with 8 axes of symmetry (Fig. 2); at the same angle $a = 22,5^\circ$ its half has the largest area.

6) Answer: The largest circle in a complex square is placed at $\alpha_0 = 2$ arctg $2 - 90^\circ \approx 36,87^\circ$; its radius $r_0$ is equal to $5q/8$, and the corresponding straight line $l$ divides the sides of the square in the ratio 1:7. A geometric experiment helps confirm this answer: if every $2^\circ$ or $3^\circ$ (from $\alpha = 0^\circ$ to $\alpha = 45^\circ$) more precisely construct the corresponding drawing (it is convenient to cut out a larger square from cardboard, pierce it in the center with a button and use it to construct the main and surface of the square, and build a larger circle - for each $\alpha$ - with a compass and ruler"), you can quite accurately plot the dependence of the radius of the largest circle $r$ on $\alpha$ (Fig. 3). On the graph of this function, several points $a_i$ are highlighted - the segment $0^\circ \leqslant \alpha \leqslant 45^\circ$ is divided into 5 pieces, on each of which the greatest circle is determined by different geometric conditions, so that its radius is expressed by different (sometimes very cumbersome) formulas. However, $\alpha_0$ is also one of these singular points, therefore at point $a_0$ the function $r = r(\alpha)$ is not arbitrary, and one has to evaluate it separately on different pieces.
