Now it is interesting to make some numerical estimates. What do you think: are there often atoms in a crystal? It is quite clear that such a question cannot be answered in general terms - the answer depends on the nature of the crystal, on the temperature, and perhaps on something else. But it turns out that some general patterns exist. Thus, for almost all crystals, the diffusion coefficients near the melting temperature (but still in the solid state) are approximately the same and equal to $10^{-8} \text{ cm}^2/\text{c}$ (for different crystals these temperatures, of course, are different: for lead - slightly below $327^\circ\text{C}$, for gold - $1063^\circ\text{C}$, for iron - $1539^\circ\text{C}$, for tungsten - $3387^\circ\text{C}$). Neglecting the numerical coefficient in accordance with (3) (orders of magnitude are important to us) and taking $\delta = 3\mathring{\text{A}} = 0,3$ nm $= 3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ cm, we find that $\Gamma \approx 10^7 \text{ c}^{-1}$. Consequently, the value of the melting temperature of an atom changes in the crystal on average 10 million times per second.

Is it a lot or a little? It would seem like a lot. But in fact, you can’t even pose the question like that. Is five publications a lot or a little? It depends on what for! How about one kilogram? What to compare with! It is natural to compare our $10^7 \text{ c}^{-1}$ with the average frequency of vibration of atoms in a crystal v (after all, vibration is the main type of thermal motion of atoms in a crystal). So here's $v \approx 10^{13} \text{ c}^{-1}$. Thus, atom steps into a neighboring node relatively rarely. Then he doesn’t measure seven times, like the last time, but “hesitates” a million times, and only then takes a step. The atom becomes even more “prudent” when the crystal is cooled. Copper has a melting point of $1083^\circ\text{C}$, and at a temperature of $1075^\circ\text{C}$ a copper atom moves the required 10 million times per second, but at room temperature its sedentary life time is no less than $3 \cdot 10^{12}$ years, that is, it just sits still. This, by the way, is the answer to the question why diffusion in solids has not been studied for so long. It is very slow, and it can only be noticed (let alone changed) at sufficiently high temperatures, and even with the help of special (and rather complex) methods.

Let us compare the mixing of atoms from the original position during diffusion (diffusionality of atoms in the original position) with the total distance that it travels in the same time ($x_\Sigma$). According to equation (1), the first value is equal to $\delta \sqrt{\Gamma t}$; the second is obviously equal to $\delta n = \delta \Gamma t$.

Taking, as before, $\delta \approx 0,3$ nm and $\Gamma \approx 10^7 \text{ c}^{-1}$ (the value of the melting temperature), we find that after 100 hours of wandering $x_{\Delta n} \approx 0,6$ mm, and $x_\Sigma \approx 1$ km, that is, having walked a kilometer in total, the atom moves less than a millimeter. This is how low the efficiency of the wandering service is compared to the direction of movement.

Studies have shown that the diffusion coefficient increases rapidly with increasing temperature. A strong dependence of the main characteristics of the process on temperature is observed in all processes in which a potential barrier must be overcome to transition from the initial state to the final state. During random walks in a crystal, the barer is very high, its height is ten times the energy of thermal motion. This is why the diffusion coefficient strongly depends on temperature: the higher the temperature, the easier it is for an atom to overcome the barrier.

But what kind of barer is this? And where does atom go if all its grid nodes are occupied? We return to the question we formulated earlier: what is the mechanism of diffusion in crystals?
