Here's another interesting observation. “In calm weather, peculiar patterns reminiscent of moire are clearly visible on the surface of the sea... Looking closely, it is easy to notice that ripples appear within a brighter colored background and that lighter spots and stripes on this background correspond to areas of the sea surface on which these ripples are extinguished,” wrote the famous Soviet geophysicist Academician V.V. Shuleikin. Under his leadership, experiments were carried out to measure the surface tension coefficient of sea water using the cone separation method (see, for example, laboratory work No. 2 in Physics 9). They showed that where ripples exist, the coefficient is the same as that of pure water, and in the area of ​​​​light spots it is significantly less. From this it was concluded that ripples on the surface of the sea are dampened by films of fat or some other substances. True, explaining this turned out to be not easy.

Studies have shown that the surface of the film is damaged mainly by small waves, but they can also prevent the appearance of foam on the crests of waves during a storm. And this relatively modest “help” can play a role: “for practice, it is important to extinguish only foamy ridges, which sometimes pose a serious danger to the ship, or even more so to the lowered boat” (V.V. Shuleikin).

What shape does the drop have?

The answer to this question depends on a number of circumstances. If everything was determined only by surface tension, the drop would always take the shape in which its surface is minimal, that is, the shape of a ball. Under the conditions of gravity, under the influence of gravity, drops most often turn out to be flattened; only very small drops will remain spherical. Is it possible to exclude the force of gravity and compensate for it with some other force?

In 1843, the Belgian physicist Plateau performed an experiment that soon received his name and was included in all physics textbooks. Plato selected the concentration of the alcohol solution in water in such a way that the density of the solution turned out to be equal to the density of olive oil, and introduced a drop of oil into the solution. In this case, the Archimedean buoyancy force balances the gravity of the drop, and the surface tension is due to its spherical shape.

Interestingly, in a state of weightlessness you can get large drops-balls of various liquids. Such drops are observed, for example, during electric welding experiments in space. They consisted of molten metal.

At home, you can repeat the Plateau experience using castor oil and water. The density of oil is slightly less than the density of water, so if you drop clean water into it, the slowly falling water drops will have a practical spherical shape. The drops can be easily painted in different colors with watercolors, and then a picture appears in a glass of oil that resembles multi-colored balls (see page 1 of the cover).

A more difficult, but also more effective experiment is obtained with liquid epoxy resin and salt solution. By pouring salt onto the bottom of the jar, you can get a solution with a concentration that decreases in height, and therefore density. In such a solution, a drop of resin will appear at a certain height. The results of the drops are left to be chosen by the reader himself.
