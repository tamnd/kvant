Mathematics in the USSR

December 30 marks sixty years since the First All-Union Congress of Soviets solemnly passed the decree on the creation of the world's first multinational socialist state - the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. This year, the gift turned out to be the united peoples and nationalities inhabiting our country.

Initially, in 1922, the Soviet Union included only four union republics - the RSFSR, the Ukrainian SSR, the BSSR, and the ZSFSR. Two years later, the Uzbek and Turkmen Union Republics became part of the USSR. In 1929 they were joined by the Tajik Republic. After the Kazakh and Kyrgyz autonomous republics of the RSFSR achieved certain successes in economic and cultural development, in 1936 they were transformed into union ones. In the same year, the Transcaucasian federation was divided along national lines into three union republics - Azerbaijan, Armenian and Georgian. The Republics of Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia and Moldova joined the Union in 1940. Fifteen equal republics went through a long and difficult path of economic and cultural development, together they withstood all the tests and won the Great Patriotic War.

The Soviet Union began to exist in very difficult conditions. Three long years of destructive imperialist war, which led to the revolutionary explosion of 1917, ruined the country. The next five years of the Civil War, intervention, famine and devastation deprived it of a huge number of producers - peasants, workers, engineers, teachers, doctors. It was necessary to immediately begin to restore the economy destroyed by the war, LINK to see the mass homelessness of children.

But tsarism also left other difficult legacies - almost 75% of the population of expensive Russia was illiterate. On the national screens the situation was many times worse. Thus, among the Kyrgyz, literacy reached only 0.6%, among the Turkmens - 0.7%, among the Tajiks - 0.5%, among the Uzbeks - 1.5%. At best, there were only a few people with higher education and belonging to the elite nationalities of the royal outskirts. In the vast expanses of Belarus, Transcaucasia, Central Asia, and Transkaikalia there was not a single higher educational institution. The peoples inhabiting these territories were virtually deprived of the opportunity to grow and flourish.

Representatives of the 2nd generation remember well how immediately after the Great Patriotic War they reached out for knowledge. Then schools were opened in the deepest corners of our homeland.

But Soviet power was not limited to the development of only the primary stage of education. The Civil War was still raging and famine had its terrible harvest, and the Soviet government began to create universities on the former outskirts of Russia, cut off from education for centuries (Irkutsk, Baku, Yerevan, Tbilisi, Tashkent). Over the years of their existence, these universities have trained decimal specialists.

During the difficult years of devastation, Civil War and famine, the revolution aroused a genuine passion for learning and scientific creativity. It is important to note that neither hunger, nor cold, nor material difficulties could extinguish this passion.

Academician P. S. Aleksadrov, one of the leading Soviet mathematicians, the creator of the Soviet school of set-theoretic topology, spoke beautifully about this time: “The period of the early 20s... was a rather unique phenomenon. An important positive factor of this era in the history of mathematics at Moscow University is the boundless scientific enthusiasm of young people. This enthusiasm was based on the great patriotic ideas of Soviet scientific culture, which aroused in students and scientists that genuine scientific fervor that did not manifest itself with such force within the walls of the pre-revolutionary mathematics department of Moscow University.”

The same picture is observed both in the few old universities and in the newly created ones. The first, revolutionary admissions of students to universities prepared and produced the older generation of Soviet mathematicians, who educated subsequent generations of Soviet scientists and laid the foundation for scientific mathematical schools.

Such schools arose in Moscow, Tbilisi, Tashkent, Kyiv, Kharkov. In Leningrad, the mathematical school continued to develop, the foundations of which were created by the works of P. L. Chebyshev, A. M. Lyapunov, A. A. Markov and other remarkable scientists and teachers. A school of theory of functions of a real variable was born in Moscow, which soon extended its interests to the theory of functions of a complex variable, probability theory, topology, differential equations, and geometry; The founders of this school were D.F. Egorov and N.N. Luzin.

The new university centers received serious assistance from the old universities, primarily from Moscow and Leningrad. In particular, young people who were brought up with boundless scientific enthusiasm moved to Tbilisi. For the mass students of plants and factories, engineers were needed - mathematicians were required to train them. There is an urgent need for scientists capable of assisting practice using mathematical methods. In the late twenties and early thirties, the construction of new types began throughout the country, admission to graduate schools was expanded, and places were allocated in the oldest colleges and institutes of the USSR Academy of Sciences for the training of specialists at all levels from among the youth of the indigenous national union and autonomous resource. This measure turned out to be extremely productive, since in a short time a larger number of talented young people were trained for active scientific work. Returning to their homeland, they became centers for accepting the scientific thought of young resource people and organizers of national scientific schools. As a result, a strong school of the theory of functions of a complex variable arose in Yerevan, a school of functional analysis and the theory of differential equations in Baku, and research on the qualitative theory of differential equations began to be developed in Alma-Ata and Frunze.

The Great Patriotic War revealed not only the strength of the Soviet system, but also the correctness of the concept according to which science should

Science, including mathematics, was able to solve a number of pressing problems of the country’s defense: theories were created that made it possible to develop actionable measures to combat the phenomena of “shimmy” and “corkscrew” in aviation, statistical methods for quality control of industrial products and statistical methods for quality management were proposed, methods for obtaining greater accuracy of the boom on fast moving objects, and much more. By the end of the war, it was necessary to urgently develop the theory of shaped charges, ballistic missiles, nuclear weapons, and modern computing facilities. Mathematicians took an active part in solving all these problems.

The end of the war raised urgent problems of restoring the national economy and its further development on a new production base. The practical requirements for mathematics grew literally every day, especially due to the fact that computer technology required the creation and development of new branches of mathematical science - programming, logic, automata theory.

Soviet mathematics has a large number of examples of the creation of general theories that entered the active circles of science and transformed the face of large scientific trends. It is enough to recall the theory of random processes, which was born from the needs of statistical physics, telephony, meteorology and biology. Now this theory is the main direction in the development of probability theory and a powerful tool in solving problems of natural science, technology, communication theory, radio engineering and many other areas of knowledge. Let us also point out the theory of optimal control, which is turning into a large branch of modern mathematics and at the same time being a powerful tool for applied research. It is appropriate to remember that a new direction of research, a better name for linear programming, was born in our country.

How many of us imagined in the pre-war period that in just fifteen years mankind would invent electronic computers? Soviet science added that in a short period of time new computer programming methods were proposed, and many thousands of specialists were convinced of their power of calculations and for producing a logical analysis of complex situations. Currently, all allied resources have not only powerful computers, but also a cadre of well-trained specialists capable of creatively examining them.

We can be proud that in the 65 years that have passed since the Great October Socialist Revolution, and in the 60 years of the existence of the Soviet Union, all resource-based mathematical scientific schools have been created.

Now the Soviet Union faces another task - to increase the pace of scientific progress and educate the younger generation in the spirit of creative quest, in the spirit of the ability and unlimited power of human development. But for this, we need to teach schoolchildren to learn not to memorize, but to delve into the meaning of what they are studying and use what they have learned for active application. Next, you should get used to independently searching for solutions to complex problems, so that the creature gets used to independent thinking and a critical approach to the problem and proposed solutions. Only creative search in studies and at the first independent steps in science allows the young generation of mathematicians to contribute to increasing the pace of scientific progress in our country.
