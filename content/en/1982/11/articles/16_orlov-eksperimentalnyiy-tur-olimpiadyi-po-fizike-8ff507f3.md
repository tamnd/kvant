8th grade

Task 1. Determine the density of the solid.

Equipment: a vessel with water, a test tube, a ruler, pieces of a solid.

Let's consider two ways to solve this task: one was proposed by the compiler, the second by the optimization participant.

Fig. 1. Fig. 2

The following method of performing the work is assumed.

First, such an amount of water is poured into the test tube so that the test tube floats in a vertical position into a vessel with water (Fig. 1). The floating condition of the test tube can be written as

$$F_{A1} = (M + m_0)g,$$

or

$$\varrho_0 S_1 h_1 g = (M + m_0)g,$$

(1)

where $F_{A1} = \varrho_0 S_1 h_1 g$ is the Archimedean force, $\varrho_0$ is the density of water, $S_1$ is the external cross-sectional area of ​​the test tube, $M$ and $m_0$ are the mass of the test tube and the water poured into it, respectively.

Then pieces of the test tube (Fig. 2) are poured into the test tube. In this case, to increase the accuracy of the experiment, it is desirable to immerse the test tube in water as much as possible. Let us write down the floating conditions of the test tube for this case:

$$F_{A2} = (M + m_0 + m)g,$$

or

$$\varrho_0 S_1 h_2 g = (M + m_0 + m)g,$$

(2)

where $m$ is the mass of the solid (pieces of aluminum plugs).

From expressions (1) and (2) we obtain

$$m = \varrho_0 S_1 (h_2 - h_1).$$

The volume of a solid can be easily determined by the change in the volume of water in a test tube when plugs are immersed in it:

$$V = S_2 (h_2' - h_1')$$

where $S_2$ is the internal cross-sectional area of ​​the test tube.

Therefore, the iscomatic of a rigid body is equal to

$$\varrho = \frac{m}{V} = \varrho_0 \frac{S_1 (h_2 - h_1)}{S_2 (h_2' - h_1')} \approx \varrho_0 \frac{h_2 - h_1}{h_2' - h_1'}$$.

This technique gave good results when working with a standard cylindrical and thin-walled test tube, when it was possible to overcome the difference in sections $S_1$ and $S_2$ and the change in the cross-sectional area of ​​the test tube along its length. However, students were offered

homemade final thick-walled test tubes, from which the method described above led to a large error of changes (up to 30%).

It is pleasant to note that some participants in the Olympics managed to find a way out of this situation. Thus, an interesting method of performing work with the proposed equipment was developed by N. Parshin (Penza) and A. Deshkovsky (Baranovich). The essence of this technique comes down to the same immersion of a test tube in a vessel with water, filled once with only pieces of wire, and another time with only water. In this case, the mass of the solid body is equal to $m = m_0 = \varrho_0 S_2 l$, where $l$ is the height of the water poured into the test tube. To determine the volume of a solid, a test tube containing water is removed from the vessel and the solid is immersed in it. Based on the height $\Delta l$ of the rise of water in the test tube, the volume of the body is determined: $V = S_2 \Delta l$ and its density:

$$\varrho = \frac{m}{V} = \varrho_0 \frac{S_2 l}{S_2 \Delta l} = \varrho_0 \frac{l}{\Delta l}.$$

As you can see, this expression for density does not include the cross-sectional area of ​​the test tube at all, which significantly reduces the changes.

Compare your results.

9th grade

Task 1. Determine the excess air pressure in a balloon relative to atmospheric pressure.

Equipment: scales, weight, balloon, thread, ruler, thermometer.

Successive weighing of the inflated balloon ($m_1 g$) and then its shell ($m_2 g$) allows us to find the difference ($\Delta m g$) between the gravitational force of the air in the balloon and the Archimedean force ($F_A$) acting on the inflated balloon:

$$m_1 g = (m_B + m_06) g - F_A, \quad m_2 g = m_06 g,$$

$$\Delta m g = m_1 g - m_2 g = m_B g - F_A = \varrho V g - \varrho_0 V g,$$

or

$$\Delta m = (\varrho - \varrho_0) V,$$

where $\varrho$ and $\varrho_0$ are the density of air in the balloon and in the atmosphere, $V$ is the volume of the inflated balloon, $m_06$ and $m_B$ are the masses of the shell of the balloon and the air in it. (Let us note a typical mistake: many people considered the value $\Delta m$ mass air in a ball obtained from experiment. This led to a big error: from experiment $\Delta m \approx 0,3$ g, and the mass of air in a ball with a volume of $V \approx 10$ liters is equal to $m_B = \varrho_B V \approx 13$ g.).

Expressing the densities $\varrho$ and $\varrho_0$ ($\varrho = \frac{p M}{RT}, \varrho_0 = \frac{p_0 M}{RT}$) using the Mendeleev-Clapeyron law, we obtain

$$\Delta m = \frac{(p - p_0) M V}{RT},$$

whence the required excess air pressure in the ball is equal to

$$\Delta p = p - p_0 = \frac{\Delta m RT}{MV}.$$

To obtain a numerical answer, it is necessary to take the thermometer reading and change the volume of the inflated balloon. If the ball were spherical, its volume $V$ could be calculated using the formula

$$V = \frac{4}{3} \pi R_0^3 = \frac{4}{3} \pi \frac{l^3}{8 \pi^3} = \frac{l^3}{6 \pi^2},$$

where $l = 2\pi R_0$ is the circumference of the maximum section of the ball, changed using threads and a ruler. Since in reality the shape of the ball is not ideal, then $l$ can be defined as the argument mean between the circumferences of two mutually perpendicular sections. Some participants considered the ball to be an ellipsid and calculated the volume using the formula $V = 4/3\pi abc$, where $a, b, c$ is the length of the resulting ellipsid. For a given maximum ball diameter of ≈ 0.3 m, the excess pressure $\Delta p$ was of the order of (2-3)·10^3 Pa. After the end of the experiment, this result was confirmed by direct experiment using a pressure gauge (Fig. 3).

Of interest is a direct, albeit rough estimate of the excess pressure, the assumption of the Olympada participant $M$. Shmakov (Moscow). By directly placing the ball on a sheet of white paper and loading it from above with weights of the known mass $m$, he determined the cross-sectional area $S$ of the ball's trace on the paper (Fig. 4). Since the small load did not cause any significant change in the volume of the ball and the air pressure in it, $\Delta p = mg/S$.
