Atoms grow larger throughout the crystal

About a hundred years ago, in 1896, the English metallurgist William Roberts-Austen performed such an experiment. He pressed the thin gold disk firmly against the polished end of a pure lead cylinder, made sure that the contact was sufficiently tight, and placed the pair in an oven at 200°C for ten days. When the annealing ended, it turned out that it was no longer possible to separate the metals. Then the experimenter cut the composite cylinder along the axis and, having measured it under a microscope, was convinced that the gold and lead had penetrated into each other and mixing had occurred.

THE INTRODUCTION OF ONE SUBSTANCE INTO ANOTHER IS CALLED DIFFUSION. It is diffusion that leads to the creation of a carburized zone in steel, which ensures the joining of metals during welding, soldering, chrome plating, nickel plating, sintering of powders and many other processes.

Why do atoms (or molecules) of one substance penetrate inside another? How to calculate the depth of this penetration? What does it depend on? In this article we will try to answer these questions.

Why will this be prescribed?

Diffusion in gases and liquids has been known for a long time. Everyone knows well that if successive portions of different gases are introduced into a vessel, then after some time all the gases will mix homogeneously: the number of particles of each type per unit volume of the vessel will become constant, and the concentrations will equalize. If we carefully pour two mixing liquids one on top of the other into a narrow test tube (for example, water and a solution of copper sulfate), we will see that the interface (between light water and dark blue sulfate), very clear at the first moment, begins to gradually melt. A transition zone will appear, which will increase in size until the entire liquid becomes homogeneous in composition (we will see this by its uniform color). The process proceeds very slowly, and if you take so much liquid several centimeters high, the difference in color (and therefore concentration) in the upper and lower ends of the test tube will be noticeable even a day after the start of the experiment. But eventually the liquid will become homogeneous.

Thus, we come to a new definition of diffusion: diffusion is the spontaneous equalization of concentrations. Atoms (or molecules) of type A will be mixed where there are fewer of them or none at all and where there are more atoms of type B, and vice versa. As a result, stirring will be prescribed.

However, diffusion in solids up to Roberts-Austen was not observed.

Let's try to figure this out.

Why does mixing happen? What causes particles of different types to mix? The answer to these questions seems very clear: mixing is the result of thermal motion of particles. But behind this “obvious” answer a legitimate question arises: why, as a result of thermal movement, particles are not separated by type? Let's take an empty vessel and let in a portion of nitrogen and a portion of oxygen. The gases will mix. Let's wait: maybe after some time they will separate - in one half of the vessel there will be nitrogen, in the other - oxygen? Not so. In the same way, it is easy to mix salt and sugar (the tell movement has nothing to do with it, they need to be shaken), but it is hopeless to wait until they separate again.

The reasons why particles mix are associated with the tendency of any system consisting of a large number of particles towards disorder, a chaotic arrangement of particles. These principles operate equally in gases, liquids, and solids, leading to random movements, random wanderings of particles that create disorder. In this sense, it would seem that there is nothing surprising in the fact that solid gold and lead mutually penetrate each other. Nevertheless, this experimental fact is extremely surprising. Why?

Let us remember what movements particles make in various states of matter. In gases, a particle, before colliding with another particle, moves rectilinearly, making a free run. After a collision, the particle flies in a new direction and with a new speed, a segment of a new straight line until the next collision. It is clear that such movement leads to mixing and diffusion.

In a mixing fluid, particles during thermal motion are comparable to their sizes. However, the positions of the particles, as in a gas, are not fixed; the particle mixes all the time and, as a result, moves far from the initial position over a sufficiently long time. This movement also leads to mixing.

This is not the case in crystalline solids. For a crystal, the character is an ordered arrangement of atoms in an order of defined places - at the nodes of the crystal lattice. The crystal lattice is a collection of repeating completely identical geometric bodies, the so-called elementary cells. No matter how much we “walk” around the crystal, everything in any place is arranged exactly the same.

The main type of thermal movements that atoms perform in solids is a small one near the equilibrium - a lattice node. Underlining - small. The amplitude of these oscillations is much less than the distance between the nodes. An atom performing such oscillations has no chance of getting into a neighboring node.

In the light of all that has been said, the mutual penetration of atoms of gold and lead is an amazing substance. How do they do it? What is the mechanism for shuffling atoms?

How does this happen?

No matter how different the mechanisms of a larger particle in gases, liquids and solids are, it is important that these larger ones are random. It is impossible to predict in advance where a gas molecule will go after the next collision. She can sing with equal probability in any direction.

An interesting problem arises. Suppose that at an arbitrary point in space, which we take as the origin of coordinates, there is a group of particles capable of making only random large particles. Suppose that at some point in time, which we take as the beginning of the cutoff, they all begin to move. Let us ask ourselves: where will the parts be located?

It is clear that it is impossible to predict the location of an individual particle after time $t$: during this time, the particles almost did not move from their place, others went quite far. We can only talk about mixing, averaging over all particles.

Since for each particle the probability of stepping to the right or to the left is the same, it is obvious that the average mixing of a particle from the line $x=0$ (that is, the sum of the mixings of all particles divided by their number) is equal to zero, since the number of particles to the left and to the right of this straight line will be the same and with equal probability the mixing can have both positive and negative values. But in general, the situation in the group changes: at first all the particles were together, and then they still diverge. How to characterize this situation?

The mixing particles after $n$ steps and $n+1$ steps are related very closely by the relation

$$x_{n+1} = x_n \pm \delta.$$

Let's square both sides of this equation:

$$x_{n+1}^2 = x_n^2 + \delta^2 \pm 2x_n\delta.$$

This equality is valid for any particle, therefore it is also valid for the average values ​​of the quantities included in it (remember that when speaking about the average value, we mean the conditions for the entire number of particles):

$$\sqrt{x_n^2} = \sqrt{\delta^2} \sqrt{t} = \sqrt{\delta^2\Gamma} \sqrt{t}.$$

Studies have shown that the diffusion coefficient increases rapidly with increasing temperature. A strong dependence of the main characteristics of the process on temperature is observed in all processes in which a potential barrier must be overcome to transition from the initial state to the final state. During random walks in a crystal, the barer is very high, its height is ten times the energy of thermal motion. This is why the diffusion coefficient strongly depends on temperature: the higher the temperature, the easier it is for an atom to overcome the barrier.

But what kind of barer is this? And where does atom go if all its grid nodes are occupied? We return to the question we formulated earlier: what is the mechanism of diffusion in crystals?

How does this happen anyway?

The answer to this question was given by one of the founders of solid state physics, the theoretical physicist Yakov Ilyich Frenkel.

In his memoirs dedicated to Frenkel, the English physicist Neville Mott, who restored the famous Cavendish laboratorium for many years, wrote that in England every physics student knows about “Frenkel defects.” What is this?
