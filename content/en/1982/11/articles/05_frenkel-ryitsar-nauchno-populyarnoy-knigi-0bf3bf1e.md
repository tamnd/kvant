⟦folio 18⟧

Knight
popular science
books

(on the centenary of his birth
Ya. I. Perelman)

Yakov Isidorovich Perelman liked to repeat the words of Laplace: “The subject of mathematics is so serious that one must not miss the opportunity to make it entertaining.” To the words of the famous French scientist, we can add that the subject of physics is no less serious. Ya. I. Perelman devoted his entire life to getting others, and primarily schoolchildren, interested in these sciences. But not only schoolchildren. He sincerely complained that educated people - doctors and botanists, physiologists and linguists - know almost nothing about physics, astronomy, and mathematics. True culture, in his opinion, should include knowledge of the fundamentals of his favorite exact sciences.

The flowering of Perelman's creative energy coincided with the greatest discoveries in physics and technology. But in order to fully understand the significance and meaning of these discoveries, thorough preparation, the meaning of the basics, is necessary. This is what he dedicated to his books “Entertaining Physics” and “Living Mathematics”, “Rocket to the Moon” and “Interplanetary Travel” and many, many others, which are certainly familiar to those reading these lines.

Perelman taught to pay attention to seemingly applied phenomena or objects and, in simple words, images, and means, showed what physics is behind them. The small sketches that form a single whole, into which Perelman’s books are divided, are somewhat similar to poems in the process. The similarity of otpid is not determined by “high calm”—the language of its ethids is simple and laconic. What is attractive about them is the grace with which the physical background of this or that phenomenon is revealed, the ability to expose or refute physical discrepancies using minimal mathematical (arithmetic) means. Often the names of the ethids already contain some paradox. Remember: “Hot ice”, “Water in a sieve”, “Seen with three eyes”, “Cold from coal”, “Ice that is not melted in boiling water” or “How do we drink?”, “Can you count?”, “Could there have been a flood?”, “How much does lightning cost?”, “Could Archimedes raise the Earth?” etc.

However, it is even more paradoxical that simple questions: “Who is Perelman?”, “When was he born?”, “Where did he live and work?”, “How and when did he start writing?”

⟦folio 19⟧

“Who did he communicate with?”, “When and where did he die?”—the questions, the answers to which constitute a person’s biography, are unlikely to be answered by many readers of his books, people skilled in giving answers to the intricate questions of nature. Neither in the first, nor in the tenth, nor in the twenty editions of Perelman’s books was there room for a brief biographical note about their author.

Fortunately, information about Yakov Isidorovich Perelman is available in the funds of the Leningrad branch of the Archive of the USSR Academy of Sciences. The archive is located on the Universitetskaya embankment in Leningrad, next to the important Kunstkamera. It stores papers issued by reporting and foreign scientists, in particular those who were found in Perelman’s books. If there are many such documents - letters, manuscripts, etc. - they form a special fund. The Yakov Isidorovich Foundation is numbered 796; it is quite general, and its description takes up four large pages. The information below is taken from this fund.

Ya. I. Perelman was born on November 22, 1882 in Bialystok into the family of an employee. In 1901, he moved to St. Petersburg and entered the Forestry Institute (now the Forestry Academy named after S. M. Kirov), where his older brother Osip, later the famous writer Osip Dymov, was already studying. (From a letter from O. I. Dymov to his younger brother, one can learn about some details of the biography of Yakov Isidorovich.) However, it is not 1901, the year of moving from the provinces to the capital, that Ya. I. Perelman identifies as a turning point in his life, but 1899 - from him he traces the absence of the life of a writer who chose exact sciences as the heroes of his works. This year in Russia, and in other countries too, a lot was written about the comet, which was supposed to pass near the Earth, and about the stream of meteorological bodies generated by it. There were rumors, as often happened, almost about the end of the world, but on September 23, 1899, an article appeared in the Grodno Provincial Gazette newspaper, refuting these rumors. It was called “Concerning the expected meteorological rain”, its author was the hypnasist Yakov Perelmen, but he hid his last name under a pseudonym. (He often spoke, published articles and notes, under various pseudonyms, one of which sounded characteristic: Tsyfirkin.) The fact is that, according to the charter of hypnasia, their students did not have the right to publish their works in print until they graduated from the educational institution.

When a catalog of his works was published in Leningrad in 1924 to mark the 25th anniversary of Ya. I. Perelman’s scientific popularization activities, it opened with reviews about him written by the patriarch of Russian physics, Orest Daniilovich Khvolson, the author of the most famous course in general physics in the 1920s. Khvolson wrote: “The author has gained well-deserved fame for his popular works, among which “Entertaining Physics” takes first place. This is truly an entertaining book, interesting even for a physics specialist. It contains erroneous and varied material; the presentation is easy and correct.” Then the catalog provided a list of 17 books written by Perelman over 25 years of work, and the total circulation of their publication was more than a million copies - a figure that was gigantic at that time. (For comparison, we indicate more figures: seven editions of the first part of “Entertaining Physics” amounted to a total of 90,000 copies, and the 17th edition (1965) available to the author of these lines was published in a circulation of 100,000 copies; after that, the book was published three more times. In 1981, another yearbook published by UNESCO, “Index Translationum,” was published. In it collected information about all the books in the world translated from one language to any other during 1977. Perelman’s books were published in translation 17 times in the USSR, as well as in the German Democratic Republic, Czechoslovakia, Spain and Japan.)

For Leningrad schoolchildren of the 30s, Perelman was not only the author of favorite books. His name is clearly associated with the “House of Interesting Science” (DZN), one of the founders and organizers of which was Yakov Isidorovich. The cultural and educational department of the Leningrad Workers' Council donated the red building of the Sheremetev Palace on the embankment of the Fontanka River for the organization of this House. DZN was opened on November 4, 1935. Its halls housed exhibits that “implemented” and clearly demonstrated many of the experiences and examples that Perelman talked about on the pages of his books. Mathematical and physical Olympids were held at the DZN, meetings with scientists were organized, and excursions went there. During school holidays, it was a place of pilgrimage not only for Leningraders, but also for children who came to Leningrad from all over the country. In 1938 alone, more than 100 thousand people visited it.

Letters are stored in the voluminous folder of fund 796, numbered 33. Business and family letters (Yakov Isidorovich had a son, Mikhail, who died at the front in the first year of the Great Patriotic War). Most of all, drafts of Perelman’s letters to various people have been preserved. His letter of 1902, addressed to the famous French astronomer Camille Flammarin, attracted particular attention. The name Flammarina was well known to Russian readers at the turn of the 19th and 20th centuries. During the years 1900-1902 alone (we specifically selected the years adjacent to the DATE letters) nine books by Flammarin popularizing astronomy were published in Russia; in the world he had a well-deserved reputation as “popularizer No. 1.” Along with “Astronomey”, “Pictorial Astronomy”, “Rudiments of Astronomy”, “Little Astronomy” and even ... “Astronomey for Dam”, Flammarin wrote several science fiction novels, including “Lumen”. The hero of this novel was a gentle two-Lumen, capable of moving in the world of space at any speed, in particular exceeding the speed of light.

In a letter from a second-year student at the Forestry Institute (where Ya. I. Perelman then studied), the sensations attributed to Flammarina to Lumen when he approaches the Earth or leaves it at a speed that is equal to or greater than the speed of light are broken down. Thus, according to Flammarin, Lumen, marveling at a speed equal to the speed of light, will observe a “frozen” picture of what was happening on Earth during his departure. According to Perelman, he will not see anything at all. Indeed, Perelman argues, can Lumen experience the effects of rays rushing near him if he is in a relative position in relation to them? “If a soldier ran from the enemy at the speed of a cannon shell, not a single cannonball would be able to hit him, even if they surrounded him with a black cloud.” The observer, Perelman concludes, will see nothing.

Let us now give another quote: “If everything began to move behind a ray of light at a speed c (the speed of light in emptiness), then I would have to perceive such a ray of light as a stationary, variable electromagnetic field in space. But nothing like this exists; this can be seen both from experience and from Maxwell's equations. Intuitively, it seemed clear to me from the very beginning that from the point of view of such an observable, everything should happen according to the same laws as for an observer stationary relative to the Earth. Indeed, how can the first observed person know or establish that he is in a state of rapidly uniform motion?

Perhaps readers have agreed that this reasoning belongs to the famous Albert Einstein. The quote is taken from his Autobiographical Notes, written in 1949. In them, Einstein recalled that there was movement above the paradox

He thought about the speed of light from the age of 16, when he first came across it (in 1895), for 10 years until 1905, when the special theory of relativity was created. “This paradox already contains the germ of a special theory of relativity,” Einstein continues. “Now, of course, everyone knows that all attempts to satisfactorily explain this paradox were doomed to failure as long as the axiom about the absolute nature of time and simultaneity remained rooted, albeit incoherent, in our thinking.”

So, in 1902, Perelman’s thoughts were in developmental correspondence with the subject of Einstein’s long thoughts. Was there a common source for these thoughts? Einstein, of course, could have read Flammarin’s novel by that time (published in its first edition in France in 1878), but later, in April 1920, in a conversation in Berlin with his first biographer A. Moszkowski and discussing this novel, he spoke about it with some irritation. (“Flammarin took it <paradox - V.F.> from others and only popularized it,” Einstein remarked to Moshkovsky.)

We dwelled in detail on this episode - one of many stories that can be gleaned from studying Perelman’s archive, because this story had a kind of continuation.

Firstly, it is important to remember the chapter “Is it possible to understand with a bullet?” from "Entertaining Physics" by Perelman. This is a wonderful illustration of the reasoning of a 20-year-old youth in a letter to Flammarin. (It must be said that Perelman’s books contain many autobiographical moments - in addition to those in which he directly emphasizes this. Thus, in “Living Mathematics” this “Can you count?” talks about ways to highlight trees of different species in the forest. And “Entertaining Geometry” even begins with the chapter “Geometry in the Forest.” Now, knowing that Yakov Isidorovich graduated from the Forestry Institute, we can guess that we can draw relevant examples im from his student practice.)

Secondly, and this is no less interesting, in the future the “world lines” (to use the term of the special theory of relativity) of Einstein and Perelman intersected to some extent. It is not surprising that Perelman knew the works of the brilliant physicist (they are written about, for example, in the appendix to one of his books) and was interested in his personality. It is significant that Einstein, for his part, not only knew, but also highly valued Perelman’s popular science books. Yakov Isidorovich knew about this from various people. Thus, Osip Dymov wrote to his brother in 1943: “Einstein knows about you, he has heard your name. I talked to him about you in Berlin three years ago.” In one of his letters to A. M. Gorky, speaking about his book “Entertaining Physics,” Perelman wrote that he had recently received “a lecture about it from Albert Einstein.” Einstein could have known about this book, for example, from Ehrenfest, with whom others were often seen. Tatyana Alekseevna Afanasyeva Ehrenfest, the wife of the famous Dutch theoretical physicist Paul Ehrenfest, said in letters to Perelman that her entire family “has long been among the fans of your books—however, you already know this from my husband.”

In besieged Leningrad, at the age of 59, on March 16, 1942, Perelman died without proving a breakthrough and lifting the blockade. But his books - the main work of his life - continue to live, winning an ever wider circle of readers in our country and abroad, attracting hundreds of thousands of young people to mathematics and physics, as in previous years, determining for many, many the choice of their life path. An enviable fate for a writer!
