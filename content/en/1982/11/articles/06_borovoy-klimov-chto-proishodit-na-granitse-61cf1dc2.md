What's happening at the border

In a school physics course, not much space is given to what happens at the interface of a liquid with another medium - surface tension, wetting, capillarity. At the same time, with this comes a surrounded spot. Now the center of this spot is touched with a soapy stick or just a piece of soap - the spot breaks and turns into a thin ring. Again, its center is touched with a feather (you can use ink of a different color), then a soapy stick is used, etc. An ornament of multi-colored concentrated rings appears on the surface of the water (Fig. 2). Just carefully place the blotting paper on top and the red patterns will transfer to it.

This experience has found practical application. With its help, in Japan they produce paper with a wide variety of patterns.

The experiments described are easy to understand if the surface of the liquid is considered to be a stretched stick. Such a model was first proposed by the famous English physicist Thomas Young. In December 1804, Jung wrote a paper in which the behavior of liquids in capillaries was explained using the “liquid surface – elastic film” analogy. Soon the model, confirmed by new observations, gained universal acceptance.

So, the surface of clean water is a stretched film, and soapy or colored water is the same film, but less elastic. As a result, the water of the plan becomes soapy or tinted, dragging with it a “rocket” or a ring surrounded by ink.

One of the simplest is using tearing drops. If you take a pipette with a pulled-out spout and, having filled it with water, carefully and slowly begin to squeeze out a drop, you can observe how it gradually increases in size, changes its shape and finally comes off the pipette (Fig. 3).

Note that the drop only comes off when it reaches a certain size. As long as the drop is not large enough, surface tension holds it to the tip of the pipette. It is obvious that at the moment of detachment, the gravity mg of the drop becomes equal in magnitude to the surface tension force F: mg=F. Or (see, for example, § 28 of the textbook “Physics 9”, M., “Prosveshchenie”, 1982)

$$mg=2\pi r\sigma$$

where r is the radius of the neck formed by the drop before separation.

By counting, say, 100 drops and using a scale to find their total mass M = 100 m, you can determine the surface tension coefficient of the liquid:

$$\sigma=\frac{mg}{2\pi r}=\frac{Mg}{200\pi r}$$

But, unfortunately, it is very inaccurate.

Firstly, it is not easy to change the radius of the neck (often, for this tearing drop, an enlarged view is projected onto the screen and measurements are already taken there). Secondly, the separation of a drop turns out to be a more complex process than simply breaking the neck (in particular, simultaneously with the main drop another, small droplet is formed). There are several other sources of error in this experiment. However, if we want to determine only the order of magnitude of σ or qualitatively compare the surface tension of, say, pure water and a soap solution, it is permissible for the wave to use a pipette, and the radius of the neck is considered equal to the internal radius of the pipette nose (the latter can be measured, for example, using a wire of a certain thickness that fits tightly into the pipette).

**Ripples on the surface of the water and how to extinguish them**

It turns out that due to the forces of surface tension, so-called capillary waves (that is, ripples in the water) arise. They can be observed using the same pipette. Fill the plate with water and, holding the pipette at a short distance from its surface, drip water - rapidly spreading concentric waves will be surface tension waves. (In general, the mechanism of formation and propagation of waves on water is not simple. Both gravity and surface phenomena are involved here. But to explain the origin of small ripples, the model of an elastic film can be fully used.)

What shape does the drop have?

The answer to this question depends on a number of circumstances. If everything was determined only by surface tension, the drop would always take the shape in which its surface is minimal, that is, the shape of a ball. Under the conditions of gravity, under the influence of gravity, drops most often turn out to be flattened; only very small drops will remain spherical. Is it possible to exclude the force of gravity and compensate for it with some other force?

In 1843, the Belgian physicist Plateau performed an experiment that soon received his name and was included in all physics textbooks. Plato selected the concentration of the alcohol solution in water in such a way that the density of the solution turned out to be equal to the density of olive oil, and introduced a drop of oil into the solution. In this case, the Archimedean buoyancy force balances the gravity of the drop, and the surface tension is due to its spherical shape.

Interestingly, in a state of weightlessness you can get large drops-balls of various liquids. Such drops are observed, for example, during electric welding experiments in space. They consisted of molten metal.

At home, you can repeat the Plateau experience using castor oil and water. The density of oil is slightly less than the density of water, so if you drop clean water into it, the slowly falling water drops will have a practical spherical shape. The drops can be easily painted in different colors with watercolors, and then a picture appears in a glass of oil that resembles multi-colored balls (see page 1 of the cover).

A more difficult, but also more effective experiment is obtained with liquid epoxy resin and salt solution. By pouring salt onto the bottom of the jar, you can get a solution with a concentration that decreases in height, and therefore density. In such a solution, a drop of resin will appear at a certain height. The results of the drops are left to be chosen by the reader himself.
