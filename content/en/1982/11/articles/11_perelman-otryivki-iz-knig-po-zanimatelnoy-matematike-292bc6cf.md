From books
Ya. I. Perelman

This year marked the anniversary of the birth of the famous popularizer of physics and mathematics, mechanics and astronomy Yakov Isidorovich Perelman. The name of this talented person is widely known both in our country and abroad; it is familiar to both venerable scientists and young science lovers.
Ya. I. Perelman is the author of more than fifty books and thousands of newspaper and magazine articles. His books open and will open to a new generation of readers the non-educational and wonderful world of science.
Here are a few excerpts from them.

How big is a million

(from the books “Evenings of Borrowing Science”, written in accordance with V.I. Pryanitsinkov)

Every day we read in newspapers and books about millions of rubles, millions of tons or kilometers. We know that a million is a thousand thousand, and yet we cannot imagine the true number of a million. There are also a lot of stars in the sky that can be seen with the naked eye; Which set is greater: the number “million” or the number of stars on the dome of the resting sky?

Scientists re-read all the stars visible in the sky with a simple eye, and it turned out that there are much less than a million of them: $3^{1/2}$ thousand; meanwhile, in a million there are 1000 thousand. This means that we see stars in the sky (without difficulty)

The life and creative work of Ya. I. Perelman is described in the article by V. Frenkel “The Knight of the Popular Science Book.”

Through the pages of Jonathan Swift

(from Zanmatel's Geometry) The author of Guliver's Travels took great care to avoid the danger of becoming entangled in geometric relationships. The reader remembers, without a doubt, that in the land of Lilliputians our foot corresponded to an inch*), and in the land of giants, on the contrary, an inch corresponded to a foot. In other words, the Lilliputians have all their things, all their productions, 12 times less than normal, while the giants have the same number of times more. These seemingly simple relationships, however, become greatly complicated when it comes to solving questions in the following research:

1) How many times did Guliver eat more at dinner than the Lilliputian?
2) How many times did Guliver demand more money for a suit than Lilliput?

*) 1 inch = 0.0254 m, 1 foot = 12 inch = 0.3048 m. (Editor’s note)

The author of “Journey” coped with this task successfully in most cases. He correctly calculated that the razliput is 12 times shorter than Gulliver, then the volume of his body is 12 × 12 × 12 smaller, that is, 1728 times; therefore, Gulliver needs 1728 times more food to feed than Lilliput. And we read in “Travel” this description of Gulliver’s dinner:

“Three hundred cooks prepared food for me. Huts were erected around the house where the cooks and their families lived. When the hour of dinner came, I took 20 servants in my hands and placed them on the table, 100 people served from the floor: one served food, the rest brought barrels of wine and other drinks on poles, slung from shoulder to shoulder. Those standing above, as needed, lifted it all onto the table with the help of ropes and blocks...”

Swift correctly calculated the amount of materials for Gulliver's costume. The surface of his body is 12x12=144 times larger than that of the Lilliputians; he needs the same number of times more materials, tailors, etc. All this is taken into account by Swift, who says on behalf of Gulliver that “300 Lilliputian tailors were assigned to him with instructions to sew a complete pair of boards in a local way.” (The haste of the work required double the number of tailors.)

The need to make such calculations arose in Swift on almost every page. And, generally speaking, he did them correctly. If in Pushkin’s “Eugene Onegin,” as Pozt claims, “time is calculated according to the calendar,” then in Swift’s “Travels” all dimensions are consistent with the rules of geometry. Only occasionally was the proper scale not maintained, especially when describing the land of giants. Sometimes there are mistakes here.

“Once,” says Gulliver, “a driving dwarf went to the garden with us. Seizing an opportune moment when I, while walking, found myself under one of the trees, he grabbed a branch and shook it over my head. A hail of apples, each the size of a barrel, fell noisily to the ground; one hit me in the back and knocked me down..."

Gulliver safely rose to his feet after this blow. However, it is easy to calculate that the impact from the fall of such an apple should be devastating: after all, an apple 1728 times heavier than ours, that is, weighing *) 80 kg, fell from a 12-fold height. The energy of the impact must exceed 20,000 times the energy of the fall of an ordinary apple and could only be compared

Swift made a small mistake in calculating the muscular strength of the giants. We have already seen in the first chapter*) that the power of animals is not proportional to their size. If we apply the messages given there to Swift's bikes, it turns out that although their muscular strength was 144 times greater than Gulliver's, their body weight was 1728 times greater**). And if Gulliver was able to lift not only the weight of his own body, but also approximately the same load, then the giants would not be able to overcome even the load of their huge body. They should lie motionless in one place, powerless to make any significant movement. Their nature, as pictured by Swift, could only appear as a result of incorrect calculation.

Several experiments taken (from the book “Physics at Every Step”)

Diving bell. An ordinary basin is suitable for this experiment, but if you can get a deep and wide jar, then it is more convenient to continue the experiment. You will also need a tall glass. This will be your “water bell”, and the bowl of water represents a smaller version of the sea or lake.

There is hardly any experience of the past. You hold the glass up, immerse it in the bottom of the basin, continuing to hold it with your hand (so that the water does not push it out). At the same time, it is easy to notice that the water inside the glass almost does not penetrate: the air does not allow it. This becomes much more clear when under our “bell” there is some easily wet object, for example a piece of sugar. Place a cork circle on the water, sugar on it and cover with a glass on top. Feel free to lower the glass into the water now. The sugar will clear below the water level, but will remain dry because the water will not penetrate under the glass.

The same experiment can be continued with a glass funnel, if you turn it with the wide end down, tightly plug its hole with your finger and then immerse it in water. The water does not penetrate under the funnel, but it stays away
