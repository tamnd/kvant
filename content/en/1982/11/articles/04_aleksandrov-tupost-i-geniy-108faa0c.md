From Euclid to Lobachevsky

Euclid himself (IV century BC) accepted the following propositions as an axiom of parallels (for Euclid it was the “fifth postulate”): If a straight line intersects two straight lines and forms internal one-sided angles in the sum of less than two straight lines, then if necessary, these two straight lines will intersect on the side where the angles are less than two straight lines. We have given this formulation of Euclid only so that we can be convinced of its complexity. Other postulates are much simpler and formulated much more briefly, starting with the first: Through any two points a straight line can be drawn.

Naturally, attempts arose to free ourselves from the complex fifth postulate, to derive it from other basic premises of geometry. I think that Euclid himself made such attempts and, in any case, there were already such attempts in his time. There is a known mention among Arabic authors of the work of Archimedes (3rd century BC) that was not written before us, “On Parallel Lines,” where, presumably, the fifth postulate was deduced from some simpler premises.

All their attempts boiled down to the fact that the fifth applicant was removed from some other position. At the same time, they did not replace this much, believing that they succeeded in the proof. Others, more insightful and critical, clearly formulated the position from which the fifth applicant was derived, as, for example, Omar Khayyam did.

The tension of Ponk proofs with the rapid development of mathematics in the 17th and 18th centuries. increased.

Significant conditions were made by the Italian monk, teacher of mathematics and grammar Girolamo Saccheri, whose work with an attempt to prove the fifth applicant appeared in 1733 - the year of his death. It is called "Euclid, cleared of all stains, or a geometric attempt to establish the first principles of all geometry." Starting from the work of his predecessors, Saccheri tries to prove the fifth proposition by contradiction - having accepted an assumption that is equivalent to the negation of the fifth proposition, he deduced consequences from it, trying to arrive at a contradiction. But since the negation of the fifth applicant is Lobachevsky’s axiom, the conclusions that Saccheri received were nothing more and no less than Lobachevsky’s theories of geometry. In other words, Saccheri developed a new geometry without, however, understanding what he was doing.

He did not come to a contradiction, but still concluded that he was able to prove the fifth entry, although, apparently, he was not in this matter. He seemed to convince himself when he wrote about the hypothesis that was tantamount to denying the fifth arrival, that he had “pulled out this harmful hypothesis by the roots.”

Of the fairly numerous (55) that appeared in the 18th century. works on the theory of parallels, the “Theory of Parallels” written in 1766 by I. G. Lamberti, a German mathematician, physicist and astronomer, especially stands out. Conducting the proof of the fifth argument by contradiction, Lamberti drew many consequences from its negation. He, one might say, largely entered into the basics of Lobachevsky's geometry. There was no contradiction in his conclusions, and he did not think he had found it, as almost all his predecessors had done. Lamberti even expressed the idea that he “almost must conclude” that the hypothesis he was refuting “takes place on some minimal sphere.” But still he remained confident that geometry based on the negation of the fifth arrival was impossible. His work did not, however, provide evidence for this belief. Therefore, one must think that he was still dissatisfied and did not publish it. It was published only in 1786 - 9 years after his death and 20 years after it was written. In general, Lamberti came very close to the discovery of a new geometry, but did not make it.

The German mathematicians Schweickart (1818) and Taurinus (1825) came close to understanding the possibility of non-Euclidean geometry, but they still did not express a clearly expressed idea that their theory would be stolbologically legitimate, like Euclidean geometry.

From persuasion to proof

So, Lobachevsky and Bolyai publicly, and Gauss in letters, expressed their conviction in the validity of non-Euclidean geometry and developed it far. However, this conviction is based only on the fact that there were no contradictions in the conclusions obtained. But one would think that contradictions would nevertheless appear in further conclusions. The real meaning of the new geometry remained completely unclear. And while it was not found, the great discovery still made an impact - Lobachevsky’s geometry remained nothing more than imaginary.

In 1839-40. two works by Professor F. Minding of Dorpat (now Tartu) University appeared, in which he studied some special surfaces - surfaces of constant negative curvature. These works essentially concluded that the geometry on such surfaces is nothing more than the geometry of Lobachevsky. But this conclusion was not explicitly stated there. It is interesting that two years ago, one of Lobachevsky’s works was published in the same magazine where Minding’s works were published!

In 1854, upon assuming the position of professor at the University of Göttingen, B. Riemann, as was customary, ate a trial lecture.

The lecture was called “On the hypotheses underlying the foundations of geometry.” It contained an extraordinary wealth of fruitful ideas - from the general concept of mathematical space to the anticipation of what later became the general theory of relativity. In addition, the lecture outlined a general theory of a certain type of spaces (now called *Riemannian*), which include, as simple partial cases, the spaces of Euclidean, Lobachevsky and the so-called *spherical* spaces. Riemann gave a purely analytical definition of these spaces; this essentially made it clear that Lobachevsky's geometry was as consistent as his analysis.
