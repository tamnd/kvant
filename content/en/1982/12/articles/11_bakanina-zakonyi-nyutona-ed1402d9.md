Newton's laws

The basic laws of dynamics were established about three hundred years ago by the great English scientist Isaac Newton (1643-1727). Newton formed them as follows (A.G. Dorfman. “World History of Physics.” M., “Nauka”, 1974):

Law I. Every body continues to maintain its state of rest or uniform rectilinear motion, since it is not forced by applied forces to change this state.

Law II. The change in the quantity of motion is proportional to the force applied to the movement and is produced in the direction of the straight line along which this force acts.

Law III. An action always meets an equal reaction, or the effects of two bodies on each other are equal and directed in opposite directions.

For 300 years, these laws have been experimentally tested many times, and there have been no violations. Only the boundaries of their applicability were clarified and the corrections necessary for the region of the microworld and speeds close to the speed of light were made.

It is obvious that the first law, in essence, applies essentially to such reporting systems (associated with material objects) in which Newton's second and third laws are valid. These reporting systems are called inertial.

The third law states that force is always the result of the interaction of two bodies. If we consider all the bodies that interact with the body under study, we can determine all the forces acting on it. In modern physics, interaction forces are divided into four large classes: gravitational, electromagnetic, strong and weak (the last two classes relate to nuclear interactions). In mechanics, in addition to graviatonic forces, it is customary to distinguish elasticity forces and friction forces. By origin, the last two forces are electromagnetic, but each of them has its own peculiarity and therefore is considered separately.

The main phenomenon, of course, is the second law, which is now most often written in the form

$$\vec{F} = m\vec{a},$$

where $\vec{F}$ is the force acting on the body, $m$ is the mass of the body and $\vec{a}$ is the acceleration acquired by the body. If all the acting forces are known, using this law one can find the acceleration of the body, and therefore the speed and coordinates at any moment in time.

Let us explain what has been said using specific tasks. Most of them were offered at the entrance exams to the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology.

Problem 1. The force acting on the body periodically changes its direction to the opposite (Fig. 1, a). How does a body move under the influence of such a force?

Let's consider how the speed of a body and its movement along the line of action of the force (let this be the X axis) depend on time. To determine this, we will assume that the initial speed and coordinate are equal to zero.

In the 0-1 section, the force is constant both in modulo and direction; therefore, the movement of the body is uniformly accelerated: the speed changes in proportion to time, and the displacement is proportional to the square of time. In this case, the velocity graph (more precisely, its projection v onto the chosen direction) is a straight line passing through the origin of coordinates (Fig. 1, b), and the displacement graph (or rather, x coordinates) is a parabolic, the vertex of which is located at the origin of coordinates (Fig. 1, c).

In section 1-2 the force is constant, but directed in the opposite direction. The speed decreases linearly with time and at point 2 becomes zero (the time of action of the force is the same as in the first section; this means that the change in momentum is the same). The coordinate graph in this section is a parabola whose vertex is at point 2. At point 1 there is a smooth transition from one parabola to another, since there is no discontinuity in speed at this point.

Further consideration is carried out in a similar way. As can be seen from the resulting graphs, the speed does not change sign, only periodic ones turn to zero, and the coordinate increases all the time. In other words, the body is constantly moving away from its initial position, its movement is not at all periodic, although this is exactly the answer one often hears from applicants for entrance exams.

Problem 3. A small ball of mass $m$ moves along the “equator” of the inner surface of a spherical shell with mass $M$ at a constant absolute speed, making a full revolution in time $T$ (Fig. 3). Assuming that there are no external forces and there is no tension, determine with what force the ball presses on the sphere. The distance between the centers of gravity of the ball and the sphere is $d$.

Since external forces do not act on the sphere-ball system, the center of mass of this system must move (which immediately follows).

Blows from Newton's laws). This means that not only the ball moves, but also the sphere, and both of them rotate around an axis passing through the center of mass of the system.

Let us denote the distance from the ball to the center of mass by $r$, then

$$mr = M(d - r), \text{ и } r = d \frac{m}{M + m}.$$

According to Newton's third law, the force with which the ball presses on the sphere is equal in magnitude to the force with which the sphere acts on the ball. It imparts centripetal acceleration to the ball.

$$a_u = \omega^2 r = \left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 \frac{Md}{(M + m)}$$

So, the required force is equal in modulus

$$F = ma_u = \frac{4\pi^2 Mm d}{T^2 (M + m)}$$

and is directed radially from the center of rotation.
