Newton's laws

The basic laws of dynamics were established about three hundred years ago by the great English scientist Isaac Newton (1643-1727). Newton formed them as follows (A.G. Dorfman. “World History of Physics.” M., “Nauka”, 1974):

Law I. Every body continues to maintain its state of rest or uniform rectilinear motion, since it is not forced by applied forces to change this state.

Law II. The change in the quantity of motion is proportional to the force applied to the movement and is produced in the direction of the straight line along which this force acts.

Law III. An action always meets an equal reaction, or the effects of two bodies on each other are equal and directed in opposite directions.

For 300 years, these laws have been experimentally tested many times, and there have been no violations. Only the boundaries of their applicability were clarified and the corrections necessary for the region of the microworld and speeds close to the speed of light were made.

It is obvious that the first law, in essence, applies essentially to such reporting systems (associated with material objects) in which Newton's second and third laws are valid. These reporting systems are called inertial.

The third law states that force is always the result of the interaction of two bodies. If we consider all the bodies that interact with the body under study, we can determine all the forces acting on it. In modern physics, interaction forces are divided into four large classes: gravitational, electromagnetic, strong and weak (the last two classes relate to nuclear interactions). In mechanics, in addition to graviatonic forces, it is customary to distinguish elasticity forces and friction forces. By origin, the last two forces are electromagnetic, but each of them has its own peculiarity and therefore is considered separately.

The main phenomenon, of course, is the second law, which is now most often written in the form

$$\vec{F} = m\vec{a},$$

where $\vec{F}$ is the force acting on the body, $m$ is the mass of the body and $\vec{a}$ is the acceleration acquired by the body. If all the acting forces are known, using this law one can find the acceleration of the body, and therefore the speed and coordinates at any moment in time.
