The properties of solids such as elasticity and plasticity are well known. There are solids that do not have these properties; they are similar to very important liquids, such as viscous pitch, flowing, albeit slowly, under any small load. But now engineers know plastics whose behavior does not relate to any of these cases. This is very clearly seen in the observation of butyl methacrylate (BMA).

If a plate made from it is inserted, then the force with which it must be held decreases significantly after a few seconds (the plastic “gets used” to the new shape), that is, there is no usual elasticity here. However, this is not plasticity, since when you let go, it slowly but completely straightens. If you help her straighten up and let her go, she bends somewhat again.

It would seem that after this the deformation should monotonically kill to zero. The behavior of this material under periodic loading is peculiar. If the vibration is very fast, then it behaves like an elastic and very hard material (line AA at figure 2). If they are very slow, then the material is also similar to elastic, but much softer (BB line). Finally, for intermediate frequencies, the deformation “lags” behind the stress; the process corresponds to a closed curve, the area of ​​which is the energy loss due to heat during one oscillation period. In this case, the properties of elasticity and viscosity are combined, that is, the material works simultaneously as a spring and as a shock absorber.
