◆ Changes in current when switching an ampere are small. This means that we can assume that the change in current in the circuit when an amplifier is connected is small and proportional to the change in the total resistance of the circuit, that is, $\Delta I = \alpha \cdot \Delta R_{06\mu}$, where $\alpha$ is the proportional coefficient. But $\Delta R_{06\mu} = R_A$ ($R_A$ is the amperor resistance). At different limits the resistance of the ampere is different.

If $I_0$ is the current in the circuit before the amplifier is connected, then

$$\Delta I_1 = I_0 - I_1 = \alpha R_{A1},$$

$$\Delta I_2 = I_0 - I_2 = \alpha R_{A2},$$

where $R_{A1}$ is the amperor resistance at the limit of 10 mA, $R_{A2}$ is at the limit of 3 mA. Considering that $\frac{R_{A1}}{R_{A2}} = \frac{3}{10}$, from (*) we obtain

$$\frac{I_0 - I_1}{I_0 - I_2} = \frac{3}{10},$$

where

$$I_0 = \frac{10I_1 - 3I_2}{7} \approx 2,97$$ mA.
