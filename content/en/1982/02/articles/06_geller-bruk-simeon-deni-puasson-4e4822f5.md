In 1981, it was the anniversary of the birth of Simeon Denis Poisson, an outstanding French scientist who we rightfully consider one of the creators of modern mathematical physics. The name Poisson appears every now and then in textbooks on mathematical analysis and electronic physics, probability theory and acoustics, in books on quantum mechanics and the theory of elasticity. In the history of science, Poisson now stands on a par with his outstanding contemporaries - Laplace, Lagrange, Fourier, Cauchy, Ampere, Gay-Lussac, Fresnel.

Poisson was born on June 21, 1781. Little is known about Poisson's parents. We know that his father was initially a soldier in the Hanoverian troops, but his military career was unsuccessful. Because of the nagging and harassment of the officials, he fled the army and settled in the small French town of Pithiviers. By the time his son was born, he held the humble but respected position of natarius.

The boy was completely ordinary, had no use for anything and did not show any special hope in early childhood. His parents even had doubts about his mental abilities. The father, of course, really wanted his son to become a natary, but the family council decided that he could not cope with this job and that it would be better for him to be a doctor. The decision of the family council is a kind of law. Simeon was sent to the town of Fontainebleau to Uncle Lanfant to train in a decent, but, in their understanding, simple repair as a surgeon. However, mastering this profession was not easy. To learn, for example, to do bloodletting (one of the main methods of treatment at that time), it was necessary to practice for long hours in piercing and stripping the veins of cabbage leaves. Poisson later told friends that even the largest veins still slipped away from under his needle at the last moment. Almost a year passed in the unbearable exercises, the uncle was pleased with his nephew, but the first self-vaccination entrusted to him ended in the death of the patient. This event shocked the young man so much that he absolutely refused to continue practicing medicine and returned to his parents in Pithiviers.

The 25-year-old Poisson took the place. In 1812, Poisson was elected academician of the Paris Academy of Sciences; since 1820 he has been a member of the Council of the University of Paris. He is entrusted with overseeing the teaching of mathematics in all colleges in France. At the École Polytechnique, Poisson is appointed examiner of artillery and examiner of applicants. The position of the examiner was in a certain sense higher than the usual professorial one: he took the final exams, thereby testing both the conditions of knowledge of the students of the Polytechnic School and how and what the professors taught them.

All the mixed French governments in those turbulent years paid great attention to Poisson's scientific merits. He received the title of baron, was awarded the Order of the Legion of Honor (the highest award in France), and became the priest of France. Poisson also received recognition abroad: he was a member of all scientific societies and academies in Europe and America, including an honorary member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences (since 1826).

Poisson, according to Arago, “possessed another advantage, which is often neglected even by those who are not highly ranked in science: the precise use of their duties.” It is known, for example, that the final exams at the Ecole Polytechnique took Poisson four weeks every day, during which he had to examine nine hours a day. “Only once,” writes Arago, “out of decency, Poisson refused to examine his eldest son, but the pupils of the Polytechnic School learned about this and sent him a delutation with an announcement that they fully believed his impartiality and asked him not to refuse the exam.” Poisson loved his teaching work, as evidenced by his famous statement: “Life is enriched by two things: being busy with mathematics and teaching it.” Poisson's lectures were distinguished by clarity and depth.

In the last years of his life (Poisson died in Paris in the spring of 1840), he set himself the task of writing a fundamental course in mathematical physics. Poisson, unfortunately, did not manage to complete this task completely.

One of the main concepts in electronics is the concept of electron potential. Potentiality depends on the magnitude and location of charges in space. Poisson in 1811 derived a differential equation relating the potential to the charge distribution density. The simplest problems in electronics can, of course, be solved without using Poisson's equations. But for any complex problems, when there are many charges and they are located in an arbitrary manner, it is possible to calculate the dependence of the potential on the coordinates only using this equation. Poisson's equation, together with the results of Euler, Gauss, Laplace, Green and Ostrogradsky, now underlies modern potential theory - an important branch of mathematical physics.

The third problem relates to physics. It is known that the process of radioactive decay of radium consists in the transformation of the nucleus of a radium atom into the nucleus of a radium atom with the emission of an alpha particle. The decay of each individual Ra nucleus occurs independently of the state of other nuclei, and the probability of such decay per unit time is constant. Let us denote this probability by the letter $p_0$. If there are only $N$ Ra nuclei in a sample, then the average number of alpha particles emitted per unit time is $\lambda = Np_0$. To define $\lambda$, it would seem that we need to know two numbers - $N$ and $p_0$. However, in the classic experiment of Rutherford, Chadwick and Alice, these numbers were not at all required by the experimenter. The purpose of this experiment was to search for a pattern that determines the probability of a radioactive image emitting a certain number of alpha particles per unit time.

If we denote by the letter ε the relative change in the length of the rod, and by the letter σ the stress arising in it, then for small deformations σ=Eε. The value of E is called the South model and is determined by the internal structure of the material of the rod.

Another important characteristic of the elastic properties of materials is the Poisson's ratio, which relates the relative changes in the transverse and longitudinal dimensions of the sample deformation. It is easy to verify that simultaneously with the change in the longitudinal dimensions the transverse dimensions also change if we consider, for example, the deformation of a rubber cord or even a hardened eraser. The question arises: how different are the relative deformations in different directions if each force is applied to the sample? This question was investigated by Poisson. Consider a cylindrical rod of length l and radius r. Let us imagine that a tensile force acts along the axis of the rod. In this case, stress σ_l and relative deformation ε_l=Δl/l>0 arise in it. The transverse dimensions of the rod also change - the radius of the cylinder decreases by Δr. Radial relative strain ε_r=Δr/r has the opposite sign to ε_l. Poisson's ratio is k=|ε_r/ε_l|.

Let us analyze the limits within which k varies. First, assume that the volume of the deformed body does not change, and write the expression for this fact:

π(r+Δr)^2(l+Δl)=πr^2l.

Opening the brackets and neglecting the products of small quantities Δr and Δl, we obtain

rΔl+2lΔr=0,

or, in other words,

ε_l=Δl/l=-2Δr/r=-2ε_r.

Poisson's ratio in this case is 1/2. In fact, the volume of a body increases when stretched, and we must write the inequality

(r+Δr)^2(l+Δl)>r^2l.

From this inequality it follows that k<1/2. (The same inequality is also true for non-cylindrical bodies.) On the other hand, by its very definition, Poisson’s ratio is a non-negative number. So,

0<k≤1/2.

A cork tree has a Poisson's ratio close to zero: when a piece of cork is stretched (or rolled off), the transverse dimensions change little (unless, of course, the deformation is too great!). That is why cylindrical corks made of balsa wood are used to seal bottles. On the other hand, cylindrical plugs are used for the same cellulite - Poisson's ratio for rubber is close to 1/2, which means that the transverse dimensions change quite significantly with longitudinal increase. If you push the rubber stopper into the neck of the bottle, it may simply get stuck. To overcome this difficulty, rubber stoppers are usually made into a final shape. It is useful to note, finally, that for the materials most often used in technology - metals, stone, concrete - the Poisson's ratio values ​​are usually between 1/4 and 1/3.
