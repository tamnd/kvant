The 25-year-old Poisson took the place. In 1812, Poisson was elected academician of the Paris Academy of Sciences; since 1820 he has been a member of the Council of the University of Paris. He is entrusted with overseeing the teaching of mathematics in all colleges in France. At the École Polytechnique, Poisson is appointed examiner of artillery and examiner of applicants. The position of the examiner was in a certain sense higher than the usual professorial one: he took the final exams, thereby testing both the conditions of knowledge of the students of the Polytechnic School and how and what the professors taught them.

All the mixed French governments in those turbulent years paid great attention to Poisson's scientific merits. He received the title of baron, was awarded the Order of the Legion of Honor (the highest award in France), and became the priest of France. Poisson also received recognition abroad: he was a member of all scientific societies and academies in Europe and America, including an honorary member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences (since 1826).

Poisson, according to Arago, “possessed another advantage, which is often neglected even by those who are not highly ranked in science: the precise use of their duties.” It is known, for example, that the final exams at the Ecole Polytechnique took Poisson four weeks every day, during which he had to examine nine hours a day. “Only once,” writes Arago, “out of decency, Poisson refused to examine his eldest son, but the pupils of the Polytechnic School learned about this and sent him a delutation with an announcement that they fully believed his impartiality and asked him not to refuse the exam.” Poisson loved his teaching work, as evidenced by his famous statement: “Life is enriched by two things: being busy with mathematics and teaching it.” Poisson's lectures were distinguished by clarity and depth.

In the last years of his life (Poisson died in Paris in the spring of 1840), he set himself the task of writing a fundamental course in mathematical physics. Poisson, unfortunately, did not manage to complete this task completely.

One of the main concepts in electronics is the concept of electron potential. Potentiality depends on the magnitude and location of charges in space. Poisson in 1811 derived a differential equation relating the potential to the charge distribution density. The simplest problems in electronics can, of course, be solved without using Poisson's equations. But for any complex problems, when there are many charges and they are located in an arbitrary manner, it is possible to calculate the dependence of the potential on the coordinates only using this equation. Poisson's equation, together with the results of Euler, Gauss, Laplace, Green and Ostrogradsky, now underlies modern potential theory - an important branch of mathematical physics.
