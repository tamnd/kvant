One hundred years ago

Many magazines have a section “What our magazine wrote about years ago,” from which the reader learns about interesting or curious, from a modern point of view, facts that took place in those times. Our magazine is still far from its centennial anniversary, so it’s too late for us to start such a section. However, let’s try to imagine what a magazine similar to ours might have written about years ago.

At that time, the phenomena of superconductivity and superfluidity had not yet been discovered; no one even suspected the possibility of creating lasers and thermonuclear reactors, satellites and space packages. Therefore, at first glance, it seems that inquisitive hypnasists interested in physics had nothing to read about. However, they also read, not unconditionally, that a generation of remarkable physicists has grown up who have created complex modern physical science.

Recently, the author of these lines came across an old book by B. Donat, “Physics in Games for Youth,” published at the end of the 19th century. In those days, the study of physics was more “experienced,” visual, and engaging in nature.

Below is a description of some fun experiments taken from the said book.

Fountain, by order

The structure of such a fountain is shown in figure 1. A bottle A (capacity about one liter) with two necks (top and side) is installed on a stand B, next to which there is a reservoir C for collecting water. Both necks are closed with rubber stoppers with curved tubes a and b inserted into them. The tube in the case of the main outlet of the fountain, and tube a, the end of which slightly does not reach the bottom of the reservoir, plays the role of a kind of lock.

If tube a is open, air enters the bottle and water flows freely out of it through tube b, increasing the amount of water into the reservoir. As soon as the water level reaches the hole in tube a, the access of air in the bottle stops and water stops flowing from tube b - the fountain has dried up!

In order for it to work again, it is necessary to open the holes in the tube a. Tube c in the bottom of the tank serves this purpose. If its diameter is smaller than the diameter of tube b, during operation of the fountain the flow of water into the reservoir exceeds its flow, and at some point in the opening, tube a closes - the fountain will immediately “go silent”. When the amount of water decreases enough that the end of tube a opens, the fountain starts again.

Empty glass

It turns out that various musical sounds can be extracted from a thin-walled glass goblet, and in a unique way. However, judge for yourself.

You can use hot water on your hands to remove grease from your fingers. Dip your finger in the water and carefully move it along the edge of the glass, constantly wetting your finger with water. At first, the glass will make an unpleasant creaking sound, but then, when the edges are well wiped, the sounds will become more melodic. By changing the pressure of your finger, you can change the tone of the sound extracted from the glass. In addition, the pitch also depends on the size of the glass, the thickness of its walls and the amount of contents in it.

Please note that not every glass is capable of producing pleasant drinking sounds, so searching for a suitable glass can be long and troublesome. It is best to drink (and not creak) very thin glasses, shaped like a rotating parabolide, with a long and thin stem. The tone of the sound can be changed by adding water to the glass - the more water there is in the glass, the lower it will sound.

(And one more note to the observation. When the water level rises to the middle of the glass, waves will appear on its surface due to the cooperation of the walls of the glass. The strongest disturbance will be in the place where your finger is currently located.)

It is interesting that on the basis of the described phenomenon, the famous American scientist Benjamin Franklin (who discovered, in particular, atmospheric electricity) created an original musical instrument. The purpose of a series of well ground glass cups, drilled in the middle, at equal distances from each other, was attached to one common axis. Under the box in which this system was located, a teacher was attached (like a sewing machine), which was rotated. From the simple touch of wet fingers to the rotating cups of the environment, the sounds intensified to fortissimo or fell to a whisper.

Now it is difficult to imagine this amazing musical instrument, but people who heard it assured that the harmony of its sounds had a stunning effect on both the performer and the listener. In 1763, Franklin gave his sample of this instrument to the English woman Miss Davis. For several years she demonstrated it in many European countries, and then this amazing instrument disappeared without a trace.

A mirror that doesn't confuse the right and left sides

Take two ordinary mirrors without frames, place them with their edges at right angles to each other with the mirror side inward and look straight into the corner along its bisector. You will see your image. Then close one eye, for example the right one, and your image will also close the right eye; raise your left hand - in the mirror your left hand also appears raised. Why?

This is not difficult to understand. You cannot directly see the image of your left half in the left mirror, since (following the law of reflection) the rays reflected from it do not go towards you, but in the opposite direction and on their way they meet the second, right mirror. This is where the final image of your left half is obtained, which you will see, since the right mirror is positioned in the appropriate manner for this. In other words, one mirror imperceptibly “transmits” the image to the other, resulting in not one reflection, but two, so that the left half naturally turns back into the left, and the right into the right.

Teller, try to gradually increase the angle of the mirrors. First, the middle of the face with the nose will disappear, then only one ear will remain, and at some angles the image of the face will disappear completely, but at an angle close to 180°, a clear image of the face will appear in a flat mirror. If you again do it in the reverse order, then everything will happen the other way around: first, the face begins to blur in width, the nose will swell, stick out in length, then a third eye will appear on the bridge of the nose, and... the like.

To understand why the image behaves this way, try to draw the path of rays in such a mirror system for different angles between the mirrors.
