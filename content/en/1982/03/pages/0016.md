⟦folio 14⟧

There are far fewer violet rays than blue rays in the light of daylight. The second culprit for the apparent discrepancy between theory and practice is our “recording device” - the eye of a person with normal vision. The fact is that the visual perception of the human eye depends on the wavelength of light. figure 2 shows an experimental curve that characterizes this dependence. From figure it is clear that the eye reacts much weaker to violet rays than to blue-green ones. That is why scattered solar rays perceived by the human eye practically do not have a phyolet component.

Why do we clearly see white clouds in the blue sky? When light is scattered by its constituent particles, aren’t the conclusions from Rayleigh’s law valid?

The fact is that clouds consist of tiny drops of water or ice crystals, the sizes of which, however, significantly exceed the wavelengths of visible light. Therefore, when sunlight is scattered on particles that make up clouds, Rayleigh’s law does not apply - light of all wavelengths is scattered with approximately the same intensity, and the clouds are perceived as white. This is what we see in the picture.
