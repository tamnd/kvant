From this condition—it is called the continuity condition for steady flow—it follows that $v_1:v_2=s_2:s_1$ and $v_2>v_1$ (at $s_1>s_2$).

Let us imagine that a certain mass of liquid, enclosed between sections $s_1$ and $s_2$, has moved in time $\Delta t$ so that section $s_1$ goes into section $s'_1$, and section $s_2$ into section $s'_2$. The gravitational potential energy of this mass remained the same, but the kinetic energy changed by the following values:
$$\Delta T = \frac{\Delta m_2 \cdot v_2^2}{2} - \frac{\Delta m_1 \cdot v_1^2}{2},$$
where $\Delta m_1$ is the mass of the fluid between sections $s_1$ and $s'_1$, and $\Delta m_2$ is the mass of the fluid between sections $s_2$ and $s'_2$. This change in kinetic energy is equal to the work done by external forces in time $\Delta t$.
The pressure forces $F_1=p_1 s_1$ and $F_2=p_2 s_2$ are external to the selected volume of liquid. Hence,
$$\frac{\Delta m \cdot v_2^2}{2} - \frac{\Delta m \cdot v_1^2}{2} = p_1 s_1 l_1 - p_2 s_2 l_2.$$ (*)
From the condition of continuity it follows that $s_1 l_1=s_2 l_2$ and $\Delta m_1=\Delta m_2=\varrho s_1 l_1=\varrho s_2 l_2$ (following fluidity and non-liquidity, unify the potential energy of gravity. In general, Bernoulli’s equation is written as follows:

With other equations, the Bernoulli equation, written for a medium with a variable density $\varrho$, forms the basis of gas dia- mics. Daniel Bernoulli, together with Leonhard Euler, owes the main credit for the development of the mechanics of fluid bodies.

It can be said without exaggeration that science was the only passion of Daniel Bernoulli. Because he was busy with science, he had a natural relationship with his father, with whom they constantly argued about nature. Father and son independently tackled the same problems and did so successfully. The following fact clearly demonstrates this. In 1732, the Paris Academy of Sciences announced a competition on the topic “On the relative inclination of the planets.” Two of the works submitted to the competition were recognized as the best, and it was decided to divide the prize between their authors. When the envelopes with the mottos were opened, it turned out that these authors were the father and son of Bernoulli. “I rejoice that your son also bears the stamp of Bernoulli and preserves the hereditary splendor of the family,” Leibniz wrote to Johann Bernoulli.

Daniel Bernoulli received prizes from the Paris Academy of Sciences several times. They noted his works “On the best method of constructing anchors” (1738), “On the marine application and ebb tide” (1740), “On the best method of constructing magnetic arrows of inclination” (1743), “On the best method of determining time at sea” (1745-1746), “The theory of the magnet” (1742, 1744, 1746)...

The scientific authority of Daniel Bernoulli was very high. Evidence of this was his election as a member of the multi-chemical Academies of Sciences (in addition to St. Petersburg) - Berlin (1747), Paris (1748), and the Royal Society of London (1750).

Until the last days of his life, he was engaged in scientific activities. On March 17, 1782, a servant found him asleep in his chair.
