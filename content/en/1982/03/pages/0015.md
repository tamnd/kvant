Note that in Rayleigh's law it is assumed that λ≥d, but the value of d itself is not included in the expression for intensity. This fact led to the fact that confusion regarding the nature of blue light scatterers in the atmosphere, thanks to Rayleigh, existed until the mid-twentieth century. And only in 1940, the Soviet physicist Academician L.I. Mandelstam showed that in reality the scattering of light in the atmosphere occurs not on the molecules of gases that make up the air, but on the scales of unexpected objects - inhomogeneity of air density. But where do these heterogeneities come from? After all, the air is in a state of thermodynamic equilibrium, and even if the wind blows, the inhomogeneities associated with it have dimensions that exceed the wavelength of light by a huge number of times, and cannot in any way affect the scattering of light.

To understand the nature of inhomogeneities in the refractive index of light, let's take a more detailed look at the understanding of thermodynamic equilibrium. For simplicity, the distance is a non-uniform macrosoppersia volume of gas in a closed state.

Physics is a distance system consisting of a gigantic number of particles, therefore the only possible way to describe the properties of such systems is statistical. The static approach means that we do not monitor the state of each molecule individually, but calculate the average values ​​of the corresponding physical quantities for the entire system as a whole. In this case, it is not at all necessary that for all molecules the values ​​of the physical quantity under consideration are equal to the corresponding average. When considering our macroscopic volume of a gas, the most important state will be in which the gas molecules are distributed evenly throughout the entire volume of the vessel. However, due to the thermal movement of molecules, there is always a non-zero probability that the concentration of molecules in some areas of the vessel will for some time exceed the average concentration of molecules in the volume under consideration (in this case, naturally, in another area of ​​the volume under consideration the concentration of molecules will decrease for this time). Theoretically, it is even possible to have a state where all gas molecules are present in one half of the volume of the vessel under consideration, and the second turns out to be completely empty. But the probability of such an event is expressed only by what we see in its implementation, even within the lifetime of the Universe, which, according to modern concepts, is 10¹³ years.

However, small deviations of physical quantities from their average values ​​are possible, but also constantly occur due to the thermal movement of molecules. These deviations are called fluctuations (from the Latin word fluctuari - to fluctuate). It is they that lead to the fact that in some areas the gas density increases, and in others it kills, which affects the value of the refractive index of light in these areas.

If we now return to the consideration of light scattering in the atmosphere, then all the discrepancies made for a limited volume of gas remain valid. In addition, just as air is a mixture of different gases, the difference in the thermal motion of molecules of different gases leads to additional possible occurrence of local inhomogeneity in the refractive index of light associated with fluctuations.
