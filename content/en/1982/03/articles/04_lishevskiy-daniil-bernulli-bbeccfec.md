Daniel Bernoulli

...for me the true pleasure of my whole life lies in the Russian imperial service...
D. Bernoulli

They say that nature, having created a genius, then rests, creating his descendants. And this is mostly true. But there are no rules without exceptions, and one of them is the Bernoulli family. Over the course of a hundred years, the Bernoulli family gave man a tree of ten outstanding scientists - mathematicians, physicists, doctors, lawyers, rhetoricians... Many of them were members of various academies, five were academicians of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences.

The more famous brothers are Jacob (1654-1705) and Johann (1667-1748) Bernoulli and Johann's son Daniel.

Daniel Bernoulli was born on January 29, 1700 in Groingen (Holland), where his father taught mathematics at the university. In 1705, the family moved to Basel (Switzerland), where Johann Bernoulli “inherited” the position of professor of mathematics after the death of his older brother Jacob.

Daniel studied at the Basel Gymnasium. After graduating from high school in 1713, he was sent to France to improve the meaning of the French language. After returning to his homeland in 1716, he received the title of Master of Philosophy.

At the insistence of his father, Daniel began studying medicine. He studied in Heidelberg, in Strasbourg, and after defending his dissertation “On Breathing” in 1720 he became a licentiate in medicine. But Daniel’s heart was not in medical practice. “...The example of his family members, namely his father and older brother Nikolai, as well as the inclinations of his own soul, attracted him to the mathematical sciences and to the study of nature,” this is how Daniel Bernoulli wrote about himself in the third person in his “Autobiography,” preserved in the archives of the USSR Academy of Sciences.

In 1724, Daniel Bernoulli's first scientific treatise, “Mathematical Exercises,” was published. In the same year he receives an offer to head the academy in Genoa. While Daniil is reasonable, an invitation came from Russia to enroll in the St. Petersburg Academy, which had just been created by Peter I.

The offer was replaceable, but Daniel did not want to part with his brother Nikolai, with whom he was connected by a touching friend. The difficulty was resolved very simply. The then president of the St. Petersburg Academy, L. L. Blumentrost, invited both Bernoulli, and in October 1725 he began to move to St. Petersburg. Daniil received the Department of Physiology, Nikolai - the Department of Mathematics.

The brothers were actively involved in the work of the academy. The minutes of the meeting dated January 14, 1726 record: “Daniel Bernoulli, professor of physiology, began mathematical studies on the Theory of Medical Needs, and decency will teach them physiologically: before lunch from 7 to 8. Nicholas Bernoulli, professor of mathematics, will read about those parts of mathematics that are tied to physics, and will read especially about mechanics from 8 to 9.”

The activities of Nikolai Bernoulli at the St. Petersburg Academy of Science did not last long. The climate of the northern capital turned out to be too harsh for him. Eight months after his arrival in St. Petersburg, Nikolai Bernoulli died.

Daniel Bernoulli remained in St. Petersburg until the summer of 1733, and then returned to Basel. Here he received the chair of medicine at the university, but was mainly engaged in experimental physics. In 1750, he headed the department of physics, which he held until the last days of his life.

After leaving Russia, Daniil Bernoulli maintained technical contact with the St. Petersburg Academy of Science and sent his works for publication. Of the 75 scientific works of Daniil Bernoulli, 47 were published in the proceedings of the St. Petersburg Academy of Science.

Daniel Bernoulli's contribution to science can hardly be overestimated. He studied the Ricatti equations $y = ax^n + by^2$, flasks of scabs and rods, probability theories, series, the limit of the expression $(1 + \frac{1}{n})^n$... Together with M.V. Lomonosov, he stood at the origins of the kinetic theory of gases. In his works one can find an anticipation of the laws of Gay-Lussac, Clapeyron and Charles...

"Hydrodynamics" was written in St. Petersburg and published in Strasbourg in 1738. In the preface to it, Daniel Bernoulli writes: “...I gladly announce that the main part of this work is due to the leadership, plans and support of the St. Petersburg Academy of Science... My present work represents the only goal: to bring use to the point of supporting the growth and social benefits of science.”

Daniil Bernoulli repeatedly emphasized that he was formed as a scientist at the St. Petersburg Academy of Science, that he made his discoveries in science thanks exclusively to the conditions that were created in Russia for the life and work of scientists. In hydrodynamics, Daniel Bernoulli gave the equation of the established motion of an ideal incompressible fluid. This equation is now called Bernoulli's equation.

From this condition—it is called the continuity condition for steady flow—it follows that $v_1:v_2=s_2:s_1$ and $v_2>v_1$ (at $s_1>s_2$).

Let us imagine that a certain mass of liquid, enclosed between sections $s_1$ and $s_2$, has moved in time $\Delta t$ so that section $s_1$ goes into section $s'_1$, and section $s_2$ into section $s'_2$. The gravitational potential energy of this mass remained the same, but the kinetic energy changed by the following values:
$$\Delta T = \frac{\Delta m_2 \cdot v_2^2}{2} - \frac{\Delta m_1 \cdot v_1^2}{2},$$
where $\Delta m_1$ is the mass of the fluid between sections $s_1$ and $s'_1$, and $\Delta m_2$ is the mass of the fluid between sections $s_2$ and $s'_2$. This change in kinetic energy is equal to the work done by external forces in time $\Delta t$.
The pressure forces $F_1=p_1 s_1$ and $F_2=p_2 s_2$ are external to the selected volume of liquid. Hence,
$$\frac{\Delta m \cdot v_2^2}{2} - \frac{\Delta m \cdot v_1^2}{2} = p_1 s_1 l_1 - p_2 s_2 l_2.$$ (*)

It can be said without exaggeration that science was the only passion of Daniel Bernoulli. Because he was busy with science, he had a natural relationship with his father, with whom they constantly argued about nature. Father and son independently tackled the same problems and did so successfully. The following fact clearly demonstrates this. In 1732, the Paris Academy of Sciences announced a competition on the topic “On the relative inclination of the planets.” Two of the works submitted to the competition were recognized as the best, and it was decided to divide the prize between their authors. When the envelopes with the mottos were opened, it turned out that these authors were the father and son of Bernoulli. “I rejoice that your son also bears the stamp of Bernoulli and preserves the hereditary splendor of the family,” Leibniz wrote to Johann Bernoulli.

Daniel Bernoulli received prizes from the Paris Academy of Sciences several times. They noted his works “On the best method of constructing anchors” (1738), “On the marine application and ebb tide” (1740), “On the best method of constructing magnetic arrows of inclination” (1743), “On the best method of determining time at sea” (1745-1746), “The theory of the magnet” (1742, 1744, 1746)...

The scientific authority of Daniel Bernoulli was very high. Evidence of this was his election as a member of the multi-chemical Academies of Sciences (in addition to St. Petersburg) - Berlin (1747), Paris (1748), and the Royal Society of London (1750).

Until the last days of his life, he was engaged in scientific activities. On March 17, 1782, a servant found him asleep in his chair.
