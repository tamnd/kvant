Mechanics of the magic cube

In response to numerous requests from readers, we are publishing an article about the structure of the “Hungarian hinged cube” («Kvant», 1980, No. 12). The article describes in detail how the “Hungarian Cube” can be made in a school workshop. We will return to the mathematical side of the matter in one of the following issues, when the cube made for you is in your hands.

Externally, the “magic cube” of the Hungarian architect E. Rubik appears to be a cube, as if cut into 27 equal small cups (of course, only 26 of them are visible). The small cubes are aligned in such a way that any layer of 9 cups adjacent to one face of the large cube can be freely rotated around its axis (see figure above). It is amazing that the entire system does not disintegrate; not a single one of the small cups is removed separately. The outer edges of the small cups have stickers in six different colors (nine stickers of each color); You need to rearrange the small cubes by rotating the layers so that each face of the large cube is painted the same color.

Many articles have been published on how to do this (for example, «Kvant», 1980, No. 12 and “Hayka and Life,” 1981, No. 3). The structure of the “Hungarian Cube” was not discussed in detail in these articles, which is quite natural. Having a cube, it is not difficult to take it apart and see how it works. My article was written for those who do not have the opportunity to solve the problem the hard way. I myself was in exactly this position, but I was lucky: I asked one of my acquaintances for the words “Hungarian Cube”, repaired it and returned it to the owner. With the knowledge I gained, it was no longer difficult for me to make my own cube.

How does he crit?

The “Hungarian Cube” is made up of 27 main parts (not counting colored stickers, springs and other little things). These parts are a three-dimensional cross hidden inside and 26 “cubes” extending out.

“Cubes” are not cubes at all. They were of three different types depending on their location on the faces of the large cube: central cubes (there are 6 of them, they are located in the center of each edge), middle cubes (there are 12 of them, they are located in the middle of each edge) and corner cubes (there are 8 of them, they are located at the vertices of the large cube).

For greater clarity, in Figures 5 and 6, the central cubes, middle cubes, corner cubes and the inner cross are shown in different colors. This coloring is not related to the colored stickers on the outer edges of the cubes.

Figures 5 and 6 show how the protrusions on the middle and corner cubes fold into an almost cylindrical protrusion on the inside of the face of the large cube, and a cylindrical ring-shaped depression is formed on the middle layer. The rotation of the layer (face) corresponds to the rotation of the cylindrical protrusion in the cylindrical recess.

This is the whole secret of E. Rubik's puzzle!

The role of spring 4 (see Fig. 4) is to be able to slightly pull back the rotated layer when turning.

From figure 4 you can see, in part, how to disassemble the “magic cube”. To do this, you need to remove the colored sticker from any one central cube, pull out the cap 1, hooking it by the edge with a needle or knife, and release the spring 4. After this, the central cube is removed from the axis of the cross, and the puzzle can be easily solved.

Let's move on to the second topic of the article - how to make a homemade “magic cube”. This is not about some inferior imitation of an industrial prototype. A homemade puzzle should be both more beautiful and more reliable than an industrial one. As one wise proverb says, if something is worth doing at least somehow, then it is worth doing properly.

A piece of nylon of the required size can be obtained by melting old stockings, pieces of fishing line, etc. at a temperature of 120° in an aluminum or tin jar. Nylon is easily cut with a knife, and a rough cross is easy to make. To give each arm of the cross an exact cylindrical shape, the following device is convenient: drill 4-5 holes in the metal plate; the narrowest one is of the desired diameter, each next one is 0.2-0.3 mm wider. When we push the nylon arm of the cross through the hole, the sharp edge of the hole removes shavings from the nylon. After pressing through the holes one by one, we will get a shoulder with an almost perfect cylindrical shape. When processing each next shoulder, you need to ensure that it turns out to be strictly perpendicular to those already processed shoulders, to which it should be perpendicular, and to the co-stems with the opposite shoulder. Perpendicularity is checked with a square for clearance. Violations are corrected with a knife. The cores are checked by eye and trimmed with a knife.

Thin cross axes are best made from steel knitting needles with a diameter of 2.5 mm, cut into pieces of the required length. The thick arms of the cross are cut to size (Fig. 11), and then axial holes with a diameter of 2 mm are drilled into them (Fig. 12). The ends of the thin axles (~7 mm) are roughly notched with a saw. These ends are heated and pressed into high-drilled holes. In order not to damage the cross, this operation is best performed by inserting the operated shoulder into the hole that was used for cutting to size. The remaining end of the thin axle is threaded. Since the start of the thread is always not very precise, it is better to make the thin axle a little longer. When the nut is screwed onto the thread, the excess piece is sawed off with a saw.

To tighten the nut, you need to make a slot (Fig. 13: the same figure shows the end of the hole for tightening such a nut).

The final fit of the cross will be checked when the puzzle is assembled, so the cross should not be perfected immediately.
