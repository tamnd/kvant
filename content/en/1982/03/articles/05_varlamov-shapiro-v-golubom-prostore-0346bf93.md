In the blue expanse

Looking around, we most often see only what we are already familiar with, what we are used to. Meanwhile, the process of cognition almost always begins with new observations.

Artists have a keen, professional observation, which is why the world reflected through the eyes of realist artists in their landscapes is especially bright and multi-colored, and individual natural phenomena appear. And at the same time, even a very good artist does not need concepts, sometimes the complex essence of phenomena that originate in nature, which he realistically displays on his canvases. But you can study the world around you from the paintings of a good landscape artist even better than in real life, or in a landscape the author seems to freeze a moment, intuitively enhancing the essential and omitting the unnecessary, the accidental.

Take a closer look at the painting “In the Blue Expanse” by Arkady Aleksandrovich Rylov. “White birds, like clouds, soar easily, bathing in the blue azure. And as smoothly as a white bird, a ship covered with sails glides over the rippling blue waves of the ocean,” this is how the famous art critic A. Fedorov-Davydov describes this picture. Admiring this wonderful painting in the Tretyakov Gallery, you forget that you are groaning in the hall of a museum, and you feel like you are a participant in this festival of nature.

And now let’s answer from painting and looking around through the eyes of an art critic. Where did we end up?

This question is not difficult to answer - the rocky island visible in the distance is covered with snow, and the sea is not at all wintery, so we are probably in polar latitudes.

Where did the artist paint the landscape from - from a rocky ledge on the shore? or from the ship?

Let's now try to estimate the speed of the wind that inflates the sails of a ship gliding in the distance. We are not the first to ask the question of estimating wind speed based on the size of waves or other manifestations in the nature around us. Back in 1806, the English admiral F. Beaufort developed a scale of twelve for an approximate assessment of wind speed by its effect on ground objects and by waves on the open sea. This scale has been adopted by the World Meteorological Organization and is also used by the concept.

Looking at the picture, we see that, according to the Beaufort table, the wind is “weak” at a speed of 5 m/s, the waves on the water are light, and small whitecaps form occasionally. By the way, why are they white and so different in color from the blue-green sea?

The color of the sea is determined by many factors, among which the most important are the position of the Sun, the color of the sky, the solution of the sea surface, the depth of the sea; if the depth is shallow, then an important factor is the presence or absence of algae in the water and the content of suspended solids in it. All these factors influence the reflection of light from the surface of the sea, the absorption and scattering of light in depth. Therefore, it is simply impossible to unequivocally mandate the visible color of the sea. But there are some things you can figure out. So,

The ratio of the wavelength of red light to the wavelength of syngo light is 6500Å/4500Å≈1.44. Raising this number to the power of four, we get 4.3. Thus, according to Rayleigh’s law, the intensity of the scattered blue light is four times higher than the intensity of the scattered red light, and a layer of air tens of kilometers thick acquires a color with a noticeable predominance of blue and cyan colors. And visible sunlight, which reaches us through the “barrier” of the atmosphere, is largely devoid of the short-wave part of its spectrum. Therefore, the Sun, which we see in the rays passing through the atmosphere, takes on a faint yellow tint. It can become stronger, oranger and redder as the Sun sets, when the sun's rays have to travel a greater distance in the atmosphere (when the Sun rises, of course, the color change occurs in the opposite order).

Note that in Rayleigh's law it is assumed that λ≥d, but the value of d itself is not included in the expression for intensity. This fact led to the fact that confusion regarding the nature of blue light scatterers in the atmosphere, thanks to Rayleigh, existed until the mid-twentieth century. And only in 1940, the Soviet physicist Academician L.I. Mandelstam showed that in reality the scattering of light in the atmosphere occurs not on the molecules of gases that make up the air, but on the scales of unexpected objects - inhomogeneity of air density. But where do these heterogeneities come from? After all, the air is in a state of thermodynamic equilibrium, and even if the wind blows, the inhomogeneities associated with it have dimensions that exceed the wavelength of light by a huge number of times, and cannot in any way affect the scattering of light.

To understand the nature of inhomogeneities in the refractive index of light, let's take a more detailed look at the understanding of thermodynamic equilibrium. For simplicity, the distance is a non-uniform macrosoppersia volume of gas in a closed state.

Physics is a distance system consisting of a gigantic number of particles, therefore the only possible way to describe the properties of such systems is statistical. The static approach means that we do not monitor the state of each molecule individually, but calculate the average values ​​of the corresponding physical quantities for the entire system as a whole. In this case, it is not at all necessary that for all molecules the values ​​of the physical quantity under consideration are equal to the corresponding average. When considering our macroscopic volume of a gas, the most important state will be in which the gas molecules are distributed evenly throughout the entire volume of the vessel. However, due to the thermal movement of molecules, there is always a non-zero probability that the concentration of molecules in some areas of the vessel will for some time exceed the average concentration of molecules in the volume under consideration (in this case, naturally, in another area of ​​the volume under consideration the concentration of molecules will decrease for this time). Theoretically, it is even possible to have a state where all gas molecules are present in one half of the volume of the vessel under consideration, and the second turns out to be completely empty. But the probability of such an event is expressed only by what we see in its implementation, even within the lifetime of the Universe, which, according to modern concepts, is 10¹³ years.

However, small deviations of physical quantities from their average values ​​are possible, but also constantly occur due to the thermal movement of molecules. These deviations are called fluctuations (from the Latin word fluctuari - to fluctuate). It is they that lead to the fact that in some areas the gas density increases, and in others it kills, which affects the value of the refractive index of light in these areas.

If we now return to the consideration of light scattering in the atmosphere, then all the discrepancies made for a limited volume of gas remain valid. In addition, just as air is a mixture of different gases, the difference in the thermal motion of molecules of different gases leads to additional possible occurrence of local inhomogeneity in the refractive index of light associated with fluctuations.

The characteristic size of the region of inhomogeneity of the refractive index of light (density inhomogeneity) arose from the need for inhomogeneity of equilibrium, and even if the wind blows, the associated inhomogeneities have dimensions that exceed the wavelength of light by a huge number of times, and cannot affect the scattering of light in any way.

Why do we clearly see white clouds in the blue sky? When light is scattered by its constituent particles, aren’t the conclusions from Rayleigh’s law valid?

The fact is that clouds consist of tiny drops of water or ice crystals, the sizes of which, however, significantly exceed the wavelengths of visible light. Therefore, when sunlight is scattered on particles that make up clouds, Rayleigh’s law does not apply - light of all wavelengths is scattered with approximately the same intensity, and the clouds are perceived as white.
