And it shines and warms

In the correspondence competition of the III Moscow Tour of Young Physicists there was the following task:

When burning, a candle shines and warms. Determine the calorific value of a paraffin candle.

The competition participants presented many different solutions to the jury. Their decisions were based on the indefinite heating of a body with a known heat capacity by a candle. The main disadvantage of this method is that the candle heats not only the selected body, but also the environment, and accounting for this is practically impossible. Other solutions were based on the phenomenon of convection, but were not completed due to the complexity of mathematical calculations. There were also such proposals: surround the candle with a metal shell of some simple shape and calculate the heat flow through it at steady temperatures inside and outside. The disadvantage of this method is the need to supply oxygen for the candle to burn, which is associated with additional heat loss.

Fig. 1.

The jury recognized the so-called compensation, or comparative, method as the most effective for solving this problem. Generally speaking, this is one of the most important methods of experimental physics. It allows you to find the values ​​of physical quantities not directly (which is often impossible to do accurately enough), but by comparing them with reference values. A classic example of the application of this method is changing unknown resistances using a Wheatstone bridge (Fig. 1). If the current through the galvanometer G is odd, the match is made (check yourself)

$$\frac{R_{3\tau}}{R_x} = \frac{R_1}{R_2},$$

Here, the unknown resistance $R_x$ is compared with the reference resistance $R_{3\tau}$ (the ratio $R_2/R_1$ is easy to change, for example, using a slider). We used this method to determine the thermal power of a candle.

Let's imagine a candle placed in a pipe through which air is forced. The heat emitted by the candle comes out partly with the air, and partly through the walls of the pipe. Now let’s replace the candle with some other emitter, similar in shape and size to the candle, but whose power is known and, moreover, can vary. For example, we heat it with electric heat. The power of the heater is added by heat flows carried away by the air in both cases. Since the experimental conditions do not change, the heat flows escaping through the pipe walls are also the same. Consequently, however, the total power of the candle and heat is also the same. Having determined the power of the electric heater as the product of current and voltage, we will find the thermal power of the candle.

Note that an important advantage of this method is the study of a stationary process, when all the combustion energy of a candle is released in the form of heat.

On figure 2 there is a photograph of the installation with which we carried out the experiments, and on figure 3 there is a diagram of this installation. Air is supplied to pipe 1 using fan 2, which allows you to adjust the speed of the air flow in order to achieve more complete combustion of fuel and steady flow. At the outlet of the installation pipe there is a temperature sensor 3; it makes it possible to control with great accuracy the equality of temperatures of the exhaust air in various experiments. A saucer 5 is placed on grid 4 to collect glass-flowing paraffin. The candle flame 6, mounted on a saucer, is surrounded by a long, smoked metal cylinder 7. Negligibly little light comes out through its upper hole (sufficiently distant from the candle flame). (In our experiments, the luminous flux emerging through the hole was about 4% of the total luminous power emitted by the candle). Almost all the light is absorbed by the cylinder and, turning into heat, is given off to the air.

For electrical heating, we used a wire rheostat (filled with ceramics) with a resistance of 550 Ohms and a nominal power of 20 W (in experiments, the power significantly exceeded the nominal and reached ~50-120 W).

To determine the heat of combustion of a candle, in addition to its thermal power, you also need to know the paraffin consumption per unit of time. This can be done by weighing the paraffin collected on a saucer and measuring the burning time of the candle (~3-4 minutes).
