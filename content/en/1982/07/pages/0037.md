What happens when light is reflected twice?

The double reflection picture presents itself to us as follows. The light falling on the first reflector is sent by a huge number of different parts of the luminous body. The oscillations in these light “messengers” are perpendicular to the direction of their propagation, but otherwise their directions are consistent and on average equal in probability (Fig. 3). Each such oscillation can be decomposed into two: one originates in the plane of incidence, the other perpendicular to it (Fig. 4). If the angle of incidence is optimal, then only one of them is reflected. (Which one remains unclear, so the directions shown in figure 4 are taken arbitrarily.) In this case, the reflected light is said to be aligned or polarized. The outcome of the second reflection depends on the orientation of the direction of oscillations and the plane of incidence: in geometry No. 2 there is a reflection, but in geometry No. 1 there is none.

If the reports are from the optimal angle of incidence on the first reflector, then both directions of oscillations will be represented in the reflected light, but to different degrees, and the light will be polarized not completely, but partially. It turns out that transmitted light is also polarized

Having a picture of the phenomenon, we began to express various ideas on its basis and test them experimentally. Here's one of them. The light from the lamp falls at an optimal angle onto several stacked glass plates, and then onto another glass plate, called the analyzer (Fig. 5). If our picture is correct, then, obviously, the transmitted light will also be polarized, but in a direction perpendicular to the polarization of the reflected light. Unlike reflected light, the polarization of transmitted light will not be complete, but the more plates in the stack, the greater the polarization will be: after all, light of one polarization is partially reflected and “pictured” from each of them. Indeed, we observed that in the geometry shown in figure 5, the light from the analyzer glass is reflected worse than in the case where the plane of reflection is perpendicular to the plane of incidence (this geometry in figure is not shown).

This essentially same experiment can be staged a little differently. Using polarized (that is, reflected at the desired angle) light, we verified that the pronunciation of a stack of plates strongly depends on the orientation of the direction of polarization and the plane of incidence.
