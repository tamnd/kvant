In any case, one can understand why comets at all times were considered by many peoples as harbingers of ill luck, bad omens, “the voice of the side” or, better said, “the devil.” The history of man is replete with wars, epidemics, palace coups and murders of persons of royal origin, so that almost always the appearance of a comet was “accompanied” by some tragic event.

In 44 BC, the death of Julius Caesar coincided with the appearance of a bright comet in the sky (this is known from the works of Roman historians), therefore in the Middle Ages and even later at European courts there was an opinion that the appearance of a bright comet could precede the death of persons of the reigning house. The bright comet of 1664 greatly frightened the French king Louis XIV, familiar to us from Dumas’s novel “The Vicomte de Bragelonne,” and the Portuguese king of that time, Alfonso VI, fearing that the comet had come for him, shot at it from a watermelon.

It is curious that the “Chinese point of view” on comets was radically different from the “European” one. According to the ancient Chinese, comets were couriers, carrying the will of the emperor throughout the “star provinces.” The planets were the rulers of the provinces, and the stars were the local authorities. Comets, moving from province to province, overshadowed government affairs.

However, one should not think that scientific study of comets began to be absent in ancient times. Already in the ancient Chinese chronicles, a fundamental scientific fact is reflected that underlies modern ideas about the physics of comets, which is that the tails of comets are always directed in the direction opposite to the Sun. The Roman philosopher and writer Luizio Seneca was also aware of this. Until the 17th century, science was dominated by the views of Aristotle, the recognized authority on all matters of natural history. Aristotle believed that comets are beyond the control of actromes, since, in his opinion, this phenomenon (a comet) arises in the earth’s atmosphere: the earth’s vapors, rising upward, ignite, colliding with the “sphere of fire,” and the comet’s tail is a flame driven by the wind. However, already in 1577, the most skillful observer of that time, Tycho Brahe, irrefutably proved the fallacy of Aristotle’s views. If the comet was within the earth's atmosphere, that is, relatively close to the Earth, then, observing it simultaneously from two different points on the earth's surface, Tycho Brahe and his students would have seen it next to different stars. But this did not happen - the comet is at the point of projection onto the same point in the starry sky, which means that the angle “observer No. 1 - comet - observer No. 2” (the so-called parallax) is very small, that is, the comet is very far from the Earth, and in any case already outside the Earth’s atmosphere. Since then, the study of comets has finally become the responsibility of actroms.

After Galileo was the first to point the telescope he built at the sky, a new profession appeared in astronomy - “comet catchers”. The fact is that a faint comet looks like a small foggy speck in the sky. But not every foggy spot is considered a comet. Now we know that a number of astronomical objects look like this: planetary and diffuse nebulae, globular and open clusters, galaxies. When the comet is still far from the Sun, it also hardly moves across the sky from night to night, so it is difficult to distinguish it from these “static” specks. Observe must be quite skillful. The French astronomer Charles Messier (1730–1817) for the first time in the history of astronomy compiled a catalog of such nebulae (most objects) precisely in order not to confuse them with comets.
