When the vector $\vec{v}$ changes, the sizes of moving bodies do not change, but continuous ones are reduced by $1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$ times. This means that in our case the area of ​​the area $S = \sqrt{1-v^2/c^2} S'$ and the electric field

$$E_\perp = \frac{E'_\perp}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}} = \frac{q/(\varepsilon_0 S')}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.$$

Similarly, for the case when the speed of the capacitor redistributes its area (Fig. 2, b), the dimensions of the area do not change, and the electric field parallel to the vector $\vec{v}$ remains the same in both reference systems:

$$E_\parallel = E'_\parallel = q/(\varepsilon_0 S').$$

The formulas obtained for the partial case of a flat capacitor are valid for any power plant (in the $K'$ system) field. Let us apply them to the relativistic electron. In the system $K'$, where the electron ends, its field $\vec{E}'$ has a well-known form - it is spherical and symmetrical (Fig. 3, a). In the $K$ system, in which the electron moves at a speed of $\vec{v}$, the field it creates is no longer symmetrical - in the direction perpendicular to the $\vec{v}$ vector it is stronger (Fig. 3, b). The greater the speed $v$, the greater the ratio $E_\perp/E_\parallel$, and the electric field of the electron will become increasingly transverse.

Finally, a transverse magnetic field $\vec{B}$ arises around the moving electron (as well as around a wire with current), the induction lines of which have the form of circles with centers on the line of motion of the electron.

Thus, in the field of the moving electron, the vectors $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ redistribute each other and the direction $\vec{v}$ of the electron’s motion.

In other words, this field is almost the same as that of an electromagnetic wave. Therefore, the electromagnetic field of a relativistic charged particle can be considered as a stream of photons accompanying this particle.

It is these photons that accompany electrons and positrons flying towards each other (in colliding electron-rositron beams) that can be used as colliding photon-photon beams with high energies. In turn, these photons, having interacted, can turn into other particles: $e^+$ and $e^-$, $\pi^+$ and $\pi^-$, etc.

The first birth of a new electron-rositron pair through intermediate collisions of Gamma beams (Fig. 4, a) was observed in 1971 at the Institute of Nuclear Physics of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences, and later (and at other accelerators) in Italy and the USA.

Peak production of pi mesons (Fig. 4, b) has been studied since 1979 at the largest accelerators in Germany and the USA.

Recently, experiments began in Novosibirsk at the new accelerator using colliding electron-rositron beams VEPP-4. The experimental program also includes a detailed study of the processes of transformation (condensation) of electromagnetic radiation into other particles.
