Comets

Almost a quarter of a century ago, people were eager to hear about the launch of an artificial Earth satellite. All over the world, radio receivers were tuned to the wavelength of his transmitter. The simple radio signals of the first satellite “beep-beep-beep” arose about the beginning of the space age in the history of man.

Today's tenth graders, not to mention the younger generation, were born in the era of space flight. Everyone gradually became vaccinated. And now even the flights of ships to other planets of the solar system - Venus, Mars, Saturn - are probably less imaginative than they once were - the launch of the first satellite, the flight of Yuri Gagarin, the landing of ame-

rican astroauts on the moon. Nevertheless, the current generation will also expect some “surprises” bordering on science fiction. However, the closest space experiments that generate imagination are expeditions to Halley’s Comet, which will be carried out in 1985-86, when this comet will once again return from the “outskirts” of the Solar System and approach the Sun.

This kind of experiment has at least two important aspects: psychological and scientific. Why psychological? The fact is that comets always spark people's imagination. The word "comet" is translated from Greek as "hairy star." Both in ancient times and in the Middle Ages, comets were most often depicted as severed heads with flying hair. A person is always in the starry sky of analogies with known, understandable images; hence the names of many constellations: Ursa Major, Gemini, Libra, etc. In this sense, comets were no exception. However, if the starry sky, even to a skilled observer, seemed practically unchanged from night to night, then the appearance of a bright “hairy star”, quite quickly, sometimes within just a few days, disappearing from the sky, was an outstanding and terrifying event.
