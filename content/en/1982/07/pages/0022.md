How can the transformation (or condensation) of light into matter be practically accomplished? Let us note, first of all, that such a transformation contradicts a well-established fact: light waves pass through each other without interaction (the principle of superposition of electromagnetic waves). It turns out that the principle of superposition is not always satisfied; for strong electromagnetic fields it is violated - after passing through each other, the light beams change. Now a new problem arises: for observing reactions like

$$\gamma + \gamma \rightarrow \pi^+ + \pi^-$$

it is necessary to push two dense beams of $\gamma$-quanta against each other. Unfortunately, dense beams of photons can currently only be obtained from lasers, but $\gamma$ lasers have not yet been invented. Conventional lasers emit photons with energies too low for our purposes. How to solve this problem?

It turned out that there is a very beautiful workaround with which one can already achieve the transformation of light into matter today: use colliding electron-nositron beams to produce the necessary $\gamma$-beams. Let's try to explain the essence of this idea.

As is known, the $\gamma$-beam can be represented either as a stream of high-energy photons, or as an electromagnetic wave of a large part.

In such a wave, the direction $\vec{E}$ of the electric field and the induction $\vec{B}$ of the magnetic field are perpendicular to each other and the speed $\vec{v}$ of the wave movement (Fig. 1). But the electromagnetic field of a relativistic electron (and positron), that is, an electron moving at a speed close to the speed of light $c$, has almost the same properties. Let us explain why this is so. To do this, consider the electric field... a moving capacitor. Yes, exactly a capacitor, because with the help of this simple example it will be easy to relate the good extracted field of a stationary electron to the field of a moving electron.

So, let's consider the electric field of a flat capacitor in two inertial reporting systems: in the $K$ system, in which the capacitor moves, and in the $K'$ system, where it ends. Let first the speed of the capacitor $\vec{v}$ be parallel to its plates (Fig. 2, a). Let us denote by $q$ the charge of the capacitor, and by $S'$ the area of ​​the capacitor plates in the system $K'$. Then the electric field $\vec{E'}$, perpendicular to the vector $\vec{v}$, is equal to $E' = E'_\perp = q / (\varepsilon_0 S')$. During the transition in the $K$ system, the charge, of course, remains the same, but the area of ​​the $S$ plates changes.
