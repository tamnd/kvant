Comets

Almost a quarter of a century ago, people were eager to hear about the launch of an artificial Earth satellite. All over the world, radio receivers were tuned to the wavelength of his transmitter. The simple radio signals of the first satellite “beep-beep-beep” arose about the beginning of the space age in the history of man.

Today's tenth graders, not to mention the younger generation, were born in the era of space flight. Everyone gradually became vaccinated. And now even the flights of ships to other planets of the solar system - Venus, Mars, Saturn - are probably less imaginative than they once were - the launch of the first satellite, the flight of Yuri Gagarin, the landing of ame-

rican astroauts on the moon. Nevertheless, the current generation will also expect some “surprises” bordering on science fiction. However, the closest space experiments that generate imagination are expeditions to Halley’s Comet, which will be carried out in 1985-86, when this comet will once again return from the “outskirts” of the Solar System and approach the Sun.

This kind of experiment has at least two important aspects: psychological and scientific. Why psychological? The fact is that comets always spark people's imagination. The word "comet" is translated from Greek as "hairy star." Both in ancient times and in the Middle Ages, comets were most often depicted as severed heads with flying hair. A person is always in the starry sky of analogies with known, understandable images; hence the names of many constellations: Ursa Major, Gemini, Libra, etc. In this sense, comets were no exception. However, if the starry sky, even to a skilled observer, seemed practically unchanged from night to night, then the appearance of a bright “hairy star”, quite quickly, sometimes within just a few days, disappearing from the sky, was an outstanding and terrifying event.

In any case, one can understand why comets at all times were considered by many peoples as harbingers of ill luck, bad omens, “the voice of the side” or, better said, “the devil.” The history of man is replete with wars, epidemics, palace coups and murders of persons of royal origin, so that almost always the appearance of a comet was “accompanied” by some tragic event.

In 44 BC, the death of Julius Caesar coincided with the appearance of a bright comet in the sky (this is known from the works of Roman historians), therefore in the Middle Ages and even later at European courts there was an opinion that the appearance of a bright comet could precede the death of persons of the reigning house. The bright comet of 1664 greatly frightened the French king Louis XIV, familiar to us from Dumas’s novel “The Vicomte de Bragelonne,” and the Portuguese king of that time, Alfonso VI, fearing that the comet had come for him, shot at it from a watermelon.

It is curious that the “Chinese point of view” on comets was radically different from the “European” one. According to the ancient Chinese, comets were couriers, carrying the will of the emperor throughout the “star provinces.” The planets were the rulers of the provinces, and the stars were the local authorities. Comets, moving from province to province, overshadowed government affairs.

However, one should not think that scientific study of comets began to be absent in ancient times. Already in the ancient Chinese chronicles, a fundamental scientific fact is reflected that underlies modern ideas about the physics of comets, which is that the tails of comets are always directed in the direction opposite to the Sun. The Roman philosopher and writer Luizio Seneca was also aware of this. Until the 17th century, science was dominated by the views of Aristotle, the recognized authority on all matters of natural history. Aristotle believed that comets are beyond the control of actromes, since, in his opinion, this phenomenon (a comet) arises in the earth’s atmosphere: the earth’s vapors, rising upward, ignite, colliding with the “sphere of fire,” and the comet’s tail is a flame driven by the wind. However, already in 1577, the most skillful observer of that time, Tycho Brahe, irrefutably proved the fallacy of Aristotle’s views. If the comet was within the earth's atmosphere, that is, relatively close to the Earth, then, observing it simultaneously from two different points on the earth's surface, Tycho Brahe and his students would have seen it next to different stars. But this did not happen - the comet is at the point of projection onto the same point in the starry sky, which means that the angle “observer No. 1 - comet - observer No. 2” (the so-called parallax) is very small, that is, the comet is very far from the Earth, and in any case already outside the Earth’s atmosphere. Since then, the study of comets has finally become the responsibility of actroms.

After Galileo was the first to point the telescope he built at the sky, a new profession appeared in astronomy - “comet catchers”. The fact is that a faint comet looks like a small foggy speck in the sky. But not every foggy spot is considered a comet. Now we know that a number of astronomical objects look like this: planetary and diffuse nebulae, globular and open clusters, galaxies. When the comet is still far from the Sun, it also hardly moves across the sky from night to night, so it is difficult to distinguish it from these “static” specks. Observe must be quite skillful. The French astronomer Charles Messier (1730–1817) for the first time in the history of astronomy compiled a catalog of such nebulae (most objects) precisely in order not to confuse them with comets. The fact is that the question of the origin of the solar system is closely connected with the problem of the origin of life on Earth and in the Universe in general, with the problem of the existence of external civilizations. These problems are no less fundamental than the problems of the wickedness of the Universe. To solve them, you need to know the initial conditions under which the Solar system was formed. And it turns out that comets may provide the necessary information about this.

According to modern concepts, all bodies of the Solar System (and, in particular, comets) arose in a single process of the wickedness of a presolar gas and dust cloud. There is currently no single, generally accepted point of view on how this happened. This is due to the great uncertainty in the initial conditions of the formation and subsequent failure of the presolar cloud. It is believed, for example, that the emergence of planetary systems around stars is an ordinary phenomenon, and in this regard, our star - the Sun - is not something exceptional. This is due to the location of the Sun in the Galaxy.

Our Galaxy is a spiral Galaxy. According to modern concepts, the spiral arms of galaxies are compaction waves. The galaxy is rotting. In this case, the angular velocity Ω depends on the distance R to its center, that is, Ω = Ω(R). And the compaction waves rotate with a constant angular velocity Ω_B. There is, very conveniently, such a circle of radius R_K in the galactic disk, the angular velocity of rotation along which coincides with the angular velocity of the compression waves (that is, the equality Ω(R_K) = Ω_B holds true). Such a circle is called shortened. In each galaxy (including ours) there is only one shortened circle.

What are comets according to modern scientific views? The masses of comets are small. As already mentioned, they are probably contained within $10^{15} \div 10^{22}$ g (Earth mass≈6·10$^{27}$ g). In the gravitational field of the colossal mass of the Sun ($\approx 2 \cdot 10^{33}$ g), they move according to the law of universal gravitation, along orbits that are finite sections, that is, curves obtained when a cone intersects a plane. These are circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola. The Sun is at the focus of the orbits. The gravitational field of the large planets (Jupter and Saturn) disturbs their orbits, changes them, so that a comet moving to the right along an elliptical trajectory can switch to a hyperbolic or parabolic one. Comets that have elliptical orbits (the appearance of a circle in such conditions is extremely unlikely) periodically return to the Sun. Such comets are called periodic. There are not many of them—several more than seventy.

Research has shown that, in fact, the primary orbits of all comets were ellipses, most of which gradually turned into hyperbolas and parabolas under the influence of the juxtaposition of Jupter and Saturn. This means that comets originated within the solar system. Previously, there was a beautiful hypothesis according to which the comet cloud arose from the icy remains of the tenth planet of the solar system - Phaethon, which once existed between the orbits of Mars and Jupter and was destroyed by the explosion. Later, such an explanation began to seem far from random. Comets will also arise as a by-product during the formation of planets, as a result of other processes in a DOS cloud filled with gas and dust. It is important, however, that they most likely arose in the Solar System, evolved little and, therefore, carry information about the initial conditions.

What are comets from a physical point of view? Almost all of the comet's mass is concentrated in its nucleus. The core appears to be a solid mixture of rocks, ice and various chemical

combined (methane, aminak, water, etc.), small particles, etc. The dimensions of the nuclei are likely to range from several kilometers to tens of kilometers. As they approach perihelion, cometary nuclei are “turned” into atmospheres consisting of various molecules and dust grains. Atmospheres appear as a result of the core being heated by the sun's rays - when heated, ice evaporates, and small particles are also released during evaporation. The dimensions of the atmosphere near perihelion are very large. For example, Halley's comet in 1910 had a head (i.e., core + atmosphere) with a diameter of over 100,000 km. A typical comet's head usually produces one or more tails, which always point away from the Sun.

The chemical composition of cometary atmospheres (heads and tails) is determined from their spectra. The most commonly observed molecules in the heads of comets are molecular carbon (C₂), cyanogen (CN), triatomic carbon (C₃) and other molecules. Comet tails are divided into three types based on their composition. Type I tails consist of plasma - ionized molecules N₂⁺, CO⁺ and electrons - with a concentration n ≈ 10÷100 particles/cm³. The spectra of type II and III tails are continuous. Type III tails are dusty, type II tails are apparently mixed - gas and dust.

Neutral molecules and dust particles in the atmospheres of comets move under the influence of two oppositely directed forces: the force of gravitational attraction to the Sun and the repelling force of solar light pressure*). (The force of attraction to the comet's nucleus is negligible compared to this force.)

The fact that the tails of comets point in the direction opposite to the Sun means that repulsion from the Sun prevails over attraction. The acceleration of the part in the tails is usually changed in fractions of the solar attraction acceleration g₀ at a given distance. In type II and III tails, the acceleration of the parts is ~(1÷20)g₀, in type I tails - on the order of (100÷1000)g₀. Can radiation pressure provide such accelerations of dust particles and molecules? A speck of dust - yes. With molecules the situation is more complicated. Mathematically, the calculation showed that the maximum possible acceleration that a molecule can receive under the influence of radiation pressure is 56g₀. This is enough to explain the behavior of tails of the second type. But not the first.
