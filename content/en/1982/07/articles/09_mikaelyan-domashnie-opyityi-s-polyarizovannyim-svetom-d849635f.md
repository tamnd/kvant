Home experiments with polarized light

Instead of introducing
The experiments described in this article are not at all similar in nature to those that are usually done in school (or even in college). There, through experience, they either check the correctness of some correspondences arising from already known laws, or, by changing part of the quantities included in these correspondences, they calculate others. For example, from Hooke’s law and Newton’s second law it follows that the external oscillation period of the load is equal to $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. You can independently measure the mass of the load $m$ and the elasticity of the spring $k$, calculate the period $T$ and compare it with the value changed experimentally. You can also measure the period and mass of the load and calculate the springs.

Such experiments are very necessary and are constantly being carried out not only in educational, but also in scientific laboratories. However, experiments with other approaches to the problem are no less important for science. Let's say you were filled with some unknown effect, and it aroused your beating. You begin to explore what factors and conditions this effect depends on. Moreover, the talk is not about checking any laws, because there is nothing to check yet. The laws are not yet known and may not be established soon. But you will make your contribution to the development of science: the future theory will have to be binding on your results. And this does not exhaust the significance of such research. Until a complete theory emerges, a number of important conclusions can be drawn. In addition, the discovered effect may even find practical application. Let us at least remember the magnetic needle, which served as a journey long before the nature of magnetism was understood.

What interested us?

A bright ray of sunlight falls on a glass plate and... is not reflected. We see illuminated specks of dust on the surface of the glass, but there is no bunny on the wall. When we introduced a piece of wireless mica into the rays going to the glass, a reflection appeared and the field of view was colored with all the colors of radgia. They shimmered, turning into each other when turning or showing mica. In our experiments we made these and several other discoveries. We are me and two eight classics.

Disappearance of reflected light

Postponing the “mica effect” for later, we took up the effect of the disappearance of reflection and the presence of its methodical study.

We first noticed this phenomenon when looking at images of bright objects outside the window after two reflections. The first was in the window glass, the second in the glass plate that one of us was holding in his hands. Measuring the position of the glasses and following with our eyes the image of an object (for example, a light cloud low above the horizon), we discovered that with some images the image seemed to fade, greatly losing its brightness. The geometry of the ray path for this case is shown in figure 1. We will often encounter it, and for brevity we will call it geometry No. 1. Note that the planes of the first and second reflections are mutually perpendicular (we will call the reflection plane the plane in which the incident and reflected rays and the perpendicular to the glass surface lie), and the angles of incidence of the rays on both glasses (they were absent from the perpendicular to the glass surface) are close to 60° to the eye.

Understanding that the brightness of the image is determined by the amount of light reflected from the first and second glasses, we asked the question: at which of the two reflections - the first or the second - does the light reflectance decrease? By examining objects reflected once in glass, we were convinced that at whatever angle of incidence of the rays their reflection does not exhibit a minimum. What happens when light is reflected twice?

The double reflection picture presents itself to us as follows. The light falling on the first reflector is sent by a huge number of different parts of the luminous body. The oscillations in these light “messengers” are perpendicular to the direction of their propagation, but otherwise their directions are consistent and on average equal in probability (Fig. 3). Each such oscillation can be decomposed into two: one originates in the plane of incidence, the other perpendicular to it (Fig. 4). If the angle of incidence is optimal, then only one of them is reflected. (Which one remains unclear, so the directions shown in figure 4 are taken arbitrarily.) In this case, the reflected light is said to be aligned or polarized. The outcome of the second reflection depends on the orientation of the direction of oscillations and the plane of incidence: in geometry No. 2 there is a reflection, but in geometry No. 1 there is none.

If the reports are from the optimal angle of incidence on the first reflector, then both directions of oscillations will be represented in the reflected light, but to different degrees, and the light will be polarized not completely, but partially. It turns out that transmitted light is also polarized

Having a picture of the phenomenon, we began to express various ideas on its basis and test them experimentally. Here's one of them. The light from the lamp falls at an optimal angle onto several stacked glass plates, and then onto another glass plate, called the analyzer (Fig. 5). If our picture is correct, then, obviously, the transmitted light will also be polarized, but in a direction perpendicular to the polarization of the reflected light. Unlike reflected light, the polarization of transmitted light will not be complete, but the more plates in the stack, the greater the polarization will be: after all, light of one polarization is partially reflected and “pictured” from each of them. Indeed, we observed that in the geometry shown in figure 5, the light from the analyzer glass is reflected worse than in the case where the plane of reflection is perpendicular to the plane of incidence (this geometry in figure is not shown).

This essentially same experiment can be staged a little differently. Using polarized (that is, reflected at the desired angle) light, we verified that the pronunciation of a stack of plates strongly depends on the orientation of the direction of polarization and the plane of incidence.

What conclusions can be drawn from the observed “mica effect”?

1. In geometry No. 1, light is incident on the second reflector and is polarized so that no reflection occurs. When mica is introduced, a reflection appears. This means that vibrations in a different direction appear in the light wave that passes through it. In other words, mica changes the polarization state of light.

2. Mica acts differently on light of different wavelengths (different colors), but this manifests itself in not very thin layers.

By using these properties of mica, we were able to greatly increase the colorivity of detecting partial polarization of light. Further, at a low degree of polarization, the introduction of mica into the rays incident on the glass analyzer gives a characteristic color in the reflection of light, which changes with the rotation of the mica. Due to the high chromaticity of the eye, with this method of detecting polarization there is no need to worry about accurately finding the corresponding position of the plane of incidence, which, of course, simplifies observations.

With this “improvement” we have, for example, established that side-lit clouds send out partially polarized rays (Fig. 7). We also observed the polarization of the cloudless sky. The effect in this case is not particularly great and is best manifested if you stand sideways to the Sun, so that

3. When we thought about the “mica effect,” we concluded that this effect had something to do with the fact that the interaction of mica with light depends on direction. More precisely: mica has different properties in different directions, and therefore the result of light passing through it depends on how the vibrations in the light wave are oriented relative to those directions. Indeed, when the plate is rotated relative to the axis of the light beam, only the orientation of the crystalline axes of the mica changes with respect to the direction of vibration. Glass, like glass, apparently does not have any special directions, that is, their positions are the same for all directions.

We were unable to advance further in understanding these issues, but we were able to confirm the correctness of the general idea through experimentation. To do this, we artificially broke the internal symmetry of the ortec with the help of mechanical directions. In geometry No. 1, in the rays incident on the second glass, we placed a strip of Ortex with dimensions $2 \times 20 \times 200$ mm. It turned out that if you bend and twist the strip, then in some places a characteristic eclipse of the field of view appears, which disappears when directed in the same direction. Then we decided to “freeze” the directions. Using an iron, we slightly softened a small section of the middle part of the strip, applied a tensile force and, without removing it, quickly cooled the strip under running water. For example, Orteklo began to behave like cellophane film.
