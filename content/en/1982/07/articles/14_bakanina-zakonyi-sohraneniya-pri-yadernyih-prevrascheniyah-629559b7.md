Conservation laws during nuclear transformations

The processes occurring in nuclei during their interactions are always complex and are far from fully studied. However, if it is known from experience what products are obtained as a result of a nuclear reaction, then their speeds (and therefore kinetic energies) can be calculated using the laws of conservation of energy and momentum.

The law of conservation of momentum is satisfied in all nuclear reactions: the total momentum of the resulting particles is always equal to the initial momentum of the system.

When using the law of conservation of energy, one must keep in mind that some nuclear reactions involve the release of energy, while others involve the absorption of energy. This is due to the fact that the total mass of the system before interaction can be either greater or less than the total mass of the products formed. There is, as they say, a mass defect $\Delta m$, which determines the energy $\Delta E$ released or absorbed during the reaction. According to Einstein's formula,

$$\Delta E = \Delta mc^2,$$

where $c$ is the speed of light.

It should be remembered that the mass defect is always much less than the mass of the nuclei both before and after the reaction. Therefore, it needs to be taken into account only in cases where it is necessary to determine the reaction energy $\Delta E$. If it is given or there is no need to determine it in a given problem, you can (and should) use rounded values ​​of nuclear masses.

Let's consider several specific tasks.

Problem 1. Radon is an alpha radioactive gas with atomic mass $A = 222$. What fraction of the total energy released during the decay of radon is carried away by the α particle (Fig. 1)?

Fig. 1.

The energy $\Delta E$ released during decay (due to a mass defect) is released in the form of kinetic energy of the reaction products: α-particle ($A_a = 4$) and residual nucleus ($A_a = 218$). Since the mass defect exists less than the mass of the original nucleus, the released energy is small compared to the rest energy of the nuclei, and a non-relativistic (classical) formula can be used for the kinetic energy. Hence,

$$\Delta E = \frac{m_a v_a^2}{2} + \frac{m_a v_a^2}{2}.$$

In addition, the law of conservation of momentum must be satisfied:

$$m_a v_a - m_a v_a = 0.$$

We believe that before the decay the radon nucleus accumulated or, in any case, its kinetic energy was much less than the energy of the reaction $\Delta E$, therefore we assume the total momentum to be equal to zero.

The fraction of the total energy carried away by the α particle is equal to

$$\frac{E_a}{\Delta E} = \frac{E_a}{E_a + E_a} = \frac{m_a v_a^2}{m_a (m_a / m_a)^2 v_a^2 + m_a v_a^2} = \frac{m_a}{m_a + m_a} = \frac{A_a}{A} \approx 0,98.$$

So, the alpha particle carries away 98% of the energy released during decay.

The task is essentially similar to the previous one. Let us write down the laws of conservation of energy and momentum:

$$\frac{m_T v_T^2}{2} + \frac{m_a v_a^2}{2} = \Delta E,$$
$$m_T v_T - m_a v_a = 0.$$

From here, taking into account that the atomic masses of tritium and α-particles are equal to $A_T = 3$ and $A_a = 4$, respectively, we find

$$E_T = \frac{m_T v_T^2}{2} = \frac{m_a}{m_T + m_a} \Delta E = \frac{4}{7} \Delta E \approx 2,74 \text{ МэВ},$$
$$E_a = \Delta E - E_T = \frac{3}{7} \Delta E \approx 2,06 \text{ МэВ}.$$
