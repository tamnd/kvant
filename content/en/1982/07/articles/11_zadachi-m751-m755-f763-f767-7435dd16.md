◆ A finite set of points on a plane can have 0, 1, 2, 3 “almost centers of symmetry” (see figure 1, a)—respectively. Let us show that a finite set M on a plane cannot have more than three “almost centers of symmetry”.

First of all, we note that the set M has a finite number of “almost centers of symmetry”, since they can only be the midpoints of the segments connecting the points of the set M. Let us now choose a straight line on which, when projected, the points of the set M and its “almost centers of symmetry” do not merge. Since upon projection onto a straight line the center of symmetry goes back to the center of symmetry, it is enough to prove our statement for a finite set of points lying on the same straight line.

Consider a set of points on a line with coordinates $x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_n$. If we throw out the first point, then the center of symmetry of the remaining set can only be the point with coordinate $(x_2 + x_n)/2$; if we throw out the last one, then only the point with coordinate $(x_1 + x_{n-1})/2$; finally, if we throw out some non-extreme point, then the center of symmetry of the remaining set can only be the point with coordinate $(x_1 + x_n)/2$ (see Fig. 2; at figure 2, and we threw out the middle point - the center of symmetry of the remaining set falls into it). This means that a finite set of points on a line, and thereby any finite set of points on a plane, cannot have more than three “almost centers of symmetry.”

M717. Given natural numbers $n$ and $r$, $1 \leq r \leq n$. The distance of the entire multiplication of a subset of $[1, 2, \dots, n]$ consisting of $r$ numbers, and in each selection the smallest number. Prove that the arithmetic mean of all chosen numbers is $\frac{n+1}{r+1}$. (For example, with $n=3$, $r=2$ we get three subsets $[1, 2], [1, 3], [2, 3]$ and the arithmetic mean is $(1 + 1+2)/3 = 4/3$.)

◆ First of all, note that the set $[1, 2, \dots, n]$ has $C_n^r = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$ subsets consisting of $r$ numbers. We will present two properties of the numbers $C_n^k$), which we will need to solve our problem:
$$1^\circ. C_n^k + C_n^{k+1} = C_n^{k+1};$$
$$2^\circ. C_n^k + C_n^{k-1} + \dots + C_k^k = C_n^{k+1}.$$

We will call subsets of the set $[1, 2, \dots, n]$ consisting of $r$ numbers $r$-subsets. It is easy to say that the smallest elements of the $r$-subset of the set $[1, 2, \dots, n]$ can only be the numbers 1, 2, ..., $(n-r+1)$. Moreover, the number $k$ is immediately the smallest for $C_n^{k-1}$ such subsets (indeed, each $r$-subset for which the number $k$ is the smallest element contains, along with $k$ and $r-1$, an element from the set $[k+1, k+2, \dots, n]$, consisting of $n-k$ elements). Therefore the sum of all chosen numbers is equal to
$$S = 1 \cdot C_n^{r-1} + 2 \cdot C_n^{r-1} + \dots + (n-r+1) C_n^{r-1},$$
(*)

and we need to show that the arithmetic mean $\frac{S}{C_n^r}$ (there are $C_n^r r$-subsets in total) is equal to $(n+1)/(r+1)$, or that $S = C_n^r \cdot (n+1)/(r+1) = C_n^{r+1}$.

Taking advantage of the fact that $C_n^{k-1} = C_n^k - C_n^{k-1}$ (see $1^\circ$), we transform the sum $S$ as follows:
$$S = 1 \cdot C_n^{r-1} + 2 \cdot C_n^{r-1} + \dots + (n-r+1) C_n^{r-1} =$$
$$= 1 \cdot (C_n^r - C_n^{r-1}) + 2 \cdot (C_n^{r-1} - C_n^{r-2}) + \dots + (n-r) (C_n^{r+1} - C_n^{r+1}) + (n-r+1) C_n^{r+1} =$$
$$= C_n^r + C_n^{r+1} + \dots + C_n^{r+1},$$

Taking into account the property $2^\circ$, we obtain $S = C_n^{r+1}$, as required.

This problem can also be solved by induction by considering the set $[1, 2, \dots, n+1]$ and noting that
$$S(n+1, r) = S(n, r) + S(n, r-1)$$
(**)

(see Fig. 2); here $S(m, k)$ denotes the sum of the smallest numbers of the $k$-subset of the set $[1, 2, \dots, m]$.

Assuming proven equality
$$S(n, r) = C_n^{r+1}, S(n, r-1) = C_n^{r+1}$$

for the $r$- and $(r-1)$-subsets of the set $[1, 2, \dots, n]$ (the induction basis is contained in the conditions of the problem), we obtain, taking into account (**) and the property $1^\circ$,
$$S(n+1, r) = C_n^{r+1} + C_n^{r+1} = C_n^{r+1},$$

which is what is required.

M719. a) For which $n \geqslant 3$ there is a set of $n$ consecutive natural numbers having the following properties: the largest of these numbers is a divisor of the least common multiple of the remaining $n-1$ numbers?
6) For what $n \geqslant 3$ is there a unique set of $n$ consecutive natural numbers that have the indicated properties?

Any natural number $n$ for some $k$ satisfies the inequality $2^k < n \leqslant 2^{k+1}$. then $2^k < n < 3 \cdot 2^k < 5 \cdot 2^k$.
Consider the set
$$A = \{3 \cdot 2^k, 3 \cdot 2^{k-1}, \dots, 3 \cdot 2^k-n+1\}$$
of $n$ consecutive natural numbers. Since $n-1 \geqslant 2^k$, among $n-1$ consecutive natural numbers from $3 \cdot 2^k-n+1$ to $3 \cdot 2^k-1$ there is at least one, is done on $2^k$. For $n \geqslant 4$ at least one of them is divisible by 3. Therefore, the number $3 \cdot 2^k$ is a divisor of the least common multiple of the remaining $n-1$ numbers in the set $A$ for any $n \geqslant 4$.

For $n \geqslant 6$, it is easy to construct an example of another set that has the indicated properties:
$$B = \{5 \cdot 2^k, 5 \cdot 2^{k-1}, \dots, 5 \cdot 2^k-n+1\}$$
(check it out!). Thus, for $n \geqslant 6$ there are at least two sets $A$ and $B$ that have the indicated properties. It remains to consider the case $n=3$, 4 and 5.

For $n=5$ there are also two required sets: $\{2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ and $\{8, 9, 10, 11, 12\}$.

Since $m+3$ is an LCM divisor, $(m, m+1, m+2)$ is divisible by $m+3$. Finally, for $n=3$ there are no such sets $\{m, m+1, m+2\}$: the LCM of $(m, m+1) = m(m+1)$ ($m$ and $m+1$ are coprime), but $m(m+1)$ is not divisible by $m+2$, since $(m-1)(m+2) < m(m+1) < m(m+2)$.
