Let us assume that during a short time interval Δt after the start of movement, the rope has moved a distance Δl and acquired a speed v. Since Δt is small, we can assume that

$$v^2 = 2a \cdot \Delta l,$$

(1)

where a is the acceleration of all points of the rope at the moment the movement begins.

From the law of conservation of energy (there is no training) it follows that

$$Mv^2/2 = \Delta \Pi,$$

(2)

where M is the mass of the entire rope, ΔП is the change in the potential energy of the rope over time Δt. Obviously, ΔП corresponds to the redistributed mass of the rope, as a result of which a piece of length Δl “passes” from point A to point B (see figure). Thus,

$$\Delta \Pi = \frac{M}{l} \cdot \Delta l \cdot gh.$$

(3)

From (1)—(3) the acceleration of the rope at the moment of the beginning of movement is found:

$$a = gh/l.$$

S. Krotov

In equilibrium, the leading force of all pressure forces acting on the ball is equal to its weight and the weight of the water flowed out by it; therefore, if we neglect the skimability of water and the material of the ball, the depth of its immersion will not change with increasing air pressure. If the skimability of the material of a floating body is high compared to the skimability of water, then the body will sink with an increase in external pressure. This happens in a well-known toy in which an upside-down test tube floats in a beaker directed by water, tightened on top with a thin rubber band.
