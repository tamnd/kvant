Now let's talk very briefly about the Cockroft and Walton experiment itself.

Artificial distribution of the lithium nucleus (April 1932)

The Cockcroft-Walton installation was located in a large, bright room. You see her in the photo. On the left are four sections of the electrical system generator, on the right is an accelerating tube of two sections. The remaining columns are condensers or insulating supports.

The idea of ​​obtaining high quality voltage is very simple. If you charge $n$ with identical em bones to a voltage $U_0$ and connect them in series, then the potential difference at the ends of the circuit will be $U=nU_0$. The most difficult thing is to come up with a mechanism for recharging such a system. And this problem was solved by Cockroft and Walton extremely wittily.

The second most important part of the installation was an accelerated tube, consisting of two sections, each of which was supplied with voltage from a generator (up to 350 thousand volts). Thus, the maximum energy of the proton beam could be ~0.7 MeV.

After the “suggestion” made by Rutherford, an installation for studying the artificial distribution of the lithium nucleus appeared at the lower end of the accelerating tube (in the photograph it is covered with black matter). At first, the experimental scheme looked as shown in figure 4. A beam of protons fell onto a lithium mine, turned at an angle of 45° to the direction of the beam. The a-particles escaping from the mine hit a screen made of zinc sulfide. When an energy (several MeV) alpha particle hits such a screen, a flash of light occurs, which can be seen using a microscope.

Having started the experiment, the researchers began to gradually increase the voltage on the tube. When it reached a value of 125 kV, the first flashes from a-particles appeared. There were still few of them (5 pieces per minute at a current of 1 μA), but the more the voltage increased, the more often light points appeared. The number of flashes turned out to be proportional to the proton current.
