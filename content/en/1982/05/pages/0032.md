“Stand on the right, pass on the left...”

If you live in a big city where there is a subway - the most convenient and reliable form of urban transport, then you, of course, have heard this phrase many times, amplified by loudspeakers, loudly sounding under the arches of the tunnel. Of course, many of you, being late for some important task, did not stop stepping on the escalator, but, like knocking your heels on the steps, rushed down, maneuvering like a slalomist between the Stories on the right and the passages on the left. But quite often, your uncontrollable forward movement somewhere down would come across a dense mass of people, for some reason not wanting to pass either to the left or to the right. You may have even noticed that during rush hours, the always-moving lighthouse is filled to the top with people who, despite the real confessions of the escalator attendant, do not want to pass. Let's stop worrying about the passengers' ignorance and look at this situation with the objective gaze of a researcher. But first, here’s an example.

Let's consider a moving belt (conveyor), on which some objects lie at approximately the same distance from each other. Let there be $n$ objects placed on a unit of the tape line; the values ​​$n$ are called concentration. In the case of the arrangement of objects along lines, it can be called as follows:

liquid linear density. It has a dimension of 1/m or, if you like, 1/cm, 1/km, 1/yard - depending on the choice of units of length. Let, further, the tape moves with speed $v$ mismo observed, which counts these objects. (However, the role of this observed can also be played by an electronic counter with photozem, similar to those used in automatic tickets at metro stations). The question arises: how many objects will be recorded observed per unit of time? This question is very easy to answer. During the time $t$ mismo the product is observed and has a dimension of 1/s (or 1/min, 1/year - depending on the choice of length units). We will denote the flow with the letter F (this is the capital Greek letter “Phi”). So,

$$\Phi = nv.$$

Now let’s imagine that our transport has a finite length and that objects located on its belt, one to the end, fall onto the belt of another transport moving at a different speed $v'$. Obviously, it is observed that standing near the second vehicle, in a unit of time it counts the same number of objects passing through the same number of objects as the first one observes. If there are fewer of them, then you can guarantee that some cinder-killers somewhere along the way will have similar objects. If it turns out that there is a significant addition, additional objects are placed on the second tape. Thus, the flow $\Phi = nv$ and $\Phi' = n'v'$, registered by both observers, must be equal to each other:

$$nv = n'v'.$$
