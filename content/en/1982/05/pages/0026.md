**M704. A parallelogram is described around a square (the vertices of the square lie on different sides of the parallelogram). Prove that the perpendluks dropped from the vertices of the parallelogram to the sides of the square form a new square (Fig. 1).**

**Answer:** $\left\{ \left(\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{2}, 1\right), \left(-\frac{1}{3}, -\frac{1}{2}, -1\right) \right\}$.

Solution. Since $\frac{x}{3(1+x^2)} = \frac{y}{4(1+y^2)} = \frac{z}{5(1+z^2)}$, the numbers $x, y$ and $z$ have the same symbols, and if $(x, y, z)$ is a solution to the system, then $(-x, -y, -z)$ is its solution, so only positive solutions can be sought.

The formula $\sin 2\varphi = \frac{2tg\varphi}{1+tg^2\varphi}$ is called for mental angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ from the interval $]0; \pi[$ as well as $\tg \frac{\alpha}{2} = x, \tg \frac{\beta}{2} = y, \tg \frac{\gamma}{2} = z$. Then (1) turns into equation $\frac{\sin \alpha}{3} = \frac{\sin \beta}{4} = \frac{\sin \gamma}{5}$.

From the second equation of the system we obtain $\frac{1}{z} = \frac{x+y}{1-xy}$ (clearly $xy \neq 1, z \neq 0$), which in the new notation has the form $\text{ctg} \frac{\gamma}{2} = \text{tg} \frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}$, or

$$\text{ctg} \left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\right) = \text{tg} \frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}.$$

The last equality under the chosen restrictions on $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ is possible if and only if $\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\gamma}{2} = \frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}$, that is, $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = \pi$.

So, we got that $\alpha, \beta$ and $\gamma$ are the angles of a triangle whose side lengths are in the ratio 3:4:5 (see (2)). This is a right triangle with $\gamma = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\sin \alpha = \frac{3}{5}$, $\sin \beta = \frac{4}{5}$ (see figure), that is, $\text{ctg} \frac{\alpha}{2} = \frac{1}{3}$, $\text{ctg} \frac{\beta}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$, $\text{ctg} \frac{\gamma}{2} = 1$.

Some readers have solved this system purely algebraically, but such solutions are much longer than the one given.
