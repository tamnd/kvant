Year of Miracles

Dirac equation
(1928—1931)

“Because I became a writer, it should not be thought that I never aspired to any worthy activity.” This joke by Bernard Shaw could well have been repeated by a young man from Bristol, who aspired to a very worthy career as an electrical engineer, and became the author of many of the “crazy ideas” of physics of the 20th century. His full name is Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac. Having just found a place in Bristol, he came to Cambridge in 1925, became interested in the still emerging field of physics - quantum mechanics - and played a large role in the creation of a new science.

The years 1925–1930, as Niels Bohr put it, marked the “golden age” of quantum mechanics. Dirac later said: “I am grateful to fate that I was born in a time: if I had been several years older or younger, I would not have had such brilliant opportunities. Everything seemed to be favorable to me...” And, above all, tempting talent, we will add.

At the age of 24, Dirac was awarded a doctorate, and at the age of 31, the Nobel Prize (together with E. Schrödinger) “for the discovery of a new fruitful form of atomic theory.”

The first part of this article was published in the previous issue of the magazine.

Already in 1927, the basic principles of quantum mechanics were developed and its mathematical apparatus was created. The new theory was able to explain the laws of nature operating on the atomic scale. However, it was not applicable to particles moving at speeds close to the speed of light.

And in January 1928, Paul Dirac derived an equation describing the behavior of fast electrons. It combined the principles of quantum mechanics and the theory of relative energy. This equation explained the interaction of an electron with a magnetic field, described the behavior of internal electrons, explained some features of spectral patterns, etc. Everything is good, but... the Dirac equation allowed solutions in which the electron could have negative kinetic energy. It seemed completely absurd.

There were debates about theoretical solutions for approximately three years until the author himself found a solution. We cannot give his reasoning here, but will report only the final conclusion.
