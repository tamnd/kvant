“Stand on the right, pass on the left...”

If you live in a big city where there is a subway - the most convenient and reliable form of urban transport, then you, of course, have heard this phrase many times, amplified by loudspeakers, loudly sounding under the arches of the tunnel. Of course, many of you, being late for some important task, did not stop stepping on the escalator, but, like knocking your heels on the steps, rushed down, maneuvering like a slalomist between the Stories on the right and the passages on the left. But quite often, your uncontrollable forward movement somewhere down would come across a dense mass of people, for some reason not wanting to pass either to the left or to the right. You may have even noticed that during rush hours, the always-moving lighthouse is filled to the top with people who, despite the real confessions of the escalator attendant, do not want to pass. Let's stop worrying about the passengers' ignorance and look at this situation with the objective gaze of a researcher. But first, here’s an example.

Let's consider a moving belt (conveyor), on which some objects lie at approximately the same distance from each other. Let there be $n$ objects placed on a unit of the tape line; the values ​​$n$ are called concentration. In the case of the arrangement of objects along lines, it can be called as follows:

liquid linear density. It has a dimension of 1/m or, if you like, 1/cm, 1/km, 1/yard - depending on the choice of units of length. Let, further, the tape moves with speed $v$ mismo observed, which counts these objects. (However, the role of this observed can also be played by an electronic counter with photozem, similar to those used in automatic tickets at metro stations). The question arises: how many objects will be recorded observed per unit of time? This question is very easy to answer. During the time $t$ mismo the product is observed and has a dimension of 1/s (or 1/min, 1/year - depending on the choice of length units). We will denote the flow with the letter F (this is the capital Greek letter “Phi”). So,

$$\Phi = nv.$$

Now let’s imagine that our transport has a finite length and that objects located on its belt, one to the end, fall onto the belt of another transport moving at a different speed $v'$. Obviously, it is observed that standing near the second vehicle, in a unit of time it counts the same number of objects passing through the same number of objects as the first one observes. If there are fewer of them, then you can guarantee that some cinder-killers somewhere along the way will have similar objects. If it turns out that there is a significant addition, additional objects are placed on the second tape. Thus, the flow $\Phi = nv$ and $\Phi' = n'v'$, registered by both observers, must be equal to each other:

$$nv = n'v'.$$

From here it is immediately clear that the concentration of $n'$ objects on the second

the conveyor will not be the same as on the first one, namely

$$n' = n \frac{v}{v'}$$

If the speed $v'$ is less than the speed $v$, then the concentration $n'$ will be greater than the concentration $n$. It may even be better that on the belt of the second transport, objects will not be able to fit without intervals, but will be piled on top of each other.

You, of course, understand that for the observed perfect it is indifferent whether objects are on a belt moving with a speed of $v$, or whether they independently move with a speed of $v$ along a stationary belt (floor, sidewalk). The only important thing is that their speed relative to the observed object recording the flow is equal to $v$. Consequently, the flow is a quantity that depends on the coordinate system in which it changes. Thus, if the observed person is himself on another belt, moving parallel to the first one with the same speed $v$, then the relative speed $v_{\text{отн}}$, and therefore the variable observed flow $\Phi = nv_{\text{отн}}$ will be equal to zero.

And now is the time to return to the question of what happens at the ecalator during rush hours. The flow of passengers standing on the right is equal to $\Phi_1 = n_1v_9$, where $n_1$ is the concentration of standing passengers, $v_9$ is the speed of the ecalator. The flow of passengers passing on the left is equal to $\Phi_2 = n_2(v_9 + v_n)$, where $n_2$ is the concentration of walking passengers, $v_n$ is the speed of pedestrians relative to the ecalator, and $v_9 + v_n$ is the speed of walking passengers relative to a stationary observable. Naturally, at the end of the descent, both groups of passengers begin to move at the same speed $v_n$. However, the right flow does not undergo any major changes, since $v_n$ is approximately equal to $v_9$. But the passengers who were on the left of the ecalator are now moving approximately twice as slow (relative to the stationary walls) as before, and, according to what has been said, the concentration of passengers in this group should approximately double.

For a variety of reasons, people do not like to walk closely together, so the left flow can move normally if people walking on the ecalator keep a distance twice as large between themselves as when walking on a platform, or if the flow below can expand to the sides. During peak hours, both of these options usually cannot be realized due to the large number of people. That's why people on the ecalator are wiser to build both on the right and on the left. Otherwise, there will definitely be a congestion below.

It seems to us that now you will not be angry about an unexpected delay at the ecalator, but will try to leave the house about five minutes earlier.

The concept of flow, of course, is not only relevant to the consideration of a case; it is very widely used in physics.

Let there be a beam of particles moving in space in the same direction with the same speed $v$. The number of particles per unit volume of the beam, as in the linear case, is also called concentration $n$ (or volume density) and has a dimension $1/\text{m}^3$. The product $\Phi = nv$ now has dimension $1/(m^2 \cdot c)$ and is equal to the number of particles passing every second through a unit sectional area perpendicular to the direction of motion of the beam. Therefore, the quantities $\Phi$ are called particle flux density; By multiplying its value by the cross-sectional area of ​​the beam, we obtain the total flow of particles in the beam.

If each moving part has some property and this property is “transferred” along with it, then it is advisable to introduce the concept of flux density of this property

$$\Phi_a = anv$$

where $a$ is the amount of character of a given property. Let, for example, $a = m$ be the mass of one particle in the flow, then $\Phi_m = mnv$ is the mass flow density (its dimension is $\kappa/(\text{m}^2 \cdot c)$). Or,

Let us now consider several specific problems that will help you better understand the concepts of flow or density.

1. Try to estimate the capacity of a highway, that is, the maximum number of automatic vehicles that can pass along a given road in an hour.

Such calculations are necessary when designing roads and for proper organization of traffic, especially in large cities and on major international highways.

Let the highway be two-way, and in each direction traffic occurs in two lanes with a maximum permissible speed of $v = 60$ km/h (these are, for example, the traffic conditions on the Moscow Automatic Ring Road). What interests us is the flow of automation, that is, the number of automations passing per unit of time mismo motionless is observed. Using the formulas obtained above, we will write $\Phi = Nnv$, where $N=4$ is the total number of traffic lanes, $n$ is the number of cars per unit length of the road, which can be associated with the distance $L$ between automatic machines: $n = 1/L$. Thus, $\Phi = Nv/L$. Manuals for operating automatic machines recommend keeping a distance between automatic machines equal in meters to half the speed expressed in kilometers per hour. In our case, at a speed of $v = 60$ km/h $L = 30$ m, and the highway capacity is

$$\Phi = N \frac{v}{L} = 8000 \text{ 1/ч.}$$

2. People stand on a downward escalator at a distance of $L = 1$ m from each other. Each flow of passengers will be prescribed:
a) the escalator duty officer standing at the bottom; b) watching while standing on another escalator moving up? The speed of movement of the escalators is taken equal to $v = 2$ m/s.

We have already said that the flow depends on the reporting system, in this case, on the observer. Relative to the person on duty on the escalator, passengers descend at a speed equal to the speed of the escalator, therefore

$$\Phi_1 = nv = \frac{v}{L} = 2 \text{ } 1/\text{c}.$$

From the point of view of an observer located on an escalator moving up with speed $v$, the flow of passengers will be twice as large, since relative to it the flow speed is $v_{\text{отн}} = 2v$, and the flow

$$\Phi_2 = nv_{\text{отн}} = 2nv = 4 \text{ } 1/\text{c}.$$

3. A truck with a carrying capacity of $M = 2$ t is loaded with arbuzami using transport. The average weight of an arbuz is $m = 5$ kg, the speed of transport is $v = 0,5$ m/s, the distance between the arbuz on the belt is $L = 0,75$ m. Find the loading time.

In this case we are dealing with mass flow. By definition it is equal to $\Phi_m = mnv = mv/L$. Then the required loading time

$$t = \frac{M}{\Phi_m} = \frac{ML}{mv} = 600 \quad c = 10 \text{ мин.}$$
