Pebbles
and chessboard
(memories
about summer holidays)

On a hot July day, Kolya and I went to the beach. Having swum to our heart's content, we lay down on the sand to sunbathe. Next to us, two men were playing chess. Naturally, we immediately began to discuss the position on the board and give hints: I - to one, and Kolya - to the other. But the players were unperturbed and did not react to our hints at all. Desperate to turn this game into a masterpiece of chess art, we began to think about what we should do.

“Shouldn’t we throw some pebbles?” - Kolya asked me. “What is this - toss and catch?” "Not really! - he answered, - Much more interesting! First, let's play this game.

There is a pile of stones. We take turns taking one, two or three stones. The one who takes the last stone wins.”

I decided to play so that I always had an even number of stones, and I lost. In the second game, I began to take stones so that I always had an odd number of stones - and again I lost. I really didn’t want to lose for the third time, and I decided to try to understand the meaning of the game.

And for the first time it turned out that before my last move I had 4 stones. If I took only one stone, Kolya took the other three. If I took two or three stones, he took the remaining two or the only last stone.

How can I avoid ending up in front of four stones again? It is impossible to leave him 5, 6 or 7 stones: having taken 1, 2 or 3 stones, he will again leave me 4 stones. What if I left him 8 stones? Then he will be forced to leave me 5, 6 or 7 stones, and I forced him to make a move in a situation where he has 4 stones. And then it dawned on me! “Of course, it’s possible,” said Kolya, “but I had something completely different in mind.”

He put two piles of stones, 7 stones in each. “We will take stones in turn: either one stone from the first pile, or one stone from the second pile, or two stones - one from each pile; Or you can not take stones, but move one stone from one pile to another. The one who takes the last stone jumps out.”

“I don’t want to play with your stupid stones,” I said indignantly, “let’s play Gelfand’s game!” Are you being bonkers?” “And this is Gelfand’s game,” Kolya replied. “Look!” And he drew on the sand near the square I had drawn the numbers from 0 to 7, first along one side, and then along the other side, as on figure 3.

“See what happens. Let the chip stand on the field, say, (5;6), that is, at the intersection of the vertical, placed with the number 5, and the horizontal, placed with the number 6 (see Fig. 3). Let's put two piles of stones aside: the first has 5 stones, and the second has 6. In which cells do I have the right to move the chip? In cells (6; 5), (5; 5), (4; 5), (4; 6) and (4; 7). How can we get piles corresponding to the new positions of the chip from our two piles containing 5 and 6 stones? Very simple! To get position (6; 5), you need to move one stone from the second pile to the first; position (5; 5) is obtained by taking one stone from the second pile; (4; 5) - if you take a stone from both piles; (4; 6) - if you take one stone from the first pile; finally, position (4; 7) is obtained if one stone is moved from the first pile to the second. Since the useful field is numbered (7; 7), at first there should be 7 stones in each pile. Of course, the useful things are numbered (0; 0) - not a single stone remains in the piles.”

“I like playing on the chessboard much better,” I said. - Look: cell (0; 0) is a loser in the sense that if the move is mine, and the chip is already in this cell, then I lost.
