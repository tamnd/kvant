Brighter than a hundred billion suns

Already in ancient times, astronomers noticed that from time to time new super-bright stars flare up. Such an outbreak was, for example, noted by Chinese astronomers in 1054 in the Crab Nebula, which is part of our Galaxy. Now “supernova explosions” are well studied and discovered not only in our Galaxy, but also in other star clusters. Over the course of a few months, a supernova emits as much light as an entire galaxy containing tens or hundreds of billions of suns. Based on the intensity and duration of the radiation, it could be established that the total energy released during a supernova explosion is $10^{43} \div 10^{45}$ J. Meanwhile, the thermal energy of the star is a thousand times less. The energy that could be released during chemical transformations is also significantly less. Where does the enormous supernova energy come from? This question remained unanswered for a long time. Hopes of declaring supernovae arose only after the discovery of nuclear reactions that release energy millions of times greater than chemical transformations.

So, the source of supernova energy could be nuclear reactions occurring inside the star. There is, however, an even more powerful source - the gravitational energy of the star. However, this energy can only be released through nuclear transformations. If during nuclear reactions the density of the central part of the star increases, then under the influence of gravitational forces the matter in the vicinity of the region begins to fall towards the center, acquiring energy. In other words, the potential energy of gravity is converted into the energy of stellar matter.

The density of a star is determined by the balance between the force of gravity and the pressure force of the star's matter. In order for a star to shrink, the pressure must decrease. A very strong decrease in pressure could occur during the formation of neutron matter, when protons and electrons turn into neutrons. Let's try to figure this out.

Pressure is proportional to the kinetic energy of the particles that make up a substance. As the temperature decreases, the kinetic energy of the particles decreases, and therefore the pressure drops. However, even at absolute zero temperature, the kinetic energy of particles is not zero. The fact is that neutrons, protons and electrons have a substitutive property - two identical particles cannot be in the same state (“Pauli exclusion”). For this reason, even at absolute zero temperature, particles do not rest and have different velocities - as they say, they are scattered in velocities. In this case, light particles have the most kinetic energy. Thus, the main contribution to the pressure in a star comes from electrons, whose mass is approximately two thousand times less than the mass of a proton or neutron. It is not surprising that the force of gravity pushes the neutron matter down to a higher density than the surrounding matter consisting of atomic nuclei and electrons - after all, in this case, light particles are replaced by heavy ones and the pressure drops sharply.

Posmot, if as a result of nuclear transformations a star could turn into a neutron star, this would lead to a sharp contraction of the star, and in a short time enormous energy would be released, sufficient to comprehend the supernatural. With such an external roll of the star, powerful elastic waves should arise, coming from the center. Under their influence, the outer part of the star is triggered and turns into hot gas, which expands at enormous speed. The glow of this gas would explain the long-lasting supernova explosions.

So, supernova explosions ceased to seem like a mysterious phenomenon - hopes arose to explain them as a consequence of the collapse of a star during nuclear transformations.

How is pure condensation related to the fate of neutron stars that interests us?

Pion condensation in neutron liquid

When the density at the center of a neutron star reaches a critical value $Q_k$, corresponding to pion condensation, a dramatic turn in the fate of the star must occur. First, an embryo of a new superdense state of neutron matter appears in the center of the star. This state turns out to be unstable—as the radius of the embryo increases, gravitational energy is released. In an equilibrium state, a significant part of the star should become superdense. Therefore, the superdense embryo begins to grow - the substance of the outer parts of the star rushes to the boundary of the embryo at high speed. By the time the radius of the superdense core reaches a value corresponding to the equilibrium state, the substance of the disturbed regions continues to move by inertia, and the radius of the core slips past its equilibrium value. Since the balance is disturbed, the reverse movement begins. Thus, the radius of a superdense embryo first increases sharply and then fluctuates around a value comparable to the radius of a neutron star. The process of forming a superdense star takes thousands of fractions of a second. In this case, the energy of the transitions is several times greater than that which is released during the revolution of a neutron star. It can be expected that under the influence of elastic waves generated by the oscillation of the radius of the superdense core, the disrupted part of the star is ejected in a highly heated state, and the explosion pattern resembles a supernova explosion.

Thus, in addition to flares caused by nuclear reactions and the previous formation of a neutron star, the emergence of a non-neutron star, flares of a different nature, resulting from pion condensation and the subsequent explosion of a neutron star.

What are the consequences of a neutron star explosion?

Black holes

If the conclusion of a neutron star explosion caused by pion condensation is convincingly proven theoretically or confirmed by observations, this will mean that neutron stars cannot have a density exceeding the critical value $Q_k$ (as calculated, $Q_k$ is of the same order as the nuclear density). Meanwhile, it is of fundamental importance whether stars exist with a density significantly exceeding the nuclear density.

According to the general relative theory, when the mass of a star exceeds 2-3 masses of the Sun, granitional instability will arise - the star begins to skim, and, after the radius becomes less than a certain critical value (gritional radius), no repulsive forces will be able to keep the material from falling to the center - the skimming pressure of the force of gravity exceeds the tensile pressure of the particle of matter. This phenomenon is called star collapse. It ends with the formation of a new object - a black hole. A black hole manifests itself practically only as a source of a boundary field. A body falling into the field of a black hole falls towards the center of the hole and ceases to be visible. Another phenomenon that arouses interest in superdense matter is that at a sufficiently high density, neutron matter can transform into a new state - quark matter.

Another transition

In recent years, physicists have come to the conclusion that all strongly interacting particle elements - such particles are called arodns - consist of several types of quarks - particles with a fractional electric charge equal to -1/3 or +2/3 of the charge of an electron. The neutron and proton (and they are arodic) consist of three quarks, and the pi-meson consists of a quark and an antiquark. Quarks apparently do not exist as free particles. Until now, all attempts to detect an individual quark have given negative results. But at small distances between them, their properties are so well studied that now most physicists have no doubt about the realization of these particles. From the analysis of experiments on the scattering of arodns on each other, it was possible to establish that when quarks merge, the interaction between them decreases. This phenomenon was called asymptotic free.

Particles are known that exist for a long time in a bound state, but do not live long in freedom. The simplest example is a neutron, which must act indefinitely, but in a free state decays in ten minutes. The reason for the “non-flight” of friend quarks. When the distance between the quarks becomes greater than the radius of the arodna, the attraction between them increases sharply, so that they cannot move away from each other at a large distance, which they received in a collision.

When two energetic arodons collide, the quarks they contain do not fly out, but turn into other nucleons or pi mesons.

For clarity, one can imagine that arodon is something like a bag in which quarks move freely, but beyond which they cannot move. If you bring two nucleons closer to a distance less than the size of the bag, you will get one common bag, in which there will already be six quarks. At a higher density of neutron matter, when the distances between neutrons are comparable to the radius of the bag, the neutrons decay into their component parts - neutron matter turns into quark matter. Calculations show that a star becomes quark when its density is 10-20 times higher than nuclear density. In this case, energy transfers, and another explosion of the star may occur.
