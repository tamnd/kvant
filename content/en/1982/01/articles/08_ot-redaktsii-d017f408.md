M685. Two subsets of a set of natural numbers are called congruent if one can be obtained from the others by combining them with an integer. (For example, the sets of even and odd numbers are congruent.) Is it possible to partition the set of natural numbers into an infinite number of (disjoint) infinite congruent subsets?

Answer: you can. Let's assume that the problem has already been solved. Let $A$ be that of the partition set that contains one. The remaining partition sets are obtained from $A$ with divisions into certain natural numbers, the set of which, complemented by zero, we denote by $B$. For each $b \in B$, let the set $A_b$ be the result of dividing the set $A$ by $b$, that is, the set of all numbers of the form $a+b$, where $a \in A$ (in particular, $A_0=A$). By condition, if $b_1 \neq b_2$, then $A_{b_1} \cap A_{b_2} = \emptyset$, and every natural number $n$ belongs to one of the set $A_b$, that is

Each natural number is uniquely represented as a sum $n=a+b$, (*) and $a \in A, b \in B$.

If, on the contrary, the data for the sets $A$ and $B$, having properties (*) and such that $0 \in B$, $1 \in A$, then the sets $A_b$, where $b \in B$, form the required partition.

We will construct the sets $A$ and $B$ in two ways.

First way. Let the sets $A$ and $B$ having properties (*) be constructed. Let us assign in correspondence to each natural number $n=a+b$ ($a \in A, b \in B$) a point in the plane $Oxy$ with coordinates $(a; b)$.

Let $M$ be the set of all obtained points of the plane. The set $M$ obviously has the following properties:

a) if $A$ is the projection of the set $M$ onto the axis $Ox$, and $B$ is the projection of $M$ onto the axis $Oy$, then the set $M$ coincides with the entire set of pairs $(a; b)$, where $a \in A, b \in B$;

b) the intersection of the set $M$ with each line $x+y=n$ ($n$ is a natural number) consists of a single point; in particular, at $n=1$ this is the point $(1; 0)$.

It is clear that by constructing at least one set $M$ possessing properties a) and b), we obtain the required partition of the set of natural numbers (consisting of the set $A_b$).

*) Exactly the same reasoning with the words “more” replaced by “less” was given in the solutions problem M262 («Kvant», 1974, No. 5) about the placement of rooks that cover the board twice.

It is easy to see that the union of the set $M_0, M_1, \dots, M_n, \dots$ (over all natural numbers $n$) has properties a), b).

Second way. As is known, every natural number $n$ can be represented in the form

$$n = a_0 \cdot 2^k + a_1 \cdot 2^{k-1} + \dots + a_{k-1} \cdot 2 + a_k,$$

where $a_i$ is equal to 0 or 1, and such a representation is unique. The double notation $n$ is based on this:

$$n_2 = a_0 a_1 \dots a_{k-1} a_k.$$

For example, $2_2 = 10, 7_2 = 111, 17_2 = 10001$, etc.

Let us now consider two sets of natural numbers: the set $A$, consisting of numbers in whose double notation the 1 is in odd x (counting from the right) digits: $A = (1, 100, 101\dots)$, and the set $B$, consisting of 0 and numbers in whose double notation the 1 is in the even x digits:

$$B = (0, 10, 1000, 1010,\dots).$$

Obviously, any natural number $n$ can be uniquely represented as a sum $n = a + b$, where $a \in A, b \in B$.

The sets $A$ and $B$ have properties (*), and therefore the sets $A_b$ ($b \in B$) give the required resolution.

F694. Along a long straight trough, inclined at an angle $\alpha$ to the horizon, $N$ identical balls move without friction. What is the maximum number of collisions that can occur in the system at arbitrary initial positions and speeds of the balls? The collisions of the balls are considered to be absolutely elastic.

Let us consider the motion of the balls in a reference frame that moves along the trough with acceleration $g \sin \alpha$. In this system, the balls move uniformly in the time intervals between events; during collisions, the balls simply exchange their velocities (absolutely elastic impacts).

Let's draw graphs $x(t)$ of the coordinates of all $N$ balls versus time (in the reference system we have chosen). From the above it is clear that the overall picture of these graphs is a “grid” of $N$ lines; the points of intersection of these lines (grid nodes) correspond to hundreds of balls (pairs of balls) with each other. Thus, the total number of collisions is equal to the number of intersections of $N$ lines. It is not difficult to show that the maximum number of intersections is

F695. In the simplest model, the star is considered as a gas ball in equilibrium in its own gravity field. Assuming that the gas consists of completely zincified hydrogen and green atoms, estimate the temperature of the star. Star mass $M$, radius $r$; the relative content of hydrogen in the gas is $n$.

The gas ball consists of a huge number and electrons; Under the influence of gravitational forces between individual gas particles, the ball should be compressed. Equilibrium of a ball in its own gravitational field is possible provided that the gravitational force balances the gas pressure force.

Near the center of the ball, the zinc pressure value $p$ should be equal to the numerical value of the weight of a gas column with a cross section $\Delta s = 1 (\text{cm}^2)$ and a height equal to the radius of the ball $r$ (cm). The mass of such a column is $m = \varrho r \cdot \Delta s$ (kg), where $\varrho (\text{кг/cm}^3)$ is the gas density, which for an approximate estimate we will consider constant. The weight of the column is equal to the force with which it attracts to the center of the ball:

$$F = G \frac{m M}{(r/2)^2}.$$

This formula is not entirely accurate, since different parts of the column are attracted to the center, generally speaking, in different ways. But for our approximate estimates, we can assume that the attractive force $F$ is the same as if all the mass of the column was concentrated at its center (at a distance $r/2$ from the center of the ball).

⟦folio 33⟧

Thus,

$$p = \frac{F}{\Delta s} = 4G \frac{\rho M}{r}. \tag{1}$$

On the other hand, from the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation

$$p = \frac{M}{V \mu} RT = \frac{\rho}{\mu} RT, \tag{2}$$

where $\mu$ is the molar mass of the stellar gas. Equating (1) and (2), we find the temperature of the star $T$:

$$T = 4G \frac{\mu M}{r R}.$$

Let's find the molar mass $\mu$. Pressure $p$ is the sum of the partial pressures of hydrogen and helium, that is

$$\frac{M}{V \mu} RT = \frac{Mn}{V \mu_H} RT + \frac{M(1-n)}{V \mu_{He}} RT,$$

where

$$\mu = \frac{\mu_H \mu_{He}}{n(\mu_{He} - \mu_H + \mu_H)} = \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-3}}{3n+1} \text{ кг/моль.}$$

In this calculation of $\mu$ we did not teach the pressure of the electron gas. However, taking this pressure into account will not change the order of magnitude of $\mu$, and for our estimation calculations we can use the found approximate value $\mu$.

Thus,

$$T = 4G \frac{2M}{rR(3n+1)} \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ K.} \tag{3}$$

When obtaining formula (3), we made some simplifications of the assumption. For a more accurate calculation, it is necessary to know the density distribution of the inner ball, and this requires a significant departure from the scope of the school mathematics course. In addition, the estimation formula (3) assumes that the temperature in the central region of the star does not change too sharply with distance from the center to the periphery, and it makes sense to talk about some average temperature. In fact, in regions far from the center, the temperature is significantly lower than in the central part of the star.
