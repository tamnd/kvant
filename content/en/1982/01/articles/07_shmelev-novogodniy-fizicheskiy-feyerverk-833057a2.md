Physics is an experimental science. Every physical study begins with the observation and study of some natural phenomenon and ends with a comparison of theories with experimental results.

Many laws and natural phenomena are not so complex at their core, and their characteristic features can be traced even in relatively simple experiments. Galileo discovered the laws of falling bodies by watching them fall from the top of the Leaning Tower of Pisa. It is established that sunlight is a complex mixture of different colors using a simple glass sign. Faraday’s many experiments were amazingly simple, but not very comprehensive in content and conclusion.

Of course, today there is very little hope that with the means at hand one can discover a new law of nature. Physics these days requires very experimental installations - charged particle accelerators, atomic reactors, electron ion microsolars, etc. But much of what science already knows can be tested in simple home experiments. The movement of magnets, crystal growth, diffraction and interference of waves, surface tension or dry friction - all this can be studied using simple, improvised means. Wooden cubes or balls, a piece of paper, a sewing needle, a soap bubble or a stream of water from a faucet, not to mention a compass and a flashlight, can all be used for varied and sensory physics experiments. Such experiments are the first step to independent research. They help the baby to penetrate into the essence of various physical phenomena, develop observation and communication.

Laboratory «Kvant» belongs to the systematic sections of our journal. In it we strive to give our readers the opportunity to perform simple but powerful experiments that do not require special laboratory equipment. From letters from readers, we see that many of them willingly carry out the experiments we propose and even strive to creatively demonstrate them (to vary the conditions - change the temperature, direction or other physical quantities involved in the experiment, select other substances and materials, etc.). At the same time, they have many unexpected and interesting questions, from which they turn to «Kvant».
When reproducing the experiments we describe, the necessary technical education and strict adherence to the recommendations in the articles of precautionary measures. We hope that Laboratory «Kvant» will help our readers better understand their abilities and develop our nature explorer.

A. Shmelev

New Year's physical fireworks

Several times already, Laboratory «Kvant» has published problem-questions from J. Walker’s book “Physical Fireworks” (see «Kvant», 1980, No. 11 and 12). The answers to many of them involve experiments or observations.

However, this publication differs from previous ones. Firstly, in order for the fireworks to turn out New Year's, we specially selected those experiments that can be easily tested in winter. Secondly, Walker's text is significantly supplemented: interesting stories related to the main topic of the problem are given, and additional experiments are described. finally, thirdly, it was decided not to publish the answers immediately, but to wait for letters from readers.

When we walk through the snow

Many people have probably seen the film “Alexander Nevsky”. But perhaps not everyone knows that the main episode of the film - Battle of the Ice - was filmed... in the summer. The heat reached thirty degrees, and it was necessary to show the snow and ice on Lake Peipsi. However, a solution was found: a large area near Mosfilm was covered with a mixture of naphthalene and salt. When the actors walked through such “snow”, he

Ice cream mystery

When ice cream first appeared, the method of making it was kept as a closely guarded secret. In vain did many European courts try to use snow or ice to freeze a mixture of cream, sugar and fruit juices. The mixture cooled, but did not freeze. I had to resort to a means that we now call industrial spinonage. What did they find out?

“When my grandmother makes ice cream at home,” Walker writes, “she lines the ice cream jug with ice and sprinkles salt on the ice. Why is she pouring salt?

So, it's all about regular salt. Do a number of simple wholesales before responding to Walker. Take snow or crushed ice and gradually add salt to it, stirring the mixture. (It’s nice, but not necessary, for the experience to be accompanied by making ice cream.)

It turns out that the temperature of such a mixture can be lowered to -20°C.

Most often, table salt NaCl is used for cooling. But rarer substances are also suitable: KCl, NaNO₃, etc. The “champion” for lowering the temperature turns out to be CaCl₂. By mixing 42 g of this salt with 100 g of melted ice, you can get a temperature of -55°C!

Which water will freeze faster?

“In cold countries, such as Canada or Iceland, it is well known that hot water, when exposed outside in the cold, freezes more quickly than cold water. This may seem like nonsense to you, but this is by no means an old wives' tale: even Francis Bacon at one time dismissed this fact.

Fill several vessels of various forms with warm and cold water and place them outside the window on a frosty day... And don’t be surprised if in any of the vessels the warm water freezes before the cold, but try to explain why?” (J. Walker).

It is difficult to add anything to this, except, perhaps, some advice: take bottles with narrow necks and wide jars as vessels of various shapes. And one more thing: think about how the temperature of the liquid will change if you lower the pressure above its surface and, thereby, allow the liquid to evaporate more intensely. (You can also check the corresponding experiment, using a regular vacuum cleaner to lower the pressure.)

On kaka
To reduce friction, lubricants are used in technology. This role can be performed by various materials, sometimes the most seemingly inferior ones. As an example, let’s talk about a case where the role of lubrication was played by...
steel.
At the end of the century, the English industrialist Harvey sent to Russia samples of new armor plates to protect ships. During testing, the heavy gun shells, instead of piercing the plates, themselves broke against the armor, without causing harm to what could be hidden behind it. But the Russians asked to repeat the tests. And the shells began to break the armor plates (and later, after some improvements, and punch holes in them).

The whole point turned out to be that the shells were now equipped with special caps made of mild steel. The cap flattened, melted and, on the one hand, prevented the projectile from splitting, and on the other hand, it served as a kind of lubricant as it passed through the armor plate. The inventor of the cap was a talented Russian scientist and sailor, Admiral Makarov.

So, under enormous pressures, steel can serve as a lubricant. And “why do skates slide on the ice?” (J. Walker). What is the lubricant in this case?

"Through the wavy mists..."
During wet winter, days are often foggy. It is commonly believed that fog is gray in color, but this is not always the case.

“The French artist Monet came to London and painted Westminster Abbey. Monet worked on an ordinary London foggy day. In Monet's painting, the preparatory lines of the abbey barely emerge from the fog. The picture was painted masterfully.

When the picture was performed, it caused confusion among Londoners. They were amazed that Monet's fog was colored crimson, while everyone knows that the color of fog is gray.

Monet's audacity initially caused outrage. But those who were indignant, going out onto the London streets, peered into the fog and for the first time noticed that it was really purple,” wrote K. Paustovsky in his book “The Golden Rose.”

Walker also agrees with Monet: “Why does the haze hovering over the city have a brown tint?” - he asks.

The question of the cause of colored fog is not an easy question. We shouldn't have resorted to it at the very end. Observe the color of the fog in the city and countryside. Or maybe you can even come up with experiments and get “mists” of different colors?
