Problem solutions

M681 — M685; F692 — F697

Let us immediately present the solution to problem 6), following W. Sierpinski (a large number of this type is contained in his book “Elementary Number Theory”): let’s construct an infinite sequence $(a_n)$ of natural numbers such that for any $n$ the sum of the squares of the first $n$ of its terms is the square of an odd number. Let's take $a_1=3, a_2=4$; if the first $n$ members have already been selected and

$$a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \ldots + a_n^2 = (2k+1)^2,$$

let's put $a_{n+1}=2k^2+2k$. From point

$$(2k+1)^2 + (2k^2+2k)^2 = (2k^2+2k+1)^2$$

we get that

$$a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \ldots + a_n^2 + a_{n+1}^2 = (2k^2+2k+1)^2$$

- again the square of an odd number.

The first five terms of this sequence are 3, 4, 12, 84, 3612:

$$3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2, 3^2 + 4^2 + 12^2 = 13^2,$$

$$3^2 + 4^2 + 12^2 + 84^2 = 85^2,$$

$$3^2 + 4^2 + 12^2 + 84^2 + 3612^2 = 3613^2.$$

The solution of the problem also implies a fact indicated in a letter from Yu. Makarov from Leningrad: for any $n$ there are $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ natural numbers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$ such that

$$a_1^2 = a_2^2 + a_3^2 = a_4^2 + a_5^2 + a_6^2 = \ldots = a_{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}^2 + 1 + \ldots + a_{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}^2$$

We, like the author of the letter, do not know whether all the numbers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$ can be different.

N. Vasiliev

M682. Inside the triangle $\Delta$ you need to place the triangle $\Delta_1$ so that each of the three squares built on the sides of the triangle $\Delta_1$ has two vertices lying on different sides of the triangle $\Delta$ (Fig. 1).

a) Prove that the medians of triangle $\Delta$ are perpendicular to the sides of triangle $\Delta_1$.

b) Is the construction possible for anyone?

a) It is easy to see that from three head triangles (see Fig. 1) with the help of three parallel translations that transfer the vertices of triangle $\Delta_1$ to some point $O$, it is possible to construct a triangle $\Delta$, similar to the original triangle $\Delta$ (Fig. 2). The segments connecting the vertices of triangle $\Delta$ with point $O$ divide triangle $\Delta$ into three equal triangles, the area of ​​each of which is equal to the area of ​​triangle $\Delta_1$. Therefore, $O$ is the intersection point of the median of triangle $\Delta$ (the distance from $O$ to any of the sides of triangle $\Delta_1$).
