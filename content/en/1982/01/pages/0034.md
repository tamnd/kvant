F694. Along a long straight trough, inclined at an angle $\alpha$ to the horizon, $N$ identical balls move without friction. What is the maximum number of collisions that can occur in the system at arbitrary initial positions and speeds of the balls? The collisions of the balls are considered to be absolutely elastic.

Let us consider the motion of the balls in a reference frame that moves along the trough with acceleration $g \sin \alpha$. In this system, the balls move uniformly in the time intervals between events; during collisions, the balls simply exchange their velocities (absolutely elastic impacts).

Let's draw graphs $x(t)$ of the coordinates of all $N$ balls versus time (in the reference system we have chosen). From the above it is clear that the overall picture of these graphs is a “grid” of $N$ lines; the points of intersection of these lines (grid nodes) correspond to hundreds of balls (pairs of balls) with each other. Thus, the total number of collisions is equal to the number of intersections of $N$ lines. It is not difficult to show that the maximum number of intersections is

$$n_{\max} = N(N-1)/2.$$

E. Surkov

F695. In the simplest model, the star is considered as a gas ball in equilibrium in its own gravity field. Assuming that the gas consists of completely zincified hydrogen and green atoms, estimate the temperature of the star. Star mass $M$, radius $r$; the relative content of hydrogen in the gas is $n$.

The gas ball consists of a huge number and electrons; Under the influence of gravitational forces between individual gas particles, the ball should be compressed. Equilibrium of a ball in its own gravitational field is possible provided that the gravitational force balances the gas pressure force.

Near the center of the ball, the zinc pressure value $p$ should be equal to the numerical value of the weight of a gas column with a cross section $\Delta s = 1 (\text{cm}^2)$ and a height equal to the radius of the ball $r$ (cm). The mass of such a column is $m = \varrho r \cdot \Delta s$ (kg), where $\varrho (\text{кг/cm}^3)$ is the gas density, which for an approximate estimate we will consider constant. The weight of the column is equal to the force with which it attracts to the center of the ball:

$$F = G \frac{m M}{(r/2)^2}.$$

This formula is not entirely accurate, since different parts of the column are attracted to the center, generally speaking, in different ways. But for our approximate estimates, we can assume that the attractive force $F$ is the same as if all the mass of the column was concentrated at its center (at a distance $r/2$ from the center of the ball).
