On kaka
To reduce friction, lubricants are used in technology. This role can be performed by various materials, sometimes the most seemingly inferior ones. As an example, let’s talk about a case where the role of lubrication was played by...
steel.
At the end of the century, the English industrialist Harvey sent to Russia samples of new armor plates to protect ships. During testing, the heavy gun shells, instead of piercing the plates, themselves broke against the armor, without causing harm to what could be hidden behind it. But the Russians asked to repeat the tests. And the shells began to break the armor plates (and later, after some improvements, and punch holes in them).

The whole point turned out to be that the shells were now equipped with special caps made of mild steel. The cap flattened, melted and, on the one hand, prevented the projectile from splitting, and on the other hand, it served as a kind of lubricant as it passed through the armor plate. The inventor of the cap was a talented Russian scientist and sailor, Admiral Makarov.

So, under enormous pressures, steel can serve as a lubricant. And “why do skates slide on the ice?” (J. Walker). What is the lubricant in this case?

"Through the wavy mists..."
During wet winter, days are often foggy. It is commonly believed that fog is gray in color, but this is not always the case.

“The French artist Monet came to London and painted Westminster Abbey. Monet worked on an ordinary London foggy day. In Monet's painting, the preparatory lines of the abbey barely emerge from the fog. The picture was painted masterfully.

When the picture was performed, it caused confusion among Londoners. They were amazed that Monet's fog was colored crimson, while everyone knows that the color of fog is gray.

Monet's audacity initially caused outrage. But those who were indignant, going out onto the London streets, peered into the fog and for the first time noticed that it was really purple,” wrote K. Paustovsky in his book “The Golden Rose.”

Walker also agrees with Monet: “Why does the haze hovering over the city have a brown tint?” - he asks.

The question of the cause of colored fog is not an easy question. We shouldn't have resorted to it at the very end. Observe the color of the fog in the city and countryside. Or maybe you can even come up with experiments and get “mists” of different colors?
