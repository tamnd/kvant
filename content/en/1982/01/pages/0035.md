Thus,

$$p = \frac{F}{\Delta s} = 4G \frac{\rho M}{r}. \tag{1}$$

On the other hand, from the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation

$$p = \frac{M}{V \mu} RT = \frac{\rho}{\mu} RT, \tag{2}$$

where $\mu$ is the molar mass of the stellar gas. Equating (1) and (2), we find the temperature of the star $T$:

$$T = 4G \frac{\mu M}{r R}.$$

Let's find the molar mass $\mu$. Pressure $p$ is the sum of the partial pressures of hydrogen and helium, that is

$$\frac{M}{V \mu} RT = \frac{Mn}{V \mu_H} RT + \frac{M(1-n)}{V \mu_{He}} RT,$$

where

$$\mu = \frac{\mu_H \mu_{He}}{n(\mu_{He} - \mu_H + \mu_H)} = \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-3}}{3n+1} \text{ кг/моль.}$$

In this calculation of $\mu$ we did not teach the pressure of the electron gas. However, taking this pressure into account will not change the order of magnitude of $\mu$, and for our estimation calculations we can use the found approximate value $\mu$.

Thus,

$$T = 4G \frac{2M}{rR(3n+1)} \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ K.} \tag{3}$$

When obtaining formula (3), we made some simplifications of the assumption. For a more accurate calculation, it is necessary to know the density distribution of the inner ball, and this requires a significant departure from the scope of the school mathematics course. In addition, the estimation formula (3) assumes that the temperature in the central region of the star does not change too sharply with distance from the center to the periphery, and it makes sense to talk about some average temperature. In fact, in regions far from the center, the temperature is significantly lower than in the central part of the star.

F696. A capacitive voltmeter is a flat air capacitor, one of the plates of which is fixed stationary, and the second can move translationally in a direction perpendicular to the plane of the plates. A stiffening spring $k$ (Fig. 1) is attached to the movable plate. A measure of the applied voltage is the change in the gap between the plates. What is the maximum voltage that can be changed with such a device? The area of ​​the plates is $S$, the gap between the plates at zero voltage is $d$.

Let us denote by $x(U)$ the displacement of the plate relative to the initial position ($x_0 = 0$ at $U_0 = 0$) at a given voltage $U$. Let us find the force $F$ of attraction between the plates of a capacitor charged to attraction $U$. This force is obviously independent of whether the capacitor is connected to a voltage source or not.

If a capacitor is charged to an attraction of $U$ and is from a source, then its charge is $Q = UC$ and its energy is $W = Q^2/2C$. When the gap between the plates changes by a small amount $\Delta x \ll d$, the energy of the capacitor changes by values

$$\Delta W = \frac{1}{2} Q^2 \left( \frac{1}{C'} - \frac{1}{C} \right) = \frac{1}{2} Q^2 \left( \frac{d - \Delta x}{\varepsilon_0 S} - \frac{d}{\varepsilon_0 S} \right) = -\frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 S} \cdot \Delta x,$$

where $C'$ is the capacity of the same capacitor with a gap between the plates $d - \Delta x$. This change in energy is equal in absolute value to the work of force $F$ on the path $\Delta x$, that is

$$F \cdot \Delta x = \frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 S} \cdot \Delta x,$$

where

$$F = \frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 S} = \frac{U^2 C^2}{2\varepsilon_0 S} = \frac{U^2 \varepsilon_0 S}{2d^2}.$$

So, for a given voltage $U$, the force $F$ is inversely proportional to the source of the distance between the plates.
