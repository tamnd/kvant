I immediately drew a square in the sand, drew a line dividing it into 64 squares, and placed a pebble on the corner field.

“Of course, it’s possible,” said Kolya, “but I had something completely different in mind.”

He put two piles of stones, 7 stones in each. “We will take stones in turn: either one stone from the first pile, or one stone from the second pile, or two stones - one from each pile; Or you can not take stones, but move one stone from one pile to another. The one who takes the last stone jumps out.”

“I don’t want to play with your stupid stones,” I said indignantly, “let’s play Gelfand’s game!” Are you being bonkers?” “And this is Gelfand’s game,” Kolya replied. “Look!” And he drew on the sand near the square I had drawn the numbers from 0 to 7, first along one side, and then along the other side, as on figure 3.

“See what happens. Let the chip stand on the field, say, (5;6), that is, at the intersection of the vertical, placed with the number 5, and the horizontal, placed with the number 6 (see Fig. 3). Let's put two piles of stones aside: the first has 5 stones, and the second has 6. In which cells do I have the right to move the chip? In cells (6; 5), (5; 5), (4; 5), (4; 6) and (4; 7). How can we get piles corresponding to the new positions of the chip from our two piles containing 5 and 6 stones? Very simple! To get position (6; 5), you need to move one stone from the second pile to the first; position (5; 5) is obtained by taking one stone from the second pile; (4; 5) - if you take a stone from both piles; (4; 6) - if you take one stone from the first pile; finally, position (4; 7) is obtained if one stone is moved from the first pile to the second. Since the useful field is numbered (7; 7), at first there should be 7 stones in each pile. Of course, the useful things are numbered (0; 0) - not a single stone remains in the piles.”
