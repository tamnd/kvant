And the key words here are “with a pendulum.” Just a mechanical watch could have good accuracy, but it improved significantly after the first, still primitive anchor (aka escapement) mechanism was invented in Europe in the 13th century, which more reliably ensured the constancy of the average speed. Then nineteen-year-old Galileo Galilei in 1583, while serving in the Pisa Cathedral, noticed the swinging of a chandelier. Using his own pulse as a timekeeping device, he discovered that the period of oscillation of a pendulum is almost independent of the amplitude of these oscillations. Applying new knowledge to practice, the famous scientist Christiaan Huygens improved the clock - in 1657 he received a Dutch patent for the design of a pendulum clock. Moreover, the watch actually worked and provided excellent accuracy for that time. This paved the way for their mass production and use. Note that Huygens did not limit himself to a pendulum in which a point moves around a circle. There is a curve describing the profile of a depression in which the period of oscillation of a material point does not depend in any way on the amplitude. It is called a cycloid, and for this property it received the additional nickname “tautochrone” - from the word “equal in time” translated into Greek. The name “cycloid” was invented by Galileo (in France this curve was first called roulette). Huygens not only established the tautochrony of the cycloid, but also figured out how to make a load suspended on a thread oscillate along the cycloid. He wrote about this and much more in his book “Pendulum Clocks” (more precisely, “Horologium oscillatorium, sive de motu pendulorum an horologia aptato demonstrationes geometrica”, short titles were not in fashion then), published in 1673. We could go on and on about how watches were gradually improved, ensuring that their readings were reliable and not subject to external influences.
