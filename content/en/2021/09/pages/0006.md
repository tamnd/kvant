As one astronomer said, “the discovery of a new culinary recipe for humanity is sometimes more important than the discovery of a new star in the sky.” The chemical equation for the reaction of baking soda with citric acid in water is written as follows:

3NaHCO 3 C 6 H 8 O 7

$3CO_{2} 3H_{2} O Na_{3} C_{6} H_{5} O_{7}$. (1)

The reaction produces sodium citrate, carbon dioxide and water. Sodium citrate $( Na_{3} C_{6} H_{5} O_{7} )$ is used as a flavoring additive (E331) in carbonated drinks and other products, as well as one of the necessary components for blood transfusions - to prevent the clotting of donor blood and its components. When a dry tablet is dropped into water, the reaction proceeds quite quickly with the formation of magical gas bubbles on the surface of the tablet and glass, which, as they float to the surface, also hiss as they leave the water surface. Some of the carbon dioxide has time to dissolve in water. The active reaction zone is located on the surface of the tablet, where carbon dioxide bubbles are born. When the tablet dissolves completely, the emission of bubbles almost completely stops. The property of a substance to prevent blood clotting is sometimes extremely important in medicine in the treatment of serious illnesses (for example, when blood clots form in small vessels of the lungs during a coronavirus infection or in other cases). The reaction of baking soda with citric acid is often used by pharmacists and industrial chemists in effervescent tablets to transport various medications with water to the patient, including antiplatelet agents and anticoagulants. Roman powder (for example, aspirin) is compressed into a single tablet with baking soda and citric acid (and some other additives). Such tablets are placed in a lightweight, water- and vapor-tight multilayer paper-polymer package (sometimes aluminized on the inside), which protects the compressed powder mixture from exposure to atmospheric moisture, random drops of water and bright light. If you open the package and drop the tablet into a glass of water, then in a short time you get a solution or suspension of the medicine in a slightly carbonated drink. This is how many effervescent drug preparations are structured, for example, effervescent Aspirin-C from Bayer or the well-known drug ACC Long in the form of tablets used for respiratory diseases. Tablets for quick preparation of non-alcoholic effervescent tonic drinks (energy tonics), vitamins, and so on are designed in a similar way. Often, in the formulation of effervescent medicines, citric acid and baking soda are not even indicated, since these substances and their reaction products in water play only an auxiliary role. Simple observations show that the dissolution time of an effervescent aspirin tablet in a glass of water clearly depends on the temperature of the water - it decreases sharply with increasing temperature. Or better yet, two or three thermometers that allow you to more accurately measure the temperature of both cold and hot water in a glass (in different temperature ranges).
