Russian vaccines are often stored in the refrigerator so that they “age” more slowly in the cold. If a cup with already diluted epoxy glue is placed in a vessel with liquid nitrogen, the glue will not quickly polymerize, it will freeze, but will retain its functional properties for a long time if it is then removed from the nitrogen and thawed. Some thrifty experimental physicists and their laboratory assistants are well acquainted with this. Some will say that this is obvious. When substances are heated, the speeds of molecules or atoms increase, particles that interact with each other collide more often and reactions proceed faster. In reality, everything happens much more complicated and interesting. The temperature dependence of the rate of processes is completely (drastically) different! And the study of one chemical reaction, or rather, direct measurements of the dependence of its speed on the temperature of the medium, will help us understand this issue. Let's consider the chemical reaction of dissolving citric acid $( C_{6} H_{8} O_{7} )$ and baking soda (sodium bicarbonate $NaHCO_{3} ).$) in water. Judging by messages from the Internet, this is the most favorite reaction among schoolchildren and chemistry experts. It is with its help that it is possible to obtain a carbonated drink similar to lemonade in a simple way. The network presents a great variety of recipes and experiments with citric acid and drinking soda, sometimes very funny and instructive. To prepare a glass of sparkling water (soda), you need to mix about half a teaspoon of citric acid and a little less than half a teaspoon of baking soda, add water to the mixture, not forgetting to add and dissolve a little granulated sugar or fruit syrup for taste. Joseph Priestley produced carbon dioxide in the quantities he needed for his amateur experiments (this gas was formed from the fermentation of beer wort in barrels). An observant chemist priest noticed that if you collect carbon dioxide over water, some of the gas dissolves in the liquid, which gives it a pleasant tart taste. Thus, Priestley received (and was the first to bottle) a drink such as seltzer or soda water. And in the history of science he is better known as the scientist who discovered oxygen (this discovery was made independently by the Swedish chemist Carl Wilhelm Scheele, 1742–1786). Priestley also discovered or was the first to obtain and study such gases as nitrous oxide, hydrogen chloride, ammonia, silicon fluoride, sulfur dioxide and carbon monoxide. His work with gases greatly contributed to the development of chemical science. Later, Joseph Priestley wrote the monograph “The History of the Doctrine of Electricity” - a work for which he was elected an honorary member of a number of academies, including the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences (1780).
