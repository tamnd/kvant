Oranges, manholes and cutting a long rectangle

MATHEMATICAL CIRCLE

A. HAKOBYAN

For the holiday table, oranges are often cut into round slices (Fig.1). Typically, all the segments are the same thickness, but have you ever wondered which of these segments contain more peel: those that belong to the part closer to the center of the orange, or those that are closer to the edge? It turns out that if the slice is not a “cap,” then the amount of peel does not depend on which part of the orange it belongs to (of course, we consider the orange to be a ball). At figure 2 two orange slices of equal thickness are cut. Which lobe do you think has more peel? Consider a cut (section) of an orange by a plane. This can be explained, for example, using Cavalieri’s principle: the volumes of two bodies with equal areas of sections of all their common secant planes parallel to a given plane are equal (Fig.4). Indeed, according to Cavalieri’s principle, the volume of the peel in a slice $толщины^{h}$ will be equal to the volume of a parallelepiped (or cylinder) with a base area $S^{R} 2^{r} 2$ and height h, i.e. $S h R^{2} r^{2}$.
