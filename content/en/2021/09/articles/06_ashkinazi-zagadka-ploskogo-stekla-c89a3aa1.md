Let us note, first of all, that a convex porthole can be designed in different ways. If the thickness “radially” within the window is the same, then the radii themselves turn out to be different, their difference is exactly equal to the thickness of the lens. And the centers from which the radii are drawn coincide - situation 5. Another option - the radii are the same, but drawn from different centers - situation 6. The points of “equal inclination” a and b in the first case are located on the same radius, and in the second - at the same distance from the axis. The actual geometry of the porthole depends on the manufacturing technology, and there are several such technologies. In particular, if the sheet softens and bends on the mandrel, then this is situation 5, and if the porthole is stamped, placed between two forms - a punch and a matrix, then most likely this is situation 6. However, in neither case, the beam arriving at point a is absolutely not obliged to hit point b! Where we see an object is determined precisely by these rays - the brain receives a signal from the eyes and reconstructs the course of the rays and says that the object is located where the continuations of the rays that entered the eye intersect. This is why a magnifying glass “magnifies” - the rays coming from an object close to it fan out, and their continuations intersect at a greater distance from the axis and the object OB appears magnified. But this, of course, is only an IZ image (it is also called “imaginary”) - situation 8. From situation 7 on figure 3 it is clear that the rays of light emanating from an object in a wider fan, after refraction in flat glass, diverge even wider, and their continuations intersect closer to the observer. The extensions of the rays of group C intersect at point C¢, the extensions of the rays of group B - at point B¢, the extensions of the rays of group A - at point A¢. Thus, the wider the fan of rays we intercept, the closer the object will appear to us. True, this “magnifying glass” will be weak, and bad at that - since it will collect the rays of all groups, the image will be blurry. The effect will weaken as the object moves away from the glass, since with a fixed pupil size, the image of a distant object is formed by a narrower beam of rays. figure 4 shows a real experiment - a black ruler was photographed as the apparatus approached an object from distances from 25 cm to 0.5 cm through a layer of water (refractive index 1.33, thickness 4 cm) and through a layer of glass (refractive index 1.5, thickness 4.5 cm). We see that the line visible through the air appears wider from frame to frame in both cases - because it is viewed by the camera from an ever-shorter distance. A ruler visible through water or glass naturally also increases, but it increases more strongly. Moreover, for glass (above) the effect is greater than for water (below). In other words, a thick, flat layer of water or glass works like a magnifying glass, but a bad one - weak and producing a blurry image. The moral of the story is this. Some physical problems seem simple to us because we solve them for some “default” conditions. But for other conditions the answer may be completely different. Thus, the world viewed through thick glass may be completely different from the world outside the window.
