In this case, however, we have a loophole - if the condition says that the collision is absolutely elastic, then the work done by the friction force is zero. But there is no such loophole for impulse, and the incorrectness remains. True, sometimes “impact” is defined differently and more accurately - as an interaction short enough that external forces can be neglected. In this case, the collision time is not zero, the forces are not equal to infinity, and we have bypassed what we ran into in the previous paragraph. But still, with absolute elasticity of the collision, there is no work done by the friction force, which means either there is no force or there is no movement. However, the balls can exchange momentum, and therefore can change their angular velocities and fly apart to the side. Hence the moral is to read the definitions carefully, because this is also part of the model, and different definitions mean that we have different models. We will return to the behavior of a rotating ball with friction later. The next step into the depth of the problem is a direct, non-central blow (Fig.2,b), the balls joyfully fly along parallel straight lines and wonder if they will collide. Here three parameters come into play - two radii and the distance between the lines. If it is equal to zero, we have the previous problem - central impact. If the distance is greater than the sum of the radii, then there is no collision at all. If a collision occurs, then these three parameters - more precisely, the relationship between them - determine the position of the collision point and the direction of the forces acting on the balls during the collision. If the collision is absolutely inelastic, then all this does not matter - we again have two equations and two unknown quantities, for example, the velocities after the collision.

If the collision is elastic, fully or partially, then new parameters – the point of impact and the direction of the forces – become important. If there is no friction, then the forces are only reaction forces, they are perpendicular to the surface and pass through the centers of the balls. Their components, directed along parallel lines, affect the longitudinal velocities, and the transverse components cause the balls to fly across the lines. The problem is completely solvable, but they don’t give it in this form at school, but they do it simpler - they kill one unknown. Indeed, we now have three equations (for energy, momentum along and across), and four unknown quantities. In a natural situation, these are the components of the velocities after the collision, and it is impossible to find them without knowing our new parameters. But in nature, but in school they do it simply, they make any three quantities unknown - according to the number of equations. For example, at least one of the components of one of the velocities after a collision is assumed to be “known.” Or they act a little more decently - they ask to find not the components themselves, but the ratio of some two, again artificially reducing the number of unknowns. If we introduce a coefficient of friction, then the collision ceases to be absolutely elastic, and we have a choice. You can ignore this, apply the law of conservation of kinetic energy, and consider the friction force to be proportional to the reaction of the support. In this case, taking into account friction will be reduced to the fact that the force with which one ball acts on another will no longer be perpendicular to the surface - it will have a tangential component. And everything would be fine, but the friction force is proportional to the reaction of the support only in the presence of movement. At the beginning of the collision, the balls actually slide over each other, but can’t they stop during the interaction? If we abandon absolute elasticity, we either lose one equation, or we need to honestly calculate the work of the friction force and subtract the result from the kinetic energy.
