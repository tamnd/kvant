Udar and its surroundings

L.ASHKINAZI

In any school physics textbook, the case of a direct central collision of two balls is considered - absolutely elastic and absolutely inelastic. Of course, you can write and solve the corresponding equations, two for each case. In real life there is no “direct central” and there are no “balls” - there are always some deviations. By the way, since there are no balls, how can we formulate the condition of “directness” and “centrality” and will this formulation be of any use to us? Well, of course, there is no “absolutely elastic” and, moreover, there is no “impact”. When introducing this concept, we usually neglect the interaction time, and to change the momentum in an infinitesimal time, we need (remember the equation m ' v F ' t ) an infinitely large force that will destroy any material. Why is all this in textbooks, and not only in school textbooks? When models are considered in physics, the choice of a specific simplification is determined by the formal possibility of obtaining a solution and the reasonableness of this solution. Moreover, “reasonableness” depends on how we are going to apply the decision. If we are talking about practical application, i.e. about engineering, about creating things, then the main thing is whether the solution obtained is sufficiently accurate under the conditions and under the restrictions for which we need it. If we are not talking about practice yet, i.e. if this is an “intraphysical” model, then the situation is more complicated. What is important here is how much the model is based on fundamental physics, whether it has incomprehensible fitting parameters and how many there are, how logical the model is, and whether it allows for development.
