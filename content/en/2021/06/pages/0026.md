Re-reading science fiction writers

S. PARNOVSKY

Comic books and special effects films based on them are full of heroes with various kinds of superpowers. They can be found a little less often in books in the fantasy genre. Do they exist in classic science fiction? Why classic, you ask. Yes, simply because in the early stages of the development of the genre, it was believed that the author must give an explanation for the superpowers of his hero, at least somehow resembling a reasonable one. They used wonderful inventions, machines secretly created by unrecognized geniuses, some elixirs or medicines, new materials (preferably a metal of unearthly origin unknown to science), and so on. A classic of the genre is H.G. Wells's Invisible Man, who became so thanks to his scientific discovery. And almost from the very moment the book about this superhero appeared, it became the object of scientific analysis and ridicule. Indeed, light falling on an object can be absorbed, reflected, scattered, and refracted. This is what makes this object visible. The young scientist Griffin came up with a way to eliminate all these phenomena and

applied it to his body. And what is the result? All the light that entered his eyes calmly passed through them, without interacting with the rods and cones - the light-sensitive receptors on the retina of his eyes. So the notorious invisible man Griffin himself should not have seen anything around him. And not only people, but also the world around us. All that remains is to feel sorry for the poor blind man. However, if you give free rein to your imagination, you can come up with a way to get around this problem. The simplest is to allow the retina to absorb light. But this is not enough, you will also have to make the lens of the eye visible, because if its refractive index becomes equal, like Wells’s, to the refractive index of air, then the lens will stop focusing light on the retina and its visibility will be of little use. You can go further and imagine a complex device that divides the light falling on it, say, in half. One half goes into the observer's eyes or other light-sensitive device, and the second half is amplified twice and continues on its way, providing invisibility. It is clear that the halves are written simply as an example. It is important that the light falling on the device is analyzed and the absorbed part is replaced by light emitted by a special emitter. The emitted light must replace the absorbed light, both in intensity and in direction and spectrum. All this should most likely be controlled using a computer. This is Professor Wagner, the hero of many stories by Alexander Belyaev.
