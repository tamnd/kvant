Re-reading science fiction writers

S. PARNOVSKY

Comic books and special effects films based on them are full of heroes with various kinds of superpowers. They can be found a little less often in books in the fantasy genre. Do they exist in classic science fiction? Why classic, you ask. Yes, simply because in the early stages of the development of the genre, it was believed that the author must give an explanation for the superpowers of his hero, at least somehow resembling a reasonable one. They used wonderful inventions, machines secretly created by unrecognized geniuses, some elixirs or medicines, new materials (preferably a metal of unearthly origin unknown to science), and so on. A classic of the genre is H.G. Wells's Invisible Man, who became so thanks to his scientific discovery. And almost from the very moment the book about this superhero appeared, it became the object of scientific analysis and ridicule. Indeed, light falling on an object can be absorbed, reflected, scattered, and refracted. This is what makes this object visible. The young scientist Griffin came up with a way to eliminate all these phenomena and

All the light that entered his eyes calmly passed through them, without interacting with the rods and cones - the light-sensitive receptors on the retina of his eyes. So the notorious invisible man Griffin himself should not have seen anything around him. And not only people, but also the world around us. All that remains is to feel sorry for the poor blind man. However, if you give free rein to your imagination, you can come up with a way to get around this problem. The simplest is to allow the retina to absorb light. But this is not enough, you will also have to make the lens of the eye visible, because if its refractive index becomes equal, like Wells’s, to the refractive index of air, then the lens will stop focusing light on the retina and its visibility will be of little use. You can go further and imagine a complex device that divides the light falling on it, say, in half. One half goes into the observer's eyes or other light-sensitive device, and the second half is amplified twice and continues on its way, providing invisibility. It is clear that the halves are written simply as an example. It is important that the light falling on the device is analyzed and the absorbed part is replaced by light emitted by a special emitter. The emitted light must replace the absorbed light, both in intensity and in direction and spectrum. All this should most likely be controlled using a computer. Professor Wagner's body in its normal state is attracted not only to the Earth, but also to the Sun, Moon, the center of the Galaxy, clusters and superclusters of galaxies. And if the professor at some point simultaneously makes his body permeable and not attracted to other bodies, then it will indeed move uniformly and rectilinearly, but not in the reference system associated with the Earth or the German castle in which the professor was kept, but relative to some not entirely clear “inertial reference system.” The castle rotates around the Earth’s axis in a day, the Earth returns around the center of mass of the Earth-Moon system in a month, and around the Sun in a year. From the point of view of those who watched the disappearance of the professor in the fireplace, the picture should look like this: the professor irradiates himself with mysterious rays, after which he begins to choke, floundering in the air, and rises higher and higher with increasing speed. If he tries to say something, then it is still impossible to hear him - after all, his throat, lungs, vocal cords and everything else calmly pass through the air, without causing it to vibrate, i.e. without causing sound waves. Probably, we don’t need to go into other details. But in conclusion, readers are invited to independently understand another signature trick from the arsenal of science fiction - time travel. It is not asked how this can be arranged, but rather where exactly the time traveler will end up, having moved forward or backward along this very time, but finding himself, according to science fiction writers, “in the same place.” And Super Professor Wagner's trajectory will help you think about this question.
