In any school physics textbook, the case of a direct central collision of two balls is considered - absolutely elastic and absolutely inelastic. Of course, you can write and solve the corresponding equations, two for each case. In real life there is no “direct central” and there are no “balls” - there are always some deviations. By the way, since there are no balls, how can we formulate the condition of “directness” and “centrality” and will this formulation be of any use to us? Well, of course, there is no “absolutely elastic” and, moreover, there is no “impact”. When introducing this concept, we usually neglect the interaction time, and to change the momentum in an infinitesimal time, we need (remember the equation m ' v F ' t ) an infinitely large force that will destroy any material. Why is all this in textbooks, and not only in school textbooks? When models are considered in physics, the choice of a specific simplification is determined by the formal possibility of obtaining a solution and the reasonableness of this solution. Moreover, “reasonableness” depends on how we are going to apply the decision. If we are talking about practical application, i.e. about engineering, about creating things, then the main thing is whether the solution obtained is sufficiently accurate under the conditions and under the restrictions for which we need it. If we are not talking about practice yet, i.e. if this is an “intraphysical” model, then the situation is more complicated. What is important here is how much the model is based on fundamental physics, whether it has incomprehensible fitting parameters and how many there are, how logical the model is, and whether it allows for development. In this case, however, we have a loophole - if the condition says that the collision is absolutely elastic, then the work done by the friction force is zero. True, sometimes “impact” is defined differently and more accurately - as an interaction short enough that external forces can be neglected. In this case, the collision time is not zero, the forces are not equal to infinity, and we have bypassed what we ran into in the previous paragraph. But still, with absolute elasticity of the collision, there is no work done by the friction force, which means either there is no force or there is no movement. Hence the moral is to read the definitions carefully, because this is also part of the model, and different definitions mean that we have different models. The next step into the depth of the problem is a direct, non-central blow (Fig.2,b), the balls joyfully fly along parallel straight lines and wonder if they will collide. Here three parameters come into play - two radii and the distance between the lines. If it is equal to zero, we have the previous problem - central impact. If the distance is greater than the sum of the radii, then there is no collision at all. If a collision occurs, then these three parameters - more precisely, the relationship between them - determine the position of the collision point and the direction of the forces acting on the balls during the collision. If the collision is absolutely inelastic, then all this does not matter - we again have two equations and two unknown quantities, for example, the velocities after the collision.

If the collision is elastic, fully or partially, then new parameters – the point of impact and the direction of the forces – become important. If there is no friction, then the forces are only reaction forces, they are perpendicular to the surface and pass through the centers of the balls. Their components, directed along parallel lines, affect the longitudinal velocities, and the transverse components cause the balls to fly across the lines. The problem is completely solvable, but they don’t give it in this form at school, but they do it simpler - they kill one unknown. Indeed, we now have three equations (for energy, momentum along and across), and four unknown quantities. In a natural situation, these are the components of the velocities after the collision, and it is impossible to find them without knowing our new parameters. But in nature, but in school they do it simply, they make any three quantities unknown - according to the number of equations. For example, at least one of the components of one of the velocities after a collision is assumed to be “known.” Or they act a little more decently - they ask to find not the components themselves, but the ratio of some two, again artificially reducing the number of unknowns. If we introduce a coefficient of friction, then the collision ceases to be absolutely elastic, and we have a choice. You can ignore this, apply the law of conservation of kinetic energy, and consider the friction force to be proportional to the reaction of the support. In this case, taking into account friction will be reduced to the fact that the force with which one ball acts on another will no longer be perpendicular to the surface - it will have a tangential component. And everything would be fine, but the friction force is proportional to the reaction of the support only in the presence of movement. At the beginning of the collision, the balls actually slide over each other, but can’t they stop during the interaction? If we abandon absolute elasticity, we either lose one equation, or we need to honestly calculate the work of the friction force and subtract the result from the kinetic energy. The core of this task is this. There is a ball of known size that is initially at rest. A body of much smaller mass flies along a straight line passing through its center and collides with this ball. The force acting between the bodies is considered known. What are the next developments? The mass condition means that the large ball is at rest, or more precisely, it moves much more slowly than the small body. Further, if the force is zero (very often this is the easiest way to start considering), then there is no interaction - the big one is at rest, the small one is flying, surprised that its speed has not changed. We begin to increase the force - an exchange of impulses begins, and we can also use the law of conservation of energy, subtracting from the kinetic energy the work of the force A FD acting between the bodies, where D is the diameter. With a further increase in force, we arrive at a “absolutely inelastic impact,” when a small body stops inside a large one. You can write the corresponding equations yourself and get the solution. If we abandon the condition on the mass ratio, then we will have to take into account that the large body also moves, and then the movement of the small body occurs not until it stops, but until the velocities coincide. You can also consider a situation where a large body has an initial speed; this will not complicate the problem much. This model has several nice complication options, some of which are found in problem books. For example, a small body can fly in a straight line that does not pass through the center of a large body. In this case, the force F has a moment, and the large body obviously begins to rotate. It is less obvious that in this case a velocity component appears in the large body, directed transverse to the initial direction of motion of the small body. Another complication option is if the initial speed of a large body is not directed along the straight line along which the small body initially flies, but, for example, perpendicularly.
