Three periods of studying probability theory

A. SHIRYAEV

J. Bernoulli. In the 17th century - early 18th century, the central figure (even earlier were Pascal, Fermat, Huygens) in the calculus of probabilities was Jacob Bernoulli, who introduced into science the classical concept of probability as the ratio of the number of possible outcomes favorable to the event under consideration to the total number of conceivable outcomes. The main result of J. Bernoulli, with which his name is associated, is the law of large numbers. This was the first theorem in the calculus of probability (published in Ars Conjectandi in 1713; he died in 1705), and it was proven absolutely strictly by arithmetic-combinatorial methods. (Therefore, J. Bernoulli is also considered the founder of combinatorics.) P.L. Chebyshev. Among the scientists of the second half of the 19th century who played an outstanding role in the calculus of probabilities was P.L. Chebyshev (then Markov and Lyapunov). If before him they operated with objects taking a finite number of values ​​(mostly two), then he was the first to introduce the concept of a random variable (as a function of an elementary event) and its mathematical expectation, thereby expanding and deepening the entire calculus of probabilities. He is responsible for the now classic Chebyshev inequality. He was the first who not only realized with complete clarity the role of random variables and their mathematical expectations, but also the first who began to study the rate of convergence of pre-limit probability laws to limit ones. Thus, Chebyshev made discrete classical probability theory part of general mathematics.

The culmination here was the monograph by A.N. Kolmogorov (1933) “Basic concepts of the theory of probability”, which gave a diagram of the logical justification of the theory of probability and made it a branch of mathematical theory. In addition to the logical justification in this monograph, the following were clearly given (constantly used) concepts such as conditional probability, conditional mathematical expectation, the zero-one law, the theorem on consistent distributions (in this monograph), which was the central point in the creation of the theory of random processes. Kolmogorov’s monograph opened a new era in the development of probability theory as a mathematical science and in expanding the scope of its influence and applications.
