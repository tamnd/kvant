On December 12, 1979, the Politburo of the CPSU Central Committee decided to send troops to Afghanistan. The protracted Afghan war began. At a special meeting of the United Nations, the actions of the USSR were condemned as unlawful by the overwhelming majority of states (104 versus 18). Sakharov, based on extensive information about the political situation in Afghanistan and the outbreak of hostilities, spoke in an interview with representatives of the Western press condemning the actions of the Soviet government. The government's response was immediate. By Decree of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR dated January 8, 1980, Sakharov was deprived of all government awards. It was not possible to only deprive him of the title of academician, although a grandiose persecution of Sakharov was launched in the press, demanding this punishment as well. The President of the Academy of Sciences M.V. Keldysh did not dare to convene the general meeting of the Academy necessary for this purpose. History has preserved his preliminary conversation with the most authoritative Soviet physicist, Nobel laureate P.L. Kapitsa, in which Kapitsa reminded Keldysh: “in 1933, Hitler expelled A. Einstein from the Prussian Academy of Sciences...” On January 22, 1980, Sakharov was detained on his way to work and, without any official court verdict, was exiled (along with his wife) to the city of Gorky, where he was entry of foreign correspondents is prohibited. Very few people then decided to visit Sakharov in his exile. Despite the fact that Sakharov received free time for scientific work (and used it profitably), Gorky’s exile cost him dearly. Three times he was forced to protest against the oppression and restriction of the rights of people close to him. He could express his protest in the only way - by declaring and carrying out hunger strikes. The hunger strikes and painful operations themselves

People's Deputy On March 11, 1985, M.S. Gorbachev became the General Secretary of the CPSU Central Committee. The era of “perestroika” began. On October 22, 1986, during a period of temporary improvement in health after a difficult hospital period, Sakharov wrote to Gorbachev with a request to stop his deportation and the exile of his wife, since her cardiac illness was becoming threatening. He promises to cease his public activities, with the proviso “except in exceptional cases.” On December 15, a telephone was installed in the apartment, and on December 16, Gorbachev received a call, apologizing for everything that had happened earlier and announcing the cancellation of the decree depriving Sakharov of state awards and the decision to exile. On the morning of December 22, three trains from Gorky arrived in Moscow. And already from early morning, thousands of people filled the Gorky “pier” at the Kursk station, not even knowing the exact time of arrival of the required train. We can consider that it was from these December days that a new era of our country began, an era of transformation, which, like all eras of change, is never simple and short. Sakharov, now a chief researcher, plunges headlong into participating in scientific seminars and in public life. He refused to get back the badges and certificates of all his state awards, which he was stripped of in 1980, “until the time when there will be no one in the country convicted on political grounds for dissent.” In 1988, he was given the opportunity to travel abroad.
