And then the fate of the ice can develop very differently, depending on many local conditions: the height of the tide, the topography of the coastal seabed, sea currents, air temperature, etc. Relatively thin ice may exhibit some elasticity, but thicker ice will break under the pressure of the tidal wave, creating cracks that run along the shore. Through these cracks, water can flood the surface of coastal or, as it is called, fast ice. If the pressure of the tidal wave is strong enough, then the ice begins to crumble near the fault, ice floes are formed, which can be thrown onto the surface of fast ice, driven under it, or stand on end, forming so-called ropakas (individual ice floes standing vertically or obliquely) or entire coastal ridges.
