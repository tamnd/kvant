For what is the largest n it is possible $за^{k}$ weighings, is it guaranteed to find among n scales lighter faulty scales? Answer: $при^{n} 3^{k} 2$. Solution. Suppose that during the first weighing, x other scales are placed on each pan of scales A and y more scales remain. Then if one of the bowls is overweight, then either the scales on the other bowl or the scales A may be faulty, i.e. There are only x + 1 suspicious weights left. Similarly, if equilibrium is obtained in the first weighing, then y 1 suspicious weights remain. Since in both cases a known good scale appears, all further weighings can be carried out on them, as in the standard problem of finding a counterfeit coin. Accordingly, a necessary and sufficient condition for the possibility of finding faulty scales for the remaining k 1 weighings will be the fulfillment of the inequalities $x 1 d 3^{k} 1, y 1 d 3^{k} 1$, which is what the above answer does. It is also interesting that if you allow complex weighings, in which several other scales are placed on the bowls of one scale, on the bowls of which scales are also placed, etc. (in one complex weighing, results are obtained from all the scales involved, except those whose bowls have nothing on them), then two weighings are enough to find one faulty scale among any number of scales greater than two. As we will see, this makes the task significantly more difficult. Answer: for n 13. Suppose that the damaged scales can be found in three weighings at $каком-нибудь^{n} t 14$. It is clear that during any weighing there is no point in comparing different numbers of scales, otherwise the bowl with a large number of scales will outweigh and this will not provide any information. After each weighing, all scales will be divided into two groups: one will contain obviously real ones, and the second will contain suspicious ones, about which it is not yet clear whether they are real or not. We will prove that there is no point in carrying out the second and third weighings on suspicious scales. Let's say we did this kind of weighing on scales X. Let's swap them with some real ones (it doesn't matter where they are, on the scales or not). If X is real, then the weighing result will not change. If X are damaged, then they could show the same result as after replacement. This means that in any case, the set of suspicious scales will either remain the same (including the information will not change whether each of the scales can be lighter or heavier), or scales X will be removed from it. Thus, we can assume that the second and third weighings were carried out on real scales.
