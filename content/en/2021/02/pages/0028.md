This means that there are at least 12 possibilities of what kind of scale this is and in what direction its weight differs from the real ones. All suspicious scales must participate in the second and third weighings, except perhaps one, otherwise we will not be able to distinguish between two scales that did not participate in the weighing, and damaged ones may be among them. This means that after the second and third weighings, at most we would not be able to find out from some scales whether they are lighter or heavier than real ones, i.e. must be able to distinguish between at least 11 situations. However, each weighing can have a maximum of three outcomes, so we will not be able to distinguish between more than nine different situations. If during the first weighing we compared at least five scales on each side, then it may turn out that one of the scales was outweighed. Then the number of suspicious scales after the first weighing is more than nine and it will not be possible to distinguish them after two weighings. Now we will show how to find damaged scales for n 13 in three weighings. As is clear from the estimate, the first weighing is to put four scales on the pans of some scales. If the scales were in equilibrium, then the real scales were compared on them, and the suspect ones were those on which the weighing was carried out and the four scales that did not participate in it. For the second weighing, let's take real scales, put three real scales on one bowl, and three suspicious ones on the other. If the suspicious ones turned out to be lighter or heavier, then the damaged scales are among them and we know in which direction their weight differs from the real ones. Then, by comparing any two of them on real scales with a third weighing, we will identify the damaged ones. If one of the bowls is overweight, for example the first one, then two heavy scales on it and light ones on the other bowl remain suspicious. Then we will compare two suspicious heavy weights on real scales and either we will find a damaged scale among them, or it will be a suspicious light one. The case is dealt with in the same way if, during the second weighing, the second cup outweighs. If the scales show balance, then the scales being weighed are real, but three scales remain suspicious. By the third weighing, we will compare on real scales the remaining suspicious heavy and light scales on one bowl with two real ones on the other. In the case of inequality, depending on the result, it is clear which of them are spoiled, and in the case of equality, those on which the first weighing was carried out are spoiled. It should be noted, however, that, unlike most weighing problems, in this problem the number of scales is limited not only from above, but also from below. Thus, for n 3, no number of weighings guarantees the finding of faulty scales! Indeed, let, for example, scales A and B be correct, scales C be faulty and heavier, and when weighing on them it turns out that A! B. Having weighed on other scales (there’s nothing else to do anyway), we also get that C! A and C! B. We could get the same system of inequalities if the scales B were faulty and lighter.
