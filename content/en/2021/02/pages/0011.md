Thermal expansion

E.SOKOLOV

WHEN HEATED, BODIES EXPAND, everyone agrees with this. (There is an important exception to this rule. Water in the range from 0 q C to 4 q C contracts when heated. Thanks to this, our reservoirs do not freeze to the bottom in winter.) Many can even give a formula for changing the length of the rod when heated: $l l_{0} 1 D' t$. But on the question of how the shape of a body changes when heated, there is no longer unanimity (Fig. 1).

⟦figure⟧

Fig. 1. Thermal expansion of the ring: a) initial shape of the ring; b and c) “swelling” and “stretching”

Some represent thermal expansion as the “swelling” of a heated object in all directions (see Fig. 1, b), while others - as its “stretching” in all directions (see Fig. 1, c). Probably, when the former do figure, they imagine a spreading ink stain, and the latter imagine a rubber film stretching in all directions. The question arises: which of these pictures correctly conveys the change in the shape of a body during thermal expansion? The well-known formula does not give us an answer to this question: it tells us only about the lengths of the rods and says nothing about the radii and thicknesses of the rings. To learn how to answer such questions, we need to try to formulate the law of thermal expansion in the field.

in general form. Namely, it is necessary to formulate it for such a geometric figure and in such a way that results for rings, circles, triangles, etc. would be obtained from it automatically, as special cases. This is not difficult to do. Let us turn to the property of heated bodies, which is familiar to anyone who has cut with a jigsaw. If we cut a circle in a plate heated to 20 q C with a thin, thin file, the circle will easily fall out of the plate. This means that, having become free, the circle did not increase its size. And if, on the contrary, we want to insert it back, then it will easily insert and completely take its old place. In other words, having become free, the circle did not reduce its size. It turns out that the state of a body at a given temperature is the same regardless of whether it was heated as a separate figure or as part of a plate. And this will happen not only at a temperature of 20 q C, but also at 37 q C, and at 156 q C and at any other temperatures. Consequently, all figures expand equally, regardless of whether they are heated by themselves or as part of the plate. This observation allows us to take an infinite plane as a general case of a heated body: drawing various figures on it, we, knowing how the points of the plane move, will always find how the points of the drawn figures move and how their shapes change. It remains to take the last step - from the well-known law on increasing the lengths of rods, obtain the law of motion of points on an infinite plane. This is also easy to do: we can consider any two points of the plane to be the ends of a drawn infinitely thin rod, so the distance between them is determined by the formula $l l_{0} 1 D' t$.
