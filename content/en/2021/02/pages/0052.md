And far from the coast, tidal fluctuations in the ice continue, along with the water rising and falling twice a day. True, continuous ice cover slightly constrains these movements: in most areas of the White Sea, where the photographs presented in the article and on the fourth page of the magazine cover were taken, the range of fluctuations decreases by a dozen or two centimeters, but at tide heights of 1.5–2 m this decrease is almost imperceptible. And then the fate of the ice can develop very differently, depending on many local conditions: the height of the tide, the topography of the coastal seabed, sea currents, air temperature, etc. Relatively thin ice may exhibit some elasticity, but thicker ice will break under the pressure of the tidal wave, creating cracks that run along the shore. Through these cracks, water can flood the surface of coastal or, as it is called, fast ice. If the pressure of the tidal wave is strong enough, then the ice begins to crumble near the fault, ice floes are formed, which can be thrown onto the surface of fast ice, driven under it, or stand on end, forming so-called ropakas (individual ice floes standing vertically or obliquely) or entire coastal ridges. In some cases (for example, in the presence of currents), non-freezing polynyas are formed along the coast. Probably, these processes can be compared to some extent with the formation of relief

But the most interesting winter marine landforms form around rocks that lie in shallow water, exposed at low water and hidden beneath the surface as sea levels rise. When severe frosts occur, when the ice manages to freeze in those few hours while the stones are hidden under water, with the onset of low tide it will sink and break on the tops of the stones. The ice will appear to open into petals around the stone (Fig. 2). Through the cracks formed around the stone, new portions of water will be poured onto the surface of the ice, creating a beautiful “skating rink” around the stone. In severe frosts, water evaporating from these cracks will condense on the stone and the surrounding ice petals, leading to the growth of a layer of ice (so-called sublimation ice). The height of such a tent can reach human height (Fig. 3) or even more. And in the ice caves in the petal walls of the tent there are algae frozen into them (Fig. 4). And now the entire surface of the sea bay is covered with these giant “molehills” sparkling in the winter sun...
