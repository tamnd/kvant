PHYSICS ELECTIVE

Mercury and water – diffusion and evaporation

L.ASHKINAZI

EVERYONE KNOWS THAT MERCURY VAPOR IS HARMFUL. Therefore, if mercury is in a container from which it can evaporate and enter the room where people are, add some water to this container. Water, as you know, is approximately 13.6 times lighter than mercury; it sits on top of mercury and inhibits evaporation. Everything seems clear, but we have already said several absurdities. Water is indeed lighter, but this does not mean that it is “situated on top”. Alcohol and acetone are lighter than water, but they do not sit on top, but dissolve in water. Moreover, their solubility is not limited in any way; in such cases they usually say: “they are mixed in all proportions.” But gasoline is located on top, but not completely - the solubility of gasoline in water under normal conditions is about $10^{5}$ (by weight). And in the opposite direction, i.e. solubility of water in gasoline is about $10^{4}$. Water and gasoline have such a complex relationship. As for the mutual solubility of water in mercury and mercury in water, they are small: 2 $10^{6}$ and 6 $10^{8}$, respectively (again, by weight). So it’s quite possible to say “on top” about water and mercury, but this does not automatically follow from the fact that water is lighter (even 13.6 times). Where did we get the idea that a water coating “inhibits evaporation”? Can any coating at all not slow down, but accelerate evaporation? The idea seems strange, but, say, thermal underwear worn on a person, the evaporation of water (i.e. sweat), as they say, can accelerate. There, if this effect occurs, it is due to the heterogeneous structure - capillaries bring water to the surface by means of surface tension,

Two years ago it was reported that graphene, i.e. carbon monolayer (a layer one atom thick), suppresses the evaporation of water from hydrophilic surfaces and accelerates evaporation from hydrophobic ones. True, considering graphene a “coating” is a little strange; the diffusion equation and the usual principles of calculation are not applicable here. But in our case, this is not important either, because mercury is not covered with either graphene or a monolayer of water. So we will assume that this is ordinary water. First, let's consider a situation without water, but in a closed jar: the surface of the mercury is open, there is a certain amount of air above the mercury, and then the lid is closed. Mercury evaporates into a closed volume of air, the concentration of mercury in the air increases, and a reverse flow of mercury from the air into the liquid phase appears. After some time, the so-called dynamic equilibrium is reached, when the flows are equal and the concentration of mercury in the air stops growing. This equilibrium concentration is known; for example, at $температуре^{15} q C$ it is 2 $10^{5}$ kg $м^{3}$. The maximum permissible concentration is 3 $10^{10}$ kg $м^{3}$, i.e. 5 orders of magnitude less. Obviously, sitting in a closed jar above a layer of mercury is very dangerous. If the mercury is covered with a layer of water, and behind it there is again a closed volume of air, then the situation becomes more complicated. The concentration of mercury in water behaves in the same way as when evaporating into a closed air space - it increases until dynamic equilibrium is established. However, the equilibrium concentration turns out to be 3 times greater than in the first case, and amounts to 6 $10^{5}$ kg $м^{3}$. There can be two reasons for this phenomenon - either water molecules attract mercury atoms with their electric field (polarizing them) and facilitate their separation from the mercury layer, or the reverse flow of mercury atoms from water to the surface of mercury at the same concentration (and temperature) turns out to be less than from air.
