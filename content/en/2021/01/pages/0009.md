The de Broglie wave and the Schrödinger equation What determines the structure of electron clouds? Is there an equation that describes quantum mechanics in the same way that Newton's laws describe classical mechanics? Yes, such an equation exists. It was proposed in 1925 by the Austrian physicist Erwin Schrödinger (1887–1961) and is the basis of atomic physics and theoretical chemistry. Schrödinger's theory generalized the revolutionary idea proposed a year earlier by the Frenchman Louis de Broglie (1892–1987), which was that any particle with momentum p can be associated with a wave of length O h p. Thus, any particle can exhibit both corpuscular behavior and wave behavior, just as light does. Similar to the wave theory of light proposed by James Maxwell (1831–1879), where the electric field E x, y, z, t is a function of time and three spatial coordinates, Schrödinger’s equation describes the state of a particle using a “wave function” \ x, y, z, t, the square of which determines the probability density of finding the particle at a given time t at point x, y, z. This approach was based on an analogy with optics, where the square of the electric field modulus determines the probability of finding a photon in a given

The difference lies in the fact that the electric field is physically measurable, for example, by its effect on electrically charged objects, while the wave function introduced by de Broglie had no clear physical meaning. Using the Schrödinger equation, it turned out to be possible to find the spatial distribution of the electron probability density for its possible states in the hydrogen atom. By depicting these probability density distributions on a plane with different colors, we obtain an image of different atomic orbitals (regions in which the probability of finding an electron is highest). Such images replace the electron orbits of the Bohr model of the atom and visually represent the behavior of electrons in an atom. Calculations based on the Schrödinger equation explain the existence of discrete energy levels, which are responsible for the line spectra observed during the emission and absorption of light. Similar, but more complex calculations allow us to understand how chemical bonds are formed between atoms. Note that the work of de Broglie and Schrödinger preceded Heisenberg's discovery of the uncertainty principle. The latter is simple, concise, elegant, but contains less information than the Schrödinger equation.

Davisson–Germer experiment. The concept of the connection between waves and particles, the so-called wave-particle duality, proposed by de Broglie, led to the idea of ​​​​using optical research methods by replacing light with streams of particles. So, in 1927, American physicists Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer bombarded a nickel crystal with electrons. As a result, they obtained diffraction patterns similar to those produced when crystals are irradiated with X-rays. To interpret the resulting diffraction patterns, the electrons had to be assigned a certain wavelength, and it coincided with the value predicted by de Broglie. Thus, the experiment brilliantly confirmed his hypothesis.
