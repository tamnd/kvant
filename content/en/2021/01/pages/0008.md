We are trying to figure out why the energy values ​​of an atom form a discrete set. At the beginning of the 20th century, the question of the nature of the atom, the smallest particle of a substance that is the bearer of its properties, was one of the central questions in physics. The proposed models, being internally contradictory or inconsistent with experiment, were refuted one after another. And so, in 1913, the Danish physicist Niels Bohr (1885–1962) proposed a mathematically simple theory of the atom that explained existing experimental data, but based on assumptions so unusual that he himself called them “postulates.”

The atom according to Niels Bohr Niels Bohr, proposing his model of the atom, knew nothing about the uncertainty principle, the discovery of which was still 14 years away. In Bohr's model, as in Rutherford's model, the electron revolves around the nucleus, just as the Earth revolves around the Sun, but the electron can only move in certain orbits. For example, circular orbits are possible only if the product of the electron's momentum mv and the radius of its orbit R, this product is called the “angular momentum,” is a multiple of Planck’s constant:

However, the momentum of the electron and the radius of the orbit are also related by the fact that the centripetal acceleration of the electron, equal to $v^{2} R$, is provided by the force of electrostatic attraction. For a hydrogen atom whose nucleus consists of a proton, the latter is equal to $e 4 SH_{0} R$. From here we can already find the radii $R_{n} 1$ of allowed orbits for each value of n. So, for n 1 we find the already familiar value $R_{0}$, which corresponds to the ground state. We leave it to the reader to derive the general formula applicable to the excited states of the electron. The Bohr model, developed in 1913, described the spectra of radiation quite well.

A decade later, Bohr's theory was conceptually expanded by introducing a probabilistic description of the location of the electron. Thus, it turned out that the value $R_{0}$ (the distance from the electron to the nucleus) in the hydrogen atom can only be considered a certain average value; The uncertainty principle does not allow us to clearly determine the distance between a proton and an electron.

Probability of finding. Let's assume that at some point we managed to establish the position of the electron. Is it possible to predict its position in a second? No, because knowing the position of the electron would inevitably lead to complete uncertainty of its speed. No instrument, no theory could predict where the electron would go. So what to do? Let's change the strategy and mark the point in space at which the electron was detected, then another point - the result of a similar measurement with another electron, and repeat this procedure many times. Although it is impossible to predict where the next mark will appear, their distribution follows a certain rule. The density of the marks, which varies depending on the point in space, indicates the probability of finding an electron during the measurement. We were forced to abandon the description of the movement of the electron, but we can now determine the probability of its location at each point in space. The behavior of an electron in the nanoworld is characterized by probability!
