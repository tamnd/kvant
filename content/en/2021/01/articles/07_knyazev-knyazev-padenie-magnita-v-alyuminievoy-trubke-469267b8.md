THE DEMONSTRATION OF A MAGNETIC PILLAR FALLING through a thick-walled copper or aluminum tube is very simple, but is always received with delight and surprise by the audience. First, through such a vertically located tube about 1 m long and with an internal diameter of 18 mm, a piece of chalk or a metal cylinder is thrown without initial speed. Obviously, their movement takes a fraction of a second. And after that we throw a magnetic column with a length and diameter of 15 mm into the same tube, i.e. approximately equal to the diameter of the tube. When the thickness of the pipe walls is about 3-4 mm, the magnet literally hangs inside the pipe - it moves (without distortion!) very slowly, only the slight rustle of its smooth movement is heard. In our demo, the drop time was over six seconds! Another demonstration showed a slow-motion slide of a flat neodymium magnet along an inclined groove of a thick-walled duralumin angle. The physics of this phenomenon is quite clear, and clear examples can be given where this effect is applied. For example, in school demonstration electrical measuring instruments it is used in the design of the needle stabilizer. At the end opposite the arrow, a copper plate (or flat copper coil) is attached, which swings near a permanent magnet. When the plate moves, a dynamic electric field arises in it and, as a result, electric currents (Foucault currents) appear. The interaction of these currents with the magnetic field slows down the plate. Another example, even closer to today’s students: a magnetic brake in a computer’s hard drive.
