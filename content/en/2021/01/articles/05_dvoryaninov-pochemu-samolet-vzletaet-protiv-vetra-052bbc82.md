Why does a plane take off against the wind?

S. DVORYANINOV

“I see you’re thinking hard about something?” – Papa Carlo asked his Pinocchio, noticing that he had been leafing through the pages of a physics textbook for a long time. – Yes, today in class the teacher said that it is easier for a plane to take off against the wind. And we need to explain why this is so. I looked through the entire textbook, but did not find the answer. “Well, let’s think logically,” Papa Carlo shook off the shavings from his apron, walked away from the workbench and sat down at the table. – What does it mean to “reason logically”? Logic is when B follows from A. But in this physical problem there are no A and B,” Buratino said disappointedly.

“You’re wrong,” Papa Carlo objected. – And now you will see for yourself. To answer your teacher's question, we will build a logical chain. I will ask you questions, and you will answer them. So, let's start. – What happens to the plane before it leaves the ground and is in the air? “The plane is accelerating, moving along the runway, that’s clear,” Buratino answered. – And relative to what is the plane moving? – Regarding the land, of course, and regarding what is on it. This, for example, is the airport terminal building, people watching the planes, trees... - Well, what’s the most important thing? Relative to what is the plane moving? The plane accelerates relative to the air! Let us first assume that the weather is windless, completely calm, such that not a single leaf on the tree moves. So, relative to this still air, the plane moves faster and faster along the runway. Agree? – Papa Carlo clarified. – Yes, I agree, it looks like the accelerated movement of a motor boat on a lake. “There is no current in the lake, the water stands still,” Buratino responded. – Just as a ship floats in the ocean, so a plane floats in the ocean of air. – Don’t be mistaken in terms, my dear Pinocchio. Ships do not float, but walk, as real sailors say. According to Archimedes' law, balloons, aerostats, airships, and even children's balloons filled with helium float in the ocean of air. And planes fly. But first they must take off. And a force called lift lifts them off the ground and lifts them up. It occurs when the air flow hits the plane,” Papa Carlo continued. – But when I ride a bicycle, I feel this oncoming flow very well. But at the same time I don’t rise up. And even a car that has picked up a lot of speed continues to roll along the ground and doesn’t fly anywhere,” Buratino objected. – What you say is true. But the plane and especially its wings have such a special shape that leads to the appearance of a lifting force directed upward. The greater the speed of the oncoming flow, the greater the lifting force becomes. Finally, it overcomes the force of gravity acting on the aircraft, and then the aircraft lifts off the ground. – But if the lifting force is greater than the force of gravity, then the plane will rise higher and higher without limit. “But that doesn’t happen,” Buratino tried to argue again. - Of course not. When necessary, the airplane pilot, by changing the angle of attack of the wing, ensures that the two forces - lift and gravity - become equal, and then the plane flies at a constant altitude,” concluded Papa Carlo. – Now compare all the facts that we just talked about and explain why it is easier for a plane to take off against the wind. - Yes, I can explain! - Buratino exclaimed, - here it is, a logical chain. To lift an aircraft, sufficient

but high speed v of the oncoming air flow. Speed ​​is a relative concept. We can say that the flow impinges on the plane, or we can say that the plane is moving relative to the air mass. When the wind blows towards an airplane on the runway, the airplane's speed relative to the air is equal to its own speed plus the wind speed. In this case, the required speed value v is obtained at a lower natural speed of the aircraft. Like this. “That’s right, well done,” concluded Papa Carlo. “That’s why they say that it’s easier for a plane to take off against the wind.” This was especially important in the early days of aviation, when engines were not very powerful. Modern airplanes can take off with a tailwind, but in any case, pilots take into account both the wind speed and its direction. So, when you grow up, you will become a pilot and remember today’s conversation! And I need to continue working. And Papa Carlo went to his workbench.
