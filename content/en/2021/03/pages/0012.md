To illustrate the achievements of modern observational astronomy, we provide brief information about one project. In 2016, the first measurements were made on the giant SKA (Square Kilometer Array) radio telescope under construction. It is a complex consisting of three objects, the distance between which is over 3000 km, in the desert areas of South Africa, Australia and New Zealand. The total number of antennas is amazing - 9,000 parabolic antennas, each of which is as tall as a three-story building, and two million so-called dipole antennas, including large ones (like trees) and small ones (lattices lying on the ground). Today, 200 parabolic antennas have been built in South Africa and tens of thousands of dipole antennas are being installed in Australia. Why are the elements of one device separated over an intercontinental distance? The fact is that the greater the distance between the devices receiving radiation, the more accurately the position of the object on the celestial sphere is measured. In an interference radio telescope, the distance between two instruments plays the same role as the diameter of a single antenna. Over enormous distances, angular resolutions of several ten-thousandths of an arcsecond can be achieved. And the name SKA itself is due to the fact that the total area of ​​perceived radio images will be a square kilometer, in which there is a million square meters, i.e. a million individual images. Such a large number of antennas ensures high sensitivity of the device and coverage of a larger area of ​​the celestial sphere. Of course, processing a huge amount of data from each device and

When the entire SKA telescope comes into operation (from 2024 to 2030), two processing centers will receive information in a volume exceeding all current Internet traffic, and two powerful supercomputers will be created to process it. Of course, this is an international project. Many countries participate in it, primarily the UK, USA, EU countries, Australia, South Africa, New Zealand, China, India, Canada - more than 20 countries in total. The total cost of the project exceeds 1.5 billion euros. To date, approximately half of this amount has been spent. Unfortunately, Russia so far only has observer status in this project, although it was Soviet scientists who came up with the very idea of ​​ultra-long astronomical radio interferometers forty years ago. The first test of the very initial stage of this structure has already given remarkable results. Hundreds of previously unknown, very distant galaxies were discovered in the studied area of ​​the sky. The main purpose of these observations is to obtain data on the very young Universe, “only” a few million years old after the Big Bang, i.e. at the moment of formation of the first stars and galaxies.
