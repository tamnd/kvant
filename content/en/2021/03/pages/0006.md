The main idea in Einstein's report was the statement that gravity is a manifestation of the distortion of space-time by objects that have mass and (or) energy. As an astronomical confirmation of his hypothesis, Einstein made an approximate calculation of the observed shift in the perihelion of Mercury's orbit, which Newtonian theory could not explain. Schwarzschild was captivated by the mathematical beauty of the new theory of gravitation, and during the last year of his life he was able to complete several theoretical works. In two of them, the first exact solutions of the field equations of general relativity were obtained - the so-called internal solutions for a non-rotating massive spherical homogeneous body and the external solution for the space around a spherically symmetric body. From these decisions followed the possibility of the existence of an “invisible star.” This is why some terms from black hole theory are named after Schwarzschild. First of all, this is the Schwarzschild radius (or radius of the event horizon) - the conventional size of a spherical massive object, making it invisible. The most surprising thing is that this size exactly coincided with the value obtained from the classical expression for the second cosmic velocity, if we equate it to the speed of light c in vacuum: $R 2 GM c^{2}$, where M is the mass of the attracting object, and G is the gravitational constant. Einstein himself, although he approved of the appearance of these works, did not develop them further. The fact is that Schwarzschild’s solutions led to non-stationary behavior of matter inside the star, which became invisible, and to the appearance of a point with infinitely high density in its center - the so-called singularity. And Einstein believed that solutions to general relativity should be stationary and not allow singularity. But other scientists began to study the problem of the existence of objects with a theoretically possible infinite density at their centers. Thus, the problem of collapsars, as invisible stars came to be called, turned out to be closely related to the problem of the origin and development of the Universe. A great contribution to the theory of the birth of the Universe was made by G. Gamow (1904–1968), who coined the term “Big Bang” in relation to the emergence of the Universe from a point of singularity. The problem of solving the equations of general relativity as applied to collapsars attracted astrophysicists who were well versed in mathematics. Among them were astronomers F. Zwicky and W. Baade, physicists R. Oppenheimer and H. Snyder, astrophysicist S. Chandrasekhar. Russian scientists A. Cherepashchuk, Y. Zeldovich, I. Novikov, A. Starobinsky made a great contribution to solving this problem. In the 1960s, a number of papers were published in which the general relativity equations were solved for increasingly complex cases. New Zealand scientist R. Kerr obtained a solution for a rotating black hole of a spherical shape, and English astrophysicist R. Penrose solved the problem for a rotating black hole of arbitrary shape. It was then that the name “black hole” first appeared (J. Wheeler). And it instantly replaced all previous names. At this time, astronomers discovered pulsars - stellar objects of large mass but very small size; they began to be called neutron stars. And in 1963, for the first time, a suspicion arose that an outdated star after the supernova stage could turn not only into a neutron star, but also into a black hole (if it has enough mass).
