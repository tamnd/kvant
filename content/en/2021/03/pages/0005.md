This “Nobel” article will provide only the most basic information about black holes. Apparently, the first person to hypothesize the existence of black holes was the English naturalist and priest John Michell (1724–1793). He was inspired by Newton's theory of universal gravitation, especially by the fact that gravitation seemed to him (like Newton) "some most elusive divine spirit" (quoted from Newton's afterword to his Principia). Everything else that Newton studied had its own visible reasons - the springs were deformed under the influence of an external force, the cannonball flew under the influence of powder gases. But invisible and intangible gravity penetrates all bodies and makes them attract each other, no matter how far they are. In fact, Newton first introduced the concept of a force field. But little is known that, long before Faraday and Maxwell, he attributed this concept not only to gravity, but also to the attraction and repulsion of electrically charged bodies. In 1783, Michell sent a letter and then spoke at a meeting of the Royal Society of London with the following message. Knowing and understanding Newtonian mechanics and the law of universal gravitation well, Michell estimated the speed that must be given to a body in order to overcome the gravity of a planet (or star). He called this quantity escape velocity. In modern terms, this is the second cosmic speed. I knew that the speed of light is very high. According to the measurements of the astronomer O. Roemer, made back in 1676 as a result of observing the movement of Jupiter’s satellites, it is equal to 214,000 km s (a more accurate value is 300,000 km s). Michell equated this speed to the escape speed and calculated the mass and radius of an imaginary star, which would hold the light emitted by its gravity. Considering its density to be the same as the average density of the Earth, he obtained a value for the radius of this star of the order of 250 solar radii. And the fact that nothing like this is observed in the sky explains that she does not let go of the light from herself. And you need to look for it not by its glow, but by its effect on other stars and planets. But the scientific community did not accept Michell’s hypothesis, since the motion of planets (Kepler’s laws) had already been perfectly explained by Newton for all the then known planets and the influence of some huge body was not detected anywhere. 12 years later, the famous French mathematician, physicist and astronomer Pierre Simon Laplace (1749–1827), in his work “Exposition of the World System,” examined Michell’s hypothesis and, in conclusion, repeated his calculations. True, Laplace excluded this consideration from subsequent editions of this work. Unlike Michell (and Newton), Laplace considered light not to be particles, but waves, and understood that the law of universal gravitation cannot be so simply applied to light. Throughout the 19th century, astronomers were not interested in the problem of the “invisible stars” of Michell and Laplace. And only in the 20th century, after Einstein created the general theory of relativity (GTR), astronomers remembered them. The first was the German mathematician, physicist and astronomer Karl Schwarzschild (1873–1916). In 1915, he attended an epoch-making meeting of the Prussian Academy of Sciences, where Einstein gave a presentation on his just completed work (he spent six years on it) - the general theory of relativity.
