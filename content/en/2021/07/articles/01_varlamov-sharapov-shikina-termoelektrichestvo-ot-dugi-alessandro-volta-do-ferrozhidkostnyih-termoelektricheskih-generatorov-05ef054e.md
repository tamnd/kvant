Thermoelectricity:

from Alessandro Volta's arc to ferrofluid thermoelectric generators

A. VARLAMOV, S. SHARAPOV, Y. SHIKINA

It is difficult to surprise a modern person with questions about what electricity is or how heat transfer occurs - everyone has their own opinion on these matters. Some are theoretical, based on the study of physics, while the majority remember the unforgettable sensations of an electric shock in the process of repairing an outlet when the voltage on the panel is not turned off or from accidentally touching a hot iron. But, of course, a much smaller number of readers are familiar with the phenomenon of thermoelectricity. In ancient Greek mythology, an important place is occupied by centaurs - wild, mortal creatures with the head and torso of a man on the body of a horse, inhabitants of mountains and forest thickets. The phenomenon of thermoelectricity is like a centaur - here the electrical voltage between the edges of the sample arises as a result of its uneven heating.

The debate between Luigi Galvani and Alessandro Volta The history of the discovery and subsequent study of thermoelectricity goes back more than two centuries and begins with a debate between Luigi Galvani and Alessandro Volta. In the mid-18th century, the study of electrical phenomena, including animal electricity, such as that generated by electric stingrays, became a popular topic in science. In view of this, physiologist Luigi Galvani, a professor at the University of Bologna, began to study what happens to a dissected frog when a current is passed through it. As a result, many

Through numerical experiments, Galvani was convinced that every time he touched the nerves of a frog with a conductor connected to an electric machine and extracted a spark from it, the frog was seized with convulsive trembling. Galvani then continued his experiments and discovered the same contractions when connecting the muscles and nerves of a newly prepared frog with a metal arc (Fig.1). At the same time, Galvani's special attention was attracted by the fact that the contraction of the frog's muscles turns out to be much stronger if the metal arc is composed in series of two different metals: for example, iron and copper (or, which is much better, silver). These experiments led the famous Italian to the conclusion that electricity is inherent in the animal itself: a fluid flows from the nerves to the muscles and a circuit is formed (as when a Leyden jar is discharged).

Professor at the University of Pavia, physicist and chemist Alessandro Volta, was distrustful of so-called animal electricity. And yet, at the insistence of his colleagues, he repeats the experiments described in Galvani’s work (Fig.2). As a result, his skepticism began to dissipate, and in $3 апреля^{1792}$ he writes to Galvani: “So, here I am at last converted. Since I myself became an eyewitness and observed these miracles, I, perhaps, have moved from distrust to fanaticism.” In a public lecture in $5 мая^{1792}$, Volta talks about Galvani’s experiments, extols them, but at the same time expresses the idea that the frog can only be a measuring device, an electrometer, tens of times more sensitive than the long-existing electrometer with gold leaves. Volta persistently draws attention to one physical feature of Galvani's experiments: in order to cause muscle contraction, the metals forming the arc must be different. He asks: Do metal conductors really serve only to, by touching each other, establish a connection, providing the electrical fluid with a path along which it naturally tends to pass from one place to another? In his earliest experiments, Volta shows that the muscle, in essence, does not participate in the creation of the phenomenon itself; its contraction is the effect of the flow of charge generated by the contact of two different metals. Thus, in fact, he discovers a phenomenon that is associated with a contact potential difference. Volta goes further in his doubts. He tries to use a single metal conductor as an arc, but places the ends of the frog muscle and the ends of the arc in vessels with cold and hot water (Fig.3). The muscle also contracts in this experiment, which allows the scientist to explain the flow of electrical fluid (charge) by uneven heating of the metal conductor. The connection between electric current and temperature difference has been discovered! In the process of experiments with a metal arc, Volta realized that the passage of current through a frog muscle is nothing more than its flow in an acidic environment. As a result, he created his famous battery, the so-called voltaic column - a sequence of alternating plates of two metals (for example, lead and copper), separated by a cloth soaked in acid (vinegar). Batteries still operate on the same principle today.

The further fate of Seebeck's discovery Thermoelectricity quickly found its application, at least in physics laboratories. Thus, the well-known Ohm's law was discovered and described by Georg Simon Ohm in two $статьях,^{1826} и^{1827}$ years. After the publication of the first article, Ohm was advised to abandon the galvanic battery, since the electromotive force of such a source changed significantly during the measurement. More accurate measurement results for the second article have already been obtained through the use of a thermoelectric current source (copper-bismuth pair) capable of maintaining a constant voltage. Further research into the phenomenon discovered by Seebeck continued $в^{1830-е}$ years in the works of Jean Peltier, where the inverse thermoelectric effect was discovered: heating or cooling of the junction of two conductors when an electric current flows through it. Its essence was explored a few years later by a Russian physicist of German origin, one of the founders of electrical engineering, Heinrich Friedrich Emil Lenz. He placed a drop of water in a recess at the junction of two rods of bismuth and antimony (with resistance R) and discovered that when an electric current (I) flowed in one direction, the drop turned into ice, and when the direction changed to the opposite, the ice melted. Until the mid-1930s, there was no understanding of devices and metals at the microscopic level. To create an appropriate theory, it was necessary to at least first understand the structure of their constituent atoms, and the mathematical and conceptual apparatus necessary for this - quantum mechanics - was formulated only in the late 20s. However, at the phenomenological level, understanding of the phenomenon of thermoelectricity has advanced. In the second half of the 19th century, in the works of Gibbs, Boltzmann and other scientists, a new field of physics developed - statistical mechanics, which studies systems of a large number of particles. The ability to describe their properties using classical mechanics is illusory: it is not possible not only to solve, but even to write down the equations of classical mechanics for a large number of particles. In return, scientists discover completely new, so-called statistical patterns, for such systems. They understand that for the necessary description of the properties, unnecessary information (coordinates, velocities of all particles) is simply not needed. You should learn to ask Nature new questions and understand how to get answers to them. Among other new concepts, Josiah Willard Gibbs $в^{1875}$ introduces the chemical potential P - the energy that must be expended to add one more particle to a system of a large number of particles. Under equilibrium conditions, this value remains constant throughout the entire volume of the system - otherwise particles would flow from one place to another. The concept of chemical potential is easy to generalize to the case of a system being in an external field, by which we will further mean an electric field with potential M. A few words about applications Thermoelectric devices have a wide range of applications, ranging from power generation to thermoelectric radiation receivers. The reliability and simplicity of thermoelectric devices allows them to be used even when traditional energy sources are more efficient. In addition to thermocouples, devices operating on the principle of the Peltier effect are quite widely used: coolers for various products, ranging from optoelectronics, small refrigerators, cooling systems for heated seats in cars. A semiconductor pair is usually used as a working material in such devices. $Bi_{2} Te_{3} –Sb_{2} Te_{3}$. there could be a thermoelectric battery that works due to the difference in water temperatures at different depths. Modern nuclear submarines, as we know, use more traditional steam turbines. But spacecraft that are sent into the depths of space actually have thermoelectric energy sources. The fact is that outside the planet Mars, sunlight is too weak to power the spacecraft with solar panels. Therefore, the electrical energy necessary for the operation of on-board systems is provided by converting the heat released during the radioactive decay of $Pu^{238}$ into electricity using thermoelectric couples. Such radioisotope thermoelectric generators have been used by NASA on various missions such as Apollo, Pioneer, Viking, Voyager, Galileo and Cassini. Voyager's power supplies, with no moving parts, are still working, allowing the spacecraft to continue scientific exploration after more than 35 years in service. A radioisotope thermoelectric source of electricity weighing 45 kg, producing a power of $в^{110}$ W, is also on the Perseverance rover, which recently began studying Mars.
