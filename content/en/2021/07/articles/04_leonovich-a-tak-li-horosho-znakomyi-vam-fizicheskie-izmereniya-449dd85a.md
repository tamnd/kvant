Are you so familiar with physical measurements?

How much paramount importance were given to measurements by outstanding scientists! Indeed, measurements are an integral attribute of physics. From the very first steps of its study at school, we are introduced to such terms as unit of measurement, division value, error, the difference between the procedures of direct and indirect measurements is explained, and we are taught to formalize the results of laboratory work. True, to some, laboratory work seems unnecessary and boring, but to many, who are inclined to work not only with their heads, but also with their hands, there is clearly not enough of them at school. Let's try, at least partly, to make up for this deficiency and carry out several measurements, sometimes using what is called improvised means. Perhaps, despite all the apparent simplicity, these practical actions will encourage you to move further in mastering the concept of “measurement”. Then we are waiting for you in the next issue of Kaleidoscope.

Measurement tasks

1. First measure your height using a ruler, marking it on the door frame, then measure the distance between the ends of the fingers of your outstretched hands, using the wall. Compare your measurements. What will you notice?

2. Extend your straight arm in front of you and, spreading your index and middle fingers, form the letter V. This way, amateur astronomers can measure angles of interest to them. Why? 3. There is a closed matchbox on a rectangular table. Can you use a ruler and pencil to measure the distance between its two most distant points? 4. How to determine the circumference (and therefore the diameter) of a soccer ball using a wooden ruler? 5. Is it possible to find the volume of one specific bean from a pile if you only have a beaker? 6. A closed, thin-walled, rectangular bottle with a flat bottom is partially filled with water. How can you determine its capacity without opening the bottle and having only a ruler? 7. Try to find the internal volume of an irregularly shaped vessel using scales and a set of weights. 8. Can you (following Archimedes) weigh an elephant? 9. How to determine using a dynamometer the mass of a cube whose weight is greater than the measurement limit of the dynamometer, but not more than twice?

It's interesting that...

... the accuracy and reliability of measurements has been monitored since ancient times. Thus, in the charter of Prince Vsevolod (XII century) punishments for violators of the unity of measurements were mentioned, up to and including the death penalty. ... the inhabitants of Ancient Egypt had measuring ropes broken into pieces, which they used as rulers and compasses. With their help, the Egyptians could approximately measure the length of any segment or the size of any angle. ... various, albeit approximate, measurements can be performed literally with bare hands, if you know your own dimensions: the width of the nail of some finger, the length of the middle joint of the index, the distance between the ends of the thumb and the little finger of the outstretched hand, “your” meter - from the end of the outstretched arm to the edge of the other shoulder, and so on. ... accurate temperature measurements carried out by J. Joule in multiple experiments made it possible to establish a connection between mechanical work, heat and energy changes that had not been obvious for a long time. All these physical quantities today have a common unit of measurement - the joule.

... thermometers and barometers, originally based on the same physical principles - the equation of state of gas - were practically indistinguishable from each other. By the way, the famous humorist Mark Twain was the first to draw the attention of physicists to this circumstance. ... according to the theory of relativity, when bodies move uniformly, their lengths shorten and the rhythm in which all processes occur slows down. However, these changes can only be measured using instruments at rest. As soon as the instruments begin to move along with the objects of observation, they turn out to be “powerless” to notice such changes. ... measurements of the speed of sound in air carried out at the end of the 20th century required important amendments to be made to reference books. The fact is that in previous experiments uncertainties were allowed that did not take into account the latest values ​​of constants and parameters describing the properties of air, as well as dubious results of converting units of measurement from one system to another. ... modern measurement methods have unusually expanded the range of studied quantities. Thus, today it is possible to find distances that differ from each other by about 40 orders of magnitude - from intergalactic scales to the sizes of elementary particles.

But... let's get back to the birds. I was always taught that Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks were replete with ingenious insights. And recently I finally decided to read these books, which, of course, have long been published by reputable academic publishing houses. In “Selected Works” of Leonardo da Vinci (M.: Publishing House of the USSR Academy of Sciences, 1955) there is a chapter “On the flight and movement of bodies in the air.” This is a detailed study with many interesting observations and wonderful figure. But understanding it is very difficult, sometimes impossible. After all, Leonardo wrote for himself, not caring about explaining the meaning of the terms used. This chapter includes a section on “Why do migratory birds fly against the current of the wind?” I couldn’t figure it out in this text, but the conclusion is obviously not correct. The pictures show how birds take off - indeed always against the wind. And when they are already high, you can’t see which way the wind is blowing. It can be assumed that this led to the error. It should be noted that Leonardo constantly refers to the laws of statics discovered by Archimedes (he studied Archimedes from the complete works, which, as is known from his notebooks, was available to him). But statics are not enough to study flight. But dynamics as a science did not yet exist, and Leonardo, where there was a lack of knowledge, constantly used intuitive guesses. So, all his life he tried to create an aircraft, but he failed. It took four centuries of development of science and technology for man to finally take to the air. So, I think I have guessed where biologists got the erroneous idea that tailwinds prevent birds from flying. It originated from the works of Leonardo da Vinci. And this idea persisted in the minds of some people because in their knowledge of physics these people had not passed the era of Galileo. But let’s not blame Leonardo for not being ahead of the genius who followed him...
