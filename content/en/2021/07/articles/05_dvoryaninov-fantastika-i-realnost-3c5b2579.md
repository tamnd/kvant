Fiction and reality

S. DVORYANINOV

Do you remember how in the book by N.N. Nosov’s “Dunno on the Moon,” did its characters represent the structure of the Moon? They considered the Moon to be hollow, and therefore Znayka dreamed of new travels: “It would be nice to build a large interplanetary ship, take a significant supply of food and air and arrange a long expedition to the Moon. We must assume that in the outer shell of the Moon there are holes in the form of caves or craters of extinct volcanoes. Through these holes it will be possible to penetrate inside the Moon and see its central core. If this core exists, and it undoubtedly does, then the lunar shorts live on its surface. There is probably a sufficient amount of air preserved between the outer shell and the central core of the Moon, so living conditions on the surface of the core should be quite favorable for short people.” But to live on such a Moon, the short ones would certainly need energy. How can you get it? Let's continue Znayka's fantasies. Each body of mass m located at a height h above the Earth's surface has potential energy $W_{=}^{mgh}$. Likewise, each stone on the outer shell of the Moon has potential energy relative to the lunar core. They loaded the stone into a basket with a rope, and wound the rope around the rotor of the electric generator - and that’s it! The basket went down, the rotor spun, and electrical energy flowed through the wires where it was needed. (Weights also operate on the same principle.

Of course, one basket is not enough; several baskets must be placed on a ring belt - like a regular conveyor (the steps of an escalator in the subway are fixed in a similar way). You will say: Znayka, Dunno, the hollow Moon - this is not serious, children's fantasies! But no. What has been said about the use of potential energy has long been implemented in the design of water funiculars for lifting people up the mountain. Only in this case, instead of stones, water is used, and instead of a basket for stones, a tank under the cable car is used. Like a regular cable car, two water cable cars are connected by a cable. At the top of the mountain, water is poured into a special tank of one of the trailers, and below, water is poured out of the tank of another trailer. In this case, the balance is disturbed, and the upper car outweighs the lower one. Then the brakes are released, and the trailers move: the heavy one with water goes down, the light one without water goes up. That's it. The first such funiculars were built almost a century and a half ago, some are still in operation. So, is it a perpetual motion machine? Of course not. If the water runs out at the top of the mountain, the funicular will have nothing to fuel it with. But it turns out that the water at the top is due to the energy of the Sun - the water on the ground evaporates, rises up and falls in the form of rain or snow on the top of the mountain.

A difficult example In the end, let's look at such a difficult problem from the All-Russian Olympiad, in which several classical plots are intertwined at once: 6. In triangle ABC (AB BC), point I is the center of the inscribed circle, M is the midpoint of side AC, N is the midpoint of the arc ABC of the circumscribed circle (Fig.15). Prove that IMA INB. The median IM of triangle AIC is immediately visible. What about the IN segment? In which triangle could the segment IN be the median? A hint to the desired direction of search can be the fact that BN is the outer bisector of angle B of triangle ABC, because point N is the midpoint of arc ABC. Therefore, let's supplement figure with the centers of excircles of triangle ABC - points $I_{a}, I_{b}, I_{c}$ (Fig.16). Then points A, B and C in triangle $I_{a} I_{b} I_{c}$ are the bases of the altitudes (why?). Consequently, the circumcircle of triangle ABC in triangle $I_{a} I_{b} I_{c}$ passes through the bases of the altitudes, i.e. is the circle of nine points of this triangle. As you know, the circle of nine points also passes through the midpoints of the sides of the triangle. Whence we conclude that point N is the midpoint of the segment $I_{a} I_{c}$. Now we have two similar triangles AIC and $I_{a} II_{c}$, whose medians IM and IN give the desired equality of angles. Having read the decision above, the reader will most likely rightly be indignant: “How could anyone have guessed such a decision?” To this we note that this task, difficult in itself, served as an impetus for the creation of a separate effective and efficient method for solving problems, which is the subject of a fascinating article [3].
