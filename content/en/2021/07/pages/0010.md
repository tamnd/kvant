Thermoelectricity:

from Alessandro Volta's arc to ferrofluid thermoelectric generators

A. VARLAMOV, S. SHARAPOV, Y. SHIKINA

It is difficult to surprise a modern person with questions about what electricity is or how heat transfer occurs - everyone has their own opinion on these matters. Some are theoretical, based on the study of physics, while the majority remember the unforgettable sensations of an electric shock in the process of repairing an outlet when the voltage on the panel is not turned off or from accidentally touching a hot iron. But, of course, a much smaller number of readers are familiar with the phenomenon of thermoelectricity. In ancient Greek mythology, an important place is occupied by centaurs - wild, mortal creatures with the head and torso of a man on the body of a horse, inhabitants of mountains and forest thickets. The phenomenon of thermoelectricity is like a centaur - here the electrical voltage between the edges of the sample arises as a result of its uneven heating.

The debate between Luigi Galvani and Alessandro Volta The history of the discovery and subsequent study of thermoelectricity goes back more than two centuries and begins with a debate between Luigi Galvani and Alessandro Volta. In the mid-18th century, the study of electrical phenomena, including animal electricity, such as that generated by electric stingrays, became a popular topic in science. Through numerical experiments, Galvani was convinced that every time he touched the nerves of a frog with a conductor connected to an electric machine and extracted a spark from it, the frog was seized with convulsive trembling. Galvani then continued his experiments and discovered the same contractions when connecting the muscles and nerves of a newly prepared frog with a metal arc (Fig.1). At the same time, Galvani's special attention was attracted by the fact that the contraction of the frog's muscles turns out to be much stronger if the metal arc is composed in series of two different metals: for example, iron and copper (or, which is much better, silver). These experiments led the famous Italian to the conclusion that electricity is inherent in the animal itself: a fluid flows from the nerves to the muscles and a circuit is formed (as when a Leyden jar is discharged).
