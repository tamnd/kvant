Does a tailwind interfere with birds?

N. KONSTANTINOV

When I had already learned to read, I came across a book about travelers - I forgot which book exactly, but I remembered only one episode from it. The travelers set up camp in the forest next to a lake, on which a flock of wild geese landed to rest during the migration. Evening came and it was no longer possible to hunt. “Tomorrow there will be a glorious hunt,” said one. – Don’t you think that the birds will fly away at dawn? - said another. “They won’t fly away, because the north wind is favorable for the geese.” Birds do not fly with a tailwind, as it blows under the flight feathers of their wings. This conversation seemed strange to me. If I were the birds, I would rejoice at the tailwind - you can fly fast on it, faster than the wind. But I was little, and everything that was written in the books was true for me. So all I remembered was that I didn't understand something. Twenty-five years have passed. I studied in graduate school, my supervisor was Alexey Andreevich Lyapunov, a famous mathematician who at that time was deeply interested in cybernetics and mathematical approaches to biology. He was connected by scientific interests and personal friendship with our major biophysicist, one of the founders of the science of biophysics - Nikolai Vladimirovich Timofeev-Resovsky. On the advice of Alexey Andreevich, I attended a seminar on biophysics conducted by Nikolai Vladimirovich at the Miassovo biological station in the Urals. childhood book. It turns out that there was a time (and I just found it) when the same idea was repeated from article to article, from book to book - that a fair wind blows under the wings of birds. And a group of biologists, including Timofeev-Resovsky, in several publications explained to biologists the fallacy of this idea and talked about Galileo’s principle of relativity. Another episode reminded me that some high school students in our time have not yet matured to understand the picture of the world that arose after the discoveries of Galileo. A construction team of students and schoolchildren worked in the summer at the White Sea Biological Station of Moscow State University (I was already a teacher). We were returning from one sea excursion on the ship “Nauchny”. A group of guys asked the captain for permission to sit not on the ship, but in a boat that was towed behind the ship (it was much more pleasant there). And behind the boat, at a distance of about three meters from it, was another small skiff, in which no one was sitting. And so the youngest of us, Lesha Kuvshinov, who then entered the tenth grade, wanted to transfer to a skiff. And to do this on the go was, in my opinion, impossible. At least, it was very difficult: even if you pulled the skiff up to the boat, it was unthinkable to transfer to it without capsizing. But Lesha came up with another way: “I’ll jump high, and while I’m going down, the skiff will already be under me.” And then all the high school students (and these were all math school students, and they are not to be trifled with) began vying with each other to explain Galileo’s principle of relativity to Lesha. The earth, they said, can be considered an inertial frame of reference. This means that if no external forces act on the body (or, more precisely, if all the forces acting on the body compensate each other), then it maintains a state of rest or uniform rectilinear motion relative to the Earth.
