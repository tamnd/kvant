The further fate of Seebeck's discovery Thermoelectricity quickly found its application, at least in physics laboratories. Thus, the well-known Ohm's law was discovered and described by Georg Simon Ohm in two $статьях,^{1826} и^{1827}$ years. After the publication of the first article, Ohm was advised to abandon the galvanic battery, since the electromotive force of such a source changed significantly during the measurement. Further research into the phenomenon discovered by Seebeck continued $в^{1830-е}$ years in the works of Jean Peltier, where the inverse thermoelectric effect was discovered: heating or cooling of the junction of two conductors when an electric current flows through it. Its essence was explored a few years later by a Russian physicist of German origin, one of the founders of electrical engineering, Heinrich Friedrich Emil Lenz. He placed a drop of water in a recess at the junction of two rods of bismuth and antimony (with resistance R) and discovered that when an electric current (I) flowed in one direction, the drop turned into ice, and when the direction changed to the opposite, the ice melted. Until the mid-1930s, there was no understanding of devices and metals at the microscopic level. To create an appropriate theory, it was necessary to at least first understand the structure of their constituent atoms, and the mathematical and conceptual apparatus necessary for this - quantum mechanics - was formulated only in the late 20s. However, at the phenomenological level, understanding of the phenomenon of thermoelectricity has advanced. In the second half of the 19th century, in the works of Gibbs, Boltzmann and other scientists, a new field of physics developed - statistical mechanics, which studies systems of a large number of particles. The ability to describe their properties using classical mechanics is illusory: it is not possible not only to solve, but even to write down the equations of classical mechanics for a large number of particles. In return, scientists discover completely new, so-called statistical patterns, for such systems. They understand that for the necessary description of the properties, unnecessary information (coordinates, velocities of all particles) is simply not needed. You should learn to ask Nature new questions and understand how to get answers to them. Among other new concepts, Josiah Willard Gibbs $в^{1875}$ introduces the chemical potential P - the energy that must be expended to add one more particle to a system of a large number of particles. Under equilibrium conditions, this value remains constant throughout the entire volume of the system - otherwise particles would flow from one place to another. The concept of chemical potential is easy to generalize to the case of a system being in an external field, by which we will further mean an electric field with potential M.
