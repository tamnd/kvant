The student sat down on a stone and indulged in heavy thoughts about the vicissitudes of life. “What is that floating there in the sea?” - thought the student. “No, it doesn’t float, but, perhaps, stands still.” I took a closer look. “Yes, this is my backpack!” – the student guessed. Luckily he was wearing boots - he walked to his backpack in shallow water. The student forgot that there are tides in the sea. But he studied this at school, and perhaps even got an A for a perfectly memorized lesson. But the fact of the matter is that you can memorize it and not think about it. Another case is again on the White Sea. A group of tourists went for a walk, and one of them, Sacha Codreanu, stayed by the fire to make tea. He was a very smart schoolboy who had just won first prize at the All-Union Mathematical Olympiad. He went to the well, and next to it was the sea - the water in it was so clear and played so beautifully in the sun. And he filled a bucket with sea water. He forgot that the water in the sea is salty, but he probably knew about it. But this was knowledge for marks, not for life. The (salty) water boiled just in time for the tired group to return. It turns out that school knowledge may be useless. This happens if they do not connect with life observations. A person must have two “schools” - one on the street and at home, the other at school, and they must be connected. But this doesn't always happen. Here is a man walking through the city, he is fifteen years old, and he has one hundred percent vision. But he doesn't see anything. He did not notice that pigeons and crows are different birds. I didn’t notice that trolleybuses have horns on the roof (they are scientifically called pantographs). He had never seen a rainbow, nor had he noticed that cumulus clouds usually have flat bottom surfaces. I did not make all this up - these are the results of observations. I once asked my circle members whether it happens that the Moon and the Sun are visible in the sky at the same time. One said that he saw it once, the rest did not notice anything like that, and some were even surprised that the Moon could be seen during the day.

But... let's get back to the birds. I was always taught that Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks were replete with ingenious insights. And recently I finally decided to read these books, which, of course, have long been published by reputable academic publishing houses. In “Selected Works” of Leonardo da Vinci (M.: Publishing House of the USSR Academy of Sciences, 1955) there is a chapter “On the flight and movement of bodies in the air.” This is a detailed study with many interesting observations and wonderful figure. But understanding it is very difficult, sometimes impossible. After all, Leonardo wrote for himself, not caring about explaining the meaning of the terms used. This chapter includes a section on “Why do migratory birds fly against the current of the wind?” I couldn’t figure it out in this text, but the conclusion is obviously not correct. The pictures show how birds take off - indeed always against the wind. And when they are already high, you can’t see which way the wind is blowing. It can be assumed that this led to the error. It should be noted that Leonardo constantly refers to the laws of statics discovered by Archimedes (he studied Archimedes from the complete works, which, as is known from his notebooks, was available to him). But statics are not enough to study flight. But dynamics as a science did not yet exist, and Leonardo, where there was a lack of knowledge, constantly used intuitive guesses. So, all his life he tried to create an aircraft, but he failed. It took four centuries of development of science and technology for man to finally take to the air. So, I think I have guessed where biologists got the erroneous idea that tailwinds prevent birds from flying. It originated from the works of Leonardo da Vinci. And this idea persisted in the minds of some people because in their knowledge of physics these people had not passed the era of Galileo. But let’s not blame Leonardo for not being ahead of the genius who followed him...
