4. Let the point $O$ have the indicated property for the convex polygon $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$. It is easy to see that the point $O$ cannot lie either outside the polygon or on any of its sides: it must be its interior point.

Let $P$ be an arbitrary point on the contour of the polygon. Since the point $O$ is internal, the line $PQ$ must intersect the contour of the polygon again; Let's denote this intersection point by $M$. Let us show that $OP=OM$. Assume that $OP>OM$ (otherwise we place the point notation). Let us reflect the point $M$ (and the part of the polygon adjacent to it) relative to the point $O$, we obtain the point $M_1$. Let’s take next to point $M$ (on the contour of the polygon) point $N$ and reflect it relative to point $O$, we get point $N_1$. Let line $NO$ intersect the polygon a second time at point $Q$. Then we will explain what “nearby” means. From figure 2 it is clear that the areas of triangles $OMN$ and $OM_1N_1$ are equal and less than the area of ​​triangle $OPQ$. But if any line drawn through the point $O$ divides the polygon into two equal-velocity parts, then the areas of triangles $OPQ$ and $OMN$ must be equal. The resulting contradiction proves that $OP=OM$ for any points $P$ and $M$ of the polygon lying on the same straight line passing through the point $O$, that is, $O$ is the center of the symmetric polygon.

All of the above is true if the point $N$ can be chosen so that $M_1N_1$ does not intersect with $PQ$. So, “nearby” means that this condition is met. It is enough, for example, to reflect the polygon relative to point $O$, find the intersection point $K$ closest to $P$, the contour of the polygon and the reflected part and select point $Q$ even closer (and already at point $Q$ there are $N$ and $N_1$).
