Gas viscosity

We have described in some detail why, when a body moves through a gas, a force acts on it - the force of friction - the result of the impacts of individual molecules on the surface of the moving body. At the same time, we were not at all interested in what happens to the gas, to its molecules after the collision. Let us think for a moment about the fate of the molecules that come into contact with the body. It is clear that if we had the opportunity to change the speed of all molecules interacting with a body, we would be convinced that their average speed is not equal to zero. Molecures, albeit slightly, are carried away by a moving body. And if you change the speed of molecules far from the body, then (in the absence of finite wind, their average speed is zero). In slightly different words: near the surface of a moving body there is an ordered movement of gas, but far from the body there is none. There is a certain impulse associated with ordered movement. A layer of gas in the surface layer collides more often with a moving body than other gas molecules. Because of this, their average momentum would have to increase if there were no process of momentum removal from the boundary layer.

This process is easy to understand. Gas molecules that escape from the boundary layer simply carry away excess momentum with them. Far from the body, they “disperse” it in multiple collisions with other molecules or in collisions with bodies at rest.

The ability of a gas to transfer impulse from one place to another and, most importantly, to waste it irreversibly is called viscousness. Viscosity and friction are the same. Viscosity is an interesting phenomenon in which collisions between molecules play a major role. We neglected when calculating the friction force acting on the moving body from the gas side.

We were wrong about this. But how? Did you exaggerate or underestimate the friction force? It's easy to understand that they exaggerated. Indeed, we believed that a molecule, once in contact with a body, would never return to it and, therefore, would not be able to “return” to the body the impulse it received. In fact, if we carefully monitor many molecules colliding with the body, we will be convinced that some of them return to the body (after colliding with any of the gas molecules), others give the excess implus acquired from the body to their collide partners, and they, in turn, can collide with the body. Strictly speaking, as a result of such new molecules interacting with each other, a partial return of the body’s impulse occurs, which means that the body loses less impulse than we thought.

The formula derived here is applicable only to very rarefied gases, in which the average stretch between stolnov molecules is much greater than the size of the body. If we calculate the friction force acting on dust particles with dimensions not exceeding several microns, our air may also fit into the category of rarefied gases.
