Simple case

There are cases when the calculation of siliprene is relatively simple and does not require a deep penetration into the theory of solids.

Let, for example, a solid body move through a fairly rarefied gas. Then silipren is caused by collisions of gas atoms with the surface of the body.

Before proceeding to the calculation of silipren, we will consider a separate act of collision. A particle with momentum $P$ falls on the surface of the body. The collision of an atom with a surface is a complex event. Let's think about what could happen. Atom can remain on the surface (float), can penetrate deeply into the body, or can be reflected from the surface of the body. With reflection (we will be interested in this particular silipran, we will explain how much later) various situations are also possible. For example, an Atom can innise or be knocked out of an atom from a surface, a molecule can disintegrate into its constituent atoms, and so on. In short, a lot will appear, but we will assume that atom was simply reflected, like a ball from a wall. Not for simplification, although this situation is indeed the simplest, but because in the overwhelming majority of silipevs, when a body moves through a gas not too quickly, this is exactly what happens - the atoms are simply reflected (bounced off) from the surface of the body *). Large complex situations (the introduction of atoms into a solid body, nucleation, etc.), of course, are also observed and studied in detail. Each of these processes occurs with most probability at a certain particle speed. For example, the higher the particle speed, the greater the likelihood that an atom will penetrate to a relatively greater depth into a solid body. To imagine what speeds we are talking about, we note: an oxygen atom incident on the surface of a solid body at a speed of $10^7$ cm/sec**) penetrates approximately 10-12 atomic distances into the depth of the solid body. The average speed of $v$ atoms of a gas with a mass $m$, as shown by the molecular-kinetic theory of gases, is equal to $\sqrt{\frac{kT}{m}}$, where $T$ is the temperature in absolute units, and $k$ is the so-called Boltzmann constant, equal to $1,4 \cdot 10^{-23}$ J/sec. At $T = 300^\circ$ K ($=27^\circ$ C) the average speed of an oxygen atom is less than $10^5$ cm/sec. If a body accumulates in a gas, then the average speed of gas molecules with the surface of the body approximately coincides with the average body speed; if the body moves, it differs from it. However, in the case when the speed of a moving body is small compared to the average solid speed (you should never go through the minimum partial silipevs, in which the question under study looks simpler), the speed of the moving body is almost no different from the solid body, and the energy of the atom is simply not enough to penetrate into the depths of the solid body.
