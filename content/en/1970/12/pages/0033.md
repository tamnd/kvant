ABOUT THE ORDIBAL PERIOD OF SATELLITES

In September 1970, the automatic station “Luna 16” was launched. On September 17, it entered a lunar orbit with a period of 1 hour 59 minutes (119 minutes). What can be said about the Moon based on these data?

Let us remember that when a satellite is launched around the Earth, its period is usually about one and a half hours (90 minutes). What can you say about the Earth?

It turns out that from these data it is possible to estimate the densities of the Moon and Earth.

If the height of the satellite is small (compared to the radius of the celestial body around which it rotates), then we can approximately assume that the satellite is moving near the surface. Let us equate the acceleration of gravity to the centripetal acceleration:

$$G \frac{M}{R^2} = \frac{v^2}{R} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 R,$$

where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ is the mass of the celestial body, $R$ is its radius, $v$ is the speed of the satellite, $T$ is its period of revolution. Next we write the mass in terms of the radius:

$$M = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \rho,$$

where $\rho$ is the average density.

Substituting this expression into the previous formula, we get a remarkable result:

$$T = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{\rho G}}.$$

It follows that the orbital period of a low satellite depends only on the size of the celestial body and does not depend on its size. Therefore, knowing the period, you can determine the density of the Moon and Earth. The table shows the period and density values ​​for different planets of the solar system.

| Central body | Density ($\rho/cm^3$) | Period |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sun | 1,42 | 166 |
| Mercury | 5,61 | 83 |
| Venus | 5,16 | 87 |
| Earth | 5,52 | 84 |
| Moon | 3,36 | 108 |
| Mars | 3,95 | 100 |
| Jupiter | 1,34 | 171 |
| Saturn | 0,64 | 238 |
| Uranus | 1,36 | 170 |
| Neptune | 1,30 | 174 |

For Earth, the period is 84 minutes. In gases, a slightly larger overall value is reported. This means that the satellite is not flying very low. If we denote the height of the satellite by $h$, then it is not difficult to obtain the exact formula for a circular orbit (try to derive it yourself):

$$T = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{\rho G}} \left(1 + \frac{h}{R}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}} \approx T_0 \left(1 + \frac{3h}{2R}\right),$$

where $T_0$ is the period of the low satellite (given in the table), and the last equality is an approximate one - true if $h \ll R$. You can also obtain a formula for an elliptical orbit if you know Kepler's laws. You will read about them in «Kvant» No. 1 for 1971.

Y. A. Smorodinsky
