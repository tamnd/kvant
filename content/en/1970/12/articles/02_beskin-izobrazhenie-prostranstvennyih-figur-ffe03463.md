1. Parallel Projection Method

Unfortunately, there is no “spatial pencil”, the tip of which would leave a mark in what has arisen. With such a pencil it was possible to “draw” a real cube by tracing its edges. A flat image cannot be an exact copy of a spatial figure. A problem arises: what rules should be used to construct an image so that it is similar to the original, that is, so that when viewing the image, a visual impression arises that is close to that which occurs when viewing the original? Most often, he uses design methods for this purpose. The simplest of them are the method of central projections and the method of parallel projections.

Fig. 1.

The method of central projections is clear from figure 1. A fixed point $S$ (the center by the projection) and a fixed plane l (the plane by the projection) not passing through $S$ are taken. Through any point $A'$ of space a line $SA'$ (projecting line) is drawn. The point $A$ of intersection of this line with the plane l is called the project point $A'$. figure 1 shows the projections of two points: $A'$ and $B'$.

Original points lying on the same projection line have the same image.

The method of central projections gives the most straightforward images, because the described construction reproduces the process of vision: the rays coming from the original point ($A', B', \dots$) or from their project ($A, B, \dots$) coincide. Thus, the eye doesn’t care whether it looks at the original or its projection onto the plane. (True, we are simplifying somewhat here: after all, the original is viewed with two eyes.)

Artists mainly use the method of central projections.

Theorem 2. If a given image of a triangle is $A'B'C'$, then the image of each point lying in the plane of this triangle is thereby uniquely determined.

Proof. Let $A'B'C'$ be the original (Fig. 5, a), and $ABC$ be the image (Fig. 5, b). This is where the arbitrariness ends: images of all other points must be constructed. In other words, a given triangle can be drawn arbitrarily, but a given quadrangle cannot be drawn.

When solving stereometric problems, the drawing plays a supporting role, and there is no point in spending more work on drawing it than on solving the problem itself. Therefore, we recommend that the reader draw mainly by eye. However, we will give some general recommendations, the implementation of which will help you avoid mistakes.

The image of a ball is more complex than that of polyhedra, a cylinder and a cone. Therefore, it is recommended to carry out drawings taking into account the ball, starting with the ball, and then attaching the remaining figures to it.
