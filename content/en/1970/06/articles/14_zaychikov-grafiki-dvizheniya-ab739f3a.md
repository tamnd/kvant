Mechanical motion is very convenient to depict on graphs. Their simplicity and clarity make it possible to immediately assess the nature of the movement at its various stages and solve some problems without resorting to calculations.

On university entrance exams, graphs are often used in additional questions. Therefore, you need to have a good understanding of graphs and be able to analyze them.

Speed ​​graph

Let us first consider linear motion. The straight line along which the body moves is used as the coordinate axis $x$. On it we will select the starting point $O$ and determine the position of the body by coordinate $x$, counting from this zero point in one direction with the plane sign, and in the other - with the minus sign. If the direction of motion coincides with the positive direction of the $x$ axis, the body speed is considered positive, otherwise - negative (Fig. 1).

Let's draw coordinate axes and plot the time $t$ along one of them, and the speed $V$ (Fig. 2) along the second. Any line in these coordinates will express the graphical dependence of speed on time. What can you learn from such a graph?

The path traveled by the body from moment $t_1$ to moment $t_2$ is equal in size to the area of ​​the upper rectangle: $S = V_0(t_2 - t_1)$.

From the graph you can also find out the change in the body coordinate $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1 = V(t_2 - t_1)$. In both cases, it is equal in abscillot value to the area of ​​the figure under the graph ($S$ or $S_1$), but in the first case it is positive, and in the second it is negative, since in the second case $x_2 < x_1$ and $V_1 < 0$ (Fig. 3).

With uniformly accelerated motion, the velocity graph is also straight, only moving at a certain angle to the time axis $t: V = V_0 + c t$ (Fig. 4). Here $V_0$ is the speed of the body at $t = 0$. The tangent of the angle of inclination of the straight line $I$ to the axis $t$ is equal in magnitude to the acceleration

$$a = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta t} = tg \alpha.$$

The higher the line rises in a circle, the greater the acceleration.

The motion that corresponds to straight line $II$ on figure 4 is motion with negative acceleration. Can we say that this is “slow motion”? It is possible, but only until moment $t_2$, while the velocity and acceleration had different signs, that is, they were directed in opposite directions. At $t > t_2$ the abscilottic value of the velocity increases, since the directions of the velocity and acceleration coincide: they are both negative.

A similar movement is, for example, the flight of a stone thrown upward: its speed first decreases, then reaches zero and, finally, changing direction, returns. In this case, the acceleration is constant and equal to minus $g$ (we neglect air resistance).

The path traveled by the body from moment $t = 0$ to moment $t = t_1$ is equal in magnitude to the area of ​​the trapezoid $A$, and the path traveled from moment $t_1$ to moment $t_3$ is equal to the sum of the areas of triangles $B$ and $C$. The change in the coordinate of the body during time $t_3 - t_1$ is equal to the difference in the areas of these triangles, since the body first moved in one direction ($V > 0$), and then in the other ($V < 0$).

A velocity graph can also be constructed for curved body motion. If we know the trajectory, then by choosing one of the directions along the trajectory as positive, we can build a speed graph in the same way as we did in the case of rectilinear motion. The law will indicate the direction of movement, and the value of the ordinate will indicate the abscissa value of the speed.

So, for example, when a body moves in a circle with a constant speed $V_0$, the velocity graph will be a straight line $I$ by figure 2. The area of ​​the figure under the velocity graph in this case is numerically equal to the distance traveled by the body. Straight line $II$ will characterize a similar movement, but in the opposite direction ($V_1 < 0$).

Path graph and coordinate graph

You need to have a good understanding of the difference between these graphs. The path traveled by the body is a positive value that increases regardless of the direction of movement of the body, therefore the path graph cannot go down and is located below $t$.

The coordinate characterizes the position of the body relative to the selected starting point. During movement, it can increase, decrease, pass through zero, be positive and negative.

This is exactly how, for example, the coordinate changes when a body moves along the $x$ axis, first in one direction and then in the other (Fig. 6). We see that the final change in coordinates

Let's look at the coordinate graph in more detail.

The straight line $I$ (Fig. 7), going at an angle $\alpha$ to the axis $t$, corresponds to uniform motion, since in equal intervals of time $\Delta t$ the body passes through identical segments of coordinates $\Delta x$. The tangent of the angle of inclination of this line, as can easily be seen from figure,

numerically equal to the speed of movement:

$$V = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta t} = tg\alpha$$

(in our example, the movement does not change direction, so the change in coordinate $\Delta x$ during time $\Delta t$ is exactly equal to the distance $\Delta S$ traveled during the same time).

The rounder the straight line rises, the greater the tangent of the angle of inclination and, therefore, the greater the speed. Point $x_0$ on line $I$ is the initial coordinate of the body. It characterizes the position of the body at the initial moment of time (at $t=0$). Over time, the body moves away from the origin in the positive direction ($x>0$ and increases).

Straight line $II$ on figure 7 also corresponds to uniform motion, but with negative speed ($tg\beta<0$). The body is located at $t=0$ at a distance $x_1$ from the origin. It moves towards the first body, reaches the origin at $t=t_1$ and continues to move in the negative direction.

Let's solve two problems.

1. Two bodies, being at a distance $S_0=3\text{ m}$, begin to move towards each other with speeds $V_1=2\text{ m/сек}$ and $V_2=1\text{ m/сек}$. Where and when will they meet?

The problem can be easily solved graphically (Fig. 12). We will assume that the first body moves from the origin of coordinates in a positive direction, that is, up on the graph, and the second body moves from point $x_1=S_0$ in a negative direction, that is, down on the graph (the coordinate increases). Both movements are depicted as inclined straight lines, moving towards each other and intersecting at the point corresponding to the coordinate and time of meeting.

The slope of the lines is determined from the condition: $tg\alpha_1=V_1=2$, $tg\beta=V_2=-1$. The graph shows what occurs at the point with coordinates $t=1\text{ сек}$ and $x=2\text{ м}$.

During exams, students are often asked to comment on a movement depicted on a graph and to show (draw) what a graph of the same movement looks like in other coordinates.
