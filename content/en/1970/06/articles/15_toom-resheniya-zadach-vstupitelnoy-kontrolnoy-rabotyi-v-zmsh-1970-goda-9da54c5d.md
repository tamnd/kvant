Problem solutions
introductory work control
in 3MSh 1970

A. L. Toom

In the first issue of our magazine (page 56), as well as in the Teacher's Newspaper (February 26, 1970), problems were published that need to be solved in order to enter the Correspondence Mathematics School. ZMSH received about 6,000 letters from eight grade students with solutions to problems, about 3,000 of them were accepted at the school.

We think that the problems are being addressed not only by those who sent letters to the ZMS, but also by many other readers «Kvant», including future candidates for the ZMS, and therefore today we are publishing the solutions to the problems. Almost all problems are analyzed in great detail, “thoroughly”, in order to clarify the solution even for not very prepared readers. For the convenience of readers, we repeat the conditions of the problems here.

It’s not enough to solve a problem and get the correct answer: you also need to be able to write it down, which is not at all easy. Therefore, we advise even those who have solved the problems to read these solutions and compare them with their own. Of course, the solutions given here are not the only possible ones, but by reading them you can understand how to write solutions to problems accurately and logically.

1. The mathematician walked home along the bank of the stream upstream at a speed one and a half times greater than the speed of the stream, and held a stick and a hat in his hands. At some points, he threw his hat into the stream, confusing it with a stick, and continued to walk up the stream at the same speed. After a while he noticed the mistake, threw the stick into the stream and ran back at twice the speed of his hat. Having caught up with the dusty hat, he instantly fished it out of the water, turned and went upstream *) at first speed. After 10 minutes, he came across a stick dusting along the stream. How much earlier would he have come home if he had not confused the stick with the hat?

2. For every triangle ABC, is there a point P such that all three points, symmetrical points P with respect to the lines AB, BC and AC, lie on the circumcircle of triangle ABC?

4. The student took 31 exams over five years of study. In each subsequent year he took more exams than in the previous one. In the fifth year there are more second exams than in the first. How many exams are there in the fourth year?

5. The bases of an equal-volume trapezoid are 4 cm and 8 cm, its area is 21 cm². Which side does the bissertis of the angle intersect with a larger base: the smaller base or the lateral side of the trapezoid?

First of all, we note that the time lost by the mathematician, that is, the time that needs to be found, consists of two components: a) the time that he ran back and which we denote by t (minutes); b) the time he spent walking the distance he had to travel; since he ran twice as fast as he walked, this time is equal to 2 t, and all the lost time is equal to 3 t.

So, it is enough to calculate t. Let us denote the flow velocity by v. Then the walking speed is $\frac{3}{2}v$ and the running speed is 3v. Let us first find all points $P$ that satisfy the third option 1). All such points $P$ form a circle symmetrically circumscribed by $AB$ (drawn on figure 2 in white).

Thus, requirement 1) imposed on the point $P$ can be reformulated as follows:

1') point $P$ lies on the circle $O_1$, symmetrical to the circumcircle with respect to $AB$.

In a completely similar way, we are convinced that requirements 2), 3) can be reformulated in the requirement that the point $P$ lie on the circle $O_2, O_3$, symmetrical to the circumcircle with respect to $BC$ and $CA$.

We need to find out whether for every triangle there is a point $P$ lying on all three circles $O_1, O_2, O_3$ at once, in other words, for every triangle these three circles $O_1, O_2, O_3$ pass through one point. Let us prove that this is true for any triangle $ABC$.

9. Let's transform:

$$(x^3 - y^3) - 7(x - y) = 0,$$

$$(x - y)(x^2 + xy + y^2) - 7(x - y) = 0,$$

$$(x - y)(x^2 + xy + y^2 - 7) = 0.$$

Since $x > y$, then $x - y \neq 0$, which means the second bracket is equal to zero:

$$x^2 + xy + y^2 - 7 = 0,$$

$$x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7.$$

Which of the three numbers $x^2, xy, y^2$ is an integer, positive and less than seven. Then $x, y$ can only be equal to 1 or 2 (already at $x = 3, y = 9 > 7$). Since $x > y$, then $x = 2, y = 1$. By substitution we make sure that this is a solution to the problem.

Answer: $x = 2, y = 1$.
