2. Using the coordinate graph (Fig. 13), draw an approximate velocity graph.

It is convenient to build graphs one below the other so that you can compare them for the same points in time. The analysis of the graph should begin with the simplest sections (in our problem these are 1 and 5), where the speed is the same and constant in magnitude (the tangents of the slope angles are equal and do not change). Having depicted these sections on the velocity graph as straight horizontal lines, you can move on to analyzing the curved part of the coordinate graph.

It is easy to see that in section 2 the speed returns (the tangent of the tangent angle increases

changes), in section 3 it decreases, at point A the speed reaches its original value (the tangent is parallel to the initial straight line), and at point B it is zero (the tangent is zero). In section 4, the body moves in the opposite direction, and the speed is constantly negative. At point C, the speed again crosses zero and then returns to its previous level.

To successfully construct graphs, training is, of course, necessary. It is easy to implement by drawing volume graphs in one coordinate system and rowing the corresponding graphs in another.
