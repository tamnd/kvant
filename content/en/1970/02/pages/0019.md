Induced radiation is called induced because the acts of its emission are induced (caused) by radiation external to the atom. As a result, unlike the spontaneous emission of many atoms, stimulated emission is coherent.

Thus, in optics there is a phenomenon suitable for obtaining corent radiation. This is stimulated radiation. But for one atom, the probability of stimulated emission during transitions from upper energy levels to lower ones is equal to the probability of absorption during transitions from lower levels to upper ones. In order for induced emission to prevail over absorption, it is necessary to have more atoms at the upper levels than at the lower ones. But under conditions of thermal equilibrium, the situation is just the opposite: at the lower energy levels there are more atoms than at the upper ones (Fig. 1). When radiation interacts with such a system of atoms, the following will happen. Part of the radiation is absorbed, and some of the atoms will move to the upper energy level (Fig. 2).
