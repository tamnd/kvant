His explanation, brilliant in its simplicity, is as follows: microsoptic particles move because they are influenced by invisible molecules and fluids surrounding them. By observing the movement of Brownian particles, we get some idea of ​​how the invisible molecules of a liquid move, in exactly the same way as we guess about the volition at sea when, standing from the shore, we see the rocking of a boat, thrown by the waves in all directions. Brownian motion is therefore the place that connects the invisible world of atoms and molecules with the world accessible to perception with the help of our senses.

Why can Brownian motion be observed only in the case where the particles are very to the right, it always turns out to be approximately equal to the number only obtained from the left, and an insignificant difference in the number will be completely insufficient to move a large and heavy particle from its place. If the particle has an insignificant mass and insignificant dimensions, then in the chaos of the molecular movements of the liquid it can always be seen that on one side of the particle at a given moment there will be by chance only more than on the other, and therefore the easily mobile particle moves in the direction where its molecules are located. After some very short period of time, the excess molecular will only merge the Brownian particle in a different direction, after another short period of time - in a third, etc.

If this explanation proposed by Carbonell is correct, then the lighter and smaller the particles, the more intense the Brownian motion should be. This is so in reality - Brown was already able to notice this. In addition, we know that the higher the temperature the higher the temperature, the faster the molecules of a liquid move; and in fact, Gouy found that as the temperature increases, the Brownian motion becomes more and more intense. When Zsigmond invented the ultramicrosopt and was able to observe tiny particles of gold in the colude solution (the diameter of the particle is less than a millionth of a centimeter), the Brownian motion of these particles turns out to be so fast that some kind of continuous fineness resulted. Zsigmond describes his first impression as follows: “This is some kind of continuous jumping, floating, galloping, colliding and flying apart, so it’s difficult to understand this confusion.”

We now move on to talk about the classic work done by the French physicist Jean Perrin (1908). In these works, it was finally verified and established that Brownian motion in liquids is caused by the movement of molecules and thereby provided decisive proof of the active existence of molecules and atoms.

Perrin took pieces of gummiguta rubber resin and rubbed them with his hand in water until it turned a bright yellow color. After this, Perren took some of this liquid under the microsopp. Under the microsopp, it turns out with the help of nerastoril in the water, and broke up into many spherical small grains, which were scattered throughout the entire volume of water. These grains are very different in size. And Perrin wanted to have a liquid in which there would be gummigut particles of exactly the same size. To do this, he used a “centrifuge” (centrifugal machine), the same one that is used on large dairy farms to separate cream from milk or in medical laboratories to remove blood globules from the blood, after which a homogeneous liquid remains - blood plasma.
