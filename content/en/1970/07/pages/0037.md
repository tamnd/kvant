Let's look at the last question. What happens when metal tries to cut quickly? Obviously metals contain impurities of various brittle substances, and if the frequency of such a substance is revealed, then the crack quickly extends beyond the inclusion. Experience shows that metals with a face-centered lattice, such as copper, resist cracking better. It does not spread throughout the corn, but “fades out” due to plastic deformation.

If the wedge moves slowly, then the dislocations already present in the metal move under the influence of the direction that appears in the air when the wedge moves, and its energy is wasted on plastic deformation. What happens if the wedge moves quickly?

Kelly, Tyson and I recently addressed this issue. We calculated that immediately near the sharp edge of the wedge, the force tending to break atomic bonds is 5-6 times greater than the force tending to cause shear. Both forces increase as the wedge approaches, but their ratio remains the same until the limit of the strength of atomic bonds to break or shear is reached.

If the ratio of the tensile strength to the shear strength is greater than the ratio of the tearing forces to the forces causing the displacement of atoms, then the failure will have the form of sliding deformation. If it's the other way around, the material will crack.
