This is an opportunity to detect an irregular network of boundaries that split the metal into small polyhedra - grains about 0.25 mm in size (Fig. 1).

Under the same lighting, some grains appeared brighter, others darker. The distribution of light and shadow varied with varying angles of illumination, showing that the etched surface of each grain was composed of small, flat, reflective steps unequally inclined toward the metal surface. It became clear that each grain, despite its irregular shape, is a separate crystal, and the entire piece of metal consists of many such small crystals, oriented in different directions and having common boundaries.

The metallography method invented by Sorbi turned out to be very fruitful for determining the shape and size of grains, which is important for studying the technical properties of metals, for detecting impurities and their accumulations along grain boundaries, for studying alloys. Gradually, researchers came to the idea that when a metal cools below its melting point, neighboring grains capture as many atoms as possible, reducing the boundary between grains to a layer only a few atoms thick, within which the crystallographic orientation changes sharply from one grain to the next.

Recently, this point of view was confirmed by the use of an inon microsop, invented by the American Erwin Muller. In this device, the tip of a metal needle is held at a high positive potential in a vacuum, so that the electric field lines start at the needle and end at a fluorescent screen. A small amount of helium is allowed into the chamber. When a helium atom collides with a needle atom, it becomes positive and flies towards the screen along the drain line. When removed from the screen, a visible image is formed. The size ratio is such that the area of ​​one atom at the tip of the needle corresponds to approximately one square millimeter on the screen; It is in this sense that we “see” the atomic structure of the needle (see photo at the beginning of the article).
