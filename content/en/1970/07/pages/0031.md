⟦folio 29⟧

If the sample is subjected to deformation, for example, by shifting its faces in different directions, then its surface is covered with lines. They usually run strictly parallel to each other within each grain, and their direction is different in different grains (Fig. 2). Careful observations showed that these lines are traces of steps formed when thin layers of crystal slide over each other. Further studies (especially with large ideal crystals of metals) showed that this sliding occurs along certain planes and in certain crystallographic directions. Interim plastic deformation of metals thus turns out to be very different from flowing liquids and gases. Plastic deformation is characteristic specifically of the crystalline structure. It happens in such a way that atoms on one side of the slip plane are separated from their previous neighbors on the other side of the plane and move, carrying with them a certain part of the crystal and finally occupying a position in which they, together with their new neighbors, form a columnarly regular structure, as before. In this case, the original properties and internal structure of the crystal are restored.
