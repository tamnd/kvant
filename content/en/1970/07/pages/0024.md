Lambert cylindrical projection

Let's consider another projection with a rectangular grid; it turns out very simple. Let us describe a right circular cylinder around the globe (contacting it along the equator and in stripes) and project onto its lateral surface the surface of the globe (without stripes!) so that the projection ray for each point of this surface is the passage through it of a perpendicular to the earth's axis (Fig. 16).

Using the usual formulas of school stereometry, it is easy to show (try it!) that the areas of any sections of the surface of the globe and their projections onto the lateral surface of the cylinder turn out to be equal, and therefore the development of this cylinder (cut along any meridian) is called an equal-volume cylindrical projection (or Lambert’s projection). Correctly conveying areas, the Lambert projection greatly distorts the shape of even small sections of the projected surface (compare the ellipses on figure 17 with the corresponding circular ones on figure 15): the circumpolar regions are solidified here
