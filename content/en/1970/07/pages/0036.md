Point defects, like dislocations, disrupt the structure of the crystal. There are three types of precise defects (from left to right): vacancy (absence of an atom), substitution (inclusion of an atom of a foreign substance).

The result is actually a collection of dislocations. Shear forces applied to the crystal cause the dislocation to move along the slip planes. If there is only one dislocation in the crystal, then during shear it leaves the crystal (Fig. 6). In reality, a crystal usually contains a complex network of dislocations connected to each other, not counting other structural disturbances and the presence of impurities. Since the ends of the dislocation are fixed either to other dislocations or to impurities, when sheared the crystal does not get rid of disturbances in its structure. In fact, the number of dislocations increases with shear.

Dislocations move along the slip plane due to “shear forces” that occur during stretching, rolling, or torsion of the sample. What is the amount of force required to move a dislocation? This question contains two questions: 1) about the natural resistance to the movement of a dislocation in an ideal crystal and 2) the influence of interference (impurities and other obstacles) found in a real crystal.

Let us consider the resistance to dislocation motion in an ideal lattice. The atoms located immediately in front of the dislocation repel it, since it tends to become interstitial atom (extra atom). Each of these defects disrupts the crystal lattice in its own way (red lines). Fine defects often interact with dislocations.

The dislocation is subject to equal and opposite forces, so the resistance to its movement through the crystal is zero! This strange property of the crystalline state occurs if the dislocation area is large enough. In this case, there are so many atoms on both sides of the dislocation, but their action completely balances each other. Otherwise, force must be applied to move the dislocation. In the limit where the thickness of the dislocation does not exceed the size of an atom, this force is almost equal to the readability of the materials.

Narrow dislocations can occur in crystals such as diamond, so such materials even with dislocations are readable by scales. Wide dislocations explain the softness of metals such as copper, gold, and aluminum. The practical problem with these metals is not to make them softer, but rather to make them hard. Metallurgists achieve this by placing various obstacles in the path of dislocation movement (Fig. 9).
