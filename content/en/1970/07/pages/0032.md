If the locations of the balls of the first layer are denoted by the letter A, and the two subsequent layers by the letters B and C, respectively, then a face-centered cubic lattice is obtained if the layer is placed in the sequence ABCABC, and a hexagonal close packing - with ABABAB.

In metal crystals, sliding occurs along those directions where the atoms are packed most densely, since the resistance to the movement of the layers relative to each other in this case is less. In addition, as we will see later, atoms moving along close-packed rows more often occupy the source positions. Because they are more symmetrical, cubic structures contain many such directions in which rows of atoms are tightly packed and planes of atoms can slide against each other in many directions. This greatly affects the flatness. Individual crystals can take such a shape that neighboring grains fit close to each other, without leaving gaps or cracks, and, therefore, the entire crystal can also take any shape without breaking. Crystals with a hexagonal structure are more fragile and less amenable to mechanical processing.

The plastic properties of crystals are usually determined, as we have seen, by the origin of the close-packed directions and planes along which the fusion occurs. Why do such crystalline structures form? To answer this question, we must turn to the electronic structure of metals.

In 1900, the German physicist Pavel Drude proposed that a metal contains free electrons that can move throughout the entire volume of the metal under the influence of an electric field, which is the reason for the high electrical conductivity of metals. This theory was subsequently improved by the application of quantum mechanics, but the basis remained the same: a gas of mobile electrons, like liquid glue, holds the positive ions of the metal together due to the forces of electrical attraction to this gas. Electron gas ions bend towards each other, trying to form a compact mass, the structure and volume of which is determined mainly by the geometry of close-packed balls.
