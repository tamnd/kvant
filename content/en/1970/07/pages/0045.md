To obtain a negative solution to Hilbert’s agent problem, it was sufficient to prove that each enumerated set is diffant, i.e., for each enumerated set $M$ one has to construct a diffantian equation $P(y, x_1, \dots, x_h) = 0$ that would have natural solutions $x_1, \dots, x_h$ for all $y$ belonging to $M$, and only for such $y$.

In fact, if this could be done, then, taking as $M$ an unsolvable set (there are such among those listed, see item 4), we would obtain that for the corresponding equation $P(y, x_1, \dots, x_h) = 0$ there is no general method (algorithm) that, for each natural number $y$, would give an answer to the question about the existence of natural solutions for this equation. After all, if this method existed, then it would be possible to find out in a finite number of steps whether the equation $P(0, x_1, \dots, x_h) = 0$ has a solution (i.e., does the number 1 belong to the set $M$), does the equation $P(1, x_1, \dots, x_h) = 0$ have a solution (i.e., does the number 1 belong to the set $M$), etc. It would turn out that there is a general method that, for every natural $y$ determines in a finite number of steps whether this $y$ belongs to the set $M$ or not. Then, by Church's test $M$, the question of choosing this set would be solvable.

5. What was done and what Matiyasevich did.

In the fifties, a group of American mathematicians (R. Robinson, H. Putnam, M. Davis, J. Robinson) received a number of significant discoveries.

current results in Japanese proofs of diffantency of enumerable sets. (The conjecture that enumerable sets are diffant was advanced by Martin Davis.)

American scientists value the significant conditions for being able to reduce the problem to proving the diffantency of the relation $y = z^u$. Julia Robinson even went a little further, showing that it is enough to construct a specific equation $R(u, v, x_1, \dots, x_h) = 0$ that does not admit a solution with $v > u^u$, but for each $n$ it has a solution with $v > u^n$. It was Yu. V. Matiyasevich who was able to construct this kind of equation and who proposed a completely elementary, but extremely witty and original construction. At the same time, we had to solve a problem that was unusual for traditional number theory. Indeed, in number theory, for a given equation, as a rule, the properties of its solutions are studied, but here, on the contrary, having given certain properties of the solutions, it was necessary to search for the required equation.

Turning to the consideration of the Fibonacci sequence, Matiyasevich noticed that if $u$ is taken to be half the number of the even term of the sequence, and $v$ is the term itself, then the inequality $v > u^u$ will always be false, and for any $n$ one can find such a cell term of the sequence.
