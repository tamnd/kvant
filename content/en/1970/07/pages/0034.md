A crystal with a regular lattice can be thought of as a package of parallel atomic planes (a). If one or more planes rotate inside the crystal, then the edges of the “extra” planes form an edge dislocation in the crystal (b). Another simplest type of defect is a screw dislocation. Here, none of the atomic planes are sliding of atoms. In the case of sliding planes, plastic deformation begins when the atom of the upper layer approaches the position of unstable equilibrium, that is, when the difference between the electrical forces tending to return the atom back and the electrical forces pushing it forward to the next “hole” is maximum. It is clear that in case a this deformation is smaller. Because of these two reasons: less resistance to shear and less deformation required to reach the yield point, sliding is more likely along closely packed planes. These reports explain why metals with a cubic structure, such as copper and aluminum, are particularly ductile, but the question of strength remains open. Calculations based on these reports indicate that metals must be elastically deformed by 3-10% before yielding occurs. However, pure metals already have a deformation of 0.01%.

A discrepancy of a thousand times is of both scientific and technical interest. When this discrepancy was first discovered, the entire theory initially seemed to be wrong. But this is not entirely true, or metal crystals grown in the form of thin “whiskers” have a strength close to the calculated value. The bones do not roll inside the crystal, but the planes themselves near the linear dislocations are no longer parallel, but are closed with each other so that all crystals seem to consist of a single internal curved atomic plane (b). When bypassing a line dislocation, this plane rises (or falls) by one pitch equal to the interplanar distance.

The lower strength of large crystals is explained by the onset of dislocations in the crystals - violations of the regular structure, which give the atomic planes the opportunity to slide relative to each other more easily than in an ideal crystal.

Suppose we need to knit together a larger, heavy carpet. If you try to pull the entire carpet together, the resistance will be too great. But you can make a fold in the carpet and move it. This action does not require a lot of energy, and as a result, the weight of the carpet is pulled together.

The movement of the carpet in this case is very similar to plastic shear caused by dislocation. However, the crystal will slide over the other not entirely, but in parts. During such a sliding, a boundary line (dislocation) will necessarily appear, separating the part that has already fused from the part that has not yet fused.

A distinction is made between “edge” and “screw” dislocations (Fig. 5). In the first case, the line dislocation is perpendicular to the sliding direction, in the second case it is parallel. Most dislocations are a combination of these two types and are shaped like a spring.
