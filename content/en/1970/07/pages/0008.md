The same can be said about the geometric concept of “segment”. In nature, we do not observe “in their pure form” objects that are long and “straight,” but “devoid of thickness.” This concept, therefore, does not arise as a result of the abstraction of identification, but is an idealization.

Idealization in general terms is as follows. The real object is replaced in our minds by an abstract model, which is obtained not only by abstracting from non-essential properties, but also by endowing the model with idealized properties, which in an exaggerated form reflect the essential properties of the object. For example, if we study the movement of a celestial body distant from us at a distance many times greater than its size, we can build an exaggerated model, considering this body to be a material point. In the same way, the concept of “touch” in an exaggerated form reflects the possibility in reality of the relative position of objects.

Of course, this does not mean that the concept that arose as a result of idealization is completely divorced from the objective world and devoid of real meaning. Allowing us to especially deeply and clearly trace the most essential properties of an object, these concepts have a well-known significance in practical applications of mathematics.

A special type of abstraction, idealization, is the abstraction of local feasibility. It is characterized by a distraction from the real boundaries of our constructive capabilities, conditioned by our limited life in space and time.

This form of abstraction allows us to reason, for example, about any large number (say, about the number $10^{10}$) and assume that we seem to have the opportunity to reach it, starting with 1, 2, 3, 4 ... etc. Obviously, this possibility is purely potential: we will not have enough for this throughout our entire life.

However, we abstract from the practical impracticability of this task, from the limitations of our capabilities, and consider it permissible to consider any numbers, thereby arriving at the concept of “an infinite series of natural numbers.”

As a result of the use of this type of abstraction, concepts such as “straight” (extending unlimitedly in both directions), “plane” and others also arise.

Modern mathematics operates with a huge number of concepts that cannot be obtained as very strong, immediate abstractions of the properties of an object in the mathematical world accessible to animal contemplation. These concepts are formed in the process of multi-stage layering of abstraction.

The original, primary mathematical concepts arise from objective reality, reflecting the abstraction of quantitative relations and spatial forms of the object of action. But when these concepts arise, they seem to live their own life, forming their own world in the human mind, and they themselves give rise to further reflections and new abstract concepts. These abstract concepts, in turn, again give rise to new abstractions, so to speak, of a higher order, etc.

In order to understand what has been said, let us turn to the concept of “numbers”. Number, which initially acts as a result of counting objects (identification abstraction), is then generalized to the concept of a natural number (potential feasibility abstraction). Then the concept of a fractional number (identification abstraction) is introduced.
