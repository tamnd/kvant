"Bust of the Earth" - globe

Before moving on to various ways of depicting the earth's surface on a map, or, as they are called, cartographic projections, let's say a few words about the “sculptural portrait” of the Earth, which can be considered an intermediate stage for obtaining other “portraits” - in other words, about the well-known globe.

The fact that our planet is close in shape to a sphere was quite clearly understood by ancient Greek scientists, who were based mainly on the observations of sailors and travelers. Of course, these observations themselves only allowed us to assert that the part of the Earth known to the Greeks, that is, the parts of Europe, Asia and Africa immediately adjacent to the Mediterranean and Black Seas and the eastern part of the Atlantic Ocean, is a bulge. It is not surprising that, given the highly developed culture of abstract thinking among the Greeks, these rather indirect data allowed scientists of that time to put forward a bold hypothesis about the closed surface of the Earth. The shape of the earth's body on the surface of the Moon during an eclipse (the realization that this is the shadow of the Earth was in itself an achievement that is difficult to overestimate), and then speculative arguments,

entailing in one form or another the idea of ​​symmetry, is led to the conviction that the Earth is a sphere.

How the Greeks imagine the “face” of the Earth can be judged from the oldest geographical map that has come down to us (in later Latin copies), compiled by the famous Greek scientist Claudius Ptolemy (about 150 BC). Ptolemy, using the coordinate grid introduced even before him by Hipparchus, tries to expand the spherical surface of the Earth onto a plane (Fig. 1). The meridans are depicted as two-dimensional broken lines, and the latitudinal circles are represented by arcs of circles, the upper of which corresponds approximately to the polar circle, the lower one corresponds to the equator, and the one separating the truncated temperate cone from the cylindrical equatorial one (latitude 0°) corresponds to the Tropic of Cancer. A constant scale is maintained along the meridans (concentric arcs of parallels are separated from each other every 10° by equal intervals). Understanding that south of the equator the parallels must contract again, and therefore the lower half of the scan must be separated from the upper half depicted by him by a cut. Ptolemy is not trying to depict the Southern Hemisphere. He had to continue Africa from the south and Asia from the east with the hypothetical Terra incognita (“Unknown Land”), south of the Indian Ocean turning into the “Starry Land” (Terra australia; this name was subsequently given to the fifth continent discovered approximately in the same region). The Ptolemy map is made, as modern cartographers would say, in an “arbitrary projection.” All the more remarkable is the accuracy with which the coordinates (especially latitudes) of many cities and ports are plotted on it.

Ptolemy also gives another projection, from his point of view more beautiful and correct, but less convenient for practical constructions.

From “model” to “portrait”
But now that globes are doing a wave of good things, what prevents you from using them in all cases when you need a map? Or are they also not free from distortions - do they not take into account, for example, whether the Earth is flattened near the stripes or its mountainous terrain? No, this is not very significant here: the difference between the polar and equatorial radii of the Earth is no more than 0.3% of either of them, and the height of the highest mountain peak - Chomolungma - is about 0.1% of the Earth's radius (and of approximately the same order - larger than the depression at the bottom of the oceans), so within the limits of the dimensional accuracy of manufacturing, these factors are generally practically invisible; It is much more convenient to depict the relief on a map or globe using so-called “level lines”, coloring the designs between them in different colors for clarity. The essence of the matter is very simple: a globe is less convenient than a map - passports are not weakened by busts and bas-reliefs of the owners!

But, having a globe, you can think about “expanding” it - at least approximately. To make a portrait of the Earth, we need a miniature model of it.

Having understood how the globe “unfolds” into a map, we will come, by the way, to understanding an equally interesting inverse problem: how the true appearance of the original—the Earth—is recreated using flat images of pieces of the earth’s surface obtained as a result of aerial photography.

Let us now recall how surface developments are made. In a polygon, for example, you can cut almost all the edges, leaving only one uncut edge at each face so that the surface does not fall into pieces. Cylinders and cones are even easier to deploy. You will receive a model of the cylinder (more precisely, the cylindrical surface),

The slade is two opposite sides of a straight piece of paper, and the cone model is just a paper function. It is enough to cut any of these models according to the image and place the resulting piece of paper on the table - and the development is ready! The lengths of the lines drawn on the original surfaces are also preserved on the developments (except that the line - if it intersected the generatrix along which the cut was made - will unfold into two parts).

But our Earth is a ball. As Leonhard Euler proved in 1777, no part of a spherical surface (no matter how angularly small!) can be turned onto a plane while maintaining all distances. The idea behind the Euler proof is very simple. Whatever small part of the spherical surface of the globe we take, we can isolate a round “cap” inside it. Due to the complete equality of each point of the sphere (each diameter is an axis of symmetry), we can consider the center of this “cap” to be a strip, and the border to be a parallel (latitudinal circle). The distances between the points of the cap must, of course, be changed along the surface of the cap. In part, the distance from the shelf to any point on the cap’s border will be equal to the length $r$ of the corresponding meridian arc (Fig. 5). This means that on a development that does not distort the dimensions, this parallel should be depicted on the plane by a circle of radius $r$ and, therefore, has a length $2\pi r$. But it’s easy to prove (do it yourself!) that in fact the length of our circle is less than $2\pi r$. The resulting contradiction proves the impossibility of accurately developing onto a plane even the smallest cap in the middle of the sphere, and in general the lower part of the spherical surface.

"Microcards" - plans

From the proof of Euler’s theorem (and simply from the messages of “common sense”) it is clear that when we are talking about very small areas of the earth’s surface, replacing such small “caps” with flat circles distorts the dimensions transferred to the map, the smaller the smaller the diameter of the circle. Ultimately it comes down to what kind of image accuracy we really need. Estimating the margin of error is very simple (see problem on page 25) and allows you to answer this question in a reasonable way in each specific case.

Let's face it, the architect planning the development of the city is not at all interested in the shape of this globe - some ravine worries him, of course, much more than such “universal” problems! The person conducting exploration of a particular deposit cares little about the roundness of the Earth and geology; all sorts of local slopes and other “incorrectities” of the decision are much more important to him, which can affect the increasing choice of well drilling sites. In short, when it comes to compiling maps of small areas, we can safely become like the ancient Egyptians, who five thousand years ago made maps (as these “micromaps” are commonly called) of sections of the earth’s surface, completely ignoring the convexity of the Earth as a whole. Very simple and visual geometric distances (see Fig. 6) convince us that all meridians (lines of constant longitude) are equal to each other, and all parallels (lines of constant latitude) decrease from the equator to the poles from $2\pi R_{земли}$ to zero (“the 90th parallel” degenerates into a point - a pole). It immediately follows that the meridian segments between adjacent parallels on the globe are equal, and the parallel segments between adjacent meridians decrease in the direction from the equator to the poles.

It is also easy to check that all angles between parallels and meridians at their points of intersection (i.e., angles between tangents to these curves at these points) are right.

Square projection

Let's imagine a globe made in the form of a regularly spherical ball, the shell of which is made of fabric that is easily stretchable in one direction and completely inextensible in the perpendicular to it. We can, for example, assume that the horizontal threads are rubber, and the vertical threads are steel, and the rubber threads are closed and pull our ball-globe along parallels, and the thin flexible steel rods perpendicular to them tightly fit the globe along the polymers, adjoining each other with ends in stripes. Since the ends of the merdians are not attached to each other, you can stretch the rubber parallels close to the strips and insert a thin hard knitting needle into the hole. However, not necessarily thin. Who is stopping us from stretching the parallels further? Let's do just that and push into the holes, stretch to the size of the equator, a “thick spoke”, which is a straight circular cylinder of the same radius as our spherical globe (Fig. 12).

So we have “overcome” Euler’s theorem: after all, the resulting cylinder, being cut along the frontal semi-meridan (one can imagine that the rubber parallels, so that they would not tighten again after this operation, were subjected to a special heat treatment before cutting, depriving them of the ability to further compress and stretch), perfectly unfolds onto a plane! Of course, this is nothing more than a joke - the miracle of “overcoming” did not happen: after all, all the parallels (except the equator) were stretched, and the next continents and islands were stretched in the transverse direction. This stretching is especially noticeable, of course, in the subpolar region.

Lambert cylindrical projection

Let's consider another projection with a rectangular grid; it turns out very simple. Let us describe a right circular cylinder around the globe (contacting it along the equator and in stripes) and project onto its lateral surface the surface of the globe (without stripes!) so that the projection ray for each point of this surface is the passage through it of a perpendicular to the earth's axis (Fig. 16).

Using the usual formulas of school stereometry, it is easy to show (try it!) that the areas of any sections of the surface of the globe and their projections onto the lateral surface of the cylinder turn out to be equal, and therefore the development of this cylinder (cut along any meridian) is called an equal-volume cylindrical projection (or Lambert’s projection). Gnomonic projection

A projection in which orthodromes are depicted as straight lines is obtained by central projection from the center of the earth's sphere: in this case, all rays directed to the points of the orthodrome (which, as we saw, are a large circle) lie in the same plane, intersecting in a straight line with any projection plane. The grid of meridians and parallels looks easiest in this so-called gnomonic projection (Fig. 19), if the projection plane touches the globe projected onto it at the pole. (A gnomon is the rod of a sundial; if we insert it onto the projection plane and consider the earth’s radius to be a gnomon, then the projection of the projecting ray will indeed appear to us as the shadow of this gnomon.)

It is easy to prove (do it yourself) that meridians are depicted in a gnomonic projection of this type as straight lines, and parallels as concentric circles. On figure 19 (where, for obvious reasons, there is no image of the screen: it “gap into infinity”) the spiral-shaped form of locodromy in the vicinity of the pole in the gnomonic projection is very clear.

Instead of a conclusion
That's the whole story for now about the basic principles and specific ways of depicting the earth's surface on a map. For anyone who would like to get acquainted with the science of “portraits of the Earth” - cartographies - more deeply, in more detail and systematically, we would advise, first of all, to carefully review some large atlas, paying special attention to the difference in the projections used in it, and try to understand independently (with the help, perhaps, of rather brief notes usually given in atlases) the reasons for this difference. True, in doing so, you may be enriched not so much with answers, but with new questions. But so much the better!

You will find a systematic (but very rich and therefore much more difficult than in this country) coverage of the subject in the article “Cartographic Projections” in the latest edition of the Great Soviet Encyclopedia. In the article “Geographical Maps,” very rich in historical material, placed there, you will find further references to the literature.
