Not a single crystallographer neglects the construction of spatial models in which all the features of the arrangement of atoms in complex structures are clearly visible. But before we start hoofing, it is necessary to make a few theoretical notes.

The existence of the crystal lattice is due to the fusion of interactions between atoms. At short distances, repulsive forces predominate, which quickly increase when trying to lick atoms. At large distances, attractive forces that decrease relatively slowly with distance predominate. When atoms merge under the influence of attractive forces, the potential energy of interaction kills in the same way as the potential energy of a stone falling to the ground kills. At a distance at which the forces of attraction and repulsion become equal, the potential energy has a minimum, after which it increases due to the work of attraction. The dependence of potential energy on distance looks like the example shown in figure 1. In a state of equilibrium, atoms will occupy the place corresponding to the minimum potential energy. When there are many atoms, this will ultimately lead to the periodic repetition of some configuration that is most favorable in terms of energy for a small group of atoms, forming the so-called unit cell.

There are substances with very complex structures, such as some slicates, the unit cell of which contains more than two hundred atoms. Other substances, for example, many metals, form a crystal lattice according to a very simple law. Naturally, we will start with the simplest formations. In our experiments, the role of atoms will be played by metal balls, repulsion will be controlled by the forces arising when the balls come into contact, and the force of attraction will be replaced by gravity.
