If we mentally add the same paramide, but with the vertex oriented downwards, we will get the third regular polyhedron after the terrahedra and the cube - an octahedron with eight faces. It is not difficult to prove that we have again obtained the densest cubic packing, only now the faces of the cube are parallel to the plane of the base. A number of physical experiments can be performed with the constructed models.

By rubbing a rubber film against each other, you can simulate the thermal movement of atoms. (You see how the correct placement of the balls is destroyed with the “increase in temperature.”)

Since one hexagonal layer fits into the relatively small holes of another, the layer turns out to be weakly connected, and slipping can easily occur in them. Try moving one hexagonal layer over another and you will see that there are three easy sliding directions in which the layer moves as a whole. The same thing happens in crystals. Sliding in these three directions is explained by the peculiarity of plastic deformation of crystals.

Models can be built from any balls. If it is not possible to remove the balls from the bearings, then you can use large beads or, in extreme cases, fish or small apples. Figures 11 and 12 show the unit cells of cubic and hexagonal packings glued together from real tennis balls. We recommend this comparative and convenient material for making a model, especially for a school physics classroom.

Arrays of collapsible spheres, both dense and otherwise, play a very important role in crystallography, and we will have occasion to talk about them later. In the meantime, stock up on marbles and build models!
