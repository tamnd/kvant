DEGREES OF FREEDOM

The difference between a system of particle materials and a continuous medium appears very clearly if you look at the number of coordinates that define the state of the system.

The position of each point in space is specified by three numbers - three coordinates. They say that a material point has three degrees of freedom. If a system contains $N$ point materials, then it is said to have $3N$ degrees of freedom.

The same reasoning can be proven for speed. The speed of one point is described by three numbers - three components of the velocity vector. The velocities of $N$ points require $3N$ numbers for their description.

How many numbers must be given to describe the state of the sea surface? Strictly speaking, for each point on the surface you need to specify three numbers - the vector of water velocity at a given point; therefore, there will be an infinite number of numbers. The surface of the sea appears to us as a system with an infinitely large number of degrees of freedom. Further, the fact that water consists of molecules, and therefore the number of degrees of freedom can be determined, the composition of the molecule, does not solve the problem: there are so many molecules that in practice the number of degrees of freedom remains infinitely large. In reality, we are not interested in the movement of each molecule. When waves move across the sea, for example from a moving ship, we can describe the pattern of wave distribution using relatively few numbers. We can set the amplitude and phase of each wave; Although there are many waves, there are still fewer waves than molecules. Moreover, the pattern basically repeats itself over time: the waves are more or less the same.

In each wave many molecules move, the movement is collective, and we can talk about collective degrees of freedom on the surface of the sea, in contrast to the individual degrees of freedom of, say, an individual water molecule.

The same collective description can be used when talking about the properties of light. In part, we do this when we try to describe the distribution of energy across the spectrum.

Light is a wave process, and its description is easiest from the standpoint of wave theory. Of course, such a description of light is completely different from a description of a system of points. There is not even a hint of any degrees of freedom - waves and particles are completely different from each other. But this is still not entirely true. Waves and particles have common properties. These are, first of all, those that appear when we begin to study thermal phenomena and think about how thermal energy is distributed between waves and particles.
