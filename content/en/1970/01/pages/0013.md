According to this law, the amount of energy studied by a “black body” with a unit surface area of ​​the hole is proportional to fourteen and the abscyltic temperature and does not depend on the nature of the body: $\varepsilon = \sigma T^4$ (Stefan-Boltzmann constant $\sigma = 5,67 \cdot 10^{-12} \frac{\partial \varepsilon}{\partial m^2 \cdot \varepsilon \rho \partial^4}$).

WINE FORMULA
AND THE RAYLEIGH-JEANS FORMULA

Now it’s time to return to the question that was posed at the beginning of the article: how to obtain from the theory a spectral function that describes the distribution of radiation energy across the spectrum, and how does it depend on temperature?

First of all, they tried to solve this issue by analogy, but the analogy with gas did not help. The number of degrees of freedom of the light flux, no matter how you count them, is infinitely large, and if for each degree of freedom you allocate an equal portion of energy, say, $kT$ (it is reasonable to compare the vibrational degrees of light waves), then the total energy will be infinite at any finite temperature. Reasoning “by analogy” leads us to the absurd conclusion that all the tell energy of the walls (and behind it everything else) should turn into electromagnetic waves, so that the temperature of all objects should tend to absolute zero. If this were so, then any object in the room would emit light (visible or invisible). But we know that this should not be the case.

Accurate physical measurements show that at each temperature the body emits waves in a relatively narrow range of the spectrum. The maximum radiation energy is concentrated in close waves, which is determined by the so-called Wien's law:

$$\lambda_{\max} = \frac{a}{T}.$$

This law was opened in 1893. The constant Guilt $a = 0,29 \cdot 10^{-2} m \cdot \varepsilon \rho \partial^4$ was determined from experience, but its origin remained unclear. We will see further that it is related to the Planck constant (as well as the Stefan-Boltzmann constant).

Wien's law shows that as the body heats up, the maximum of the spectrum will mix towards shorter wavelengths, that is, towards higher numbers (this law is often called the law of mixing).

So, the Stefan-Boltzmann law talks about the total radiation energy, and Wien’s law talks about the position of the maximum in the spectrum. In other words, we know where the spectral curve has a maximum and what the area under the curve is. Now it's time to discuss the shape of this curve in more detail.

By the beginning of the 20th century, there were two formulas that tried to describe the shape of the energy distribution curve across the spectrum. However, two of them were proposed by Englishmen - this is the Rayleigh-Jeans formula *)

$$f(v) = \frac{8\pi v^2}{c^3} kT.$$

Comparison with experiment showed that the Rayleigh-Jeans formula correctly describes the spectrum only for the lowest frequencies (to the left of the maximum of the curve).
