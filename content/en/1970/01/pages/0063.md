Nowadays, science is developing incredibly quickly, and textbooks, naturally, cannot keep up with this development. If you get acquainted with some modern science only from a textbook, then it may seem that everything has already been done in it, all the problems have been solved, the mine, so to speak, has already been worked out, the ore has been finished off and only waste rock is left to your share. It even becomes a bit of a shame that I was born late. All countries have already been discovered and there are no blank spots on the map of the Earth, all the laws have been found - Newton, Coulomb, Ampere, Joule - Lenz, etc., the theory of relative mechanics and quantum mechanics have been created. It would seem, what else? But in reality this is not the case, and physics today faces many unsolved problems.

What substance does elementary particles consist of (electrons, neutrons, protons, etc.) and what is their structure? Perhaps they are homogeneous components of unknown matter, or perhaps, as the famous Russian poet Valery Bryusov once wrote,

“these electrons are Worlds where there are five continents, Arts, knowledge, wars, thrones and the memory of forty centuries!”

What is the nature of gravitational forces, and how do they arise? What are nuclear forces? Where does the periodic system of elements end and what other atoms can be added to it? Is there somewhere in the bottomless depths of space at least one anti-world, built from anti-morality? How many elementary particles does nature have and what are their properties?

Such problems are discussed in the collection “What Physicists Are Thinking About.” Moreover, as a rule, it is told first-hand by major scientists who themselves conduct these studies. Therefore, reading the article, as if entering their laboratory, you learn many interesting details and begin to better understand how science is done.

Despite the fact that in these collections the conversation is always about the latest problems in physics, which are generally very difficult to understand, they invariably remain completely accessible to high school students. The authors with great skill use the entire arsenal of truly scientific means: they provide simple examples and analyses, use clear models of various physical processes, and practically do without complex mathematical apparatus. The stats are written in a very interesting, lively, figurative language and sometimes read like works of art, although they have a global physical meaning.

Here is a brief description of the main content of these collections.

Two issues (the first and fourth) talk about the research of atomic nuclei. First of all, it will decide about research tools - accelerating charged particles, sophisticated cameras, special highly sensitive photographic plastics, spectrometers. Other articles examine the results of studying the structure of atomic nuclei and the nature of nuclear forces. Work on the creation of transwound elements is also described in detail.

The second and third issues contain articles on elementary particles. In addition, it talks about many individual types of particles - pions, neutrinos, antiprotons, antineutrons,

K-mesons, hyperons, resonances, their discovery, properties and the place they occupy in the microamp.

The fifth issue includes an article on macrosoptical effects and phenomena whose nature is fundamentally quantum in nature. This is primarily quantum electronics, which created molecular generators and successfully produced various radiations, ultra-precise atomic clocks. This also includes the recently discovered quantum phenomena associated with superconductivity and superfluidity, as well as the famous Mössbauer effect.

The sixth issue is entirely devoted to the problems of modern astronomy. It talks about new branches of astronomy - infrared, reptgen, neutrino, gamma and radio astronomy - and adding amazing information to them. What, for example, makes hail worlds—Galaxies—explode? Why do stars pulsate? How old is the Orin Nebula? What physical processes occur in the Large Magellanic Region? These are some of the problems discussed in this book.

Unfortunately, among the authors of the collections there were very many Soviet scientists, many of whom made a worthy contribution to the development of modern physics. In some articles, this contribution is not even acknowledged not only in the main text, but also in the applying editors.

Most of these collections are available. They were published in relatively small editions, and now they can only be found in libraries. But everyone who takes the trouble to read at least one of these books will feel the powerful breath of modern science with its intersecting solved and unresolved problems.

V. A. Leshkovce
