WHERE DID IT ALL START?

If you pass light through a prism, a multi-colored spectrum will appear on the screen placed behind it. (It was first observed by Newton.) Later I learned that the spectrum gives not only sunlight, but also radiation from any heated body. The higher the body temperature, the more blue rays are in the spectrum. A body that is not very heated (degrees up to 500 C) is red, a body that is very heated (degrees up to 1000 C) is white. Gradually, the researcher was faced with two questions: how does the spectrum of a body depend on its temperature and how is energy distributed along the spectrum?

If you apply thermometers to different parts of the spectrum, you can change what proportion of energy falls on each part of the spectrum. If the best ones are not thermometers, but direct calorimeters. The measured amounts of heat that fall on, say, a strip of spectrum 1 cm wide will be the quantities we need. The geometric length of the spectrum depends on the distance to the screen; therefore, the energy falling not by 1 cm is usually measured, but by the portion of the spectrum corresponding to the radiation frequency $v$ or a certain wavelength $\lambda$.

The ratio of the amount of energy concentrated in a narrow band of the spectrum in the frequency range from $v$ to $v + \Delta v$ to $\Delta v$ is called the spectral energy density or simply the spectral function and is designated $f(v)$.

What form does the spectral function $f(v)$ have? It is clear that it depends on body temperature. Generally speaking, $f(v)$ is different and different bodies. How to determine the type of spectral function? It was a difficult task, and it would take a little effort to tell how it was solved. But first, a few more words about the spectral function.
