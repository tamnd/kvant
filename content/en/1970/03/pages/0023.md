systems. After many years of flawless attempts to find the harmony of the world in the system of regular polyhedra that enclose the orbits of the planets, Kepler came to the great law: the planets move around the Sun, but they move in ellipses!

This law reflected not only Kepler’s readiness to express an insane idea that rejected the views of twenty centuries. Kepler was also extraordinarily lucky. Due to the enormous distance of the planets (especially heavy ones) from each other, their orbits are close to an ellipse - one of the three curves well known at that time (ellipse, hyperbola, parabola).

Kepler was the first to establish the laws of planetary motion. How he arrived at his second law: in equal periods of time, the planets cover equal areas - remains almost incomprehensible. After all, interral calculus does not yet exist, and the calculation of areas was not only a science, but an art: each curve required its own approach. Where did the idea of ​​creating unusual areas even come from? Probably, Kepler’s early passion will also have an impact - the specific methods for calculating areas that he invented in his work “On the Stereometry of Wine Barrels” (a genius from an applied problem is created by science!), and an almost mystical belief in the existence of unified laws of nature *), and, believe it, just a love of calculations for the sake of calculations, and what is called genius **).

Kepler's irreconcilable scientific opponent was his contemporary Galileo. He was the first to raise the question of what moves the planets, causing their orbits to bend. Galileo considered the influence of the Sun at such a distance mystical, as well as linking the tides with the Moon. How do the planets know that in the next hour they must cover the same area as in the previous hour?
