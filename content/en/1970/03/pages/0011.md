He imagined the ball to consist of pyramidal needles, the tops of which are located in the center of the ball. Aristotle wrote:

“According to Democritus, the ball also cuts, since it is also angular.”

This approach allows Democritus to solve many problems that geometers faced. So, he was the first to calculate the volume of a cone and a pyramid, he was the first to calculate the volume of a ball and to calculate the area of ​​a sphere. This is apparently how he dealt with the pyramid.

First he proved the theorem that two pyramids with equal bases and equal heights have the same volume. Here there were no additions for him - pyramids with the same height consisted, according to Democritus, of an equal number of corresponding triangles, and their volumes were the sum of the “volumes” of the triangles (after all, according to Democritus, triangles were material and had a minimum thickness). Since the areas of the bases of the pyramids were the same, the “volumes” of the corresponding triangles in both pyramids coincided, and therefore the volumes of the pyramids coincided. And then he divided the triangular prism into three pyramids, each having the same height and equal bases (see figure). In this way, Democritus “proves” that the volume of a pyramid is equal to one third of the volume of a prism having the same height and the same base. And the equality of the volumes of a pyramid and a cone, having the same height and the same base area, was proven quite simply - they consisted of the same number of figures of the same “volume”.

It is also clear how Democritus approached the problem of the volume of a sphere. Since the ball consists of needle-pyramids, then the volume of the ball is equal to the sum of the volumes of these pyramids. And he already knew how to find the volume of each pyramid. Summarizing these volumes, he comes to the answer: “The volume of a sphere is equal to one-third of the product of the radius of the sphere and the surface area of ​​the sphere.” He was unable to calculate this area.
