EXHAUSTION METHOD

So, mathematicians had to respond to both Democritus' method of weeks and infinite processes - Zeno's criticism undermined any confidence in the results obtained by using the concept of infinity. As a result, a difficult situation arose - the methods of Democritus, although through loose calculations, provided the correct formulas for calculating areas and volumes; now it was necessary to develop a new procedure for calculating geometric quantities, in which not a word would be said about infinity, or infinitesimals, or weeks. This procedure was created in the 4th century. BC e. Euclid's predecessor was the mathematician Eudoxus, who developed the “method of exhaustion” (or, otherwise, “exhaustion”), in which many see the ancestor of the modern method of limits. This method was widely used by Euclid in his Elements.

For example, here is how Euclid proves that the area of ​​two circles is the same as the square of their diameters:

$$\frac{S_1}{S_2} = \frac{d_1^2}{d_2^2}.$$

This may not be true. Then $$\frac{d_1^2}{d_2^2} = \frac{S_1}{S},$$ where $S$ is either less than or greater than $S_2$.

Let us first assume that $S < S_2$. Let us inscribe a square in a circle with diameter $d_2$ and construct regular inscribed polygons, successively doubling the number of sides. It is easy to prove that after a few steps the difference between the area of ​​the circle and the area $P_2$ of the inscribed polygon

became than $S_2 - S$, that is, $S_2 - P_2 < S_2 - S$, and therefore $P_2 > S$. Now let’s inscribe in a circle with diameter $d_1$ a polygon similar to that inscribed in a circle with diameter $d_2$; Let's denote the area of ​​this polygon by $P_2$. Since the areas of similar regular polygons are related as the squares of the diameters of circumscribed circles, then

$$\frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{d_1^2}{d_2^2}.$$

But we admitted that $$\frac{d_1^2}{d_2^2} = \frac{S_1}{S},$$ and therefore

$$\frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{S_1}{S}$$

or, due to the properties of proportions,

$$\frac{P_1}{S_1} = \frac{P_2}{S}.$$

But $P_1 < S_1$ (the area of ​​the inscribed polygon is less than the area of ​​the circle), and $P_2$, as just shown, is greater than $S$. So, the left side of the equality (*) is less than one, and the right side is greater than one, which is absurd. This means that the inequality $S < S_2$ is impossible.

In the same way, it is proved that the inequality $S > S_2$ is impossible (do the proof yourself!).

So, the assumptions $S < S_2$, $S > S_2$ are determined and remain the only possible one: $S = S_2$, that is, $$\frac{d_1^2}{d_2^2} = \frac{S_1}{S_2}.$$

But if for a circle the result could be easily guessed, and the calculations were not too complicated, then calculating the volume of a cone or the parameters of the source was a complex and confusing matter. However, it was still possible to cope with the cone and the parameter - after all, the answer was known at the time of Democritus, and Eudoxus only had to clarify Democritus’s messages, replacing the division of parameters into weekly given triangles with differences according to the source method.
