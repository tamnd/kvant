Problem 4. Among the proposed problems in planology, this turned out to be one of the most difficult for applicants: out of 93 who solved the corresponding option, only 3 people completely solved it. Meanwhile, it has a very simple and, most importantly, perfect natural solution in two replacement books by D. Polya “How to solve a problem.”

Indeed, what does it mean that the point $K$ (Fig. 2) lies on the indicated circle? This means that a circle can be circumscribed around the quadrilateral $BCKD$. When can a circle be drawn around a quadrilateral? When the sum of its perpendicular angles is equal to $180^\circ$. Therefore, from the conditions of the problem it is necessary to extract some statement about the equality of angles.

What theorems about the equality of angles can come to mind in connection with this problem? Only theorems on angles between tangents and chords and inscribed angles. Based on these theorems, the angles marked identically on figure 2 are equal. And now one cannot help but report that the sum of the opposite angles of the quadrilateral $BCKD$ at the vertices $B$ and $K$ is equal to the sum of the angles $\Delta CKD$, that is, $180^\circ$. Of course, it already follows from this that the sum of the other two opposite angles is also equal to $180^\circ$, and the required statement is proven.

The failure of the majority of those who solved this problem is apparently simply explained by the fact that schoolchildren in the 9th and 10th grades largely forget the simplest planimetric theorems and do not bother to fill this gap in the process of direct preparation for exams. It is quite likely that 8th grade graduates would cope with this task with greater success than applicants who are wise in general knowledge.
