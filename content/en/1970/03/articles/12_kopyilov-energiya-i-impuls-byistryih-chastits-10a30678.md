MASS AND SPEED

There is nothing in the world faster than light. And one light cannot be faster than the other. Any light (in emptiness) always moves equally quickly. Therefore, it is convenient to take the speed of light as unity. Any other movement, for example the movement of any body, cannot occur faster than the propagation of light, that is, the speed of any body is always less than unity. But what then to do with a body that some force accelerates for a very long time? After all, any force causes acceleration, and acceleration increases speed, and won’t there come a time when the speed of the accelerated body exceeds this amount of one? But this is impossible; This means that with increasing speed, the acceleration should gradually decrease - decrease so quickly that it does not have time to bring the body’s speed to unity. But what does it mean that when building up the action of our force, the acceleration decreases? How can this be?

Another property of motion is known: acceleration is inversely proportional to the mass of a body - the heavier the body, the more difficult it is to accelerate it with the same force. This means that we can conclude that the acceleration decreases due to the fact that the mass increases. Then the ends meet: as the speed increases, the body becomes heavier, and the previous force can no longer give it the same acceleration. The acceleration drops and the speed remains almost unchanged. Eischstein derived a formula for the increase in mass as the speed of the body $v$ approaches unity:

$$M = \frac{m}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}.$$

Here $m$ denotes the mass of the body when it is motionless, that is, when $v=0$. At speed $v$, they approach unity, the substitute will become less and less, and the fraction itself will become more and more.

ANSWERS TO QUESTIONS

“This is all good,” you say, “but why has no one noticed that bodies, as they accelerate, become heavier?”

This is really difficult to notice: everything that surrounds us moves too slowly. slow compared to the speed of light, with one. After all, the accumulation of mass in a body becomes noticeable only as the body’s speed approaches the maximum, and the speed of the fastest speed is less than 0.0001 - that’s how high the speed of light is. Now, if the speed of light were, say, 10 km/sec, then the designer would have to use Einstein’s formulas in his calculations, taking into account the deterioration of inertia as the speed approaches this speed. And if the speed of light were even lower, for example 1 km/sec, then quite a number of phenomena in the world would flow completely differently, and Einstein’s mechanics would seem to us as natural as Newton’s mechanics now.

“But then,” you ask more questions, “don’t they contradict each other given our thoughts and grafting speeds?” No, it doesn't contradict. Einstein conducted his deliberations in such a way that at low speeds he did not require Newton’s repeatedly tested laws. If the speed $v$ is very small, the fraction $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}$ turns into $1+\frac{v^2}{2}$ with good accuracy (check by substituting, for example, $v=0,0001$), and the mass growth formula turns into the formula

$$M = m + \frac{1}{2} mv^2.$$

This is what can be called the energies of existence.

If a new grain of matter arose somewhere, then some work was expended on its creation, some other body had to equalize or from some source the energy reserve for the construction of this grain of matter, and this supply will already be in it, even if the grain does not move. For ordinary, large bodies made of atoms, this sounds incredible: after all, we always create them from ready-made building materials (atoms) and do not waste energy on creating atoms. This means that in this case the energy of existence does not seem to be of particular importance: everything that is needed already exists. Transformations of the smallest particles are another matter.
