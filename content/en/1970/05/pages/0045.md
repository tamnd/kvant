If the saturated solution is cooled, the excess substance precipitates. figure 1 shows the dependence of the solubility of aluminum alum on temperature. The graph shows that, for example, when cooling to 10°C 100 g of a solution that was saturated at 30°C, more than 10 g of the substance should precipitate. Therefore, one way to grow crystals is to allow the saturated solution to cool.

Crystals can also be grown by evaporation. After all, if a saturated solution evaporates, its volume decreases, but the amount of dissolved substance remains the same. In other words, an excess of substance is again created, which precipitates.

Let us now consider how the release of excess substance occurs.

Take a saturated solution and heat it. Cover the vessel with the resulting undersaturated solution with glass and allow the solution to cool quietly to a temperature lower than the saturation temperature. To form a crystal, a “seed” is necessary. It can be a small crystal of the same substance or a simple speck of dust. Sometimes it is enough to quickly remove a vessel with a supersaturated solution or remove the glass covering it, as instant crystallization begins. In this case, many small crystals are usually formed. In order to grow a large crystal, it is necessary to limit the number of “seeds”. It is best to introduce an artificial “seed”, the role of which will be played by one of the crystals obtained earlier.

The “seed” is prepared as follows. Take two glass jars and wash them thoroughly. Pour warm water into one of them and saturate it with alum. When placing the solution, watch for dissolution. When the substance stops dissolving, carefully pour the solution into the second jar so that no undissolved substance gets into it. Then cover the jar with glass. When the solution has cooled, chemistry the glass. After a while, you will see many crystals forming in the jar. Let them grow and select the largest ones for “seeding”.
