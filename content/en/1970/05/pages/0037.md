The published article is taken from the collection of scientific works of A. Einstein, published by Nauka in 1967 (vol. 4, p. 22). Einstein wrote his work in 1916. He was not familiar with the works of N. E. Zhukovsky, who back in 1910 created theories of the flight of heavier-than-air vehicles.

Where does the lifting force of the wings of our airplanes and birds soaring in the air come from? There is complete uncertainty in these matters. I must admit that I could not find even the simplest answer to them in the specialized literature. I hope, therefore, that the reader will be pleased enough if I tried to take advantage of this gap with the help of the following simple considerations from the theory of fluid motion.

The incompressible fluid, whose internal friction we will neglect, flows along the existing pipe (Fig. 1) in the direction indicated by the arrows. We will be interested in the pressure distribution in the pipe. Since the same amount of liquid must flow through each section per unit time, the flow rate $v$ will be greatest where the cross-sectional area is minimal, and least where the cross-sectional area is maximum. Therefore, at figure 1, ​​the velocity of part of the fluid is the smallest at point $L$ and continuously increases towards $R$. The reason that causes such acceleration of a part of the liquid is nothing more than the pressure force acting on them. Let us consider a particle $F$ of a liquid occupying a cylindrical volume. In order for a fluid particle $F$ to have an acceleration directed to the right at a given moment, the pressure on its rear surface $A$ must be greater than the pressure on its front surface $B$. The pressure on surface $A$ exceeds the pressure on surface $B$. Repeating these arguments, we come to the conclusion that the pressure in the pipe continuously drops from $L$ to $R$. We will obtain the same pressure distribution (pressure decrease from $L$ to $R$) using similar reasoning in the case when the direction of fluid flow changes to the opposite.

Summarizing what has been said, we can formulate the following well-known theorem of hydrodynamics of a non-appearing fluid. If we follow the trajectory of any fluid particle in a steady flow, then the pressure $p$ will always be greater where its speed $v$ is less, and vice versa.
