The velocities $v_1'$ and $v_2'$ cannot, of course, have arbitrary values. Firstly, they must satisfy the law of conservation of momentum (momentum):

$$m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' ,$$

(1) secondly, they must satisfy the law of conservation of energy:

$$\frac{m_1 v_1^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 v_2^2}{2} = \frac{m_1 v_1'^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 v_2'^2}{2} .$$

In the case when the particles before and after the impact move in one straight line (such an impact is called central), these equations are solved quite simply. Instead of the vector equation (1), we can write exactly the same scalar equation for the lengths of the vectors. Then we get two scalar equations with two unknowns: $v_1'$ and $v_2'$. But more often you have to deal with an off-center impact, in which particles are separated at different angles. Having projected the particle velocity vectors into two mutually perpendicular directions, one of which is conveniently chosen to be parallel to the velocity of any of the particles before scattering, together with equation (1) we can write two exact scalar equations for the vector projections. Therefore, the conservation laws in the case of an off-center impact should be useful for the particles after scattering and the two angles that these vectors make with the direction we have chosen.
