If this convexity did not exist, then no forces would act on the surface of the wall, except for the inevitable friction. The convexity will affect the flow of fluid at both the upper and lower surfaces of the wall, and thus creates additional pressure.

For a flow flowing around a bluish wall, the bulge creates a local increase in the cross section and, consequently, a slowdown in the flow; as a result of this, the pressure at point U will increase. On the upper surface, on the contrary, convexity means a decrease in the cross section, which means a local increase in the flow velocity and thereby a drop in pressure at point O. Thus, the dynamic forces of pressure, arbitrary by the flow, create a force acting on the wall and directed upward. It is clear that for the appearance of this force it is only necessary that the piece of the wall be as large as required for noticeable bending of the fluid flow. We get a non-existent wing of an airplane or a bird (not flapping its wings in flight).

Already from these simplest arguments it is clear that flight requires only a certain power, since it is necessary to overcome the resistance of inevitable friction. If there were no friction, birds could fly any horizontal distance without expending any work.
