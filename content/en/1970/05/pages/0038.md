As a second example, consider the spray gun (Fig. 3). The air flow passing through the tube $L$, after leaving the hole, expands in all directions, while reducing its speed. Therefore, the pressure at point $P$ is less than at point $G$, and, therefore, less than in the air at rest surrounding point $P$. Due to the applied pressure at point $P$, the liquid from the vessel $A$ rises upward and is sprayed into small particles by the air flow

droplets. (The fact that in this example we are dealing with a flow of air, and not with a flow of undischarged liquid, does not essentially change anything in our reasoning.)

After these preparations, let us turn to the consideration of waves on water. Let $W$ be a solid wall in the form of a wavy cylinder and located perpendicular to the drawing plane, on one side bordering on a fluid flow flowing from left to right (Fig. 4). We will be interested in the force with which the liquid acts on the wall. It is clear that the cross-section of the fluid flow at points $B$ is larger than at points $T$. Consequently, near points $B$ the liquid will flow slower, and near points $T$ - faster than at those points inside the liquid that are located away from the wall $W$. Therefore, near points $B$ the fluid flow will create excess pressure, and near points $T$ a vacuum will be observed.
