There are two glasses in front of you. One with boiling water, and the second with tap water. In which of them will the water freeze first if the glasses are exposed to frost?

The answer seems obvious: of course, boiling water will take longer to freeze. After all, by the time the hot water cools down to the cold temperature, it will already begin to freeze.

However, if you place two wooden tubs with cold and hot water outside in winter, the hot water will freeze faster than the cold. Let's close the tubs with lids - the effectiveness will disappear. Let's pour water into metal barrels - again nothing unusual will happen.

The phenomenon, which seems paradoxical at first glance, has a simple physical explanation.

Water will freeze at 0° C. First it cools to this temperature, and then ice begins to form. This applies equally to both cold and hot water. How is the water already cooled?

When answering this question, two processes should be taken into account: heat exchange with the environment and evaporation. If the vessels are made of a good insulator, for example, wood, then heat transfer through the walls is difficult and cooling occurs mainly due to evaporation from the surface.

During evaporation, a region of vapor is formed above the surface of the liquid, the pressure of which changes all the time. If the vessel is closed, then this vapor quickly becomes saturated with a pressure $P_H$ equal to the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature.

Then further cooling occurs almost entirely due to heat exchange.
