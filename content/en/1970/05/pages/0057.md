A unit of time is taken to be the time during which water freezes, having an initial temperature of 0°C and placed in a vacuum.

the liquid evaporates until equilibrium with steam is established, that is, until the pressure above the surface of the liquid becomes equal to $P_H$. The saturated vapor pressure $P_H$ depends on temperature and increases with increasing temperature. Therefore, the vapor pressure over the hot liquid is less than $P_H$, and due to intense evaporation it quickly cools. During evaporation, the mass of water naturally decreases all the time, and therefore, when the temperature becomes equal to 0°C, there will be less hot water left in the “hot” vessel than in the “cold” one. Further cooling of the liquid in both vessels will occur under the same conditions. And since there is less water left in the “hot” vessel, it will freeze even faster. The phenomenon described was studied by the Canadian physicist Kell. He took two thermoses with a wide neck and filled each with 1550 grams of water, and in one thermos the water temperature was $t_1=88°С$, and in the second $t_2=56°С$.

The thermoses were placed outside at an air temperature of minus 6.5°C. When $t_1$ became equal to 39°C, only 1430 grams of water remained in the “hot” thermos, and in the end it froze even more than the water in the “cold” thermos.

It turned out that during cooling from 100°C to 0°C, water loses 16% of its mass, and when freezing - another 12%, that is, the ice obtained is 28% less than the original amount of water. It was also found that from the boiling point to 50°, water cools about 9 times faster than from 50° to 0°. Thus, under certain conditions, when heat exchange with the environment is difficult and the free surface of the liquid is exposed to liquid, boiling water will freeze faster than tap water.
