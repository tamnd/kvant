A LITTLE INTRODUCTION.

One of the problems that one often encounters in modern physics is the problem of elastic scattering. It is usually put like this: it is necessary to determine the velocities (in magnitude and direction) of two parts after they collided and scattered (as they say, scattered), if the directions of their motion and energy before the collision were known.

A collision is called elastic if it is not accompanied by the release of heat, and we can assume that the sums of the kinetic energies of the part before and after the collision are equal. In addition, particles obey the law of conservation of momentum.

When two particles - gas molecules or two nucleons - collide with each other, the result of the collision can be different. If the particles fly at a sufficiently large distance from each other,

Each collision process can be associated with a quadrilateral composed of velocity vectors. Using this method, the determination of angles and velocities can be reduced to the solution of triangles known from trigonometry. This method is also convenient because it can be generalized to more complex processes, for example, nuclear reactions, and even to solving problems in the mechanics of the theory of relativity. Here we will talk only about the simplest tasks.

In order to use the geometric method, it is necessary to introduce the concept of velocity space. Since velocities are vectors, the operations that can be performed with it are very similar to the operations that are performed with displacement (distance) vectors. Therefore, just as all possible displacements form (or, if you like, fill) a common space (we will call it coordinate space), so all possible velocities form a similar geometric object - velocity space. It is in the velocity space that we will construct and solve triangles.

It is most convenient to talk about the properties of velocity space based on an analogy with coordinate space. Therefore, we will first fill in some properties of the latter.

KINEMATIC DIAGRAM

Let us now move on to solving the scattering problem. Let a particle with mass $m_1$ and speed $v_1$ collide with another particle having mass $m_2$ and speed $v_2$, and let after the collision the velocities of the corresponding particles be $v_1'$ and $v_2'$). The task is to find the velocities

$v_1'$ and $v_2'$, if the vectors $v_1$ and $v_2$ and the particle masses are known.

The velocities $v_1'$ and $v_2'$ cannot, of course, have arbitrary values. Firstly, they must satisfy the law of conservation of momentum (momentum):

$$m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' ,$$

(1) secondly, they must satisfy the law of conservation of energy:

$$\frac{m_1 v_1^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 v_2^2}{2} = \frac{m_1 v_1'^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 v_2'^2}{2} .$$

In the case when the particles before and after the impact move in one straight line (such an impact is called central), these equations are solved quite simply. Instead of the vector equation (1), we can write exactly the same scalar equation for the lengths of the vectors. Then we get two scalar equations with two unknowns: $v_1'$ and $v_2'$. But more often you have to deal with an off-center impact, in which particles are separated at different angles. Having projected the particle velocity vectors into two mutually perpendicular directions, one of which is conveniently chosen to be parallel to the velocity of any of the particles before scattering, together with equation (1) we can write two exact scalar equations for the vector projections. Therefore, the conservation laws in the case of an off-center impact should be useful for the particles after scattering and the two angles that these vectors make with the direction we have chosen. If one of the quantities is specified, then the equation can be solved.

The collided part in any reporting system. Let's take, for example, one in which particle 1 rummaged around before impact (such a system is called a laboratory system). It contains $v_1 = 0$, and the law of conservation of momentum looks like this:

$$m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' .$$

This means that three vectors

Let us prove that these points lie at the vertices of an equal trapezoid. To do this, we introduce a new reporting system: center of mass systems. It is selected so that the impulses of the colliding particles in it are equal in size and opposite in direction. For particles 1 and 2, point $C$—the velocity of the center of mass—lies on a straight line connecting these points and divides this straight line into segments inversely proportional to the masses of the particles. If we denote the particle velocities in the center of mass system by $u_1$ and $u_2$, then $m_1u_1 = m_2u_2$.

This equality is similar to the lever equilibrium rule. The point that corresponds to a light particle is located further from the center. It is clear that the total momentum of the particle in the center of mass system is zero. Due to the law of conservation of momentum in this system, the sum of the particle impulses after calculation will also be equal to zero. This means that the point $C$ lies on the line connecting these points and divides this line into segments inversely proportional to the masses of the particles.

It is not difficult to show that, due to the law of conservation of energy in the center of mass system, the magnitudes of the impulses of each of the particles do not change, but only their directions change. To do this, the energies of the particles must be expressed through the magnitudes of their impulses and, writing the law of conservation of energy in the form

$$\frac{(m_1u_1)^2}{2m_1} + \frac{(m_2u_2)^2}{2m_2} = \frac{(m_1u_1')^2}{2m_1} + \frac{(m_2u_2')^2}{2m_2},$$

replace in this equation the impulses of particle 2 with impulses of particle 1 equal in magnitude or vice versa. In the first case we get that $m_1u_1' = m_1u_1$ or $u_1' = u_1$, and in the second that $m_2u_2' = m_2u_2$ or $u_2' = u_2$. And this means that at figure 5 the result is an equal trapezoid.

If the masses of both particles are equal, then this trapezoid turns into a rectangle. (Since $m_1 = m_2$, then $u_1 = u_2 = u_1' = u_2'$.)
