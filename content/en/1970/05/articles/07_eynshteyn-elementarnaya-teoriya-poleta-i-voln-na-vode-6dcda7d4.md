The published article is taken from the collection of scientific works of A. Einstein, published by Nauka in 1967 (vol. 4, p. 22). Einstein wrote his work in 1916. He was not familiar with the works of N. E. Zhukovsky, who back in 1910 created theories of the flight of heavier-than-air vehicles.

Where does the lifting force of the wings of our airplanes and birds soaring in the air come from? There is complete uncertainty in these matters. I must admit that I could not find even the simplest answer to them in the specialized literature. I hope, therefore, that the reader will be pleased enough if I tried to take advantage of this gap with the help of the following simple considerations from the theory of fluid motion.

The incompressible fluid, whose internal friction we will neglect, flows along the existing pipe (Fig. 1) in the direction indicated by the arrows. We will be interested in the pressure distribution in the pipe. Since the same amount of liquid must flow through each section per unit time, the flow rate $v$ will be greatest where the cross-sectional area is minimal, and least where the cross-sectional area is maximum. Therefore, at figure 1, ​​the velocity of part of the fluid is the smallest at point $L$ and continuously increases towards $R$. The reason that causes such acceleration of a part of the liquid is nothing more than the pressure force acting on them. Let us consider a particle $F$ of a liquid occupying a cylindrical volume. In order for a fluid particle $F$ to have an acceleration directed to the right at a given moment, the pressure on its rear surface $A$ must be greater than the pressure on its front surface $B$. The pressure on surface $A$ exceeds the pressure on surface $B$. Repeating these arguments, we come to the conclusion that the pressure in the pipe continuously drops from $L$ to $R$. We will obtain the same pressure distribution (pressure decrease from $L$ to $R$) using similar reasoning in the case when the direction of fluid flow changes to the opposite.

Summarizing what has been said, we can formulate the following well-known theorem of hydrodynamics of a non-appearing fluid. If we follow the trajectory of any fluid particle in a steady flow, then the pressure $p$ will always be greater where its speed $v$ is less, and vice versa. After these preparations, let us turn to the consideration of waves on water. Let $W$ be a solid wall in the form of a wavy cylinder and located perpendicular to the drawing plane, on one side bordering on a fluid flow flowing from left to right (Fig. 4). We will be interested in the force with which the liquid acts on the wall. It is clear that the cross-section of the fluid flow at points $B$ is larger than at points $T$. Consequently, near points $B$ the liquid will flow slower, and near points $T$ - faster than at those points inside the liquid that are located away from the wall $W$. Therefore, near points $B$ the fluid flow will create excess pressure, and near points $T$ a vacuum will be observed. As a result, the liquid will press on the wall as if it were trying to increase its bend. This means that the flow could not be sustained,

The explanation for the reasons for the appearance of the lifting force of the wing looks completely similar. Let a solid wall be inserted into a flow of liquid or air, located parallel to the

If this convexity did not exist, then no forces would act on the surface of the wall, except for the inevitable friction. The convexity will affect the flow of fluid at both the upper and lower surfaces of the wall, and thus creates additional pressure.

For a flow flowing around a bluish wall, the bulge creates a local increase in the cross section and, consequently, a slowdown in the flow; as a result of this, the pressure at point U will increase. On the upper surface, on the contrary, convexity means a decrease in the cross section, which means a local increase in the flow velocity and thereby a drop in pressure at point O. Thus, the dynamic forces of pressure, arbitrary by the flow, create a force acting on the wall and directed upward. It is clear that for the appearance of this force it is only necessary that the piece of the wall be as large as required for noticeable bending of the fluid flow. We get a non-existent wing of an airplane or a bird (not flapping its wings in flight).

Already from these simplest arguments it is clear that flight requires only a certain power, since it is necessary to overcome the resistance of inevitable friction. If there were no friction, birds could fly any horizontal distance without expending any work.
