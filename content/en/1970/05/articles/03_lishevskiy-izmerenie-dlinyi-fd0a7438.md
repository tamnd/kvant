Therefore, there was a need to return to a natural standard that occurs in nature and is easily reproducible. Otherwise there would be a terrible mess if suddenly the international meter stage was lost, cleaned or damaged for some reason. How does the length change? Once the unit of measurement has been determined, this is not difficult to do. You just need to see how many times the meter (or any part of it) fits at the measured distance.

But what if you need to measure the distance to an object located in the mountains or on very rough terrain? Then another method of determining length, called triangulation, comes to the rescue. Of two points, the distance between which is known, is located on the object (Fig. 3). From the known distance $L$ and the angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ we can calculate all the elements of the triangle.

The exact method of triangulation used the conditions when they measured the length of the meridian from Dunkirk to the island of Formation. Then, knowing the distance between these two points in some units of length and in degrees, it was possible to calculate the length of the entire meridian.

Let us now see how small extensions are determined.

It is not difficult to divide a meter into a thousand parts. It is a little more difficult to divide a millimeter into a thousand parts. For this you only need a good microcopper. This is how we get a micron - a millionth of a meter. But further division is difficult, since it is impossible to see objects shorter than the wavelength of visible light (about 5·10⁻⁷ m).

With the help of an electron microcoppe, we can see and change objects with dimensions up to 10⁻⁸ m. The shorter the wavelength of electron radiation, the smaller objects we can “see”. For example, gamma rays allow you to “examine” objects whose dimensions do not exceed 10⁻¹¹ m.
