The main part of the accelerator is the pressing chamber - a giant underground ring 1480 m long. The chamber is located in a magnetic field formed by 120 magnetic sections weighing 200 tons, between which 54 accelerating devices (resonance) are placed. The creator of the accelerator had to offer enormous difficulties associated with unusually strict requirements for the characteristics of the magnetic field and the arrangement of the blocks. It is enough to indicate that the deviation of any block from the specified position by more than 0.1 mm disrupts the operation of the accelerator!

The accelerated particles make about 400,000 revolutions in 2.6 seconds and reach speeds very close to the speed of light in vacuum. When they hit targets, they generate streams of secondary particles (mesons, antiprotons, neutrinos, etc.), which are sorted by special separators into different channels for further research.

During the short period of operation of the accelerator in Protvino, a number of unique experiments have already been performed on it. One of them was devoted to the Japanese so-called “quarks” (hypothetical elemental particles with fractional values ​​of electron charges), the other ended with the creation of atomic nuclei of the anti-helement - antihelium with a mass of 3. Research on elementary particles requires not only accelerators capable of creating such particles, but also special methods for recording traces of these particles. There are few such methods, and each of them brings fundamental discoveries to science. A track spark chamber is one of the best modern methods for recording elementary particles. It consists of several electrodes (plates or wires) connected to a high voltage source and placed in a vessel filled with a neutral gas. Passing through the gas gaps between the electrodes, a rapidly charged particle destroys gas atoms. A track appears - a visible trace of a particle formed by a chain of bright sparks. The design of the cameras is such that they are capable of recording particles for very short periods of time, amounting to millionths of a second. This allows experimenters to target the camera to record specific types of particle elements and their characteristic interactions.
