To the article “Non-hanging polyhedra”

1. Let us show that from each face of an arbitrary convex polyhedron one can go to any other, moving from funny face to funny face. To do this, take two arbitrary faces and select a point in them that does not lie on the boundary. Let's connect the selected points with a segment. This segment does not intersect the planes of the faces of the polyhedron, or the polyhedron is convex. Let us draw a plane through this segment that does not contain the vertices of the polyhedron: this is possible, since the polyhedron has only a finite number of vertices. The drawn plane carves two broken lines on the surface of the polyhedron. They do not pass through the vertices of the polyhedron, i.e., moving along them, we will move from one adjacent face to another and pass from the vertex face to the second. Telerfficium some face of a polyhedron, say $\Gamma_1$, and two points $O_1$ and $O_2$, distant $r_h$ from all its vertices. Let us assume for simplicity that three faces converge at all vertices of the polyhedron; In general, the essence of the matter remains unchanged. the vertices of any face adjacent to $\Gamma_1$ are removed by $r_h$ either from $O_1$ or from $O_2$, but not from both, or all points equidistant from $O_1$ and $O_2$ lie in the plane of face $\Gamma_1$. Let us assume that there are also faces $\Gamma_2$ and $\Gamma_3$, that the vertices of the first lie at a distance $r_h$ from $O_1$, and the vertices of the second lie at a distance $O_2$. It is clear that then there are two such faces that converge at one vertex, and therefore, by virtue of an additional assumption, are adjacent. Then the second end of their common edge is equal to $O_2$ and $O_1$, which contradicts the convexity of the polyhedron.

So, the vertices of all faces adjacent to $\Gamma_1$ are equal to the same point, for example, $O_1$. This is also true for the vertices of faces adjacent to $\Gamma_2$ or $\Gamma_3$, etc., i.e., for all vertices of the polyhedron. The opposite statement is obvious.

6. Let us show that any closed broken line composed of edges of a polyhedron is even-linked. It is clear that it is sufficient to prove this for broken lines without self-intersection.

Let us consider some broken line that does not contain self-intersection and cut the polyhedron along this broken line. Let there be $K$ faces in one part, and $L$ and $L \leq K$ in the other. The integer $L$ is called the girth of the broken line. If the girth is equal to 1, then the statement we prove follows from the conditions of the problem. Let us apply induction, let the problem be solved for broken girths less than $L$. Let us choose a face $\Gamma$ whose edge belongs to the broken line and which lies in that part of the polyhedron where there are fewer faces. Let $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_k$ be the edges of the polyline that are the edges of the face $\Gamma$ and $P_{k+1}, \dots, P_s$ the remaining edges of the face $\Gamma$. Let's move on to a new broken line, which is obtained from the original by replacing the edges $P_1, \dots, P_k$ with $P_{k+1}, \dots, P_s$. It is possible that the new broken line has self-intersections and even consists of several unconnected pieces, but the girth of each of its non-self-intersecting parts is certainly less than $L$, which means that the new broken line is even-linked. Let's assume that it has $2N$ ringing. The face chosen at the beginning of the proof has $2Q = s$ lines, then the number of links in the original broken line is $2N+2Q-2K$—an even number (Fig. 1).
