“Real numbers are called infinite decimal fractions.”* Having given this definition, we begin from time to time to pronounce the phrase in the title of the stat. What does it mean? Are we not contradicting the worldly wisdom of Kozma Prutkov, or a similar statement by the great French mathematician A. Poincaré, which appears as epigraphs to the statue, when we intend to consider the infinite decimal fraction? The final decimal fraction can be "examined" by, for example, writing it on the board and looking at what is written. In the case of an infinite fraction, we are, of course, deprived of such an opportunity. This is due, to varying degrees, to the limitations of the size of the chalkboard, man of life, our Galaxy, etc. However, let us agree that in this statue we will focus on the mythical giant, which does not care about all these limitations and any finite considerations are within our power.

On the other hand, it is not difficult to understand that the infinity of the decimal fraction is not yet necessarily acquainted with “immenseness.” Thus, when studying rational numbers, their representation in the form of infinite periodic decimal fractions is sometimes used, and to specify the latter it is enough to indicate a finite number of decimal places preceding the period, and the period itself (the same finite number of digits). As a result, we can consider such fractions without conflicting with our “finitude.” Arthmetic and geometric progressions (infinite sequences!) are determined by two numbers (the first term and the difference or denominator). You probably know other examples of finite sequences, the definition of which requires only finite information (for example, the formula of a common term). All this suggests that the criminal, from the point of view of Kozma Prutkov, of the phrase given in the title, is not completely unconditional, and therefore requires a more thorough study, to which we move on.

So, we know that there are finite decimal fractions that can be described finitely. Let’s agree to call an infinite decimal fraction “encompassing” if there is a “verbal portrait” of it - a finite description in Russian that is uniquely defined (i.e., only one fraction satisfies this description). If there is no “verbal portrait” of a fraction, then the fraction is called “immense”. Our goal is to prove the existence of “immense” fractions.

How can you prove this?

Let us first consider a similar situation, relative, however, to finite sets (for better understanding we describe it in a humorous form). The director of the cinema learned that there were spectators at the screening, and the seller had ninety tickets at the box office. You probably won't be surprised that from a comparison of these two facts he concluded that there were free riders in the hall. He simply noticed that there were more spectators than tickets, and there could not be enough tickets for all the spectators. Please note that this reasoning only makes it possible to prove the existence of a ticketless spectator, and removing a specific “hare” requires additional research (for example, checking tickets at the exit).

Let us first discuss the venerable story of the universal library.

How many volumes should a library contain, including all kinds of books that have ever been written, are now being written, or will be written in the future? As a rule, a person who hears this question for the first time is surprised that such a universal library consists of a finite number of books (cf. the second aphorism of K. Prutkov). Each of these contradicts our idea of ​​limitless human progress... But let’s turn to the calculations. We will assume that all books consist of 500 pages (otherwise we will divide them into volumes) and that each page consists of 40 lines of 50 characters each. Now let's discuss the issue of the printing base. We will be interested in books in Russian. For typing, you will need both characters (letters) corresponding to letters, punctuation marks, and some characters for typing special texts (however, if you do not save space, you can replace formulas, tables, etc. with their verbal descriptions). One way or another, we will assume that the capital letter is sufficient. It is convenient for us to introduce a special space character between words. By typing this character many times in a row, you can create arbitrarily long spaces (in part, thanks to this, among the five-page books you can include books consisting of a smaller number of non-blank pages). As a result, a book can be imagined as a sequence of 50 × 40 × 500 = 10⁶ characters, each of which is one of 100 characters of the typesetting alphabet (letters), i.e., like one word out of a million letters in a language whose alphabet consists of 100 letters. Note that this reduction is made possible by the detriment of the space character, otherwise the sequence of characters might not uniquely identify the book (it can be broken down into words in different ways).
