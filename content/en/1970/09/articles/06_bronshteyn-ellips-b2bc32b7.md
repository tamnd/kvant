Deformation *)

Let us take a certain straight line l in a given plane Π and a given positive number k. We will call the deformation of the plane P relative to the l axis with a coefficient k such a transformation of the plane into itself, in which each of its points m goes to point M according to the rules:

1) both points m and M lie on the same perpendicular to the l axis on one side of it; points lying on the axis itself remain motionless;

2) the distance of the point from the axis is multiplied by k, that is, $m_0M = k \cdot m_0m$, where $m_0$ is the base of the perpendicular from point m to the l axis (Fig. 2, a, b).

If $k > 1$, then all points of the plane (except for the point on the axis itself) move away from the axis - the tension plane, and if $k < 1$, then they approach the axis -

Ellipse equation

An ellipse with semi-axes $a$ and $b$ is obtained from a circle of radius $a$ by deformation with a coefficient $k=\frac{b}{a}$ (Fig. 7). Let us establish coordinate axes on the plane where both of these lines are located; Let's direct the $OX$ axis along the deformation axis, and the $OY$ axis through the center of the circle (also the center of the ellipse) perpendicular to the $OX$ axis. Let $m$ be any point on the circle, and $M$ be the point of the ellipse at which $m$ passes.

 the coordinates of the point $m$ are denoted by $(x_{окр}, y_{окр})$, and the coordinates of the point $M$ by $(x_0, y_0)$. Obviously, by definition of deformation

$$x_0=x_{окр}, y_0=k \cdot y_{окр},$$

$$x_{окр}=x_0, y_{окр}=\frac{y_0}{k}.$$

$$x^2_{окр}+y^2_{окр}=a^2,$$

for any point $M$ $(x_0, y_0)$.

If the point $M'(X', Y')$ does not lie on the ellipse, then the point $m'(x', y')$, which fell into $M'$ as a result of deformation, did not lie on the circle, and the equation $(x')^2+(y')^2=a^2$ is incorrect; This means that equation $(X')^2+\frac{(Y')^2}{k^2}=a^2$ is also false.

From the definition of the equation of a line we obtain the following equation of the ellipse

$$x^2+\frac{y^2}{k^2}=a^2.$$

Noticing that $ka=b$, we can reduce this equation to the form

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1.$$

This is the equation of the ellipse in the most convenient (so-called canonical) form.

Let us consider the following geometric problem (Fig. 8).

The segment $PQ$ moves in a plane so that one of its ends $Q$ slides along the straight line $OX$, and the other $P$ slides along the straight line $OY$ perpendicular to it. What line does the point $M$ of this segment $(MP=a, MQ=b)$ cover?

Let us prove that this curve is an ellipse. Let's apply the coordinate method (Fig. 9) - for the coordinate axes used along which the segment slides. Let the coordinates of the point be $M-x$ and $y$ (they depend on the angle $PQO$). From the similarity of triangles $QNM$ and $MLP$ we have $\frac{NM}{LP} = \frac{QM}{MP}$, or $\frac{y}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}} = \frac{b}{a}$.

Equation (1) cannot yet be called the equation of the desired trajectory: it is compiled in relation to figure 9, where the segment lies in the quarter and both coordinates of the point $M$ are positive. Only under this condition can we assume that $ON=x$, and $NM=y$; if the segment is in the II, III or IV quarter at figure 8, then you need to count $ON=\pm x$, $NM=\pm y$, choosing the necessary signs each time.

Fig. 10. Ellipsograph.

However, equation (1) is also suitable for the second quarter, since $x$ is squared and changing the sign of $x$ will not change this equation. For III and IV quarters, equation (1) should be replaced with this:

$$-\frac{y}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}} = \frac{b}{a}.$$

The pair of equations (1) and (2) can be replaced by a single equation

$$\frac{y^2}{a^2-x^2} = \frac{b^2}{a^2},$$

which is easily reduced to the form

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$

The required curve is an ellipse.

The design of the ellipsograph is based on the result obtained. A cross is placed on the paper or drawing board, in which there are two mutually perpendicular slots (Fig. 10). Three couplings are connected to the straight bar, which can be secured at any point on the bar. The outer muffs $P$ and $Q$ can go in the slots of the cross, and the middle one $M$ is equipped with a pencil, which, when moving the bar, draws an ellipse. Having secured the muffs along the given half-axes $PM=a$, $MQ=b$, we can draw an ellipse with these half-axes.

Inclined sections of a circular cylinder - ellipses

By intersecting a round cylinder with a plane that is neither perpendicular nor parallel to its axis, we obtain more or less elongated ovals, depending on the angle of inclination of the section. This means that every housewife cuts the sausage into slices for guests and often (to “save”) making the corner sharp enough so that the oval has a larger area.

Let us prove that these sections are not just ovals, but real ellipses. To do this, it is enough to establish that the oval is obtained from a circle (the base of the cylinder) by stretching; coefficient $k$ is equal to $\frac{1}{\cos \alpha}$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the planes of the section and the base of the cylinder.

Let us draw (Fig. 11) at the base of the cylinder two mutually perpendicular diameters: $cd$ - parallel and $eh$ - perpendicular to the line of intersection of both planes, and draw through them the planes $cdDC$ and $ehHE$, parallel to the axis of the cylinder, as well as the plane $M_0Mmm_0$, parallel to $ehHE$ passing through the point $M$ of the section. $$\frac{M_0M}{m_0m} = \frac{OE}{oe} = \frac{b}{a} = \frac{1}{\cos \alpha} = k.$$

Focal property of an ellipse

We move on to the most important property of the ellipse - focal. This is an adjective from the word “focus”*) - this is the name given to two remarkable points lying inside the ellipse on its major axis symmetrically relative to the center of the ellipse. The distance of each focus from the ends of the minor axis of the ellipse is equal to half the length of the major axis (Fig. 15).

Ellipsograph for gardeners

The well-known construction of an ellipse by continuous movement using a thread ring is based on the focal property of an ellipse. Let's put a sheet of paper on a lined board and stick two canile pins on it. Having thrown a thread ring onto both cops (the perimeter of the ring should be greater than twice the distance between the cops), pull the thread with a sharp pencil so that you get a triangle ($\Delta F_1F_2M$). If you move the pencil along the paper so that the thread remains taut, the triangle will be deformed, but its perimeter remains constant. The length of the side $F_1F_2$ also remains constant, which means $F_1M + F_2M = const$ and the pencil will describe the ellipse.

We have a new, very simple ellipsograph. This method has been successfully used with elliptical flower terminals. Instead of kopki, two stakes are driven into the ground, a ring is made of a thick rope, and an ellipse is drawn on the ground not with a pencil, but with a stick (Fig. 17).
