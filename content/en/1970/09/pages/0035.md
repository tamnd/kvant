Inclined sections of a circular cylinder - ellipses

By intersecting a round cylinder with a plane that is neither perpendicular nor parallel to its axis, we obtain more or less elongated ovals, depending on the angle of inclination of the section. This means that every housewife cuts the sausage into slices for guests and often (to “save”) making the corner sharp enough so that the oval has a larger area.

Let us prove that these sections are not just ovals, but real ellipses. To do this, it is enough to establish that the oval is obtained from a circle (the base of the cylinder) by stretching; coefficient $k$ is equal to $\frac{1}{\cos \alpha}$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the planes of the section and the base of the cylinder.

Let us draw (Fig. 11) at the base of the cylinder two mutually perpendicular diameters: $cd$ - parallel and $eh$ - perpendicular to the line of intersection of both planes, and draw through them the planes $cdDC$ and $ehHE$, parallel to the axis of the cylinder, as well as the plane $M_0Mmm_0$, parallel to $ehHE$ passing through the point $M$ of the section. Knowing that the length of the projection of a segment onto a certain line is equal to the length of the segment itself,

multiplied by the cosine of the angle between it and its projection, we get

$$\frac{M_0M}{m_0m} = \frac{OE}{oe} = \frac{b}{a} = \frac{1}{\cos \alpha} = k.$$

If we now put both figures $CEDH$ and $cedh$ on the same plane so that $CD$ is aligned with $cd$, then they will take the same position as on figure 5, with $M_0M = m_0m \cdot \frac{1}{\cos \alpha} = m_0m \cdot k$, where $k > 1$.

This proves our statement.

It is interesting that for each ellipse you can specify a position such that it turns out to be a cross section of a round cylinder, the radius of which is equal to the small area of ​​the ellipse. To do this, you first need to take a perpendicular section of the cylinder and rotate the plane of this section around one of the diameters of the resulting circle by an angle whose cosine is equal to $\frac{a}{b}$.
