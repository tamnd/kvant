⟦folio 4⟧

Let us limit ourselves only to the additions concluded between 0 and 1, that is, expressions of the form

$$0, \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \dots \alpha_k \dots,$$

(1) where $\alpha_k$, $1 \leq k < \infty$, are some numbers 0, 1, ..., 8, 9. In the theory of real numbers, there is quite a lot of trouble with the fact that any additional number with a “tail” of nines (all signs, starting from a certain place, are equal to 9) is equal to some additional number with a “tail” of zeros (for example, 0.099... = 0.100...). To avoid them, we will not consider dobies that contain nine in their entries. So, we will consider dobes of the form (1), that is, sequences of the number $0, \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \dots \alpha_k \dots$, where $\alpha_k$ can be equal to 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8*). We must know that even among such great ones there are “immense ones.”

How can you prove this?

Let us first consider a similar situation, relative, however, to finite sets (for better understanding we describe it in a humorous form). The director of the cinema learned that there were spectators at the screening, and the seller had ninety tickets at the box office. You probably won't be surprised that from a comparison of these two facts he concluded that there were free riders in the hall. He simply noticed that there were more spectators than tickets, and there could not be enough tickets for all the spectators. Please note that this reasoning only makes it possible to prove the existence of a ticketless spectator, and removing a specific “hare” requires additional research (for example, checking tickets at the exit).
