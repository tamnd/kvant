Electrical resistance is a quantum phenomenon

D. A. Frank-Kamenetsky

The resistance of a conductor to the flow of electric current is not at all as simple a thing as it seems at first glance. As we will see, we cannot do without quantum here either. Let's first see what can be done without its help.

Classical theory

We are used to the fact that “classic” is a similar word. They say: Streltsov scored a “classic goal.” Instead of “classic,” you could say “cool” or even “iron” in more modern jargon. A physicist has a completely different attitude towards this word. In modern physics, classical are called theories created by the classes of science of the past, before the emergence of quantum physics and the theory of relativity. Most often, the word “classical” is used by physicists as the opposite of the word “quantum”. The classical theory is different in that it does not involve quanti. Such theories are very simple; to modern physicists they seem somewhat naive. They often bring a big role, but, as a rule, they do not fully resolve the issue. In classical theory, there is always something missing for a modern physicist.

In many books, including textbooks (and even very well), it is written that the electrical resistance of a metal originates from modern electricians,

carrying current, with atoms*) of the crystal lattice. Is this concept of resistance correct?

It is best not to say either “yes” or “no”, but to say that it expresses the classical theory of electrical resistance. Everything we just said about classical theories in general applies to it. But in this case, the classical theory turns out to be very weak: it cannot explain many of the main features of electrical resistance. In the classical theory, one thing is certainly true: resistance comes from the fact that electrons transfer part of their energy and momentum (momentum) to the crystal lattice. But exactly how this transmission originates is not a simple and very interesting question.

Temperature dependence

The resistance of pure metals increases greatly with temperature. For many of them it is applied in proportion to the absolute temperature. At low temperatures, this simple dependence breaks down.

*) It would be more accurate to say - with ponies, but this does not exist for us here. The mechanism of electrical resistance is that electrons hitting the atoms of the crystal lattice cause them to vibrate—electrical energy is converted into thermal energy.
