Otherwise, the wave would “hang” in the void between two atoms. The path traveled by a sound wave in one oscillation is equal to $c\tau$. So, the atomic structure of matter imposes a limitation on the periods of sound vibrations: $c\tau \geq a$. Frequency $v$ is the number of periods per unit time, hence $v\tau=1$. Thus, the frequencies of sound vibrations are limited:

$$v \leq \frac{c}{a}.$$

During thermal motion, sound waves (in other words, phonons) with different frequencies are excited, as they say, a whole spectrum of frequencies. But the phonon spectrum has a limiting frequency. Waves with higher frequencies would not have time to jump from one atom to the next and therefore cannot be excited. Detailed calculations show that the Debye temperature can be determined in the same way as we did, if only the limiting frequency of the background spectrum in a solid is taken as the frequency $v$. This is what we will do.

All we have to do is estimate the distance between the atoms $a$. If the number of atoms per unit volume is $n$, then $n=\frac{1}{a^3}$.

From here and from formula (*) it turns out:

$$\frac{v^3}{c^3} \leq n.$$

A more accurate calculation, which we cannot provide here, is given by the additional factor $4\pi$. Thus, we obtain a formula connecting the limiting frequency $v$ with the speed of sound $c$:

$$4\pi \frac{v^3}{c^3} = n.$$

This very important formula is closely related to the Rayleigh-Jeans formula, which was discussed in the article by Ya. A. Smorodinsky («Kvant» No. 1). Expressing the frequency $v$ from formula (**) and substituting it into the expression for the Debye temperature, we obtain that

$$\theta = \frac{hc}{k} \sqrt[3]{\frac{n}{4\pi}}.$$
