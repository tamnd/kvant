If $k < 1$ (compression), then $b < a$ (Fig. 6). With $k = 1$ (identity transformation) the circle turns into a circle. In order not to violate the generality of the definition of an ellipse, we agree to consider a circle to be a special case of an ellipse ($b = a$). The larger of the segments $CD$, $EH$ is called the major axis of the ellipse, the smaller - the minor axis. So, if $k > 1$, then $a$ is the semiminor axis and $b$ is the major one, and if $k < 1$, then vice versa.

In both cases $k = \frac{b}{a}$.

Point $o$—the center of the circle—will transform into itself ($o \rightarrow O$) during deformation. O is called the center of the ellipse, the points $C$, $D$, $E$ and $H$ are its vertices.

The square $pqrs$, circumscribed around the circle (the “mother” of the ellipse), one side of which is parallel and the other perpendicular to the deformation axis, is transformed into a rectangle $PQRS$ from the sides $2a$ and $2b$, circumscribed around the ellipse; it is called characteristic of our ellipse, since it completely determines it. The sides of the Characteristic rectangle touch the ellipse at its vertices.

How to make an ellipse thicker?

If the axes of the ellipse are given, then the easiest way is to measure the ellipse by hand by first constructing a characteristic rectangle and then inscribing an oval curve into it. But this method, with little practice, can confidently draw an almost real ellipse). We already know that there are many oval curves with the same axes. You can accurately construct how many corner points of the ellipse. The most common and simple way of such construction is shown in figure 6.

On this figure two concentric circles have radii $a$ and $b$. Figure out this construction yourself and prove its legitimacy, that is, prove that on figure $6$ $m_0M = m_0m \cdot \frac{b}{a}$.

By making this construction for several points $m_1$, $m_2$, $m_3$, ... of a circle of radius $a$, we can get how many corner points of our ellipse. All that remains is to connect them with a smooth line by hand or using a pattern.

But it is interesting to measure an ellipse with a continuous movement, that is, without lifting the pencil from the paper, just as we draw a circle with a compass. This can be done with a special device - an ellipsoid. We will talk about it below when we get acquainted with the equation of the ellipse. Equation of lines *)

One of the replacement methods discovered in the 17th century is the coordinate method, which allows one to translate geometric concepts into algebraic language. This method makes it possible to establish a correspondence between lines lying in the plane on which the coordinate system is installed and equations containing two letters $x$ and $y$.
