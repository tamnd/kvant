It seems that it has three poles - two northern ones at the edges and a southern one in the middle.

Igor does not write how he managed to obtain a magnet with several poles. It can be done like this. Take a knitting needle and stroke it simultaneously with the same poles of two magnets from the middle to the right and left. You will get a magnet with three poles. A large number of stripes can be obtained by magnetizing only the ends of the spokes, or using coils. It is necessary to turn on several coils so that the magnetic fields created by neighboring coils are opposite. If you now place a rod made of high quality magnetic steel inside the coils and then turn off the current, the rod will have several stripes. In this case, the coils must be long enough, since short magnets are very easily demagnetized. This is connected with this. Any magnet can be demagnetized by placing it in an external magnetic field directed against the field inside the magnet itself. But the magnetic fields created by the magnet itself inside and outside are directed in the opposite direction (Fig. 3). The external field created by the magnet itself demagnetizes it. The ability to retain magnetization depends not only on the distances between the poles, but also on the properties of the materials from which the magnet is made. For example, a tape on which music is recorded consists of a large number of short transverse magnets. These magnets differ from each other in the magnitude of the field they create. You can estimate the number of such magnets per 1 mm of tape. Let's assume that the sound frequency is 1000 Hz and the tape moves at a speed of 10 cm/sec. Then the number of magnets in one centimeter will be $\frac{1000}{10} = 100$. That is, 10 magnets in every millimeter!
