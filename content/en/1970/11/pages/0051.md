N. A. Mints

Of course, everyone knows that a magnet has two poles - north and south. It is quite easy to verify this. If you take a metal iron needle or ruler and stroke it with a permanent magnet all the time in the same direction, the needle will become magnetized. Using a compass, it is easy to find out which end of the spoke has become the north pole and which has become the south pole. If the spoke is broken, then each of its pieces will be a separate magnet, and each will, of course, have two poles - north and south. No matter how many pieces we break a knitting needle, each piece will always have two poles - north and south.

An experiment with magnetizing an iron strip was carried out by Igor Gofman, an 8th grade student from the city of Artemovsk, Donetsk region, but he ended up with a magnet with amazing properties. Here is what he writes: “I used a steel magnet ruler as a strip magnet, and metal filings to determine the lines of force. The result is a picture in which 5 poles are visible (Fig. 1). Driving the compass along the ruler, I noticed that the arrow turns first one end, then the other, which proves the presence of opposite poles.”
