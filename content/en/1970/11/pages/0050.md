Ice slipperiness

One English physicist admitted in print that we are so accustomed to the slipperiness of ice that if he had been asked casually - before he himself thought about this question - why ice is slippery, he would have answered: “because it is slippery,” and only after thinking about it would have come to the correct explanation.

In places where ice or snow is pressed, they float under the influence of pressure caused by the weight of the body - and between the foot and the soil a layer of liquid lubricant is obtained, which significantly reduces the friction force and makes the snow and ice slippery, just like a slippery panel is covered with a layer of liquid mud. When the Hora leaves its place, this lubricant freezes again, forming an icy trail on the snow.

That this is not a matter of degree of smoothness can be seen from the fact that the roughest and most lumpy ice is almost more slippery than mirror-smooth ice, because the latter has a larger area of ​​contact with the foot, and therefore the pressure of the foot on the ice is less.

In general, the smaller the surface where the pressure of the body on the ice is concentrated and the lower the temperature of the ice, the more slippery it should be. Therefore, you can skate further down the mountain than without skates, and skating is easier if the temperature is close to 0°. When the air temperature is above 0°, the ice becomes loose, at least in the upper layers, and skates slip into the ice over time. In severe frosts, the skates seem to stick to the ice: very little liquid lubricant is formed, and it freezes before the hora has time to move from its place.

Prof. B. Weinberg.
