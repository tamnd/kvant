The directivity of the field of the exact charge changes in the same way: $E = \frac{q}{r^2}$.

Thus, the density of field lines is proportional to the direction of the field: $n \sim E$. It is only necessary that the power lines are not interrupted anywhere. For an exact positive charge, the lines of force must begin at the charge and go to infinity. In the case of a negative charge, the lines of force must go from infinity and end at the charge.

It turns out that it is always possible to draw a picture of the field lines so that their density is proportional to the direction of the field. To do this, the lines of force must be driven out on positive charges and end on negative ones (or go to infinity), and the number of lines of force leaving the charge or going to it must be proportional to the magnitude of this charge.

Let's mentally draw a sphere with the center at the point where we find the charge. If we agree to assume that $4\pi q$ lines of force emerge from each positive charge (and end at each negative charge), then their density on the spherical surface will be equal to $\frac{4\pi q}{4\pi r^2} = \frac{q}{r^2}$, that is, it will be equal to the direction of the field. The total number of lines of force intersecting the surface of the sphere does not depend on its radius and is equal to $4\pi q$. It is clear that the same will be the number of lines of force coming out of any closed surface inside which we find a charge (or going into it), no matter what shape this surface has.

This statement is called Gauss's theorem. It is true, of course, for any charge distribution. It can be shown that for any system of charges the number of force

lines emerging from a closed surface of arbitrary shape, minus the number of force lines entering it, is equal to $4\pi Q$, where $Q$ is the total charge that is located in the volume limited by this surface. If the charge $Q$ is negative, then the number of lines that leave the volume bounded by the surface is less than the number of lines of force entering it. If there are no charges in the volume bounded by the surface, then the same number of lines of force come out of it as they enter.

Potential and equipotential surfaces

Before we begin to implement our knowledge - solving specific problems, let us remember one more characteristic of the field - potential. The fact is that for it you can find a geometric picture that is closely related to the picture of lines of force. This connection often helps to draw and analyze a picture of field lines.

Let us first recall what an electric field potential is.

If, by moving a charge in the field, we return to the starting point, then the total work that is done when crossing an exact charge from one point in the field to another does not depend on the shape of the path. Otherwise, it would be possible to first follow the path where it is smaller, and return along the path where it is greater. The total work would not be zero. Due to the fact that the work does not depend on the shape of the path, the concept of potential can be introduced. The field potential at a given point is equal to the work done by the field when crossing a unit exact positive charge from a given point in the field to infinity.
