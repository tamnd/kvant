Thus, the law of reflection follows entirely from Fermat's principle.

Interestingly, the law of reflection was first obtained by Heron of Alexandria, who lived in the 1st century. Heron believed that light travels along the shortest path. For a single-sided medium, this coincides with Fermat's principle of least time. But it turned out to be impossible to explain the law of refraction in this way.

3. Law of refraction. Light from point $A$ falls on the flat interface between two media (Fig. 5) and is refracted, then hitting point $B$. If the speeds of light in the first (upper) and second (lower) media are different (for definiteness, we will assume that $v_1 > v_2$), then it is clear that the straight path $AB$ requires no minimum time. The time will be shorter if the light travels a slightly longer path in the first medium,

Suppose that light takes the least time if its path passes through the point $C$. Then any other path to the left and right of point $C$ will take a long time. An arbitrary beam trajectory $AFB$ can be set using the variable $x$—the distance between point $C$ and point $F$ on the interface between the media. The curve of light propagation time versus $x$ should have a minimum at point $C$, that is, at $x=0$ (Fig. 6). Near the minimum, time changes very little with $x$. Approaching at very small $x$ this time can generally be considered constant: on a very small segment in height $x=0$ the curve can be seen as a small segment of a straight line parallel to the $x$ axis.

In the second medium, the additional path is already passed by the ray propagating along the path $AFB$ ($CN \perp FB$).
