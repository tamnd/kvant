⟦folio 63⟧

To the article “Electrostatics in the language of power lines”

1. The picture of the field lines looks like that shown in figure. The left charge is twice as large as the right.
2. No, they can't. If there were a closed line of force, then by crossing a charge along it, we would do non-zero work.
3. No. The direction of the tangent to the force line coincides with the direction of the force acting on the charge, and therefore with the direction of acceleration of the charge. The trajectory of the charge is a line, the direction of the tangent to which coincides with the direction of the charge’s velocity.
4. Let us draw a sphere of radius $r$ with the center coinciding with the center of the charged sphere. From symmetry it follows that the density of lines of force crossing the surface of this sphere is the same at all points. This means that the direction of the field, equal to the density of the field lines, depends on the distance to the center of the sphere. The total number of field lines intersecting the surface of the sphere we drew is $4\pi r^2 E$, where $E$ is the direction of the field a on the surface of the sphere. According to Gauss's theorem
$$4\pi r^2 E = 4\pi Q,$$
or
$$E = \frac{Q}{r^2},$$
where $Q$ is the charge that is inside the sphere.
If $r < R_1$, then $Q = 0$ and $E = 0$; if $R_1 \leq r < R$, then $Q = q_1$ and $E = \frac{q_1}{r^2}$; if $r \geq R_2$, then
$$Q = q_1 + q_2 \text{ и } E = \frac{q_1 + q_2}{r^2}.$$
5. Drawing, as we did when solving the previous problem, a sphere of radius $r$ with a center at the center of the charged ball, we find that $E = \frac{Q}{r^2}$, where $Q$ is the charge that is inside the sphere. If the charge density (charge per unit volume) is $ρ$, and the radius of the charged ball is $R$, then at $r < R$ $Q = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 ρ$ and
$$E = \frac{4}{3} \pi r ρ,$$
that is, the direction of the field returns in proportion to the radius of the sphere we draw.
At $r < R$ $Q = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 ρ$ and $E = \frac{4\pi R^3 ρ}{3r^2}$ - outside the ball, the direction of the field changes in the same way as it would change if the charge $Q$ was concentrated in the center of the ball.
6. This problem is solved in the same way as the previous ones, only in order to use Gauss’s theorem, you need to build not a spherical surface, but a cylindrical one with an axis coinciding with the axis of the thread. Since the thread is infinite and uniformly charged, the lines of force intersect the cylindrical surface along radii, and their density is the same at all points of the surface. This means that the direction of the field depends only on the distance to the thread. If the height of the constructed cylinder is 1, and the radius of its base is $r$, then the surface of the cylinder is intersected by $n = 2\pi r \cdot 1 \cdot E$ force lines. According to Gauss's theorem
$$2\pi r \cdot 1 \cdot E = ρ \cdot 1$$
