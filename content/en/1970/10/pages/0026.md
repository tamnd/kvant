COLLISION OF MOLECULES WITH A WALL

When a molecule collides with a wall and bounces off it, the wall receives some momentum. Let us first assume that the molecule is reflected from the wall in an elastic manner. figure 1 shows a diagram of such a blow. Axis $Oz$ is directed perpendicular to the wall, axes $Ox$ and $Oy$ are somehow located in the plane. Let us decompose the speed of molecules into components along those axes. During an elastic impact, the angle of incidence $\alpha$ is equal to the angle of reflection $\beta$, therefore the velocity components $v_x$ and $v_y$ do not change. Component $v_z$ changes sign during an elastic impact. Thus, it is easy to calculate that the molecule will change its momentum (amount of motion) by the amounts
$$mv_z - mv'_z = mv_z - (-mv_z) = 2mv_z = 2mv \cos \alpha,$$
where $v$ is the speed of the molecule.

The problem, as you can see, is not difficult, and Waterston solved it correctly. The next person to take up this task (and publish his work in 1856) was Crang. He incorrectly calculated that the molecule upon impact transfers all its momentum $mv_z$ to the wall, and got a result that was half as large.

One might, however, think that our conclusion made one very significant simplification: we assumed that the impact was elastic. In reality, this assumption, surprisingly, is insignificant. a molecule can be reflected as carbon, but the result does not change. The assumption about the elastic nature of the impact does not change the result; this makes the conclusion very simple.

This remarkable property - the independence of the result from the law of reflection - is a consequence of the thermal equilibrium between the gas and the walls of the vessel (which are maintained at a constant temperature). Again (as with the collision of molecules), the final result - thermal equilibrium - does not change depending on how exactly the molecules are reflected from the wall.
