**METRICS ON THE PLANE**

Having taken up several exotic examples of metric spaces, we left aside the most natural one.

Example $4$. $X$ is the set of all points of the plane, $\rho$ is the general distance with which we deal in school geometry, that is, $\rho(A, B)$ is the length of the segment connecting two points $A$ and $B$. The properties of $1^\circ$ and $2^\circ$ here and in all the following examples are completely obvious, and we will not say more about them. And the property $3^\circ$ here is nothing more than the statement “in a triangle, each side is no greater than the sum of the other two”*). In the general construction of a geometry course, this statement is a simple theorem.

You probably know that on a plane with a rectangular coordinate system $Oxy$, the distance $\rho(M_1, M_2)$ between two points $M_1(x_1, y_1)$ and $M_2(x_2, y_2)$ is expressed by the following formula (see Fig. 3):

$$\rho((x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2)) = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_2)^2 + (y_1 - y_2)^2}.$$
