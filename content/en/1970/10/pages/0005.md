We will never be able to reach absolute zero. The physical, school, definite definition of absolute zero says that this is the temperature at which the thermal motion of matter stops. But this definition is imprecise. From a modern point of view, based on quantum theory, the existence of movements at absolute nULE is allowed. The energy of this movement is completely defined and is the minimum molecular movement that can exist in a given substance. Let me give you a simple example. If a substance is heated strongly, then the electrons of the atoms, which move around the atomic nucleus in certain orbits, will, under the influence of temperature movements, be torn off, separated, and so-called dissociation will occur. When a substance is cooled, the movement of atoms slows down, the electrons begin to circulate again in their orbits and maintain their movement until absolute zero. But, in addition to the movement of electrons along the orbits of each atom separately, there is also a goal of a series of combined movements in a solid body, which from a modern point of view should be maintained to the lowest temperatures. Thanks to this so-called degeneracy of motion in this temperature range, completely new phenomena can appear that we cannot observe at ordinary temperatures.

One of these interesting phenomena, which has already become widely known, was discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes - the phenomenon of superconductivity. It lies in the fact that at very low temperatures, electric current is able to flow through some conductors without resistance, without forming a body. Experience shows that if a current is excited inductively in a closed superconductor, it flows without emitting a body or decreasing, only for as long as the experimenter was able to observe it. Another of these phenomena

which can only be detected at very low temperatures is the superfluidity we discovered 5 years ago in liquid helium.

Studies of this and other phenomena were carried out using liquid helium itself as a refrigerant. Liquid helium is the only known substance that, even at the lowest temperatures down to thousandths of a degree from absolute zero at normal pressure, remains liquid and does not turn into a solid state. It can only be turned into a solid under pressure starting at 25 amps.

Liquid helium itself is an extremely interesting object to study.

Helium is expected at a temperature of 4.8°K and forms a light, weighing 7-8 times less than water, and transparent liquid. Due to its low thermal capacity, during the experiment, liquid helium should be held in a vacuum Dewar flask surrounded by another similar flask with liquid air. Experimenting with liquid helium presents significant technical difficulties. This explains the fact that until now only a few cold laboratories in all countries produce liquid helium in sufficient quantities.

This temperature is called the λ-point.
