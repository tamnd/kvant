DISTANCES BETWEEN FUNCTIONS

Distance is conveniently determined not only for numbers or points of the plane and space, but also for many other mathematical objects. Here are two examples from geometry. For the set of all lines passing through a given point O, the distance between two lines can be taken to be the smaller of the angles it forms (in figure 6 we show what a neighborhood of one of the lines looks like). The distance between two convex polygons $M_1$ and $M_2$ on a plane can be determined as follows: for each vertex of polygons $M_1$ and $M_2$ we find the distance $^*$ to the vertex of another polygon closest to it, and from all these numbers we take the most 贴近; Thus, the $r$-neighborhood of a given polygon $M_0$ (Fig. 7) contains polygons $M$ such that all vertices lie in circles of radius $r$ with centers at vertices $M_0$, and each circle contains at least one vertex $M$; check that the set of convex polygons with such a distance is a metric space. The number of such examples could easily be increased.

But the most important applications, which the theory of metric spaces owes its emergence and development, are associated not with geometrics, but with the analysis and theory of functions.

Very often, in order to examine the data of functions or simply calculate their values, it is convenient to approximately replace them with other, simpler functions, say, polynomials. You've probably heard that when $x$ is close to zero, sin$x$ is approximately equal to $x$ (here sin$x$ means the sine of the number $x$, that is, the sine of an angle of $x$ radians). An even more accurate formula: $\sin x \approx x - \frac{x^3}{6}$. Let, for example, $x$ change on the segment $0 \leqslant x \leqslant \frac{\pi}{4}$.

How to evaluate how well function $f_1(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{6}$ approximates function $f_2(x) = \sin x$, how large is the “distance” between these functions?

One of the natural distances is also: for each $x$ we find the difference $f_1(x) - f_2(x)$, take that $x = x_0$, where this difference is largest (in absolute value), and put $\rho(f_1, f_2) = |f_1(x_0) - f_2(x_0)|$.

*) This refers to the “obvious” distance (4).
