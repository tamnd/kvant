6. A metric space $X$ is called bounded if there is a number $c$ such that the distance between any two points $X$ does not exceed $c$.

Prove that if for some $\varepsilon$ a space has a finite $\varepsilon$-net, then it is bounded.

7. Let $N(\varepsilon)$ be the smallest number of points in the $\varepsilon$-network of the space $X$, $M(\varepsilon)$ be the smallest number of points in $X$, the distance between any two of which is at least $\varepsilon$.

Prove that $M(2\varepsilon) \leq N(\varepsilon) \leq M(\varepsilon)$.

8. Let $C$ be a set of functions defined on the interval [0, 1] and taking values ​​on the same interval, whose graphs are broken lines. We will determine the distance in $C$ using formula (7).

Prove that it is possible to extract an infinite number of functions from $C$, all pairwise distances between which are equal to one. Deduce from this that for $\varepsilon < \frac{1}{2}$ in $C$ it is impossible to steal the finite $\varepsilon$-net.

9. In some metric space, can it be that the 3-neighborhood of a point $A$ is entirely contained in the 2-neighborhood of another point $B$ and does not fill it entirely? What will be the answer if we replace 3 and 2 with other numbers?

10. Prove that among $n$-digit numbers, it is impossible to extract more than $\frac{2^n}{n+1}$ numbers from two digits 1 and 2 so that any two of them differ from each other in at least three digits.

11. Using problem 4 a) prove that two different points $A$ and $B$ cannot be limits of the same sequence.

12. Construct an example of a sequence of functions defined on the interval [0, 1], which tends to the limit $f_0$, if we use distance (8), and does not have $f_0$ limit, if we use distance (7).

13. Let $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ — two distances on some set $X$ — have the property that $\rho_1(A, B) \leq k\rho_2(A, B)$ for any two points $A$ and $B$, where $k$ is some positive number (the same for all $A$ and $B$).

Prove that if $P$ is the limit of the sequence $M_1, M_2, M_3, \dots$ in the sense of the distance $\rho_2$, then $P$ will be the limit of this sequence in the sense of the distance $\rho_1$. Thus, prove that on the plane the statement “the limit of the sequence $M_1, M_2, M_3, \dots$ is the point $P$” has the same meaning, regardless of which of the distances (4), (5), (6) we obtain.
