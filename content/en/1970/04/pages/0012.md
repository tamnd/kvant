This challenge to "common sense" reached its climax in 1924, when the dual concept of "wave" and "particle" was extended by theoretical reasoning to electrons and atoms. In other words, the atom and the electron, which since their discovery have all the properties of particles, must also behave like waves. And very soon experiments confirmed these conclusions.

Physicists had to understand the situation more deeply, which led to the creation of modern quantum mechanics (or, as it was previously called, wave mechanics).

The foundations of quantum mechanics were laid in 1925-1927. A significant contribution to its development was made by the German physicist Werner Heisenberg with his formulation of the so-called uncertainty correspondence, in which

circle of which a wide-ranging philosophical discussion flared up. This correspondence led many famous physicists to the camp of philosophical idealists.

At first glance, the correspondence of the following is completely harmless:

$$\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{h}{2\pi}.$$

Here $\Delta x$ is the uncertainty (inaccuracy) of the particle coordinate, $\Delta p$ is the uncertainty of the momentum (or speed) of the particle, and $h$ is Planck’s constant. Translated into ordinary language, this means that it is impossible to accurately know the location and speed of a particle at the same time. In other words, if we try to fix a particle in a certain place, then our attempt will lead to the fact that we will not be able to determine where and at what speed it will then fly. On the contrary, if we make a particle move very slowly at a certain speed, then we will not be able to indicate where it is, that is, the particle will appear diffuse. From here it is easy to make the “simplest” conclusion of a clearly idealistic nature that human knowledge is fundamentally limited, since we are not given the opportunity to answer such a simple question. Moreover, from here we can draw a conclusion, idealistic in its meaning, that events in the world are unpredictable, that is, principles are violated.

Indeed, we are accustomed to the fact that classical mechanics allows us to foresee the future movement of a body if its initial position and speed, as well as the forces acting on it, are known. All mechanics are based on this. For example, the successes of astronautics are based on the fact that, knowing the launch site of the rocket (initial coordinates) and setting the extracted initial speed, we can, according to the laws of classical mechanics, foresee in advance where it will be.
