⟦folio 5⟧

The nature of our actions provides proof of the correspondence (Ubereinstimmung) of our perceptions with the objective (gegenständlich) nature of the perceived things*).

This brings up the next question. The history of science knows many examples indicating that the ideas of scientists that are clearly incorrect from a modern point of view nevertheless led to “the ruin of our actions.”

As an example, take the old theory of magnetism, in which a magnetized piece of steel was viewed as a magnetic dipole, consisting of two magnetic poles or “magnetic charges” (similar to an electric dipole). Using this idea, physicists create a magnetostat, on which all practice and technology of using magnets is based. This practice quickly confirms the theory of “magnetic charges”. Meanwhile, it is now well known that no magnetic charges actually exist. And when they now talk about magnetic poles, they always stipulate that this is a fictitious concept.

It seems that the criterion of practice cannot serve as a reliable basis for ascertaining the truth of our ideas about a particular subject? This, of course, is not a separate question.

One of the greatest modern physicists, Richard Feynman, trying to figure out what the philosophical interpretation of a physical law is, illustrates the significance of this issue with the following example.

“Let those who insist that the only thing that matters is the agreement between theory and experiment, imagine a conversation between a Mayan astronomer and his student. Maya was able to imagine with amazing accuracy, for example, the time of an eclipse, the position of the Moon, Venus and other planets in the sky. All this was done with the help of arithmetic... They did not have the slightest idea about the rotation of celestial bodies.

Imagine that a young man comes to our astronomer and says: “This is what came to my mind. Maybe all this is believable, maybe these are balls of stone... and their movement can be calculated completely differently.” Further, having learned that the young man has not yet reached the point of calculating the movements of the planets, the Mayan astronomer will answer him that we can already accurately calculate eclipses, so there is no need to bother with your ideas.

As we see,” Feynman concludes, “it is not an easy task to decide whether or not it is worth thinking about what lies behind our theories.”

This is indeed no easy task. After all, if we accept unconditionally the statement that the criterion of truth is practice, then such a statement, as can be seen from the examples given, can lead to stagnation in science.

This difficult task was brilliantly solved by V.I. Lenin.

Arguing that “the point of view of life and practice must be the first and fundamental point of view of the theory of knowledge,” Lenin adds the following significant paragraph.

“...of course, we must not forget that the criterion of practice can never, in its very way, expose or refute the position of the Maya and his student. Maya was able to imagine with amazing accuracy, for example, the time of an eclipse, the position of the Moon, Venus and other planets in the sky. All this was done with the help of arithmetic... They did not have the slightest idea about the rotation of celestial bodies.

*) Agnosticism is a philosophical movement that affirms the irreducible limitations of human knowledge.
