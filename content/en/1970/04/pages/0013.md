The situation is different with a particle that obeys quantum mechanics. Since we, in accordance with the uncertainty relation, cannot accurately indicate its coordinates and speed, then, obviously, we cannot predict what they will become in the future. It is not difficult to agree that all this is a consequence of the fact that particles (electrons, atoms, neutrons, etc.) have wave properties.

In fact, let us consider the following roughly schematized experiment, which, nevertheless, is very close to actually carried out experiments. Let's imagine (Fig. 3) that electrons emitted by some source, for example, incandescent wiring, fly through two branches in the screen. From each answer, electrons can fly in all directions. Behind the screen we can move the electron counter parallel to the answer screen. The electron counter allows recording of every electron that enters it. This means that the number of electrons can be directly counted.

It is natural to assume that each electron entering the counter has gone through one of two reactions. Therefore, if we count the number of electrons that entered the counter through the counter with the second closed, then do the same with the electrons that entered the counter through the counter with the first closed, then we can rightly estimate that the number of electrons that entered the counter through both branches will be equal to the sum shown by the counter. This requires common sense. We are sure that this would be the case if we fired a machine gun through an armored shield with two arms, behind which in some place there was a box of sand where the leaking bullets would get stuck. There is no doubt that the number of bullets that fall into a box of sand with open holes in the shield is equal to the sum of the number of bullets that fall into the same box through each of the holes separately (of course, over the same period of time, say, an hour).

But when we “shoot” electrons, this doesn’t work! Moreover, it may turn out that a counter installed in the proper place, which registers the same number of electrons as they pass through each answer separately (when one of the answers is closed), will not register a single electron when both answers are open (Fig. 3).

The question naturally arises: how can this be?

It is not difficult to see here the same phenomenon that is observed when a screen is illuminated by two identical light sources, that is, the phenomenon of integration.

Let us now try to answer the question why quantum mechanics is incomprehensible. This will help us find the roots of the idealistic conclusions that some philosophers and physicists have come to when analyzing the current situation in physics.
