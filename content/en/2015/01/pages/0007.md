True purple can sometimes be seen as a very beautiful purple tint to the black plumage of the rook. But this is an example of the second method of coloring that Nature uses - structural coloring. Structural coloring occurs due to the interference of light when reflected from surfaces with a microstructure

Examples of structural coloring in artistic miniatures are swarms (insect wings, bird feathers, mollusk shells), as well as in the scattering of light in a volume of matter (the color of our blue sky).

So, pelargonidin absorbs everything except red, delphinidin absorbs everything except blue, and cyanidin leaves red and blue “unabsorbed.” Now it becomes clear why poppy is red and cornflower is blue: poppy contains pelargonidin, and cornflower contains delphinidin. One thing remains unclear - where does the blue cornflower get its scarlet pelargonidin to turn red? Abstract thinking and physical principles will help us solve this riddle. Let's take another look at figure with anthocyanins. What's in front of us? You can, following tradition, say: “Three molecules.” Or you can say it differently: “This is one system of atoms, located in three different states.” The right move! Let's call our system of atoms a “supermolecule”, and its states – “neutral” (cyanidin), “hydrogen” (pelargonidin) and “hydroxyl” (delphinidin). If our supermolecule lived in isolation, its state would remain unchanged all the time. But in living organisms it is found in an aqueous solution. And in an aqueous solution there are always chemically active parts of water molecules - the hydrogen atom H and the hydroxyl group OH. The state (and color) of our supermolecule changes when these wandering particles manage to change their “antipodes” in chemical bonds. Did you get the main point? Cornflower already contains supermolecules! Only they are in the “hydroxyl” state. In order for them to turn red, they need to be transferred to the “hydrogen” state. Le Chatelier's principle will help us figure out how this can be done: in stable systems, under the influence of external influences, changes occur that tend to compensate for the results of external influence. When applied to our situation, this principle works like this. Let's imagine that we (“external influence”) add an additional number of hydrogen atoms to the solution in which our supermolecule is located.

So, an explanation of the mechanism of operation of the “red-blue” color line of plants has been constructed. Our supermolecule changes its red-blue color depending on the acidity of the plant juice. In an acidic environment (poppy, geranium, rose), it gives up a hydrostrong group into the solution and adds a hydrogen atom in its place. At the same time, it turns red. In an alkaline environment (cornflower, delphinium, forget-me-not), it attaches a hydroxyl group to itself in place of hydrogen and turns blue. Therefore, according to our theory, blue cornflower begins to turn red when, due to some reason, the acidity of its juice begins to increase. The cornflower turns blue-red at first, and then completely red.
