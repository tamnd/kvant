SCIENCE NEWS

Let there be light!

L. BELOPUKHOV

The 2014 NOBEL PRIZE IN PHYSICS went to Japanese physicists Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura “for the invention of efficient blue LEDs, which led to the advent of bright and energy-efficient white light sources.” The keyword “LED” indicates that this invention is from the field of semiconductor technology. And other, simple and understandable words reflect the fact of the enormous significance of this achievement for our lives, or, in the words of Alfred Nobel’s will, the achievement that “brought the greatest benefit to humanity.” Many people have been working on LEDs. For the first time, the glow of some natural semiconductors was recorded by radio engineers - in 1907 by an employee of the G. Marconi company Henry Round, and in 1923 by Oleg Losev at the Nizhny Novgorod radio laboratory. For almost 40 years, this effect was called “Losev glow.” But only after the creation of the modern quantum theory of solid state physics in the mid-20th century did this phenomenon become understood. The first LED emitting red light was invented in 1961 in the USA by Nick Holonyak and quickly found use in indicator and signaling devices. But bright enough red LEDs were initially very expensive. Since 1980, work has continued in a number of countries (USA, Japan, Russia) to create LEDs of all colors of the rainbow - from red to blue. However, their cost continued to be high, and their efficiency was not sufficient to create lighting devices that compete with incandescent or discharge lamps. The ability to obtain numerous light shades has become widely used in design, show business and expensive toys. But two groups of Japanese researchers continued to work hard to create efficient LEDs not for entertainment, but for illuminating streets and rooms. The main difficulty in this case was represented by sufficiently intense blue LED sources. Only they, in combination with already created sources of red, yellow and green colors, could provide a source of white light. It is enough to consider only crystalline semiconductors in which the atoms are connected to each other by pairs of shared valence electrons (the so-called covalent bond). For example, the elements of the fourth group of the periodic table are silicon, germanium. Each atom of such elements has 4 electrons, which are on average further from the nucleus than the other electrons. These electrons are valence electrons, i.e. capable of bonding with neighboring atoms. When atoms are close to each other, a symmetrical pattern is established - the atoms are connected to each other by four pairs of electrons. This covalent bond is quite strong, which means that it is impossible for the valence electron to receive a small portion of energy to break away from its bond. According to quantum solid state physics, a covalent electron cannot, with a slight increase in its energy, remain partially attached to the atom. It can only have two states - either strongly bound or free. Anything else is prohibited by nature (quantum laws). Therefore, the minimum energy required to release an electron is called the energy gap in solid state physics. An electron can sometimes receive such energy from a neighboring atom when the latter, in its vibrational motion, has an amplitude significantly different from the average. The vibrational motion of atoms in the crystal lattice of a solid body exists at any temperature (according to quantum theory, even at absolute zero). At a temperature of the order of 300 K (room temperature), the average energy of vibrational motion of atoms is 0.025 eV. And the energy gap in semiconductors is at least 50 times greater. In order for a valence electron to acquire such energy and become free, one of the neighboring atoms must experience a very strong vibration, the energy of which will differ significantly from the average. This is an extremely rare event, so there are few detached electrons. But these electrons are capable of more or less free chaotic movement throughout the crystal. And in an electric field, their movement becomes oriented, similar to the directed movement of free conduction electrons in a metal. The crystal conducts current, although it is quite weak. The specific conductivity of such substances is $10^{12}$ times less than that of conductors (metals). Therefore, they are called semiconductors. But semiconductors have a very important qualitative difference from metals - the formation of “holes”. The vacant place in the bond structure, formed after the release of one of the electrons, does not remain so for long - it is replaced by some valence electron from one of the neighboring atoms.
