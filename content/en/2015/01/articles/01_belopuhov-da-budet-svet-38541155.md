Let there be light!

L. BELOPUKHOV

The 2014 NOBEL PRIZE IN PHYSICS went to Japanese physicists Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura “for the invention of efficient blue LEDs, which led to the advent of bright and energy-efficient white light sources.” The keyword “LED” indicates that this invention is from the field of semiconductor technology. And other, simple and understandable words reflect the fact of the enormous significance of this achievement for our lives, or, in the words of Alfred Nobel’s will, the achievement that “brought the greatest benefit to humanity.” Many people have been working on LEDs. For the first time, the glow of some natural semiconductors was recorded by radio engineers - in 1907 by an employee of the G. Marconi company Henry Round, and in 1923 by Oleg Losev at the Nizhny Novgorod radio laboratory. For almost 40 years, this effect was called “Losev glow.” But only after the creation of the modern quantum theory of solid state physics in the mid-20th century did this phenomenon become understood. The first LED emitting red light was invented in 1961 in the USA by Nick Holonyak and quickly found use in indicator and signaling devices. But bright enough red LEDs were initially very expensive. Since 1980, work has continued in a number of countries (USA, Japan, Russia) to create LEDs of all colors of the rainbow - from red to blue. However, their cost continued to be high, and their efficiency was not sufficient to create lighting devices that compete with incandescent or discharge lamps. The ability to obtain numerous light shades has become widely used in design, show business and expensive toys. But two groups of Japanese researchers continued to work hard to create efficient LEDs not for entertainment, but for illuminating streets and rooms. The main difficulty in this case was represented by sufficiently intense blue LED sources. Only they, in combination with already created sources of red, yellow and green colors, could provide a source of white light. It is enough to consider only crystalline semiconductors in which the atoms are connected to each other by pairs of shared valence electrons (the so-called covalent bond). For example, the elements of the fourth group of the periodic table are silicon, germanium. Each atom of such elements has 4 electrons, which are on average further from the nucleus than the other electrons. These electrons are valence electrons, i.e. capable of bonding with neighboring atoms. When atoms are close to each other, a symmetrical pattern is established - the atoms are connected to each other by four pairs of electrons. This covalent bond is quite strong, which means that it is impossible for the valence electron to receive a small portion of energy to break away from its bond. According to quantum solid state physics, a covalent electron cannot, with a slight increase in its energy, remain partially attached to the atom. It can only have two states - either strongly bound or free. Anything else is prohibited by nature (quantum laws). Therefore, the minimum energy required to release an electron is called the energy gap in solid state physics. An electron can sometimes receive such energy from a neighboring atom when the latter, in its vibrational motion, has an amplitude significantly different from the average. The vibrational motion of atoms in the crystal lattice of a solid body exists at any temperature (according to quantum theory, even at absolute zero). At a temperature of the order of 300 K (room temperature), the average energy of vibrational motion of atoms is 0.025 eV. And the energy gap in semiconductors is at least 50 times greater. In order for a valence electron to acquire such energy and become free, one of the neighboring atoms must experience a very strong vibration, the energy of which will differ significantly from the average. This is an extremely rare event, so there are few detached electrons. But these electrons are capable of more or less free chaotic movement throughout the crystal. And in an electric field, their movement becomes oriented, similar to the directed movement of free conduction electrons in a metal. The crystal conducts current, although it is quite weak. The specific conductivity of such substances is $10^{12}$ times less than that of conductors (metals). Therefore, they are called semiconductors. But semiconductors have a very important qualitative difference from metals - the formation of “holes”. The vacant place in the bond structure, formed after the release of one of the electrons, does not remain so for long - it is replaced by some valence electron from one of the neighboring atoms. and again. Each freed electron gives birth to a freed “apartment” - a hole in the harmonious structure of covalent bonds. The movement of electrons from one position to another is chaotic - the hole moves chaotically throughout the crystal. But in an electric field these movements become oriented. The movement of no negative charge is equivalent to the movement of a positive charge. The current corresponding to this movement is the current of positive charges - holes, and the associated conductivity is called hole conductivity. The number of holes corresponds to the number of released electrons, therefore the electron and hole conductivity of the semiconductor are approximately the same value. This total conductivity of a pure semiconductor is called intrinsic conductivity. The difference between the intrinsic conductivity of semiconductors and the purely electronic conductivity of metals is not only quantitative. The conductivity of semiconductors changes completely differently, for example, with a change in temperature. As the temperature rises, the probability of electrons breaking away from an atomic bond sharply (exponentially) increases, which means the concentration of electrons and holes sharply increases. The conductivity of semiconductors increases exponentially with temperature, while for metals when heated, on the contrary, it decreases (according to a linear law). Obviously, the conductivity of semiconductors can be affected by the presence of foreign impurities, and with the help of impurities the semiconductor can be purposefully “damaged.” For example, make its conductivity become almost purely electronic - without the formation of holes. To do this, you need to add a strictly controlled amount of a pentavalent element - arsenic or phosphorus - to the tetravalent element. During cooling and crystallization, impurity atoms will be built into the structure of tetravalent atoms, but each impurity atom will have an “orphan” fifth electron. In an electric field, these electrons will be the same conduction electrons as the semiconductor’s own free electrons. But, firstly, there will be much more of them (usually $10^{6}$ times more), and secondly, no holes will form. Such an impurity semiconductor will have almost 100% only electronic conductivity and only a small fraction of a percent – ​​hole conductivity. It is called an n-type semiconductor (from negative), and the impurity is called a donor (giving). But you can do the opposite - add an admixture of a trivalent element - gallium or indium - to the tetravalent element. When its atoms are integrated into the structure of tetravalent atoms, they will lack one electron to form four pair-electronic bonds with their neighbors - a hole is formed. Just as in the case of intrinsic conductivity, the hole will begin to be replaced by electrons of neighboring atoms and move throughout the crystal. In an electric field, this movement will be in the nature of a current of positive charges. Such an impurity semiconductor will have almost 100% only hole conductivity and only a fraction of a percent – ​​electronic conductivity. It is called a p-type semiconductor (from positive), and the impurity is called acceptor (receiving). The main application of impurity semiconductors is the creation of semiconductor devices based on the properties of the p–n junction - the contact between n- and p-type semiconductors. Upon contact, diffusion of conduction electrons from the n-type semiconductor into the contact area and diffusion of holes from the p-type semiconductor into the contact area begins. But

diffusion quickly ends - it is interrupted by the fact that the n-type semiconductor, having lost some electrons, acquires an excess positive charge, and the r-type semiconductor acquires an excess negative charge. A potential barrier appears on the path of further movement of electrons and holes. The internal electric field of this barrier is directed from the n-type semiconductor to the p-type semiconductor, thereby preventing further movement of electrons and holes. Therefore, the p–n contact is often called a blocking layer. But if this contact is connected to a current source so that an electric field appears in the blocking layer, opposite in direction to the internal field of the potential barrier (to do this, the positive pole of the current source must be connected to a p-type semiconductor), then the potential barrier in the path of charges will significantly weaken. A current will flow through the circuit, the strength of which will increase exponentially with increasing applied voltage. At the same voltage, it will be thousands of times greater than the current through a pure (pure) semiconductor. This connection of the p-n junction is called direct. If the current source is connected in the opposite direction, then the potential barrier, on the contrary, will increase, and there will be practically no current through the contact area (or rather, it will be very weak). This connection is called reverse. Thus, current flows through the p–n junction in almost only one direction. The alternating current is “rectified” (in this case, during half the period of the alternating sinusoidal current, the contact does not allow current to pass through). By analogy with the first radio tubes that appeared at the turn of the twentieth century, the device was called a diode. The main advantage of semiconductor rectifiers compared to tube rectifiers is their compactness. The semiconductor diode quickly became more complex with the addition of other electrodes for the purpose of amplifying and generating signals; the combination of p-n junctions (called transistors) became the main element of semiconductor radio circuits and many other devices in which the use of radio tubes was difficult or even impossible due to their bulk. The creators of the first semiconductor devices, William Bradford Shockley, John Bardeen and Walter Hauser Brattain, became Nobel laureates in 1956 “for their research into semiconductors and the discovery of the transistor effect.” The semiconductor revolution has changed the face of human civilization so much that even the most daring science fiction writers could not have imagined it. In 1961, research began on another property of the p–n junction – its generation of light. Indeed, electromagnetic radiation should arise in the contact area - the birth of quanta of radiant energy. Indeed, when a p–n junction is directly connected to a current source, the intense movement in the contact area of ​​free electrons and holes towards each other should lead to a random (probabilistic) replacement of holes with electrons. In physics, this process is called recombination of an electron and a hole. In this case, the electron abruptly acquires negative potential energy (negative because it is the energy of attractive forces). And just as during the recombination of an electron and an ion, a radiation quantum should appear, the energy of which is equal to the value of the potential binding energy that appears in the electron. This is the reverse process of the release of an electron when a covalent bond is broken. Therefore, the radiation quantum arising from the recombination of an electron and a hole has an energy equal to the band gap.
