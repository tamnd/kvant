Let us emphasize that we are interested in the distances at which the forces of attraction act. Here the potential energy is negative and is determined mainly by electrostatic interaction. Inside a liquid, the potential energy for a single molecule can be estimated as the sum of the interaction energies of all molecules adjacent to it. On the surface there will be almost two times fewer neighbors, therefore, the resulting energy of the surface molecule will be almost twice as large (let’s not forget that the interaction energy here is negative). As a result, surface molecules become more active than internal ones - their further behavior is aimed at reducing their energy in any way: either going deeper into the substance, or advantageously attaching a molecule of a foreign substance to themselves. The larger the surface of a liquid, the greater its activity. Applying the same reasoning to a solid substance, let’s say: with the same volume, the substance will be more active with a larger proportion of surface molecules in it - i.e. with a greater degree of crushing. So, if a substance is crushed into cubes containing 27 molecules, then it will contain only one internal molecule and can, for example, dissolve in just one stage - it is enough for the upper layer of 26 active molecules to dissolve (react). It is easy to calculate in the same way how many times longer it will take a cube of 1000 molecules to dissolve. So we are beginning to understand one of the main ideas of nanotechnology - even simple grinding of a substance changes its activity. Now we can write down the main formula that describes the phenomenon of surface tension. Indeed, in the absence of chemical reactions, the change in the interaction energy of a certain volume of a substance is proportional to the change in the number of surfaces

This coefficient $\sigma$ is called the surface tension coefficient. Its calculation is a complex theoretical problem, the solution of which requires information about the shape of the molecules. In practical applications, it is determined experimentally, especially when it comes to liquids. For some liquids, its approximate value can be found in the tables. Thus, for mercury $\sigma = 470$ mJ $м^{2} = 470$ mN m; for water $\sigma = 71$ mN m; for glycerol $\sigma = 64$ mN m; for kerosene $\sigma = 29$ mN m. Using dimensional considerations, the basic formula can be rewritten as follows: $F = \sigma L$, where L is the length of the liquid-solid boundary, and F is the force acting on this boundary perpendicular to the boundary line in each section. Here, a reduction in the width of the variable area is simply made in the notation for the work of the potential force and in the expression for the reduction in area. Why do reference books list the surface tension coefficient only for liquids? Mainly because for crystals its value will depend on the location of the cleavage plane - by splitting the crystal in different directions, we expose a different number of molecules or atoms per unit of its surface. As we see, the force of surface tension differs from the force of elasticity in that it is always determined only by the properties of the substance, the forces of interaction of its molecules with each other, and does not depend either on the degree of stretching of the surface or on the area and thickness of the liquid layer.
