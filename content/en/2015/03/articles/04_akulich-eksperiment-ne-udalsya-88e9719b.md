"QUANT" FOR JUNIOR SCHOOLCHILDREN

The experiment failed

I. AKULICH

LAST YEAR, ONE LYCEUM ORGANIZED a MATHEMATICS evening, at which all schoolchildren were asked to do the following experiment: “Take the year of your birth, write down its numbers in reverse order and add them with the original number. Also write down the digits of the resulting amount in reverse order and subtract the smaller number from the larger number. The result must be divisible by 99." Petya, a C student, did just that, but mixed up the order of actions and first found the difference, and only then the sum. Therefore the experiment failed. What result did he get?

* * * Let's start with an example. Let Petya be born, say, in 1999. If he performed the actions in the right order, then first he would receive the sum 1999 + 9991 = = 11990. This number in reverse order will give 09911, i.e. 9911, and the desired difference – 11990 – $9911 = 2079 = 99 \cdot 21$ – is indeed divisible by 99. And if the actions are mixed up, first Petya will receive the number 9991 – 1999 = 7992, and then the number 7992 + 2997 = 10989. But this number is equal to $99 \cdot 111$, i.e. is also divisible by 99. It is not yet clear why the experiment succeeds with the correct order, or why it might not have worked for Petya when he mixed up the steps.

⟦column⟧

We invite those who wish to answer the questions themselves, and then check our explanation.

* * * The solution to the problem (like planet Earth) rests on three pillars - auxiliary theorems. Here they are. 1st whale If the digits of a four-digit number are written in reverse order and added to the original number, the result will be divisible by 11. The proof is as follows. Let's denote the digits of a four-digit number (from left to right) by the letters a, b, c and d. Then it is equal to 1000a + 100b +10c + d, the “reversed” number is 1000d + 100c +10b + a, and their sum is 1001a + 110b +110c + 1001d, which is clearly divisible by 11 (since each term is divisible by 11). (In general, the statement is true for any number with an even number of digits, but we don’t need a different number of digits.) 2nd pillar. If the digits of a number divisible by 11 are written in reverse order, then the result will also be divisible by 11. The proof of this whale is based on the following test for divisibility by 11: a number is divisible by 11 if and only if the difference between the sums of its digits in even and odd places is a multiple of 11 (we will not prove this test here - it is widely known and often found in the literature). Obviously, the sums of the digits in even and odd places will not change when writing the digits in reverse order, and therefore the difference between them, as it was a multiple of 11, will remain so. 3rd whale If you rewrite the digits of a number in any other order and subtract it from the original number, then the result will be divisible by 9. This theorem immediately follows from the test for divisibility by 9, so we won’t dwell here either. So, first, let’s figure out why the required result was sure to be obtained if all the actions were performed as expected. In fact, the year of birth of any schoolchild is a four-digit number, so after adding it with the “reversed” year, the result is a number divisible by 11 (1st whale!). Further, if the digits of this number are written in reverse order, then the result will also be divided by 11 (2nd pillar!). Therefore, after subtracting the smaller number from the larger number, the difference will also be divided by 11. In addition, it will be divided by 9 (3rd pillar!). Therefore the final result must indeed be divisible by $11 \cdot 9 = 99$.
