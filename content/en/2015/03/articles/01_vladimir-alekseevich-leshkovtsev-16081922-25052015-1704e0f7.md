Vladimir Alekseevich Leshkovtsev (08/16/1922–05/25/2015)

On May 25, 2015, the oldest employee of our magazine, Vladimir Alekseevich Leshkovtsev, passed away. The idea of ​​​​creating the magazine «Kvant» is more than half a century old, but it was published only after several years of intensive preparation and careful selection of the editorial board and editorial staff. In order for «Kvant» to be “emitted” regularly, month after month, it took not only the creative efforts of its authors and members of the editorial board, but also the ability of the editorial staff to ensure the uninterrupted publishing process. One of those who, from the first issue of the magazine for twenty years, was responsible for its publication was Vladimir Alekseevich Leshkovtsev, a full-time deputy editor-in-chief. It is difficult to say who Vladimir Alekseevich did not know at the USSR Academy of Sciences and the USSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences, the founders of the journal. His close, literally personal contacts with a huge number of scientists and teachers greatly helped in solving the complex problems of publishing the journal. However, Vladimir Alekseevich was not only engaged in “routine” - his articles on the history of Soviet physics, reviews and reviews of popular science books, editorials related to memorable dates repeatedly appeared on pages «Kvant». Vladimir Alekseevich’s specialty was the “Philatelist’s Corner” section, which presented a whole collection of stamps on scientific and technical topics. One cannot fail to mention his active work as one of the best lecturers of the All-Union Society “Znanie”; the employees of «Kvant» had the good fortune to constantly be convinced of his oratory skills without leaving their workplace. Those whom fate brought closely together with Vladimir Alekseevich Leshkovtsev will not forget their joint work, confidential communication with him, his lessons of wisdom, the value of which only increases with time.

Let us now consider the process of nuclear fission. Let the nuclear “drop”, having absorbed a neutron entering it from the outside, begin to oscillate and at some point stretch out. A spherical drop has the minimum surface area for a given volume. For an elongated drop, the surface energy increases, and the core binding energy correspondingly decreases. On the other hand, when the nucleus is stretched, the average distance between nucleons increases, and the energy of their electrostatic repulsion decreases, as a result of which the binding energy should increase. If electrostatic interaction wins in this struggle, the nucleus will rupture; if there is surface tension, the core will return to its original state.

But in the process of nuclear fission, as a result of bombardment by a neutron, several more neutrons are always emitted (for uranium, usually 2-3). These neutrons, in turn, can cause further nuclear fission, even more free neutrons will appear, and so on - an increasing chain reaction will occur. It was the practical implementation of such a nuclear fission chain reaction, accompanied by the release of a huge amount of thermal energy, that refuted Rutherford’s pessimistic prediction. However, this self-sustaining process occurs only if the mass of the uranium sample exceeds a certain “critical” value. Indeed, if the mass of the selected sample is small, then each neutron emitted during the fission of a nucleus has a high probability of leaving its volume before colliding with another nucleus and causing a new fission. If the mass of the sample exceeds a critical value, then the chain reaction “accelerates”: per unit time, more and more nuclear fissions occur, which lead to more and more heat release. Without heat control, an explosion inevitably occurs. Estimating the critical mass of uranium-235 required to initiate a chain reaction became a critical challenge for physicists on both sides of the front during World War II. It seems that the Germans, including the great Werner Heisenberg, greatly overestimated this value - the resulting bomb seemed to them too heavy to be delivered to the target by plane. Perhaps that is why their atomic project turned out to be only one of several directions for creating a “wonder weapon” (wunder waffe). On the other side of the English Channel, the German-born physicist Rudolf Peierls, who emigrated from Germany to Britain when Hitler came to power in 1933, came to much more optimistic conclusions in his assessment of the magnitude of the critical mass. He even published his calculations, but without indicating the possibility of military application of these results. The latter, while reading Peierls's work, occurred to Otto Frisch, an Austrian physicist who also emigrated to Great Britain. In 1940, Frisch and Peierls wrote a memorandum, this time strictly confidential, which was presented to the British authorities. In this document, the authors described the main stages of the process of creating an atomic bomb and assessed its destructive power. Soon the Allies got down to business in earnest, and a grandiose nuclear research program was developed - the Manhattan Project. It started in the USA in 1942 and was implemented within three years as a result of the efforts of a large team of scientists, among whom, in addition to Frisch and Peierls, were such outstanding minds as Enrico Fermi, Robert Oppenheimer, Richard Feynman and many others. The explosion of two atomic bombs in Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 ended the Second World War and began the atomic era in human history. Currently, nuclear fission is finding its peaceful application in the energy sector of many countries. The conversion of nuclear energy into easily usable electrical energy occurs, as a rule, as follows. In a reactor, nuclear fuel, usually uranium, breaks down into nuclei of lighter elements on the periodic table when bombarded by neutrons. With each act of such division, significant energy is released - and this is the essence of the process.
