Alternative geographies, geology, technology

Not only physics can be alternative. In science fiction literature there are examples of the alternative nature of geography and geology. This is a world without oil (Evgeniy and Lyubov Lukin, “Missionaries”, “Good Intentions”), the many diverse worlds of Hal Clement, worlds with life built not on oxygen, but on fluorine (Ivan Efremov, “Heart of the Snake”), a world with an eternal source of mechanical energy (perpetually rotating rods sticking out of the ground) and a world with an excess of isotopes. Alternative geographies—especially the distribution of oil or hemp—bring alternative policies, and the distribution of uranium and diamonds is also important. Most authors consider social consequences, but we are interested in physics and technology. The existing human civilization uses the movement of fuel (coal by rail, oil by pipe) or electricity (by wire) to transfer energy. Electricity is used to work with information - mechanical computers existed in reality, and are now mentioned in some science fiction works (steampunk). Are there any reasonable grounds for speculating about an alternative civilization without electricity at all, or without electricity, but with information electricity (communications and computers), or, finally, with electricity, but without information electricity? Differences in the order and significance of the sections: on Mercury, less attention would be paid to ferromagnetism, and on the Moon, less attention would be paid to magnetism in general; on Jupiter, the physics of gases would begin not with ideal gases, but with Van der Wals gas; the octopuses of the Earth would consider the laws of hydrodynamics in detail, and Newton's first law would be in small print in their textbook. Why else?

Discussions about what can be made from materials with new properties can be found in the literature. Most often, these are discussions about ultra-strong materials and superconductivity at room temperature. But for some reason no one thinks about what will change if, for example, an ideal heat insulator is created. Try to imagine how such materials could be used and what it would lead to. Discussions about varying the parameters of materials and the reached limit values ​​are useful for training the brain and developing imagination, but not only. Firstly, today we have a certain material, and tomorrow there will be a better one - how do we apply it? It is useful to know this, at least partially, in advance. Secondly, if we were able to figure out what we would use it for, and it is clear that the application will be useful, will open up new opportunities, and so on, then it makes sense to focus our efforts on the development of such material. Thirdly, such reasoning is useful - perhaps surprisingly - for the development of physics. Because solving any problems is useful for development. Problems from your problem books are for the development of your brain, and serious physical problems are for the development of physics itself. And it is likely that after some time these two developments will be connected...
