geometry and optics

Well, why was it necessary to separate these concepts if it is clear that we will be talking about the well-known section of the physics course - about geometric optics? However, not everything is so simple. If we turn to the place of its origin - Ancient Greece and to the time - the 3rd century BC, it turns out that there and then the conceptual apparatus of that part of mathematics that studies the spatial relationships and shapes of bodies was only formed, i.e. geometry itself. Optics, as a discipline that studies the properties of light, was called “visual art,” as if hinting at its less noble origin. Of course, even then these two cognitive directions in the search for a connection between observed reality and methods of describing it strongly influenced each other. The problems they exchanged contributed to the creation of a language of rigorous science and tested the effectiveness and efficiency of this language. But only by the 17th century were the laws of geometric optics established and refined, which reigned supreme for the next two centuries and embodied the synthesis of the two sciences. Finally, the 19th century separated it from physical (wave) optics and put it in its place in the “table of ranks” that has survived to this day. The history of geometric optics is one of the most interesting examples of the interweaving and enrichment of various fields of physics and mathematics. It is natural to begin today's conversation with the first open laws of geometric optics - the rectilinear propagation and reflection of light. And we will turn to other patterns that connect optics and geometry in the next issue of Kaleidoscope.
