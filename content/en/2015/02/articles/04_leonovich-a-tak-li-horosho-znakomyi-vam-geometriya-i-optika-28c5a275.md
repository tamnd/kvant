geometry and optics

Well, why was it necessary to separate these concepts if it is clear that we will be talking about the well-known section of the physics course - about geometric optics? However, not everything is so simple. If we turn to the place of its origin - Ancient Greece and to the time - the 3rd century BC, it turns out that there and then the conceptual apparatus of that part of mathematics that studies the spatial relationships and shapes of bodies was only formed, i.e. geometry itself. Optics, as a discipline that studies the properties of light, was called “visual art,” as if hinting at its less noble origin. Of course, even then these two cognitive directions in the search for a connection between observed reality and methods of describing it strongly influenced each other. The problems they exchanged contributed to the creation of a language of rigorous science and tested the effectiveness and efficiency of this language. But only by the 17th century were the laws of geometric optics established and refined, which reigned supreme for the next two centuries and embodied the synthesis of the two sciences. Finally, the 19th century separated it from physical (wave) optics and put it in its place in the “table of ranks” that has survived to this day. The history of geometric optics is one of the most interesting examples of the interweaving and enrichment of various fields of physics and mathematics. It is natural to begin today's conversation with the first open laws of geometric optics - the rectilinear propagation and reflection of light. And we will turn to other patterns that connect optics and geometry in the next issue of Kaleidoscope.

It is curious that... ...in the famous “Elements”, which summed up the achievements of ancient Greek mathematics, Euclid, trying to logically construct geometry, often relied on intuition and trusted the eye. Apparently, the concept of a straight line arose from the idea of ​​​​the rectilinear propagation of light in a homogeneous medium - the so-called “optical rays”. The word “ray” itself is correlated in meaning with the word “arrow”. ...Archimedes studied both “pure” mathematics and its applications, including military ones. Investigating the properties of mirrors, he studied the construction of reflections in them, especially in “incendiary” mirrors, whose properties were associated with conic sections. Thus, he proved that rays emerging from the focus of an elliptical mirror, after reflection, are collected at its other focus. ...geometric constructions using compasses and a ruler, which are often impossible to do without when solving problems in optics, were considered by ancient Greek scientists to be the most elegant and valued higher than constructions using other tools. ...along with mathematics and astronomy, medieval Arab scientists diligently studied optics, which, in their opinion, aroused interest in geometry. For example, the famous author of the optical treatise Alhazen talks in it not only about flat, but also about spherical, cylindrical and conical concave and convex mirrors; receives images in them, determining their position and size. He also examines the ability of a paraboloid of revolution to reflect rays emanating from its focus in a parallel beam. ...the English philosopher and naturalist of the 13th century Roger Bacon, who laid the foundation for catacaustics - the study of incendiary devices, believed that optics was “the decoration of all philosophy.” However, for sharply criticizing the works of Aristotle and engaging in such things incomprehensible to his contemporaries as the camera obscura, whose invention was attributed to him by legend, he was accused of heresy and magic and thrown into prison.

...strict rules for constructing images in spherical mirrors were established at the end of the 17th century by Christian Huygens and Count Ehrenfried von Tschirnhaus, known as the inventor of European white porcelain. In addition, these scientists, thanks to the study of the properties of “incendiary” curves, created extremely large focusing lenses and mirrors, with the help of which they were able to demonstrate the flammability of diamond. ...the famous mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss left a noticeable mark in geodesy, improving optical signaling for its needs and inventing a special device - heliotrope, and in physics, where, among other things, he developed the mirror counting method used in sensitive electromagnetic devices. ...the outstanding naturalist Hermann Helmholtz, who first gave a mathematical interpretation of the law of conservation of energy, was also the designer of original measuring instruments. Thus, he invented the ophthalmoscope - an eye mirror that allows you to observe the bottom of the human eye. ...the ability of cat's eyes to glow in the dark is associated with the reflection of light from them in an almost parallel beam. Based on this effect, in the 30s of the 20th century, a device called the “cat's eye” was developed. It was installed on dangerous sections of roads, warning drivers with the reflected light of the headlights of their own approaching cars. ...when constructing complex engineering structures - bridges, tunnels, high-rise buildings, large scientific installations - it is necessary to control distances of up to several kilometers with an accuracy of fractions of a millimeter. Laser rangefinders can cope with this task - their beams diverge very little. A compact example of such a device is a laser pointer. ...a few years ago a television was created with a liquid crystal screen that, when turned off, performed the functions of a mirror. The development of modern opticians and engineers, recognized as one of the most daring inventions of the early 21st century, has led to a new design of the home space, successfully competing with mirrors made of glass and amalgam.
