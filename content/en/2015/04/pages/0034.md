optics and geometry

The laws of rectilinear propagation and reflection of light, which were discussed in the last issue of Kaleidoscope, have practically been known since ancient times. Then they were repeatedly checked and confirmed both in experiments and calculations. But the history of the search for the law of light refraction turned out to be both longer and incomparably more dramatic. If physicists sometimes lacked geometric considerations in their conclusions, mathematicians were often carried away exclusively by the formal side of the issue, not paying attention to the physical uncertainty of the concepts they introduced. It happened that, arguing with each other, scientists managed to prove what they hoped to refute. However, the interaction of sciences in these disputes brought very tangible results. Thus, the optical principle established in the 17th century by Pierre Fermat led to the development of a method for solving extremal problems; in fact, all further development of ray - geometric - optics and the construction of wave optics were based on it. Fermat's achievement became the founder of a whole family of so-called variational principles in physics. The 20th century brought no less amazing practical results. The predicted and then discovered properties of new optical media—metamaterials—forced changes in many conventional ideas. Today, physicists, engineers and mathematicians, in creative collaboration, create, by manipulating these properties, new optics for microelectronics, computing devices, communications, film, photography and video equipment. Let’s try to equip ourselves with “optical tools” to connect to this exciting work.
