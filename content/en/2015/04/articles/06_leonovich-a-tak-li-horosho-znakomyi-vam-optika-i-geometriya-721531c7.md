optics and geometry

The laws of rectilinear propagation and reflection of light, which were discussed in the last issue of Kaleidoscope, have practically been known since ancient times. Then they were repeatedly checked and confirmed both in experiments and calculations. But the history of the search for the law of light refraction turned out to be both longer and incomparably more dramatic. If physicists sometimes lacked geometric considerations in their conclusions, mathematicians were often carried away exclusively by the formal side of the issue, not paying attention to the physical uncertainty of the concepts they introduced. It happened that, arguing with each other, scientists managed to prove what they hoped to refute. However, the interaction of sciences in these disputes brought very tangible results. Thus, the optical principle established in the 17th century by Pierre Fermat led to the development of a method for solving extremal problems; in fact, all further development of ray - geometric - optics and the construction of wave optics were based on it. Fermat's achievement became the founder of a whole family of so-called variational principles in physics. The 20th century brought no less amazing practical results. The predicted and then discovered properties of new optical media—metamaterials—forced changes in many conventional ideas. Today, physicists, engineers and mathematicians, in creative collaboration, create, by manipulating these properties, new optics for microelectronics, computing devices, communications, film, photography and video equipment. Let’s try to equip ourselves with “optical tools” to connect to this exciting work.

It is curious that... ...the refraction of light was first described in detail by the ancient Greek scientist Cleomedes in the 1st century AD. He knew about the change in angle when a beam passes from a denser medium to a less dense one and back. ...Ptolemy's experiments in the 2nd century AD to determine the amount of refraction of light when passing from air to water, from air to glass and from water to glass is one of the extremely rare examples when ancient scientists were not satisfied with just observations, but resorted to experiments. ...although by the time of the publication of Kepler's Dioptrics in 1611, the law of light refraction had not yet been precisely formulated, the author of this work was able to describe the properties of various lenses. For the first time, it was explained how to construct an image using the path of two light rays and looking for the intersection of these rays or their extensions. ...in the papers of the Dutch mathematician Snell, who died in 1626, an optical treatise was found containing the formulation of the law of refraction of light. The first printed publication of this law in modern form appeared in 1637, by the French scientist René Descartes. The French mathematician Pierre Fermat reduced the problem of finding a law to solving a purely geometric problem. A much simpler derivation of the law, using, however, the principle put forward by Fermat, was given by the author of the wave theory of light, Christian Huygens. ...the complexity of Fermat's calculations turned out to be associated with the absence at that time of one of the most important mathematical concepts - derivatives. The derivation of Snell's law based on their application, included in classical textbooks, was found by the outstanding German scientist Leibniz in 1684. ...immediately after the discovery of the law of light refraction, attempts were made to perform detailed calculations of lens systems. Thus, the Italian Bonaventura Cavalieri discovered in 1647 the dependence of the focal length of a lens on the curvature of the surfaces limiting it. The positions of images in thin lenses for an arbitrary position of an object were geometrically found in 1669 by Newton's teacher Isaac Barrow. And in 1693, the English researcher Edmund Halley obtained an algebraic formula for a thin lens. ...even Leonardo da Vinci, in his “Code on the Eye,” put forward the idea of ​​​​contact lenses. It was only in the 18th century that one of the creators of wave optics, Thomas Young, was able to apply it in practice. He applied a layer of transparent gel to the eye of the English astronomer John Herschel, which allowed him to eliminate his vision defect. ...the development of geometric optics was contributed by such remarkable mathematicians as Leonhard Euler, who in 1766 found the connection between the refractive index of the substance of a prism, its refractive angle and the deflection of a light ray during its passage, and Carl Gauss, who developed the theory of images in paraxial rays of light - thin beams that deviate slightly from the optical axis of the system. ...progress achieved in materials science and leading to the emergence of media with negative refraction of light makes it possible to create so-called superprisms, capable of forming images of details smaller than the wavelength of the light used, which is impossible for lenses with positive refraction. ...unlike a conventional converging lens, in a gravitational lens formed by massive space objects, rays passing closer to the observation axis are refracted more strongly, i.e. to the attracting center. Recently, a galaxy that found itself in the path of radiation emitted by the material surrounding the black hole amplified this radiation, turning it into four sources. Their analysis made it possible to measure the rotation speed of a black hole six billion light years away.
