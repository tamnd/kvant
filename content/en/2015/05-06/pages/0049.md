Here is another photograph taken inside the same camera obscura (Fig.3). We use a lens. At the end of last year, we gave two lectures for schoolchildren at a weekly interval. One was called “Camera Obscura - a well-forgotten old thing”, the other was called “Descartes-Newton-Young’s Rainbow”. After each lecture, we decided to show the students how our camera works. A lot of people signed up for the lectures, so, firstly, the visit to the camera obscura had to be divided into sessions, and secondly, there was no way to spend half an hour each time adapting to the dark. In connection with all this, we replaced the millimeter hole in the diaphragm with a weakly positive lens with a diameter of 4 cm. The brightness of the image increased significantly, and it now took a matter of minutes for the eye to adapt. 10/25/2014 – observation of sunspots. On the day the second lecture was given, we were lucky. The weather was sunny, and with the help of a pinhole camera with an entrance hole of 3 mm in diameter, it was possible to show the students spots on the Sun. In the first photograph (Fig.4, left), taken from a screen 5 m away from the window, a bare branch against the background of the Sun and a group of sunspots are visible. By turning your room into a large camera obscura, you can conduct them without leaving home. To begin with, the diameter of the inlet hole in the diaphragm can be taken as 3 mm, then try other sizes. If you want to use a lens instead of a small hole, and the distance from the window to the opposite wall is L meters, then the easiest way is to take a lens for glasses with an optical power of +1/L diopters. Observations are best made on a sunny day.
