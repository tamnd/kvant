KVANT 2015/№5-6

Many new elementary particles were discovered at these installations, which led to the need for a theoretical rethinking of the results obtained. In 1964, Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig proposed the quark model to explain the accumulated evidence in accelerator and cosmic ray experiments on the production of large numbers (on the order of 100) of new strongly interacting particles, called “hadrons.” To describe all hadronic states known at that time, it was sufficient to introduce three quarks: u (up), d (down) and s (strange). For example, the well-known hadrons, the proton and the neutron, which make up the nuclei of the atoms around us, are formed from three quarks: uud for the proton and ddu for the neutron. In addition, in order to correctly describe the spins and charges of observed hadrons, quarks must have a spin of 1/2 and a fractional electric charge - in units of electron charge, the charge of the u-quark is 2/3, the charge of the d- and s-quarks is –1/3 each. Accordingly, the masses of u- and d-quarks were expected to be about 1/3 of the proton mass, i.e. about 300 MeV, and the mass of the s-quark is approximately 500 MeV. However, after the discovery of positively and negatively charged delta resonances, the quark composition of which should be uuu and ddd, respectively, a serious problem arose for the quark model. In order for the spin of a hyperon, in accordance with experimental data, to be equal to 1/2, two quarks of the same type must be in the same spin state, which contradicts the Pauli principle, according to which two fermions (particles with half-integer spin) of the same type cannot be in the same quantum state. The solution to this problem was found in the work of Nikolai Bogolyubov, Boris Struminsky and Albert Tavkhelidze, and then Moyen Khan and Yoichiro Nambu, who postulated the presence of a new quantum number for quarks, called “color”. Subsequently, this hypothesis found brilliant confirmation and led to the creation of the quantum theory of strong interactions - quantum chromodynamics, an integral part of the modern Standard Model, which will be discussed further. The introduction of the quark hypothesis was the next step in establishing the “minimum building blocks” of which matter is composed, as we penetrate deeper and deeper into its structure, from molecule to atom, nucleus, proton and neutron. Further, the discovery in 1974 of a new hadron with a mass of 3.1 GeV was understood as the bound state of a new quark, called a charm quark, and its corresponding antiquark. Previously, the existence of this quark was predicted theoretically to interpret data on the decays of charged K mesons. By the same time, elementary particles of another class, called leptons, were also discovered. In addition to the well-known electron, the electron neutrino $( \nu_{e} ),$ muon $( \mu )$ and the corresponding muon neutrino $( \nu_{\mu} ).$ were discovered. Leptons do not participate in strong

interactions, but are subject to electromagnetic interactions and participate in weak radioactive decays. A remarkable symmetry of the existence of two generations of quarks (u, d) (c, s) and two generations of leptons $( \nu_{e}, e ), \nu_{\mu}, \mu$ emerged. But this symmetry was broken when a third charged lepton was discovered in 1976 - the tau lepton $(\tau ),$ and in 1977 a new meson was found - ϒ (upsilon) with a mass of the order of 10 GeV, which was interpreted as a bound state of a new b quark (bottom) and its corresponding antiquark. Further experiments showed that the b quark has a spin of 1/2 and a charge of –1/3, just like the d and s quarks, while its mass is about 5 GeV. If we assume that the found tau lepton and b quark belong to the new third generation, then to preserve symmetry by analogy with the existing pairs of two generations, it was necessary to assume that there is another quark, similar to u and c, and another neutrino, similar to the muon and electron neutrino. Both of these particles were discovered in further experiments. The tau neutrino $(\nu \tau )$ was discovered in direct observations in the decays of tay leptons in 2000, and the predicted sixth quark, called the top quark, was discovered five years earlier at the Tevatron collider. Note that the found mass of the top quark (approximately 173 GeV) turned out to be many times greater than the quark masses assumed in the quark model of Gell-Mann and Zweig. Unlike the situation with all previous quarks, no new hadrons were found that would require introducing a new top quark to interpret. The discovery of the top quark and tau neutrino completed the holistic picture of the fundamental particles that make up matter and are described by the Standard Model - SM, which is the modern theory of elementary particles and their interactions: electromagnetic, weak and strong. The creation of this model is one of the main global intellectual achievements over the past 60 years, the result of the work of a large number of scientists, theorists and experimenters. Its creation was recognized by the award of more than a dozen Nobel Prizes in physics. The SM is based on several basic ideas, in particular, the gauge nature of interactions and spontaneous breaking of electroweak symmetry. The presence of symmetry in nature leads to the law of conservation of the corresponding charge, and vice versa, the existence of a conserved charge indicates the presence of symmetry associated with it (E. Noether’s theorem). Thus, the gauge theory of electromagnetic interactions is based on the conservation of electric charge and the corresponding symmetry of equations with respect to gauge transformations of the so-called Abelian group. Symmetry with respect to gauge transformations of a more complex (non-Abelian) type made it possible to construct a theory of strong interactions and describe electroweak interactions from a unified point of view.
