Modern detectors almost completely cover the areas of particle scattering ($( 4\pi$ -geometry) and can register almost all emerging particles. Muon chambers located on the outer part of the detector record muons and determine their energy and direction of motion with high accuracy. An electromagnetic calorimeter detects electrons and photons. A strong magnetic field in the detector makes it possible to determine the sign of the particle's electric charge in the direction of deflection, and the magnitude of the deflection characterizes the energy of the particle. The hadron calorimeter absorbs a stream of charged and neutral hadrons, which are secondary particles resulting from the fragmentation and hadronization of quarks or gluons, which, in turn, are the products of collisions of initial particles. A stream of hadrons flying in a narrow cone is called a hadron jet. Spatial localization in a narrow cone is due to the large momentum of the particle that generated this flow of hadrons. The energy released in the hadron calorimeter, localized in the jet cone, characterizes the momentum of the initial quark or gluon, and the direction of the cone axis characterizes the direction of emission of the quark or gluon that generated this hadron jet. Despite the fact that quarks and gluons are not observed in a free state, hadronic jets characterize with a high degree of accuracy the momenta of the quarks and gluons that generate them. Moreover, jets originating from high-energy b-quarks can be identified with high probability. B-mesons formed during the fragmentation process have a relatively long lifetime and manage to fly a significant distance (about 1 cm) before the formation of a hadron jet. This leads to a shift of the top of the cone of the resulting hadronic jet relative to the top of the primary collision, and the magnitude of this shift characterizes the probability of identifying the birth of a b-quark in a hard process. For such identification of b-quarks, it is important to have a vertex detector that makes it possible to localize the beginning of tracks of flying particles. The heaviest top quark, which has a mass comparable to the mass of a gold nucleus, manages to decay before the formation of bound hadronic states and is well identified by its decay products. Neutrinos interact too weakly with matter and require a significant mass of absorbing material in the detector for direct detection. For example, the Ice Cube Neutrino Observatory uses a cubic kilometer of ice in Antarctica as an absorbent detector, and the detectors of the Baikal Deep Sea Neutrino Telescope penetrate the water of Lake Baikal for about 1 kilometer. Thus, it is possible to determine the total transverse momentum of all neutrinos in an event as the vector sum of all particles registered in the event, taken with the opposite sign. This quantity is called the missing or lost impulse. Massive vector and scalar bosons decay almost at the point of primary interaction and are well recorded from the decay products. Additional indirect methods and information on the masses of particles in the decays of which neutrinos were formed make it possible, using the measured missing momentum, to reconstruct the longitudinal component of the neutrino momentum. When conducting an experiment, predictions within the framework of existing theories are tested, various parameters of the models are measured, and possible deviations from predictions are searched for, which may indicate the existence of new phenomena. Within the framework of the Standard Model, it is possible to calculate with a good degree of accuracy the main rigid processes, simulate the data obtained and carry out the corresponding measurements. For example, the level of accuracy in measuring the topquark mass achieved in 2015 is 0.38% $( m_{top} = 172,38 \pm 0,10$ (stat.) $\pm 0,65$ (syst.) GeV, where “stat.” – statistical error, and “syst.” - systematic). Achieving such high measurement accuracy became possible thanks to the development of various effective methods at all stages of research, ranging from theoretical methods, computer algebra and numerical calculations, as well as modern methods of multivariate analysis and statistical data processing. The complexity of the equations describing the physics of elementary particles does not allow finding exact analytical solutions. Therefore, approximate calculation methods are used, based on expansion in powers of a small dimensionless parameter inherent in this theory, and subsequent calculations using perturbation theory methods. In some theoretical approaches, there is no small dimensionless parameter, and such calculations require large-scale lattice numerical calculations using supercomputers. The large number of calculations required to account for all possible processes occurring in modern experiments required the development of methods of symbolic algebraic calculations performed automatically on computers. Accurate computer modeling of all events occurring in modern experiments has become a necessary component of ongoing research.
