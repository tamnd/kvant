## Condition

The pedestrian needs to get from field point $A$ to field point $B$ in the shortest possible time, the distance between which is 1300 m. The field is crossed by a straight road so that point $A$ is located at a distance of 600 m from it, and point $B$ is at a distance of 100 m. The speed of movement of the pedestrian across the field is 3 km/h, and road - 6 km/h. Which path should a pedestrian take? What is the minimum time? Consider the case when points $A$ and $B$ lie on the same side of the road and when they lie on opposite sides of the road.

S. S. Krogov

## Solution

A pedestrian needs to get from field point $A$ to field point $B$ in the shortest possible time, the distance between which is 1300 m. The field is crossed by a straight road so that point $A$ is located at a distance of 600 m from it, and point $B$ is at a distance of 100 m. The speed of movement of a pedestrian across the field is 3 km/h, and along the road - 6 km/h. Which path should the pedestrian take?

What is the minimum time? The distance occurs when points $A$ and $B$ lie on the same side of the road and when they lie on opposite sides of the road.

To solve the problem, we will construct the front of the reachability region - the boundary of the region at any point of which one can be in a given period of time.*)

We will construct this boundary for the moment in time when it passes through the point $B$ (the distance is the case when points $A$ and $B$ lie on opposite sides of the road). For this case, we draw through the point $B$ (see figure) a straight line forming an angle $\varphi$ with the road such that $\sin \varphi = v/u$, where $v$ is the speed of movement along the field, $u$ is the speed of movement along the road. From the data of the problem it follows that $\sin \varphi = 1/2$, $\varphi = \pi/6$. Let this straight line intersect the road at point $D$. From point $A$ we draw a circle tangent to line $BD$. From the data of the problem it follows that point $B$ lies between the point of tangency $C$ and point $D$.

So, at the moment the front passes the point $B$ of interest to us, the front section is represented by a circle with radius $AC$ and a tangent segment $CD$. **) Theler can easily understand that the optimal trajectory from $A$ to $B$ will be the broken line $AFGB$, and $CFD = BGD = \pi/2 - \varphi = \pi/3$.

Similarly, if points $A$ and $B(B')$ lie on the same side of the road, then the optimal trajectory will be the broken line $AFKB'$, with $B'KD = \pi/2 - \varphi = \pi/3$. Note that with a different mutual arrangement of points $A$, $B$ and the road (at other distances), the optimal trajectory can be a straight segment without road sections (in this case, point $B$ lies on a section of the front, which is part of a circle with radius $AC$).

Teler will find the minimum time of movement from point $A$ to point $B$. If the points lie on opposite sides of the road -

$$t_{\min} = \frac{AF}{v} + \frac{FG}{u} + \frac{GB}{v} \approx 23,5 \text{ мин.}$$

If points $A$ and $B(B')$ lie on the same side of the road -

$$t_{\min} = \frac{AF}{v} + \frac{FK}{u} + \frac{KB'}{v} \approx 24,6 \text{ мин.}$$

S. S. Krotov

*) I talk in detail about the principle of constructing the front of the reachability region in the article *On optimal trajectories of movement* in «Kvant» No. 6 for 1982.

**) The entire front is symmetrical relative to the perpendicular to the road passing through the point $A$; the left version of its part is a straight section from point $D$ to the intersection with a circle of radius $AC$, making an angle $\varphi = \pi/6$ with the road, and part of a circle with radius $AC$.
