## Condition

A ball made of a solid dielectric was placed in a uniform electric field with intense E. In this case, the dielectric turns out to be completely polarized. Using the superposition principle, find the electric field strength at the center of the ball and at points at a distance r from the center of the ball (r less than the radius of the ball).

Dielectric molecules can be represented as dumbbells of length l with charges +q and -q at the ends. The number of molecules per unit volume is n.

## Solution

The ball, depicted as a solid dielectric, was placed in a single-voltage electric field with a voltage of $E$. In this case, the dielectric turns out to be polarized. Using the principle of superposition, find the electric field voltage at the center of the ball and at points at a distance $r$ from the center of the ball ($r$ less than the radius of the ball).

Dielectric molecules can be represented as occupancies of length $l$ with charges $+q$ and $-q$ at the ends. The number of molecules per unit volume is $n$.

In an external electric field, all dumbbells are oriented along the field (Fig. 18, a), and charges appear on the ball, distributed in a certain way over its surface. Inside the ball, where the densities of positive and negative charges are equal, the charges cancel each other.

If you look at the given figure, you can imagine that a ball of a fully polarized dielectric is equivalent to two balls laid out inside each other. One ball is charged only negatively, the other - only positively (Fig. 18, b, and f). The centers of these balls are displaced relative to each other by a distance of $l$ (the length of the dumbbell), and relative to the center of the real ball they are displaced by a distance of $\frac{l}{2}$ (Fig. 19).

Thus, the field at any point consists of the external field and the fields created by positively and negatively charged balls. Therefore, first we will solve an auxiliary problem: we will find the direction of the field inside a uniformly charged ball at a distance $r$ from its center.

As is known *), the direction of the field inside a uniformly charged sphere is zero, and outside the sphere the field is the same as if it were created by an exact charge located in the center of the sphere. The volume of a uniformly charged ball is divided into thin spherical elephants, the thickness of which is much less than the ball. According to the superrosicini principle, the field direction at a distance $r$ from the center of the ball is equal to the sum of the field directions of the charges of such spherical elephants. But the direction of the field created by the charges of those elephants whose radii are greater than $r$ is equal to zero, and the direction of the layer whose radii are less than $r$ is the same as if the charges of these layers were in the center of the ball. Consequently, the direction of the field at a distance $r$ from the center of the ball is $E_r = \frac{Q_r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$, where $Q_r = qn \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3$ and $E_r = \frac{qn r}{3\varepsilon_0}$.

Using the results obtained, it is not difficult to find the direction of the field at any point on a dielectric ball.

Consider the center of the ball. the directionality at this point is equal to the sum of three vectors (Fig. 20): vector $E$ of the directionality of the external field, vector $E_1$ of the directionality of the field created at this point by a positively charged ball, and vector $E_2$ of the directionality of the field created by a negatively charged ball. Now let's find the direction of the field at some point $A$ inside the ball. Let us denote the distance from this point to the center of the positive charged ball $r_1$, and to the center of the negative charged ball $r_2$. The field direction vector at point $A$ consists of three vectors (Fig. 21):

$$E, E'_1 \text{ и } E'_2,$$ where $E'_1 = \frac{qnr_1}{3\varepsilon_0}$ is the field of a positive charged ball, $E'_2 = \frac{qnr_2}{3\varepsilon_0}$ is the field of a negative charged ball.

From the similarity of the triangles it follows that the vector $E'$ is parallel to the line $O_1O_2$.

The magnitude of the vector $E'$ can be found from the correspondence

$$\frac{E'}{E'_1} = \frac{O_1O_2}{O_1A},$$

where

$$E' = E'_1 \frac{O_1O_2}{O_1A} = \frac{qnl}{3\varepsilon_0}.$$

Then the final direction of the field at point $A$ is equal to

$$E_A = E - E' = E - \frac{qnl}{3\varepsilon_0}.$$

As you can see, the direction of the field at point $A$ does not depend on either $r_1$ or $r_2$, that is, it does not depend on the position of point $A$ inside the ball. This means that the field inside a completely polarized ball is uniform.
