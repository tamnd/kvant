## Condition

To clearly demonstrate the effect of laser radiation on a substance, a target was made in the form of a small silver ball fixed at the top of a light pistol cone (Fig. 2). One side of the ball is polished and the other is blackened. The target is placed with the base of the cone on a rough horizontal plane, and radiation from a powerful pulsed laser is focused on the surface of the target ball.

Estimate the minimum energy of the light pulse at which the target will topple over. Which side of the ball (reflecting or absorbing) should the radiation be directed to?

Assume that the mass of the ball is $m=3$ g and much greater than the mass of the final stand; cone height $h=6$ cm, base diameter $d=3$ mm; reflection coefficient of the polished surface $r=0,99$, absorption coefficient of the blackened surface $a=0,99$; laser pulse duration $\tau=10$ ns.

E. N. Yunosov, I. V. Yaminsky

## Solution

To clearly demonstrate the appearance of a manufacturing substance, a target is in the form of a small silver ball, closed in the upper thin hollow of a cone (see figure). One side of the ball is polished and the other is blackened. The target is installed from the base of the cone on a rough horizontal plane and the study from a powerful pulsed laser is focused on the surface of the target ball.

Estimate the minimum energy of the light pulse at which the target will topple over. Which side of the ball (reflective or positive) should the study be directed to?

Assume that the mass of the ball is $m=3$ g and the masses of the final support are much larger; cone height $h=6$ cm, base diameter $d=3$ mm; reflectance of the polished surface $r=0,99$; coefficient of position of the blackened surface $a=0,99$; laser pulse side diameter $\tau=10$ ns.

This problem originated from a real existing demonstration experiment. A light beam from a neodymium pulsed laser with a pulse energy of 2 J and a pulse duration of 10 ns is directed to the upper edge of a vertically standing target - a wooden wedge; Moreover, if the light beam is focused using a lens and falls on the blackened surface of the wedge, then the target is overturned. If the beam is not focused or hits a blackened surface, the target does not overturn. To simplify calculations in the conditions of the problem, the wooden wedge-shaped target was replaced with a silver ball.

In order for the target to tip over, it is necessary that, under the influence of a light beam, the ball acquires kinetic energy sufficient to raise the center of gravity of the ball to a height

$$H = \sqrt{h^2 + \frac{d^2}{4}} - h \approx \frac{d^2}{8h},$$

i.e. the condition must be met

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = mgH.$$

This means that the impulse imparted to the ball must be equal to

$$P = mv \approx \frac{md}{2} \sqrt{\frac{g}{h}} \approx 6 \cdot 10^{-5} \text{ кг} \cdot \text{м}/\text{с}.$$

Let us assume that the target is tipped over due to light pressure. In fact, each absorbed photon transfers momentum $P_0 = E_0/c = hv/c$ to the ball, where $E_0 = hv$ is the energy of the photon, $c$ is the speed of light. Multiplying this expression by the number of photons in the study, we obtain the total momentum $P$ transferred to the ball by photons, from which we find the total energy $E$ of the light beam required to overturn the target:

$$E = Pc \approx 2 \cdot 10^4 \text{ Дж}.$$

To overturn the target, 4 orders of magnitude are not enough! This means that light pressure does not play a major role in this experiment.

The key to efficiency is that the energy of the laser beam, absorbing the blackened surface, produces rapid local heating and evaporation of the target substance in the beam focusing area. The jet of vapor creates the reactive force that overturns the target. Let's make the appropriate calculations.

Let all the light energy be absorbed and used to evaporate the mass $\Delta m$ of the substance. Then

⟦folio 39⟧

$$\Delta m \approx \frac{E}{L},$$

where $L$ is the specific heat of vaporization (here we do not take into account the energy required to heat the substance to the melting point, for melting and for further heating to the boiling point, since it is several times less than the energy required for evaporation). The mean square speed of thermal motion of vapor molecules is equal to

$$u = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}},$$

where $R$ is the universal gas constant, $T$ is the boiling point, $M$ is the molar mass of the substance. For the medium this speed is

$$u = 0,7 \cdot 10^3 \text{ m/c.}$$

The maximum impulse carried away by the parametrium and, according to the law of conservation of impulse, transferred to the ball, is equal to $\Delta mu$. To overturn the target, it is necessary that this impulse be equal to the value $P$ we found:

$$\Delta mu = P.$$

Then the required radiation energy

$$E = \Delta mL = \frac{PL}{u} \approx 0,2 \text{ Дж},$$

where $L = 2,2 \cdot 10^6 \text{ Дж/кг.}$. And this is an order of magnitude less than the radiation energy of our laser.

When obtaining this estimate, we did not take into account that part of the light energy is reflected to the target, part is contributed to its heating (and not just partial evaporation) and, finally, that the vapor jet will flow out in a diverging beam and, therefore, will impart a slightly smaller impulse to the ball. All this, of course, means that the estimate is underestimated, but, as it turns out, by no more than an order of magnitude.

And one last thing. When irradiating not the absorbing, but the reflecting surface of the ball, the energy required to overturn the target will be at least $1/(1-r)=100$ times greater.

Consequently, if the target, when its blackened surface is irradiated, overturns at the energy of the light pulse

$$E > 0,2 \text{ Дж},$$

then when a reflective surface is irradiated, energy will be required to flip it over

$$E > 20 \text{ Дж},$$

which is an order of magnitude greater than the energy of a neodymium pulsed laser.
