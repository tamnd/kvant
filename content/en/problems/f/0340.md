## Condition

An Earth satellite with mass $m = 10$ kz, moving in a circular orbit in high layers of the atmosphere, experiences resistance from rarefied air. Resistance force $F = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}$ n. Determine how much the satellite's speed will change during one revolution around the Earth. The satellite's flight altitude above the Earth's surface is considered small compared to the radius of the Earth.

## Solution

An Earth satellite with a mass of $m=10$ kg, moving in a circular orbit in the high layers of the atmosphere, experiences resistance from rarefied air. Resistance force $F=5 \cdot 10^{-4}$ n. Determine how much the satellite's speed will change during one revolution around the Earth. The height of the satellite's flight above the Earth's surface is considered small compared to the radius of the Earth.

The total energy $E$ of a satellite with mass $m$, moving in an orbit of radius $R$, adds up its kinetic energy $K = \frac{m v^2}{2} = \gamma \frac{m M}{2 R}$ (the speed of the satellite in a circular Earth orbit is $\sqrt{\gamma \frac{M}{R}}$, where $R$ is the radius of the Earth) and potential energy $\Pi = -\gamma \frac{m M}{R}$ (the “minus” sign is due to the nature of the attractive forces between the satellite and the Earth: as the bodies get closer, the potential energy decreases). Thus,

$$E = K + \Pi = -\gamma \frac{m M}{2 R}.$$

As a result of the action of the drag force, the energy of the satellite changes, therefore, the radius of the orbit changes. Let's calculate the change in radius per revolution.

The energy of the satellite per revolution changes by values ​​$\Delta E$, numerically equal to the work of the resistance force on the path $2\pi R$, that is, $\Delta E = -2\pi RF$. Thus,

$$\Delta E = -\gamma \frac{m M}{2 (R + \Delta R)} + \gamma \frac{m M}{2 R} = \gamma \frac{m M}{2} \frac{\Delta R}{(R + \Delta R) R}.$$

Assuming $|\Delta R| \ll R$, we can write

$$\gamma \frac{m M}{2} \frac{\Delta R}{R^2} = -2\pi RF.$$

Considering that $\gamma \frac{M}{R^2} = g$, we finally get

$$\Delta R = -\frac{4\pi RF}{g m} \approx -4 \cdot 10^2 m$$

($g = 9,8 \text{ m/сек}^2$, $R \approx 6,4 \cdot 10^6 \text{ m}$).

It is clear that the assumption $|\Delta R| \ll R$ we made is correct.

So, as a result of the action of the drag force, the radius of the satellite’s orbit decreases. Consequently, the speed of the satellite increases. Let's find the change in speed,

The kinetic energy of a satellite in an orbit of radius $R + \Delta R$ is equal to

$$K_{R+\Delta R} = \frac{m (v + \Delta v)^2}{2} = \gamma \frac{m M}{2 (R + \Delta R)}.$$

This paradox is easy to resolve if we take into account that when the circuit is closed, the free charges of the conducting liquid have a velocity component directed perpendicular to the plate. Consequently, a component of the Lorentz force appears, directed against the velocity v. So the total work of the Lorentz force consists of the work of its two components. And this total work is zero. Let's make sure of this.

The movement of charge inside the capacitor from one plate to another occurs due to the action of the Lorentz force component perpendicular to the plates. The meaning of this component

$$F_1 = qvB.$$
The work done by force $F_1$ per unit time (i.e. power) is equal to

$$N_1 = F_1v_1 = qvBv_1,$$

where $v_1$ is the charge velocity component directed perpendicular to the plate.

The Lorentz force component directed against the velocity v is equal in magnitude

$$F_2 = qv_1B.$$
The work done by this force per unit time is

$$N_2 = -F_2v_1 = -qv_1Bv$$

(force $F_2$ acts as a braking force, and therefore its work is negative).

Thus, the work of the Lorentz force (if we take into account all the components of the force) actually turns out to be zero:

$$N_1 + N_2 = 0.$$
The energy source in this circuit is obviously a pump that draws liquid through a capacitor. This pump must constantly do work, since a resistance force acts on the liquid, and all this work ultimately turns into heat released in the circuit.
