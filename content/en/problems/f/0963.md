The “components” of the movement will be uniformly accelerated with $v_{0x}=v_{0y}=0$ and accelerations $g_x=g \sin \alpha$ and $g_y=g \cos \alpha$. Elastic impacts of the ball on the planes P and Q make these movements periodic. Let us denote the distances from point A to planes Q and P by l and h; travel times for these distances (“there” or “back”) -

$$\tau_x = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{g \sin \alpha}}, \quad \tau_y = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g \cos \alpha}}.$$

The return of the ball to point A will be realized if it simultaneously appears at point A both in movement along the X axis and in movement along the Y axis, that is, if the equality

$$\tau_x = k \tau_y,$$

where k is a rational number. In this case, there will always be certain numbers m and n equal to the “number of moves” of the ball along the X and Y axes from the beginning of the movement to its appearance at point A:

$$m \tau_x = n \tau_y.$$

Let's find a path along which the ball can again get to point A. Using the correspondence of segments of paths traversed in uniformly accelerated motion at equal successive time intervals (at $v_0=0$), we can graphically accurately show the places where the ball hits the plane P and the points of contact of its trajectory (segments of parabolas) with the X axis.

At figure 1, the descent trajectory from point A to impact with plane Q is shown in dark color. Returning to point A is possible only if the ball, after being “reflected” from the plane Q, somewhere again “sits” on the same trajectory and moves “up” along it. No other trajectory will bring the ball to point A (at that point there must be $v_x=v_y=0$).

In particular, if the plane Q meets the ball on line $A_1B_1$ ($m=2, n=2$), or on line $A_2B_2$ ($m=2, n=4$), or on $A_3B_3$ ($m=2, n=6$), etc., then immediately after the elastic impact it finds itself on the blue trajectory and will definitely return to point A.

The plane $P$ will again be on the same trajectory and almost along it in the opposite direction (the red trajectory corresponds to the movement of a ball thrown from point $B$ with a speed $v_0$ such that $v_{0y} = \sqrt{2gh} \cos \alpha$, $v_{0x} = 0$).

At the points of intersection of the red and blue trajectories $|\vec{v}_{x1}| = |\vec{v}_{x2}|$ and $|\vec{v}_{y1}| = |\vec{v}_{y2}|$, according to the law of conservation of energy, it becomes clear that if at one of these points (at point $D$ or at point $E$) there is a plane $Q$, then the ball from the blue trajectory at this point will “transfer” to the red trajectory and pass along it to the point $B$ and back to the plane $Q$ and, having “reseeded” a second time onto the blue trajectory, will return to point $A$. Moreover, for the point $D$ $m_D = 4$, $n_D = 6$; for point $E$ $m_E = 4$, $n_E = 10$.

But in the general case, transfers from the blue trajectory to the red one and back must be carried out through “intermediate parabolas” (they are shown in green on figure 2), and some of them, having a tangency with the $X$ axis, will necessarily have a tangency with the $Y$ axis. (At figure 2 the entire trajectory of the ball at $m = 8$ and $n = 10$ is shown.)

If $k$ is an irrational number, on the $Q$ plane the green parabola will never intersect the red one, and the ball will never end up at the point $A$.
