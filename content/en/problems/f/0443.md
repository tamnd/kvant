## Condition

As you know, the globe makes a complete revolution around its axis in 23 hours 56 minutes 04 seconds. Consequently, in a day all hours whose digits are different

divided by 24 hours, should lag by almost 4 minutes. This amounts to almost half an hour a week. Why don’t we notice this lag and don’t constantly let down all the clocks?

B. Bukhovtsev

## Solution

As you know, the globe makes a complete revolution around its axis in 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. Therefore, during the day, all watches whose dial is divided into 24 hours must stand for almost 4 minutes. This amounts to almost a complete rotation around its axis in 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. Therefore, it is still observed on each earth that the stars in the sky in time $T$ make a complete revolution around their axis in 23 hours 56 minutes 4 s, the Sun “circulates” the Earth in 24 hours (and it is precisely 24 hours, in connection with this, that the dial of all earthly clocks is divided).

This happens because during a full revolution around its axis, the Earth in its orbital motion around the Sun manages to advance approximately 1/365 of an hour of the orbital length (Fig. 5). For example, although the globe makes a complete revolution around its axis, therefore the earthly observer saw the Sun, roughly speaking, in the same place (and noted, for example, the onset of noon on his watch), the globe must additionally make 1/365 of an hour of rotation, which takes almost 4 minutes.
