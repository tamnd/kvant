## Condition

There are two translucent mirrors, each of which, as measured, transmits approximately 1/5 of the light flux, and reflects the rest of the light. If both mirrors are installed on the path of a parallel beam of light so that their planes are perpendicular to the beam, then it would seem that they should transmit 1/25 of the incident light flux, whereas in fact the light is attenuated not by 25 times, but noticeably less (about 10 times). Explain the phenomenon.

V. V. Golubev

Problem solutions

M1121—M1124, F1133—F1136

I. One of the most straightforward solutions is based on dropping from the point $B$ the perendiculars $BH$, $BA_1$, $BC_1$ to the straight lines $AC$, $AK$, $CK$ (they are equal according to symmetry messages) and considering the receiving angles with the vertex $B$. Triangles $KA_1B$ and $KC_1B$ are equal, and $\angle A_1BK = \angle KBC_1$ (their sum is $2\angle B$, since $\angle A_1BA = \angle ABH$, $\angle HBC = \angle CBC_1$). On the other hand, the radius $OB$ of the circumscribed circle in any triangle $ABC$ also makes the same angles on the side $AB$ and $BC$ as the height $BH$ on the side $BC$ and $AC$, respectively:

$$\angle ABO = |90^\circ - \angle AOB/2| = |90^\circ - \angle C| = \angle HBC,$$
$$\angle CBO = |90^\circ - \angle COB/2| = |90^\circ - \angle A| = \angle HBA.$$

Therefore, $\angle A_1BO = \angle OBC_1 = \angle B$, i.e. $OB$ and $KB$ lie on the same straight line - the bisserp of the angle $A_1BC_1$.

The difficulty in formulating a solution to this problem is that it is necessary to take into account the various possibilities of the case: 1) $\triangle ABC$ is acute; 2) one of the angles $A$ or $C$ is not less than $90^\circ$; 3) $\angle B > 90^\circ$ (see figure 1-3; because the case $\angle B = 90^\circ$ is impossible: in this case the constructed lines are parallel). However, the reasoning is similar in all cases.

Let us outline two more solutions.

II. The angle between lines $AK$ and $KC$ complements $2\angle B$ to $180^\circ$, more precisely

$$\angle A_1KC_1 = 180^\circ - \angle A_1BC_1 = |180^\circ - 2\angle B| = 180^\circ - \angle AOC,$$

By definition, the center of $O$ lies on the circle passing through $K$, $A$ and $C$, and (since $AO=OC$) bisects the arcs $AC$. Therefore, $KO$, like $KB$, is a bicsectrix of the angle $A_1KC_1$.

This solution also requires an external analysis of cases 1-3. As usual, solutions with geometric transformations are free from this difficulty.

III. The composition of the symmetrical relative lines $AB$ and $BC$, i.e., rotation by an angle $2\angle B$ with center $B$, transforms line $AK$ into line $KC$. A rotation through the same angle $2\angle B$ with center $O$ translates the radius $OA$ into the radius $OC$, so it must transform the straight line $AK$ into a straight line passing through $C$ and having the same direction as $KC$, i.e. into the straight line $KC$. It follows that the point $O$, as well as $B$, is equally distant from the lines $AK$ and $AC$, and both of them lie on the bicsectrix of the angle between $AK$ and $KC$.

N. V. Vasiliev, V. N. Dubrovsky

## Solution

There are two translucent mirrors, each of which, as measured, transmits approximately 1/5 of the light flux, and reflects the rest of the light. If both mirrors stand in the path of a parallel beam of light so that their planes are perpendicular to the beam, then it would seem that they should transmit 1/25 of the incident light, whereas in fact the light is attenuated not by 25 times, but noticeably less (by about 10 times). The explanation is that the rays of light reflected by the second mirror return to the first mirror, are partially reflected from it and, falling on the second, increase the intensity of the transmitted beam.

Let $I$ be the intensity of the initial light beam (see figure; the incident light beam is shown inclined for clarity). Then the intensity of light passing through the first mirror will be equal to $i_0 = I/k$, where $k$ is the attenuation coefficient of the mirror ($k = 5$ according to the problem conditions). With successive reflections, the intensity of the light “captured” between the mirrors gradually decreases due to the partial energy of the mirrors, and after a sufficiently large number of reflections, almost all of the “captured” light will be released, almost equally to the right and left. Thus, the total intensity of transmitted light will be equal to

$$I' \approx \frac{i_0}{2} = \frac{I}{2k} = \frac{I}{10}.$$

The problem also allows for an exact solution. Let us write down how the intensity of the light “captured” between the mirrors changes during successive reflections:

$$i_1 = i_0 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2, \quad i_2 = i_1 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2 = i_0 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^4,$$

With each fall on mirror 2, the light partially comes out to the right:

$$I_1 = \frac{i_0}{k}, \quad I_2 = \frac{i_1}{k}, \quad I_3 = \frac{i_2}{k},$$

Total transmitted light intensity

$$I' = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + \dots = \frac{1}{k} \left(i_0 + i_1 + i_2 + \dots\right) = \frac{i_0}{k} \left(1 + \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2 + \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^4 + \dots\right)$$

represents the sum of an infinite decreasing geometric progression with value $q = (1 - 1/k)^2$, so

$$I' = \frac{i_0}{k} \frac{1}{1 - q} = \frac{i_0}{k} \frac{k^2}{k^2 - (k - 1)^2} = \frac{i_0 k}{2k - 1} = \frac{I}{2k - 1} = \frac{I}{9}.$$

At $k = 5$ the exact solution differs by only 10% from the approximate one.

V. V. Golubev
