## Condition

A long cylindrical vessel is filled with an ideal gas to a pressure of $p_0$. First, the temperature of the cylinder is kept constant and equal to $T_0$. Then the temperature of one of the end walls of the vessel is increased $\Delta T$, and the temperature of the opposite wall remains the same. Begin the steady pressure in the vessel and the position of the center of mass of the gas. Consider that $\Delta T \ll T_0$.

◆ After equilibrium is established in different places of the vessel, the gas pressure will be the same, but the temperature will be different: more penetration of the warmer wall and less penetration of the opposite one. If $\Delta T \ll T_0$, then we can assume that the temperature changes according to a linear law

$$T(x) = T_0 \left( 1 + \frac{\Delta T}{T_0} \frac{x}{l} \right)$$

(see figure). Let us mentally divide the vessel into thin layers of thickness $\Delta x$ such that within each layer the gas temperature can be considered constant. For gas in each such layer the following conditions are satisfied:

$$p = n(x) \cdot k \cdot T(x),$$

where $p$ is the pressure in the vessel, $k$ is Boltzmann’s constant, $n(x)$ is the volume concentration of gas in a given layer. It can be seen that the concentration also depends on $x$

$$n(x) = \frac{p}{kT_0 \left( 1 + \frac{\Delta T}{T_0} \frac{x}{l} \right)} \approx \frac{p}{kT_0} \left( 1 - \frac{\Delta T}{T_0} \frac{x}{l} \right)$$

(we used the approximate equality $\frac{1}{1+\alpha} \approx 1-\alpha$ at $\alpha \ll 1$) and according to a linear law it changes from $n_0 = \frac{p}{kT_0}$ at the “cold” wall to $n_l = \frac{p}{kT_0} \left( 1 - \frac{\Delta T}{T_0} \right)$ at the “warm” wall. Average concentration

$$n_{cp} = \frac{n_0 + n_l}{2} = \frac{p}{kT_0} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0} \right).$$

Let us express through $n_{cp}$ the total number of molecules in the vessel:

$$N = Sln_{cp} = \frac{pSl}{kT_0} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0} \right)$$

($S$ is the area of ​​the cylinder end). On the other side,

$$N = Sln_0 = \frac{p_0Sl}{kT_0},$$

where $n_0$ is the gas concentration in the vessel at $p_0$, $T_0$. From (1) and (2) the pressure in the vessel at steady equilibrium is found:

$$p = \frac{p_0}{\left(1 - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0}\right)} \approx p_0 \left(1 + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0}\right).$$

Current position of the gas center of mass:

$$x_{\pi, \text{M}} = \frac{S}{N} \int_0^L dx \cdot x \cdot n(x) =$$

$$= L \left(1 + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0}\right) \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0}\right) \approx L \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{12} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0}\right).$$

Thus, the center of mass of the mixer is towards the colder wall at

$$\Delta l = \frac{1}{12} \frac{\Delta T}{T_0} L.$$

The mixing of the center of mass of each is paradoxical at first glance, but is naturally explained by the interaction of gas molecules with the walls of the vessel. The fact is that in the process of establishing equilibrium, the change in momentum of the molecules that interact with the warmer wall will be greater than that of those that interact with the opposite wall. This will lead to the transfer of momentum from the walls of the vessel to the gas, and therefore to a mixing of the center of mass of the gas.
