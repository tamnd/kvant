## Condition

As is known, the maximum size of various image details on film is determined by the size of the crystals of the photosensitive layer. As the crystal size decreases, the image clarity increases. Estimate the maximum crystal size at which erithel ceases to replace the increase in image clarity on the cinema screen. It is assumed that you have a good understanding that you can set the quantities necessary for the solution yourself, select sufficiently correct numerical values ​​and obtain a numerical result.

In the case of the simplest projection film installation, the image on the screen is created as a result of the passage of a diverging light beam through the film film, providing the necessary magnification of the frame. It is important that there are no linear distortions on the screen. This means that the ratio of the size $\Delta h$ of a detail on the film to the size $h$ of the entire frame must be exactly the same as the ratio of the size $\Delta H$ of the image of this detail on the screen to the size $H$ of the entire image of the frame on the screen, that is

$$\frac{\Delta h}{h} = \frac{\Delta H}{H}.$$

Let $\Delta H$ be the maximum size of a detail, still visible to the viewer’s eye from a distance $l$, $H$ be the size of the screen, coinciding with the size of the frame image on it, $\Delta h$ be the minimum size of a detail on film, coinciding with the size of the photosensitive crystal (“grain”) of the photographic emulsion, $h$ be the size of the frame on the film (see Fig. figure).

The ratio $\Delta H/l$ is determined by the so-called “angular resolution of the eye” of the viewer, that is, the minimum angle $\theta$, at the boundaries of which the eye separates (sees not disjointed) two points or two lines. The magnitude of this angle is easy to estimate. Let us recall, for example, visiting an ophthalmologist: from a distance of several meters, looking at a table of letters in the same way as we watch a movie, we distinguish the details of letters that do not change a few millimeters; that is, in order of magnitude, the angle $\theta$ is equal to

$$\theta \sim \frac{\Delta H}{l} \sim 10^{-3}.$$

Thus, $\Delta H \sim l\theta$, and

$$\Delta h = h \frac{\Delta H}{H} \sim \frac{hl\theta}{H}.$$

At $h=1$ cm, $l=20$ m, $H=5$ m, $\theta \sim 10^{-3}$ for the minimum size of photoemulsion crystals, beyond which the viewer stops replacing the increase in image clarity on the screen, we obtain

$$\Delta h \sim 0,04$$ mm.

This assessment is performed with known parameters of film and photographic materials.
