## Condition

Point sources located on the main optical axis of the lens form a uniformly luminous filament (passing through the focus of the lens). Find the law of change E(x) of illumination at points lying on the same axis on the other side of the lens at large distances x from it (x≫F, where F is the focal length of the lens).

M. A. Vorontsov, V. E. Kuntstsyn

## Solution

Precision sources located on the main optical axis of the lens form a uniformly luminous filament (passing through the focus of the lens). Find the law of change $E(x)$ of illumination at points lying on the same axis on the other side of the lens at large distances $x$ from it ($x \gg F$, where $F$ is the focal length of the lens).

Segments of luminous filament located at distances $y > F$ from the lens produce actual images on the optical axis to the right of the lens. To find the illumination $E(x)$ units of length on the optical axis, that is, the power of the image falling per unit length, consider how the transformation of a small segment of an object $dy$ into an image segment $dx$ occurs (see figure).

Let the power, expressed in units of the length of the luminous thread, be equal to $E_0$. Then the total power of the image falling on the element $dx$ of the image is equal to

$$E(x) \cdot dx = E_0 \frac{\Omega(y)}{4\pi} dy.$$ (*)

Here it is taken into account that the lens focuses only part of the image of element $dy$ - the image falling on element $dx$ of the image is given by the formula $$\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{y} = \frac{1}{F}.$$ Therefore, $dy$ in formula (*) can be expressed through $dx$ as follows:

$$dy = \left| \frac{dy}{dx} \right| dx = \frac{F^2}{(F-x)^2} dx.$$

(The derivative is taken modulo, since the lengths of the segments and powers are positive.) Substituting the value of $dy$ into (*) and taking into account that for $x \gg F$ $y \approx F$ and the solid angle $\Omega(y \approx F)$ does not depend on $x$, we obtain

$$E(x) \approx E_0 \frac{\Omega(F)}{4\pi} \frac{F^2}{(F-x)^2} \approx E_0 \frac{\Omega(F) \cdot F^2}{4\pi x^2} \sim \frac{1}{x^2}.$$

M. A. Vorontsov, B. E. Kuntsyn
