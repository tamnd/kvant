Let's draw graphs of the dependence of train coordinates on time. We will select the point at which the passenger train began braking as the reference point, and take the direction of the train speeds as the direction of the coordinate axis. In such a system, the coordinates of trains at time $t$ are equal

$$x_{\text{TОВ}} = v_2 t + s_0,$$

where $v_1 = 108$ km/hour = 30 m/sec, $v_2 = 32,4$ km/hour = 9 m/sec.

From the graphs (Fig. 6) it is clear that at time $t'$ and $t''$ the coordinates of the trains are equal. This means that at moments $t'$ and $t''$ a new train will occur. Let's find the values ​​$t'$ and $t''$ from the acceleration $x_{\text{пас}} = x_{\text{TОВ}}$:

$$30t - 0,6t^2 = 9t + 180,$$

or $t^2 - 35t + 300 = 0$, from where $t' = 15$ sec, $t'' = 20$ sec.

 the coordinates of the trains at moment $t = t'$ are equal to $x_{\text{пас}} = x_{\text{TОВ}} = 315$ m.

According to the voltmeter and ammeter $A_1$ shown, the resistance of the entire circuit shown in figure 7 is $R = \frac{U}{I} = 2$ ohm.

Let us find what the resistances $R_1, R_2, R_3$ and $R_4$ are. Since the ammeter $A_2$ is ideal, its resistance can be considered equal to zero, and points $a$ and $b$ can be considered short-circuited to each other when calculating the resistance. Let's put $R_1 = 1$ ohm. Then by simple search we are convinced that $R_2 = 4$ ohm, and for resistance $R_3$ and $R_4$ there are two options: either $R_3 = 2$ ohm and $R_4 = 3$ ohm, or $R_3 = 3$ ohm and $R_4 = 2$ ohm.

Consider a chain branch point, for example, point $a$. The algebraic sum of the currents converging at this point is equal to zero:

$$I_1 - I_3 - I_5 = 0.$$ (*)

Therefore, to determine the current $I_5$ flowing through the ammeter $A_2$, it is enough to know $I_1$ and $I_3$. Let's find them.

Currents $I_1$ and $I_2$ flowing through resistances $R_1$ and $R_2$ are inversely proportional to the values ​​of these resistances:

$$\frac{I_1}{I_2} = \frac{R_2}{R_1} = \frac{4}{1}.$$

At the same time

$$I_1 + I_2 = I = 5a.$$

From here

$$I_1 = 4a.$$

Similarly, we can write the corresponding expressions for the currents $I_3$ and $I_4$:

$$\frac{I_3}{I_4} = \frac{R_4}{R_3}$$
$$I_3 + I_4 = I.$$

If $R_3 = 2$ ohm and $R_4 = 3$ ohm, then

$$\frac{I_3}{I_4} = \frac{3}{2}$$
$$I_3 + I_4 = I = 5a,$$

where

$$I_3 = 3a.$$

Then from equality (*)

$$I_5 = I_1 - I_3 = 1a.$$

If $R_3 = 3$ ohm and $R_4 = 2$ ohm, then

$$I_3 = 2a,$$
$$I_5 = 2a.$$

Thus, there are two possible values ​​for the current flowing through the amplifier $A_2$.

F410 *). In an electron vacuum device, pure tungsten camode is placed in a large flask containing residual oxygen at a pressure of $p = 10^{-7}$ atom and a temperature of $T = 300^\circ\text{K}$. Assuming that every molecule that gets on the chamber touches it, estimate the time of formation of the monolecular layer. The molecules can be considered balls with a diameter of $d \approx 3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ cm.

Let us find the average number of molecules $z$ that settle on a unit area of ​​the cathode surface in a time of $t = 1$ sec. To evaluate the use of the proposed gas model, according to which all molecules in the state move in those mutually perpendicular directions (in both directions in each direction) with a root mean square speed $\bar{v}$. Let the average number of molecules per unit volume (concentration of molecules) be $n$. Of the total number of molecules, $1/6$ part moves towards the cathode. A unit area of ​​the cathode in time $t$ makes all the molecules appear in a column of unit section and height $\bar{v}$. From here

$$z = \frac{n}{6} \bar{v}.$$

A more accurate expression, taking into account the movement of molecules in all possible directions and with different absolute velocities, has the form $z = \frac{n}{4} \bar{v}$. This difference in the numerical coefficient is insignificant, since according to the conditions of the problem we must give only an approximate estimate of the result in order of magnitude.

Mean square speed of molecules

$$\bar{v} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}}$$

where $k = 1,38 \cdot 10^{-23}$ J/deg is Boltzmann’s constant, $T = 300^\circ\text{K}$ is the absolute temperature of the gas, $m = \frac{\mu}{N_A} = 5,3 \cdot 10^{-26}$ ke is the mass of oxygen molecules. The concentration of molecules can be expressed in terms of gas pressure and temperature:

$$n = \frac{p}{kT}.$$

Substituting the numerical values ​​into the expression for $\bar{v}$ and $n$ gives $\bar{v} \approx 5 \cdot 10^2$ m/sec, $n \approx 2,5 \cdot 10^{18}$ m$^{-3}$.

Thus,

$$z = \frac{n}{6} v \approx 2 \cdot 10^{20} \text{ m}^{-2} \cdot \text{cek}^{-1}.$$

The number of molecules $z_0$ in a monomolecular layer covering an area of ​​$1\text{ m}^2$ can be estimated by the formula

$$z_0 \approx \frac{1}{d^2} \approx 10^{19} \text{ m}^{-2}.$$

It takes time for such a number of molecules to precipitate

$$\tau = \frac{z_0}{2} \approx 5 \cdot 10^{-2} \text{ сек}.$$

The above estimates are quite rough. We did not take into account, for example, the possibility of denser packing of molecules or uneven formation of layers. A simplified gas model was used to calculate $z$. Therefore, the obtained result is correct only in order of magnitude. Estimates of this kind play an important role in the implementation of physical problems, since they allow one to orient themselves to the scale of the phenomenon being studied.
