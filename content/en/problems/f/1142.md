## Condition

The formation of the Jupterian cometary family is described by the following scheme. The comet falls from a great distance without initial speed onto the Sun and flies not far from Jupter (Fig. 3). After the noticeable influence of Jupter’s gravitational field ceases, the comet again moves in the field of the Sun, and its speed turns out to be directed opposite to the speed of Jupter, and the apheal new orbit of the comet is located near Jupter, i.e. at a distance of $R=5,2$ a. e. from the Sun. At what distance from the Sun will the perihelion orbit of such a comet be located?

V. E. Belonuchkin

## Solution

The formation of the cometary family of Jupiter is described by the following scheme. The comet falls from a great distance without initial speed onto the Sun and flies a short distance from Jupiter (see figure). After the noticeable influence of the gravitational field of Jupiter ceases, the comet again moves in the field of the Sun, and its speed turns out to be directed counter-directionally with the speed of Jupiter, and the new orbit of the comet is located greater than the orbit of Jupiter, i.e. at a distance of $R=5,2$ a. e. from the Sun. At what distance from the Sun will the entire orbit of such a comet be located?

Note that the Sun is more than $10^3$ times as massive as Jupiter, so the size of the region where Jupiter's gravity is comparable to the influence of the Sun is $10^6$ times less than the radius of the orbital plan. At the same time, the time of significant interaction of the comet with Jupiter is incomparably short compared to the periods of revolution of Jupiter and the comet around the Sun, therefore, the meaning of the comet during this time is negligible. Therefore, we can divide the movement of the comet into three independent stages: 1) movement of the comet from a distant position towards the Solar system under the influence of the Sun’s gravity, 2) an “instant” turn in the field of Jupiter, 3) movement in an elliptical orbit around the Sun (and at this stage the influence of Jupiter is not taken into account).

From the condition of Jupiter’s motion in a circular orbit under the influence of the Sun’s gravity with mass $M$

$$\frac{v^2}{R} = \frac{GM}{R^2}$$

we get the speed of Jupiter $v$:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}.$$

The speed of the comet $v_k$ when approaching Jupiter at the end of the first stage is determined from the conservation law

energy (at an infinite distance the energy, as usual, is assumed to be zero):

$$\frac{v^2_k}{2} - \frac{GM}{R} = 0, \quad v_k = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = v\sqrt{2}.$$

The speed directions of the comet and Jupitera were initially redistributed to each other, which means that relative to Jupiter the speed of the comet is equal to

$$v'_k = v\sqrt{3}.$$

After leaving the gravitational field of Jupiter (at the beginning of the third stage), the speed of the comet relative to the planet changes only in direction, but in relation to the Sun it becomes equal

$$v_1 = v(\sqrt{3} - 1).$$

Now the comet again interacts only with the Sun. At aphelion and perihelion, the speed of redistribution to the radius vector drawn from the Sun, which means that Kepler’s second law will be written as follows:

$$v_1R = v_2x.$$

From the law of conservation of energy -

$$\frac{v^2_1}{2} - \frac{GM}{R} = \frac{v^2_2}{2} - \frac{GM}{x}.$$

Solving the jointly obtained equations, we will find two answers:

$$x_1 = R = 5,2 \text{ а. e.}$$

$$x_2 = R(2 - \sqrt{3}) / (\sqrt{3} - 1) = 0,37 \quad R = 1,9 \text{ а. e.}$$
