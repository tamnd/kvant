## Condition

Exploring the newly discovered planet, which has the shape of a ball with a radius of $R=6400$ km and is covered over its entire surface by an ocean with a depth of $H=10$ km of physical water, scientists have established that the acceleration of gravity remains unchanged with a high degree of accuracy when immersed in the ocean to different depths. Using these data, determine the acceleration of free fall on the planet. Gravitational constant $G=6,67 \cdot 10^{-11} \text{H} \text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$.

## Solution

Exploring the newly discovered planet, which has the shape of a ball with a radius of $R=6400$ km and is covered over its entire surface by an ocean with a depth of $H=10$ km of ordinary water, scientists have established that the acceleration of gravity remains unchanged with a high degree of accuracy when immersed in the ocean to different depths. Using these data, determine the acceleration of free fall on the planet. Gravitational constant $G=6,67 \times 10^{-11} \text{H} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{кг}^2$.

Inside a homogeneous sphere, the gravitational force is zero, but the sphere attracts external bodies as if all the mass is concentrated in the center. Let us calculate the acceleration of gravity at a depth $x$ in the ocean covering the planet.

Consider a spherical layer of thickness $x$ consisting of water. It can be considered composed of thin homogeneous spherical shells, each of which does not create gravitational force within itself. Therefore, free gravitational attraction is subject to attraction on the surface of a ball with radius $R-x$. The mass of this ball

$$m = M - \frac{4\pi\rho}{3} (R^3 - (R-x)^3),$$

where $M$ is the mass of the entire planet, $\rho=10^3 \text{kg/m}^3$ is the density of water. Then the acceleration of free fall

$$g = \frac{Gm}{(R-x)^2} = G \frac{M-4\pi\rho(R^3-(R-x)^3)/3}{(R-x)^2}.$$

Note that this expression is valid only for small ones, compared to $R$, $x$ - after all, not the entire planet consists of water. Therefore, on the graphs depicting the dependence of $g$ on $x$ (see figure), only the initial sections in the surrounded strip are described by the resulting formula. Let's analyze the resulting expression. When the planet reaches a greater mass $M$, the acceleration $g$ during immersion increases. When reaching low masses, the acceleration immediately begins to fall. There is a boundary mass, above which the acceleration increases, and below which it decreases. The value of this boundary mass corresponds to the case when, near the ocean surface, $g$ changes the least, i.e., remains almost constant.

Now let's do the calculations. Acceleration $g_0=GM/R^2$ on the ocean surface in the case of gravitational mass is “almost” equal to acceleration $g$ at shallow depths $x$, and their ratio is “almost” equal to unity:

$$\frac{g}{g_0} = \frac{1-4\pi\rho R^3(1-(1-x/R)^3)}{(1-x/R)^2} \approx 1.$$

The meaning of this “almost” is that we need to take into account the first power of the small quantity $x/R$ and neglect the second and third powers. Then the previous expression reduces to equality
