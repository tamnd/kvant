## Condition

A balloon, the lifting force of which is created by hot action, is designed in such a way that the volume of its heated chamber is practically constant, and the pressure in it is equal to the external pressure, since the chamber in its lower part communicates with the atmosphere. Heating in the chamber is carried out constantly to compensate for the heat supply to the environment. Such a ball, at constant heated power, floats in the atmosphere at a certain height. How much will the floating altitude change if, with increasing heater power, the average air temperature in the chamber increases by Δt=0.1 °C from the initial temperature t=57 °C? Ambient temperature t₀=17 °C.

E. N. Yunosov, I. V. Yaminsky

## Solution

A balloon, the lifting force of which is created by hot air, is designed in such a way that the volume of its observable chamber is practically built up, and the pressure in it is equal to the external pressure, since the chamber in its lower part communicates with the atmosphere. An arbitrary constant is observed in the chamber to compensate for the heat transfer into the environment. At constant cutting power, such a ball floats in the atmosphere at a certain height. How much does the floating altitude change if, with an increase in power, the cutting average air temperature in the chamber increases by $\Delta t = 0,1$ °C from the initial temperature $t = 57$ °C? Ambient temperature $t_0 = 17$ °C.

◆ The condition of a balloon floating at a certain height

$$mg + \rho V g = \rho_0 V g,$$

(1) where $m$ is the mass of the air shell of the balloon and the cargo compartment, $\rho$ is the density of hot air, $\rho_0$ is the density of atmospheric air at the floating altitude of the ball, $V$ is the volume of the chamber, $g$ is the acceleration of free pressure.

From (1) it follows that at the new height equilibrium will be possible if the values ​​$\rho_0$ change so that

$$\Delta \rho = \Delta \rho_0,$$

(2) where $\Delta \rho_0$ is the change in the density of atmospheric air, $\Delta \rho$ is the change in the density of hot air in the chamber.

For small changes in altitude, the pressure decreases with increasing altitude ($\Delta h$) by

$$\Delta p = \rho_0 g \cdot \Delta h.$$

Since the temperature of the atmosphere remains constant, for the density of atmospheric air at different altitudes we have:

$$\frac{\rho_0 - \Delta \rho_0}{\rho_0} = \frac{p_0 - \Delta p}{p_0},$$

where

$$\Delta \rho_0 = \rho_0 \frac{\Delta p}{p_0} = \rho_0^2 \frac{g}{p_0} \Delta h.$$

(3) The density of hot air in the chamber changes due to changes in temperature and pressure:

$$\Delta \rho = \frac{p_0 \mu}{RT} - \frac{(p_0 - \Delta p) \mu}{R (T + \Delta T)} =$$

$$\frac{\mu (p_0 \cdot \Delta T + \Delta p \cdot T)}{R (T + \Delta T)} = \frac{p_0 \mu \left( \frac{\Delta T}{T} + \frac{\Delta p}{p_0} \right)}{RT \left( 1 + \frac{\Delta T}{T} \right)},$$

and since $\frac{\Delta T}{T} \ll 1$,

$$\Delta \rho \approx \frac{p_0 \mu}{RT} \left( \frac{\Delta T}{T} + \frac{\Delta p}{p_0} \right).$$

(4)

Substituting (3) and (4) into (2), we find:

$$\Delta h = \frac{RT_0^2 \cdot \Delta T}{\mu g T (T - T_0)} \approx 18 \text{ m.}$$
