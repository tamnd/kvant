## Condition

Find the radius of the largest drop of water that can evaporate without absorbing the body from the outside.

Problem solutions

M326—M329; F339—F342

Let us introduce the following notation (Fig. 1): $O$ is the center of the circle, $|AB| = a$ is the length of the side of the large square, $|A'B'| = a'$ is the length of the side of the smaller square, $(OH) \perp (BB')$. Points $A$, $H$ and $A'$ lie on the same line. Let's drop the peredicle from point $O$ to $AA'$: $(OK) \perp (AA')$. Let

$$k = \frac{a}{|AH|} = \frac{a'}{|A'H|} = \frac{|KH|}{h}.$$

Then

$$a - a' = k(|AH| - |A'H|) = 2k|KH| = 2k^2h.$$

From $\triangle ABH$:

$$|AB|^2 + |BH|^2 = |AH|^2,$$

i.e.

$$|AB|^2 + \left(\frac{|AB|}{2}\right)^2 = |AH|^2,$$

or

$$k^2 + \left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^2 = 1,$$

where

$$k^2 = \frac{4}{5},$$

and therefore $a - a' = \frac{8}{5}h.$

## Solution

The largest radius of the largest drop of water, we can evaporate the comoram without emitting the menla from the outside.

When a drop evaporates without absorbing heat from the outside, it is necessary for the evaporation of quantitative heat $Q$ to be obtained due to a decrease in the surface energy of the drop $U_\pi$ with a decrease in its surface area.

Let the radius of the drop decrease by $\Delta R$, then the volume of the drop decreases by

$$\Delta V = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 - \frac{4}{3} \pi (R - \Delta R)^3 = 4\pi R^2 \Delta R - 4\pi R (\Delta R)^2 + \frac{4}{3} \pi (\Delta R)^3.$$

For small $\Delta R$ we can neglect the second and third terms of this equality compared to the first term, so that

$$\Delta V = 4\pi R^2 \Delta R.$$

The mass of water evaporated in this case is equal to

$$m = \rho \Delta V = 4\pi\rho R^2 \Delta R.$$

To evaporate it, a quantity of heat is required

$$Q = Lm = 4\pi L\rho R^2 \Delta R$$

where $L = 2,26 \cdot 10^6 \text{ дж}/\text{кг}$ is the specific heat of water vaporization. The surface area of ​​the drop decreased by

$$\Delta S = 4\pi R^2 - 4\pi (R - \Delta R)^2 = 8\pi R \Delta R$$

(we will neglect the term containing $(\Delta R)^2$). Therefore, the surface energy decreased by

$$\Delta U_\pi = 8\pi R \Delta R \sigma.$$

where $\sigma = 7,2 \cdot 10^{-2} \text{ дж}/\text{м}^2$ is the coefficient of surface tension of water.

Let's equate the expressions for $Q$ and $\Delta U_\pi$:

$$4\pi L\rho R^2 \Delta R = 8\pi R \Delta R \sigma.$$

where

$$R = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho L} \sim 10^{-10} m = 10^{-8} \text{ см}.$$

Such a drop essentially cannot, since we found that $R$ is of the order of intermolecular distances in water. Consequently, no drop can evaporate without absorbing heat from outside.

When solving the problem, we assumed that the temperature of the drop and, therefore, its internal energy does not change. This change can, in principle, be taken into account, but it will not change the answer. Indeed, the specific heat capacity of water is 1 cal/€·deg, and the specific heat of vaporization is 539 cal/€·deg. Consequently, if the droplet temperature is room temperature (20°C), then when it is cooled to 0°C, approximately $\frac{20}{539} \approx 0,04$ of the droplet mass can evaporate. Therefore, it is clear that taking into account the change in the internal energy of the drop cannot change the answer.
