## Condition

A children's pistol, which can be thought of as a finite-mass weapon attached to a stationary wall, fires a ball, giving it a velocity v. If you shoot a ball with twice its mass, its speed will be v√2/3. What will be the speed of a ball with three times its mass?

P. I. Zubkov

## Solution

A child's pistol, which can be thought of as a spring of finite mass attached to a fixed wall, fires a ball, giving it a speed v. If you shoot a ball with twice its mass, its speed will be $v\sqrt{2/3}$. What will be the speed of a ball with three times its mass?

The acceleration of the ball by the spring continues until the spring is in an equilibrium state. If by this moment the ball is picking up speed v, then the speed of the free (not fixed to the wall) end of the spring is the same. The kinetic energy of a spring, in which in an equilibrium state one end is accumulated and the other has a speed v, can be represented in the form $aMv^2/2$, where M is the mass of the spring, uniformly distributed along its length, a is a numerical coefficient, does not depend on the speed of the end of the spring.

Let us write the law of conservation of energy for all three cases (for balls with masses m, 2m, 3m), considering the initial state of the spring to be the same each time and equal to $x_0$:

$$\frac{kx_0^2}{2} = \frac{m}{2} v^2 + \frac{1}{2} aMv^2,$$

$$\frac{kx_0^2}{2} = \frac{2m}{2} \left(\frac{2}{3} v^2\right) + \frac{1}{2} aM \left(\frac{2}{3} v^2\right),$$

$$\frac{kx_0^2}{2} = \frac{3m}{2} u^2 + \frac{1}{2} aMu^2.$$

Solving these equations together, we find the speed u that a ball of mass 3m gains when fired:

$$u = v \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}.$$

P. I. Zubkov
