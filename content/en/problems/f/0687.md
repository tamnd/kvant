Lemma 3. The symmetry axes of any polygon intersect at one point.

Suppose that $l$, $m$ is a disjoint axis of symmetry of the polygon $M$. Let $n$ be the general perpendicular $l$, $m$; distance rectangular coordinate system with origin at point $O = l \cap n$, with axis $Oz$ directed along ray $OA$, where $A = n \cap m$; let $|OA| = a$. Then, with symmetry of the relative axis $l$, the coordinate $z$ of any point goes into $(-z)$, and with symmetry of the relative $m$ - into $(2a - z)$. Therefore, when these two symmetries are composed, $z$ changes to $2a$. By repeating this composition a sufficient number of times, we will “drive” any point beyond the boundaries of the polygon $M$. Contradiction!

Here is an even shorter proof of Lemma 3 (though using a concept borrowed from mechanics): let $O$ be the center of mass of identical weights placed at the vertices of the polygon $M$; it is clear that for any self-alignment of the polygon $M$ the weights only change places, so the point $O$ turns into itself; in particular, all axes of symmetry $M$ pass through the point $O$.
