## Condition

Inside a ball of radius $R$, uniformly charged with volume density $\rho$, there is a grounded metal sphere of radius $r$ (Fig. 2). Find the dependence of the potential of this system on the distance to the center of the sphere.

## Solution

Inside a dielectric ball of radius $R_0$, uniformly charged with a volume density $\rho$, we find a grounded metal sphere of radius $r$ (Fig. 6). The dependence of the room and the electrical field of this system depends on the distance to the center of the sphere.

Let us denote the charge of the grounded metal sphere by $q$ (later we will find its value); obviously $q < 0$. This charge outside the sphere (at $R \geq r$) creates a field the same as the exact charge $q$ placed at the center of the sphere: the projection of the charge $\vec{E}$ in a chosen direction from the center $E = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R^2}$. There is no field inside the sphere (at $R < r$): $E = 0$. The graph of $E$ versus $R$ for the field of a charged sphere is shown on figure 7 blue lines.

The potential $\varphi$ of this field outside the sphere changes according to the law $\varphi = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R}$, and inside the sphere it is equal to the potential of the surface of the sphere: $\varphi = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r}$. figure 8 blue lines show a graph of $\varphi$ versus $R$.

A uniformly charged ball outside itself (at $R \geq R_0$) creates a field the same as the exact charge, equal to the charge of the ball $Q = \frac{4}{3} \pi \rho R_0^3$, we are in its center: $E = \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R^2} = \frac{\rho R_0^3}{3\varepsilon_0 R^2}$. The field potential of a charged ball outside the ball is equal to the field potential of the exact charge $Q$ placed at the center of the ball: $\varphi = \frac{Q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 R} = \frac{\rho R_0^3}{3 \varepsilon_0 R}$.

On the surface of the ball $\varphi = \frac{\rho R_0^2}{3 \varepsilon_0}$, and as it approaches the center of the ball, the potential increases: the work done by the electric field in crossing a unit charge from the point under consideration inside the ball on its surface is added. This work is equal to the average field strength in the area under consideration, multiplied by the movement of a unit charge. Since the tension varies linearly inside the ball,

$$E_{cp} = \frac{E_R + E_{R0}}{2} = \frac{\rho (R + R_0)}{6 \varepsilon_0}.$$

Then the field potential inside the ball is equal to

$$\varphi = \frac{\rho R_0^2}{3 \varepsilon_0} + E_{cp} (R_0 - R) = \frac{\rho R_0^2}{3 \varepsilon_0} + \frac{\rho (R_0^2 - R^2)}{6 \varepsilon_0} = \frac{\rho (3 R_0^2 - R^2)}{6 \varepsilon_0}.$$

The corresponding graph is shown at figure 8 black line.

In order to construct a graph of the dependence of the potential $\varphi$ field of the system on $R$, it is necessary to add up the graphs constructed separately for the fields of a sphere and a ball. At figure 8 the results of this graph are shown with a red line.

It remains to find the magnitude of the $q$ charge on the sphere. Since the sphere is displaced, its potential is zero. This means that on the surface of the sphere, at $R = r$, the field potential of the system is zero:

$$\frac{q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 r} + \frac{\rho R_0^2}{3 \varepsilon_0} + \frac{\rho (R_0^2 - r^2)}{6 \varepsilon_0} = 0.$$

Let us denote by $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{-v}$ the velocities of charges moving away from each other, and by $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{-u}$ the velocities of charges approaching each other. Since the thread is inextensible, the following equality holds for the velocity projections onto the thread:

$$|\vec{v}| \cos \alpha = |\vec{u}| \sin \alpha, \text{ или } |\vec{u}| = |\vec{v}| \text{ ctg} \alpha.$$

For small displacements of charges from the equilibrium position, the angle is $\alpha \approx 45^\circ$, cot $\alpha \approx 1$, therefore $|\vec{u}| \approx |\vec{v}|$. So the kinetic energy of the system is

$$2 \frac{mv^2}{2} + 2 \frac{mu^2}{2} \approx 2 mv^2.$$

The potential energy of the system is equal to the work that must be expended to create the system, that is, to transfer charges $q$ from infinity to given positions.

Let charges 1 and 3 be displaced from the equilibrium position by a small distance $x$ (Fig. 10). In this case, charges 2 and 4 are displaced by a distance $y$ also, which

$$\left( \frac{l}{\sqrt{2}} + x \right)^2 + \left( \frac{l}{\sqrt{2}} - y \right)^2 = l^2$$

(since the length of the thread $l$ does not change).

When transferring the first charge, the equation is not perfect, since there is no electric field; when moving the second charge in the field of the first, the following happens:

$$A_1 = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 l}.$$

To introduce a third charge against the field created by the first two charges, you need to perform a reverse

$$A_2 = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 l} + \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (\sqrt{2} l + 2x)};$$

and when the fourth charge is introduced, a revolution occurs

$$A_3 = 2 \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 l} + \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (\sqrt{2} l - 2y)}.$$

Thus, the potential energy of the system is equal to

$$A_1 + A_2 + A_3 \approx \frac{q^2}{\pi\varepsilon_0 l} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}} \right) + \frac{3q^2 x^2}{2\sqrt{2} \pi\varepsilon_0 l^3}$$

(here we used the connection between $x$ and $y$ given by formula (3)).

The total energy of the system consists of kinetic and potential energy and does not change over time:

$$2mv^2 + \frac{q^2}{\pi\varepsilon_0 l} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}} \right) + \frac{3q^2 x^2}{2\sqrt{2} \pi\varepsilon_0 l^3} = \text{const.}$$

Moving the constant terms to the right side of the equality, we get

$$2mv^2 + \frac{3q^2 x^2}{2\sqrt{2} \pi\varepsilon_0 l^3} = \text{const.}$$
