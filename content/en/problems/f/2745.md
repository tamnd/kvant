In this case, immediately after contact with the surface, a shock wave occurs, which creates a pressure in the steel equal to approximately $U uv_{cт} | 18$ GPa. Consequently, when a bullet hits a boron carbide surface, the material of the protective plate can split into pieces, but these parts acquire approximately the same speed, and since the plate material (boron carbide) will move in the direction of the bullet, the relative speed of the bullet and the plate becomes less, and therefore the pressure in the shock wave will become less than the given estimate of 18 GPa. The propagation of both longitudinal and transverse waves in the plate material leads to the fact that the mass of the plate material involved in the movement will be comparable and even greater than the mass of steel, which has already “felt” the impact. Estimation of this mass ratio is based on the assumption that the thickness of the plate should be approximately the same as the longitudinal size of the bullet, i.e. L | 1 cm. In this case, during the complete stop (relative to the plate) of the bullet, longitudinal waves in the plate manage to travel three times a distance equal to the thickness of the plate, and transverse waves manage to spread to a distance 1.5 times the size of the bullet.
