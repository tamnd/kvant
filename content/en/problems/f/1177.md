## Condition

The oscillating circuit consists of a coil with inductance L=1 H with resistance R=1 Ohm and a capacitor with capacity C=1 µF. The capacitor is neudel - its insulation resistance Rx is finite. At what value of Rx in the coil will 1/3 of the initial circuit energy be released as heat?

Z. A. Rafailov

Like other simple geometric problems, this problem has many solutions. Let's list three of them.

The first two use the inequality m≥2r, where m is the midline of the trapezoid, and r is the radius of the inscribed circle, t. e. 2r is the height of the trapezoid (Fig. 1).

If the diagonal makes an angle greater than 45° with the base, then its projection onto the base is less than 2r of the trapezoid. The sum of the projections of both diagonals, very/no, is equal to the sum of the bases, i.e. 2m. Therefore, if the statement of the problem is not true, then 2m<2r+2r=4r, which contradicts the inequality m≥2r.

## Solution

The oscillatory circuit consists of a coil with inductance $L = 1$ Gi and resistance $R = 1$ Ohm and a capacitor with capacity $C = 1$ µF. The capacitor is not ideal - its insulation resistance $R_x$ is finite. At what value $R_x$ in the coil is released in the form of heat 1/3 of the initial energy of the circuit?

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

LET'S CLEAR THE QUESTION ABOUT CIRCUIT DAMPING, first without taking into account losses in the capacitor (Fig. 1). If we consider the current fluctuations in the coil to be harmonics, then over the period the losses in the coil will be

$$\Delta W = \frac{I_0^2 R}{2} T,$$

where $I_0$ is the amplitude of the current in the coil, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}$ is the oscillation period. The maximum circuit energy is $W = \frac{LI_0^2}{2}$.

Then $$\frac{\Delta W}{W} = \frac{I_0^2 RT}{LI_0^2} = 2\pi R \sqrt{\frac{C}{L}}.$$

In our case

$$\frac{\Delta W}{W} \approx 6 \cdot 10^{-3} \ll 1,$$

that is, the vibrations can really be considered harmonious.

Note that the result will not change even taking into account the additional losses in the capacitor, which, according to the conditions of the problem, are only twice as large as the losses in the coil. So, in this case (Fig. 2) the losses in the coil are

$$\Delta W_2 = \frac{I_0^2 R}{2} T,$$

losses in the capacitor -

$$\Delta W_2 = \frac{U_0^2}{2R_x} T,$$

where $U_0$ is the voltage amplitude across the capacitor. Considering that $\Delta W_2 = 2\Delta W_1$ and $$\frac{LI_0^2}{2} = \frac{CU_0^2}{2},$$ we get

$$\frac{U_0^2}{2R_x} = \frac{CU_0^2 R}{L},$$

where does the resistance come from

$$R_x = \frac{L}{2CR} = 5 \cdot 10^5 \text{ Ом.}$$
