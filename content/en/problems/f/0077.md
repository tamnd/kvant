## Condition

A rectangular block, the height of which significantly exceeds its length and width, stands on a horizontal surface. How to determine the coefficient of friction between the block and the surface, having only one measuring device - a ruler?

Having made a loop from the thread, we throw it over the block and pull it in the horizontal direction (Fig. 9). If the thread is draped over a block close to the surface

If necessary, the block will slide. If we lift the thread along the block, then when it is at a certain height $h_0$ from the surface, the block will no longer slide, but will begin to turn over. In this case, the force $F$ with which we pull the block is equal to the friction force:

$$F = kmg,$$

and the moment of force $F$ relative to the axis passing through the point $A$ is equal to the moment of gravitational force:

$$Fh_0 = mg \frac{a}{2},$$

where $a$ is the side of the square at the base of the block.

Solving these two equations together, we find

$$k = \frac{a}{2h_0}.$$

By measuring $a$ and $h_0$ using a ruler, we find the coefficient of friction of the block on the surface.

Having tilted the block and holding it at a certain angle to the vertical, we apply a certain force $F$ to the block, directed along the block (Fig. 10). In this case, the reaction force $N = mg + F \cos \alpha$ and the friction force $F_{trp}$ will also act on the block. If the friction force does not reach the maximum value equal to $kN = k (mg + F \cos \alpha)$, then the end of the block will not slide. In this case, the friction force is equal to $F \sin \alpha$. Thus, the end of the bar does not slip when

$$F \sin \alpha < kmg + kF \cos \alpha,$$

that is, when

$$F < \frac{kmg}{\sin \alpha - k \cos \alpha}.$$

It is easy to see that at $k > tg \alpha$ the block cannot be made to move, no matter how large the force $F$. Let us find the angle $\alpha_0$ at which the block begins. Using a ruler, measure the height from the horizontal plane to the edge of the tilted block and the width of the block and calculate $tg \alpha_0$, we find the friction coefficient $k$.

20 helium contained in a cylinder under the piston is very slowly transferred from state I ($P_1 = 4,1$ amm, $V_1 = 32$ l) to state II ($P_2 = 15,5$ amm, $V_2 = 9$ l). Each gas with the highest temperature access is simpler if the graph of pressure versus volume is a straight line (Fig. !i)?

Let us plot the isotherm grid on the graph—hyperbola $PV = \text{const} \frac{m}{\mu} RT$. The higher the gas temperature, the farther the vertex of the hyperbola is from the origin. Therefore, it is clear that during process I-II the gas reaches such a high temperature at which the corresponding hyperbola does not intersect the line I-II, but only touches it.

Since the point with coordinates $P$ and $V$, corresponding to the maximum temperature, lies on both the hyperbola and the straight line, its coordinates must satisfy two equations: the equation of the hyperbola

$$PV = \frac{m}{\mu} RT$$

and the equation of the line

$$P = \alpha V + \beta$$

(α and β are constants. We will define them a little later). Substituting $P$ from the second equation into the first, we obtain a quadratic equation for $V$:

$$\alpha V^2 + \beta V - \frac{m}{\mu} RT = 0.$$

Since a hyperbola and a straight line must have only one common point, this equation can only have one root, that is, its determinate must be equal to zero:

$$\beta^2 + 4\alpha \frac{m}{\mu} RT = 0.$$

Solving the last equation for $T$, we find

$$T = -\frac{\beta^2 \mu}{4\alpha m R}.$$

In this expression you need to substitute the values ​​of the coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Since points I and II belong to the same line $P = \alpha V + \beta$, then $P_1 = \alpha V_1 + \beta$ and $P_2 = \alpha V_2 + \beta$. Solving this system of equations, we find

$$\alpha = -\frac{P_2 - P_1}{V_1 - V_2}, \quad \beta = \frac{P_2 V_1 - P_1 V_2}{V_1 - V_2}.$$

$$T = \frac{(P_2 V_1 - P_1 V_2)^2 \mu}{4 (P_2 - P_1) (V_1 - V_2) m R}.$$

Substituting the numerical values ​​of the quantities included in this expression, we obtain

$$T \approx 490^\circ \text{ K}.$$
