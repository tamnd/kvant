## Solution

If you look squinting at distant bright lamps, you usually see vertical or slanted columns of light coming down and up from the lamp. Explain this phenomenon. Create and carry out experiments to test your explanation.

Clue. The surface of the rose is not dry. It is always covered with a layer of tear fluid.

Fig. 8.

“Along the edges of the eyelid, the tear fluid forms a small meniscus in which light rays are refracted. As shown in figure 8, $a$, the rays are refracted from the upper eyelid so that they each go bluish, and the light source receives a “tail” formed downwards. In a similar way, a light “tail” appears at the lower eyelid, directed upward. The appearance of these “tails” can be clearly observed if, having closed one eye, slowly close the other, or raise and lower your head, keeping both eyes half-closed. The rays appear at the very moment when the eyelid begins to close the pupil, which is easy for a myopic person to notice, since the light source, which appears to him as a blurry circle, is at this moment partially eclipsed.

The rays are not completely parallel, even when viewed with only one eye. Look straight at the light source, and then turn your head slightly to the right and roll your eyes so that this source is again. Now the rays are directed obliquely (Fig. 8, 6). The reason seems to be that the edges of the eyelids, where they cross the pupil, are no longer horizontal, and each ray of rays is located at right angles to the edge of the eyelids. The apparent directions of the rays are consistent with this explanation. Now you can understand why the rays are not parallel if you look straight ahead: the curvature of the eyelids affects even with a small pupil width (Fig. 8, 8). Close the right edge of the pupil with your finger, and the rays located on the left will disappear.”

From the book “Light and Color in Nature” by M. Minnarma
