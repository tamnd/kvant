The system described in terms of conditions is a physical matrix. Finding the period of oscillation of a physical matrix is ​​a rather difficult task. The fact is that the period depends on the shape of the physical matrix, its configuration. But for any physical matrix it is possible to select a mathematical matrix whose oscillation period will be equal to the oscillation period of the given physical matrix. This is what we will do: we will select a mathematical matrix “equivalent” to the system depicted in figure 11.

The period of small oscillations of a mathematical matrix depends only on its length. So we need a fairly long “equivalent” mathematical matrix - the so-called reduced length of the physical matrix.

figure 11 shows the equilibrium position of our system. In this case, the center of gravity of the system (point $M$) is located on the vertical passing through the center of rotation $O$; $d$ is the radius vector of point $M$ relative to $O$. Let $l$ be the reduced length of the physical matrix. If during oscillations the angular velocities $\omega$ and $\omega'$ of rotation of the vectors $d$ and $l$ around the point $O$ are the same, then the periods of oscillation of the matrices are the same. Let's find $\omega$ and $\omega'$.

Let $\varphi$ be the maximum angle of deviation of the radius vector $d$ from the vertical. Let us find the angular velocity of a point in the system at the moment when the radius vector $d$ makes an angle $\theta$ with the vertical. The potential energy of the system at the moment of maximum deviation is equal to (Fig. 12)

$$\Pi_{\varphi} = (m_1 + m_2) g h_{\varphi} = (m_1 + m_2) g d (1 - \cos \varphi).$$

(The potential energy of the system in the equilibrium position is assumed to be zero.) At the moment of deviation by an angle $\theta$, the potential energy is equal to

$$\Pi_{\theta} = (m_1 + m_2) g h_{\theta} = (m_1 + m_2) g d (1 - \cos \theta),$$

and the kinetic energy is equal to

$$K_{\theta} = \frac{m_1 v_1^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 v_2^2}{2},$$

where $v_1 = \omega l_1$ and $v_2 = \omega l_2$ are the linear velocities of the balls at this moment. According to the law of conservation of energy $K_\theta = \Pi_\varphi - \Pi_\theta$, i.e.

$$\frac{m_1 \omega^2 l_1^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 \omega^2 l_2^2}{2} = (m_1 + m_2) gd (\cos \varphi - \cos \theta),$$

where

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 l_2^2}} (\cos \varphi - \cos \theta).$$

For a mathematical matrix (a point of mass $\mu$ suspended on a thread of length $l$), the potential energy at the moment of maximum deflection by an angle $\varphi$ is equal to (Fig. 13)

$$\Pi'_\varphi = \mu g l (1 - \cos \varphi).$$

When deflected by an angle $\theta$ —

$$\Pi'_\theta = \mu g l (1 - \cos \theta),$$

$$K'_\theta = \mu \frac{(v')^2}{2} = \mu \frac{(\omega')^2 l^2}{2}.$$

From the law of conservation of energy

$$\mu g l (1 - \cos \theta) + \mu \frac{(\omega')^2 l^2}{2} = \mu g l (1 - \cos \varphi)$$

let's find $\omega'$:

$$\omega' = \sqrt{\frac{2g}{l}} (\cos \varphi - \cos \theta).$$

From (1) and (2) it is clear that if we choose a mathematical matrix of such length $l$ that

$$\frac{2g}{l} = 2gd \frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 l_2^2},$$

then the oscillations of this matrix will be synchronous with the oscillations of the system depicted in figure 11.

So, the reduced length of the physical matrix is ​​equal to

$$l = \frac{m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 l_2^2}{d (m_1 + m_2)}.$$

Let us now find the distance $d$ from the center of swing $O$ to the center of gravity. Let $\alpha$ be the angle that, in the equilibrium position, forms a rod of length $l_1$ with the vertical (Fig. 14). Then $m_1 g r_1 = m_2 g r_2$, or

$$m_1 g l_1 \sin \alpha = m_2 g l_2 \sin \left( \frac{\pi}{2} - \alpha \right) = m_2 g l_2 \cos \alpha,$$

where

$$tg \alpha = \frac{m_2 l_2}{m_1 l_1}, \sin \alpha = \frac{m_2 l_2}{\sqrt{m_1^2 l_1^2 + m_2^2 l_2^2}},$$

$$\cos \alpha = \frac{m_1 l_1}{\sqrt{m_1^2 l_1^2 + m_2^2 l_2^2}}.$$

We assumed the potential energy of the system in the equilibrium position to be zero. This means that $m_2 g h_2 - m_1 g h_1 = 0$ (see Fig. 14), i.e.

$$m_1 g (d - l_1 \cos \alpha) + m_2 g (d - l_2 \sin \alpha) = 0.$$

The equalities have been studied, and by substituting the found expressions for sin α and cos α, we find

$$d = \frac{\sqrt{m_1^2 l_1^2 + m_2^2 l_2^2}}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

Substituting this expression for d into formula (3), we find the reduced length of the physical matrix:

$$l = \frac{m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 l_2^2}{\sqrt{m_1^2 l_1^2 + m_2^2 l_2^2}}.$$

Thus, the period of free oscillations is equal to

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{1}{g}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 l_2^2}{\sqrt{m_1^2 l_1^2 + m_2^2 l_2^2}}}.$$
