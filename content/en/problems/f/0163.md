## Condition

According to one of the first models of the hydrogen atom (Thomson's model), it is a uniformly charged electric ball with an electron in the center. In general, atom is neutral. Find the radius of such an atom if it is known that the minimum energy that must be imparted to an electron to remove it from the atom over a large distance is equal to W. The charge of the electron is e. (10 grades)

Fig. 1. Fig. 2.

## Solution

According to one of the first models of the hydrogen atom (Thomson's model), it is a uniformly charged polymetric electric ball, in which there is an electron. In general, the atom is neutral. The maximum energy that must be imparted to an electron to remove it from an atom over a long distance is equal to $W$. The magnitude of the electron charge is $e$.

The value $W$ is equal to the work that must be expended in order to remove an electron from an atom.

It is easy to find the work that must be expended in order to remove an electron from the surface of an atom “to infinity” (infinitely far from it). This work is equal to the potential energy of the electron and the surface of a polymetrically charged ball, that is, the potential electric field on the surface of the ball $\varphi = \frac{e}{R}$ (e is the charge of the ball and $R$ is its radius), multiplied by the value of the electron charge

$$A_1 = \frac{e}{R} \cdot e = \frac{e^2}{R}.$$

In order to find the work that must be expended to move an electron from the center of the atom to its surface, divide the ball into thin spherical elephants of thickness $\Delta r$ so that throughout each of these elephants the force acting on the charge can be considered constant. To do this you need $\Delta r \ll R$. For example, the field created by the charges of each of the words is known to be zero inside this layer, and outside the layer it is the same as it would be if the weight of the charge were concentrated in the center of the ball. This means that the direction of the electric field inside an atom at a distance of $r$ from its center is

$$E = \frac{q}{r^2},$$

and the force acting on the electron in this field is equal to

$$F = \frac{qe}{r^2},$$

where $q$ is the charge that is inside the sphere of radius $r$.

Since the charge of the ball is $e$ and it is distributed uniformly over the ball, then the charge is concentrated in a unit volume of the ball

$$\frac{e}{4\pi R^3}.$$

This means that inside a ball with radius $r$ there will be a charge

$$q = \frac{e}{4\pi R^3} \cdot \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 = e \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^3.$$
