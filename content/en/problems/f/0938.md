## Condition

The pilot of a spacecraft moving at a speed of $v=1$ km/s was then directly on the course of an astroid with a diameter of $d=7$ km, when a distance of $l=8,5$ km remained to its surface. The cosmonaut immediately turned on the emergency thrusters, which in a negligibly short time impart to the ship an additional speed of $\Delta v=300$ m/s, the direction of which is set by the astronaut. Can a ship avoid a collision?

A. V. Andranov

## Solution

The pilot of a spacecraft moving at a speed of $v = 1$ km/s, then at the rate of an astropod with a diameter of $d = 7$ km, when a distance of $l = 8,5$ km remained to its surface. The astronaut immediately turned on the emergency engines, which in a short time imparted to the ship an additional speed of $\Delta v = 300$ m/s, the direction of which was set by the astronaut. Is it possible for a ship to avoid a collision?

♦

To avoid a collision, the astronaut must also change the speed of the ship so that the angle between the original direction “toward the asterid” and the new course is greater than the angle $\alpha_0$ determined by the conditions (Fig. 1)

$$\sin \alpha_0 = \frac{d/2}{l + d/2} = \frac{d}{2l + d} \approx 0,292.$$

The maximum deviation from the initial initial course when imparting additional speed $\Delta v$ to the ship is ensured in the case when the vector $\vec{\Delta v}$ turns out to be perpendicular to the vector $\vec{v}_1 = \vec{v} + \vec{\Delta v}$ (Fig. 2), that is, when

This means that when the emergency engines are turned on, the astronaut can change the ship’s course by an angle of $\alpha > \alpha_0$, and a collision with the asterid will not occur.
