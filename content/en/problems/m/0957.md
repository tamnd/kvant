## Condition

Prove that from 1985 different natural numbers, all of whose prime factors are contained among the first 9 prime numbers 2,3,...,23, you can choose four numbers whose product is the fourth power of an integer.

## Solution

Prove that from 1985 different natural numbers, all of whose prime factors are contained among the 9 prime numbers 2,3,...,23, you can choose four numbers whose product is the fourth power of an integer.

M956—M960; F968—F972
Let us present one of many solutions to the problem.
Let us denote the centers of the circles by $O_1, O_2, O_3, O_4$, and the points of their pairwise intersection by $K, L, M, N$ (see figure). Since the circles are equal, the point $P$ of intersection of the common chord $AK$ of circles $O_1$ and $O_2$ with their linear centers $O_1O_2$ divides both segments $AK$ and $O_1O_2$ in half. Similarly, the segments $O_3O_2$ and $AL$ have a common midpoint $Q$. Therefore, the segment $PQ$ is the midline of two triangles - $O_1O_2O_3$ and $AKL$, that is, $\overrightarrow{O_1O_3}=2\overrightarrow{PQ}=\overrightarrow{KL}$. It is proved in the same way that $\overrightarrow{O_1O_3}=\overrightarrow{NM}$, and from the equality $\overrightarrow{KL}=\overrightarrow{O_1O_3}=\overrightarrow{NM}$ it follows that $KLMN$ is a parallelogram.

Prove on your own that $A$ and $B$ are the points of intersection of the altitudes of triangles $BKL$ and $AMN$; this fact can be used for another solution to the problem (cf. the final remark in the solution to problem M951 in the previous issue «Kvant»).

Let us prove that from these numbers we can choose quite a lot of pairs $(a,b)$ such that the product $ab$ is the square of an integer; more precisely: from $N$ numbers (with 9 prime factors) you can choose at least $(N-2^9)/2$ such pairs. In our problem there are $N=1985$, so we can identify at least $(1985-512)/2$, that is, at least 737 pairs $(a,b)$. Then to $N=737$ with the integer $\sqrt{ab}$ one can apply the same reasoning again and obtain such a pair $(\sqrt{ab}, \sqrt{cd})$—even $(737-512)/2+1/2=113$ such pairs—that the product $\sqrt{ab} \cdot \sqrt{cd}$ is the square of the integer, that is, $abcd$ is the fourth power of the integer.

It remains to prove the stated fact. According to the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, every natural number can be uniquely represented as a product of prime factors. Product $ab$ of two integers

$$a=2^{\alpha_1}3^{\alpha_2}...23^{\alpha_9} \text{ и } b=2^{\beta_1}3^{\beta_2}...23^{\beta_9}$$

will be a perfect square if and only if the exponents $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ have the same parity (for each $i$ from 1 to 9); in this case, we will call the sets $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,...,\alpha_9)$ and $(\beta_1,\beta_2,...,\beta_9)$ comparable. From $N$ sets of indicators corresponding to $N$ given number (with the set 2,3,...,23), we will select pairs of comparable sets one after another, as long as this is possible. a set without a pair remains at most $2^9$, since there are only $2^9$ pairwise incomparable sets of indicators (each of the 9 places can have either an even or an odd number - a total of $2^9$ options).

Let us give one more reasoning - by induction on $i_1$. For $i_1=0$ our statement is obvious $(w(0)=1)$.

Let us prove that if it is true for some $i_1=j_1$, then it is also true for $i_1=2j_1$ and $i_1=2j_1+1$. To do this, using the indication assumption and formulas (2), (3) and (4) in the fields, we study separately the coefficients for even and odd powers $x$ in the polynomials $(1+x)^{i_1}$ and $P(x)$. Setting, depending on the parity, $i_8$, $i_8=2j_8$ or $i_8=2j_8+1$ and applying the formulas in the margins, we write the polynomial $P(x)$ in the form $F_1(x^2)+xF_2(x^2)$.

If $i_1=2j_1$, then, according to (2), $w(i_1)=w(j_1)$. Moreover, if $i_2>i_1+1$, then the notation $P(x)$ begins with $(1+x^2)^{j_1}$ and includes the coefficients at even powers $x$ in the weight $P(x)$ (that is, the weight $F_1(x)$) is no less than $w(j_1)$. If $i_2=i_1+1$, then, using (4), we begin writing $P(x)$ from $(1+x^2)^{j_1}$ and, by the indication assumption, the contribution of $W(F_2)$ coefficients for odd powers $x$ to the weight $P(x)$ is no less than $w(j_1)$.

If $i_1=2j_1+1$, then, according to (3), $w(i_1)=2w(j_1)$ and includes in the weight $P(x)$ both odd and odd powers $x$ no less than $w(j_1)$, so $W(P) \geqslant 2w(j_1)=w(i_1)$.

Finally, we outline the most respectful solution, using the general rule for describing all odd coefficients of the polynomial $(1+x)^i$. Let us represent $i$ in the form

$$i=2^{m_1}+2^{m_2}+\dots+2^{m_r}, m_1<m_2<\dots<m_r;$$

such a representation always exists and the only one $(m_1,...,m_r)$ is the numbers of digits of the double notation of the number $i$, in which there is 1). Then according to formula (1) on the fields $(1+x)^i=(1+x^{q_1})\dots(1+x^{q_r})$, where $q_i=2^{m_i}$. Therefore, the coefficient $C_i^k$ of the polynomial $(1+x)^i$ for $x^k$ is odd if and only if k is equal to the odd subsum of the sum (*). Denoting by $M(i)$ the set of exponents $\{m_1,...,m_r\}$ in the double expansion $i$, this condition can be written as $M(k)\subset M(i)$. Consequently, the coefficient of $x^k$ of the polynomial $P(x)$ is odd if and only if the set $B=M(k)$ is contained in an odd number of polynomials $A_s=M(i_s)$, $s=1,2,...,n$; We will also call many $B$ odd. So, we need to prove the following combinatory fact: the number of all subsets of the plurality $A_1$ is not less than the number of all odd pluralities. To do this, it is enough to associate all $C \subset A_1$ distinct odd subsets $F(C)$.

If $C$ itself is odd, we put $F(C)=C$. Otherwise, we add to $C$ a set chosen as follows. Let's look at everything $A_s$. containing $C(s \geqslant 2)$ - their number is odd - and the set $D_s$, consisting of all elements $A_s$ not included in $A_1$. Among these sets there may be identical ones; Let us leave only those that are represented by an odd number of copies, from them we select the odd largest (not containing in others) $D_h$ and put $F(C)=C \cup D_h$. It is easy to see that the entire set $F(C)$ are odd and distinct $(F(C) \cap A_1=C)$.
