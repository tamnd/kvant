## Condition

Several numbers are now sent to. It is allowed to assign to these numbers a number equal to the arithmetic mean of the length or several of them, if it is different from all the numbers already written. Prove that, starting with two numbers 0 and 1, using the described operations you can get
a) number 1/5;
b) any rational number between 0 and 1.

Fig. 4.

$$\frac{1}{3} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{8}}{3};$$
$$\frac{1}{5} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{3}{32} + \frac{1}{32}}{5}$$

Fig. 2.

Let's start by representing the number 1/5 and 1/q in general. The numbers 1/2, 1/4, 3/4 are very easy to obtain:

$$\frac{1}{2} = \frac{0+1}{2}, \frac{1}{4} = \frac{0+\frac{1}{2}}{2}, \frac{3}{4} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}+1}{2}.$$

It is also possible, very indo, to obtain any double double-rational fraction, that is, a fraction of the form p/2^m, p<2^m (Fig. 1).

To get the fraction 1/q, it is enough to represent 1 as the sum of q different proper double-rational fractions: their arithmetic mean will be exactly 1/q. Let us prove by induction that this is possible. 1 can be represented as the sum of two such fractions (for example, 1=3/4+1/4).

From representation 1 as a sum of n-1 different double-rational fractions, we can obtain its representation as a sum of n such fractions:

$$1 = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_{n-1} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{a_1}{2} + \frac{a_2}{2} + \dots + \frac{a_{n-1}}{2}.$$

In particular, following this construction, we obtain

$$1 = \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{8} =$$
$$= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{16} + \frac{1}{16} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{3}{32} + \frac{1}{32},$$

which gives the representation of the number 1/5.

Geler can go about proving the second statement of the problem in many different ways. Let's give two of them.

1. Let us prove that if a natural number p can be represented as a sum of n-1 different proper double-rational fractions:

$$p = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_{n-1} \quad (0 < a_{n-1} < \dots < a_2 < a_1 < 1),$$

then it can be represented as a sum of n such fractions. Let 2^m be the lowest of the values ​​of the terms a_i. Replacing a_n-1 with the sum $\left(a_{n-1} - \frac{1}{2^m+2}\right) + \frac{1}{2^m+2}$, we get the desired representation. So, each of the fractions $\frac{1}{q}, \frac{2}{q}, \dots, \frac{q-1}{q}$ can be represented as the arithmetic mean of various proper double-rational fractions, then the fractions $\frac{1}{q+1}, \frac{2}{q+1}, \dots, \frac{q-1}{q+1}$ can be represented in a similar form. To prove the proof by induction (the base of induction is the representation of the number 1/2), it remains to represent the fraction $\frac{q}{q+1}$ in the required form. It can be obtained from the representation of the number $\frac{1}{q+1}$ by replacing all fractions a_i with 1—a_i.

2. Let's consider the three numbers already obtained: 0, 1/q, 1. However, of the two middle ones, $\frac{0+1}{2}, \frac{1/q+1}{2}$ are fractions of the form p_0/q, 1<p_0<q (если q>2). However, of the two middle ones $\frac{0+p_0/q}{2}, \frac{1/q+p_0/q}{2}$ -

Fraction $p_1/q$, where $1<p_1<p_0$ (if $q>3$). It is clear that by continuing this process we will obtain the fraction $2/q$. Having then separated the triple of numbers $1/q, 2/q, p_0/q$, we get the fraction $3/q$ using the same descent, then (if $q>4$) $4/q$, ..., $(q-1)/q$, etc. (Fig. 2).

Note that we need to find the arithmetic mean of more than two numbers only once.
