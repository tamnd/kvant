## Condition

Each square of the board $n \times n$ has a chip. Is it possible to rearrange them so that any two pieces that threatened each other with a knight move, after the rearrangement, begin to threaten each other with a king move, if $n=3$, $6$ $n=6$, c) $n=4$?

S. Stefanov

## Solution

Each square of the board $n \times n$ has a chip. Is it possible to rearrange them so that any two chips that cut each other with the move of the knight, after the rearrangement, begin to cut each other with the move of the king, if $a) n=3, b) n=6, c) n=4$?**

◆
a) Answer: yes. The required permutation is shown at figure 1.

6) Answer: no. To prove this, we introduce two “distances” between the squares of the board: $d_1(a, b)$ is the smallest number of knight moves required to move from cell $a$ to cell $b$, $d_2(a, b)$ is the most similar number of king moves. Let us denote by $a'$ and $b'$ the cells on which the chips from cells $a$ and $b$ fall after the rearrangement. Then the condition considered in the problem is very indo, which implies the inequality $d_1(a, b) \geqslant d_2(a', b')$. If $a'$ and $b'$ are opposite corner cells, then (for $n=6$) $d_2(a', b')=5$, but for any two cells $a$ and $b$ $d_1(a, b) \leqslant 4$. This can be verified directly, and it is enough to consider the case when $a$ is a corner cell (for example, $d_1$-distance between the head cells on figure 2 is completed does not exceed $d_1$-distance between pink cells; the numbers on this figure are equal to $d_1$-distances from the corresponding cells to lower left).

The case of any $n > 6$ is considered in the same way: it can be shown that the most value of $d_1(a, b)$ for the board $n \times n$ is equal to $[2(n+1)/3]$ (at $n \geq 5$)*, that is, it is smaller than the $n-1 - d_2$-distance between opposite corners.

c) Answer: no. Let $a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4$ be the squares on which the pieces were built, which ended up in the corners of the board after the rearrangement. then $d_1(a_i, a_j) \geq 3$ at $1 \leq i < j \leq 4$ (see point 6)). On figure 3, for three significantly different positions (taking into account symmetries) cells $a_1$, cells located at $d_1$-distance 3, 4 or 5 from $a_1$ are offset. Using this figure, it is not difficult to check by direct search that in no case can you suggest cells $a_2, a_3, a_4$ with the condition $d_1(a_i, a_j) \geq 3$ met.

It is similarly proved that for $n=5$ we require no permutation.
