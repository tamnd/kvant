## Condition

On a sphere of radius 1, a) a curve is drawn whose length is less than π;
b) a closed curve whose length is less than 2π. Prove that there is a plane passing through the center of the sphere that does not intersect the drawn curve. (We can consider that a curve on a sphere is a “broken line” consisting of two large circles.)

V. V. Prasolov, G. A. Galperin

## Solution

On a sphere of radius 1, a) a curve is drawn whose length is less than l; b) a closed curve whose length is less than 2l. Prove that there is a plane passing through the center of the sphere that does not intersect the drawn curve. (We can assume that a curve on a sphere is a “broken line”, consisting of arcs of great circles.)

Fig. 1.

The modulus of a point A, and if there is an odd number of points of the set M on at least one grid line, take A on this line. Using the induction hypothesis, color the remaining points as required by the condition. Teler needs to prove that point A can be colored in such a way that this condition is not violated on two lines l and m passing through A.

After preliminary coloring, on each of the lines l and m there will either be an equal number of red and white points, and then our condition will not be violated for any coloring of A, or there will be 1 more points of one color than the other, and then A must be colored with the missing color. This would be impossible only if there were more white dots on one line, say on l, and more red dots on the other. But then on each of the lines l and m there is an even number of points of the set M (including A), and, therefore, by virtue of the choice of A, on all grid lines there is an even number of points from M. In this case, on all lines other than l and m, the number of white points would be equal to the number of red ones. Counting the total number of white and red points along lines parallel to lines l (including l), we would get that there are 1 more white points than red ones; when counting along lines parallel to m, it would turn out that there are more red dots. This contradiction shows that the only unpleasant option cannot occur, and point A can always be colored in accordance with the conditions.

A. P. Savin

The solution to this problem becomes shorter and clearer if it is presented in the language of spherical geometry. Therefore, with some words of this language.

The great circle, or, as is more often said, the greater circle, is the circle ω along which the sphere intersects an arbitrary plane α containing its center O; the ends of the diameter of the sphere perpedicular to the plane α are called stripes of the circle ω, the spherical distance s(A,B) between two points of the sphere A and B is defined as the length of the minor arc of the great circle connecting A and B; if the radius of the sphere is 1, then obviously

s(A,B)=∠AOB.

It is known that each of the plane angles of a trihedral angle is less than the sum of the other two (the proof can be found, for example, in the textbook: Geometry 9-10 / ed. 3. A. Skopts. - M.: Prosveshchenie, 1981, p. 224), therefore the spherical distance satisfies the “triangle inequality” *):

s(A,B)<s(A,C)+s(C,B).

In planimetry, from a similar inequality it is deduced that a segment is the shortest of the lines on a plane connecting two given points; In spherical geometry it is proved in the same way that the shortest line between two points on a sphere is an arc of a great circle.

In the second solution we will only prove that the required plane exists, without specifying it specifically. The advantage of this solution is that it is suitable for any set of curves on a sphere whose total length is less than π.

Let's consider the set of all large circles intersecting our curve, and for each we note its stripes. Let us prove that the plane of the set M of all marked stripes is less than the plane of the sphere, i.e. 4π. This will mean that there is a larger circle whose stripes do not belong to the set M, i.e., does not intersect our curve.

It is clear that the stripes of all great circles passing through some point X themselves form a larger circle ω_X with a stripe X. When point X runs past each AB of the great circle, the circles ω_X “sweep” two equal bigons (Fig. 2) on the sphere: the vertices of these bigons are stripes of the great circle AB, and the “raster angle” of the bigons is equal to the length s(A,B) of the other ABs. The area of ​​a bigon with angle α on a sphere of radius 1 is equal to 2α (obviously, it is proportional to α and equal to 2π for α=π). Consequently, the plane of the set of stripes of great circles intersecting each AB is equal to 4s(A,B), and the area of ​​the entire set M does not exceed 4(α₁+...+αₙ)<4π, where α₁,...,αₙ are the lengths of the stripes that make up a given curve (or several curves).

6) Let us select two points A and B on this curve and divide it into parts of equal length. Let us draw a larger circle through A and B and take the midpoint P on the smaller of its two ends A and B. Now, as in the first solution to problem a), we must prove that for any point X of our curve s(P,X)<π/2. And this inequality follows from the lemma given below (the sum of the lengths of the arcs AX and XB does not exceed the length of the section of the curve AXB and therefore is less than π).

Lemma. Let P be the midpoint of side AB of the spherical triangle ABX (on a sphere of radius 1). Then the length of its “median” s(P,X) is less than, equal to or greater than π/2 if the sum of the length is its side s(A,X)+s(B,X) corresponding to less than, equal to or greater than π.

Proof. Let first s(P,X)=π/2 and Y be a point on the sphere diametrically opposite to X (Fig. 3). Spherical triangles PXB and PYA are congruent (they are “spherically symmetrical” about the center P), so s(X,B)=s(Y,A) and

s(A,X)+s(X,B)=s(A,X)+s(Y,A)=π.

If s(P,X)>π/2, put on the arc PX a point X₁ such that s(P,X₁)=π/2 (Fig. 4), then by the proven sums s(A,X₁)+s(X₁,B)=π, but from the triangle inequality it follows (see Fig. 4) that it is less s(A,X)+s(X,B). The case s(P,X)<π/2 is treated similarly.

Problem 6) also admits a solution similar to the second solution to problem a). Let the set M be formed by the stripes of all great circles intersecting a given curve. Any of these circles intersects the curve at least 2 times (since it is closed), therefore the bigons considered in the solution to problem a) cover M in at least 2 layers, and therefore the area of ​​the set M is less than 4 2π/2=4π, i.e. less than the area of ​​the sphere.
