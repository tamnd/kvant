## Condition

The convex n-gon $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ contains an n-gon $B_1B_2 \dots B_n$ whose area is P (the vertex $B_i$ lies on the side $A_iA_{i+1}$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots, n-1$, and the vertex $B_n$ lies on $A_1A_1$). Near the same n-gon $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ there is an n-gon $C_1C_2 \dots C_n$ whose area is equal to Q, so that $C_1C_2||B_1B_2, C_2C_3||B_2B_3, \dots, C_nC_1||B_nB_1$

## Solution

The convex n-district $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ contains the n-district $B_1B_2 \dots B_n$, the area of ​​which is equal to P (the vertex $B_i$ lies on the side $A_iA_{i+1}$ for $i=1, 2, \dots, n-1$, and the vertex $B_n$ on $A_nA_1$). Near the model of the n-county $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ an n-county $C_1C_2 \dots C_n$ is described, the area of which is equal to Q, so that $C_1C_2 \parallel B_1B_2$, $C_2C_3 \parallel B_2B_3$, ..., $C_nC_1 \parallel B_nB_1$ (the vertex $A_i$ lies on the side $C_i-1C_i$ for $i=2, \dots, n$, and the vertex $A_1$—on the side $C_nC_1$; see Fig. 13). Find the area of ​​the n-district $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$.

Let's first solve the problem for n=3. Let us prove that in this case the area S of the triangle $A_1A_2A_3$ is equal to the geometric mean of the areas of the inscribed and circumscribed triangles:

$$S^2 = QP.$$  (1)

Since the triangles $B_1B_2B_3$ and $C_1C_2C_3$ are similar, the lines $B_1C_1$, $B_2C_2$ and $B_3C_3$ intersect at one point O (prove it!), while $\Delta B_1B_2B_3$ can be obtained from $\Delta C_1C_2C_3$ by a similar slope (homoteic) with center at point O and coefficient

$$k = \frac{B_1B_2}{C_1C_2} = \frac{B_2B_3}{C_2C_3} = \frac{B_3B_1}{C_3C_1} = \frac{OB_i}{OC_i}$$  (2)

($i=1, 2, 3$).

Let us denote by $q_i$, $s_i$ and $p_i$ the parts of the areas Q, S and P. Accordingly, the lying interior triangles $C_1OC_2$ (at $i=1$), $C_2OC_3$ (i=2) and $C_3OC_1$ (i=3); for example, $q_1$ is the area of ​​the head triangle on figure 14, $s_1$ is the sum of the areas of the head and pink triangles (with a common base), $p_1$ is the sum of the areas of the head triangle and the shaded trapezoid (that is, the area of ​​the triangle $C_1OC_2$). Then for

⟦folio 25⟧

each $i = 1, 2, 3$
$$\frac{q_i}{s_i} = k, \quad \frac{q_i}{p_i} = k^2$$

and therefore $\frac{s_i}{p_i} = k$.

Because
$$\frac{Q}{S} = \frac{\sum q_i}{\sum s_i} = k$$

And
$$\frac{S}{P} = \frac{\sum s_i}{\sum p_i} = k$$

whence follows (1).

The same answer is obtained for an arbitrary $n$ if the $n$-gons $B_1B_2 \dots B_n$ and $C_1C_2 \dots C_n$ are similar. The conditions of the problem contain a clever LOB (most of the readers who sent us letters about this problem fell into it): it only says that the sides of the polygons are parallel; This, of course, implies equality of angles, but does not imply similarity of polygons - proportionality of the corresponding sides. Already for $n = 4$ it is not difficult to construct examples (Fig. 15 and 16) showing that equality (1) is not necessarily satisfied (four of our readers: V. Tarasov (Leningrad), S. Fiashin (Leningrad), A. Vyatchin (Pavlov) and A. Goncharov (Nikopol) noticed this), that is, $S$ is not uniquely determined by the given $P$ and $Q$. In this case, the question of the natural problem can be understood as follows: what values ​​can $S$ take given $P$ and $Q$?

It is relatively easy to come up with examples in which $S$, $Q$ and $P$ are related by the inequality
$$S^2 \geq QP.$$

Check that already figure 16 (where $B_1B_2B_3B_4$ and $C_1C_2C_3C_4$ are equilateral boundaries) allows us to realize any triple of values ​​$S$, $Q$ and $P$ related by inequality (3). A much more difficult and interesting problem is this: is inequality (3) necessarily satisfied?

We leave it to the reader to think about this issue; We will dedicate a note to him in one of the next issues of Kvanat.
