## Condition

At the trial, 14 coins were presented as evidence. The expert discovered that seven of them were fake, the weakened ones were real, and he found out which ones were fake and which ones were real. The court only knows that counterfeit coins have the same value, real coins have the same value, and counterfeit coins are lighter than real ones. The expert wants to prove to the court by weighing on cup scales without grime that all the counterfeit coins he discovered are indeed counterfeit, and the rest are real. Will he be able to do it? (8th, 9th grade)

R. V. Freivald

## Solution

At the trial, 14 coins were presented as evidence. The expert discovered that seven of them were file ones, the rest were abbots, and he found out which ones were file ones and which ones were abbots. The court only knows that file coins weigh the same, the original coins weigh the same and the file lese coins weigh the same. The expert wants the time of weighing on cup scales without digital judgment, that all the file coins he discovered are indeed file coins, and the rest are originals. Can he do it?

Figures 4, a, b, c show by what weightings an expert can convince the court that coins $\Phi_1, \Phi_2, \dots, \Phi_7$ are file coins, and $H_1, H_2, \dots, H_7$ are real coins. Each time the right cup moves, and this is only possible if there are more file coins on the left cup than on the right (and there are more infusions on the right than on the left).

This method is easy to generalize, and then the fact that the data $n$ coins are file ones, and the others $n$ are real, can be proven by performing $[\log_2 n] + 1$ weighing. Despite the fact that such a weighing system is very economical (at $n = 1000$ 10 weighings are enough), we cannot prove that it is optimal. It would be interesting to prove that the minimum weighting number still grows without limit as $n \rightarrow \infty$ (or to disprove this).

**A. L. Toom**
