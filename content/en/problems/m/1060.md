## Condition

There are two closed broken lines on the plane, each with an odd number of links. All straight lines containing links of these broken lines are different, and no three of them intersect at one point. Prove that from each broken line one can select one link so that they are opposite sides of some convex quadrilateral.

A. Serdyukov, D. G. Flaas

## Solution

There are two closed broken lines on the plane, each with an odd number of zenes. All straight lines containing the zenev of these broken lines are different, and no three of them intersect at one point. Prove that one zen can be selected from each broken line so that they are opposite sides of a certain convex quadrilateral.

If one of the links of the first broken line is parallel to a certain zen of the second, then the statement of the problem is obvious (these two zenes are the bases of the trapezoid). Therefore, we will further assume that no two zenins of different broken lines are parallel.

The line containing the zeno will be called the carrier line of this zeno. Since both broken lines consist of an odd number of zenevs, the number $N$ of intersection points of the carrier lines of one broken line with the non-support lines of the other is odd.

Let us assume that the statement of the problem is false. Then, no matter how you choose a zen on each broken line, the point of intersection of their non-existing straight lines will lie on one of these two zens. Therefore, the number $N$ can be represented as (the number of intersections of the first broken line with the non-existent lines of the second) + (the number of intersections of the second broken line with the non-existent lines of the first) - (the number of intersections of the broken lines with each other),
since the last number is counted twice in the sum of the first ones. Let us show that each of these numbers, and therefore the number $N$, is even, in contradiction with what was said above. Thus, the problem will be solved.

We will bend one broken line in a certain direction until it stops intersecting with the other, and watch how the number of intersection points changes. It is clear that it can change only at the moment when some vertex $A$ of one of the broken lines falls on a certain zeno $l$ of the other. For example, when you can choose so that the vertex $A$ cannot coincide with the vertex or self-intersection point of another broken line. Then it is enough to consider two cases: when two zenens emerging from the vertex $A$ lie on opposite sides of $l$ (Fig. 1) and on the same side (Fig. 2). In the first case, the number of intersections does not change, in the second it increases or decreases by 2. In any case, the parity of it is preserved, and since there will be no intersection at the end, the number of intersection points of two closed broken lines is even. The parity of the intersection number of a closed loop is proved in a similar way.
