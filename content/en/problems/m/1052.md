## Condition

Prove that of the $n$ quadrilaterals cut off from a convex $n$-gon by diagonals, at most $n/2$ can be circumscribed. Give an example of an octagon that has 4 such quadrilaterals.

N. M. Sedrakyan

## Solution

Prove that out of n quadrilaterals cut off from a convex n-gon by diagonals, no more than n/2 can be circumscribed circles. Give an example of an octagon that has 4 such quadrilaterals.

If it were possible to count more than n/2 of the described quadrilaterals, then among them there would be two neighboring ones - having two common sides. Let's denote them ABCD and BCDE (Fig. 1). For each of them, the sums of opposite sides are equal:
$$AB + CD = BC + AD, \quad BC + DE = CD + BE.$$
From here we get that
$$AB + DE = AD + BE.$$ (*)

The original n-gon is convex, so its diagonals AD and BE intersect at some point P. By

⟦folio 25⟧

triangle inequality

$$AD + BE = AP + BP + PD + PE > AB + DE,$$

which contradicts (*).

To construct the required octagon, we describe an equilateral trapezoid $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ around a circle with angles of 45° at the base $A_1A_4$, and then build it up to an octagon $A_1A_2...A_8$ as shown in figure 2. Analogously, an n-gon is constructed, from which we can count the diagonals [n/2] of the described quadrangles.
