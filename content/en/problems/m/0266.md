## Condition

Given a convex $n$-gon.
a) Prove that if for each three successive vertices of a $n$-gon one constructs a circle passing through these three vertices, and from the $n$ obtained circles selects the one with the largest radius, then this circle contains within itself the entire given $n$-gon;
6) Prove that if for each three consecutive sides of a $n$-gon one constructs a circle tangent to these sides, and from the $n$ obtained circles choose the one with the largest radius, then it will contain inside the given $n$-gon.

A. B. Karzanov

## Solution

** *Given a convex n-gon.*

*a) Prove that if for each three successive vertices of an n-gon we construct a circle passing through these vertices, and from the n obtained circles choose the one with the largest radius, then this circle contains the entire given n-gon within itself;*

*b) Prove that if for each three consecutive sides of an n-gon we construct a circle tangent to these sides, and from the n obtained circles choose the one with the smallest radius, then it will be contained inside the given n-gon.*

a) Let $s$ be one of the circles with the largest radius. Let us denote its center by $S$, radius by $r$, and the vertices of the polygon lying on $s$ by $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$. Suppose that the polygon $M$ is not entirely contained within $s$; then there is a vertex - let's denote it by $A_k$ - lying outside $s$; Let's draw a straight line through the points $S$ and $A_2$. Let $A_k$ be in the right half-plane (see Fig. 14). Consider the vertex $A_l$, lying outside $s$, whose number $3 < l \leqslant k$ and number $l$ is the minimum possible. Check for yourself that the radius $R$ of the circle $s_1$ circumscribed about the triangle $A_2A_3A_l$ is greater than $r$, and that the vertices $A_2$, $A_3$, ..., $A_l$ lie in $s_1$ (see Fig. 15). Let's now try to come to a contradiction - construct a circle passing through three *neighboring* vertices of the polygon, the radius of which is greater than $r$. If $A_3$ and $A_l$ are neighboring vertices, then the contradiction has already been obtained. Let $A_3$ and $A_l$ be non-neighboring. Consider all the circles passing through the points $A_3$, $A_l$ and $A_i$, where $3 < i < l$ are the radii of these circles not less than $R$. Let the one with the smallest radius pass through the vertex $A_{l_1}$, where $3 < l_1 < l$. If $A_3$, $A_{l_1}$ and $A_l$ are a triple of successive vertices, then a contradiction is obtained. If not, then we will move from the chain of vertices $A_3$, ..., $A_l$ to a shorter chain: $A_3$, ..., $A_{l_1}$ or $A_{l_1}$, ..., $A_l$. Having repeated this transition several times, we will come to three neighboring peaks. Since the radii of the circles did not decrease during each transition, the radius of the circle described around them is not less than $R$, that is, more than $r$, the promised contradiction has been obtained.

b) Let $s$ be one of the circles of minimum radius touching the sides $a_1$, $a_2$ and $a_3$. Let's designate its center

Prove yourself that the radius $r$ of the circle tangent to sides $a_2$, $a_3$ and $a_l$ is less than $R$, and that sides $a_2$, $a_3$, ..., $a_l$ lie outside this circle (see Fig. 17). Let us now arrive at a contradiction in the same way as in point a). If $l = 4$, then the contradiction occurs. If $l > 4$, then choose from the circles tangent to the sides $a_3$, $a_l$ and $a_i$, where $3 < i < l$ is the circle with the maximum radius. Its radius is no more than $r$; let it correspond to the triple $a_3$, $a_{l_1}$, $a_l$. If $l_1 = 4$, $l = 5$, then a contradiction is obtained. Otherwise, from the pairs $a_3$, $a_{l_1}$ and $a_{l_1}a_l$ we choose the non-neighboring one and apply to it the same construction as for the pair $a_3$, $a_l$. In a finite number of steps we will arrive at three neighboring sides, and the radius of the circle touching them will be no more than $r$. Contradiction received.
