## Condition

On the circle there are $n$ points, the numbers 1, 2, ..., $n$ measured in a row. The two play the next game. Everyone takes turns drawing a chord connecting two points with the numbers of one quarter. Any chord should not have common points (further ends) with those drawn earlier. The one who makes the last move wins. For each $n=4, 5, 6, ...$, find out which player has a winning strategy: the starter or his partner.

V. G. Chevaov

## Solution

On the circle there are $n$ points numbered consecutively with the numbers 1, 2, ..., $n$. Two scratch into the next cerra. Everyone takes turns drawing a chord connecting the points with the numbers of one quarter. Each chord should not have common points (further ends) with those drawn earlier. The one who makes the last chord wins. Find out which of the players - the beginner or his partner - has a winning strategy (for every $n = 4, 5, 6, 7, \dots$).

Answer: when $n$ is a multiple of 4, as well as when $n$ is odd, the first (starting) player wins, and when $n = 4k + 2$ ($k$ integer) the second one wins.

It is convenient to assume that the even-numbered and odd-numbered points are represented by different colors—say, white and black—and that the points divide the circle into equal parts.

In all cases, the main idea of ​​a winning strategy is to use symmetry.

The strategy of the 1st player at $n = 4k$ is to check the diameter with a move, and then respond to each move of the 2nd by drawing a chord symmetric relative to this diameter - see Fig. 1. Let $n$ be odd (then there are two adjacent black points on the circle, numbered 1 and $n$). With $n=4k+1$, the 1st player will ensure a win if on the first move he connects, for example, the black dots with numbers 1 and 3 (Fig. 2), thereby reducing to a similar game with $n=4(k-1)+2$, where the beginner loses. The 1st player has a similar, but even more cunning move at $n=4k+3$. Here he can cut off three points so that they maintain symmetry around the axis - to do this, it is enough to connect the black points with numbers $2k+1$ and $2k+3$ (Fig. 3).

If we make sure to arrange the remaining $4k$ points so that they are located at equal distances around the circle, then the diametrically opposite points will be of different colors (although the white and black points do not alternate), and therefore the beginner here (i.e., the 2nd player) loses.

More subtle messages are required in the version of the game in which the player who makes the last move loses. We leave the analysis of this option to the readers.
