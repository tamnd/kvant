## Condition

If the difference between the cubes of two consecutive natural numbers is the square of some natural number $n$, then the number $n$ is represented as the sum of the squares of two consecutive natural numbers.

a) Prove this statement.

6) Bot is one example of such numbers: $8^3-7^3=(2^2+3^2)^2$; Give at least one more example.

b) Prove that there are infinitely many such examples.

P. Lyness (UK)

## Solution

If the difference between the cubes of two consecutive natural numbers is the square of some natural number $n$, then the number $n$ is represented as the sum of the squares of two consecutive natural numbers.**

a) Prove this statement.
b) Here is one example of such numbers: $8^3 - 7^3 = (2^2 + 3^2)^2$; Give at least one more example.
b) Prove that there are infinitely many of them.

a) Let $(m+1)^3 - m^3 = n^2$. Opening the parentheses and multiplying both sides of the equality by 4, we obtain that $3 \cdot 4^2 + 3 \cdot 4m + 4 = 4n^2$ or $3(2m+1)^2 = (2n+1)(2n-1)$. (1)

Since the numbers $2n+1$ and $2n-1$ are coprime, equality (1) is possible only when one of these numbers is a natural square and the other is a triple square. But the number $n$ is odd ($n^2$ is the difference between numbers of different parities), so $2n+1$ gives remainder 3 when divided by 4, and the square of an integer is either divisible by 4 or leaves remainder 1 when divided by 4; therefore, the perfect square is $2n-1$; that is, $2n-1 = (2l+1)^2$ or $n = 2l^2 + 2l + 1 = l^2 + (l+1)^2$.

6), c) Having put $2n=a$, $2m+1=b$ in equality (1), we rewrite it in the form $a^2 - 3b^2 = 1$.

Obviously, each solution $(a, b)$ of this equation with an odd $b$ (and, therefore, an even $a$) is associated with a pair $(m, n)$ that satisfies the conditions of the problem. Let us show how using one solution $(a, b)$ one can construct an infinite sequence of solutions. We will use the general method of constructing solutions to the so-called equation $\Pi_1 x^2 - dy^2 = 1$, which was discussed in detail in the article by N. B. Vasiliev and V. L. Gutenmacher “Pairs of numbers and actions with them” (Kvant, 1985, No. 1, p. 20).

Let $(a_1, b_1)$ be some solution to (2). Let us define the numbers $a_k$ and $b_k$ by the equality $(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{3})^k = a_k + b_k\sqrt{3}$. It is clear that then $(a_1 - b_1\sqrt{3})^k = a_k - b_k\sqrt{3}$, therefore $a_k^2 - 3b_k^2 = (a_k + b_k\sqrt{3})(a_k - b_k\sqrt{3}) = ((a_1 + b_1\sqrt{3})(a_1 - b_1\sqrt{3}))^k = (a_1^2 - 3b_1^2)^k = 1$.

Consequently, the pairs $(a_k, b_k)$ for all $k$ also satisfy equation (2). Moreover, if $b_1$ is odd $(a a_1$ is even), then if $k$ is odd, the number $b_k$ is represented as $a_1 \cdot A + b_1^k (k-1)/2$ and is also odd.

Thus, starting with, say, $(a_1, b_1) = (2, 1)$ and sequentially calculating the odd powers
