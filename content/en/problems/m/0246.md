## Condition

On a plane, two lines m and n and a point O are given. Construct a triangle whose two altitudes lie on the given lines m and n, and the center of the circumscribed circle is at point O.

Yu. A. Gryaznov

## Solution

On the plane there are two straight lines $m$ and $n$ and a point $O$. Construction of a triangle whose two altitudes lie on the given lines $m$ and $n$, and the center of the circumscribed circle is at point $O$.

In order not to consider various cases of relative positions of straight lines $m$, $n$ and points $O$, we will solve the problem without a drawing.

Let us first assume that the point $O$ does not coincide with the intersection point of the lines $m$ and $n$. Let's note the following:

1) If through the point $O$ we draw lines $m'$ and $n'$, parallel lines $m$ and $n$, respectively, then we should obtain perpendiculars passing through the midpoints of the side of the desired triangle (this immediately follows from the fact that $O$ is the center of the circumscribed circle).

2) The straight line $m''$, symmetrical to the straight line $n'$, must not pass through the vertex of the desired triangle (say, through the point $C$). Similarly, line $n''$, symmetrical to line $n$ relative to line $m'$, must also not pass through point $C$. (Both of these statements follow from the first remark.)

3) If the lines $m''$ and $n''$ intersect (and they may not intersect; that’s why we wrote the word up to false on a) everywhere in 1) and 2), then the point of their intersection is the vertex $C$ of the desired triangle.

Once vertex $C$ is found, vertices $A$ and $B$ are easy to find: we know the directions of sides $CA$ and $CB$ and that they intersect with lines $m$ and $n$ at points $A$ and $B$, respectively.

So, we have described the construction process. However, it remains unclear whether this construction always leads to a goal; in other words, is it possible to arbitrarily define the lines $m$, $n$ and the point $O$? From our construction it follows that this is not so: if the lines $m''$ and $n''$ are parallel, then the vertex $C$ cannot be found. Check that lines $m''$ will be parallel to $n''$ if and only if either the acute angle between lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$, or when $m \parallel n$.

If $m \parallel n$, then there are no solutions. If the angle between lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$, then lines $m''$ and $n''$ can coincide, and in this case a whole family of solutions is obtained. This will happen only when the parallel to the frame formed by the straight lines $m$, $n$, $m'$ and $n'',$ is a rhombus with an angle $60^\circ$ at the vertex $O$.

Finally, if the point $O$ coincides with the intersection point of the lines $m$ and $n$, then the triangle either degenerates into a point, or the solution is a whole family of regular triangles, in which case the angle between the lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$.

Let us note in conclusion that if we slightly change the formulation of the problem—assuming that the lines $m$ and $n$ contain not the altitudes, but the bisectors of the triangle—then we get a problem that is, in some senses, dual to the solution. (However, it can be easily solved without using this duality.) Try to solve this problem and understand what the duality consists of.
