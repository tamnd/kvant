## Condition

What is considered “beautiful” is the division of a triangle into similar triangles, no two of which are equal in size.

a) Prove that for every right triangle there is a red partition.

6) * Is it possible to arrange a red division of an equilateral triangle?

c) For which equilateral triangles is there a red partition?

A. V. Savkin

## Solution

What is considered “beautiful” is the division of a triangle into similar triangles, no two of which are equal in size.
a) Prove that for every right triangle there is a red partition.
b) For which equilateral triangles is there a red partition?

a) A beautiful division of an unequal right triangle (this is the simplest case) is shown at figure 1, $a$, an equilateral one - at figure 1, ​​$6$.

6) Answer: no. We will carry out the proof by contradiction. Let us assume that a red partition of the equilateral triangle $\Delta$ exists, and estimate the number $N$ of “internal” sides of its constituent triangles, that is, the sides lying inside the triangle $\Delta$. The ends of these sides (vertices of the partition triangles) were of three types:
1st type - points lying inside the triangle (example - point $A$ on figure 2, $a$); from each such point there are 4 internal sides ($AB, AC, AD$ and $AE$ on figure);
2nd type - points inside the triangle $\Delta$, in which there are 6 partition triangles; 12 internal sides emerge from such a point (Fig. 2.6);
3rd type - points lying inside any side of the partition triangle (Fig. 2.8); such a point serves as the end for 6 internal sides.

Let us denote the number of points of each type by $n_1, n_2, n_3$, respectively. Since each side has two ends, the number of internal sides is equal to

$$N = \frac{1}{2} (4n_1 + 12n_2 + 6n_3) = 2n_1 + 6n_2 + 3n_3.$$

At the same time, each point $A$ of the 3rd type can be associated with three internal sides: the side $s$, the internal point is the point $A$, and two sides adjacent to $s$, emerging from the vertex $A$ (at Fig. 2,8 point $A$ the sides $s=BC, AD$ and $AE$). In this case, each inner side will be

It is convenient to solve, as it were, the inverse problem: an additional triangle $ABC$ to a large similar triangle with other similar and pairwise different triangles. Obviously, this problem is equivalent to the original one. Let for definition $|BC|:|AC| = k > 1$. Moreover, to triangle $\Delta_0 = ABC$ there are similar triangles $\Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3, \Delta_4$ and $\Delta_5$ in accordance with figure 3. It is easy to see that in this case the similarity coefficient of triangles $\Delta_{n+1}$ and $\Delta_n$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3$ is equal to $k$, therefore the similarity coefficient of triangles $\Delta_0$ and $\Delta_n$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$ is equal to $k^n$, and $\Delta_5$ and $\Delta_0$ are $|CM|:|AB| = (|CK| + |CM|):|AB| = k + k^3$. From here it is clear that the only pair of congruent triangles here can only be $\Delta_5$ and $\Delta_4$ - if $k + k^3 = k^4$. In the latter case, the complement of congruent triangles is $\Delta_6, \Delta_7$, and $\Delta_5$ is replaced by $\Delta_8 = CNL$ (see Fig. 3). Again, the only pair of congruent triangles among $\Delta_0, \Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3, \Delta_4, \Delta_6, \Delta_7$ and $\Delta_8$ can only be $\Delta_8$ (similar to $\Delta_0$ with a coefficient of $|CN|:|AB| = k + k^3 + k^5$) and $\Delta_7$ (similar to $\Delta_0$ with a coefficient of $k^6$). But if $k + k^3 = k^4$, then
