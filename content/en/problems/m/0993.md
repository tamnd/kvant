## Solution

a) Find 11 consecutive natural numbers whose sum of squares is the square of the natural number.
b)* Prove that for $2 < n < 11$ there are no n consecutive natural numbers whose sum of squares is a square.

Let $A$ be an arbitrary graduate. It is enough to show that there is a graduate $B$ who is friends with two friends $A$ at the same time (then $A$ can invite $B$ and these two friends).

If such a graduate $B$ is among friends $A$, everything is in order. Otherwise, each of the $m(m \geqslant 10)$ friends $A$ is friends with at least 8 graduates (not counting $A$), who themselves are not friends with $A$. If all these graduates were different, then it would turn out that there are no less than $8m \geqslant 80$ graduates different from $A$ and his friends; but the number of such graduates does not exceed $90 - m - 1 \leqslant 79$. This means that some two friends $A$ have a common friend $B$, which is what we needed to prove.

$$nx^2 + n(n + 1)x + \frac{n(n + 1)(2n + 1)}{6} = y^2$$

has no solutions in integers $x$ and $y$, $x \geqslant 0$, $y \geqslant 1$, for $3 \leqslant n \leqslant 10$. (The well-known proof of $1^2 + 2^2 + \dots + n^2 = n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6$ is used here, the proof of which we leave to the reader; however, it is sufficient to verify it by calculating for $n \leqslant 10$.)

If equality (1) is satisfied, then the remainder of division of $y^2$ by $n$ is equal to the remainder of division of $a = n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6$ by $n$. But for $n = 3, 4, 9$, when the corresponding remainders are 2, 2 and 6 ($a = 14, 30$ and 15·19), this is impossible for any $y$ (see table in the margin).

With $n = 5$ and $n = 7$, the number $a$ is divisible by $n$, therefore the number $y^2$, and therefore $y$, is divisible by $n$ ($n$ is prime). By fielding $y = nz$, $t = x + (n + 1)/2$, we can rewrite our equation as $t^2 + (n^2 - 1)/12 = z^2$ or
$$z^2 - t^2 = \frac{n^2 - 1}{12}.$$
No, this equation has no solutions for the admissible values ​​of $n$, $t$ and $z$, since
$$z^2 - t^2 \geqslant z + t \geqslant 2t \geqslant 6,$$
$$\frac{n^2 - 1}{12} \leqslant \frac{7^2 - 1}{12} = 4.$$
For $n = 6$, 8, 10, equation (1) can be conveniently rewritten in the form

Hence, at $n=6$ we obtain that $x^2+y^2$ must be divisible by $n+1=7$. The table in the margin shows that this is possible only when $x$ and $y$ are simultaneously divisible by 7. In exactly the same way it is proved that for $n=10$ our equation has no solutions. Finally, at $n=8$ on the left side of (2) there is a number that, when divided by 9, gives a remainder of 6 and, as can be seen from our table, cannot be represented in the form $x^2+y^2$.
