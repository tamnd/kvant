## Condition

A triangle whose sides are all larger than 1 cm is called “large”. Given a regular triangle ABC with a side of 5 cm. Prove that a) 1000 “large” triangles can be cut from triangle ABC; b) triangle ABC can be cut into 1000 “large” triangles; b)* triangle ABC can be triangulated into 1000 “large” triangles, that is, it is divided so that any two triangles either have no common points, or have only a common vertex, or have a common side; d) do steps 6) and b) for a regular triangle with a side of 3 cm. (8-9 cells)

S. Fomin

## Solution

A triangle whose sides are all larger than 1 cm is called “large”. Given a regular triangle ABC with a side of 5 cm. Prove that: a) 1000 “large” triangles can be cut from triangle ABC; b) triangle ABC can be cut into 1000 “large” triangles; c) triangle ABC can be cut so that any two triangles either do not have common lobes, or have a common vertex, or have common sides; e) do points b) and c) for a regular triangle with a side of 3 cm.

We will solve points b) and c) of the problem for a triangle with a side of 3 cm (Fig. 1). Let K and L be the points dividing sides AC into three equal parts, P the midpoint of side BC. Let us restore from points K and L the perediculars KN and LM to side AC; let $N_1 = [AP] \cap [KN)$, $M_1 = [CN_1] \cap [LM)$, $N_2 = [AM_1] \cap [KN)$, $M_2 = [CN_2] \cap [LM)$, etc. We obtain a partition of the triangle into triangles ABP, CPN$_1$, AN$_1$M$_1$, CM$_1$N$_2$, AN$_2$M$_2$, etc. All these triangles are the “large” sides of each of them are more than 1 cm (|BP| = |CP| = 1.5 cm, and the remaining sides are more than 1 cm, since their projections on AC are not less than 1 cm). It is clear from the solution that together with the number 1000 one could write any other number, however large.

*) The solution to problem M398 will be published in the next issue of the journal; Problem M399 was solved in the article by A. Savin “From a school problem to a problem” (see «Kvant», 1976, No. 12).

The construction of triangulation of triangle $ABC$ into “large” triangles (point c) of the problem is based on the same idea.

Let points $K, L$ and $P$ be the same as before, $KN$ and $LM$ be perpendiculars to $AC$, point $N_1 = [AP] \cap [KN]$.

Take a point $N_2$ on the segment $KN_1$ and connect it with points $A, P$ and $C$ (Fig. 2). Let us denote by $M_1$ the intersection point of $[LM]$ with $[CN_2]$: $M_1 = [CN_2] \cap [LM]$. On the segment $LM_1$, take the point $M_2$ and connect it with the points $A, N_2$ and $C$. Let $M_3$ denote the point of intersection of $[KN]$ with $[AM_2]$; on the segment $KM_3$, take the point $M_4$, connect it with the points $A, M_2$ and $C$, etc.

Continuing this process, we obtain (for the same reason as above) the eliminated triangulation of triangle $ABC$ into “large” triangles $ABP, APN_2, CN_2P, AN_2M_2, CN_2M_2$, ... (the number of triangles can be made as desired).
