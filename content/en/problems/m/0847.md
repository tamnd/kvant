## Condition

The square is divided into $n \times n$ cells. Two players take turns tracing one side of the same square (you cannot trace the same side twice). Who will win if played correctly if
a) the player who first builds a closed line wins;
b) the player who is forced to build a closed line first loses.

I. Vetrov, A. Kodan

## Solution

The square is divided into n×n cells. Two cerroks take turns circling one side of the same cell (you cannot circle the same side twice). Who gives out at the right ceres, if
a) the first cerrok to build a closed line wins;
b) the cerrok passes, who is forced to be the first to build a closed line.

a) Answer: if the game is played correctly, the cerrok who goes second always wins. To do this, he must make moves symmetrical to the moves of the first (beginner) relative to one of the diagonals of the square, until the first one builds an “almost-cycle,” that is, a position in which it is enough to circle one side to get a cycle (closed broken line). By circling this side, the second cerrok will win.

We need to prove that with such a strategy the first cerrok. Let us assume that, on the contrary, after he has circled some side a, an “almost-cycle” first appears, and before the cycle he will not reach one side b; let x₁, ..., xₙ be the remaining sides of this cycle. These sides had already been circled before the last move (a) of the second cerok, therefore the sides x'₁, ..., x'ₙ, symmetrical to it relative to the diagonal, as well as sides a', symmetrical to a, were circled before this move. And since x₁, ..., xₙ and a form an “almost-cycle”, x'₁, ..., x'ₙ and a' also form an “almost-cycle” (which will not reach side b' before the cycle). Consequently, the “almost cycle” arose as the last move of the second cerok. Contradiction.

6) Answer: if n is even, the second cerrok wins, if n is odd, the first one wins; more precisely, the first cycle rotates in exactly (n+1)² moves (assuming, of course, that the cerroks move correctly, that is, they do not close the cycle as long as this is possible).

Indeed, consider a position in which there are no cycles yet, but any new move creates a cycle. We will prove the following two statements:

1) any two nodes of the “playing field” in this position can be connected by a whole of the circled sides; in other words, a (branched) polyline, made up of all the circled sides, passes through all the nodes and is connected (from any of its vertices you can go along it to any other vertex);

2) any connected broken line without cycles with k vertices has k—1 links.

From these statements it follows that in the position under consideration there are (n+1)²—1 sides circled, which is what we need.

To prove the first statement, take any two nodes A and B and any connecting circled side XY (see figure). If you circle it, a cycle will appear. Obviously, it must contain the XY node, so the X and Y nodes should already contain a "bridge" of outlined sides. By replacing each uncircled zeno of the cerule with such a “bridge”, we get a broken line from the circled sides passing through A and B.

Let's move on to the second statement. If the broken line does not contain cycles, then it very well has at least one vertex X, from which only one zeno XY emerges. Due to connectivity, at least one more Zeno leaves point Y. Let's remove XY from the broken line; One vertex, X, is removed along with it, and the broken line remains connected.
