It is inconceivable to even imagine how all this could have been created in half a century by one person who was almost blind for the last 17 years (it is estimated that just rewriting Euler's works with eight hours of work every day would take 50 years). It seems to me that if we collect everything published by the greatest creators of our science, starting with Archimedes and ending with Poincaré (excluding, however, Cauchy), we will get a collection of works only comparable in scale to what was done by Euler alone. The development of mathematical science began in the seventeenth century, the century of true giants. It is enough to name the names of Galileo, Kepler, Descartes, Fermat, Newton, Leibniz and Huygens. The eighteenth century is the century of two titans: Euler and Lagrange. In this century, mathematics was more unified than ever and developed in close ties with natural science. In 1727, Euler received the prize of the Paris Academy of Sciences. This academy annually announced competitions for works in applied natural science. That year it was necessary to find the best way to position the mast on the ship. The great shipbuilder Alexei Nikolaevich Krylov, a master of bright and memorable words, bowing to the genius of Euler, expressed his admiration for this engineering work of Euler (it was published in 1728): “In mountainous Switzerland, from which Euler had not left until that time, he, of course, had the opportunity to see the ship only in pictures” (but he coped with the task). Euler did not receive the first prize at that time, which went to Pierre Bougues, whose name was preserved in history as the name of the “father of naval architecture.” Subsequently, Euler took part in competitions of the Paris Academy of Sciences 12 times and invariably took first prizes. In 1725, the Bernoulli brothers - Nikolai and Daniil - arrived in St. Petersburg and took positions: Nikolai - professor of physics, and Daniil - professor of mathematics. Euler wrote to Daniil that he had a desire to also go to the Russian capital. Some time after his arrival, Daniil informed Euler that a vacancy had opened for an adjunct in physiology. Euler agrees to come. Johann Bernoulli, in connection with the departure of three young people to Russia, wrote: “It is better to suffer somewhat from the harsh climate in a land of ice, where the muses are welcomed, than to die of hunger in a country with a temperate climate, in which the muses are despised and offended.” Euler arrived in Russia on May 17, 1727 and stayed there until 1741, when he moved to Germany, returning again in 1766. It would be nice if one of the historians compiled a register of what Euler did for the benefit of Russia. He almost single-handedly processes Russian census data; writes textbooks for high schools; organizes Russian cartography (he wrote that through his works Russian geography was brought into

This list is endless, and it is not so easy to figure out with the activities of which of our scientists in the entire history of Russian science can be compared with the gigantic work that Euler did for the benefit of our country. Euler never refused any work or any assignments. He initially filled a medical position and performed his duties conscientiously. On behalf of the Academy of Sciences, Euler wrote the “Manual to Arithmetic” (1738), which played an outstanding role in mathematical education in our country, as well as the two-volume monograph “Marine Science”, published in St. Petersburg in 1749. As for the instructions, it is difficult to resist quoting A.S. Pushkin, who wrote that in 1740, “when Ivan Antonovich was born, Empress Anna Ioannovna sent an order to Euler to draw up a horoscope for the newborn. Euler initially refused, but then was forced to obey. He took up horoscopes with another academician. They compiled it according to all the rules of astrology, like conscientious Germans, although they did not believe it. The conclusion they drew frightened both mathematicians - and they sent the empress another horoscope, in which they predicted all sorts of prosperity for the newborn. Euler, however, kept the first one and showed it to K.G. Razumovsky when the fate of the unfortunate Ivan Antonovich was accomplished.” Euler started his family in St. Petersburg. In 1733 he married Ekaterina Gzel. She was the daughter of a painter originally from Switzerland. Catherine bore Euler thirteen children, but only five—three sons and two daughters—survived their early years. Euler loved children very much, played with them, taught them. And he worked without interruption. Contemporaries recall how he wrote his works holding a child on his lap while other children frolicked around him. The descendants of Euler's children played a prominent role in Russian culture in the 19th century. In 1733, Daniel Bernoulli returned to Basel, and Euler took the position of leading mathematician at the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences. In 1735, Euler began to have health problems: he almost died from a fever and began to go blind.
