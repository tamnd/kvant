Accordingly, at thermal equilibrium, absorption of photons with energy $h \nu = E_{2} - E_{1}$ will prevail over stimulated emission. Note that in addition to atoms, these processes can also occur in molecules, ions, and semiconductors. In order to obtain gain due to stimulated emission, it is necessary that the population of the upper level exceeds the population of the lower one. It is required to remove the system from thermal equilibrium through some external influence so that the population of the upper level is higher than the population of the lower one. This state is called population inversion, and the substance in which it is obtained is called the active medium. For the first time, population inversion in ammonia molecules and the generation of electromagnetic waves using stimulated emission were obtained in the radio frequency range by C. Townes (USA), N.G. Basov and A.M. Prokhorov (USSR). The device they created was called a maser, or molecular generator. Soon after this, T. Maiman (USA) created a device operating in the visible range - a laser. Let us consider, using the example of the first laser using a ruby ​​crystal, how population inversion can be achieved. At the same time, we note that in real substances energy levels usually have a certain width, i.e. they correspond to a certain spread of energy. Accordingly, transitions between such wide levels occur not at a fixed frequency $\nu$, but with a certain frequency spread $\Delta \nu$, i.e. in the form of spectral lines. Moreover, many expanded levels can merge to form wide zones of energy. This manifests itself in wide bands of emission and absorption spectra. Ruby crystal is crystalline aluminum oxide $( Al_{2} O_{3}$ corundum) with a small admixture of chromium ions (no more than 5%), which give ruby ​​its pink color. Spectroscopic studies have shown that these ions have a system of energy states, a simplified diagram of which is shown in figure 2. Ruby absorbs light in a very wide band of the blue-green region of the spectrum and emits light (luminescence) in the form of a rather narrow spectral line with a maximum at a wavelength of 694.3 nm. The ratio of the energy of absorbed light to luminescence light turned out to be very high - almost every photon of absorbed light turns into a luminescence photon. This process occurs as follows (see Fig.2). After absorption of light, a transition occurs from the ground state 1 to a wide range of excitation energies 2, from which this energy is quickly transferred to a rather narrow level 3. This is the so-called non-radiative transition, since it is not accompanied by the emission of light. Excited chromium ions can remain (live) in state 3 for quite a long time, about 3 ms, before returning to the ground state with the emission of a luminescence photon. This is the so-called metastable transition. Thus, when a ruby ​​crystal is illuminated with blue-green light, the main level 1 is depleted with the accumulation of excited ions at level 3. If the intensity of the exciting light is high enough, then the population of level 3 may be greater than the population of level 1. An inverse population will arise, which is necessary to obtain amplification. This scheme for obtaining population inversion is called a three-level system. Its disadvantage is the need to transfer more than half of the particles from the ground level, which requires high intensity of exciting radiation (the ruby ​​was illuminated using a powerful flash lamp). More effective is the four-level system for creating population inversion, shown in figure 3. Between the main and metastable levels there is an additional level 4,

This, in turn, will lead to a decrease in the power of the exciting light (pump). Modern lasers usually operate using this four-level pumping scheme.
