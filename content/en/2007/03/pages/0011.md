Laser operating principle

The history of the creation of the laser is closely connected with the history of radio. Since the advent of radio wave generators, it has been clear that they differ fundamentally from light sources not only in wavelength. The fact is that the emitted light of conventional light sources - such as the Sun, gas-filled lamps and incandescent lamps - is due to a combination of independent, i.e. processes of emission of light inconsistent with each other by each atom (molecule) of the emitting body. In contrast, radio waves are caused by the uniform, orderly movement of electrons in the form of electrical currents. In other words, the radiation emitted by a radio wave transmitter is highly coherent, which means that the amplitude and phase of the oscillations in the wave are consistent in space and time. The high degree of coherence is due to the fact that to produce radio waves, generators are used that are capable of creating continuous (during the operation of the generator) oscillations in the form of a sine wave. It is obvious that all periods of the sinusoid are consistent with each other. The fundamental feature of a laser is that it is also a generator in which the emission of light by individual atoms (molecules), unlike conventional light sources, occurs in a strictly coordinated manner. Figuratively, the radiation of an ordinary light source can be imagined as a roaring crowd in a stadium, and the radiation of a laser can be imagined as the same crowd, but singing in unison. The electromagnetic wave generator includes an oscillatory system with a source of energy for its excitation, which determines the period and frequency, and positive feedback. It is known that in any oscillatory system, left to its own devices, the oscillations decay due to inevitable losses. To obtain continuous oscillations, it is necessary to compensate for these losses. Positive feedback means that some of the oscillatory energy is output, amplified, and returned to the oscillatory system in the desired phase. If this returned energy is equal to the sum of the energy lost in the oscillatory system and the energy removed outside, then continuous, undamped oscillations will be maintained in the oscillatory system. So, for the generator to work, amplification and positive feedback are required. The development of physics in the twentieth century led to the recognition of the fact that atoms and molecules are systems of vibrations with certain frequencies characteristic of each type of atoms and molecules, and are capable of absorbing or emitting electromagnetic waves with these frequencies. According to Bohr's model, these frequencies correspond to transitions between certain energy states (levels) of the atom with the emission or absorption of a portion (quantum) of radiation, and the frequency and, accordingly, the wavelength are determined by the difference in the energies of these states. For simplicity, two levels with energies $E_{1}$, corresponding to the ground state, and $E_{2}$, corresponding to the excited state (Fig.1), were considered. When interacting with a photon with energy $h \nu = E_{2} - E_{1}$, where h is Planck’s constant, and $\nu$ is the frequency of light, the atom goes from the ground state to the excited state with the absorption of the photon (see Fig. 1, a). From an excited state, an atom can return to the ground state by emitting a photon. Einstein called this process spontaneous emission (see Fig. 1, b), since it occurs on its own with a certain probability. But Einstein suggested that an atom could also go to the ground state under the influence of an external photon. Einstein called this process stimulated emission (see Fig. 1, c). Moreover, the emitted photon is exactly identical to the one that causes it (frequency, direction, polarization). Thus, instead of one photon, two exactly the same ones are obtained. In the case of many atoms, the fundamental possibility of “multiplying” photons arises, i.e. amplification of light interacting with the corresponding system of atoms. This is the key point for the implementation of the laser. However, stimulated emission photons are also absorbed by atoms in the ground state. For the generator to operate, it is necessary that the gain exceeds the losses (absorption and escape of photons beyond the radiated medium). But under normal conditions, absorption exceeds amplification. The reason is that the number of photons emitted as a result of stimulated emission and the number of photons absorbed are determined by the number of atoms located at the corresponding levels, i.e. population levels.
