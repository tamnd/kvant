Traditional solid-state lasers do not require water cooling. Gas lasers. In these lasers, the active medium is produced by an electrical discharge in low-pressure gases. The main feature of these lasers is their narrow laser transition lines, which contributes to extremely high monochromatic laser radiation. It is possible to obtain laser radiation lines only a few hertz wide. Since the frequency of laser radiation is $\nu = 10^{14} - 10^{15}$ Hz, the degree of monochromaticity reaches the value $\Delta \nu \nu$ » $10^{-} 14$. Thanks to this feature, gas lasers are used in precision metrology and control systems, laser gyroscopes and ultra-precise optical clocks. A special type of lasers are ultrashort pulse lasers (USPs). Their description deserves a separate story.

Ultrashort pulse lasers

A remarkable achievement of laser science has been the generation of radiation in the form of pulses of ultrashort duration, close to the fundamental limit - the period of the light wave. In other words, it is possible to concentrate light in a time interval of the order of only a few femtoseconds (1 fs $= 10^{-} 15$ s). It is difficult to imagine the brevity of a femtosecond—one femtosecond is to a second as one second is to 32 million years. Light, the speed of propagation of which is known to be the maximum possible in nature, travels a distance of 0.3 microns in 1 fs (note that the thickness of a human hair is about 40 microns). Why are such short pulses of laser radiation needed? One of the reasons for the desire to reduce the duration of light flashes is their use for studying rapidly occurring phenomena. Such a flash allows you to “freeze” the movement of an object in an interval equal to the duration of the flash, and take “instant” photographs. Under normal conditions, even the fastest objects cannot move noticeably in such short times. But in the microcosm of atoms, molecules and electrons in solids, movements on the scale of micro and nanometers play a significant role. Therefore, to study movements in the microcosm, time resolution in the pico- and femtosecond range is required. Ultrasound lasers provide just such an opportunity. It is not for nothing that the invention of femtosecond pulse lasers has been compared to the invention of the microscope. In particular, the use of such lasers made it possible to directly “observe” the processes of formation and destruction of molecules. These processes occur over tens and hundreds of femtoseconds, and before the advent of ultrashort pulse lasers, information about these processes was obtained only from the results of indirect experiments.
