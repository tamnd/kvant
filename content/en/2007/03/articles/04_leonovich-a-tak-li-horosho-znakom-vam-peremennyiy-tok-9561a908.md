AC

Of course, yes - after all, every house has sockets into which we plug in all our home appliances and lighting fixtures, “powered” by 220 volt alternating current. In school workshops there are machine tools - they are also supplied with alternating current, only at a higher voltage. In all microdistricts there are booths with the inscription “Tr-r”, in which there are transformers that convert alternating current; power lines, again alternating current, stretched along the roads and through forest clearings. Millions and millions of generators, transformers, electric motors all over the world produce, transmit and use electrical energy thanks to the peculiarities of this type of current, discovered almost two hundred years ago. The greatest scientist of the 19th century, Hermann Helmholtz, said that as long as people enjoy the benefits of electricity, they will always remember the name of Faraday with gratitude. The phenomenon of electromagnetic induction, a fundamental scientific discovery made by the English physicist Michael Faraday, formed the basis of modern technical civilization and radically transformed the world around us. For many decades there was an active search for the best implementation of this discovery - up to a desperate struggle between supporters of direct current and supporters of alternating current. True, the “war” that began more than a hundred years ago has long since ended in close and fruitful interaction, when the disadvantages of one type of current are compensated by the advantages of the other. However, one should not think that all the solutions to the physical problems of electric power and telecommunications took place in the past, and now only their technical improvement remains. The scale of energy production and distribution, the transition to alternative sources, and modern means of communication have reached such a milestone that new problems have arisen related to the reliability and safety of gigantic systems covering entire continents. And this requires unconventional ways to manage them, involving non-standard scientific approaches, for example, chaos theory or network theory. But for now, let's try to find out what we know about the simplest properties of alternating current.

This phenomenon, discovered by Arago in 1824 and then explained by Faraday as a consequence of the appearance of induced currents in the disk, was adopted by the electrical engineer Dolivo-Dobrovolsky, who created on its basis an asynchronous three-phase current motor, which produced a genuine technical revolution and is widely used to this day. ...the first practically used transformers were proposed by the “father of Russian light” Yablochkov, who switched from direct to alternating current to ensure uniform combustion of carbon electrodes in his arc “candles”. Yablochkov was the first to use a capacitor in alternating current circuits. ...by the end of the 19th century, Edison won a victory over the supporters of gas lighting, who tried with all their might to prevent the introduction of electric lighting. But soon Edison himself took an irreconcilable position in relation to the use of alternating current, frightening with all sorts of dangers of its use, and even proposed passing a law banning it. ...until 1891, only direct current power plants were built. However, the advantages of alternating current in the production and transmission of electricity led to the fact that already in 1896, a hydroelectric power station with three alternating current turbines of 5 thousand horsepower each came into operation at Niagara Falls in the USA. ...the forces of interaction between currents in nature rarely reach large values, but in technology they play a major role - as, for example, in current generators or electric motors. In contrast to these forces, the more powerful Coulomb forces hardly manifest themselves in technology due to the impossibility of creating sufficiently large electrostatic charges. ...when flowing through wires, alternating current is not distributed evenly over their cross-section, but is concentrated mainly near the surface - this phenomenon is called the skin effect (from the English skin - skin). Thus, at a frequency of 50 hertz in a copper conductor, the current penetration depth is approximately 9 millimeters; As the frequency increases, this depth decreases, which leads to an increase in resistance.
