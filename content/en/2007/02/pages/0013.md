Perception of mental phenomena - emotion scale

Using several examples, we will be convinced that not only physiological, but also mental scales that determine the strength of our emotions are also logarithmic in nature - for our subjective assessments of the impression made on us, we subconsciously choose “steps” in the form of a geometric progression. As a well-known example, let's start with the Landau scale, according to which the famous physicist assessed the merits of his colleagues. Here is how academician V.L. Ginzburg recalls this: “... Landau had a “scale of merit” in the field of physics. The scale was logarithmic (class 2 had achievements 10 times less than those of class 1). Of the physicists of our century, only Einstein had class 0.5; class 1 included Bohr, Dirac, Heisenberg and a number of others...” Other sources talk about the Landau scale a little differently: “Landau assigned “star” numbers to great physicists around the world. You know that a star of the first magnitude is a very bright star, a star of the second magnitude is less bright, etc. Landau assigned half the magnitude to Einstein, Bohr and Newton - 0.5. Dirac, Heisenberg are stars of the first magnitude. He assigned himself the second value.” It remains unclear whether the logarithm based on what base - 10 or 2.512 - was used by Lev Landau to determine the level of genius of theoretical physicists. He used a logarithmic scale for subjective assessments. It has already been noted that in everyday life we ​​also often use the logarithm scale. The examples could go on for a long time. So, we divide rich people into millionaires and billionaires. We divide cities by population into million and hundred thousand people. When buying groceries in a store, we try to save rubles, but when thinking about buying a new refrigerator or TV, we only pay attention to hundreds of rubles. As in the case of physiological scales, in everyday emotional issues we perceive not an absolute, but a relative difference. Moreover, it becomes noticeable and significant for us when it exceeds several percent of the measured value. It seems that the sensitivity of our “emotion meter” is close to the sensitivity of the eye, ear and other physiological receptors. Let's look at examples of several emotional scales that have been proposed in recent years.
