FROM THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE

Jan Hevelius

A.VASILIEV

AMONG THE BRILLIANT FOUNDERS of the European astronomical school, an important place belongs to Jan Hevelius (28.01.1611–28.01.1687), a hereditary brewer and chancellor of the glorious Hanseatic port of Gdansk (later Danzig, and then again Gdansk). While still at the gymnasium, Jan acquired the skills of cartography and designing astronomical instruments, and expanded his horizons in conversations with Pierre Gassendi, Maren Mersenne and Athanasius Kircher, whom he visited during his first European tour. Jan returned from his trip in 1634 to inherit the brewery from his father Abraham Hevelius and marry the owner of two neighboring houses, Katharina Rebeshka. This circumstance - the presence of three houses standing wall to wall, and thus a common roof - played a decisive role in the future fate of the young Polish astronomer. As is known, Galileo’s first telescopes, designed by him at the beginning of the 17th century, were a little more than a meter in length. These telescopes suffered from chromatic aberration, which at that time could only be overcome by increasing the size of the telescope. Chromatic aberration is caused by the dispersion of light passing through the lens, i.e. the dependence of the refractive index of the lens material on the wavelength of light. As a result, white light is decomposed into its constituent colored rays. The refractive index of blue rays is greater than that of red rays, so their focus is closer to the rear principal point of the lens than the focus of red rays. It follows that for a ray of white light there is no single focal length, but rather a set of focal lengths of rays of all colors. Therefore, on the screen located in the image formation area, instead of one bright dot, a collection of colored circles is observed. Hevelius created his first observatory in 1641, using space on the roof. A few years later, he built a telescope about 4 meters long, which magnified distant objects more than 50 times. When designing astronomical instruments, Hevelius relied on a deep understanding of the principles of refraction in lenses. The flatter the lenses, the longer the focal length they have. Long-focus lenses produce clearer images than short-focus lenses, but the lens and eyepiece in such telescopes must be placed at greater distances from each other. Developing in this direction, Hevelius first built a 20-meter telescope, and then his main achievement - a 50-meter telescope. Hevelius's design - the so-called aerial telescope - was an instrument without a tube and without a rigid connection between the lens and the eyepiece. The giant telescope shook at the slightest breath of wind from the Baltic, wooden supports and hemp ropes sagged and strained with changes in temperature and humidity. Under these conditions, adjusting the lens and eyepiece to a single optical axis presented almost insurmountable difficulties. Nevertheless, and this is the sign of a true scientist, Hevelius mastered his instruments to perfection and carried out a whole series of brilliant astronomical observations. In addition, Hevelius was a consummate observer, observing stars of the seventh magnitude with the naked eye. Using a sextant of his own making, Hevelius cataloged the positions of 1,564 stars to the nearest minute of arc. He made these observations without the use of optics, believing that lenses could introduce errors in the measurements. Hevelius's first scientific work was “Selenography, or Description of the Moon,” published in 1647 in Gdansk. It contained a detailed description of the visible surface of the Moon. The work, printed in the author's own printing house, contained 133 engravings depicting 60 sections of the lunar surface and the general appearance of the Moon in various phases. Hevelius proposed names for various objects on the surface of the Moon, some of which have survived to this day, correctly estimated the height of the lunar mountains, and discovered the phenomenon of optical libration (observed periodic pendulum-like oscillations of the Moon relative to its center of mass, as a result of which 0.59 of the entire lunar surface is accessible to direct observations). Subsequently, Hevelius carried out a wide variety of observations, and he was responsible for astronomical discoveries in various fields. He continued to study the issues of lunar motion and estimated the distance from the Earth to the Moon, the period of revolution of the Moon, and the period of the Sun's own rotation. He was engaged in observations of double and variable stars, and determined the orbital periods of the Galilean satellites of Jupiter. Hevelius discovered 4 comets and published the work “Cometography” in 1668, where he outlined the history of observations of all comets known at that time and showed that some comets move in parabolic orbits. It was Hevelius who introduced Europeans to the main work of Ulugbek - the so-called “New Astronomical Tables”. Hevelius was one of the most respected astronomers of his time.
