Having solved the problem, we will understand how quantum mechanics describes the motion of a particle. We will understand, even if we cannot imagine it. In this article, a rigorous formulation of the problem means the ability to write down the Schrödinger equation and establish the necessary boundary and initial conditions. The solution to the problem posed consists not only in finding the $\psi$ -function that satisfies the Schrödinger equation with initial and boundary conditions, but also in calculating the physical quantities that interest us. Only those problems will be solved where the $\psi$ -function describes the motion of one particle under the influence of a force that does not depend on time. However, the specific physical results that are “contained” in the $\psi$ -function of one particle and which can be compared with the results of experiment, relate not to one particle, but to an ensemble, a collective of similar particles in all respects. What does the Schrödinger equation describe: the movement of one particle or many particles? Alone! But in order to take advantage of the results obtained, they need to be translated into the language of macroscopic, classical physics. It is for this that a transition to an ensemble of many particles is necessary. The transition from the microworld to the macroworld does not occur “with impunity”: the description of the causal relationship between events changes. In the macrocosm, ordinary mechanical determinism reigns. In any case, when we are talking about the movement of one particle under the influence of a given force. Knowing the initial conditions, we can trace the movement of the particle at all subsequent moments. Let us clarify: let the coordinate $x_{0}$ and velocity $v_{0}$ of the particle be known at t = 0. Having solved Newton's equation (the force is known), we find the position of the particle at any time, i.e. function x = x(t) equal to $x_{0}$ at t = 0. The derivative dx(t) dt is the velocity of the particle v(t). Naturally, when t = 0, then $v = v_{0}$. It is this statement that bears the pompous name of mechanical determinism. Don't let the word "macroworld" fool you. Various events occur in the macrocosm, the results of which are absolutely impossible to predict with absolute certainty. Sometimes this is due only to incomplete knowledge of the initial conditions. A classic example is throwing a dice. If you knew, absolutely exactly (!), how to throw a die so that it would fall out with the required side up, the problem would have a unique solution. And so we must be content with assessing the likelihood of what will happen. It happens, and quite often, differently: the force that sets the macrobody in motion is precisely known, and it is known how the body will move under the influence of this force. But if a body moves, for example, in the air or slides along an inclined plane, then the body, in addition to a certain force, is also acted upon by forces from air molecules or atoms of the body on which it is sliding. Of course, it is impossible to describe in all details the movement of the body and all particles (air, inclined plane). Usually it is enough to limit ourselves to an averaged description of the influence of the environment using the friction force or atmospheric resistance. Taking into account the force of friction (resistance), it is possible to describe the motion of a macroscopic body quite reliably, but the important features of the motion change significantly. Thus, part of the body’s energy is irreversibly lost due to friction, turning into heat - reversibility disappears. The reversibility of the equations of mechanics is a formal consequence of the fact that they

The friction force is proportional to the speed of the body - the first derivative of the coordinate, which changes with such a replacement. This circumstance is the formal reason for the loss of reversibility. In such a description of the friction forces, the attentive reader will sense inconsistency. Each molecule of air or an inclined plane obeys the same laws as the body itself (now we are not talking about the changes introduced by quantum mechanics). Why does irreversibility appear? This is a very difficult question. The answer to this is based on the phenomenon of dissipation, caused by the transition of energy from a small number of mechanical degrees of freedom of a macroscopic body to an essentially infinite number of degrees of freedom of microscopic particles. The movement of microparticles is so chaotic that the probability of energy returning back to the body is zero. In this regard, they talk about irreversible entanglement, and in more strict language - about the growth of entropy, which serves as a measure of disorder. Let's return to one quantum particle, the movement of which is described by the $\psi$ -function - the solution of the Schrödinger equation. The wave function is a function of coordinates and time. Having specified the initial state, we will find a well-defined solution $\Psi = \Psi ( x, t )$. It would seem to be a completely deterministic process. But, having asked ourselves the simplest question about where the particle is at time t, we are forced to limit ourselves to the statement that we only know the probability of finding the particle in the interval dx around point x, and

2 this probability is equal to $\Psi ( x, t ) dx$. We emphasize: undoubtedly, we are talking about an ensemble of particles, and not about one particle. Without an ensemble of identical particles, the concept of probability loses its meaning. And it’s not a matter of incomplete knowledge or interaction with a large number of other particles. Probability is the primary concept of quantum mechanics. Rigorous consideration has shown that it is impossible to achieve greater accuracy of predictions; the use of probability cannot be avoided when describing the movement of microscopic particles. The wave-particle duality of quantum particles not only makes their motion very different from the motion of classical bodies, but forces us to consider the behavior of an ensemble of particles even when, it would seem, the equation describes the motion of a single particle. This is another psychological difficulty in understanding quantum mechanics.

Trajectory, steady state

The general description of the motion of microscopic particles by quantum mechanics convinces us: quantum laws of motion are not similar to classical ones. But in order for a feeling of miracle to arise, the impression that something is happening that “cannot be”, it is necessary to consider examples. It is possible to clearly distinguish the behavior of quantum microparticles from macroscopic bodies if one clearly imagines the motion of classical macrobodies under conditions that exactly coincide with the conditions under which the motion of quantum particles will be considered. When describing the motion of classical bodies and quantum particles, we will try to use the same terms where possible. In the one-dimensional case it is somehow inconvenient to talk about a trajectory. A classical particle in a one-dimensional world, of course, always moves in a straight line. A comprehensive description of the motion of a particle under the action of an external, time-independent force F = F ( x ) is reduced to determining the dependence of the particle coordinate on time - to finding the function x = x ( t ).
