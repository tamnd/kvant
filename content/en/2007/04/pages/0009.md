Using such a ruler, you can measure the frequency of a narrow laser line $\nu_{лаз}$. Let this frequency fall on the red edge of the comb, and its 2nd harmonic on the violet edge, as shown in figure 21. Then, with the appropriate adjustment of the length of the femtosecond laser cavity, the relation $2 \nu_{лаз} - \nu_{лаз} = \nu_{лаз} = N \delta \nu$ is satisfied, and the measurement of the laser frequency is reduced to counting the number of “teeth” of the comb. This principle, which, as stated earlier, was awarded a Nobel Prize, underlies the creation of ultra-precise optical clocks.

To summarize, we note that with the help of lasers it is possible to study extremely short periods of time and measure the current time with extremely high accuracy. According to modern views, the age of our Universe is about 20 billion years. If the ultra-precise optical clocks that are currently being created were launched at the moment of the “birth” of the Universe, their readings would differ by no more than a few seconds. Conclusion

Work on lasers and their applications is developing rapidly. The most important part of this work is the desire to improve the lasers themselves, making them more efficient and accessible. This, in turn, will contribute to the development of various scientific and technological applications. In the field of basic science, ambitious projects are being considered such as detecting gravitational waves or finding out whether the world's physical constants change over time (the Dirac hypothesis). Curious readers who want to devote themselves to science and technology have a chance to take part in this exciting and promising activity.
