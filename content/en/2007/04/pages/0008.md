But in optics, unlike radio physics and electronics, there are no direct methods for measuring frequency; wavelengths are measured there. It would seem that there is no problem in determining the frequency, knowing the wavelength, since $\nu = c \lambda$, but the speed of light c itself must be measured with appropriate accuracy. The problem of accurately measuring time is precisely related to the desire for a more accurate measurement of c. Femtosecond pulse lasers have provided a remarkable way of matching the frequency of a laser ultra-narrow line to that of a cesium standard. Let us discuss the essence of this method using the example of a laser operating in continuous mode, i.e. emitting a periodic sequence of pulses (Fig. 20,a). As has been shown, this sequence is obtained by adding many waves with equal frequency intervals between them. In other words, the spectrum of such radiation represents a “comb” of equidistant discrete frequencies (Fig.20, b). The width of the entire spectrum of the comb $\Delta \nu$ is determined by the pulse duration: $\Delta \nu_{:} 1 \tau$, and in-

The frequency of this interval lies in the region of fractions and units of gigahertz, and it can be compared with the frequency of the standard second. Thus, the radiation of a continuous femtosecond laser produces a kind of “line” of optical frequencies with “divisions”, the values ​​of which can be accurately determined in relation to the frequency standard.
