In the phrase “impossible” figures, the first word is in quotation marks. Why? It turns out that “impossible” objects in our three-dimensional world are quite possible, but they look completely different from what our stereotypes of perceiving drawings dictate to us. The real object - the prototype of the illusion - may have tears, deformations, but from some point of view it is easy not to notice - and here is a miracle in all its glory! The tearing trick was used by artist Brian McKay and architect Ahmad Abas to create an architectural landmark in Perth, Australia. Aluminum ribbed structure 13.5 meters high (Fig. 8) with a specific

Mathematics professor from the University of Tokyo Kokichi Sugihara, the author of original calculation methods for engineering graphics (www.simplex.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/~sugihara), proposes to translate this idea into paper. To do this, he calculated the “patterns” of three paper bars from which amazing images are glued together (Photoshop, for example, is well suited for these purposes), print them out, and then call on scissors and glue to help. It is recommended to look at the glued model with one eye from a distance 20 times greater than the characteristic dimensions of the model.
