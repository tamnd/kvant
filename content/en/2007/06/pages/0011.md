EXPLOSION

description of the explosion from the Great Soviet Encyclopedia (1971 edition). Its author is K.E. Gubkin, my senior in one course in Physics and Technology (and, accordingly, also one of A.F. Belyaev’s students), whose talent can rightfully be called a “brilliant” scientist - in him the abilities of an outstanding theoretical physicist were combined with understanding and knowledge of experiment. Here is his definition: “An explosion is the process of releasing a large amount of energy in a limited volume in a short period of time. As a result of the explosion, the substance filling the volume in which the energy is released turns into a highly heated gas with very high pressure. This gas exerts great force on the environment, causing it to move. An explosion in a solid medium is accompanied by its destruction and fragmentation.” Both definitions are very similar. They characterize the explosion as an unusually complex process involving a number of chemical and physical phenomena. Therefore, it is impossible to define an explosion briefly - the first definition has 44 words, the second 54. The main phenomena during an explosion are the release of energy, the formation of highly compressed heated gas, the involvement of the environment in the movement (its destruction, if it is solid), the formation of a shock wave in this environment.

The most characteristic thing is the suddenness of the phenomenon

In both definitions of an explosion, the quantitative uncertainty of the characteristics is alarming - “sudden”, “extremely fast”, “intense”, “limited volume”, “large amount of energy”, “short period of time”, “highly heated gas with very high pressure”. In encyclopedias intended for the general public, such uncertainty in assessments may be justified. But this is not allowed in scientific papers. What's the matter? This uncertainty can be justified by the difference in the scale of explosive phenomena and the heterogeneity of their internal processes. For example, the formation of microcracks is explosive in nature. The energy released in this case is small by technical standards, which cannot be said about the consequences that can be the destruction of a structure or the irretrievable loss of a work of plastic art. An example from a completely different area is an explosive, abrupt change in the intensity of the magnetic field in the Earth’s atmosphere, when a portion of plasma ejected by a solar explosion approaches the Earth. In the earth's magnetic field, a shock magnetogasdynamic wave arises (as a result of the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction), in which the intensity of the magnetic field increases several times. The change in the energy density of the earth's magnetic field is small - it is much smaller, for example, than changes in the thermal energy of the earth's atmosphere. We know the consequences. In both of these exotic cases of explosion, dissimilar in their internal situation, there is a common thing - the factor of surprise for a person. Perhaps this should be highlighted in the definition of an explosion as the most

characteristic. In the first case, suddenness is characterized by fractions of a second (the psychological “was” and “wasn’t”). In the second, the time is about a minute, but for the human body this is also a surprise - it does not have time to rebuild and adapt in a timely manner. But in almost all other explosive processes, suddenness is characterized by a rise time of the effect (for example, pressure in a sound wave), much less than the natural human measure - our pulse. The difficulty in understanding the explosion lies in the explanation of this very suddenness - why the release of energy occurs so quickly and why the released energy scatters around so quickly.

Not so much energetic as powerful phenomenon

There is an inaccuracy in the second definition of an explosion. The words “the process of releasing a large amount of energy in a limited volume” are not entirely correct. Explosives are not particularly energetic. The specific energy released during the explosion of TNT is about 4.2 H $10^{6}$ joules, which corresponds to the energy of burning firewood (the worst is aspen) or the energy received by the body when digesting 500 grams of white bread. The combustion energy of kerosene or gasoline (or the energy when the body absorbs good lard) is 10 times greater. Explosives do not have a high specific energy, but rather a high specific power - due to the short time of energy conversion.
