In 1936, in the Urals at the Korkinsky coal field, 1808 tons of explosives were simultaneously detonated - a large trench was formed for open-pit coal mining. In the post-war years, attention increased to the use of powerful explosions in the construction industry - to create dams, in the mining industry - to create underground cavities and open mineral deposits. In 1966–67, using a series of directed explosions of explosives with a total mass of 5,300 tons, a dam was created on the Malaya Almaatinka River in the Medeo tract near Alma-Ata. The dam reliably protected the then capital of Kazakhstan from the danger of mudflows. In 1968, several explosions with a total mass of 2000 tons of explosives collapsed the banks of the Vakhsh River in Tajikistan. A dam 40 meters high was created. Since then, the waters of the artificial reservoir have been directed through tunnels for irrigation and industrial needs in the valley area. When justifying this work and developing the explosion project, the negative experience of the United States was taken into account, where in 1929 a similar idea ended in failure - it was not possible to create a reliable dam. The success of large explosions in the USSR was based on the creation of a theory of the action of an explosion on rocks, which should lead to their directed release. This theory was created after a series of experiments conducted at the Institute of Chemical Physics of the USSR Academy of Sciences in 1955–1960 under the general leadership of Academician M.A. Sadovsky. Experiments were carried out in the laboratory with charges of the order of one gram, and at testing sites - from one to a thousand kilograms. In 1957, near Tashkent, in the same soil conditions, a series of ejection explosions were carried out with a charge mass ranging from one to a thousand tons. At the beginning of the article, I talked about the final, thousand-ton explosion of this series of experiments. The explosion crater (explosion crater) still exists. It is the only lake in this waterless area with a diameter of 200 meters and is successfully used for agricultural needs. But the explosion was carried out, of course, not for this purpose, but with the aim of obtaining experimental data on the scattering of soil during such powerful explosions. The corresponding theory, taking into account the influence of gravity on the movement of matter during an underground explosion, was just being created at that time, and this powerful explosion made it possible to obtain reliable calculation formulas and carry out the successful design of large underground explosions, some of which I have characterized. The author of the article took part in this experiment as the head of the cinematographic observation detachment. Several dozen devices were used for filming with a frequency of one to a thousand frames per second. The article provides a series of photographs of this explosion in various stages of its development. In order to find out where the rocks directly adjacent to the charge would end up, “tagged atoms” - rapidly decaying isotopes - were introduced into the charge. They turned out to be securely buried deep under the bottom of the current lake. After all, if the charge were nuclear, these rocks would be dangerously radioactive. “Tagged atoms” showed the possibility of using nuclear explosions for similar purposes - pieces of radioactive rock do not fly apart, but remain buried. In conclusion, I would like to mention that nuclear explosions were also widely used for peaceful purposes (and not only in our country) - right up to the ban in 1996 on any nuclear explosions, including underground ones. Usually this was not advertised, since it was also associated with defense tasks - testing new bomb designs. But since it was still impossible to hide these explosions due to their seismic effect, today in the open press you can find all the information about the time, place and purpose of their implementation. During the period from 1965 to 1988, 116 underground nuclear explosions were carried out in our country (USSR) for peaceful purposes. Of these, 39 were carried out for scientific purposes - to study the bowels of the earth using powerful seismic waves generated during an explosion. While preparing this article for publication, I was glad to have the opportunity to once again remember my friends - the head of the expedition V.N. And once again to feel gratitude to all my teachers, and above all to the “chief explosives expert” of the country, Academician M.A. Sadovsky, our unforgettable “Michal,” as we, his students, and all the scientists who studied explosions lovingly called him.
