back in 1950, in the second year of Physics and Technology, majoring in “Physics of Explosion”. For over 30 years, Alexander Fedorovich Belyaev headed the laboratory of physics of explosive processes at the Institute of Chemical Physics of the USSR Academy of Sciences. A prominent scientist who possessed extraordinary experimental talent, that thoroughness in setting up experiments and analyzing them, which leaves no room for doubt in assessing the results of research and in the conclusions from them. An important detail is that over the many years of operation of this laboratory, when explosions (130 grams of TNT equivalent) rumbled every day in the reinforced concrete chamber, not a single accident occurred. But experimental charges were created from mixtures of explosives; they were molded and equipped with detonators by researchers and laboratory assistants, students and trainees. And I remember the manager’s attention to all the little details, his unwavering severity and intransigence in matters of careful adherence to safety rules, which I sometimes wanted to break in order to speed up the experiment and, as it seemed to us, ultimately, for the sake of science. But explosion remains a phenomenon that can be extremely dangerous, especially when used without proper theoretical analysis. An example of this is the well-known “Oppau disaster” in the field of explosives. On the outskirts of this small German town, at the end of the First World War, there was a large warehouse of ammonium nitrate, which Germany learned to synthesize primarily for the production of artillery gunpowder to replace the natural Chilean sodium nitrate that had become inaccessible. If sodium nitrate mixed with coal and sulfur (gunpowder) is flammable and burns quickly, then it is much more difficult to set fire to ammonium nitrate mixtures. In its pure form it was considered completely safe. After the war, when Germany no longer had the right to produce weapons and gunpowder, this saltpeter began to be used as fertilizer. But in combination with air moisture, saltpeter forms a rock-cracked monolith, and it was decided to extract it not mechanically, but like rock - to break it off using dynamite explosions. And the work initially went well, until one day in 1920 the entire mass of saltpeter reacted as an explosive (and there were about 11,000 tons of it). The town of Oppau was completely destroyed, the number of victims was in the hundreds. Now ammonium nitrate is a fertilizer in powder or granules, completely safe from an explosive point of view and bad only when it is abused (the notorious “nitrates”). But at the same time, ammonium nitrate is the main component (up to 80%) of explosive mixtures: ammonite, ammotol and dynamon, the most commonly used explosives in the mining and construction industries all over the world.
