Explosion

L. BELOPUKHOV December day exactly fifty years ago. Deserted steppe, lightly sprinkled with snow, not far from Tashkent. There are several dozen people on a small high-rise. Among them are venerable academicians and corresponding members - M.M. Lavrentyev, L.I. Sedov, M.A. Sadovsky, doctors and candidates of science. And we are young scientists who graduated from university just a few years ago, and at this moment we are developers and participants in the upcoming event. All eyes are fixed on one point in the steppe, where one and a half kilometers from the high-rise building one can see a small pillar - a benchmark. Three o'clock in the afternoon - noon in Moscow. In those days, at noon, precise time signals sounded on the radio - “pi”, “pi”, “pi”. And exactly at the moment of the third “pi” something happened. Where the benchmark had just stood, the ground began to rise. It was as if a living flower had blossomed in the steppe, its petals rising higher and higher - up to a 600-meter height, then bending and falling down. There was no powerful sound, only the earth trembled throughout the entire area, and a seismic wave spread over thousands of kilometers through the bowels of the earth, as if during an earthquake. This was an earthquake, but not natural, but created by man in a desert area and therefore safe for people. On December 19, 1957, a unique scientific experiment took place - a powerful underground explosion. At a depth of 40 meters, an explosive charge equivalent to a thousand tons of TNT was detonated. For two days, trucks from the nearest railway station carried this deadly cargo - fifty railway cars - in a continuous stream. The experiment became the final point in scientific work on the study of underground explosions “to release” and the rationale for their further use in mining and construction. This was a major scientific achievement.

For military purposes, people began to use explosions almost a thousand years ago, when the use of gunpowder for artillery and sapper purposes began. In 1552, during the siege of Kazan, sappers from the army of Ivan the Terrible (apparently, these were foreign specialists) dug under the walls of the Kazan Kremlin, and with the explosion of a ton of gunpowder at the right moment before the assault, the wall was destroyed. In TNT equivalent, the charge was about 50 kilograms. The apotheosis of the use of explosions in war to destroy people was the Hiroshima tragedy, which claimed tens of thousands of lives. Well, the largest man-made explosion occurred on October 30, 1961 on Novaya Zemlya. Its TNT equivalent is $5\cdot 10^{10}$ kilograms, or 50 million tons. More than half the length of the earth's equator would be occupied by trains lined up one after another to transport such a quantity of TNT. Explosions also occur in nature. Thunder is nothing more than a consequence of an electrical explosion in a lightning channel. Explosions thunder during volcanic eruptions and occur in the solar envelope. Stars explode - novae and supernovae, even galaxies explode. It’s true that explosions don’t thunder in space; there’s nothing to thunder there—there’s no gas medium of sufficient density. Lost in the depths of history is the time when people first began to make explosive or fast-burning mixtures - gunpowder. Like almost all inventions, it was made in China to entertain the rulers with rockets and fireworks. (It is curious that even now firecrackers and fireworks in Russia are mainly made in China.) Probably, from travelers like Marco Polo, Arabs and Europeans in the Middle Ages learned that a mixture of coal, sulfur and saltpeter burns so quickly that it can be used to throw cannonballs and destroy walls.

Blast worker

For almost all people, the word “explosion” evokes negative emotions. Roar, flames, destruction, loss of life - today's television often “decorates” its programs with such stories. Explosions thunder in war films and modern action films. Veterans of wars - from the Great Patriotic War to Afghanistan and Chechnya - know about explosions from their own experience. But there were also nuclear (“atomic”) explosions. And now in underground bunkers there are nuclear charges capable of destroying earthly life if they were all detonated.

But even then gunpowder began to be used for peaceful purposes. The explosion became a worker. In the same distant 16th century, powder explosions were used in mines and quarries to crush rocks. And the first Russian engineer, Emperor Peter I, is credited with the invention of an ingenious explosive fire extinguisher. A powder charge was placed in a barrel of water with fire cords connected to it from flammable areas of the room.
