The players of the fourth group received M - ( L + 1 ) 2 points - no more than N 2 - 1. In the second and third groups, the result is ( N - 1 ) 2. It follows that the second group consists of strange ones, the first - strong ones, the third - average ones, and the fourth - weak ones. In this case, the number of strange ones is equal to [ N 2 ] - L, and by choosing L it can be made any natural number ranging from 1 to [ N 2 ] - 1. Problem 18. Prove that in any tournament the strong together with the strange ones make up less than 2/3 of the total number of players, and that the same is true for the weak along with the strange ones. If there are no averages at the tournament, then the strong and weak each make up more than a third of the tournament participants, and the strange ones make up less than a third. More precisely, if N has the form 3M, 3M + 1 or 3M + 2 (M is natural), then the number of strange ones does not exceed, respectively, M – 2, M – 1, M. Solution. Let's consider the “arithmetic average” strong player (i.e., add up the points scored by the strong ones and divide by the number of such participants). This conditional player won against as many strong people as he lost to. At the same time, he lost to all the strange ones. Therefore, the sum of the points of the “arithmetic average” strong player is no more than the number of average and weak players plus half the number of strong ones. Likewise, the result of the “arithmetic average” of a strange player is no less than the number of strong ones plus half the number of strange ones. But this amount is less than in the first case. As a consequence, the total number of average and weak ones is more than half the total number of strong and strange ones. And this means that the strong and strange make up less than two-thirds of the entire roster of players. The statement about the number of strange people along with the weak ones is proven in a similar way. Problem 19. Construct an example (for arbitrary N ³ 5), when there are strange ones at the tournament, there are no average ones, and the number of strong ones, like the number of weak ones, is equal to the smallest integer greater than N/3. Solution. Let's divide the tournament participants into three groups, with the first two including [N 3] + 1 people. Let all participants in a round-robin tournament score different numbers of points. Construct an example where there are exactly two draws and there is a strange player. Solution. Let the number of players be odd, they are numbered from 1 to 2M + 1 and the smaller numbers won against the larger ones with the following exceptions: the first one played a draw with the Mth and (M + 2)th with the last one, the player with the number M + 1 won against all the previous ones and lost against all the subsequent ones. It is easy to see that the sum of points decreases as the number increases and the (M + 1)th player is strange. Problem 21. There was only one draw in the round-robin tournament. Any two participants scored different numbers of points. Prove that there are no strange players. Solution (found by participants of the Tournament of Cities conference). Let's assume that there is a strange player. Since he is alone, he takes neither first nor last place. Consequently, the first player lost to the strange one and, therefore, scored no more than n – 2 points, and the last player won against the strange one and, therefore, scored at least one point. On the other hand, since there was exactly one draw in the tournament, two players have half-integer scores and n – 2 players have integer scores. Thus, there are players who scored 1, 2,..., n – 2 points. Let's look at the last player. Since he won against the strange one, he scored exactly 1 point, i.e. lost to everyone else. Then the penultimate player (if it is not the strange one) won against the strange one and the last one and, therefore, lost to everyone else. Repeating this reasoning, we find that none of the weak players could play a draw. Likewise, none of the strong players could play a draw. Contradiction. (End to follow)

Two unfamiliar puzzles made from familiar pieces

Screw and nut. It is very possible that this puzzle was invented around the same time as the pieces that make it up. The place of her birth has not yet been established even to the accuracy of the continent. Its variants have been found in Africa, Europe and Asia, consisting of sticks with rings attached to them. Among the Koryaks living on the Kamchatka Peninsula, this toy is considered a national puzzle and has been known since time immemorial. The task is to move one of the rings onto the loop with the other ring. The design shown in the photo requires connecting a screw and a nut. Like most good puzzles, the problem seems impossible at first. And it is solved, one might say, “head-on”! You need to stubbornly push the nut along the rope until it connects with the screw. Just don't try to push a large nut through a small hole. Instead, pull the rope knot through the hole and slide the rope over the nut, not the other way around. Bolt and washer. This wonderful puzzle was created by Moscow inventor Kirill Grebnev. To appreciate it, you should keep in mind that it is much more difficult to come up with a puzzle that is apparently simple but difficult to solve than one consisting of many parts. Not every puzzle inventor can do this. Based on the totality of its advantages, the Bolt and Puck toy can be called the best puzzle of 2006, and not only in Russia. It turned out to be the most popular at the annual October meeting of the Dutch club NKC, which unites more than 400 puzzle lovers from 38 countries. The task of the puzzle is obvious - you need to remove the washer from the bolt. To make this possible, you need to unhook the cord holding it from the washer. The loop that encloses the puck can be moved in two directions, but only one direction leads to the goal. Let's hope this hint is enough for you. I wish you success! A. Kalinin
