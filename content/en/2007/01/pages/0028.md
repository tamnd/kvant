KVANT 2007/№1

THE MOST CONFUSED CASE-2

V.CHINESE

ALICE, GOING TO THE EDGE OF THE FOREST, SAW NEARBY her old acquaintance, the White Knight, standing next to his faithful horse. Their meeting was joyful, and the White Knight bombarded Alice with questions about her adventures in Wonderland. The girl willingly told him about her adventures and about the trial she attended at the end of her stay there. “I also recently attended a trial in the Royal Court,” noted the White Knight. “But it seems it wasn’t me who was judged.” - How interesting! Tell us! - asked Alice. - Oh, I remembered! “At the trial that I want to tell you about,” the White Knight began, “there were four defendants, and only one of them was guilty. By the way, you know them all! – he exclaimed joyfully. – These are your old friends from Wonderland: the March Hare, the Cheshire Cat, the Hatter and the Footman-Bream. When the March Hare was asked whether the Hatter was guilty, he answered either “yes” or “no,” but I don’t remember what exactly he said. The strict judge, the King of Hearts, asked the Cheshire Cat one single question: “Is the Footman-Bream guilty?” I have forgotten what he answered now, but I remember exactly that he said either “yes” or “no.” The judge's assistant, the Knave of Spades, asked the Hatter about the veracity of the Cheshire Cat's testimony, but I completely forgot his answer. After a short break, the court resumed and the last suspect, Lackey-Bream, was questioned. The second assistant judge, the Knave of Clubs, asked him a rather complicated question: “Is it true that the Cheshire Cat is guilty and the March Hare told the truth?” Whether Lackey-Bream said “yes” or answered “no” has now slipped my mind. But I remember, as now, that of all the testimony, only two were false. “Last month,” continued the White Knight, “I met Jabberwocky and told him everything you already know about.” At that time, I still remembered what exactly each of the four defendants said (i.e., “yes” or “no”), and when I told Jabberwocky their statements, he had no difficulty in identifying the culprit. - I know, I know! - Alice interrupted him. – Next you will say that if I had additional information about the Jabberwocky, I would certainly have to solve this problem. Isn't that right? “No,” the White Knight said thoughtfully. – This data is not enough for you to solve the problem. – What else do I need to know? - asked Alice.

“Here’s what,” continued the White Knight. – A week after my conversation with the Jabberwocky, I met the Carpenter, a great expert and lover of this kind of questions, and told him everything you already know about. Of course, the Carpenter was no further along in solving the problem than you. However, after thinking, he asked: “Did all the suspects answer yes?” The answer was still known to me at that time, and I frankly communicated it to the Carpenter. After thinking for a while, he said: “Could you remember whether the March Hare told the court the truth or whether he lied?” And I gave him an answer to this question, but it did not help: The carpenter was never able to solve the problem. – It turns out interesting! - Alice exclaimed. - The carpenter, knowing everything the same as me, could not solve the problem, but I have to solve it? “Of course not,” the White Knight reassured her, “my story is far from over.” “Another week later,” continued the White Knight, “I met the Walrus and told him everything I had just told you about.” After much thought, the walrus asked me a question about the veracity of the Cheshire Cat's testimony. True, now I have already forgotten what I answered him, but then I gave a comprehensive answer. But, despite such extensive information, Walrus was never able to determine who the court found guilty. “Do you really think that if the Walrus and the Carpenter couldn’t decide who was guilty,” asked Alice, “that I can do it?” Or maybe you will remember or tell us something else? “Well, of course, I’ll tell you,” the White Knight answered joyfully. “I still remember a lot and I won’t hide anything from you.” Soon after meeting the Walrus, I went to a tea party with Sonya the Mouse and during a conversation with her I mentioned this process, telling her everything that I had just told you, and, in addition, I told her that the Cheshire Cat and the Hatter either both lied, or both told the truth, or one of them told the truth. But, unfortunately, this information did not help her: Sonya was unable to solve this difficult problem. “Here’s another thing,” he continued his story. – Half a month ago, while walking along the seashore, I met my friend, Griffin, and during our conversation I remembered this difficult matter. The griffin became very interested in him, and I told him everything without hiding. He listened to me carefully, and then thought for a long time. Then he asked if I remembered what the March Hare answered at the trial: “yes” or “no.” After receiving my answer and thinking a little more, he was still unable to decide who was to blame.
