Up and down through the atmosphere

K. BOGDANOV

Is it possible to fly into space in a hot air balloon?

For many years, this question worried the famous Russian scientist and inventor in the field of aerodynamics and rocket dynamics K.E. Tsiolkovsky. At first he tried to justify the possibility, and then the impossibility of such flights. And only in 1903 the scientist gave a final negative answer in his now classic work, “Exploration of World Spaces by Reactive Instruments.” Calculating the required dimensions of a balloon weighing 1 kg, filled with hydrogen, which could lift 1 kg of payload to a height of 27 km, Tsiolkovsky came to the conclusion that “...even tissue paper will be 5 times heavier than the matter that should be used for our balloon. Such matter, when applied to a balloon, is impossible, because a shell made of it will tear and let gas through greatly.” And further: “What can we say about raising instruments to great heights? The dimensions of balloons should be much larger, but we must not forget that as the size of the balloon increases, the forces tearing the shell increasingly prevail over the resistance of the material. Lifting instruments beyond the atmosphere using a balloon, of course, is completely unthinkable...” So, according to Tsiolkovsky, the flight of balloons into space is impossible, if only because of the lack of sufficiently thin and durable materials for balloon shells. More than a hundred years have passed, and now quite a lot of materials have been created that Tsiolkovsky could not even dream of. Thus, the use of polyethylene film with a thickness of 3.4 microns made it possible for Japanese scientists from the Institute of Space Research to produce a stratospheric balloon with a volume of $60000 м^{3}$ (diameter 50 m), the mass of which was only 35 kg. In May 2002, this stratospheric balloon set a record, rising to an altitude of 53 km with a payload of about 5 kg, consisting of two television cameras and an altitude device. The graph of the record rise of this stratospheric balloon is depicted in figure 1. Thus, the development of technology has shown that Tsiolkovsky’s prophecy regarding balloons, generally speaking, turned out to be not entirely true. We estimate the energy of the descending atmospheric column

The balloon moves upward because the Archimedes buoyancy force from the surrounding air acts on it from below. As it rises, the balloon frees up space underneath itself, which is occupied by the air displaced by the balloon from above. The potential energy of this air moving from top to bottom decreases, and part of this energy turns into the mechanical energy of the stratospheric balloon.
