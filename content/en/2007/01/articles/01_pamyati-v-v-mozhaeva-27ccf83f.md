Soon after his return to his hometown, Celsius took part in the expedition of the French astronomer P. Maupertuis to northern Sweden. The goal of this famous “Lapland” expedition was to measure the length of the earth’s degree along the meridian in close proximity to the pole and compare the result obtained with the data of a similar expedition in Peru near the equator (see the article “Jorge Juan de Santasilla” in «Kvant» No. 3, 2006). The expeditions confirmed Newton's hypothesis that the shape of the Earth is an ellipsoid flattened at the poles, and Celsius' participation in this expedition made him famous. This, in turn, allowed Celsius to obtain sufficient funds from the Swedish authorities to equip the astronomical observatory in Uppsala with the most modern equipment. Some of the equipment, however, was purchased by Celsius even earlier during a long trip to Europe. The Celsius Observatory opened in 1741. In those years, the task of an astronomy professor was considered not only to observe the starry sky, but also to meteorology and geographical measurements. In particular, he made an important contribution to the cartography of Sweden and established that the level of the lands of the northern countries has been slowly rising above sea level since the melting of the glaciers. Long before his follower Birkeland, Anders Celsius came to the understanding that the northern lights are caused by magnetic phenomena, and he made this conclusion by observing the deflection of the compass needle during this natural phenomenon. Celsius published a catalog of 300 stars using a photometric system he himself created. The idea of ​​this system was to superimpose identical translucent plates on top of each other. So, to exclude light from Sirius, the brightest star in the sky, he needed 25 plates. Celsius was an active supporter of the introduction of Grego-

When 1704 and 1708 were mistakenly declared leap years, Sweden returned to the Julian calendar. After Celsius, Sweden introduced a new calendar, shifting the old calendar by 11 days. For meteorological observations, Celsius designed his famous thermometer, where as a zero reference point, i.e. The boiling point of water at normal atmospheric pressure was taken as 0 degrees, and its freezing temperature as 100 degrees. After his death, the Celsius scale was converted to its modern form. Thanks to the precise fixation of measurement methods and conditions, the Celsius scale provided greater reproducibility and accuracy compared to the Fahrenheit and Reaumur scales then in use. In 1948, the temperature scale unit was officially given the name “degree Celsius”. Although the boiling and freezing points of water approximately correspond to modern calibration concepts, formally the original definition of the Celsius temperature scale requires adjustment. The fact is that the boiling and freezing points of water depend on atmospheric pressure, which, in turn, depends on the definition of temperature. In the modern interpretation, $0,01 ^\circ$ C is set at the triple point of water, and a degree Celsius is equal to 1/273.16 of the difference between the triple point of water and absolute zero temperature. According to this definition, one degree on the Celsius scale is equal in absolute value to one degree on the Kelvin scale. To convert the Celsius scale to the Kelvin scale, you must use the relation K = 273.15 + C, where K is the temperature on the Kelvin scale, and C is the temperature on the Celsius scale. To convert the Celsius scale to the Fahrenheit scale, you must use the ratio F = 1.8 C + 32, where F is the temperature on the Fahrenheit scale. The Reaumur scale is currently practically not used.

It is with deep regret that we inform you that Viktor Vasilievich Mozhaev, one of the active authors and members of the editorial board of the journal «Kvant», is no longer with us. V.V. Mozhaev was a brilliant teacher, associate professor at the Department of General Physics at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, author of various manuals and problem books for students and schoolchildren, and member of the jury of many All-Russian Olympiads in Physics. Viktor Vasilyevich was distinguished by his extraordinary efficiency and absolute reliability. Over the past fifteen years, while he was running the “Applicant's Practicum” section in physics, the editorial board was calm - there would be an article in the issue, it would be good and, what is important, on time. We had the pleasure of communicating with this modest, but very worthy and interesting person. We will really miss Viktor Vasilievich Mozhaev.
