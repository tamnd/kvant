What unites bridges and parachutes is the separation flow of air around them. Let's consider a system of two bodies: a descent module (DA) + a parachute (P) and a bridge at the construction stage using the longitudinal sliding method, when a multi-beam system, so far lightweight from the floor slabs, extends from one bridge pier to another (see Fig.1). Both here and here we have a system of several bluff bodies and a significantly non-stationary (i.e. changing with time) separated nature of the air flow. When studying such flows, we can assume that the flow around all corner edges occurs with flow separation, and the nature of the flow around the entire system of bodies depends on the distance between the bodies - be it SA+P or extended bridge beams (front-backs) during their transverse flow. Let's discuss the process of lowering cargo by parachute. By changing the distance between SA and P, you can change the magnitude of the resistance force. When the distance L between them is less than a certain critical value $L^{*}$, there is a flow around with an “open wake” (unsteady flow; Fig. 3,a), and at $L > L^{*}$ – with a “closed wake” (Fig.3,b). from an aerodynamic point of view, they have something in common: both the bridge and the parachute are bluff bodies. What does it mean - bad or good? If, when flowing around a body, streams of air are separated in its bow area and then reunited at the stern point, this is good for an airplane or airship, because the drag force is minimal. But a parachute, in principle, should have as much resistance as possible, i.e. be poorly streamlined. Counter streams of air flow, having separated in front of a poorly streamlined body, no longer connect behind it, but form vortices. As aerodynamicists say, flow separation occurs.
