On October 9, 2007, the famous scientist and teacher Valentin Aleksandrovich Fabrikant would have turned 100 years old. During his long creative life, Valentin Aleksandrovich managed to do a lot - make wonderful discoveries (he is the author of works on parametric amplification of light, which laid the foundation for the creation of lasers), educate a galaxy of students, write excellent books and articles. V.A. Fabrikant was also at the origins of the creation of the magazine «Kvant». As a permanent member of the editorial board, he was a great friend of readers and the editors. We are pleased to bring to your attention an article by V.A. Fabrikant, published in «Kvant» No. 7 for 1978 and included in the Appendix to the journal «Kvant» No. 4/99, dedicated to the memory of Valentin Aleksandrovich.

Green glass surprises

V.FABRIKANT

What color is green glass? This question may cause a feeling of natural bewilderment. The reader will say with irritation: green glass is called green because it... However, there is no need to rush with condescending explanations. A simple experience will show you that the question of the color of green glass is not at all so simple. If you have a piece of green glass, carefully break it into several not very small pieces. Then look through one of them at the filament of the incandescent lamp. As you would expect, the thread will appear green. Place a second piece of glass on this piece and look at the thread again. You probably won't notice a change in the color of the thread; it will still be green. But if you put a third one on two pieces of glass and look through all three pieces at the thread, you will see it already uncolored - whitish in color. Through four pieces the thread will appear reddish, and through five pieces it will appear ruby ​​red! The result is completely unexpected and very instructive. It turns out that the color of glass depends on its thickness, and glass that is green in a thin layer becomes red when the layer is thick enough. Of course, not every green glass has this property, but the most common cheap varieties of green glass do. It is curious that the same property is inherent in a solution of the most important coloring substance on earth - chlorophyll. As you know, chlorophyll colors plant leaves green. By placing the leaves in alcohol, you can obtain a solution of chlorophyll in alcohol and conduct this experiment. Place a glass on a piece of white paper and slowly pour the chlorophyll solution into it. At first, the bottom of the glass will appear in the light

green, and then, with a large layer thickness, the solution will acquire a rich dark red color. Let's return to green glass. You can further confuse the question of the color of the glass if, after looking through the incandescent light bulb, you look through the pieces of glass at the hot end of the poker. After just three pieces of glass it will appear ruby ​​red. Here's the second unexpected result: the visible color of glass depends not only on its thickness, but also on what kind of luminous object we look at through this glass. A layer of three pieces of glass appears colorless when observing the filament of an incandescent lamp and red when observing the end of a red-hot poker. One more experiment can be done with a poker, from which a practically important conclusion follows. Once removed from the stove, the poker cools down quickly. Try following the end of the poker through the glass as it cools. As we have already said, the end of a red-hot poker is visible red through three pieces of glass. The end of a somewhat cooled poker appears red after just two bites. If you wait a little longer, you will see the end of the poker red even through one piece of green glass. From this experiment it follows that the higher the temperature of the hot body, the thicker the layer of glass must be in order for its color to change. This means that by the thickness of the glass layer required to change color, one can judge the temperature of the hot body. Experiments with a poker make clear the structure of an extremely ingenious and simple device used to determine the temperatures of hot bodies - a pyrometric wedge.
