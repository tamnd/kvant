The table problem showed that in the process of studying a physical object, it is sometimes necessary to significantly modify the description of its properties, abandoning an overly simplified model in favor of a more realistic one. In the case we considered, the original simple model turned out to be incomplete - it did not allow us to find the only correct solution. However, this is only one side of the coin. If you do not look at its other side, you can come to the deeply erroneous belief that simplified representations (models of objects) are traps for inexperienced researchers. The size of the article does not allow us to develop this idea any fully, so we will limit ourselves to two examples so as not to be completely unfounded. Kepler's laws give us the simplest possible picture of the motion of planets around the Sun: planets move in ellipses. The presence of other planets somewhat clouds this picture, but this is of interest only to astronomers. If you like, Kepler's laws are very democratic. Indeed, if these laws remained in the scientist’s manuscripts as raw material, as a first approximation for processing observational results, and only refined formulas were included in journals and textbooks, what would the general public know about our Solar System? And the scientists themselves “would have had a hard time.” Let us remember that the planet Neptune was discovered due to the observed deviations from Kepler’s laws in the motion of Uranus, which (deviations) could not be explained by the influence of all the other planets known at that time. The second example is the theory of small oscillations. Science and technology owe it such fundamental concepts as natural frequencies and oscillations, frequency multiplicity and their splitting, spectra, overtones, etc. Meanwhile, all these concepts are impossible, or at least very difficult, to define without referring to the obviously approximate theory of small oscillations. This theory itself arose as a result of the linearization of relevant systems of differential equations, i.e. “unceremonious” discarding of squares, cubes and all higher degrees of the sought-after small deviations of the system being studied from its equilibrium state. Conclusion - consciously turning to approximate models can be very fruitful.
