SURPRISES

GREEN

The intensity of dark red and green rays is not yet large enough to cover the preponderance in the initial brightness of the green rays. The main role is played by green rays, which gives the corresponding color. With increasing glass thickness, according to Bouguer's law, the transmission of green rays decreases much faster than dark red rays (the number of “green” and “red” runners over long distances). With a sufficiently large thickness, the difference in transmission is already so great that it covers the initial preponderance in the brightness of the green rays, and practically only a dark red stripe remains from the entire spectrum. All that remains is to explain the role played by the temperature of the hot body that we look at through the glass. It is known that the hotter we heat any metal object, the whiter the light it gives. No wonder they say: “bring $до_{Рис.} 4$ to white heat.” So, with insufficient heat, an incandescent light bulb produces a reddish light, and with normal heat, it produces a much whiter light. This is explained by the fact that with increasing temperature, the brightness of green and blue rays grows much faster than red ones. This means that at a higher temperature the difference in the brightness of the green and dark red parts of the spectrum is greater, and it is more difficult to cover it by absorption in glass. This is why at higher temperatures of a hot body, thicker glass is needed to change the color of the observed radiation.

Old Russian icons and observations of Leonardo da Vinci

On some ancient Russian icons, the unusual colors of the saints’ robes are striking. The folds are depicted with paint that has a sharply different color from the color of the smooth parts of the robe. For example, red folds on a green cloak (Fig.4) or orange folds on a blue robe. The keen eye of the ancient Russian bogomaz noticed that some fabrics have two colors and in folds take on a different color than on a flat surface. The reason for the two-color fabrics is the same as in the experiment with a pyrometric wedge. If a beam of light reflected from a two-color fabric is passed through a prism, then two colored stripes will definitely remain in the spectrum. For green two-color fabric, the picture will be the same as with green glass: red and green stripes will remain, the rest of the rays will be absorbed. Two-color green fabric reflects red rays better than green ones, but with a single reflection from the smooth surface of the fabric, the green rays are more bright in the incident light. Therefore, green rays still predominate in the once reflected light. In the folds of fabric, light experiences at least two successive reflections. Multiple reflections enhance this effect. Most ordinary fabrics have exactly the opposite properties. In folds you get a more saturated, but the same color tone as on a flat surface. This is again explained by repeated reflections. The light reflected from such fabrics, after decomposition by a prism, gives only one stripe in the spectrum instead of two stripes for two-color fabrics. For example, light, once reflected from yellow velvet, produces a broad band in the spectrum with the greatest brightness in the yellow part. In addition to yellow rays, the spectrum also contains green and blue rays. With double reflection, the band in the spectrum becomes narrower, since blue rays practically disappear completely, and green rays are greatly weakened. The same law of geometric progression works. As a result, the yellow-orange reflected light becomes more saturated. One of the most versatile geniuses who ever lived, Leonardo da Vinci, not only noticed this feature of fabric folds with his artist’s eye, but also, as a scientist, gave a completely correct explanation for the observed phenomenon. In his Treatise on Painting, he writes: “Reflected colors have a much greater beauty than the natural color of these bodies, as can be seen in the opening folds of golden fabrics... when one surface is reflected from another opposite, and this one in it, and so on successively ad infinitum.” The same “Treatise on Painting” says: “The reflexes (reflections) from a living body receiving light from another living body are redder and more excellently flesh-colored than any other part of a living body that a person can have.” Contemplating with pleasure the amazing paintings of Van Dyck and Rubens in the halls of the Hermitage, it is easy to notice that for these great masters the effect of multiple reflections was not a secret.
