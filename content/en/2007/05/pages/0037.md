SCHOOL AT KVANT

Practical problem in mechanics

Y.NOSOV

LET'S TAKE A WOODEN BAR WITH SIZES 2 x 3 x 10 CM.

We will clamp the block in a vice, as shown in figure, and begin to saw it along the long side into two parts. During the work, we will make sure that the hacksaw does not touch the screw of the vice. Let's cut the block completely from edge to edge, loosen the vice and take out two new thinner bars. We emphasize that the entire operation is performed with the same initial compression of the vice and no rearrangements or movements of the bar are made. How is it that the vice, despite the fact that it compresses the block, does not interfere with cutting it into two parts?

Of course, this problem is a practical (laboratory) work in mechanics, for which a rigorous solution cannot be given. But we can be quite satisfied with the following qualitative reasoning. In our experiment, the holding of the wooden block is ensured by the compressive force of the vice F created by the screw (see figure). To move the block down, it is necessary to apply a force $F_{вниз}$ that exceeds the static friction force

$F_{тр} = kF$, where k is the coefficient of friction between the jaws of the vice and the block. The magnitude of the force F is so great that it is impossible to move the block by hand in a compressed vice. Why do we manage to move the saw blade up and down when cutting a block longitudinally? It turns out that for this pair of materials (saw blade - wood) the static friction force $F_{тр1}$ is significantly less than $F_{тр}$, and, as a consequence, the force required to move the saw is significantly less than $F_{вниз}$. For this couple

$F_{тр1} = k_{1} F_{1}$,

⟦column⟧

where $k_{1}$ is the coefficient of friction between the saw blade and the block, $F_{1}$ is the compressive force. The compression force of the saw blade $F_{1}$ is as many times less than the total compression force of the vice F, how many times the area of ​​the compressed saw blade is less than the total area of ​​the vice jaws, i.e.

$F_{1} =$ l F, L where l is the width of the saw blade and L is the total width of the vice jaws. To reduce the force $F_{1}$, in our experiment we use a saw with a small blade width l, such as a hacksaw blade. Using the approximate dimensions l = 15 mm and L = 150 mm, we obtain $F_{1} = 0,1 F$. The coefficient of static (and sliding) friction $k_{1}$ is also much less than k, since in the case of a saw, it is mainly the smooth surface of the steel blade that comes into contact with the wood, and not the corrugated surface of the vice jaws. Thus, in our experiment $F_{1} = F$ and $k_{1} = k$, which leads to the conclusion

$F_{тр1} = F_{тр}$.

However, in our experiment, we not only move the saw up and down, but also destroy the wood fibers when sawing. This requires the application of an additional force $F_{2}$ along the cutting direction. You can reduce the magnitude of force $F_{2}$ by using a saw with a very fine tooth, for example, again a hacksaw or a jigsaw. One note. At first glance, it may seem that when sawing a block, area B, where the cut has already been made, ceases to take part in holding the block in the vice. This is wrong. Due to the elasticity of the wood and the small thickness of the saw blade (: 0.5 - 0.7 mm), area B is compressed with almost the same force as area A, where there is no cut yet. Thus, during the action of the saw, the block is always compressed across the entire width of the vise jaws. Real success in carrying out the experiment under discussion shows that the experimenter's hand is quite capable of developing a force exceeding $F_{тр1}$ and $F_{2}$, and successfully sawing a block compressed in a vice. I would like to hope that the given solution to the problem will allow you to independently answer (at a qualitative level) the following questions. How does the result of the experiment affect: the compression force of the vice; saw blade width; bar wood species; forces applied to the saw?
