The first nut to crack On a plane, the shortest distance between two points is a straight line segment connecting these points, and on a sphere (the surface of a ball) it is a segment of a great circle arc connecting these points. However, passenger planes from the point of departure to the destination do not fly in a great circle, but along some broken line. To a first approximation, we can assume that this line consists of circular arcs connecting adjacent ground control towers. Such points are located at major airports. And large airports are “tied” to large cities. Well, big cities on Earth are not located in a strict geometric order. Let's assume that on a perfectly round Earth, control towers are very evenly spaced. For example, like this. Let's choose 24 meridians through $15 ^\circ$ from each other. Along each meridian we will place control points through $15 ^\circ$ from the equator to each of the poles. Question 1. How many control centers will there be on Earth? How many sections will there be connecting neighboring control centers along parallels and meridians?

The second nut As soon as the mathematician completes this task, he will think about plans for further travel. In such an ideal world, let passenger planes fly from the point of departure to the destination not “directly” along a great circle arc, but only along a broken line consisting of sections (circular arcs) connecting two neighboring control towers along one parallel or one meridian. In other words, the plane can only change direction over control towers. Question 2. Is it possible to fly around the entire Earth, i.e. fly over all control towers, so as to fly through each area and only once? If such a journey is feasible, does it matter where it starts? Question 3. Is it possible to fly around the entire Earth so as to fly over each control tower and only once? If such a journey is feasible, does it matter where it starts?

The third nut This is perhaps the most difficult nut to crack, but it gives an idea of ​​the problems that modern applied mathematics deals with. Question 4. Let two arbitrary control points be given by their coordinates, for example, point A (30 $^\circ$ north latitude, $60 ^\circ$ east longitude) and point B (75 $^\circ$ north latitude, $75 ^\circ$ west longitude). How to find the shortest flight route from the first point to the second, and what will it be? (Assume that the radius of the Earth is 6400 km.)

But there is another way to calculate the number of sections. Let's call the degree of the control center the number of sections that “begin” at this point. Then all 264 points except the two polar ones have degree 4, and the two polar points each have degree 24. Since each section between neighboring points makes a “contribution” of one to the degree of two points located at the ends of the section, you can notice that the sum of the degrees of all points in general is equal to twice the number of all sections. But the sum of the degrees of all points is easy to find: it is equal to 4 ѣ 264 + 24 ѣ 2 = 1056 + 48 = 1104. Therefore, the number of sections is 1104/2 = 552, which coincides with the result of direct calculation.
