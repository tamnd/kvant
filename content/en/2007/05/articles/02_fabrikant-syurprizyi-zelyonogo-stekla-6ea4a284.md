Green glass surprises

V.FABRIKANT

What color is green glass? This question may cause a feeling of natural bewilderment. The reader will say with irritation: green glass is called green because it... However, there is no need to rush with condescending explanations. A simple experience will show you that the question of the color of green glass is not at all so simple. If you have a piece of green glass, carefully break it into several not very small pieces. Then look through one of them at the filament of the incandescent lamp. As you would expect, the thread will appear green. Place a second piece of glass on this piece and look at the thread again. You probably won't notice a change in the color of the thread; it will still be green. But if you put a third one on two pieces of glass and look through all three pieces at the thread, you will see it already uncolored - whitish in color. Through four pieces the thread will appear reddish, and through five pieces it will appear ruby ​​red! The result is completely unexpected and very instructive. It turns out that the color of glass depends on its thickness, and glass that is green in a thin layer becomes red when the layer is thick enough. Of course, not every green glass has this property, but the most common cheap varieties of green glass do. It is curious that the same property is inherent in a solution of the most important coloring substance on earth - chlorophyll. As you know, chlorophyll colors plant leaves green. By placing the leaves in alcohol, you can obtain a solution of chlorophyll in alcohol and conduct this experiment. Place a glass on a piece of white paper and slowly pour the chlorophyll solution into it. At first, the bottom of the glass will appear in the light

You can further confuse the question of the color of the glass if, after looking through the incandescent light bulb, you look through the pieces of glass at the hot end of the poker. After just three pieces of glass it will appear ruby ​​red. Here's the second unexpected result: the visible color of glass depends not only on its thickness, but also on what kind of luminous object we look at through this glass. A layer of three pieces of glass appears colorless when observing the filament of an incandescent lamp and red when observing the end of a red-hot poker. One more experiment can be done with a poker, from which a practically important conclusion follows. Once removed from the stove, the poker cools down quickly. Try following the end of the poker through the glass as it cools. As we have already said, the end of a red-hot poker is visible red through three pieces of glass. The end of a somewhat cooled poker appears red after just two bites. If you wait a little longer, you will see the end of the poker red even through one piece of green glass. From this experiment it follows that the higher the temperature of the hot body, the thicker the layer of glass must be in order for its color to change. This means that by the thickness of the glass layer required to change color, one can judge the temperature of the hot body. Experiments with a poker make clear the structure of an extremely ingenious and simple device used to determine the temperatures of hot bodies - a pyrometric wedge. Newton's experiment and landscape painting

Probably, many of you remember Newton’s famous experiment with the decomposition of a solar ray into a multi-colored spectrum using a glass prism. This experiment showed that sunlight is a mixture of rays of different colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. For some reason, Newton did not try to complicate this experiment somewhat: by placing colored glass or a vessel with a colored liquid in the path of the sun's ray. In any case, Newton does not describe such an experience in his writings. The experiment with red glass, in fact, would not have yielded anything interesting. Instead of a multi-colored strip of the spectrum, only the section corresponding to the red rays would remain. The result could have been predicted in advance: red glass is red because it transmits only red rays and absorbs all others. Much more interesting is the experiment with green glass or a vessel filled with a chlorophyll solution. In these cases, not one, but two stripes will remain from the spectrum: green and dark red. This means that green glass and chlorophyll solution transmit not only green, but also red rays. Regarding chlorophyll, the remarks of the famous Russian botanist K.A. Timiryazev are very interesting: “You can very easily make sure that chlorophyll transmits red rays: it’s worth looking at a landscape flooded with bright sunlight through a special blue glass that transmits red and blue rays, but retains green ones, so that before our amazed eyes all of nature is completely transformed - under an ordinary blue sky we will see blood-red vegetation <Fig.1>. Is it not this peculiarity of the color of chlorophyll that lies behind the difficulties with which landscape painting obviously has to struggle? On the painter’s palette, apparently, there are no those green tones that brightly lit greenery represents up close.” Let us, however, leave painting aside for now and return to the pyrometric wedge. Sea captain and geometric progression

The French sea captain Pierre Bouguer, who lived in the first half of the eighteenth century, was, perhaps, not a simple sailor. He wrote voluminous treatises on ship design, navigation and other branches of maritime affairs. The French Academy of Sciences awarded Bouguer three prizes for his work in maritime affairs and elected him as a member. Buger inherited his taste for marine science from his father, a professor of hydrology. If Buger was involved in the sea by inheritance, then he took up optics on his own initiative. Booger was the first to draw attention to the problems associated with changes in light intensity and illumination. He invented the first instruments for measuring the intensity of light and found that the intensity of light from the Sun is 300 thousand times greater

To understand the meaning of this law (it is called Bouguer’s law), we will use a not very plausible, but clear analogy from the field of sports. Let's imagine that we are present at a poorly prepared mass competition in a seven-kilometer run. The poor training of the participants began to tell immediately, and the fans quickly established the following curious law - only one third of the runners who start a given kilometer of distance reach the end. 2187 participants took the start, by the end of the first kilometer there were 729 remaining on the distance, by the end of the second - 243, by the end of the third - 81, fourth - 27, fifth - 9, sixth - 3 participants. Finally, only 1 runner finishes the seventh kilometer and is declared the winner. The judges didn't even have to use a stopwatch to determine who touched the finish line first. Let's write down the number of runners who ran different distances:

2187, 729, 243, 81, 27, 9, 3, 1.

It is easy to see that these numbers form a decreasing geometric progression, in which each subsequent number is three times smaller than the previous one to the left of it. Let's return from sports to optics. Take a piece of painted glass. Let's say that it transmits one third of the light incident on it. Let's add a second similar piece. It will transmit one third of the luminous flux that passed through the first piece, i.e., one ninth of the luminous flux incident on the first piece. Putting another piece, we get one twenty-seventh part and so on. It is clear that the same result would have been obtained simply by doubling the thickness of the piece of glass, then doubling it, etc. As the thickness of the glass increases, the proportion of transmitted light decreases exponentially. This is the law discovered by Bouguer. In the example of runners, we have already seen how the numbers quickly decrease exponentially.

The intensity of dark red and green rays is not yet large enough to cover the preponderance in the initial brightness of the green rays. The main role is played by green rays, which gives the corresponding color. With increasing glass thickness, according to Bouguer's law, the transmission of green rays decreases much faster than dark red rays (the number of “green” and “red” runners over long distances). With a sufficiently large thickness, the difference in transmission is already so great that it covers the initial preponderance in the brightness of the green rays, and practically only a dark red stripe remains from the entire spectrum. All that remains is to explain the role played by the temperature of the hot body that we look at through the glass. It is known that the hotter we heat any metal object, the whiter the light it gives. No wonder they say: “bring $до_{Рис.} 4$ to white heat.” So, with insufficient heat, an incandescent light bulb produces a reddish light, and with normal heat, it produces a much whiter light. This is explained by the fact that with increasing temperature, the brightness of green and blue rays grows much faster than red ones. This means that at a higher temperature the difference in the brightness of the green and dark red parts of the spectrum is greater, and it is more difficult to cover it by absorption in glass. This is why at higher temperatures of a hot body, thicker glass is needed to change the color of the observed radiation.

Old Russian icons and observations of Leonardo da Vinci

On some ancient Russian icons, the unusual colors of the saints’ robes are striking. The folds are depicted with paint that has a sharply different color from the color of the smooth parts of the robe. For example, red folds on a green cloak (Fig.4) or orange folds on a blue robe. The keen eye of the ancient Russian bogomaz noticed that some fabrics have two colors and in folds take on a different color than on a flat surface. The reason for the two-color fabrics is the same as in the experiment with a pyrometric wedge. If a beam of light reflected from a two-color fabric is passed through a prism, then two colored stripes will definitely remain in the spectrum. For green two-color fabric, the picture will be the same as with green glass: red and green stripes will remain, the rest of the rays will be absorbed. Two-color green fabric reflects red rays better than green ones, but with a single reflection from the smooth surface of the fabric, the green rays are more bright in the incident light. Therefore, green rays still predominate in the once reflected light. In the folds of fabric, light experiences at least two successive reflections. During the second reflection, the red rays are again reflected stronger than the green ones, and as a result of a double

reflection occurs in the same way as in thick green glass: the brightness of the red rays becomes greater than the brightness of the green rays and the fabric changes color. Multiple reflections enhance this effect. Most ordinary fabrics have exactly the opposite properties. In folds you get a more saturated, but the same color tone as on a flat surface. This is again explained by repeated reflections. The light reflected from such fabrics, after decomposition by a prism, gives only one stripe in the spectrum instead of two stripes for two-color fabrics. For example, light, once reflected from yellow velvet, produces a broad band in the spectrum with the greatest brightness in the yellow part. In addition to yellow rays, the spectrum also contains green and blue rays. With double reflection, the band in the spectrum becomes narrower, since blue rays practically disappear completely, and green rays are greatly weakened. The same law of geometric progression works. As a result, the yellow-orange reflected light becomes more saturated. One of the most versatile geniuses who ever lived, Leonardo da Vinci, not only noticed this feature of fabric folds with his artist’s eye, but also, as a scientist, gave a completely correct explanation for the observed phenomenon. In his Treatise on Painting, he writes: “Reflected colors have a much greater beauty than the natural color of these bodies, as can be seen in the opening folds of golden fabrics... when one surface is reflected from another opposite, and this one in it, and so on successively ad infinitum.” The same “Treatise on Painting” says: “The reflexes (reflections) from a living body receiving light from another living body are redder and more excellently flesh-colored than any other part of a living body that a person can have.” Contemplating with pleasure the amazing paintings of Van Dyck and Rubens in the halls of the Hermitage, it is easy to notice that for these great masters the effect of multiple reflections was not a secret.
