After all, the textbook says: “if the figure Φ can be mapped onto the figure Φ$_1$ so that the distance between any two points of the figure Φ is equal to the distance between the corresponding points of the figure Φ$_1$, then the figure Φ is said to be congruent to the figure Φ$_1$.” Otherwise, it can be said this way: if there is a mapping from one figure to another, in which the distances are preserved, then these figures are called congruent.

What did Styopa determine?

Stepin’s error raises an interesting question: are there cases in which both the school definition of congruence and Stepin’s will produce the same result? In other words, are there figures that can be mapped onto one another only while preserving their distance?

To answer this question, let's start with the simplest cases. A figure is a set of points. The simplest figure is a figure consisting of one point. Let $F_1 = \{A\}, F_2 = \{B\}$, where $A$ and $B$ are points. It is clear that there is a unique mapping of the figure $F_1$ to the figure $F_2$, it maps the point $A$ to the point $B$:

$$A \rightarrow B.$$

Since the figures $F_1$ and $F_2$ each contain one point, we can talk about points $A$ to point $A$ and from point $B$ to point $B$. But $|AA| = |BB| = 0$, so all mappings from $F_1$ to $F_2$ are preserved.

Let now $F_1 = \{A, B\}, F_2 = \{C, D\}$, where $A, B, C, D$ are points.

Thus, figures that map onto each other only while preserving their distance exist.

Let us now take two figures $F_1$ and $F_2$, each of which consists of three points belonging to the same line (Fig. 3), and $|AC| = |KD| = 5$ cm, $|BC| = |ED| = 3$ cm, $|AB| = |KE| = 2$ cm. These figures are constructive to each other, since the mapping $A \rightarrow K$, $B \rightarrow E$, $C \rightarrow D$ saves distances. Let us now map $F_1$ to $F_2$ like this:
$A \rightarrow K$, $B \rightarrow D$, $C \rightarrow E$.

In this case, the mappings of the points $A$ and $B$ were mapped onto the points $K$ and $D$, but $|AB| \neq |KD|$, i.e. the distance was not preserved. Consequently, the figure $F_1$ can be mapped onto the figure $F_2$ without preserving the distance, i.e. “according to Stepa” $F_1$ and $F_2$ are not constructive.

Now let’s see which figures “according to Stepa” are constructive in themselves. Let the figure $F$ consist of two points: $F = \{A, B\}$. This figure can be mapped onto itself in only two ways:
1) $A \rightarrow A$, $B \rightarrow B$;
2) $A \rightarrow B$, $B \rightarrow A$.

In both mappings the construction is preserved: $|AB| = |AB| = |BA|$. Thus, for any mapping of a given figure $F$ onto itself, the distance is preserved.

Let now $F_1 = \{A, B, C\}$ be the figure of three points depicted on figure 3. Let us map it onto ourselves like this:
$A \rightarrow A$, $B \rightarrow C$, $C \rightarrow B$.

Because it does not preserve distance, and according to Stepin’s definition, this figure is not constructive to itself.
