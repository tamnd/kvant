Folding figures

We will consider plane figures bounded by a closed line that has no self-intersections.

In this article, congestive figures are deferred justia (do not differ), i.e., the set of figures congestive to a given figure is considered one figure. For classes of figures that are congruent with each other, one could introduce some new term, for example, calling such classes “abstract figures.” However, since this article will only consider “abstract figures,” we will simply call them figures. Let us denote the set of figures under consideration by G. Thus, G contains one triangle from sides 3, 4 and 5 (after all, all such triangles are congruent!), exactly one circle of radius 17, etc.

If the figure A has an axis of symmetry, then this axis divides the figures into two congested parts. Let's call each of these parts the result of folding the figure A (along the axis under consideration). The result of folding may, in turn, have an axis of symmetry. Thus, the original figures can, generally speaking, allow repeated folding.

There are shapes that allow endless folding. Such figures are, for example, a circle, a sector of a circle, a circular trapezoid (a figure bounded by arcs of concentrated

ical circles and corresponding segments of radii), equilateral right triangle and rectangle. Otherwise, the word “for example” is redundant in the previous phrase: it can be proven that there are no other figures in Γ that allow infinite folding (an infinite strip bounded by parallel lines is not included in Γ!).

Let us denote by Aᵇ the set of all figures, either congruous figures of A, or those obtained from figures A as a result of one or more foldings.

If the figures A do not have an axis of symmetry, then the set Aᵇ consists of the circle A itself and its sectors with central angles $\frac{2\pi}{2n}$ ($n=1, 2, 3, \dots$).

If A is a rhombus that is not a square, then Aᵇ consists of four figures. figure 1 depicts this set; The arrows indicate the folding order. The results of folding figures B and D are the same. Then we mark each of the figures of the set Aᵇ with a point, designated by the same letter as the figure itself. The point corresponding to the figure contained by the segment with the point corresponding to the result of folding this figure, and place the first point above the second. figure2 shows the resulting diagram.

A similar folding diagram can be constructed for any shape. The folding diagram is used to visualize the “degree of symmetry” of the figure.

Let us now pose the inverse problem: given a diagram (like those shown in Figures 2-4; we do not clarify the concept of a diagram); find a figure whose folding diagram is the given diagram.

To solve such problems, it is useful to introduce the concept of unfolding: we call the figure $A$ the result of unfolding the figure $B$ along the straight line $l$, if the figure $B$ is the result of folding the figure $A$ along this straight line.

The construction of the figure $A$ according to the diagram figure 3 can be carried out by unfolding the right triangle $F$ with an acute angle $22,5^\circ$ sequentially along the corresponding straight lines into figures $E, D, C, B, A$. The figure $A$ from the triangle $F$ can be obtained by two more “chains of layouts” (see diagram).

For the diagram shown in figure 5, a similar problem is solved as follows (make a drawing!). Let us take as $B_0$ the scalene convex quadrilateral $MNPQ$, which has $\hat{M} = \frac{\pi}{2m-1}$, $\hat{N} = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $Q < \frac{\pi}{2}$; let us expand it sequentially $m$ times along straight lines $c_1 \supset MN, c_2, \dots, c_m$ passing through $M$ into figures $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_m$; the figure $B_m = C_1$ is a convex polygon with a center of symmetry $M$, the opposite sides of which are parallel. Let us now expand $C_1$ successively $n$ times along parallel lines $a_1 \perp c_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ into figures $C_2, C_3, \dots, C_{n+1}$. In the polygon $C_{n+1} = D_1$ we fix the connecting sides $l, l_1$ such that $l \perp c_1$; Let us expand it sequentially $k-1$ times along parallel lines $b_1 \supset l_1, b_2, \dots, b_{k-1}$ (such lines will be found, since the side $l_1$ of the polygon $D_1$ is parallel to its opposite side) into figures $D_2, D_3, \dots, D_h$. The polygon $D_h = A$ is exclusive.

For each specific “inverse problem” the difficulty lies in finding the initial figure for unfolding.

Exercise

1. Prove that a triangle can only be folded infinitely if it is a rectangular isosceles triangle.
2. Prove that a quadrilateral can only be folded infinitely if it is rectangular. For $n > 4$, any folding of a $n$-gon results in a polygon with fewer sides.
4. Construct diagrams of the folding of a circle, a sector of a circle, a circular trapezoid and an isosceles right triangle.
5. Construct a diagram of the folding of a rectangle if the ratio of the length of its side: a) is equal to $2^i$ ($i=0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$); b) is not equal to $2^i$.
6. Construct diagrams of folding the figure shown in figure 6.
7. Prove that any triangle admits infinite folding.
8. Find the figures whose folding diagrams look like those shown in Figures 7 and 8.
