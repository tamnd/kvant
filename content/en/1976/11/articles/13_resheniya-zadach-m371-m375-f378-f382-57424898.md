M371. Each cell of the maximum board contains a cycle number from 1 to 64, with different cycles in different cells. In one question, by specifying any set of fields, you can find out the set (set) of the cycle standing on these fields. What is the minimum number of questions to find out the cycle in each cell?

Fig. 1.

Let us prove that the smallest required number of questions is 6. To do this, firstly, we will show that in 6 questions you can learn all the cycles, and, secondly, we will show that this cannot be done in less than a cycle of questions. But first, let's look at the formulation of the problem.

When asking one question, we indicate the required number of questions $A$ fields and find out which of the numbers from 1 to 64 are written in the fields from the set $A$, and which ones are written in the remaining fields (we denote the set of additional fields by $A$). Thus, each question is a certain partition of the board into two sets $A$ and $A$. We must ask several questions $(A_1, \bar{A}_1), (A_2, \bar{A}_2), \dots, (A_q, \bar{A}_q)$ so that from them we can unambiguously determine what number is written in each field, that is, in such a way that for any two different fields there must be a partition $(A_i, \bar{A}_i)$ of our $q$ that separates these two fields: one of the fields belongs to $A_i$, and the other to $\bar{A}_i$. In other words, it is necessary that the intersection of any $q$ set, the $i$th of which - $A_i$ or $\bar{A}_i$ - contains at most one field. In this case, the number of questions $q$ should be as small as possible.

1. An example of six partitions that allow you to know all the cycles is shown in figure 1 (the sets $A_i$ that we imagine asking are pink, $\bar{A}_i$ are white; $i = 1, 2, \dots, 6$). It is not difficult to make sure that our plan is correct: for the first three questions we will learn many numbers written in each line, for the last three - in each column, so as a result we will guess the number in each cell. (Here the intersection of any set of six subsets, the $i$th of which - $A_i$ or $\bar{A}_i$ - contains exactly one element!)

2. Let us prove that it is impossible to determine all the numbers in 5 questions. Let the first question be $(A_1, \bar{A}_1)$. Then in one of the set $A_1, \bar{A}_1$ (we can assume that in $A_1$ - after all, they are completely equal) there are at least 32 fields. Let the second question be $(A_2, \bar{A}_2)$. This question divides the set $A_1$ into two: $A_1 \cap A_2$ and $A_1 \cap \bar{A}_2$, and one of them (we can assume that $A_1 \cap A_2$) has at least 16 fields. And finally, after the fifth question, there will be a set $A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3 \cap A_4 \cap A_5$ of two or more “indifferent” fields. It is clear that we will not be able to find out how the numbers are arranged in this set based on the questions asked.

**M374. Let $a, b, c$ be positive numbers, $a > c$ and $b > c$. Prove the inequality $\sqrt{c(a-c)} + \sqrt{c(b-c)} \leq \sqrt{ab}$.**

**Fig. 9.**

We will give two solutions, the first of which is algebraic, and the second is geometric.

1. Let us put $a = (1 + \alpha)c, b = (1 + \beta)c$, where $\alpha, \beta > 0$. Then $\sqrt{c(a-c)} + \sqrt{c(b-c)} = c(\sqrt{\alpha} + \sqrt{\beta}), \sqrt{ab} = c\sqrt{(1 + \alpha)(1 + \beta)}$, and we need to prove that $\sqrt{\alpha} + \sqrt{\beta} \leq \sqrt{(1 + \alpha)(1 + \beta)}$. Let's write the obvious inequality: $(\sqrt{\alpha}\beta - 1)^2 \geq 0$, or $2\sqrt{\alpha}\beta \leq 1 + \alpha\beta$, and add $(\alpha + \beta)$ to both sides of it. We get

$$\alpha + \beta + 2\sqrt{\alpha}\beta \leq 1 + \alpha + \beta + \alpha\beta,$$

that is, $(\sqrt{\alpha} + \sqrt{\beta})^2 \leq (\sqrt{(1 + \alpha)(1 + \beta)})^2$, which is what was required.

Equality occurs if $\alpha\beta = 1$, that is, $\frac{a}{c} - 1) \left( \frac{b}{c} - 1 \right) = 1$, or $(a - c)(b - c) = c^2$, revealing $c = \frac{ab}{a + b}$.

2. Let's make a quadrilateral $ABCD$ from two equilibrium triangles with bases equal in length to the number $2\sqrt{c}$ and with sides of length $\sqrt{a}$ and $\sqrt{b}$, respectively (Fig. 9). Since the lengths of the heights dropped to the bases of these triangles are $\sqrt{a} - c$ and $\sqrt{b} - c$, respectively, the area of ​​the quadrilateral $ABCD$ is $\sqrt{c(a-c)} + \sqrt{c(b-c)}$. On the other hand, $S_{ABCD} = 2S_{ABD} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} \sin(\widehat{BAD}) \leq \sqrt{ab}$, opening and withdrawing
gives the required inequality.

The equality will be in the case $\sin(\widehat{BAD}) = 1$, that is $\widehat{BAD} = \frac{\pi}{2}$; then $|AB|^2 + |AD|^2 = |BD|^2$, or $a + b = (\sqrt{a} - c + \sqrt{b} - c)^2$, opening $c = \frac{ab}{a + b}$.

**M375. Inside a convex manifold of volume 1, $3 \cdot (2^n-1)$ points are offset.**

Prove that it is possible to cut out from it a convex manifold of volume $(1/2)^n$ that does not contain any displaced points inside.

We will carry out the proof by induction.

Base and indexes. $n=1$, that is, in a convex polygon of volume 1, three points are offset. Let's draw a plane through two of these points, dividing our polygon into two polygons of equal volume, we find that in one of them (volume 1/2) there is not a single offset point.

Step and inductions. Let us now assume that the statement of the problem is proven for all $n \leq k$, and we will prove it for $n=k+1$ (but first note that if the statement of the problem is true for a polygon with $N$ offset points, then it is even more true for a polygon with a smaller number of offset points). So, now we have $3(2^k+1-1)=6 \cdot 2^k-3$ points offset inside the polygon of volume 1. Let's draw a plane through two of them, dividing our polygon into two polygons of equal volume, we find that one of them (volume 1/2) contains no more than $3 \cdot 2^k-3=3(2^k-1)$ points. By the induction hypothesis, from such a polyhedron we can cut out a polyhedron of volume $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^k=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k+1}$ that does not contain displaced points, which proves the statement of the problem.

**F378. Airplane passengers did not experience indirect assessments unless their weight more than doubled during the flight. What is the maximum acceleration in level flight allowed by this condition?**

An airplane passenger flying with horizontal acceleration $a$ is acted upon by two forces from the side of the plane: horizontal force $ma$ and vertical force $mg$, compensating for the force of gravity. Forces of the same absolute magnitude, but directed in opposite directions, act (according to Newton’s third law) from the passenger side to the vehicle. The absolute value of the resulting force acting on the support, i.e., the weight of the passenger, is

$$P = \sqrt{(ma)^2 + (mg)^2}.$$

According to the conditions of the problem

$$P \leq 2mg.$$

From here

$$a \leq \sqrt{3}g.$$

The system described in terms of conditions is a physical matrix. Finding the period of oscillation of a physical matrix is ​​a rather difficult task. The fact is that the period depends on the shape of the physical matrix, its configuration. But for any physical matrix it is possible to select a mathematical matrix whose oscillation period will be equal to the oscillation period of the given physical matrix. This is what we will do: we will select a mathematical matrix “equivalent” to the system depicted in figure 11.

The period of small oscillations of a mathematical matrix depends only on its length. So we need a fairly long “equivalent” mathematical matrix - the so-called reduced length of the physical matrix.

F381. In a device for determining the isomotic state (mass spectroeraphy), singly charged potassium units with atomic weights $A_1 = 39$ and $A_2 = 41$ are first accelerated in an electric field, then enter a uniform magnetic field peredicular to the direction of their movement (Fig. 15). During the experiment, due to the imperfection of the equipment, the accelerating mechanism is placed around the average value $u_0$ by amounts of ±Δu. With what relative power $\frac{\Delta u}{u_0}$ must the accelerating value be maintained so that the potassium isomotive does not overlap?

A moving charged particle in a magnetic field is acted upon by the Lorentz force, the perpendicular velocity of the particle is equal in absolute value to qvB. Here q is the charge of the particle, v is its speed, B is the magnetic field induction. In a uniform magnetic field, a particle will move in a circle, the radius R of which can be found from Newton’s second law:

$$m \frac{v^2}{R} = qvB, \quad R = \frac{mv}{qB},$$

where m is the particle mass. If we use the law of conservation of energy

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = qu,$$

then the radius of the trajectory can be expressed in terms of the accelerating potential u:

$$R = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{1}{B} \sqrt{\frac{2um}{q}}.$$

This correspondence shows that the radius of the trajectory depends on the product um. When the accelerating potential changes, the radius of the trajectory of each of the potassium beams will change (see Fig. 15). To obtain non-overlapping, the following condition must be met:

$$(u_0 + \Delta u)m_1 < (u_0 - \Delta u)m_2,$$

or

$$m_1 + \frac{\Delta u}{u_0}m_1 < m_2 - \frac{\Delta u}{u_0}m_2,$$

where m₁ and m₂ are the masses of potassium ions, proportional to the atomic weights A₁ and A₂, respectively. From here

$$\frac{\Delta u}{u_0} < \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_2 + m_1} = \frac{A_2 - A_1}{A_2 + A_1} = 0.025 = 2.5\%.$$

Note that modern experimental technology makes it possible to record the accelerating potential with much higher accuracy (by several orders of magnitude).

When a drop evaporates without absorbing heat from the outside, it is necessary for the evaporation of quantitative heat $Q$ to be obtained due to a decrease in the surface energy of the drop $U_\pi$ with a decrease in its surface area.

Let the radius of the drop decrease by $\Delta R$, then the volume of the drop decreases by

$$\Delta V = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 - \frac{4}{3} \pi (R - \Delta R)^3 = 4\pi R^2 \Delta R - 4\pi R (\Delta R)^2 + \frac{4}{3} \pi (\Delta R)^3.$$

For small $\Delta R$ we can neglect the second and third terms of this equality compared to the first term, so that

$$\Delta V = 4\pi R^2 \Delta R.$$

The mass of water evaporated in this case is equal to

$$m = \rho \Delta V = 4\pi\rho R^2 \Delta R.$$

To evaporate it, a quantity of heat is required

$$Q = Lm = 4\pi L\rho R^2 \Delta R$$

where $L = 2,26 \cdot 10^6 \text{ дж}/\text{кг}$ is the specific heat of water vaporization. The surface area of ​​the drop decreased by

$$\Delta S = 4\pi R^2 - 4\pi (R - \Delta R)^2 = 8\pi R \Delta R$$

(we will neglect the term containing $(\Delta R)^2$). Therefore, the surface energy decreased by

$$\Delta U_\pi = 8\pi R \Delta R \sigma.$$

where $\sigma = 7,2 \cdot 10^{-2} \text{ дж}/\text{м}^2$ is the coefficient of surface tension of water.

Let's equate the expressions for $Q$ and $\Delta U_\pi$:

$$4\pi L\rho R^2 \Delta R = 8\pi R \Delta R \sigma.$$

where

$$R = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho L} \sim 10^{-10} m = 10^{-8} \text{ см}.$$

Such a drop essentially cannot, since we found that $R$ is of the order of intermolecular distances in water. Consequently, no drop can evaporate without absorbing heat from outside.

When solving the problem, we assumed that the temperature of the drop and, therefore, its internal energy does not change. This change can, in principle, be taken into account, but it will not change the answer. Indeed, the specific heat capacity of water is 1 cal/€·deg, and the specific heat of vaporization is 539 cal/€·deg. Consequently, if the droplet temperature is room temperature (20°C), then when it is cooled to 0°C, approximately $\frac{20}{539} \approx 0,04$ of the droplet mass can evaporate. Therefore, it is clear that taking into account the change in the internal energy of the drop cannot change the answer.
