F427. Two coils with numbers of turns $n_1 = 125$ and $n_2 = 1000$ are wound on a toridal ferromagnetic core with a diameter of $d = 5$ cm and a cross-sectional area of ​​$S = 1$ cm$^2$. The first coil carries a current $1$a, the second coil is connected to a galvanometer. When the circuit of the first coil is opened, a charge of $10^{-3}$ k passes through the galvanometer. The total resistance of the circuit of the second coil is 100 ohms. Determine the magnetic permeability of the materials from which the core is made.

M371. Each cell of the maximum board contains a cycle number from 1 to 64, with different cycles in different cells. In one question, by specifying any set of fields, you can find out the set (set) of the cycle standing on these fields. What is the minimum number of questions to find out the cycle in each cell?

Fig. 1.

Let us prove that the smallest required number of questions is 6. To do this, firstly, we will show that in 6 questions you can learn all the cycles, and, secondly, we will show that this cannot be done in less than a cycle of questions. But first, let's look at the formulation of the problem.

When asking one question, we indicate the required number of questions $A$ fields and find out which of the numbers from 1 to 64 are written in the fields from the set $A$, and which ones are written in the remaining fields (we denote the set of additional fields by $A$). Thus, each question is a certain partition of the board into two sets $A$ and $A$. We must ask several questions $(A_1, \bar{A}_1), (A_2, \bar{A}_2), \dots, (A_q, \bar{A}_q)$ so that from them we can unambiguously determine what number is written in each field, that is, in such a way that for any two different fields there must be a partition $(A_i, \bar{A}_i)$ of our $q$ that separates these two fields: one of the fields belongs to $A_i$, and the other to $\bar{A}_i$. In other words, it is necessary that the intersection of any $q$ set, the $i$th of which - $A_i$ or $\bar{A}_i$ - contains at most one field. In this case, the number of questions $q$ should be as small as possible.

1. An example of six partitions that allow you to know all the cycles is shown in figure 1 (the sets $A_i$ that we imagine asking are pink, $\bar{A}_i$ are white; $i = 1, 2, \dots, 6$). It is not difficult to make sure that our plan is correct: for the first three questions we will learn many numbers written in each line, for the last three - in each column, so as a result we will guess the number in each cell. (Here the intersection of any set of six subsets, the $i$th of which - $A_i$ or $\bar{A}_i$ - contains exactly one element!)

2. Let us prove that it is impossible to determine all the numbers in 5 questions. Let the first question be $(A_1, \bar{A}_1)$. Then in one of the set $A_1, \bar{A}_1$ (we can assume that in $A_1$ - after all, they are completely equal) there are at least 32 fields. Let the second question be $(A_2, \bar{A}_2)$. This question divides the set $A_1$ into two: $A_1 \cap A_2$ and $A_1 \cap \bar{A}_2$, and one of them (we can assume that $A_1 \cap A_2$) has at least 16 fields. And finally, after the fifth question, there will be a set $A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3 \cap A_4 \cap A_5$ of two or more “indifferent” fields. It is clear that we will not be able to find out how the numbers are arranged in this set based on the questions asked.
