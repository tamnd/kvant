If $(q + nq + mq \neq 0)$ is zero, then the main role in formula (2) will be played by the first term, and the field potential of the system of charges will be inversely proportional to the first power of the distance. If the sum of charges is zero (the system as a whole is neutral), then the second term plays the main role: the potential is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the charges to the observation point. But you can also choose such an arrangement of charges in which both the first and second terms are equal to zero. From formula (2) it is clear that for this two conditions must be met:

$$q + nq + mq = 0 \quad (3a)$$

And

$$q (na + mb) = 0. \quad (36)$$

Condition (3a) says that the total charge of the system is zero, that is, the charges cannot be of the same sign. Reducing the arbitrary charge $q$ in (3a) and (36), we obtain two equations with four parameters $n$, $m$, $a$, $b$. This means that you can choose an infinite number of options for the location and magnitude of charges that satisfy equations (3). Let us choose two parameters, for example, characterizing the arrangement of charges, as we wish. Let $b = 2a$, that is $AB = BC$. In this case, from equation (3a) and (36) we obtain $n = -2$, $m = 1$, that is, at point $A$ (see Fig. 1) there is a charge $q$, at point $B$ there is a charge $-2q$, and at point $C$ there is a charge $q$, $AB = BC = a$. Such a system of charges at large distances creates a field whose potential (see formula (2))

$$\varphi = \frac{2qa^2}{r^3} + \frac{6qa^3}{r^4} + \dots$$

The main role is played here by the first term, that is, the potential is inversely proportional to the third power of the distance.

If we require that, along with conditions (3a) and (36), the condition

$$na^2 + mb^2 = 0, \quad (3b)$$

Fig. 1.

then, as follows from formula (2), the potential will be proportional to $r^{-4}$. However, the system of equations (3a)–(3b) has no solution. Indeed, let us multiply equation (36) by $a$ and compare the resulting equation with (3b). We obtain $a = b$, after which (36) turns into equation $n + m = 0$, and (3a) leads to a contradiction $1 = 0$ (unless $q \neq 0$, and the case $q = 0$ is not of physical interest). We will obtain a similar result for all other terms in formula (2).

Thus, the three charges depicted at figure 1 can, depending on their magnitude and relative position, create a field over large distances, the potential of which will be proportional to $1/r$, $1/r^2$ or $1/r^3$.

In a similar way, we can consider the field of any system of charges. In addition, one can indicate an elegant method for constructing a system of charges created at large distances by a field with potential

$$\varphi_n = \frac{C_n}{r^{n+1}}, \quad (4)$$

where $n$ is an integer, $C_n$ is some constant depending on the magnitude of the charges and their relative position. A system of charges created by a field with potential (4) is called an electric multipole of the $n$ order or $2^n$-field. The simplest case $n = 0$—zero-order multipole—corresponds to an exact charge. In general, a $n$-order multipole contains $2^n$ charges.
