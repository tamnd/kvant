And even the straight line $AC$ is not congruent with itself, because it was mapped onto itself in such a way that the distances were not preserved: $|A_1C_1| = 2 |AC|$! Likewise, the angle $AOB$ is not congruent to itself. So,” Styopa said firmly, “the straight line may not be congruent with itself; there are straight lines that are not congruent to each other!

Who is right in this dispute?

Eternal confused Styopa Moshkin

Have you already found out who is right? Well, of course, Styopa is repeating old mistakes - he replaced the definition of congruent figures, without realizing it, with another. After all, the textbook says: “if the figure Φ can be mapped onto the figure Φ$_1$ so that the distance between any two points of the figure Φ is equal to the distance between the corresponding points of the figure Φ$_1$, then the figure Φ is said to be congruent to the figure Φ$_1$.” Otherwise, it can be said this way: if there is a mapping from one figure to another, in which the distances are preserved, then these figures are called congruent.

And Styopa decided that congruent figures are also called those that are displayed on each other in a small way while maintaining the distance! If there is a mapping of one figure to another that does not preserve distance, then such figures, Styopa believes, are incongruent. True, the word “only” was not uttered by Stepa, but it was implied by him as a matter of course.

With his example, Styopa showed that there is a mapping of a figure onto a congruent figure that does not preserve distance. And Gesha had to indicate another mapping of the line $p$ to the line $p_1$, preserving distances, say, central symmetry with the center in the middle of the segment $AA_1$, or axial symmetry with the axis, passing through the midpoints of the segments $AA_1$ and $BB_1$, or parallel translation, in which the point $A$ is mapped to point $A_1$. Of course, the angle $AOB$ is also congruent to itself; it is enough to indicate the identity mapping or axial symmetry with the axis, which is the bissertrix of the angle $AOB$.

What did Styopa determine?

Stepin’s error raises an interesting question: are there cases in which both the school definition of congruence and Stepin’s will produce the same result? In other words, are there figures that can be mapped onto one another only while preserving their distance?

To answer this question, let's start with the simplest cases. A figure is a set of points. The simplest figure is a figure consisting of one point. Let $F_1 = \{A\}, F_2 = \{B\}$, where $A$ and $B$ are points. It is clear that there is a unique mapping of the figure $F_1$ to the figure $F_2$, it maps the point $A$ to the point $B$:

$$A \rightarrow B.$$

Since the figures $F_1$ and $F_2$ each contain one point, we can talk about points $A$ to point $A$ and from point $B$ to point $B$. But $|AA| = |BB| = 0$, so all mappings from $F_1$ to $F_2$ are preserved.

Let now $F_1 = \{A, B\}, F_2 = \{C, D\}$, where $A, B, C, D$ are points.
