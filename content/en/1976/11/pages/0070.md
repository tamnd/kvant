⟦folio 68⟧

Ask - we answer

Alfiya Suleymanova, student of Drozhzhanovskaya secondary school (Staroye Drozhzhannoye village, TASSR); is inquiring whether it is possible to create so-called superaccumulators, i.e. batteries based on the phenomenon of superconductivity, and use them as energy sources.

We asked the consultant of the physics department of our journal A. Volodin to answer these questions.

Energy accumulators, the operation of which is based on the phenomenon of superconductivity, are no longer science fiction. In the laboratories of research institutes, similar devices called superconducting energy storage devices have been built and studied. In general terms, such a storage device is a superconducting coil through which an undamped current flows. The energy in it is stored in the form of magnetic field energy. In principle, this is the most compact way to store energy, since very large amounts of energy can be stored in a small volume occupied by an intense magnetic field. This cannot be said about other energy storage devices. For example, an electric energy accumulator cannot only be compact, since for the electric field there is a limiting voltage value associated with the possibility of electrical breakdown. For a magnetic field, there are no restrictions associated with “breakdown”.

The only fundamental limitation on the energy density stored in a magnetic field is due to the pressure of the magnetic field on the coil material. This pressure arises due to the interaction of the current in the coil with the magnetic field and increases in proportion to the square of the magnetic field induction. Exceeding the maximum permissible mechanical loads on the coils can lead to its destruction.

The development of durable coil designs is just one of the problems that need to be solved for the practical use of superconducting energy storage devices. Other problems are associated with the search for new superconductors with high critical parameters (i.e., a large continuous current and a high temperature of transition to the normal, non-superconducting state), with the creation of effective cryogenic systems (after all, superconductivity so far “works” only at very low temperatures, less than 250°C). Scientists are successfully working to solve these problems, and the practical use of superconducting energy batteries is a matter of the near future.
