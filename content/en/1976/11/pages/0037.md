Let us note in conclusion that although the chessboard helped us describe the example of six partitions, in fact it had nothing to do with this problem. How the example on figure 1 turned out can be explained using the double number system. If all the fields are filled in order with double numbers from 000000 to 111111 ($2^6$ is exactly equal to 64 - that’s what’s good about the board), as shown in figure 2, and the references to the set $A_i$ are those numbers whose $i$th digit is 0 (and to $\bar{A}_i$ those with 1), then we get our example. The above messages are enough to solve the problem in general form - for any number of $N$ fields (located as corners - for definition, in a row) on which any permutation of numbers from 1 to $N$ is written. If $2^{q-1} < N \leq 2^q$, then all numbers can be found out in $q$ questions, but not in $q$ - 1.

A similar use of the double system for mathematical tricks was repeatedly discussed in «Kvant» for younger schoolchildren (see «Kvant», 1976, No. 6, p. 67, articles by A. Bendukidze “On the double system of number” and No. 7, p. 55, articles by A. Orlov “Ponsk subject”).

In connection with this task, the following one will naturally arise. Let ABC be the given triangle. For what point on the plane is the sum of its distances to vertices A, B and C called? Let's solve this problem. The most interesting answer is obtained for the case when all angles $\widehat{A}, \widehat{B}, \widehat{C}$ of the triangle are less than 120° (Fig. 3).

(T1) The required point $T$ is ma for which $\widehat{ATB} = \widehat{BTC} = \widehat{CTA} = 120^\circ$. Pointa $T$ is called the "Toricelli point" of triangle ABC. A beautiful proof of Theorem (T1) uses the following Lemma 1: let $A_1B_1C_1$ be an equilateral triangle with height length $h$; The sum of the distances to the lines $A_1B_1$, $B_1C_1$ and $C_1A_1$ from any point $P$ lying inside or on the contour of the triangle is equal to $h$, and from the point $P$ lying outside the triangle is large $h$.

To prove Lemma 1, it is sufficient to replace the sum of distances from $P$ to straight distances with the sum of the area of ​​triangles $A_1PB_1$, $B_1PC_1$, $C_1PA_1$ and compare it with the area of ​​triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ (Fig. 4).

To prove (T1), let us draw through the points $A, B$ and $C$ lines perpendicular to the segments $TA, TB$ and $TC$. We get an equilateral triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ (Fig. 5). For any point $P$, the sum of the distances from $P$ to the point $A, B$ and $C$ is not less than the sum of the distances from $P$ to the straight lines $B_1C_1$, $C_1A_1$ and $A_1B_1$ - after all, the oblique is not shorter than the perpendicular - and the last sum by Lemma 1 is equal height $h$ of triangle $A_1B_1C_1$. Thus, $|AP| + |BP| + |CP| \geq h$, and at point $T$ the sum is $|AT| + |BT| + |CT| равна $h$.
