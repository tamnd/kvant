You can start induction from $N = 1$, where everything is very convenient. By word we mean below “a word of syllables.”

Induction step. Let the statement be proven for $N - 1$ classes, but we have $N$ of them. Let our fraction be cut into syllables (Fig. 8). Then either because for each syllable in a fraction (starting with some) there is a word of the first class starting with this syllable, or because there are an infinite number of syllables such that all words starting with them are not of the first class. The first point is clear. In the second layer, we will cut the whole fraction into pieces, starting with the “red” syllables, and declare these larger pieces with syllables. Since all words consisting of such enlarged syllables are not of the first class, we can now have words of no more than $(N - 1)$ different classes; and by the induction hypothesis we can cut our fraction in the required way.

The proof of the existence of the required cut presented here is non-constructive—it does not provide any general way to construct (construct) a cut for arbitrary rules that specify the sequence and partition of words into classes.

**M374. Let $a, b, c$ be positive numbers, $a > c$ and $b > c$. Prove the inequality $\sqrt{c(a-c)} + \sqrt{c(b-c)} \leq \sqrt{ab}$.**

**Fig. 9.**

We will give two solutions, the first of which is algebraic, and the second is geometric.

1. Let us put $a = (1 + \alpha)c, b = (1 + \beta)c$, where $\alpha, \beta > 0$. Then $\sqrt{c(a-c)} + \sqrt{c(b-c)} = c(\sqrt{\alpha} + \sqrt{\beta}), \sqrt{ab} = c\sqrt{(1 + \alpha)(1 + \beta)}$, and we need to prove that $\sqrt{\alpha} + \sqrt{\beta} \leq \sqrt{(1 + \alpha)(1 + \beta)}$. Let's write the obvious inequality: $(\sqrt{\alpha}\beta - 1)^2 \geq 0$, or $2\sqrt{\alpha}\beta \leq 1 + \alpha\beta$, and add $(\alpha + \beta)$ to both sides of it. We get

$$\alpha + \beta + 2\sqrt{\alpha}\beta \leq 1 + \alpha + \beta + \alpha\beta,$$

that is, $(\sqrt{\alpha} + \sqrt{\beta})^2 \leq (\sqrt{(1 + \alpha)(1 + \beta)})^2$, which is what was required.

Equality occurs if $\alpha\beta = 1$, that is, $\frac{a}{c} - 1) \left( \frac{b}{c} - 1 \right) = 1$, or $(a - c)(b - c) = c^2$, revealing $c = \frac{ab}{a + b}$.

2. Let's make a quadrilateral $ABCD$ from two equilibrium triangles with bases equal in length to the number $2\sqrt{c}$ and with sides of length $\sqrt{a}$ and $\sqrt{b}$, respectively (Fig. 9). Since the lengths of the heights dropped to the bases of these triangles are $\sqrt{a} - c$ and $\sqrt{b} - c$, respectively, the area of ​​the quadrilateral $ABCD$ is $\sqrt{c(a-c)} + \sqrt{c(b-c)}$. On the other hand, $S_{ABCD} = 2S_{ABD} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} \sin(\widehat{BAD}) \leq \sqrt{ab}$, opening and withdrawing
gives the required inequality.

The equality will be in the case $\sin(\widehat{BAD}) = 1$, that is $\widehat{BAD} = \frac{\pi}{2}$; then $|AB|^2 + |AD|^2 = |BD|^2$, or $a + b = (\sqrt{a} - c + \sqrt{b} - c)^2$, opening $c = \frac{ab}{a + b}$.
