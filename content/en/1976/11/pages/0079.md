Shita is given the following: It may seem to you that when solving some of them it is enough to refer to the “obviousness”, but such a “reference” will not be a solution to these problems. It is necessary to carry out logical reasoning based on the definition of congruous figures, say, to prove that for the figures indicated in the problems there is no mapping that transforms one of them into another and preserves the distances.

Exercise
1. Find all the figures $F_1$ and $F_2$, each of which consists of three points and which can be mapped onto each other only while preserving the distance.
2. Find all figures consisting of three points that map onto themselves only while maintaining distance.
3. Is it possible to map the set of all vertices of a square onto itself so that none of the distances between different points are preserved?
4. a) Prove that for any two congruous figures on the plane consisting of more than three points, there is a mapping of one of them to the other that does not preserve distance.
6) Prove that for any figures on a plane consisting of more than three points, there is a mapping of one of them to another that does not preserve distance.

How does the atomic nucleus work?
(For the beginning see pp. 30, 42, 55)

Europa can be considered as a nucleus consisting of a number of protons (which have unequal sizes and charges) and several neutrons (without charge and low mass). They are all kept in their places with the help of a colossal force that prevents them from flying apart and is called geography. This nucleus is non-ismetric, since it contains on its western side a very powerful proton (meaning Great Britain), which has “wave characters” unique to it. In the south there will be something that can be called a neutrino (Italy). It can be suggested that this particle also obeys the laws of quantum mechanics. It is characteristic in this regard that its central part (Rome) is eternal, while this cannot be said about the rest of the territory.

The difficulties begin with considering electrons, which orbit in orbits far from the nucleus. In this case, the role of electrons is played by colonies. They belong, so to speak, to individual protons. But some time ago the nucleus subjected them to a terrible bombardment, which caused some electron redistribution between protons. However, the influence of the proton, usually expressed in mathematical language

connection, began to behave in this regard very and very restlessly, which threatens to disrupt the stable state of the nucleus. It is possible, however, that if a given proton increases its charge (meaning, it rearms), then, although the directed state between the protons will remain, the nucleus as a whole will become more stable.

I believe that I have illuminated this extremely complex situation sufficiently and in terms that are quite accessible to physicists.
