We finally move on to problem M372 itself. Now, to completely solve it, all that remains is to take a small logical step.

Conditions M372 require proving two statements:

the necessity of the condition $\hat{C} > 120^\circ$ and it is sufficient to satisfy the inequality $|AP| + |BP| + |CP| \geq |AC| + |BC|$ for any point $P$ of the plane. We have just proved enough: this is Theorem (T2). Let us prove the necessity of condition $\hat{C} < 120^\circ$, then there exists $P = P_0$ for which this inequality is not true. Let's consider two cases. If all angles in the triangle $ABC$ are less than 120°, then, according to (T1), we can take the Toricelli point $T$ as such a point $P_0$. If one of the angles - say $\hat{A}$ - is greater than or equal to 120°, then the vertex $A$ itself can act as the point $P_0$ (since $|BC| > |AB|$).

Solving this problem requires only careful logical deductions for infinite sets. Here is the most typical example: “Let $P$ be some property of a member of the sequence $x_n$. Then either for all terms, starting from some, the property $P(x_n)$ is satisfied, or there are infinite polynomials for which $P(x_n)$ is not satisfied. Reasoning of this type is especially common in textbooks on analysis - when studying limits, etc.

a) We will call the final pieces of a sequence of numbers (natural numbers) words (for us these are real words - in the alphabet of “letters” 0, 1, ..., 9). Then, for each sign of our decimal fraction (starting from some) there is a word of the first class, starting with this sign, or there are infinite polynomials such that all words starting with them are not of the first class (that is, all of the second class).

The first case: we can (starting from a certain place) cut off one first class after another from our fraction, that is, cut the decimal fraction into words of the first class (except, perhaps, the initial word; Fig. 7).

Second case: let's mark (at figure 7 - in red) the signs of our fraction, with which words of only the second class begin. It is clear that words starting from a certain place, going to the next red letter, give the required cutting of the fraction into words of the second class.

b) Let the words be divided into $N$ classes. We will prove by induction on $N$ the statement of the problem in a somewhat successful form (as often happens, it turns out to be easier to prove more *). Namely, we will prove that if a fraction is already somehow (starting from some place $n_0$) divided into pieces - let's call them syllables - then it can be cut (starting from some place $n_1$) into words of the same class without cutting the words, that is, in such a way that each word will be made up of several words. You can easily check that for $N = 2$ this successful version is proved in exactly the same way as the previous one (the role of the “signs” of the fraction is played not by numbers, but by syllables).
