Quadratures of parabolas and hyperbolas. Calculating Curve Lengths

Do Ferma systematicne methods of quadratures, i.e., calculation of areas, were developed by the English scientist B. Cavalieri (1598-1647). He wrote only the book “Geometry of Weeks” (1635), in which he calculated the area of ​​a figure bounded by the parabola $y = x^2$, the abscissa axis and the straight line $x = 1$. Subsequently (1647) he calculated similar areas for the parabolas $y = x^3, y = x^4, \dots, y = x^9$.

No already in 1642 Ferma discovered a method for calculating the areas of figures bounded by any “parabolas” $y = ax^{p/q}$ ($p/q > 0$) and any “hyperbolas” $y = \frac{a}{x^{p/q}}$. Ferma's presentation was only 10 pages long. For the “hyperbola”, which has $\frac{p}{q} > 1$, he first calculated the area of ​​an unbounded figure located between the x-axis, the straight line $x = x_0$ and the curve (Fig. 2). Thus, it was shown that the area of ​​an unbounded figure can be finite.

Ferma, one of the first, took up the problem of straightening curves, that is, calculating the lengths of their arcs. He managed to reduce this problem to the calculation of non-ctor areas. Thus, he showed that calculating the length of other parabolas $y^2 = 2px$ from the point (0, 0) to the non-octoral point $(x_1, y_1)$ (Fig. 3) ​​is evidence of the location of the parabolas $x^2 - y^2 = p^2$, the ordinate axis, the abscissa axis and the straight line $y = y_1$ (Fig. 4).

Ferma had only one step left to move from the “area” to the abstract concept of the “intergral”. This step was taken by Newton and Leibniz after his death.
