To find the extremum of the polynomial $F(x)$, Fermat proposes the following rules: 1) substitute in $F(x)$ instead of $x$ the expression $x + h$; 2) equate $F(x)$ and $F(x + h)$: $F(x) = F(x + h)$; 3) after bringing similar terms, we reduce by $h$; 4) we assume $h = 0$. As a result, we obtain the equality $A(x) = 0$. Fermat states that all values ​​of $x$ for which the polynomial $F(x)$ has an extremum are necessarily roots of the equation $A(x) = 0$. The function $A(x)$ itself, which, according to Fermat’s rule, turns out to be purely algebraic (that is, without passing to the limit), is now called arbitrary from $F(x)$.

How does Fermat justify his rule? He offered several different rationales. Let us present one of them in a slightly modernized form. Let the polynomial $F(x)$ reach its maximum at the point $x_0$. This means that for small $h > 0$ we have $F(x_0 + h) < F(x_0)$ and $F(x_0 - h) < F(x_0)$. Arranging the terms of the polynomials $F(x_0 \pm h)$ by degree $h$, we obtain the system:

$$\begin{cases}
F(x_0) + h \cdot A(x_0) + \dots \\
\dots + h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0, \\
-A(x_0) + h \cdot B(x_0) - \dots \\
\dots + (-1)^n \cdot h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0.
\end{cases}$$

where

$$A(x_0) + h \cdot B(x_0) + \dots \\
\dots + h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0, \\
-A(x_0) + h \cdot B(x_0) - \dots \\
\dots + (-1)^n \cdot h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0.$$

But for small $h$ the sign of the sum will depend only on $A(x_0)$. Then from the first inequality it follows that $A(x_0) \leq 0$, and from the second: $A(x_0) \geq 0$. So, it is necessary that $A(x_0) = 0$.

Note that all of Fermat's methods were quite rigorous. After him, mathematicians abandoned the requirement of rigor and moved on to uncritical operations with infinitesimal quantities, which they did not know how to determine. Rigorous methods in mathematical analysis appeared new only at the beginning of the last century in the works of Gauss and Cauchy.

Fermat was the first to see his difference between problems of finding extrema and problems of determining tangents. He gave a method for finding the latter, based on the same principle as his method of extremmas. Now both methods are based on finding an arbitrary one.

Further advances in methods for determining “areas,” on the one hand, and “tangents and extrema,” on the other, consisted in establishing their mutual connection. There are indications that Fermat already knew that “area problems” and “tangent problems” are mutually inverse. But he never developed his discovery in detail. Therefore, part of it, according to the rules, belongs to I. Barrow, I. Newton and G. W. Leibnitz, who made this discovery possible to create differential and integral calculus.

If in the works we have described Fermat explored questions that were the focus of attention of other mathematicians of his time (Kepler, Cavalieri, Torricelli, Blaise Pascal, Wallis), then in number theory he was a pioneer.
