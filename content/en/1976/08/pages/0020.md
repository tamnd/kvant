Marrying abandoned bodies to spread light. Even less justified, according to Fermat, is the assertion that light should travel in a denser medium at a higher speed than in a less dense one. From all this, Fermat concludes that the formulation of the law of propagation given by Descartes is not correct.

Mersenne forwarded Fermat's review to Descartes. In order for Descartes to feel the authority of a decision whose works he was not familiar with, Mersenne also included in the letter Fermat’s work “On the Method of Finding the Most and Most Values.” This was the area of ​​mathematics in which Descartes had significant results and considered himself a pioneer.

Descartes' response to the decision was harsh and in many ways unfair. After a few emails were exchanged, each side remained unconvinced and the discussion switched to the method of finding the most and most values.

Fermat returned to the optical problem only 20 years later, after the death of Descartes. The impetus for some new ideas about the way to establish the law of propagation was a book on optics by his friend Curau de la Chambre. This book presented the conclusions of the laws of light reflection based on the “recognition of the shortest path” formulated by the ancient Greek scientist Heron (1st century AD).

In his treatise “Catoptrics,” Heron, considering the reflection of light from flat and spherical mirrors, made the following statement: light is reflected in such a way that the path of the ray falling from a given point and reflected to a given point is minimal.

However, in many cases the light does not follow this principle. In particular, when light propagates in an inhomogeneous medium, the path of light from one point to another turns out to be far from minimal.

Having again taken up the Japanese law, Fermat decided to try to visualize Heron's principle. Its main idea is as follows. The “shortest” paths chosen by light should be understood as the “easiest” ones, as the paths along which the light experiences the least “sport” from the medium in which it propagates. Fermat did not give a clear physical explanation for this “sporting”. The fact is that he considered the propagation of light to be instantaneous. The introduction of the concept of “sports” undoubtedly evoked the idea of ​​the spread of light in time. Fermat, of course, understood this, but he was speaking primarily as a mathematician. And bypassing this question with vague speculations about the “antipathy” of light to matter, Fermat moves on to a purely mathematical formulation of the problem.

Let a ray of light leaving point A in medium I (Fig. 1) hit point B in medium II. Of course, the shortest path between these points is straight line AB. In this case, the light would pass the boundary of the media at point D. But if the “sporting” of media I and II is different, then the path ADB may not be the easiest for light.
