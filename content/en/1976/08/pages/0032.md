Experiments with powder figures

What does dust—fine, loose powder—have to do with physics?
If you think that a physicist will only remember about dust in those not very pleasant moments when he needs to clean his experimental setup, then you are deeply mistaken. It is impossible even to list everything that the study of the properties of fine powders and their suspensions (suspensions) has given science and technology, or to indicate those areas where these properties have found application. Here are some examples.

Using the powder, the length of a sound wave in air was measured quite accurately for the first time; The powder is still used in the study of sound vibrations of various plates and membranes. Powder suspended in a liquid allows molecular motion to occur (think Brownian motion).

Metal powder is used to observe ferromagnetic domains. Ferromagnetic powders are used in flaw detection.

Colossal installations for collecting dust are being developed and used (knowledge of the properties of dust is indispensable here). But equally intensive work is underway to create installations that disperse various substances.

Any experiments with fine powders are interesting in themselves. In addition, they are also respectable because they show how much the study of common substances, to which we often do not pay attention, gives.

We offer you several simple but very effective experiments. They do not require any equipment, so you can do them even at home.

Obtaining powder figures

Spread an even thin layer of Protacryl dental plastic powder (sold in all pharmacies) onto an aluminum plate. Let's connect the plastic conductor to a capacitor (capacitor capacity $C = 0,01$ uF, operating voltage 400 $\theta$). We attach the second capacitor to the conductor with the plug. Let's plug the plug into one of the sockets of the electrical lighting network (127 or 220 $\theta$). We run our finger over the powder poured onto the aluminum plate - there is a trace on the residual plate (Fig. 1).

Let's try to bind the result of experience. Alternating electric current is supplied to the apartment's sockets through two wires. One of these wires is replaced - its potential is equal to the ground potential, which is usually considered equal to zero. The human body is also more or less well replaced, so its potential can also be taken to be zero. The aluminum plate is connected through a capacitor to other wires of the network, which is called phase. The capacitor “passes” alternating current. Thus, between the finger passing through the powder and the aluminum plate itself there is a variable potential difference.
