In 1631, Fermat married his distant relative on his mother’s side, Lunza de Long. Pierre and Lunza had five children, of whom the eldest, Samuel, became a poet and scientist. We owe him the first collected works of Pierre Fermat, published in 1679. Pierre Fermat died on January 12, 1665 during one of his business trips.

Here is a list of those dry facts that we know about the life of this wonderful mathematician.

Unfortunately, Samuel Fermat left no memories of his father. True, the life of a scientist, as a rule, is poor in external events. Its main content is revealed only in creativity, which constitutes a huge spiritual feat of the scientist.

None of Fermat's works were published during his lifetime. The collected works that he repeatedly tried to prepare were never written by him. However, he attached several treatises to a fully finished form, and they became known in manuscript to most of the scientists of his time (these were treatises on analytical geometry, on maxima and minima, and on the squaring of parabolas and hyperbolas). In addition to these treatises, his extensive and extremely interesting correspondence remains.

In the 17th century, when there were no special scientific journals yet (“The Journal of Scientists” was one of the first, it began publishing in 1665), correspondence between scientists played a special role. It posed problems, reported methods for solving them, and discussed pressing scientific issues.

Fermat's correspondents were the greatest mathematicians of his time: P. Descartes, Etben and Blaise Pascal, B. Frenicle de Bessy, X. Huygens, E. Torricelli, J. Wallis. Letters were sent either directly to the addressee or to Abbot Mersenne in Paris, who multiplied them and sent them to mathematicians working on analytical issues. But the letters were almost never just short mathematical topics. The community in them reveals the living feelings of the authors, which help to recreate their images.

When Fermat sent Mersenne his “Method of Finding Maxima and Minima” in 1636, Descartes, not understanding Fermat’s method, sharply criticized it. In June 1638, Fermat sent Mersenne a new, more detailed statement of his method to forward to Descartes. A letter to its contents, but not without internal irony: “... Thus, it turns out that either I explained myself, or Mr. Descartes understood my Latin work... I still sent him what I had already written, and he will undoubtedly find things there that will help him open up from his opinion that I found this method by accident and its true foundations are unknown to me...” The farm never changes its capable tone. He feels his depth as a mathematician, so he does not get involved in petty polemics, but patiently tries to explain his method, as a teacher would do to a student.

Fermat is known to the general public (further distant from mathematics) primarily thanks to the Great Theorem, which bears his name. However, Fermat deals not only with his most beloved number theory, to which this theorem relates, but also with mathematical problems that were the focus of attention of scientists of the 17th century, namely the problem of determining maxima and minima, finding tangents, calculating the areas and lengths of arcs of curves, in short - those questions that we now refer to as mathematical analysis.
