One of Diophantus’s problems led him to this question. In it, conditions were formed that must satisfy the number $a$ in order for it to be represented by formula (1); the co-writers, not understanding the meaning of these conditions, reproduced them incorrectly. By the time of Fermat, the text was hopelessly damaged, and in order to restore it, he had to solve the problem of the commandment.

The reader can easily prove that numbers of the form $4n + 3$ cannot be represented by formula (1). On the other hand, numbers of the form $4n + 1$ can be either representable (for example, $5 = 1^2 + 2^2$) or non-representable (for example, 21). Fermat agreed that we first need to investigate which prime numbers are represented by formula (1). He discovered that all prime numbers of the form $4n + 1$ are represented by formula (1), and, moreover, in a unique way. (This statement was later called the first addition to the law of reciprocity.) Fermat’s own proof has not reached us. None of his contemporaries were able to verify it either. The first proof was given only by Leonhard Euler.

Having established the law of representability of prime numbers, Fermat easily proved that the number $a$ is representable by formula (1) if and only if its decomposition into prime factors does not include any prime number of the form $4n + 3$ to an odd degree. The guiding thread for him was Diophantus’s last statement that the product of two numbers representable by formula (1) is also representable, and in two different ways, by formula (1):

$$\begin{align*}
(x^2 + y^2)(u^2 + v^2) &= (xu + yv)^2 + (xv - yu)^2 \\
&= (xu - yv)^2 + (xv + yu)^2.
\end{align*}$$

From formula (1), Fermat moved on to consider the form $x^2 + 2y^2$, $x^2 - 2y^2$, $x^2 + 3y^2$. He found the type of prime numbers that can be represented by each of these formulas. For the formula $x^2 + 2y^2$ these will be prime numbers of the form $8n + 1$ and $8n + 3$. Prime numbers of the form $8n + 5$ and $8n + 7$ cannot be represented by such formulas. This proposal is called "much additional to the law of reciprocity." It was proven by J. L. Lagrange (1736-1813).

These works of Fermat laid the foundation for the fruitful research of Euler, Lagrange and Legendre. The entire previous development of this issue was summed up by Gauss, who at the beginning of the last century created a harmonious theory of quadratic forms, that is, a form of the form $ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2$, where $a, b, c$ are integers. It turns out that the question of which prime numbers are representable by quadratic forms with the same discripant $D = ac - b^2$ is completely solved using the quadratic reciprocity law. The law of reciprocity was discovered by Euler, and then rediscovered and proven by Gauss *).
