Fermat himself left a proof of the Last Theorem for four powers.

In his proof, Fermat used the “method of indefinite or infinite descent.” He described this method in a letter to Karkavi (August 1659) as follows:

“If there were a certain right triangle for numerical purposes, which would have an area equal to a square, then there would be another triangle, smaller than this, which would have the same properties. If there were a second, smaller than the first, which had the same property, then, by virtue of a similar reasoning, there would exist a third, smaller than the second, which would have the same property, and, finally, a fourth, a fifth, descending to infinity. But, if a number is given, then there is no infinity in the descent of smaller numbers (I always mean whole *) numbers). Whence it follows that there is no right triangle with a square area.”

It was by this method that many propositions of number theory were proven.

The Great Theorem for $n=4$, and 20 years later for $n=3$. The fact is that he looked at the last proof only with the help of completely new ideas, namely, a generalization of the concept of an integer. We are accustomed to associate this concept only with natural numbers, but it turns out that there are other mathematical lenses that behave like integers. Among these objects one can distinguish “prime numbers” and develop arguments similar to the usual one. Such numbers are now called “algebraic integers.” In the last century, E. Kummer, working on the Great Theorem, constructed arguments for algebraic integers of a certain type. This allowed him to prove the Great Theorem for a certain class of prime exponents $n$. Currently, the validity of the Great Theorem has been verified for all exponents $n \leq 5500$.

Let us also note that the Great Theorem is connected not only with algebraic theory, but also with algebraic geometry, which is now being developed in an integrated way *).

In his letter to Carcavi, who after Mersenne’s death took his place in the group of Paryazh mathematicians, Fermat wrote:

“Perhaps posterity will recognize me for showing him that the Ancients did not know everything, and this may bring into the consciousness of those who come after me for the traditio lampadis ad filios**), as the Great Chancellor of England *** says), following the feeling and motto of which I will add: Multi pertransibunt et augebitur scientia ****.”

This letter was called the “Testament of Fermat.”
