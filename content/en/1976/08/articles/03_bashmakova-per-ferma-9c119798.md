In this issue we celebrate the anniversary of the greatest mathematician of the 17th century, Pierre Fermat. Fermat contributed to almost every branch of mathematics in the 17th century. His works predated the advent of infinitesimal calculus and probability theory. Fermat can deservedly be considered one of the creators of analytical geometry. But his work in number theory is especially important.

Martynov, Fermat’s analysis polynomials are formulated and proven. Finally, in the article by L. And Fermat approached this problem from a purely mathematical point of view: in it he applied his method of solving extremal problems. In essence, this was the first application in physics of that branch of mathematics that is now called calculus of variations.

In 1631, Fermat married his distant relative on his mother’s side, Lunza de Long. Pierre and Lunza had five children, of whom the eldest, Samuel, became a poet and scientist. We owe him the first collected works of Pierre Fermat, published in 1679. Pierre Fermat died on January 12, 1665 during one of his business trips.

Here is a list of those dry facts that we know about the life of this wonderful mathematician.

Unfortunately, Samuel Fermat left no memories of his father. True, the life of a scientist, as a rule, is poor in external events. Its main content is revealed only in creativity, which constitutes a huge spiritual feat of the scientist.

None of Fermat's works were published during his lifetime. The collected works that he repeatedly tried to prepare were never written by him. However, he attached several treatises to a fully finished form, and they became known in manuscript to most of the scientists of his time (these were treatises on analytical geometry, on maxima and minima, and on the squaring of parabolas and hyperbolas). In addition to these treatises, his extensive and extremely interesting correspondence remains.

In the 17th century, when there were no special scientific journals yet (“The Journal of Scientists” was one of the first, it began publishing in 1665), correspondence between scientists played a special role. It posed problems, reported methods for solving them, and discussed pressing scientific issues.

Fermat's correspondents were the greatest mathematicians of his time: P. Descartes, Etben and Blaise Pascal, B. Frenicle de Bessy, X. Huygens, E. Torricelli, J. Wallis. Letters were sent either directly to the addressee or to Abbot Mersenne in Paris, who multiplied them and sent them to mathematicians working on analytical issues. But the letters were almost never just short mathematical topics. The community in them reveals the living feelings of the authors, which help to recreate their images.

When Fermat sent Mersenne his “Method of Finding Maxima and Minima” in 1636, Descartes, not understanding Fermat’s method, sharply criticized it. In June 1638, Fermat sent Mersenne a new, more detailed statement of his method to forward to Descartes. A letter to its contents, but not without internal irony: “... Thus, it turns out that either I explained myself, or Mr. Descartes understood my Latin work... I still sent him what I had already written, and he will undoubtedly find things there that will help him open up from his opinion that I found this method by accident and its true foundations are unknown to me...” The farm never changes its capable tone. He feels his depth as a mathematician, so he does not get involved in petty polemics, but patiently tries to explain his method, as a teacher would do to a student.

Fermat is known to the general public (further distant from mathematics) primarily thanks to the Great Theorem, which bears his name. However, Fermat deals not only with his most beloved number theory, to which this theorem relates, but also with mathematical problems that were the focus of attention of scientists of the 17th century, namely the problem of determining maxima and minima, finding tangents, calculating the areas and lengths of arcs of curves, in short - those questions that we now refer to as mathematical analysis. Analytical geometry

One of Fermat's first mathematical works was the restoration of two approved books of Apollonius "On flat places". We would now classify the methods that Apollonius used as analytical and design geometry. However, at the time of Apollonius there was no letter symbolism, so he had to write down algebraic formulas and equations of geometric curves. For example, our formula $(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2$ was written down (and proven!) by the ancients using a drawing (Fig. 1).

The main difference between modern methods for solving geometric problems and Apollonius’ methods is the use of letter algebra. The first person to understand how the new algebra should be applied to problems in geometry was Fermat.

To find the extremum of the polynomial $F(x)$, Fermat proposes the following rules: 1) substitute in $F(x)$ instead of $x$ the expression $x + h$; 2) equate $F(x)$ and $F(x + h)$: $F(x) = F(x + h)$; 3) after bringing similar terms, we reduce by $h$; 4) we assume $h = 0$. As a result, we obtain the equality $A(x) = 0$. Fermat states that all values ​​of $x$ for which the polynomial $F(x)$ has an extremum are necessarily roots of the equation $A(x) = 0$. The function $A(x)$ itself, which, according to Fermat’s rule, turns out to be purely algebraic (that is, without passing to the limit), is now called arbitrary from $F(x)$.

How does Fermat justify his rule? He offered several different rationales. Let us present one of them in a slightly modernized form. Let the polynomial $F(x)$ reach its maximum at the point $x_0$. This means that for small $h > 0$ we have $F(x_0 + h) < F(x_0)$ and $F(x_0 - h) < F(x_0)$. Arranging the terms of the polynomials $F(x_0 \pm h)$ by degree $h$, we obtain the system:

$$\begin{cases}
F(x_0) + h \cdot A(x_0) + \dots \\
\dots + h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0, \\
-A(x_0) + h \cdot B(x_0) - \dots \\
\dots + (-1)^n \cdot h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0.
\end{cases}$$

where

$$A(x_0) + h \cdot B(x_0) + \dots \\
\dots + h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0, \\
-A(x_0) + h \cdot B(x_0) - \dots \\
\dots + (-1)^n \cdot h^{n-1} \cdot Q(x_0) < 0.$$

But for small $h$ the sign of the sum will depend only on $A(x_0)$. Then from the first inequality it follows that $A(x_0) \leq 0$, and from the second: $A(x_0) \geq 0$. So, it is necessary that $A(x_0) = 0$.

Note that all of Fermat's methods were quite rigorous. After him, mathematicians abandoned the requirement of rigor and moved on to uncritical operations with infinitesimal quantities, which they did not know how to determine. Rigorous methods in mathematical analysis appeared new only at the beginning of the last century in the works of Gauss and Cauchy.

Fermat was the first to see his difference between problems of finding extrema and problems of determining tangents. He gave a method for finding the latter, based on the same principle as his method of extremmas. Now both methods are based on finding an arbitrary one.

Further advances in methods for determining “areas,” on the one hand, and “tangents and extrema,” on the other, consisted in establishing their mutual connection. There are indications that Fermat already knew that “area problems” and “tangent problems” are mutually inverse. But he never developed his discovery in detail. Therefore, part of it, according to the rules, belongs to I. Barrow, I. Newton and G. W. Leibnitz, who made this discovery possible to create differential and integral calculus.

**Number Theory**

If in the works we have described Fermat explored questions that were the focus of attention of other mathematicians of his time (Kepler, Cavalieri, Torricelli, Blaise Pascal, Wallis), then in number theory he was a pioneer. None of his contemporaries and none of the mathematicians who lived after him up to Euler understood either the meaning of the gaps he raised or their internal connection.

Two large works devoted to questions of number theory remain from antiquity: Euclid’s “Principia”

⟦folio 8⟧

(III century BC) and “Arithmetic” of Diophantus (apparently, mid-3rd century AD). The first of them outlined the elements of arithmetic of integers, proved the uniqueness of decomposition into prime factors and the infinity of the set of prime numbers.

Disfant's "Arithmetic" still seems to be one of the most mysterious phenomena in the history of science. In its style it differs sharply from the classical works of Euclid, Archimedes and Apollo. It introduced notations for the unknown and its first six positive and negative powers; In addition, negative numbers and a negative sign for them, corresponding to our minus, were introduced there. When solving problems, “number” was understood not as a natural number, as it was before, but any rational one. Of the 13 books that make up Arithmetic, 6 have reached us *). They all involve solving indefinite equations in positive rational numbers. These books do not contain number theorems in the proper sense of the word, but when solving problems, restrictions were sometimes imposed on certain integers included in the problem conditions. Essentially, each such “constraint” is represented by a theorem of number theory.

For a long time, the replacement book of Diophantus was not known in Europe. But in the 16th century. its manuscript was found in the Batikana library and at the end of the same century its Latin translation was published (with many “dark places”, since the translator was not familiar with mathematics). In 1621, a new translation of Basshe de Meziric was published, which paralleled the Greek text and Basshe's comments. This book became real for Fermat. He read many of his theorems from it, others were inspired by his thoughts about certain problems, and he wrote down his thoughts and discoveries in the margins of this book. As a result, all these comments were published *). They form a significant part of his legacy in number theory. His other results in this area are written in letters. In general, he posed them as problems to other mathematicians.

Fermat himself wrote: “Arithmetic has its own field, the theory of integers, this theory was only slightly touched upon by Euclid and was not sufficiently developed by his successors (unless it was contained in those books of Diophantus, which the ravages of time have deposited); arithmeticians, therefore, must develop or renew it.”

Despite the lack of evidence (of which only one has survived), it is difficult to overestimate the importance of Fermat’s work for number theory. He alone managed to isolate from the chaos of problems and particular questions that immediately arise before a researcher when studying the properties of integers, those problems that became central to the entire classical theory of numbers. He is also responsible for the discovery of a powerful general method for proving number-theoretic propositions - the so-called method of indefinite, or infinite, descent (see below). Therefore, Fermat can rightfully be considered the founder of number theory.

Fermat's Little Theorem

In a letter to Frenicle de Bessy dated October 18, 1640, Fermat made the following statements: if the number $a$ is not divisible by the prime number $p$, then there is an exponent $\lambda$ such that $a^{\lambda} - 1$ is divisible by $p$, with $\lambda$ being a divisor of the number $p - 1$. In particular, $a^{p-1} - 1$ is always divisible by $p$. This statement is called Fermat's little theorem. It is fundamental in all elementary number theory.
