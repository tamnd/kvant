Lorenzo Romano Amedeo Carlo Avogadro di Quaregna Eddie Cerreto was born on August 9, 1776 in Turin, the capital of the Italian province. Piedmont in the family of judicial officer Filipto Avogadro. Amedeo was the third of all children. Since the 12th century, his ancestors had been lawyers in the service of the Catholic Church, and according to the tradition of that time, their professions and positions were inherited. When it was time to choose a profession, Amedeo also took up legal law. He quickly succeeded in this science and already at the age of twenty received the degree of Doctor of Church Jurisprudence.

Amedeo was not interested in legal practice; his interests were far from legal. In his youth, he briefly attended the so-called school of geometry and experimental physics. It was she who awakened in him a love for these sciences. But, not having received enough systematic knowledge, he was forced to educate himself. When he was already 25 years old, he began to devote all his free time to the study of physical and mathematical sciences. Hard work and realism bore fruit: in 1803 and 1804, he, together with his brother Fellze, presented two works, enlightened theories of electrical and electrochemical phenomena, to the Turin Academy of Sciences, for which he was elected in 1804 as a corresponding member of this academy. In his first work, entitled “Analytical Note on Electricity,” he explained the behavior of conductors and dielectrics in an electric field, in particular, the phenomenon of polarization of dielectrics. The ideas he expressed were then more fully developed in the works of other scientists.

In 1806, Avogadro received a position as a tutor in the city of Turin, and then, in 1809, he was transferred as a teacher of physics and mathematics in the city of Vercelli, where he worked for about ten years. During this period, he becomes acquainted with a huge amount of scientific literature, making numerous extracts from the books and journal articles he read. These extracts, which he did not stop reading until the end of his days, amounted to 75 volumes of approximately 700 pages each!
The contents of these volumes testify to the encyclopedic interests of Avogadro, the colossal work that he did, “re-qualified” from a lawyer to a physicist.

In September 1819, Avogadro was elected a member of the Turin Academy of Sciences. By this time, he had already gained fame among his colleagues for his work in the field of molecular theory, electricity and chemistry. In 1820, by royal decrees, Avogadro was appointed the first professor of the new department of higher physics (we would say mathematical physics) at the University of Turin.

Avogadro's views on the teaching of physics, expressed by him when taking up this position, are interesting. Italian science at this time was still very poorly developed. In an effort to help his homeland equalize the level of development of natural sciences with other European countries, Avogadro outlined a broad plan of action. His main idea was the need to combine teaching with scientific activity. To do this, it is necessary to organize two classrooms at the physics department - one for training sessions, the other for testing scientific discoveries and conducting original research. This is especially important for those students who decide to devote themselves to teaching in the field of physical and mathematical sciences. Scientific office share

wives be equipped with modern equipment and appropriate staff. It is also very important that the necessary amounts are regulated for the purchase of new equipment, which the head of the department could spend at his own discretion, submitting only a written report on the expenses incurred. In addition, there is a need for a scientific journal in which local physicists can publish their research.

These program ideas were not realized due to military and political events in Italy in the early twenties. In 1822, after student unrest, the University of Turin was closed by the authorities for a whole year, and a number of its new departments, including the department of higher physics, were LINKed. However, in 1923, Avogadro received the honorary title of emeritus professor of higher physics and was appointed senior inspector in the chamber for control of public expenditures (a position of financial and legal research, scales away from science. Let us remember, however, that Isaac Newton was the director of the mint for many years!). Despite his new responsibilities, Avogadro continued to engage in scientific research.

In 1832, the University of Turin again received the chair of higher physics, but it was offered not to Avogadro, but to the famous French mathematician Augustin Louis Cauchy, who left his homeland in 1830. Only two years later, after Cauchy’s departure, Avogadro was able to take this chair, where he worked until 1850. This year he left the university, transferring the department to his student Feltsa Kew.

Avogadro arranged his family life quite late, when he was already over thirty. While working in Vercelli, he met his future wife Anna Marnea Maciedi Giuseppe, daughter of Potarius,

From this marriage he had eight children—two sons and six daughters. None of them inherited his profession.

Contemporaries in their memoirs depicted Avogadro as a man with very sons and six daughters. They note his friendliness and sincerity in dealing with other people. “Highly educated without pedagogy, wise without a person, despising luxury, not caring about wealth, not striving for honors, indifferent to his own merits and his own fame, modest, knowledgeable, benevolent” - this is how one of his contemporaries characterizes Avogadro. In his indifference to honors, he represented a rare temptation among scientists of that time.

After leaving the university, Avogadro briefly held the position of senior inspector of the Chamber of Control, as well as a member of the Higher Statistical Commission, the Higher Council of Public Education and the representation of the Commission of Weights and Measures. Despite his advanced age, he continued to publish his research in the proceedings of the Turin Academy of Sciences. His last work was published three years before his death, when Avogadro turned 77 years old. He died in Turin on July 9, 1856 and was buried in the family crypt in Vercelli. The year after Avogadro's death, in recognition of his services to science, his bronze statue was installed at the University of Turin.

How did this wonderful man enrich science, in particular physics and chemistry, during his life?

It is not surprising that Avogadro began his scientific career with the study of electrical phenomena. Electricity and magnetism have long attracted the attention of scientists. This interest is especially

intensified after Volta invented the first source of electric current (voltaic) in 1800, and also in connection with the debate between Galvani and Volta about the nature of electricity. These questions were at the forefront of science at the time, and it was natural that young Avogadro decided to try his hand here. Avogadro's works, devoted to various problems of electricity, appeared until 1846. He also paid great attention to research in the field of electrochemistry, trying to find a connection between electrical and chemical phenomena, which led him to create his electrochemical theory. In this regard, his research came into contact with the works of the famous chemists Davy and Bercellius. But Avogadro entered the history of physics as the discoverer of one of the most important laws of molecular physics.

Almost until the very end of the 18th century, chemistry did not know any quantitative laws. The first such law was the law of conservation of mass

At the beginning of the 19th century, two more laws were discovered - the law of constancy of composition and the law of multiple ratios. The first of them argued that no matter how a given chemical compound was obtained, its composition is always the same. In other words, during the formation of a given complex substance, the elements of which it consists are always combined with each other in such a way that their masses are in a strictly defined ratio. For example, in water the mass ratio of hydrogen and oxygen is always 1:8.

To explain these laws, the famous English chemist Dalton put forward the hypothesis that all processes of matter (elements) consist of simple atoms, and complex ones - of “complex atoms”, which during chemical reactions can decompose into atoms of simple substances. Before this, there was no clear distinction between the concepts of “atom” and “molecule” in chemistry. Both of these concepts were merged into the term “particle,” which was considered as a structureless unit of matter. Dalton compiled the first table of relative atomic masses of elements, taking the mass of hydrogen per unit. (As Avogadro later showed, this table was incorrect, since Dalton believed that hydrogen gas participates in reactions in the form of a monatomic substance (H), but in reality the reactions occur with the participation of molecular hydrogen (H₂). For the same reason, Dalton’s ideas about the composition of molecules turned out to be incorrect.)

According to Dalton's ideas, these reactions should look like this:

$$2\text{H} + \text{O} = \text{H}_2\text{O}, \quad \text{H} + \text{Cl} = \text{HCl}.$$

In this case, one volume of water vapor and hydrogen chloride should be formed. So it was impossible to reconcile Gay-Lussac’s experimental data with Dalton’s theory.

It was in this situation that Avogadro’s article “Essay on a method for determining the relative masses of the elements of the molecules of bodies and the proportions according to which they are included in compounds” appeared in 1811. Outlining the basic concepts of molecular theory, Avogadro showed that it not only does not contradict the data obtained by Gay-Lussac, but, in its direction, perfectly agrees with it and opens up the possibility of accurately determining atomic masses, the composition of molecules and the nature of the chemical reactions occurring. To do this, first of all, it is necessary to assume that the molecules of hydrogen, oxygen, chlorine and some other simple substances consist not of one, but of two atoms.

In the same work, Avogadro came to the following important conclusion: “... the number... of molecules is always the same in equal volumes of any gases.” (Of course, the volumes are changed at the same pressures and temperatures.)

He further wrote that there is now “a means of very easily determining the relative masses of the molecules of bodies that can be obtained in the gaseous state, and in relation to the number of molecules in the compounds.”

In fact, the ratio of the masses of molecules is the same as the ratio of the densities of gases at the same pressures and temperatures. If we have two different gases whose masses are $M_1$ and $M_2$, and whose volumes change at the same temperatures and pressures, respectively

$$V_1 \text{ и } V_2,$$

then, following Avogadro, we can write the following correspondence:

$$\frac{M_1}{M_2} = \frac{m_1 N_1}{m_2 N_2},$$

(1)

where $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of molecules of the first and second gases, and $N_1$ and $N_2$ are the number of such molecules in volumes $V_1$ and $V_2$. Dividing the numerators in this expression by $V_1$, and the denominators by $V_2$, we get:

$$\frac{\frac{M_1}{V_1}}{\frac{M_2}{V_2}} = \frac{m_1 \frac{N_1}{V_1}}{m_2 \frac{N_2}{V_2}}.$$

(2)

But $M_1/V_1$ and $M_2/V_2$ are the densities of gases $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$, and $N_1/V_1$ and $N_2/V_2$ are the number of molecules per unit volume. According to Avogadro $\frac{N_1}{V_1} = \frac{N_2}{V_2}$. In this case, correspondence (2) takes the following form:

$$\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} = \frac{m_1}{m_2}.$$

Avogadro gives the following example: the densities of oxygen and hydrogen are 1.10359 and 0.07321 (the density of atmospheric air is taken as one). Consequently, the ratio of the masses of oxygen and hydrogen molecules takes $\frac{1,10359}{0,07321} \approx \frac{15}{1}$.

From Gay-Lussac's experiments it follows that for the formation of any two identical volumes of water vapor, two identical volumes of hydrogen and one volume of oxygen are needed. This means that for every oxygen molecule there are two hydrogen molecules. If the molecules of hydrogen and oxygen consisted of one atom, then we would have a reaction $2\text{H} + \text{O} = \text{H}_2\text{O}$, as a result of which two volumes of hydrogen and one volume of oxygen would produce one volume of water. But this contradicts the results of Gay-Lussac's experiments. Therefore, we have to assume

that in each elementary reaction act two molecules of water (2H₂O) appear, and molecules of oxygen and hydrogen contain two atoms (O₂ and H₂). So we arrive at the correct reaction formula:

$$2H_2 + O_2 = 2H_2O.$$

This well-known formula was first obtained by Avogadro.

In 1814, Avogadro's second article appeared, “Essay on the relative masses of the molecules of simple bodies, or the estimated densities of their gases, and on the constitution of some of their compounds.” Here Avogadro's law is clearly formulated: “... equal volumes of gaseous substances at equal pressures and temperatures correspond to an equal number of molecules, so that the densities of different gases represent a measure of the masses of the molecules of the corresponding gases.” The rest of the article discusses applications of this law to determine the composition of the molecules of numerous inorganic substances.

Since molar mass (the mass of one mole of a substance) is proportional to the mass of an individual molecule, Avogadro's law can be formulated as the statement that a mole of any substance in the gaseous state at the same temperature and pressure occupies the same volume. As experiments have shown, under normal conditions ($p=1\text{ amm}, t=0^\circ\text{C}$) it is equal to 22.414 liters. The number of molecules in a gram-moleculeULE of any substance is the same. It is called Avogadro's number. It is usually denoted by the letter $N_A$. According to modern data

$$N_A = 6,022094 \cdot 10^{23} \text{ mol}^{-1}.$$

This number is one of the most important universal constants of modern physics and chemistry. It is used in determining a number of other universal constants, for example, Boltzmann's constant, Faraday's constant, etc.

Avogadro's number can be determined by many independent methods. The excellent coincidence of the values ​​obtained is convincing evidence of the real molecules and the validity of the molecular kinetic theory.

In 1821, in the article “New communications on the theory of certain proportions in compounds and on the determination of the masses of molecules of bodies,” Avogadro divided the result of his ten years of work in the field of molecular theory and extended his method of determining the composition of molecules to a whole range of organic substances. In the same article, he showed that other chemists, primarily Dalton, Davy and Berzeluus, who were not familiar with his work, continued to hold incorrect views on the nature of many chemical compounds and the nature of the reactions between them.

This work is interesting in another respect: in it for the first time the name of Ampere appears, in the words of Avogadro, “one of the most skillful physicists of our day,” in connection with his research in the field of molecular theory. This side of Ampere's work is usually not mentioned, since his services in the field of electronic mathematics overshadow all other works. However, Ampère also worked in the field of molecular physics and independently of Avogadro (but somewhat later) when he approached some of the ideas expressed by Avogadro. In 1814, Ampère published a letter to the chemist Bertholla, in which he formulated a position that essentially coincided with Avogadro’s law. Here he points out that the corresponding work of Avogadro became known to him after writing a letter to Bertolla.

To finish the story about Avogadro’s work in the field of molecular theory, we note that in 1837-1841 he published a four-volume work, “Physics of Possible Bodies, or Practiata on the General Constitution of Bodies.”
