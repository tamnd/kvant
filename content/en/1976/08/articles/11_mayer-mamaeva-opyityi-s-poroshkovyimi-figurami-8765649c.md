The described experimental setup can be seen. Let's replace the aluminum plate with electrically conductive paper. It's not difficult to prepare. A large sheet of paper (approximately 20x30 cm in size) should be covered on one side with thickly diluted black ink. After the paint dries, a thin electrically conductive layer is formed, which has a fairly high resistance. A sheet of electrically conductive paper should be attached to the plywood with thumbnails. The conductor can be connected to electrically conductive paper using a regular paper clip.

Instead of Protacryl plastic powder, it is better to use dry tooth powder in experiments - the intermittent mark is more stable. It can be applied to electrically conductive paper in a thin, even layer using a cotton swab. If necessary, tooth powder can be dried on an electric stove by pouring the powder into a metal jar. You can also use linkopodium or "sulfur color" (finely ground sulfur). The first powder is sold in pharmacies, the second can be found in the school chemistry lab.

What can powder figures be used for?

To answer this question, consider two experiments. Let's unfold the conductor going from the capacitor to the electrically conductive paper. We will insert a dielectric into the conductor gap: for example, we will wrap the cleaned ends of the wires around a glass tube several times so that the distance between the turns is 1-2 cm. Glass is a very good dielectric through which electronic current *practically* does not pass (or rather, very little current passes). But a broken trace appeared on the paper! Consequently, extremely small currents can be recorded using powder figures.

Let's do one more experiment. In the gap of the conductor coming from the capacitor to the electron-conducting paper, we turn on a switch (toggle switch), which allows us to blur and close the circuit. Run the wires over the powder with your finger, turn the toggle switch on and off quickly, without stopping the movement of your finger. In this case, while the toggle switch is turned on, the trace becomes intermittent. We will call the resulting light or dark areas along the trajectory of the finger movement temporary marks. The time elapsed from the moment of the appearance of one mark to the moment of the appearance of the next one should be 0.02 seconds (alternating current particle $f=1/T=50$ sch). Thus, by counting the number of marks on the trajectory of the finger, you can determine the time during which the toggle switch was turned on.

In experiment it is convenient to use a voltage divider (see Fig. 2). The fact is that bodies that we are accustomed to consider non-conductors are not dielectrics in the full sense of the word. Therefore, even when the toggle switch that breaks the phase wire is turned off, a small current flows through the circuit, and powder figures appear on the paper. To establish this undesirable phenomenon, it is necessary to reduce the potential difference between the electron conductive paper and the finger. A voltage divider (potentiometer) allows you to select the optimal potential difference. Note that it is more convenient to carry out this experiment together (you can invite your friend to check his reaction to turning the toggle switch on and off).

So, the described experiments show that powder figures can be used to detect small currents and changes in small periods of time.

Telery - a few specific examples.

**Air ionization by flame**

We insert two brass or hard strips approximately $10 \times 80$ mm in size into the gap in the phase conductor of the network, made after the capacitor. We will strengthen the resulting electrons in matchboxes, which will act as tents. Let's arrange the electrons parallel to each other so that the gap between them is 2-5 mm (Fig. 4). Let's run our finger along the layer of tooth powder on the electron-guided paper - the trace is continuous, without time marks.

Let's bring the flame of a lit match or a burning candle into the gap between the electrons and check it again with a finger on the paper. Immediately, temporary marks appear on the trajectory of the finger movement (see Fig. 4).

In this and similar experiments, it is convenient to innocently move your finger in a circle; then it will not be difficult to notice the moment of appearance and disappearance of time marks. Long-term experience shows that under normal conditions air is a dielectric. The introduced flame ionizes the air, making it a conductor. The electrical circuit closes, and an electric current appears in the circuit.

**Measurement of gravity acceleration**

It is possible to measure short periods of time using powder figures and use them in experiments to determine the acceleration of gravity.

If a body raised above the ground begins to fall, then, as is known from mechanics, let it pass $h$ in time $t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}$. Therefore, the acceleration of free fall can be found by the formula

$$g = \frac{2h}{t^2},$$

(*)

if the height $h$ from which the body falls and the time of fall $t$ are known. The height can be measured with a ruler, and the time of fall can be determined using the powder figure method.

A diagram of the experimental setup is shown in figure 5. The conductor running from the capacitor to a sheet of electrically conductive paper is broken by two pairs of contacts. Contacts 1 are normally open (body 2 is suspended from the lower contact using a thread, pulling it down). Contacts 3 - normally open. In the initial state, the target is open. When the body begins to fall, contacts 1 are connected and the target becomes short-circuited for the period of time during which the body falls. In this case, temporary marks are formed on the trajectory of the finger (on electrically conductive paper 4). As soon as the body hits the lower contact 3, the target opens again and the appearance of marks stops.

The appearance of the installation is shown in figure 6, a. The upper pair of contacts (Fig. 6, 6) is made of brass strips attached with insulating tape to the control pencil (you can use a contact pair from a suitable relay). The lower pair of contacts is even simpler: a brass strip is fixed to the pencil, on which a second strip lies freely, soldered to a soft conductor (Fig. 6, 6). When the falling body hits the first strip, the second strip falls and the whole opens.

Along the trajectory of the finger movement, an area with distinct time marks is formed. In our experiment, the path traversed by the body when falling was 27 cm. The number of marks turned out to be 12. Consequently, the body fell for 0.24 seconds. Based on these data, the acceleration of gravity is $g \approx 9,4 \text{ m/sec}^2$. Considering the simplicity of the experiment, this result should be accepted quite well. The accuracy of changes in the acceleration of gravity can be significantly improved by increasing the height from which the body falls.

To successfully carry out the experiment, it is necessary, using a voltage divider, to select such a potential difference so that when the circuit is open, marks do not appear (remember that area insulation leads to the actual closure of the circuit, and marks are formed even when the contacts are open). It is not at all necessary to use a tripod in the experimental setup: at home, you can attach it to a support with contacts to a wooden rail or to the back and leg of a chair.
