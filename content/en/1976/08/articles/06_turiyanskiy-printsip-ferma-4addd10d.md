The history of the Japanese law of refraction stretches over almost two thousand years. All the necessary “tools” for discovering the law were in the arena of ancient scientists. Thus, Claudius Ptolemy (2nd century AD) experimentally obtained fairly accurate values ​​for the angles of incidence and refraction of light. He also compiled the first tables of values ​​of trigonometric functions. But Ptolemy tried to establish a pattern in the correspondence of the angles of incidence and refraction and without agreeing to replace the angles with their trigonometric functions.

In subsequent centuries, until the 17th century, many scientists studied the refraction of light. Among the average were the largest medieval optician Ibn al-Khaitan (Alhazen), and the creator of celestial mechanics, the great Kepler. However, none of them managed to establish the law of refraction of light.

...In 1637, Abbot Mersenne sent Pierre Fermat the first chapters of Descartes' treatise "Dioptrics" so that Fermat could express his opinion about this work. One of the chapters is devoted to the law of reflection and refraction.

Descartes considers the motion of a ball thrown at an angle onto a loosely stretched net. If the ball breaks through the net, its direction of movement changes. Using simple geometric messages, Descartes obtains a correspondence between the trigonometric functions of the angles of incidence and refraction of a ball:

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = \text{const.}$$

And then, without any justification, he will re-examine this condition in the case of light propagation when it passes the boundary of two media*. At the time of Descartes, it was believed that a denser medium always refracts light, and in order to take his theoretical results into account with this fact, Descartes concluded that the speed of light in a denser medium is greater than in a less dense medium (which, of course, is not true!). It must be said that all of Descartes’ physical reasoning was very low, and it is impossible to understand what Descartes means by light.

Fermat sent his review of Dioptrics to Mersenne. In it he made mainly two comments concerning the derivation of the law of refraction. Fermat considered the act to be an unlawful transfer of property

In his treatise “Catoptrics,” Heron, considering the reflection of light from flat and spherical mirrors, made the following statement: light is reflected in such a way that the path of the ray falling from a given point and reflected to a given point is minimal.

However, in many cases the light does not follow this principle. В частности, при распространении света в неоднородной среде путь света от одной точки до другой оказывается далеко не минимальным.

Having again taken up the Japanese law, Fermat decided to try to visualize Heron's principle. Its main idea is as follows. The “shortest” paths chosen by light should be understood as the “easiest” ones, as the paths along which the light experiences the least “sport” from the medium in which it propagates. Fermat did not give a clear physical explanation for this “sporting”. The fact is that he considered the propagation of light to be instantaneous. The introduction of the concept of “sports” undoubtedly evoked the idea of ​​the spread of light in time. Fermat, of course, understood this, but he was speaking primarily as a mathematician. And bypassing this question with vague speculations about the “antipathy” of light to matter, Fermat moves on to a purely mathematical formulation of the problem.

Very soon the sharp reservations about Fermat's principle began to soften. The same Huygens, having repeated Fermat’s calculations and convinced of their impeccability, believed in this principle so much that he used this wonderful “Fermat phenomenon” when creating the wave theory of light.

Almost all further development of ray optics and the construction of wave optics was in one way or another connected with Fermat’s principle. As experimental and theoretical data accumulated, its formulation was refined and modified. Establishing the dependence of the speed of light propagation on the density of the medium made it possible to reveal the meaning of CONSTANTS in the expansion law:

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = n = \frac{v_1}{v_2},$$

where $n = v_1/v_2$ is a relative expansion index equal to the ratio of the speeds of light in media I and II (see Fig. 1). This removed the ambiguity associated with the concept of resistance, and allowed us to take the next step in determining the path of light: the “easiest” paths should be understood as the paths that light takes the least time to travel. However, in a number of cases (partly when reflected from wave mirrors), the light clearly “preferred” the paths that took the longest time...

In the end, Fermat's principle came up with a more general formulation than that given by Fermat himself: light will propagate along a path whose optical length is extreme, that is, it is either the maximum of all possible, or the minimum, or remains constant.

The optical path length in the case of a homogeneous medium is the product of the geometric path length $s$ and the absolute indicator of the filling of the substance: $l = ns$. If the medium is poor, then the optical path length is equal to the sum of the optical lengths in various sections for which the fill indicators are plotted.

Fermat's principle turned out to be the first clearly and consciously formed extremal, or more precisely, variational principle.

At the end of the 18th century, I. Bernoulli, using Fermat's principle, solved the problem of the brachistochrone - the curve of shortest descent. (We talked about this in the 12th issue of our magazine for 1975.) Maupertion and Euler in the 18th century, starting from the same Fermat principle, discovered the varnation principle in mechanics. Hamilton in the 19th century created a generalized varnation principle for both mechanics and optics. The modern theory of quantum mechanics is based on Hamilton's principle. Thus, Fermat’s principle was the founder of a whole family of variation principles in physics, and his influence can be traced to the present day.

And in conclusion, we suggest that you try, based on Fermat’s principle, to derive the laws of reflection and expansion of light.
