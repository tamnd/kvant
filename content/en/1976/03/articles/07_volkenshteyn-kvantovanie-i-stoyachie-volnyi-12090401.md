Quantization and wave structure

M. Volkinitain

Quantum mechanics is the science of the laws of motion of microparticles (atoms, electrons, neutrons, etc.). This is the physics of the microworld. It explains many important phenomena - radioactive decay, the electrical conductivity of metals, and the nature of chemical bonds. But first of all, quantum mechanics explains the line spectra of atoms - it answered the question of why atoms are capable of emitting or absorbing light only from one hundred certain frequencies of light wave oscillations. Quantum mechanics has shown that electrons in atoms can only have fixed, variable energy values. Physicists usually say that electrons are located at discrete energy levels $E_1$, $E_2$, ... - the energy of an electron in an atom is quantized. The absorption or emission of light by an atom occurs when an electron passes from one energy level to another. In the first case, the electron moves from a level with lower energy to a level with higher energy, in the second case - vice versa. The frequency of the emitted (or absorbed) light wave is determined by the conditions of the Bohr frequencies.

$$v_{nn'} = \frac{E_n - E_{n'}}{h},$$

(1)

where $E_n$, $E_{n'}$ are energy levels, $n$, $n'$ are the values ​​of the quantum number characterizing these levels.

One of the main tasks of quantum mechanics is to find quantized energy values ​​for a given system of particles, in particular for atoms. According to equation (1), solving such a problem means simultaneously calculating the spectrum of the system, that is, determining the set of frequencies of light emitted by this system. A rigorous solution to this problem comes down to solving the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics, the so-called Schrödinger equation. This is a differential equation in parts

derivatives of the second order, and the student, placed, cannot solve it. However, a number of interesting results related to energy quantization can be obtained without solving the Schrödinger equation, based on fairly simple considerations.

The events occurring in the microcosm became explainable only after the idea of ​​the wave nature of microparticles appeared. This idea was first expressed by de Broglie. He proposed that, along with corpuscular properties, material particles have wave properties and there is an unambiguous connection between them. A particle moving with speed $v$ and having momentum $p=mv$ corresponds to wave-

$$n \frac{\lambda}{2} = L,$$

or

$$\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n},$$

where $n=1, 2, 3, \dots$ (Fig. 1).

Using the model of standing waves on a structure, we will try to find quantization conditions for several problems of quantum mechanics.

Electron
"in the potential box"

Let us assume that the electron can move freely along a segment of length $L$ without the action of any forces. In other words, the potential energy of the electron in this interval is zero. And at the boundaries of the segment, the potential energy of the electron returns to infinity. The electron behaves as a heavy body would behave, having the ability to move freely along the bottom of a box with infinitely high vertical walls, elastically reflecting from its walls. Asks, what are the possibilities for the value of electron energy?

If the ends of the structures are fixed, then the nodes of a standing wave are necessarily located on them, which does not propagate beyond the boundaries of the structure. Similarly, a heavy body in a box cannot go beyond its limits, since this requires an infinite amount of energy. An electron moving in a “box” with a speed $v$ corresponds to a de Broglie wave $\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$. Since the electron cannot go beyond the boundaries of the segment $L$ (it is elastically reflected from the walls of the “box”), then the de Broglie wave must be reflected from

⟦folio 28⟧

the walls of the box. Therefore, the wave pattern corresponding to the motion of the electron can be represented as steady de Broglie sink waves. The nodes must be located at the ends of the segment $L$, and on the segment itself they must contain an integer number of half-waves (Fig. 2), that is, they must fulfill the condition

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{2L}{n} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots).$$

It follows that the speed of the electron inside the box is quantized:

$$v_n = \frac{h}{m\lambda_n} = \frac{nh}{2mL} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots).$$

The correspondence is quantized and so is its kinetic energy. Inside the box, the potential energy of the electron is zero and, therefore, the total energy coincides with the kinetic energy:

$$E = \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

Substituting here the possibility of speed value (3), we find

$$E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots).$$

At first glance, this result seems unexpected. It means that the energy of a heavy body moving in a box with infinitely high walls must be quantized. However, even for a very small particle, quantization becomes noticeable only in the case when the “box” has dimensions on the order of atomic ones. Indeed, for an electron whose mass is $m = 9,8 \cdot 10^{-28} e$, placed in a box of length $L = 1$ cm, the possible energy values ​​are

$$E_n = n^2 \frac{(6,62 \cdot 10^{-34})^2}{8 \cdot 9,8 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 10^{-4}} \approx n^2 \cdot 5,6 \cdot 10^{-34} \partial x = n^2 \cdot 3,5 \cdot 10^{-15} \partial v$$

and the “distance” between adjacent levels

$$\Delta E_n = E_{n+1} - E_n = (2n+1) \cdot 3,5 \cdot 10^{-15} \partial v.$$

These are very large “distances”, and the energy spectrum is real.

The energy quantization condition (4), which we obtained by analogy with string oscillations, turns out to be completely strict - it exactly coincides with the one given by the solution of the Schrödinger equation for this problem. We will return to formula (4) and show what interesting results follow from it. For now, let's turn to another problem.

Harmonic oscillator

Let's consider the quantization of a harmonic oscillator. This is the name of a particle that is under the influence of a force $f = -kx$, proportional to the deviation $x$ of the particle from the equilibrium position and tends to return the particle to this position.

The proportionality coefficient $k$ is a constant characterizing the other properties of the system from which the other force acts. For example, in the case of a squash, $k$ is the rigidity of the squash.

The oscillator removed from the equilibrium position coincides with the harmonic

$$v = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}. \quad (5)$$

The energy of the harmonic oscillator at any moment of time (for any deviation $x$ from the equilibrium position) consists of potential $\Pi = \frac{kx^2}{2}$ and kinetic $K = \frac{mv_x^2}{2}$ energies, i.e.

$$E = \frac{kx^2}{2} + \frac{mv_x^2}{2}.$$

If a microparticle oscillates, then its total energy turns out to be quantized. Let's find the quantization conditions.

The graph of the potential energy of the oscillator is a parabola $\Pi = \frac{kx^2}{2}$ (Fig. 4).

A microparticle oscillating with a maximum deviation $x_0$ essentially moves along a segment of length $2x_0$, and the speed of this movement depends on the coordinate $x$ ($v = \sqrt{\frac{k}{x}(x_0^2 - x^2)}$).

Therefore, the de Broglie wavelength corresponding to the particle will not remain constant. This is a significant condition for solving the problem. To simplify the solution, we will make some assumptions - assume that between points $x_0$ and $-x_0$ on the entire segment, the total energy of the particle is equal to the kinetic energy.

The assumption we made means that the oscillator can be considered as a particle in a “potential box”, the “depth” of which is $\frac{kx_0^2}{2}$ (see Fig. 3). On the “walls” the total energy is equal to the potential, i.e.

$$E = \frac{kx_0^2}{2}.$$

Therefore, the distance between the walls

$$L = 2x_0 = 2\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}.$$

The fact that inside the “box” throughout the entire segment $L$ the kinetic energy is constant $\left(\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{kx_0^2}{2}\right)$ means that the speed of the particle inside the “box” is constant. Such a particle corresponds to a standing de Broglie wave, the lengths of which are determined by the conditions.

$$\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n} = \frac{4}{n}\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}.$$

Therefore, the possible value of the particle speed is

$$v_n = \frac{nh}{4m}\sqrt{\frac{k}{2E}}.$$

Let's find the possible value of the oscillator energy:

$$E = \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{m}{2}\left(\frac{nh}{4m}\sqrt{\frac{k}{2E}}\right)^2,$$

where

$$E_n = \frac{nh}{8}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},$$

or, taking into account (5),

$$E_n = \frac{\pi}{4}nhv \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots).$$

The second solution to the problem using the Schrödinger equation has the form

$$E_n = nhv + \frac{1}{2}hv (n=0, 1, 2, \dots).$$

It is important that the non-burning solution (6) reflects the main feature of oscillator quantization -

Hydrogen atom

Let's consider another problem - about quantizing the energy of hydrogen atoms. This problem was first solved by Niels Bohr. He calculated the spectrum of hydrogen atoms using the so-called planetary model. Although the calculated electron energies matched the observed spectrum, this model does not allow the calculation of other characteristics of atoms.

According to modern concepts of quantum mechanics, the planetary model only very conditionally reflects the actual behavior of electrons. In this hydrogen, a single electron seems to form an electron cloud around the nucleus - a cloud of negative charge, the density of which characterizes the probability of finding an electron in a given region inside the atoms. In the unexcited state of atoms, the radius from the sphere of greatest density is approximately 0.53 Å. (In the planetary model this distance

corresponds to the smallest electron orbital radius ~0.53 A.)
As we have already said, questions about the structure and properties of the electron shells of atoms are answered by solving the Schrödinger equation for a given system. We will try to solve the problem of quantizing the energy of a hydrogen atom, without using either the planetary model or the Schrödinger equation - we will still limit ourselves to the mechanical analogy with the oscillations of a string with fixed ends.

The potential energy of an electron due to its interaction with the nucleus is equal to

$$\Pi = -\frac{e^2}{r},$$

Fig. 5a.

where $e$ is the value of the electron charge, and $r$ is the radius of the sphere of the most probable presence of an electron in the atom (i.e., the radius of the sphere of the highest density of the electron cloud).

The graph of the dependence $\Pi(r)$—the potential curve—has the form of a hyperbola (Fig. 5a). We can assume that for a given value of $r$ the electron moves in the atom inside a region whose linear size is equal to $r$.

We will assume that at the “border” of this region all the energy of the electron is equal to its potential energy, i.e.

$$E = \Pi = -\frac{e^2}{r}.$$

“Inside” the segment, the energy of the electron at each point $x$ consists of kinetic and potential:

$$E = \frac{mv^2(x)}{2} + \Pi(x) = -\frac{e^2}{r}.$$

The speed of the electron changes over the segment $r$; therefore, the de Broglie wavelength corresponding to the electron will not be constant. This conditions the solution to the problem. To forgive him, we will make the following assumption: we will assume that the speed of the electron is constant. For example, let the speed be equal in magnitude to the value $v(r/2)$.

Since $\Pi(r/2) = -\frac{2e^2}{r}$, then from (9) we find the value $v = v(r/2)$

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2e^2}{mr}} = \sqrt{\frac{-2E}{m}}.$$

The assumptions we have made essentially mean that we represent the electron in an atom as a particle in a “potential box” whose “depth” is $-\frac{e^2}{r}$ and whose “width” is $r$ (see Fig. 5a). Such a particle corresponds to the structure of a de Broglie wave, perhaps the lengths of which are determined by the conditions

$$\lambda_n = \frac{2r}{n} = -\frac{2e^2}{nE}.$$

Therefore, the possibility of electron velocity

$$v_n = \frac{h}{m\lambda_n} = -\frac{hnE}{2me^2}.$$

According to (10), the total energy of an electron can be expressed in terms of its speed: $E = -\frac{mv^2}{2}$. Substituting the possibility of the value $v_n$ from (11), we obtain

$$E = -\frac{mv_n^2}{2} = -\frac{h^2n^2E^2}{8me^4}.$$

Therefore, the possibility of electron energy value is

$$E_n = -\frac{8me^4}{n^2h^2} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots).$$

The second solution to this problem (solution to the Schrödinger equation) has the form

$$E_n = -\frac{2\pi^2me^4}{n^2h^2} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots).$$

Solutions (12) and (13) differ from each other only in numerical factors. So the solution we obtained qualitatively correctly describes the spectrum of the hydrogen atom. The electron energy levels in an atom come closer together as the quantum number $n$ (Fig. 56) increases. Energy values ​​at different levels are related as

$$E_1:E_2:E_3:\ldots = 1:\frac{1}{4}:\frac{1}{9}:\ldots$$

As $n$ increases, the electron energy is calculated (recall that $E_n < 0$), and at $n \to \infty$ ($r \to \infty$) it becomes zero. An electron is removed from the atom. The energy of a free electron is positive, and the spectrum of its possible values ​​is continuous.

Thus, the reports of de Broglie standing waves in our case study help to explain the quantization of the energy of the system.

Metallic molecule model

Let us now return to a potential box with infinite walls. Unlike other problems, we obtained a rigorous solution here. We ask, where can such a model come across? In the theory of molecules.

Molecules of limited compounds, in which carbon-carbon double bonds are conjugated with single bonds, i.e., alternate with it, have mobile electrons. Each double bond has two mobile electrons (called pi electrons). These electrons can move freely along the bonding system, but, of course, in order to leave the molecule, the electrons must acquire very high energy.

Infections whose molecules contain a large number of conjugated bonds, i.e., pi-electrons, exhibit: This means that, unlike most limited compounds, which are non-pseonic, these substances absorb light in the visible region of the spectrum. The larger the conjugated bonds, i.e., the larger the pi-electrons, the larger the conjugated bonds, which are precisely such substances. These substances absorb light in the ultraviolet region of the spectrum, but with a further increase in the number of conjugated bonds, the absorption plateau shifts to the visible region. We see that λ is roughly proportional to the number of pi electrons.

Let us calculate the wavelengths λ using the potential box model.

The width of the box $L$ is equal to the length of the chain of conjugated bonds, i.e. $L = Nl$, where $N$ is the number of conjugate bonds, $l$ is the length of one chain link (link $C - C = C$) (Fig. 6). The longest wavelength absorption shelf corresponds to the transition of an electron from the last occupied energy level to the first free one. The number of pi electrons in a molecule with $N$ double bonds is $2N$. According to the rules of quantum mechanics (Pauli principle), two electrons (with antiparallel spins, i.e., the momentum inherent in an electron) can be located at each energy level. Consequently, $2N$ electrons occupy $N$ levels, and the longest wavelength absorption shelf corresponds to the transition of an electron from the $N$ occupied level to the $N + 1$ level, which is free. According to formula (1), this transition corresponds to the frequency of the light wave

$$v_{N, N+1} = \frac{E_{N+1} - E_N}{h}.$$
