Applications of Tell Process Diagrams

The chapter “Thermodynamics” of the school physics course has many problems that examine the behavior of the simplest thermodynamic system - an ideal gas. Typically, this system is represented as a fairly rarefied gas enclosed in a cylinder under a piston (Fig. 1). At the same time, thermodynamics does not consider the motion of the system as a whole and does not take into account the potential energy of the system in external force fields.

Let us recall that the state of an ideal gas is characterized by the following parameters: pressure $p$, volume $V$, temperature $T$, mass $m$ and molar mass $\mu$.

A system is said to be in thermodynamic equilibrium if

Fig. 1. The simplest thermodynamic system.

whether each parameter has the same value at any point in the volume of the system. For example, a gas left to itself is always distributed evenly throughout the volume, so that the pressure is always the same. This state corresponds to equilibrium. If at least one of the parameters changes from point to point, then the state of the system is nonequilibrium; various processes are possible in the system: diffusion, heat transfer, etc. An equilibrium state differs from a nonequilibrium state in that the system can remain in an equilibrium state for as long as desired, unless, of course, external conditions change.

Under the influence of external influences, the system moves from one state to another. A change in the state of a system is called a process: the process is always associated with an imbalance. Thermodynamics considers processes in which the initial and final states are in equilibrium. But intermediate states can also be nonequilibrium. We will consider the simplest case, when the process occurs infinitely slowly, so that the state of the system at any moment can be considered equilibrium. In this case, they say that an equilibrium process is occurring in the system, which is a continuous sequence of equilibrium states.

The concept of “equilibrium state” and “equilibrium process” is, of course, an idealization, but an idealization that is very convenient for quantitative analysis of the behavior of a system.

Equilibrium states and equilibrium processes can be represented graphically. To do this, the values ​​of any two parameters are plotted on two mutually perpendicular axes, for example, pressure p and volume V, volume V and temperature T. In this way, so-called state diagrams of the system are obtained. In any of the diagrams (p, V), (V, T) or (p, T), each equilibrium state is denoted by a dot, and the equilibrium process is linear.

Generally speaking, this transition can be accomplished in an infinite number of ways, depending on how the individual parameters change. Problem 1. How will we draw a graph of the steep process $1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 1$ (Fig. 4) on the diagram $(p, V)$? The mass of the gas and its chemical composition will remain unchanged.

First of all, let’s “decipher” the graph shown in diagram $(p, T)$ and find out what processes were carried out over the gas?

In the section $1 \rightarrow 2$, the pressure is constant and has values ​​$p_1$, and the temperature increases from value $T_1$ to value $T_2$. Only one parameter can be changed - volume $V$. Consequently, at the first stage the isobaric volume increases from $V_1$ to $V_2$. On the diagram $(p, V)$ this process will be depicted as a straight line $1 \rightarrow 2$, parallel to the axis $V$ (Fig. 5).

In section $2 \rightarrow 3$, the pressure changes in direct proportion to the temperature (primary $2 \rightarrow 3$ passes through the origin). This indicates that the gas volume does not change in this section: $V_2 = V_3$. In diagram $(p, V)$ process $2 \rightarrow 3$ will be depicted as a vertical segment, and flowing downward, as the pressure decreases.

Process $3 \rightarrow 4$ isobaric ($p = p_3$). The temperature, and therefore the volume, decreases. Process $3 \rightarrow 4$ on diagram $(p, V)$ will be represented by a segment parallel to axis $V$. But for now it remains unknown where the process $3 \rightarrow 4$ ends, since the volume $V_4$ is unknown. Therefore, we first examine the process $4 \rightarrow 1$. From figure 4 it is clear that in process $4 \rightarrow 1$ the pressure increases in direct proportion to the absolute temperature. This means that the process occurs at $V = const$ and $V_4 = V_1$. The final plot of cycle $1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 1$ on diagram $(p, V)$ is as shown in figure 5.

It is easy to see that the cycle $1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 1$ consists of the process of isochoric heating $1 \rightarrow 2$, isothermal distribution $2 \rightarrow 3$ and isobaric cooling $3 \rightarrow 1$. In coordinates $(p, V)$ it takes the form shown in figure 7.

The cycle $3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3$ consists of the processes of isochoric heating $3 \rightarrow 4$, isobaric cooling $4 \rightarrow 2$ and isothermal distribution $2 \rightarrow 3$. Since the processes $2 \rightarrow 3$ for both cycles coincide, it is reasonable to display the dependence graphs $p(V)$ for them on the same coordinate axes (Fig. 7).

It is quite obvious that the area of ​​the figure $1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 1$ is less than the area of ​​the figure $3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3$. It immediately follows that, according to the gas work model, $A$ in the first process is less than in the second, i.e. $|A_{1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3}| < |A_{3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3}|$. But we must not forget that the work done by the gas is an algebraic quantity: if the process occurs with an increase in volume, then $A > 0$, and if the volume of the system decreases, then $A < 0$. For circular processes, one can formulate the following rule for determining the sign of the work done by the gas: if the circuit of the circular process is traversed clockwise, then $A > 0$; if the process of traversing the contour proceeds counterclockwise, then $A < 0$. Applying this rule to this problem, we get

$$A_{1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3} > 0 \text{ и } A_{3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3} < 0.$$

The final answer to the problem can be given as follows:

$$A_{1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3} > A_{3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3},$$

but at the same time

$$|A_{1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3}| < |A_{3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3}|.$$

Task 3. We perform a gas cycle $1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 1$ (Fig. 8), consisting of two isotherms ($1 \rightarrow 2$ and $3 \rightarrow 4$) and two adιαbas ($2 \rightarrow 3$ and $4 \rightarrow 1$). Which of these processes occur when mellot is supplied to the system, and which require the removal of mellot?

Students rarely give the correct answer to this question. The most common mistake is that two different concepts are identified: temperature and amount of heat. It is stated that if the temperature is constant (processes $1 \rightarrow 2$ and $3 \rightarrow 4$), then heat does not need to be supplied or removed. This is, of course, not true.

To solve the problem, we will use thermodynamics:

$$Q = \Delta U + A.$$

Here $Q$ is the amount of heat transferred to the system, $\Delta U$ is the change in the internal energy of the system and $A$ is the work done to the system.

The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature (of course, provided that $m = const$ and $\mu = const$). Therefore, for an isothermal process $1 \rightarrow 2$ the change in internal energy is $\Delta U = 0$, and equality (2) takes the form

$$Q = A.$$

Processes 2→3 and 4→1 are adnabate. By definition, an adnabate process is a process without heat release from the environment, that is, $Q = 0$. Consequently, in sections 2→3 and 4→1, heat is neither supplied to the system nor removed from it.

Note that the adnabates in the diagram $(p, V)$ are steeper than the isotherms. This is easy to understand if we again use the first law of thermodynamics, written for adabate processes:

$$\Delta U + A = 0.$$

During adnabate expansion (process 2→3), the system performs work due to internal energy:

$$A > 0, \quad \Delta U < 0.$$

The internal energy of the gas decreases and the temperature of the system decreases (the system moves from isotherm 1→2 to isotherm 4→3, corresponding to a lower temperature). With adabate sciatia (process 4→1)

$$A < 0, \quad \Delta U > 0.$$

The internal energy increases, the gas temperature rises.
