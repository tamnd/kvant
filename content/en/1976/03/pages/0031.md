The energy of the harmonic oscillator at any moment of time (for any deviation $x$ from the equilibrium position) consists of potential $\Pi = \frac{kx^2}{2}$ and kinetic $K = \frac{mv_x^2}{2}$ energies, i.e.

$$E = \frac{kx^2}{2} + \frac{mv_x^2}{2}.$$

If a microparticle oscillates, then its total energy turns out to be quantized. Let's find the quantization conditions.

The graph of the potential energy of the oscillator is a parabola $\Pi = \frac{kx^2}{2}$ (Fig. 4).

A microparticle oscillating with a maximum deviation $x_0$ essentially moves along a segment of length $2x_0$, and the speed of this movement depends on the coordinate $x$ ($v = \sqrt{\frac{k}{x}(x_0^2 - x^2)}$).

Therefore, the de Broglie wavelength corresponding to the particle will not remain constant. This is a significant condition for solving the problem. To simplify the solution, we will make some assumptions - assume that between points $x_0$ and $-x_0$ on the entire segment, the total energy of the particle is equal to the kinetic energy.

The assumption we made means that the oscillator can be considered as a particle in a “potential box”, the “depth” of which is $\frac{kx_0^2}{2}$ (see Fig. 3). On the “walls” the total energy is equal to the potential, i.e.

$$E = \frac{kx_0^2}{2}.$$

Therefore, the distance between the walls

$$L = 2x_0 = 2\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}.$$

The fact that inside the “box” throughout the entire segment $L$ the kinetic energy is constant $\left(\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{kx_0^2}{2}\right)$ means that the speed of the particle inside the “box” is constant. Such a particle corresponds to a standing de Broglie wave, the lengths of which are determined by the conditions.

$$\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n} = \frac{4}{n}\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}.$$

Therefore, the possible value of the particle speed is

$$v_n = \frac{nh}{4m}\sqrt{\frac{k}{2E}}.$$

Let's find the possible value of the oscillator energy:

$$E = \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{m}{2}\left(\frac{nh}{4m}\sqrt{\frac{k}{2E}}\right)^2,$$

where

$$E_n = \frac{nh}{8}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},$$

or, taking into account (5),

$$E_n = \frac{\pi}{4}nhv \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots).$$

The second solution to the problem using the Schrödinger equation has the form

$$E_n = nhv + \frac{1}{2}hv (n=0, 1, 2, \dots).$$
