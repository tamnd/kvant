Applications of Tell Process Diagrams

The chapter “Thermodynamics” of the school physics course has many problems that examine the behavior of the simplest thermodynamic system - an ideal gas. Typically, this system is represented as a fairly rarefied gas enclosed in a cylinder under a piston (Fig. 1). At the same time, thermodynamics does not consider the motion of the system as a whole and does not take into account the potential energy of the system in external force fields.

Let us recall that the state of an ideal gas is characterized by the following parameters: pressure $p$, volume $V$, temperature $T$, mass $m$ and molar mass $\mu$.

A system is said to be in thermodynamic equilibrium if

whether each parameter has the same value at any point in the volume of the system. For example, a gas left to itself is always distributed evenly throughout the volume, so that the pressure is always the same. This state corresponds to equilibrium. If at least one of the parameters changes from point to point, then the state of the system is nonequilibrium; various processes are possible in the system: diffusion, heat transfer, etc. An equilibrium state differs from a nonequilibrium state in that the system can remain in an equilibrium state for as long as desired, unless, of course, external conditions change.

Under the influence of external influences, the system moves from one state to another. A change in the state of a system is called a process: the process is always associated with an imbalance. Thermodynamics considers processes in which the initial and final states are in equilibrium. But intermediate states can also be nonequilibrium. We will consider the simplest case, when the process occurs infinitely slowly, so that the state of the system at any moment can be considered equilibrium. In this case, they say that an equilibrium process is occurring in the system, which is a continuous sequence of equilibrium states.

The concept of “equilibrium state” and “equilibrium process” is, of course, an idealization, but an idealization that is very convenient for quantitative analysis of the behavior of a system.
