⟦folio 20⟧

We will now consider as a displacement any mapping resulting from the sequential execution of several elements of displacement.

Elementary displacements, and therefore displacements, translate all points of any chord into points of a certain chord, and the image of the intersection of two chords during any displacement coincides with the intersection of the images of these chords.

Our movements transform straight lines into straight lines, and any point of the first straight line can be translated to any point of the second (check this).

Problem 1. An equilibrium trapezoid is inscribed in a circle, the lateral sides of which intersect at point P, and the diagonals at point Q; d is the diameter lying on the straight line PQ. Prove that $\varphi_d = \varphi_P \varphi_Q = \varphi_Q \varphi_P$ ($\varphi_M \varphi_N$ means sequential execution of moves $\varphi_M$ and $\varphi_N$).

Now we need to determine the “distances” between the points of our circle and the “angles” between straight lines so that they are preserved during the described movements.

Distances and angles

Let C and D be two points of our circle. Let us draw a “straight line” through them - the chord AB, connect points A, C, D and B with some point P that does not lie on the circle, and denote the angles formed at point P by α, γ and β (see Fig. 2). Let us distance the relation $\frac{|AD| \cdot |BC|}{|AC| \cdot |BD|}$. We have:

$$\frac{|AD| \cdot |BC|}{|AC| \cdot |BD|} = \frac{S_{APD} \cdot S_{PBC}}{S_{PAC} \cdot S_{DPB}} = \frac{|AP| \cdot |PD| \sin(\alpha + \gamma) \cdot |PC| \cdot |PB| \cdot \sin(\beta + \gamma)}{|AP| \cdot |PC| \cdot \sin\alpha \cdot |PD| \cdot |PB| \cdot \sin\beta} = \frac{\sin(\alpha + \gamma) \cdot \sin(\beta + \gamma)}{\sin\alpha \cdot \sin\beta}.$$

(Here $S_{XYZ}$ is the total area of ​​triangle $XYZ$.)

It is clear that if $C'$ and $D'$ are the images of points C and D under elementary displacements $\varphi_P$ (or $\varphi_d$), then

$$\frac{|A'D'| \cdot |B'C'|}{|A'C'| \cdot |B'D'|} = \frac{|AD| \cdot |BC|}{|AC| \cdot |BD|}$$

(since the angles α, β and γ do not change their magnitude), and, therefore, elementary movements, and therefore movements in general, preserve this relationship.

Let's define the “distance” between any two points C and D like this:

$$\rho(C, D) = \log_q \frac{|AD| \cdot |BC|}{|AC| \cdot |BD|},$$

where q > 1 is some fixed number.

Important note. When determining the distance, the order of the points on the line AB is important, namely: if we are looking for the distance $\rho(C, D)$, then point A is the point adjacent to C, and point B is the point adjacent to D; if we are looking for $\rho(D, C)$, then A is a point adjacent to D (accordingly, B is a point adjacent to C).

For the total distance between points C and D, we will keep communication $|CD|$. From formula (1) it follows that $\rho(C, D) \geq 0$, and $\rho(C, D) = 0$ only in the case when points C and D coincide (that is, $\gamma = 0$). Also, $\rho(C, D) = \rho(D, C)$. If there are three points, C, D and E, lying on the same line, and point D is between points C and E, then

$$\rho(C, D) + \rho(D, E) = \rho(C, E)$$

(check both statements).
