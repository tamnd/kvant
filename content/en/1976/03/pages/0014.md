The difference in the geometry of Poincaria Oeuclidean appears at the relative distance of a pair of n-lines. We already know that two different n-lines can intersect at most at one point. If they do not intersect, then they have a common infinite distant point (invisible!) or do not have common points even on the invisible border. In the first case we will call such n-direct parallels, and in the second - supernapales. If there is an n-line $L(\alpha, \beta)$, then through a point outside it there pass only two parallels $L(\alpha, \beta)$ n-lines (corresponding to the infinite distant points $\alpha$ and $\beta$, respectively; Fig. 4) and an infinite set of super-parallels lying between the parallels. Thus, in Poincaria the axiom of parallels is untrue (to us, the observer, in the opening, this is not very surprising - after all, the Poincarians do not know that their “straight” ones are “not real”!); this allows us to hope that Poincaria's geometry will turn out to be Lobachevsky's geometry.

The main thing we need to do now is determine distances and movements in Poincaria.

Distances and movements

From the point of view of optics, it is natural to take as the distance between points $A$ and $B$ in Poincaria the time during which light should travel from point $A$ to point $B$: then the n-straight lines will be the shortest lines between the points lying on them. From physical considerations it follows that defining the distance $\rho(A, B)$ in this way has the general properties of the Scolid distance:

1) $\rho(A, B) = \rho(B, A)$;
2) if $A, B, C$ lie on the same n-line
