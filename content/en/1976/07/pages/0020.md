⟦folio 18⟧

Look closely at the photographs of the produced crystals on figure 1. These are crystals of calcium carbonate (calcium) or, as it is sometimes called, Spanish spar. The edges of the crystals, which we see through their thickness, appear to be double. The ability of calcium crystals to “crumple” an image was discovered back in the 17th century. But the explanation of this phenomenon, which is called double refraction, was given only after the acceptance of the electromagnetic theory of light.

In a light wave emitted by a normal source, the direction vectors E of the electric field and the induction B of the magnetic field oscillate in all possible directions perpendicular to the directions of wave propagation. This kind of light is called natural light. If in a light wave the direction of oscillations of vector E (and therefore vector B) is constant all the time, then such light is called plane- or linearly polarized. the direction perpendicular to the direction of oscillations of the vector E is called the polarization direction. Natural light can be thought of as a sum of waves with all possible directions of polarization. In many substances, the speed of propagation of light vibrations (and therefore exponential, exponential) is constructed independent of the direction of vibration of the vector E of the light wave. For two mutually perpendicular directions of vector E, there are two values ​​of the refractive index. These explain the phenomenon of double refraction. The light beam entering the crystal is divided into two beams in which the oscillations of the vector E are mutually perpendicular. Due to the transversality of light waves, the direction of oscillations of the vector E in each ray lies in the plane containing the ray and is perpendicular to the ray. So, when entering a Spanish spar crystal, a beam of natural light is divided into two beams of plane-polarized light with mutually perpendicular planes (directions) of polarization. You can verify this using any polarizer. (We talked about how to make a homemade polarizer in the 11th issue of the magazine for 1975.)
