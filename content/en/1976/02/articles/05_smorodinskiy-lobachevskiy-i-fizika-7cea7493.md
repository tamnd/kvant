## Lobachevsky and physics

Mathematics has always served physicists as a research tool. Kepler would not have been able to formulate his laws if he did not know the theory of conic sections. In order to solve problems in mechanics, Newton had to create a new branch of mathematics: differential and integral calculus. But even against the background of these great discoveries, the discovery of non-Euclidean geometry stands out due to the influence it had on our ideas about the physical picture of the world.

In those years when Nikolai Lobachevsky worked, no one doubted the correctness of Newton’s mechanics, and questions about the nature of space and time, although they were of interest to scientists, seemed completely abstract and had no practical significance.

Lobachevsky is compared to Copernicus. By depriving the Earth of its position as the center of the world, Copernicus opened the Universe to exploration. Lobachevsky, having shown that Euclidean geometry is not the only one, made natural scientists think that the space in which the Universe develops should also be a subject of study. Geometry after Lobachevsky lost its absolute infallibility and turned to experience for confirmation or denial of its laws.

Lobachevsky understood that the possibility of changing the laws of geometry could entail the possibility of changing the laws of mechanics. “...Some forces in nature,” he wrote, “follow one, others their own special geometry... forces all produce the same: motion, speed, time, mass, even distances and angles.”

Physicists ignored these warnings for a very long time. Only after the discovery of the special (SRT) and general theories of relativity (GTR) did it become clear what deep meaning was contained in these words of Lobachevsky. Physicists should consider Lobachevsky one of the great creators of their science. The history of non-Euclidean geometry still serves as a striking example of how the most abstract theories can lead to a complete revision of views on our real world.

Let us give several examples that will show how the ideas of Lobachevsky’s geometry were reflected in the theory of relativity. Let's start with a special theory.

### STO: speed addition

When solving problems in kinematics, we use the rules of vector addition prescribed by Euclidean geometry. In this case, we add both the displacement vectors and the velocity vectors in the same way.

With the discovery of the special theory of relativity, it became clear that adding velocities according to the rules of Euclidean geometry gives a practically correct answer only if the velocities encountered in the problem are small compared to the speed of light $c$. When the speeds are comparable to the speed of light, to obtain the correct result it is necessary to use other rules. Let's consider this example.

Suppose that we are observing from a lighthouse a ship that is moving away at a constant speed $\bm{u}$. Rockets are fired vertically upward from the ship at different speeds $\bm{v}_i$ relative to the ship ($i=1$, 2, 3, $\ldots$). How to find the velocities of the rockets relative to the lighthouse (i.e., the velocities of the $\bm{v}_i'$ rockets in the frame of reference associated with the lighthouse)?

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 1

From the point of view of Newtonian mechanics
$$
\bm{v}_i'=\bm{u}+\bm{v}_i\tag{*}
$$
— the total vector $\bm{v}_i'$ can be found using the triangle rule (Fig. 1).

The solution to this problem in the special theory of relativity contains mathematical difficulties that most of our readers will not be able to overcome. Therefore, we will only give the answer:
$$
\bm{v}_i'=\bm{u}+\bm{v}_i\sqrt{1-\dfrac{u^2}{c^2}}.\tag{**}
$$

In cases where the speeds $\bm{u}$ and $\bm{v}_i$ are much less than the speed of light $c$, formula (**) practically turns into formula (*).

But when $\bm{u}$ and $\bm{v}_i$ become comparable to the speed of light, the exact value of the speed $\bm{v}_i'$ can only be found using formula (**) - it is the kinematics of SRT that describes the movement of bodies at high speeds.

It is obvious that when adding the vectors $\bm{u}$ and $\bm{v}_i$ according to the Euclidean triangle rule, the answer (**) cannot be obtained.

Let us once again draw attention to the fact that we use geometric terms and rules, but apply them not to ordinary space, but to a set of velocities, which are said to form the space of velocities. Using this terminology, from all that has been said we can conclude: as long as we remain within the framework of classical mechanics, the velocity space coincides with Euclidean space; in SRT mechanics, Euclidean geometry is unacceptable for describing the velocity space.

What geometric image can be compared to the velocity space in relativistic mechanics? It turns out that Lobachevsky space can serve as this geometric image. But the transition from physical quantities to their geometric images in the relativistic case is not as simple as in the classical case. By depicting velocities on the Euclidean plane (on a sheet of paper), we draw segments whose lengths (on a certain linear scale) are equal to the velocities. In order to imagine how to move from velocities to segments on the Lobachevsky plane, let us return again to the problem of a lighthouse, a ship and a rocket.

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 2

So, for the speed $\bm{v}'$ of the rocket in the frame of reference associated with the lighthouse, special relativity gives the answer (**). It turns out that on the Lobachevsky plane the velocities $\bm{u}$, $\bm{v}$, $\bm{v}'$ correspond to a right triangle, the lengths of the sides of which are equal to the values ​​of some nonlinear function $f$ of the velocities $u$, $v$, $v'$. We will assume that the triangle $BAD$ (Fig. 2) is drawn on the Lobachevsky plane,
$$
a=f(|\bm{u}|),\quad b=f(|\bm{v}|),\quad d=f(|\bm{v}'|).
$$
Finding the speed $\bm{v}'$ is reduced to finding, according to the rules of Lobachevsky geometry, the side $d$ of the triangle $BAD$ using two known sides $a$ and $b$ and the right angle between them. Angle $\beta$ of triangle $BAD$ is the angle that velocity $\bm{v}'$ makes with the horizon. The rule by which the side $d$ is constructed is the triangle rule we are familiar with. However, the formulas that allow us to determine the length of the side $d$ are different in Lobachevsky geometry than in Euclidean geometry (for example, in the problem we are considering, the Pythagorean theorem is not applicable). But in the case when the speeds $u$ and $v$ are much less than $c$, these formulas practically coincide with the formulas of Euclidean geometry. We can say that STR is in the same relationship to Newton's classical mechanics as Lobachevsky's geometry is to Euclid's geometry.

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 3

In the problem we have considered, it is possible to give a “physical” representation of the geometric concept of parallelism. The greater the rocket speed $\bm{v}_i$, the greater the angle $\alpha$, which the speed $\bm{v}_i'$ makes with the horizon. In classical mechanics, the speed $\bm{v}_i$ can be as high as desired. At $v_i\to\infty$ $\alpha\to90^\circ$. That is, in this limiting case, the directions $\bm{v}_i$ and $\bm{v}_i'$ become almost parallel. Thus, parallel lines of Euclidean geometry from the point of view of physics are the velocity vectors of an infinitely fast rocket, considered from different reference systems, moving relative to each other uniformly with finite speeds (Fig. 3, *a*).

According to SRT, the speed of a rocket $\bm{v}_i$ cannot be greater than the speed of light $c$. In the case when $|\bm{v}_i|=c$—a quantum of light (photon) is released from the ship—the magnitude of the speed $\bm{v}_i'$ is also equal to $c$. Indeed (see (**)),
$$
(v_i')^2=u^2+v_i^2{\left(1-\dfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)}.
$$
Substituting $v_i=c$ into this expression, we get $v_i'=c$.

On the Lobachevsky plane in this case, the sides $b$ and $d$ (corresponding to the velocities $v_i$ and $v_i'$) become infinitely long (see Fig. 3, *b*). But the angle $\beta$, which the side $d$ makes with the side $a$ (corresponding to the speed $u$), remains less than $90^\circ$. In Lobachevsky geometry this angle is called the angle of parallelism and is denoted $\Pi(a)$. But $\beta$ is the angle that, in the frame of reference associated with the lighthouse, makes the speed $v'$ with the horizon. The magnitude of this angle is determined by the condition
$\cos\beta=\dfrac u{v'}=\dfrac uc$. This is the limiting, maximum angle at which an observer in this system can “see” the speed of a photon. Therefore, a “physical” image of parallel straight lines of Lobachevsky’s geometry naturally arises: these are photon velocities observed from different uniformly moving reference systems.

To everything we have said, we can add that the special theory of relativity developed independently of Lobachevsky’s geometry. All its formulas were obtained by Einstein using mechanical methods. Only in 1909, the German physicist Sommerfeld discovered the identity of the entire mathematical apparatus of SRT with the geometry of Lobachevsky.

The development of modern physics, such areas as nuclear physics and elementary particle physics, is impossible without the theory of relativity. If you read a textbook on non-Euclidean geometry, then each theorem, each problem can be associated with a problem from elementary particle physics. And when calculating the paths of particles in accelerators, the processes of collision of elementary particles, Lobachevsky’s geometry formulas turn out to be very useful - calculations become shorter and simpler.

### GTR: curvature of space

Probably everyone can imagine what the surface of a ball looks like from the point of view of a two-dimensional beetle crawling on it. Obviously, his perception does not coincide with ours. We can look at the ball “from the side,” from three-dimensional space, and see that its surface is a sphere. But for a two-dimensional beetle, which cannot do this - there is no third dimension for it - the surface of a sphere appears to be a plane. And if the size of the beetle is much smaller than the radius of the sphere, then he will conclude that this plane is Euclidean. But if the beetle is “literate”, then after taking some measurements, it can make sure that the geometry on its plane differs from Euclidean. For example, if he measures the sum of the angles of a very large triangle, he will find that it is different from $180^\circ$.

Living in three-dimensional space, we are to some extent in the position of a beetle. We model the geometry of this space based on everyday experience. And as long as we are dealing with earthly distances that are usual for us, experience says that the space surrounding us is Euclidean. What can we say about the geometry of space on the scale of our galaxy, the entire Universe? This question, in essence, was first posed by Lobachevsky. Are Euclid's laws of geometry valid at enormous (from the Earth's point of view) distances? Is it true, for example, that the sum of the angles of a very large triangle is $180^\circ$? Based on data obtained from astronomical observations, Lobachevsky tried to find an answer to this question. His conclusion was as follows: “... in a triangle, the sides of which are almost equal to the distance of the Earth to the Sun, the sum of the angles cannot differ from two straight lines by more than 0.0003 seconds of a degree.”

Although Lobachevsky did not find deviations from Euclid’s laws, the very idea that the world in which we live may not be Euclidean was truly revolutionary.

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 4. In the plane passing through the Sun, the sum of the angles of a large triangle whose vertices are stars is greater than $180^\circ$.

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 5. This experiment was carried out during a total solar eclipse. According to calculations, the star at that time was beyond the edge of the Sun. However, due to the bending of light rays coming from the star in the gravitational field of the Sun, the star was observed at some distance from the solar disk (during an eclipse, the sunlight is “blocked” by the Moon, and stars located near the Sun become visible). According to general relativity, the angular displacement of stars directly at the edge of the Sun should be $\approx1''\mathord,75$ ($1''$ is the angle at which the matchbox is visible from a distance $\sim10$ km). Experiments conducted during solar eclipses give slightly larger values. However, these results undoubtedly confirm the curvature of space.

Over the 150 years that have passed since Lobachevsky created his geometry, science has made a huge step towards understanding the real world. And the most important stage on this path was the creation by Einstein of the general theory of relativity. She established a connection between the force of universal gravity and the properties of space. The main conclusion that follows from General Relativity is that the space in which we live is curved. Near heavy bodies, for example, near the Sun, mechanics becomes not Newtonian, and the geometry of space becomes non-Euclidean (see Fig. 4).

Light, passing by the Sun, moves along a curve similar to a parabola. Since a straight line is, by definition, a ray of light propagating in a homogeneous medium, then in space near the Sun a straight line is a curved light ray. The degree of curvature of space is characterized by the curvature of the beam. The radius of curvature of a light beam at a point located at a distance $r$ from the Sun is called the radius of curvature of space at this point. General Relativity gives the following formula for it:
$$
R=\sqrt{\dfrac{r^3}{2R_{\text{гр}}}}.
$$
Here $r$ is the distance to the Sun, and $R_{\text{гр}}$ is the so-called gravitational radius of the Sun, the value of which characterizes the degree of influence of the Sun on the geometry of the space surrounding it:
$$
R_{\text{гр}}=\dfrac{2\gamma M}{c^2}\approx3~\text{км},
$$
where $M\sim10^{33}$ g is the mass of the Sun, $c=3{,}00\cdot10^8$ $\dfrac{\text{м}}{\text{с}}$ is the speed of light, $\gamma=6{,}67\cdot10^{-11}$ $\dfrac{\text{Н}\cdot\text{м}^2}{\text{кг}^2}$ is the gravitational constant.

In 1919, during a solar eclipse, astronomical observations were made, the results of which confirmed the conclusions of General Relativity (Fig. 5).

The theory of relativity is an indispensable apparatus in the study of the properties of the Universe. Currently, there are many models of the Universe. The validity of them, which of them actually describes the properties of the Universe, can be judged by the results of experiments. But these experiments are extremely complex, many of them are still only “mental”, and modern science does not have the capabilities to carry them out. But among these models there are those according to which the geometry of space is the geometry of Lobachevsky.

The geometric properties of the Universe change over time; astronomers see galaxies and quasars scattering; quite recently, the first “black hole” was discovered in the constellation Cygnus - a point where densities turn to infinity and lengths become zero. And science can explain these phenomena only because 150 years ago, on the banks of the Volga, a young Kazan mathematician Nikolai Lobachevsky asked: is it so obvious that through a point outside a line only one line can be drawn that does not intersect the given one? Who would have thought then that this was one of the most tricky questions that man has ever asked nature.
