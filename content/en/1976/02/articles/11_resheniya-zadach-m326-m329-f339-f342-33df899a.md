F339. The tennis ball hits a soft racket and bounces off it elastically. The mass of the ball is much less than the racket, and the speed of the ball before colliding with the racket is equal to $v$ and makes an angle $\alpha = 60^\circ$ with the perpendicular to the racket. At what constant speed must my racquet build up so that the ball is reflected from it at right angles to the direction of the original movement? The ball cannot be treated either before or after a collision with the racket.

◆ When a ball collides elastically with a stationary racket, the speed of the ball does not change in abscylite value, but changes direction. In this case, the velocity component $v_y$ (Fig. 7, a) remains constant ($v_y' = v_y$), and the component $v_x$ changes direction to the opposite ($v_x' = -v_x$).

According to the conditions of the problem, the speed of the racket must be such that the ball is reflected at an angle $\beta = 30^\circ$ (for a stationary observer), i.e. it must fulfill the conditions (see Fig. 7, b).

$$\frac{|v_y'|}{|v_x'|} = \mathrm{tg} \beta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.$$ (*)

After trim, how the speed of the ball changes when colliding with a moving racket. To do this, consider the movement in the coordinate system associated with the racket, moving relative to the fixed coordinate system with a constant speed $w$ (since the mass of the racket is much larger than the ball, the speed of the racket practically does not change after a collision). Since the system is inertial, reflection will occur according to the law “the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.”
The speed of the ball in this system before the collision is equal to $u = v$ - $w$ (see Fig. 7, $\theta$). The velocity components $u_x$ and $u_y$ are equal to the corresponding $u_x = v_x - w$, $u_y = v_y$. During a collision, the $u_y$ component remains constant ($u_y' = u_y = v_y$), and the $u_x$ component changes direction to the opposite: $u_x' = -u_x = -v_x + w$.

F340. An Earth satellite with a mass of $m=10$ kg, moving in a circular orbit in the high layers of the atmosphere, experiences resistance from rarefied air. Resistance force $F=5 \cdot 10^{-4}$ n. Determine how much the satellite's speed will change during one revolution around the Earth. The height of the satellite's flight above the Earth's surface is considered small compared to the radius of the Earth.

The total energy $E$ of a satellite with mass $m$, moving in an orbit of radius $R$, adds up its kinetic energy $K = \frac{m v^2}{2} = \gamma \frac{m M}{2 R}$ (the speed of the satellite in a circular Earth orbit is $\sqrt{\gamma \frac{M}{R}}$, where $R$ is the radius of the Earth) and potential energy $\Pi = -\gamma \frac{m M}{R}$ (the “minus” sign is due to the nature of the attractive forces between the satellite and the Earth: as the bodies get closer, the potential energy decreases). Thus,

$$E = K + \Pi = -\gamma \frac{m M}{2 R}.$$

As a result of the action of the drag force, the energy of the satellite changes, therefore, the radius of the orbit changes. Let's calculate the change in radius per revolution.

The energy of the satellite per revolution changes by values ​​$\Delta E$, numerically equal to the work of the resistance force on the path $2\pi R$, that is, $\Delta E = -2\pi RF$. Thus,

$$\Delta E = -\gamma \frac{m M}{2 (R + \Delta R)} + \gamma \frac{m M}{2 R} = \gamma \frac{m M}{2} \frac{\Delta R}{(R + \Delta R) R}.$$

Assuming $|\Delta R| \ll R$, we can write

$$\gamma \frac{m M}{2} \frac{\Delta R}{R^2} = -2\pi RF.$$

Considering that $\gamma \frac{M}{R^2} = g$, we finally get

$$\Delta R = -\frac{4\pi RF}{g m} \approx -4 \cdot 10^2 m$$

($g = 9,8 \text{ m/сек}^2$, $R \approx 6,4 \cdot 10^6 \text{ m}$).

It is clear that the assumption $|\Delta R| \ll R$ we made is correct.
