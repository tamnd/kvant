## The great discovery of Lobachevsky

It is easier to stop the Sun, easier to move the Earth, than to reduce the sum of angles in a triangle, reduce parallels to convergence, and move perpendiculars to a straight line by a distance.

V. F. Kagan

The history of great discoveries is always instructive. But how often the circumstances under which they were made and the train of thought that led the scientist to them remain unknown! Instead, legends are created: about Archimedes, who discovered his law in the bath, or about Newton’s apple. It is known, however, that when asked how he came to his discovery, Newton answered: “By constantly thinking about it,” but he did not describe his thoughts anywhere until he brought them to full clarity.

This year marks the hundred and fiftieth anniversary of the discovery of non-Euclidean geometry by Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky. The date of this discovery is conventionally considered to be the day of February 24, 1826, when the young professor presented his report to the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Kazan University. The text of the report has not survived, but, according to the author, its contents were published in 1829 in an article entitled “On the Principles of Geometry.”

Lobachevsky's discovery made it possible already in the second half of the last century to call him the “Copernicus of geometry,” but only now is his full significance for mathematics in general and for physics becoming clear. We will try to briefly describe the ideas of the great geometer and highlight the train of thought that led to them.

The history of the issue resolved by Lobachevsky spans two millennia. In the third century BC, the manuscript of Euclid's Elements appeared in Alexandria, in which geometry was presented in a strictly systematic form, not much different from the one that was accepted in geometry textbooks until very recently. Euclid's construction is based on a number of initial statements (*axioms*), which are not proven. All other statements (*theorems*) are derived logically from the axioms. Among all the axioms of Euclid, the “*parallel axiom*” stands out: *Two straight lines $a$ and $b$, intersected by a third, intersect each other on the side where the sum of the internal one-sided angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is less than the sum of two right angles* (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1

This position attracted the attention of Euclid's successors and commentators for various reasons. Firstly, it was more complex than all other axioms - for example, such as “A straight line passes through two points” or “All right angles are equal.” Secondly, the converse statement “*The sum of two angles of a triangle is less than two straight lines*” was easily proven. And so the opinion was created that Euclidean “parallel axiom” is provable, but this proof still needs to be found.

This belief became universal, and the search for a proof of Euclid's axiom continued for two millennia in Greece and Byzantium, in the East and Western Europe. All these searches led to nothing. The Principia was still considered a model of scientific exposition, and only the theory of parallels seemed in need of improvement.

By the end of the 18th century, interest in this theory increased. In France, at the initiative of D'Alembert, one of the authors of the famous Encyclopedia, the first attempts were made to improve Euclid's presentation methodologically. Legendre’s geometry textbook met this requirement, and in each of its ten editions more and more “proofs” of Euclid’s axiom were given, until in the last edition the author recognized all these proofs as erroneous.

In Germany, interest in Euclid's axiom received a philosophical overtone. As the outstanding idealist philosopher I. Kant (1724-1804) argued, the immutability of arithmetic and geometry over thousands of years indicates that they are not based on experience, but are teachings about time and space, which, according to Kant, do not exist in the external world, but only correspond to the peculiarities of human cognition. Therefore, axioms are fully justified if they are “self-evident”; since Euclid's axiom lacks this self-evidence, it must be proven.

Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky (1792-1856) approached the theory of parallels differently than his predecessors. He began teaching geometry immediately after graduating from Kazan University. The notes of his lectures of 1816-17, which have reached us, still contain an ingenious attempt to prove Euclid’s axiom, but already in 1823 he wrote in his geometry textbook: “A rigorous proof of this truth has not yet been found.” Where can we look for a justification for the theory of parallels if Euclid’s axiom cannot be logically deduced from other axioms of geometry? In answering this question, Lobachevsky is helped by his consistent materialist attitude. “Leave,” he writes, “to work in vain, trying to extract all wisdom from one mind; ask nature, she keeps all the truths and will answer all your questions without fail and satisfactorily.” Elsewhere he writes, clearly rejecting Kant's idealistic a priori concept: “The first concepts with which any science begins must be clear and reduced to the smallest number. Then only they can serve as a solid and sufficient foundation for the teaching. Such concepts are acquired by feelings, innate - should not be believed.”

So, axioms, like all results of knowledge, have an experimental origin. But if other axioms of geometry are based on simple everyday observation, then the axiom of parallels is more complex. To substantiate it, an experiment and a theory of this experiment are required. This theory, however, cannot be Euclidian geometry, since that is what we want to substantiate. It should be more general and deeper.

This is the theory Lobachevsky creates. He first carefully calls it “*imaginary*” *geometry*, and then, at the end of his life, “*Pangeometry*,” i.e., universal geometry.

Let's return to figure 1, assuming for simplicity that the angle $\alpha$ is a straight line (Fig. 2). Through the point $B$ we can draw various rays in the right half-plane intersecting the straight line $a$. As we rotate counterclockwise, we will finally encounter the first of the non-intersecting rays; it is called *parallel* to the line $a$; The angle that this ray makes with the ray $BA$ is called *the angle of parallelism*. The straight line $b$ containing such a ray is also called *parallel* $a$.

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 2

All of the above is true regardless of Euclid’s axiom, which states that the angle of parallelism is a straight line, so that at $\beta\lt d$ the ray $b$ intersects the straight line $a$. If we do not accept this axiom, then we will have to limit ourselves to a more general and less definite statement that *the angle of parallelism $\Pi$ does not exceed $d$*. Lobachevsky bases his further reasoning on this assumption.

If $\Pi=d$, then Euclid’s axiom is observed, and all propositions of ordinary geometry—*Euclidean*, or, as Lobachevsky calls it, “*common*”—follow from it. If $\Pi\lt d$, then completely different consequences follow from this assumption, which constitute the content of the new geometry - Lobachevsky calls it “*imaginary*”, and we now call it *Lobachevsky geometry*.

Many of the theorems of “imaginary” geometry, to which Lobachevsky arrives, clearly contradict our usual ideas, and great strength of thought was needed not to accept these contradictions as logical. Lobachevsky owned this power and developed his geometry with great completeness, completing it with the construction of a kind of trigonometry (which is now commonly called *hyperbolic*).

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 3

An important section of Lobachevsky's geometry is the doctrine of the sum of the angles of a triangle. Consider the triangle $ABC$ (Fig. 3), and rotate the line $BC$ ($b$) counterclockwise around the point $B$. If we do not accept the axioms of Euclid, then we must assume that the straight line $b$ will “break away” from the straight line $a$ and become parallel to it even before the sum of the angles $\alpha+\beta$ becomes equal to $2d$. On the other hand, it is easy to show that when the straight line $b$ rotates, the value of the angle $\gamma$ at point $C$ tends to zero. It follows that at some position of the line $b$ the sum of the angles $\alpha+\beta+\gamma$ will be less than $2d$. It can also be shown that inequality $\alpha+\beta+\gamma\lt2d$ is valid for any triangle, so that *in Lobachevsky geometry, the sum of the interior angles of a triangle is less than two straight lines*.

Looking at the number
$$
\delta=2d-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma),
$$
which is called *triangle defect*, Lobachevsky derives the following remarkable formula:
$$
\delta=\dfrac S{R^2},\tag1
$$
where $S$ is the area of ​​the triangle, and $R$ is a number that is the same for all triangles. The quantity $R$, which has the dimension of length, is called *the radius of curvature of the Lobachevsky space*, and the negative quantity $K=-\dfrac1{R^2}$ is the curvature of this space. In Euclidean space there is $\delta=0$, and its curvature is considered to be zero.

The radius of curvature is involved in all formulas of hyperbolic trigonometry; so, for example, the formula holds
$$
\tg\dfrac\Pi2=e^{-\frac{\scriptstyle\rho}{\scriptstyle R^2}},\tag2
$$
in which $\Pi$ is the angle of parallelism (Fig. 2), $\rho$ is the “arrow”, is the length of the perpendicular $AB$.

We should now assume that $R=\infty$, i.e. that the curvature is equal to zero, and we get
$$
\tg\dfrac\Pi2=1,
$$
and this means that the angle of parallelism is straight, a statement equivalent to Euclid’s axiom. That is why Lobachevsky believed that Euclidean geometry is a particular (or better said, “limiting”) case of his “imaginary” geometry.

Formulas (1) and (2) show that the greater the radius of curvature of space, the smaller the differences between Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry. On the other hand, these differences will primarily affect figures of large sizes: for example, for a triangle these differences increase along with its area, and for parallel lines - along with the growth of the “arrow” (It follows that in Lobachevsky’s geometry there are no such figures. Two triangles, for example, with correspondingly congruent angles are congruent with each other.)

All this was clear to Lobachevsky when he raised the question of experimental testing of the theory of parallels. Here is what he wrote about this: “Vain efforts since the time of Euclid, over a period of two thousand years, made me suspect that the concepts themselves do not yet contain the truth that they wanted to prove, and which can only be verified by experiments, such as, for example, astronomical observations.”

Why exactly astronomical observations? Yes, because astronomy observes the largest figures available to us.

Let's consider one of them. Let $B$ be the point at which the Earth is located, and $B'$ be another point at which it arrives after six months, so that $|BB'|=2|AB|$ is the diameter of the Earth's orbit, $A$ is the center of the orbit, and $|AB|$ is the radius (Fig. 4). Let us assume that there is a fixed star at point $C$, and let us assume for simplicity that the straight line $CA$ is perpendicular to the plane of the earth’s orbit (ecliptic). If $(BM)$ is also perpendicular to this plane, then the angle $\eps=\widehat{CBM}$ is called the *annual parallax* of the star $C$ - in other words, half of the angle $2\eps$ is called parallax, by which the line of sight moving from an earthly observer to a fixed star deviates over six months. (In astronomy textbooks, parallax is the angle at which the radius of the earth’s orbit would be visible from a star; but only the angle $\eps$ is observable, and such a definition presupposes in advance that space is Euclidean.)

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 4

Let's construct a ray $BN$ parallel to the ray $AC$, and call *parallelism defect* the angle $\delta=d-\Pi$, which complements the parallelism angle $\Pi$ to a right angle. The parallelism defect vanishes if the space is Euclidean, and in any case satisfies the inequality
$$
\delta\lt\eps.\tag3
$$

Thus, *the parallelism defect corresponding to the arrow $\rho$, equal to the radius of the Earth's orbit, is less than any annual parallax.*

Astronomers use parallaxes to determine distances to fixed stars. Parallaxes have been measured in large numbers and with good accuracy; the smallest of them are of order $0{,}05''$ (one twentieth of an arcsecond). Taking this into account and inequality (3), it can be obtained from formula (2) that with the help of direct astronomical observations it is impossible to detect the difference between Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries if the radius of curvature of the latter is more than sixty light years. On the other hand, it turns out that if the geometry of outer space differs from Euclidean, then this difference is insignificant.

Based on similar reasoning, says Lobachevsky, “our theory of parallels justifies the accuracy of all calculations of ordinary geometry and allows the accepted principles of this latter to be considered as if strictly proven.”

This statement, as well as others from Lobachevsky’s early writings, shows that initially he looked at his “imaginary” geometry only as a means to substantiate the Euclidean theory of parallels. However, in the mid-30s, by the time of the publication of his “New Principles of Geometry with the Complete Theory of Parallel”, he had a different point of view. “In nature,” says Lobachevsky, “we actually only know movement, without which sensory impressions are impossible. So, all other concepts, for example, geometric ones, are artificially produced by our mind, being taken in the properties of movement, and therefore space by itself, separately, does not exist for us. After which there can no longer be any contradiction in our mind when we admit that some forces in nature follow one, others their own, special geometry.”

Lobachevsky's ideas did not meet with understanding among his contemporaries. Only the great German mathematician Gauss (“king of mathematicians”) appreciated them, but he wrote about this to his friends in private letters that were published after Lobachevsky’s death. In Russia, Lobachevsky's works were met only with rude negative reviews and even insulting ridicule. Only one mathematician, Lobachevsky’s junior colleague in the department, Kazan professor P.I. Kotelnikov, decided to utter prophetic words in a public speech: “Lobachevsky’s work will sooner or later find its connoisseurs.”

However, Lobachevsky did not give up, publishing works containing various approaches to the new geometry and its applications to the calculation of integrals. In the last of his works, “Pangeometry,” which he, already blind, dictated shortly before his death, the question of the feasibility of “imaginary” geosmetry in outer space is discussed.

The ideas of the great Russian geometer received worldwide recognition only after his death. To understand how this happened, you need to go back. In 1828, Gauss’s book “Studies on Curved Surfaces” was published, in which “surface geometry” or its *internal geometry* was constructed. Gauss gave formulas that make it possible to calculate the lengths of arcs of curved lines on a surface, determine the angles between curves or the areas of areas limited by them, find *geodesic* (i.e., shortest) lines on a surface and determine the so-called *Gaussian curvature* - a value characterizing the deviation of the internal geometry of a surface from the geometry of a plane.

Gauss's student, F. Minding (1806-1885), who worked in Dorpat (now the city of Tartu), studying the so-called *pseudosphere*, i.e., a surface of constant Gaussian curvature, obtained back in the 30s of the last century some formulas for the trigonometry of geodesic triangles on this surface. In 1868, the Italian geometer E. Beltrami drew attention to the fact that the formulas. Minding coincide with the formulas of Lobachevsky’s hyperbolic geometry. It followed that the internal geometry of the pseudosphere is Lobachevsky’s geometry.

Thus, it turned out that in Euclidean space it is possible to construct a *model* of the Lobachevsky plane; This fact dispelled doubts about the consistency of non-Euclidean geometry and quickly led it to universal recognition.

The interest aroused by familiarity with Lobachevsky's ideas had important consequences. Since the 70s of the last century, problems of the foundations of geometry began to be intensively developed. By the beginning of our century, quite strict expositions of this science were constructed (D. Hilbert, V.F. Kagan), and work began on the substantiation of arithmetic, set theory, probability theory and logic, which continues to this day. In recent decades, this work has become very relevant, having received applications to the theory of mathematical machines.
