Pendulum with vibrating suspension

As is known, a stationary body is in a state of stable equilibrium when its potential energy is minimal. In particular, for a mathematical (or physical) matrix, the potential energy is minimal when its center of gravity is under the suspension point. Of course, such a matrix also has an equilibrium position in an inverted state, when the center of gravity is located above the suspension point, but this position is unstable. Let us agree that we will consider a mathematical matrix in which the inexhaustible thread is replaced by a rigid inexhaustible core. However, the position of the body's center of gravity above the fulcrum does not always exclude stable balance. For example, a children's toy spinning top does not fall as long as it rotates quite quickly. And it's not just rotation that can improve body stability.

It turns out that with rapid vertical oscillations of the matrix suspension (i.e., the attachment point), the inverted position of the matrix becomes stable. Under these conditions, he is able to stand in a vertical position or sway around him (Fig. 1). The amount of research into this phenomenon is associated with significant mathematical difficulties. In 1951, Academician P.L. Kapitsa managed to approximately describe the behavior of a matrix with a vibrating suspension for the case when the length of the matrix $L$ is much greater than the vibration amplitude of the suspension $x_0$ and the vibration frequency far exceeds the natural frequency of vibration of the matrix. We will limit ourselves to qualitative reasoning only.

If, when observing, you do not pay attention to the small movement of the matrix associated with the vibration of the suspension, then its floating movement will be as if a moment of force was acting on the matrix, tending to bring it closer

Let's try to understand the cause of the vibration moment. Let us first assume that there are no gravity forces. Let the suspended matrix oscillate along the direction of vibration with amplitude $x_0$ and frequency $\omega$ according to the harmonic law $x = x_0 \cos \omega t$. Let us assume that the matrix makes an angle $\varphi$ (Fig. 2) with the direction of vibrations. The movement of the suspension can be decomposed into two movements: along the matrix and perpendicular to it. The first one only mixes the load along the line of the rod, and the second one turns the rod without mixing the load (rotation angles are assumed to be small). Therefore, with the greatest deviations, the matrices will occupy positions I and II (see Fig. 2).

It is more convenient to consider the behavior of the matrix in the reporting system associated with the suspension. The positions of the matrix I and II in this system are shown in figure 3. The selected reporting system is non-inertial*), therefore, in it, an inertial force equal to $F = -ma$ will act on the magnet (i.e. the mass contained at its end), where $a$ is the acceleration of the suspension at a given moment in time.

Since the suspension oscillates according to the harmonic law, the projection of its acceleration also changes according to the harmonic law: $a = -\omega^2 x = -\omega^2 x_0 + \omega^2 x_0$. Therefore, the force $F$, the projection of which is equal to $F = \pm m\omega^2 x_0$ in these positions, is directed as shown in figure 3. Obviously, the moment of force $F$ relative to the suspension point in position I is greater than in position II, and since the time of its action “up” is equal to the time of action “down” (according to the position of the vibration period, then in the end the matrix should mix upward, i.e. the shortest path to the direction of vibration.

The vibration moment $M_B$, under the influence of which this change occurs, is proportional to the difference in the moments of force $F$ in positions I and II. Let's find this difference.

Let the magnet be deflected by an angle $\varphi$ from the direction of vibration of the suspension. As can be seen from figure 2, the angular amplitude of small vibrations of the matrix is

A more rigorous analysis carried out by P. L. Kapitsa leads to the correspondence
$$M_B = \frac{m\omega^2 x_0^2}{4} \sin 2\varphi. \quad (1)$$

Using the understandable vibration-moment, we will now write the conditions for the equilibrium of the matrix in the field of gravity. We will assume that vibrations of the suspension occur in the vertical

direction. In addition to the vibration moment $M_B$, a gravitational moment $M_T = mgL \sin \varphi$ also acts on the magnet. In the equilibrium position these moments are equal:
$$mgL \sin \varphi = \frac{m\omega^2 x_0^2}{4} \sin 2\varphi,$$
or
$$\sin \varphi \left( \frac{2gL}{\omega^2 x_0^2} - \cos \varphi \right) = 0. \quad (2)$$

The values ​​of $\varphi$ that satisfy this equation are the equilibrium positions. They are:
$$\varphi_1 = 0, \quad \varphi_2 = 180^\circ, \quad (3)$$
$$\varphi_3, 4 = \pm \arccos \frac{2gL}{\omega^2 x_0^2}.$$

Here $\varphi_1 = 0$ is the upper position of the weight, $\varphi_2 = 180^\circ$ is the lower position of the weight.

Having considered the correspondence between the magnitude of the vibration moment and the moment of gravity near the equilibrium positions, we can be convinced that $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_2$ are stable positions, and $\varphi_3$ and $\varphi_4$ are unstable. Thus, if the magnet is in the area $A$ (Fig. 4), then it will oscillate around the position $\varphi_1$, and if in the area $B$, around the position $\varphi_2$.
