GTR: curvature of space

Probably everyone can imagine what the surface of a ball looks like from the point of view of a two-dimensional beetle crawling on it. Obviously, his perception does not coincide with ours. We can look at the ball "side", from three-dimensional space, and see that its surface is a sphere. But for a two-dimensional beetle, which cannot do this—for it there is no third of a change—the surface of the sphere appears to be a plane. And if the size of the beetle is much smaller than the radius of the sphere, then he will conclude that this plane is Euclidean. But if the beetle is “grammatical,” then by making some changes, it can make sure that the geometry on its plane differs from Euclidean. For example, changing the angles of a very large triangle, he will find that it is different from 180°.

Living in three-dimensional space, we are to some extent in the position of a beetle. We model the geometry of this space based on the Euclidean surface. What can we say about the geometry of space on the scale of our galaxy, the entire Universe? This question, in essence, was first posed by Lobachevsky. Are Euclid's laws of geometry valid at enormous (from the Earth's point of view) distances? Is it true, for example, that the sum of the angles of a very large triangle is 180°? Based on data obtained from astronomical observations, Lobachevsky is trying to find an answer to this question. His conclusion was this: “... in a triangle whose sides are almost equal to the distance of the Earth to the Sun, the sum of the angles cannot differ from two straight lines by more than 0.0003 seconds of a degree.”

Although Lobachevsky did not discover any deviations from Euclid’s laws, the very idea that the world in which we live may not be ενclidian was truly revolutionary.
