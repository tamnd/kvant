Lobachevsky will create such a theory. He first calls it carefully “imaginary” geometry, and then, at the end of his life, “Panceometry,” that is, universal geometry.

Let us return to figure 1, assuming for simplicity that the angle α is right (Fig. 2). Through point B we can draw various rays in the right half-plane that intersect straight line a. As we rotate counterclockwise, we will finally encounter the first of the non-intersecting rays; it is called parallel straight line a; The angle that this ray makes with ray BA is called the angle of parallelism. The line b containing such a ray is also called parallel a.

All of the above is true regardless of Euclid’s axiom, which states that the angle of parallelism is right, so that for β < d*) ray b intersects straight line a. If we do not accept this axiom, then we will have to limit ourselves to a more general and less definite statement that the angle of parallelism Π does not exceed d. Lobachevsky bases his further delusions on this assumption.

If Π = d, then Euclidean’s axioma is observed, and all assumptions of general geometries—Euclidean, or, as Lobachevsky calls it, “usable”—follow from it. If Π < d, then from this we assume

*) d is the accepted designation for the size of a right angle.

Completely different consequences emerge that make up the content of the new geometry - Lobachevsky calls it “imaginary”, and we now call it Lobachevsky’s geometry.

Many theorems of “imaginary” geometry, to which Lobachevsky refers, clearly contradict our usual understanding, and it took great strength of thought not to accept these contradictions as logical. Lobachevsky mastered this power and developed geometry on a large scale, completing it with the construction of his figurative trigonometry (which is now commonly called “ziperbolic”).

An important section of Lobachevsky's geometry is the doctrine of the sum of the angles of a triangle. Consider the triangle ABC (Fig. 3), and we will rotate the straight line BC (b) counterclockwise around the point B. If we do not accept the axioms of Euclid, we must propose that the straight line b will “break away” from the straight line a and become parallel to it even before the sum of the angles α + β becomes equal to 2d. On the other hand, it is easy to show that when straight line b rotates, the angle γ at point C tends to zero. It follows that for some straight lines b the sum of angles α + β + γ will be less than 2d. It can also be shown that the inequality α + β + γ < 2d is valid for any triangle, so that in Lobachevsky geometry the sum of the interior angles of a triangle is less than two right angles.
