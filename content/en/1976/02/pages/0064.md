We dealt with the poor students and Hockens students relatively quickly. Let's try to understand how to reason in more complex cases. The first example is with multi-colored balls. Let's imagine that urine (a small box) may contain, say, white and red balls. All balls are the same size and feel indistinguishable to the touch. Someone dips their hand into the urine and takes out a ball. The ball turns out to be red, for example. What can you say about the color of the remaining balls in the urine?
Some will say: “All the balls are red.” In fact, this is not true: we can only say that not some (not necessarily all) balls are red. There may also be white balls. It may also be that the drawn ball is the only red one among the white ones.
A red ball was pulled out. This means that the statement “All balls are white” is false. What is true?—“Some (at least one) of the balls are red.” Let's get lost. Let each of the following statements be false.
1. All balls in urine are red.
2. Some balls in urine are red.
3. Some balls in urine are white.
4. All equilateral triangles are right triangles.
5. All students in the class were at the meeting.
6. Some girls were at the meeting.
Form true statements.
And now the general scheme for solving all such problems is our “magic solution”. Let us have a set M of such and such objects (this could be several balls, or several books, or all the students of a certain class). Each of the elements of this set may or may not have some property A. For example, the ball may or may not be red; hokkenst - to be or not to be a loser.
The law is this: either a statement or its negation can be true. However, only one of two!
