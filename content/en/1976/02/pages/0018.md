⟦folio 16⟧

A. Norden

The great discovery of Lobachevsky

The cure is to stop the Sun, the cure is to move the Earth, to reduce the sum of the angles in a triangle, to reduce the parallel to a skew, and to develop a redistribution of the curve to a straight line at a distance.

V. F. Kagan

The history of great discoveries is always awe-inspiring. But how often the circumstances under which they were made and the train of thought that led the scientist to them remain unknown! Instead, legends will be created: about Archimedes, who discovered his law in the bath, or about Newton’s apple. It is known, however, that when asked how he came to his discovery, Newton answered: “Constantly thinking about it,” but he did not describe his thoughts anywhere until he had achieved complete clarity.

This year marks the hundred-fiftieth discovery of non-Euclidean geometry by Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky. The date of this discovery is conventionally considered to be the day of February 24, 1826, when the young professor presented his report to the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Kazan University. The text of the report has not survived, but, according to the author, its contents were published in 1829 in an article entitled “On Initial Geometry.”

Lobachevsky's discovery made it possible already in the second half of the century to call him the “Copernicus of geometry,” but only now is his full significance for mathematics in general and for physics becoming clear. Let us try to briefly describe the ideas of the great geometer and highlight the train of thought that led to them.

The history of the issue resolved by Lobachevsky takes two thousand years. In the third century BC, the manual of Euclid's Elements appeared in Alexandrinus, in which geometry was presented in a strictly systematic form, little different from the one that was accepted in geometry textbooks until very recently. Euclid's construction is based on a number of initial statements (axioms) that are not proven. All alternatives (meorems) are derived from logical axioms. Among all the axioms of Euclid, the “parallel axiom” stands out: Two straight lines a and b, intersected by premium, intersect each other on my side, where the sum of the internal one-sided angles a and β is less than the sum of two right angles (Fig. 1).

This position attracted the attention of successors and commented on Euclid on various principles. Firstly, it was more complex than all other axioms—for example, such as “A straight line passes through two points” or “All right angles are equal.” Secondly, the converse statement “The sum of two angles of a triangle is less than two right angles” is easy to prove.
