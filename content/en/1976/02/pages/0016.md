It is all the more pointless to inquire about whether two given “physical lines” (for example, two light rays) will intersect or not, since never in any physical experience are these “lines” given in their entire infinite extent, but only more or less of their segments are given. Therefore, the only thing that we can assert, remaining on the basis of experience, is that Euclidean geometry is an adequate idealization of spatial representations obtained in the conditions of observing phenomena occurring on the earth’s surface, or, say, on the scale of the Solar system, but not going too far beyond these scales in either direction, i.e., on the one hand, not becoming a question about the geometry of the “world as a whole,” and on the other hand, not going into a “microworld”, say, within the confines of the atomic nucleus.

The geometry of “world regions” of average size is, of course, Euclidean geometry - in the sense that Euclidean geometry describes with sufficient accuracy everything that we actually observe in these regions. If you go beyond their limits, then, as you discover in modern physics, you may come across systems, cities that are more complex than even non-Euclidean geometry, in the sense that Lobachevsky defined it. Moreover, it is impossible to talk about a “single”, motionless geometry, since it forever embraces the entire variety of spatial relationships that can be abstracted by our cognition from the material world around us.

Lobachevsky's immortal merit is that he was the first to break through the perception of geometry as the only conceivable logical system.

It is quite understandable that when he came to his conclusions, Lobachevsky could not count not only on recognition, but even on a simple understanding of his ideas - it took half a century for these ideas to enter mathematical science and become its integral part. Therefore, during his life, Lobachevsky found himself in the difficult position of an “unrecognized scientist.” His contemporary, the Hungarian scientist Janos Bolyai, found himself in the same situation, who came to non-Euclidean geometry independently of Lobachevsky, although he published his results somewhat later than him. But what a difference between these two scientists!
If Bolyai was, so to speak, oppressed by the lack of princedom of his ideas and never found any way out of the truly deeply dramatic situation created for him by virtue of this lack of princedom, then Lobachevsky found this way out in varied, vigorous activity. The strength of Lobachevsky’s personality triumphed not only over all the difficulties of the time in which he lived, it also triumphed over what is perhaps most difficult for a scientist to survive: over the idea of ​​isolation, over a complete and comprehensive misunderstanding of what was dear to him and most needed - his scientific discoveries and ideas. Having not discovered any contradiction in the geometric system he created, and having received full confidence that there is no contradiction in it and cannot be, Lobachevsky did not give proof of consistency and thereby opened the possibility for the development of subjective assessments of the revolution he had made in geometry. He died without the main work of his life being understood and appreciated.
