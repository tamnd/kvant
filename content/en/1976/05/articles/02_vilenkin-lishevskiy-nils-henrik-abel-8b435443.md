In the center of Oslo, in the Royal Park, there is a monument to the outstanding Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel. But Abel himself erected a more impressive monument to himself. Abel's theorems, Abelian integrals, Abelian equations, Abelian groups, Abelian transformations have forever entered various branches of mathematics, and Abel obtained all his results in seven years of creativity: he died when he was not yet twenty-seven years old.

Abel was born on August 5, 1802 in the small village of Finneina in the south of Norway, in the family of a pastor. Niels was the second son; He is a rude sick boy.

In the fall of 1815, Pastor Abel sent his two eldest sons to Christanya (as Oslo was then called), to the Cathedral School. Until then, he taught them himself.

The school did not live up to Nils' expectations. Back in 1811, the best teachers left for the newly organized university. The new teachers were not very knowledgeable. They often beat students. The mathematics teacher Bader was especially cruel. It is not surprising that Nils soon lost interest in his studies and stopped getting excellent grades even in mathematics. Only visits to the theater, which Abel loved dearly, playing chess and conversations with friends brightened the teenager’s life.

Everything changed one day in 1818. Bader beat one student so badly that he died a few days later. Bader was fired, and a new mathematics teacher, Bernt Mikel Holmboe, joined the school. Holmboe himself did almost nothing in mathematics, so he was the first to notice and nurture the mathematical talent of Niels Abel.

However, the work was devoted to finding the line along which a material point falls according to a predetermined law. Abel reduced the solution to this problem to an equation in which the desired function is under the integral sign. Nowadays equations are also called integral.

No one had solved integral equations before Abel: mathematicians of that time were interested in differential equations, that is, equations containing the product of the desired functions. Only at the end of the 19th century did the general theory of integral equations begin to be developed, and then they realized that Abel anticipated future mathematical research by many decades.

In the winter of 1822-1823, Abel wrote a work devoted to the integration of functions.

If you differentiate any elementary function, you will again get an elementary function. But with interconnected things it’s more complicated. To integrate a function means to find a new function whose derivative is equal to the given one. The search for this function is usually based, as Abel himself wrote, on the basis; The scientist was waiting for insight that would allow him to calculate one or another integral. The results of the searches were presented in the fundamental work of one of the greatest mathematicians of the 18th century, Leonhard Euler, “Integral Calculus”. But many

Abel approached the issue from a new point of view. He decided to find out under what conditions the integral of a given function can be expressed in terms of elementary functions.

His work apparently contained very interesting mathematical ideas; it is impossible to give an accurate assessment of it: the manuscript subsequently disappeared without a trace, and we can judge it only by the dry lines of the minutes of the academic council and hints scattered in other Abel manuscripts.

She played an important role in Abel’s life: after nine months of study (apparently, the memory of Abel’s failure with equations of the fifth degree was still alive), the professor approved this work and decided that Abel deserved mathematical support from the state. In addition, he spent his last holidays in Copenhagen and was promised to be sent abroad after graduation to continue his education.

The Copenhagen holidays (1823) were full of unforgettable impressions. Abel met with local mathematicians and thought about Fermat's Last Theorem («Kvant», 1972, no. 8 and 1974, no. 5, p. 29).

Although Abel was unable to prove this theorem (it has not been proven, and understandably!), he obtained a number of interesting results. For example, he proved that if the natural numbers a, b, c are a solution to the equation $a^n = b^n + c^n$ (n > 2), then a must have one of the formulas $\frac{1}{2} (x^n + y^n + z^n)$, $\frac{1}{2} (x^n + y^n + n^{n-1} z^n)$, where x, y, z are coprime numbers. These results were published only after Abel's death.

Professor Degen advised Abel to study the theories of so-called elliptic integrals.

Mathematicians have long divided all functions into algebraic and transcendental. Functions $y = f(x)$ that satisfy some equation of the form (1) are called algebraic and are called transcendental. These include, in particular, the exponential, logarithmic, trigonometric and inverse trigonometric functions studied in school.

When differentiating some transcendental functions, they turn into algebraic ones, for example

$$\ln x)' = \frac{1}{x}, \arcsin x)' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}.$$

The inverse of differentiation is called integration. This means that when integrated, transcendental functions are obtained from some algebraic functions. However, not for all algebraic functions their integrals are expressed through the elementary functions studied in school. For example, when calculating the length of other ellipses, an integral of the form is obtained

$$\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{(1-x^2)(1-k^2x^2)}}.$$

If $k=0$, we get an integral equal to arcsin x (the fact is that the service $k=0$ corresponds to an ellipse with equal half-symmetry, that is, a circle, and arcsin x is related to the length of other circles). For other values ​​of $k$ it was not possible to express this integral through elementary functions. It was necessary to introduce a class of transcendental functions - elliptic integrals. This is the name given to integrals containing square roots of fourth-degree polynomials. The goal of a series of replacement outputs on such integers was obtained by Euler, Gauss, and Legendre.

Abel satisfies everything with a general formula, special cases of which were polynomials known relations for such integrals. He soon came up with an idea that allowed him to radically change his topic in this direction: instead of elliptic integrals, study their inverse functions

For $k=0$ this corresponds to the transition from studying the functions $y=\arcsin x$ to studying the inverse function $y=\sin x$. The new functions are called elliptic. Since $(\sin x)'=\cos x=\sqrt{1-\sin^2x}$, the function $y=\sin x$ satisfies the differential equation $y'=\sqrt{1-y^2}$. Abel set himself the task of obtaining differential equations for elliptic functions and coped with it brilliantly. He also proved that elliptic functions are periodic. It turned out that, unlike trigonometric functions, elliptic functions have two periods, one of them is real, and the other is complex. This required deepening into the theory of functions of a complex variable, which had just been created at that time.

Returning from Copenhagen, Abel again took up algebraic equations. Analyzing his solution to a fifth-degree equation, he realized that not only this solution was false, but also the approach to the problem itself. This is what he wrote about it later:

“One of the most interesting problems in algebra is the algebraic solution of an equation. Almost all eminent mathematicians have studied this question. Without difficulty, general expressions for the roots of equations of the first four degrees were obtained *). A single method was discovered for solving these equations, and it was found to be applicable to equations of any degree; but, despite all the conditions of Lagrange and other outstanding mathematicians, the set goal was not achieved... They were supposed to solve the equation, not knowing whether this solution was possible. If a solution existed, they could obtain it without knowing anything about it in advance; but if, unfortunately, a solution did not exist, then the whole thing could search for it six times. In order to obtain certain results on this issue, it was necessary to choose a different path, giving the probleme such a form that it would always be solvable, and this can be done with any problem. Abel was able to propose these difficulties: he proved that a general equation of the fifth degree is unsolvable in radicals - the solutions of such an equation cannot be expressed in terms of its coefficients using arithmetic operations and knowledge of the roots.

Thus, the problem that mathematicians were investigating, in order to quickly make the result available to mathematicians, Abel printed a pamphlet with the proof in French at his own expense; due to lack of funds, he had to reduce the presentation to 6 pages and provide the reader with access to the details of many arguments. It is not surprising that few mathematicians were able to fully understand the content of this work. Further, Gauss, who was most interested in the theory of algebraic equations, lost Abel's pamphlet among his papers. Subsequently, Abel published a development of the proof of his theorem, which took up several dozen pages.

It soon became clear that a few years before Abel, the Inalian scientist Paolo Ruffini had obtained a similar result. And although Ruffini’s proof was incomplete, the theorem on the unsolvability of a fifth-degree equation in radicals is now called the Ruffini-Abel theorem.

But, although the general equation of the fifth degree cannot be solved in radicals, there are a number of special cases.

For example, divide the angle $\alpha$ into $n$ equal parts to mean cos $\alpha$ through cos $\frac{\alpha}{n}$ and solve a better equation for cos $\alpha/n$. Since cos $\alpha = 4\cos^3\alpha/3$ - 3 cos $\alpha/3$, then for $n = 3$ we obtain the Cubian equation $4x^3 - 3x = \cos \alpha$, which, as in the case of the Cubian equation, is solved in radicals. It turns out that such equations can be solved in radicals for any values ​​of $n$. Similar equations are obtained when passing from trigonometric functions to elliptic ones. Abel was able to write these equations by expressing the elliptic functions of the argument $x$ in terms of the function of the argument $x/n$.

Abel knew that the question of the solvability of an equation in radicals is connected with the correspondence between the roots of the equation. For example, all the roots of the equation

$$x^{n-1} + x^{n-2} + \dots + x + 1 = 0,$$

arising when dividing a circle into $n$ parts (the pose equation (2) is called the equation of dividing a circle), can be expressed through one of them as follows:

$$x_2 = x_1^2, \quad x_3 = x_1^3, \quad \dots, \quad x_{n-1} = x_1^{n-1}.$$

The functions $y = x, y = x^2, \dots, y = x^{n-1}$ are rational. Moreover, they have the following remarkable property: if you take any two such functions, replace $x$ in one of them with another function and substitute $x_1$ for $x$ together, then the resulting number will again be one of the roots of the equation. In fact, from equation (2) it follows that $x_1^n = 1$ and then $(x_1^k)^l = x_1^{kl} = x_1^m$, where $m$ is the remainder of dividing $kl$ by $n$.

Abel realized that it was precisely this property that was associated with the solvability of the equation for dividing a circle in radicals. Therefore, he considered equations such that:

a) all roots of each of them can be represented as rational functions of one of the roots, for example, of $x_1$:

$$x_1 = \theta_1(x_1), \quad x_2 = \theta_2(x_1), \quad \dots, \quad x_n = \theta_n(x_1);$$

b) the function $\theta_1(x)$, $\dots, \theta_n(x)$ are such that for any $k$ and $l$ there is a $m$ such that $(l \theta_k(x_1)) = \theta_m(x_1)$.

It turned out that for the equation to be solvable in radicals, it is sufficient to fulfill one more condition: for any $k$ and $l$ $\theta_k(\theta_l(x_1)) = \theta_l(\theta_k(x_1))$. In other words, it is necessary that it does not matter whether we substitute $\theta_k(x_1)$ into $\theta_l(x)$ or $\theta_l(x_1)$ into $\theta_k(x)$. When studying elliptic functions and interpoles, Abel made extensive use of the theory of power series. (A power next is an expression of the form $\sum a_n x^n = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n + \dots$)

He proved that the set of values ​​$x$ for which the series $^*$) converges

$$a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_n x^n + \dots,$$

is either an interval of the form $(-l, l)$ (where $l$ can also equal zero or infinity) or the same interval to which one or both ends are attached. He proved that on the interval of convergence the power series can be differentiated and internalized, and the behavior of the sum of the series can be used as $x$ approaches the ends of the interval of convergence. All these results, after their pollination, became classical and were included in all mathematics calculation courses.

He promoted in Germany, Austria, Italy, Switzerland, France, Belgium, and became acquainted with Jacob Steiner, Andrien Legendre, Augustin Cauchy and many other mathematicians.

On October 30, 1826, at the next meeting of the French Academy of Sciences, its permanent secretary Fourier presented his own Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel and his “Memoir on the general properties of scales of a wide class of transcendental functions.” Legendre and Cauchy were commissioned to give an opinion on the presentation of the work.

Cauchy was approved as the speaker. It would be difficult to find a less suitable candidate: Cauchy was so passionate about his own research that he had no time left for anything else. Abel's "Memoir" was lost among the manuscripts cluttering Cauchy's office. In vain did Abel wish for the recognition of French scientists: the “Memoir” was found only after his death *).

Unfortunately, meetings with French mathematicians gave Abel little help. Some of them, having stopped scientific research, pursued their own careers. Others were only interested in applied mathematics - Abel's research seemed to them completely inapplicable in practice. They could not foresee that after a hundred years of theoretical meetings with the French, Cauchy’s role was played in the fate of another young genius - Evariste Galois («Kvant», 1973, No. 10, p. 5).

Tico-group methods, originating from the work of Lagrange, Abel and Galois, will become the basis of all computational methods of quantum mechanics.

The memoirs of one mathematician about the Parisian period of Abel’s life have been preserved: “Abel spoke French, German, Danish and Norwegian equally well. He was slightly above average height, and his thin, sickly face bore the stamp of fatigue and anxiety. He behaved with extraordinary speed and easily got lost, showing an amazing gentleness of character. Judging by the simplicity with which he dressed, by the fact that he allowed himself to buy some food no more than once a day and was content with a very poor apartment on the Rue Sainte-Marguerite, he had very meager means at his disposal. However, Abel met a man who showed him marks of beatings on his body and advised him to beware of robbers.

While remaining in Paris, Abel continued to work on the general theory of elliptic functions. In a letter to Bernt Holmboe, he said: “I have discovered so many wonderful theorems that I simply can’t believe it.”

Soon Abel realized that the money allotted to him for his business trip abroad would not be enough for the entire time, and decided to return it home. On the way back he fell ill with tuberculosis. This disease subsequently brought him to the grave.

On May 20, 1827, Abel returned to Oslo. After the enthusiastic welcome and ceremonial reception, a disappointing day began. Nils realized that he was never needy; he was not even offered a job with a permanent salary, for example, a university teacher or even a school teacher. Abel was forced to apply to the College: “At the moment I do not have any means of subsistence. I have been in this position since the moment I returned home and will remain until I am ready. In order to somehow earn a living, I will have to almost completely abandon scientific pursuits... That is why I dare to turn to the highly respected Collegium with a request to provide me with assistance in the form that will be most convenient.” On September 4, 1827, the College decided to pay Abel 200 dalers a year - exactly as much as he received as a student.

In March 1828, Professor Christopher Hansteen, who supervised Abel's scientific studies at the university, went on an expedition to Siberia. Hanstin recommended Abel as his deputy for the duration of the scientific trip.

Niels began teaching classes in mechanics and astronomy at the university and at the Military Academy, where Hatzsteen had previously taught. Now Abel received a significant salary for those times: 533 dalers per year, but he still lived very poorly. All the money went to pay off accumulated debts.

In the same year, Niels Henrik Abel was elected to the Royal Scientific Society of Norway. This was the only official recognition of Abel's services that he received during his lifetime.

But March 1828 brought Abel not only joy: while leafing through the Astronomical News magazine, he discovered that another scientist was very successfully working on his topic - the German mathematician Carl Gustav Jacobi.

The whole of 1828 passed in the maintenance of two young scientists. The Göttingen hermit Gauss followed this “competition” with great interest: after all, his youthful diaries, written when neither Abel nor Jacobi were yet in the world, contain many discoveries he made. But Gauss was in no hurry to publish his results: they became known only after the posthumous publication of his diaries. Neither Abel nor Jacobi were alive at that time. They had no idea about the work of their great predecessor, although not only their theorems, but even sometimes their notation coincided with Gauss’s. Yet in one area Abel Prev rose not only to Jacobi, but also to Gauss. He developed a general theory of intergoals from algebraic functions, a special case of which is elliptic functions. It was this topic that lay unread among Cauchy's papers.

The rivalry with Jacobi was not easy for Abel. He didn't get enough sleep, didn't get enough clothes; all this affected his health. Abel often felt feverish and his cough worsened.

In mid-December 1828, Abel decided to go to his friends in Froland for the Christmas holidays. Although Abel objected to this journey, Nils still decided to go.

He arrived in Froland on December 19th. On the way, Abel caught a bad cold. He was bothered by a cough and chills, but Nils continued to study mathematics.

The departure from Frolanda was set for January 9th. On this day, a harnessed sleigh was waiting for Abel in the morning. But just before leaving, Nils became ill. The doctor prescribed bed rest and complete rest.

It was a real two or three weeks, but it turned out to be short-lived. On February 21, due to an emergency, Nils wrote in a letter to the College that due to illness, Abel could not begin to fulfill his duties at the university before the spring of 1829.

Hopes of calling Abel would not have come true. Nils grew weaker and weaker. In March he hardly got out of bed. On April 6 at 4 o'clock in the afternoon, Abel died.

Abel's genius was recognized only after his death. On June 11, 1829, at a meeting of the French Academy of Sciences, Legendre announced Abel's death. Now Cauchy took only a week to prepare his conclusion. At the next meeting, the Academy heard Cauchy’s message and decided to publish Abel’s “Memoir” in a series of works by foreign scientists and award Abel, together with Jacobi, the Grand Prize for outstanding mathematical discoveries.

In conclusion, we present a few statements from mathematics about Abel.

From Gauss’ letter to Schumacher: “...I have not seen a single report in the newspapers about Abel’s death. This is a big loss for science. If a biography of this eminently remarkable man is published somewhere and you get your hands on a copy, let me know.”

From Legendre’s letter to Jacobi: “...To my greatest grief, I received the news that your worthy rival, Mr. Abel, died in Oslo from a more severe illness, which he had been suffering for some time and which was aggravated by the harsh winter. This is a heavy loss for everyone who values ​​developing the higher branches of mathematical analysis. Having lived a very short life, he managed to do so much that this is enough to leave a long memory of him; It’s easy to imagine what he would have achieved if fate had decided otherwise.”

From Jacobi’s letter to Legendre: “...The sad news came about the death of Abel... The range of issues that he dealt with is amazingly wide! Finding a necessary and sufficient condition that allows one to propose whether a given algebraic equation can be resolved in radicals, a similar condition for determining the possibilities of expressing an arbitrary interval in a closed form, replaced by the discovery of the general property of all functions that are inter-algebraic functions, etc., etc. All these problems are extremely unusual for Abel, no one even dared to pose them before him. He left us, but the mark he left is indelible."
