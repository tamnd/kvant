The billiards described in this article are not very similar to ordinary ones. Great many: The billiard table is not always straight, there is only one ball and there are no pockets or cues. But don’t be upset, we will try to show that even one ball on the Billiards field can pose many puzzles to math lovers.

§ 1. General Billiard problem
According to the laws of mechanics, when an absolutely elastic Billiard ball is reflected from a straight side MN, the “angle of incidence” of the ball is equal to the “angle of its reflection” - more precisely, the angle APM is equal to the angle BPN.
Below we will refer to this fact as the law of elastic reflection.
Already here we have assumed that the billiard ball is urine, i.e., as physicists say, “the dimensions of the ball at given distances can be neglected.” Thus, we believe that the Billiard ball is a moving point, and therefore we can talk about the trajectory of the Billiard ball - about the broken line along which the ball moves in accordance with the law of elastic reflection (Fig. 1). The break points of this broken line lie on the “sides of the billiard table”. We will be looking at Free Shape Billiard Tables.
In principle, the Billiard sides can also be curved - then the law of elastic reflection is formulated as before, only the angles of incidence and reflection should be understood as the angles between the corresponding (“incident” and “reflected”) parts of the trajectory and the tangent MN to the curved board at the point P of the ball’s collision with the side. § 3. Extremal properties of billiard trajectories

The method of constructing a billiard trajectory leading from one given point A to another given point B after one reflection from a straight side MN follows from the law of elastic reflection: move point B′, symmetrically to point B relative to straight line MN, and connect A to B′ (Fig. 9); the point P of the intersection of straight lines AB′ and MN will be the point of collision of the ball with the side MN, and the broken line APB is the desired trajectory (explain). From the construction it is very clear that the billiard trajectory APB is the shortest of all broken lines of the form AP′B with point P′ on the straight line MN leading from A to B! (Give a detailed proof.) This statement is part and the simplest case of the fundamental principle of classical mechanics - the principle of least action (in optics it is called Fermat's principle)*. In our situation, this principle can be formulated as follows: the billiard ball is “smart” and chooses the shortest of all possible paths. So far, we have established the “principle of the shortest path” only in the case of one reflection from a straight side. In problems 5-7 it is proposed to check it in cases where the ball is reflected not once, but several times, but from straight sides.

Addition to § 2

(A) Jacobi's theorem.
Let us recall how it is formulated.

Let $\alpha$ be a number incommensurable with $\pi$, $\{P_0, P_1, P_2, \dots\} = \{P_k\}$ be a sequence of mokas of the circle $\Gamma$, implying that each subsequent moka sequentially $P_{k+1}$ is obtained from the previous moka $P_k$ by repeating by an angle $\alpha$ radians. Then for any arc $\Delta$ of the circle $\Gamma$ at least one moka sequentially $\{P_k\}$ lies on this arc $\Delta$.

Proof. Let $\Delta$ be an arbitrary arc and $\varepsilon$ its radian measure. Let us choose a natural number $N$ such that $2\pi/N < \varepsilon$. Dimension of the circle $\Gamma$ into $N$ equal in length to the arc $\Delta_1, \Delta_2, \dots, \Delta_N$; the radian measure of each of them is $2\pi/N$ and less than $\varepsilon$. Let's consider the first $N+1$ points of the sequence $\{P_k\}$, i.e. points $P_0, P_1, P_2, \dots, P_N$. According to the Dirichlet principle, at least one of the $N$ arcs $\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_N$ contains at least one of these points. Let, for example, the points $P_n$ and $P_m$, where $m=n+1>n$, lie on the arc $\Delta_i$. Since $\alpha$ is incommensurable with $\pi$, then $P_m \neq P_n$ (explain!). Let the radian measure of the arc $P_nP_m$ be equal to $\delta$; then $\delta < 2\pi/N$ and even more so $\delta < \varepsilon$.

Let us now consider points of the sequence $P_n$ with numbers $n, n+l, n+2l, \dots$ (Fig. 14). They form a subsequence $\{P_n, P_{n+l}, P_{n+2l}, P_{n+3l}, \dots\}$, each subsequent point of which $P_{n+(r+1)l}$ is obtained from the previous point $P_{n+rl}$ by rotating through an angle $l\alpha$, i.e., in exactly the same way as point $P_{n+l}=P_m$ is obtained from point $P_n$. Thus, neighboring points of the selected subsequence are separated from each other by an arc of radian measure $\delta$. Since $\delta < \varepsilon$, at least one of the points $P_n, P_{n+l}, P_{n+2l}, \dots$ must fall on the arc $\Delta$. Thus, Jacobi's theorem is proven.

(B) DERIVATION OF THEOREM 1 FROM Jacobi’s THEOREM.

Let $\alpha$ be a number incommensurable with $\pi$, $\Gamma_1$ be a circle that is touched by the links of each billiard trajectory corresponding to angle $\alpha$ inside the circle $\Gamma$. Let $K$ be the ring between $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma$, and $D$ be an arbitrary circle inside the ring $K$. Let us draw through its center a chord $MN$ of the circle $\Gamma$ tangent to the circle $\Gamma_1$ (Fig. 15). Obviously, if we take a sufficiently small arc $\Delta$ of the circle $\Gamma$ with the midpoint at point $M$, then for any other point $M'$ of the arc $\Delta$ the tangent to the circle $\Gamma_1$ drawn from the point $M'$ on the same side of $\Gamma_1$ as $MN$, necessarily intersects the circle $D$ (explain!).

Let us now consider any trajectory $P_0 P_1 P_2 \dots$ of the billiard inside the circle $\Gamma$ corresponding to the angle $\alpha$. We need to prove that at least one of its links intersects the circle $D$. According to Jacobi's theorem, the points $P_0, P_1, P_2, \dots$ densely fill the circle $\Gamma$, and therefore at least one of them, say $P_n$, lies on the arc $\Delta$ chosen above. Due to the choice of this arc $\Delta$, one of the two links of the trajectory leaving the point $P_n$ necessarily intersects the circle $D$. Thus, Theorem 1 is proven.
