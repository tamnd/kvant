It is easy to understand that the magnetic field of an electric current has the most energy by analyzing electromagnetic induction. Let us mentally carry out the experiment with the diagram shown in figure. If you move the resistat slider so that the resistance of the resistat decreases, then the electric current in the circuit should increase. The greater the current value, the greater the magnetic field induction value created by this current. As the magnetic field increases, the flux of the magnetic induction vector through the plane bounded by the current-carrying circuit increases. But according to Faraday’s law, when the flux of magnetic induction changes in the circuit, an electromotive force of self-induction arises equal to

$$E_{is} = -\frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t},$$

where $\Delta \Phi$ is the change in magnetic induction flux over time $\Delta t$. The induced current caused by this $3. d. c.$ creates a magnetic field, the direction of induction of which is opposite to the induction of the magnetic field of the main current. Thus, the magnetic flux of the created field tends to compensate for the change in the magnetic flux of the main field, i.e., it actually tends to keep the main current constant. To counteract this effect, some work must be done against the electromotive force of self-niduction. Let's calculate this work.

If the current during time $\Delta t$ increased from the value $I$ to the value $I + \Delta I$ and at the same time $\mathcal{E}_{is}$ appeared, then the work of the current source against this value $3. d. c.$ is equal to

$$\Delta A = -\mathcal{E}_{is}I\Delta t.$$

Substituting here expression (1) for this $3. d. c.$ self-niduction, we obtain

$$\Delta A = \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}I\Delta t = I\Delta \Phi.$$

Now remember that each circuit can be characterized by a certain value $L$, called the self-induction coefficient or inductance. The value of $L$ is usually defined as the coefficient of proportionality in the formula relating the magnetic flux $\Phi$ to the magnitude of the force of the token $I$ that causes this token, i.e.

$$\Phi = LI.$$

Therefore, if the current changes by values ​​$\Delta I$, then the flux of magnetic inductance changes by values

$$\Delta \Phi = L \Delta I.$$

Thus, we can write expression (2) for work as follows:

$$\Delta A = LI \Delta I = \Phi \Delta I.$$

figure 1 shows the dependence of the magnetic flux $\Phi$ on the token force $I$. The work $\Delta A$ is numerically equal to the area of ​​the trapezoid $ABCD$.

If the current in the circuit changes from a value $I = 0$ to a certain value $I_0$, then the work of the token source against e. d.s. self-induction can be obtained by summing all elementary works $\Delta A$. This total work is numerically equal to the area of ​​the right triangle $OEF$ by figure 1:

$$A = \frac{LI_0^2}{2}.$$

Let us now make sure that there is no additional heat release in the circuit associated with the occurrence of e. d.s. self-induction.

Let's imagine that before starting the experiment we deformed the contour so that the area limited by it became equal to zero (Fig. 2). In this case, when the token in the chain increases from zero to $I_0$ e. d.s. self-induction does not return. The resistance of the wiring from which the circuit is made has not changed. Therefore, in a deformed circuit, when token increases from zero to $I_0$, the same amount of heat is released as in an undeformed circuit. (Of course, provided that in both cases the rate of increase in token to the value $I_0$ is the same. Let us recall that in the first experiment we must increase token, i.e., decrease the resistance, quite slowly, so that at each value $R_t$ a current $I_t = \frac{E}{R_t}$ is established in the circuit (if the internal resistance of the claim source is small).)

So, the heat release in the circuit does not depend on whether the source does additional work (6) or not.

Since we know that energy does not disappear and does not arise again, but only passes from one form to another, and the numerical work done is equal to the change in the energy of the system, we have the right to pose the question: what did the perfect source of additional work (6) turn into? The reason that this work had to be done is the loop's ability to "resist" attempts to increase magnetic flux through the area enclosed by the loop. Therefore, it is natural to call the energy in which the perfect work was transferred the energy of the magnetic field of the circuit with current. The fact that it is the magnetic field of the circuit with current that has the specified energy can be verified if an experiment is carried out that would show that this energy can be converted into some other types of energy (for example, thermal). Let us imagine that a short circuit instantly occurred between points $A$ and $B$ of our circuits. The potential difference between points $A$ and $B$, created by an external current source, became equal to zero. The current in the deformation circuit will immediately stop. In the case of a non-deformation circuit, a decrease in current to zero cannot occur instantly, since according to the law of electromagnetic induction, when the current decreases, an emission will occur. d.s. self-niduction, which seeks to keep the current at the same level. As a result, a decrease in current from the value $I_0$ to zero will occur over some time. During this time, a certain amount of heat will be released in the circuit. The energy due to which heat will be released is drawn from the magnetic field energy “stored” by the circuit. Try to show on your own that when there is a short circuit between points $A$ and $B$, an amount of heat is released in the circuit equal to the energy of the magnetic field of the current circuit found above, i.e. $L I_0^2/2$.

So, we come to the conclusion that the circuit characterizing the inductance $L$, when current $I$ flows through it, creates a magnetic field around itself, the energy of which is equal to

$$W_M = \frac{L I^2}{2}.$$

The considered example of insulation is the case when the energy of the magnetic field of the current is created by an external current source. It is obvious that the energy of the magnetic field of the current should not depend on the method of obtaining the current, just as $\frac{mv^2}{2}$ - the kinetic energy of the body's motion - does not depend on the way in which the body's speed was imparted. To verify this, let's consider another example that will use the possibility of direct conversion of the kinetic energy of a body's motion into the energy of a magnetic field. This example will also help us understand the mechanism of the emergence of a magnetic field of current at the so-called “microsoppers” level. By words at the “microsoppers” level, we mean that the occurrence of electric current will be traced by analyzing the equation of motion of electrons and ions.

Let us consider a metal ring of length $l$, uniformly rotating around an axis passing through the center of the ring perpendicular to its plane (Fig. 3). We will assume that the koludo is thin enough so that the linear velocity of all its sections is constant and equal to $v_0$. Let us assume that at time $t = 0$ a constant braking force $F$, directed tangentially to the circle, begins to act on the wheel. As a result of the braking process, the injection stops. However, this does not exhaust the role of force $F$.

It is easy to see that an electric current should arise in the ring. To see this, let us recall in general terms how solid metals are structured. The atoms of the metal substance are located at the bottom of the crystal lattice and perform only small vibrations around the equilibrium. We can consider that the crystal lattice forms a solid frame of a metal body. In contrast to onons, conduction electrins in a metal can mix quite freely in the spaces between lattice sites, forming a kind of electric gas.

Let us now turn again to our metal ring. We can imagine it as a set of two rings inserted into each other, similar to how it is depicted in figure 4. The blue color here refers to the crystal lattice, red to the electron gas. At first, when the ring rotated at a constant speed, both the crystal lattice and the electric gas took equal part in this movement. When the layer is decelerated, $F$ is applied, so to speak, to the solid frame of the ring, i.e., to the crystal lattice. The electron gas should begin to decelerate only as a result of the direct interaction of electrons with the lattice. This interaction determines the electrical resistance of the metal. Therefore, for the sake of simplicity, we can skip them altogether, considering, for example, that our ring was cooled before braking to such low temperatures that the metal passed into a superconducting state, i.e., its resistance became equal to zero.

Thus, when the ring slows down, conduction electrins in the metal begin to move around the crystal lattice. This difference in the speed of movement of ons and electrins leads to the appearance of electric current. Let's calculate the current values.

Let us denote by $v_i$ the speed of the electrons of the crystal lattice, and by $v_e$ the speed of the directional movement of conduction electrons. Then the total charge flowing through the section $s$ of the ring in time $\Delta t$ is composed of the charge of the crystal lattice, enclosed in an arc of length $v_i \Delta t$, and the charge of the electric gas, enclosed in an arc of length $v_e \Delta t$ (see Fig. 4). If we denote the total charge of all electrons in the crystal lattice by $Q$, then the total charge of conduction electrons in the ring will be equal to - $Q$ (since the metal ring as a whole is electrically electrical). Therefore, the charges are in accordance with their directions, $Q \frac{v_i \Delta t}{l}$ and $-Q \frac{v_e \Delta t}{l}$, where $l$ is the length of the ring. Now we can find the total amount of energy of the metal ring per unit time, i.e. calculate the electric current in the ring:

$$I = \frac{1}{\Delta t} \left[ Q \frac{v_i \Delta t}{l} - Q \frac{v_e \Delta t}{l} \right] = \frac{Q}{l} \left(v_i - v_e\right).$$

We agreed to neglect the direct interaction of conduction electrons with the units of the crystal lattice. However, in the process of braking, forces come into play that will still slow down the electrons. These are the forces of the vortex electric field.

When a current occurs in the ring, a magnetic field is induced. Because the current varies, this field is not constant. Consequently, it generates an eddy electric field in the metal of the ring. The forces acting on the charges from the vortex field prevent the change in current in the ring, i.e., they tend to reduce the relative speed of electrons and ions (see (8)). Consequently, these forces slow down the electrons and accelerate the ions of the crystal lattice. The work of these forces to move a single positive charge along the ring is represented by e. d.s. induction (recall that the work of an eddy field on a closed path is not zero).

In order to more clearly imagine the dynamics of the movement of the crystal lattice and conduction electrons (electron gas), it is convenient to mentally separate them and consider them separately. On figure 5, a, all the movements of the crystal lattice related to the diamnic are shown separately (blue ring), and on figure 5, b - all the movements of the electron gas related to the diamnic (red ring). Now we can easily write down Newton's law as applied to the crystal lattice and electrons. Let the masses of the blue and red rings be equal to the corresponding $M$ and $m$, and their accelerations $a_i$ and $a_e$. Then

$$Ma_i = QE - F, \quad (9)$$

$$ma_e = -QE,$$

where $E$ is the intense vortex electric field. Let us calculate the value of this tension.

Since the work done by the vortex field forces to move a single charge along the ring is equal to e. d.s. self-induction, i.e. $El = \mathcal{E}_{is}$, then

$$E = \frac{\mathcal{E}_{is}}{l} = -\frac{1}{l} \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t} = -\frac{L}{l} \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} = -\frac{QL}{l^2} \left( \frac{\Delta v_i}{\Delta t} - \frac{\Delta v_e}{\Delta t} \right) = -\frac{QL}{l^2} \left( a_i - a_e \right).$$

Thus, equation (9) of the motion of the crystal lattice and electron gas will take the following:

$$Ma_i = -\mu \left( a_i - a_e \right) - F, \quad (10)$$

where for brevity we introduce the notation $\mu = \frac{Q^2 L}{l^2}$. Solving system (10) with respect to $a_i$ and $a_e$, we find the braking acceleration of our rings:

$$a_i = -\frac{F}{M + \frac{m\mu}{m + \mu}}; \quad a_e = \frac{\mu}{m + \mu} a_i. \quad (11)$$

The minus sign indicates that during the braking process, both the crystal lattice and the electron gas will be replaced.

Knowing the accelerations, we can find the time elapsed from the start of braking to the moment the kolyz crystal lattice stops:

$$t = -\frac{v_0}{a_i} = \frac{v_0}{F} \left( M + \frac{m\mu}{m+\mu} \right).$$

Now it’s easy to find the speed $v_e = v$ that the electrons will have at the end of braking:

$$v = v_0 + a_e t = \frac{m}{m+\mu} v_0.$$

Consequently, the final value of the current in the wheel (at $v_e = v$, $v_i = 0$) is equal to

$$I = -\frac{Q}{l} v = -\frac{Q}{l} \frac{m}{m+\mu} v_0.$$

Now we can move on to solving the problem posed at the beginning: determining the energy of the magnetic field of the current arising in the wheel as a result of its braking. To do this, it is convenient to use the law of conservation of energy.

At the initial moment of time ($t = 0$), the entire energy of the kolys consists of the kinetic energy of electrons and the crystal lattice, i.e.

$$E_0 = \frac{mv_0^2}{2} + \frac{Mv_0^2}{2}.$$

During braking, part of the energy went to work against the force $F$. This “loss” of energy is obviously equal to $\Delta E = A = FS$, where $S$ is the path traveled by any point of the ring (crystal lattice) during braking:

$$S = v_0 t + \frac{a_i t^2}{2} = \frac{v_0^2}{2F} \left( M + \frac{m\mu}{m+\mu} \right).$$

So

$$A = \frac{v_0^2}{2} \left( M + \frac{m\mu}{m+\mu} \right).$$

The electron current arising in the braking process in the wheel induced a magnetic field. Consequently, by the end of braking, when the velocity of the crystal lattice is zero, the energy of the wheel consists of the kinetic energy of the electrons

$$K_e = \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{mv_0^2}{2} \left( \frac{m}{m+\mu} \right)^2$$

and magnetic field energy $W_M$. According to the law of conservation of energy

$$E_0 = K_e + W_M + A,$$

where

$$W_M = E_0 - A - K_e = \frac{Mv_0^2}{2} + \frac{mv_0^2}{2} - \frac{v_0^2}{2} \left( M + \frac{m\mu}{m+\mu} \right) - \frac{mv_0^2}{2} \left( \frac{m}{m+\mu} \right)^2 = \frac{mv_0^2}{2} \frac{m\mu}{(m+\mu)^2}.$$

Substituting the value $\mu$ into this expression and taking into account that $\frac{mv_0}{m+\mu} = -I \frac{l}{Q}$ (see (14)), we obtain

$$W_M = I^2 \frac{l^2}{Q^2} \frac{Q^2 L}{2l^2} = \frac{LI^2}{2}.$$

So, we again came to the previously obtained expression for the magnetic energy of the circuit with current $I$.

Let us note in conclusion an interesting circumstance. As is known, the arithmetic mean of two positive numbers cannot be less than the geometric mean of these numbers, i.e. $m + \mu \geq 2\sqrt{m\mu}$, and therefore $\frac{m\mu}{(m+\mu)^2} \leq \frac{1}{4}$. Therefore, from expression (15) we can conclude that

$$W_M \leq \frac{1}{4} \frac{mv_0^2}{2}.$$

Thus, we come to the conclusion: when braking the first wound untwisted circuit, no more than 25% of the initial kinetic energy of the directional movement of conduction electrons can transfer into the energy of the magnetic field of the current. This limitation does not depend on the value of the circuit inductance.
