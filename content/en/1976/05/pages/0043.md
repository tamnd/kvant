⟦folio 41⟧

Now calculate the differences in distances from the foci to the points belonging to different lines of the drawing. You will notice that when moving from the first line to the second, this difference increases by 3 times, from the first to the third - by 5 times, etc. For distance differences - let's denote it by δ - we can write the formula experimentally

$$\delta = (2k + 1) a, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Here $a$ is some constant having the dimension of distance. It is not difficult to verify experimentally that the value of this constant does not depend on the position of the point of contact of the tube with a sheet of paper, but is determined only by the length of the tube: the longer the tube, the greater the value $a$. On the other hand, the length of the tube sets the frequency of its own vibrations (similar to the free vibrations of a cord or string; see the textbook for grade 10), and the tube returns vibrations in the paper sheet. What are these quantities that depend on the oscillation frequency and have the dimension of distance? Obviously, this is nothing more than the wavelength, or at least a value proportional to it.

Thus, we come to the assumption that some kind of wave is propagating in a sheet of paper. But which one? A traveling wave cannot produce a stationary distribution of powder on a sheet, but in experiment it can be achieved. Consequently, if there is a wave here, then it is not a traveling one, but a standing one, which is formed as a result of the interference of two traveling waves.

The source of one wave is the end of an oscillating tube. Where does the second wave, corresponding to the first, come from? It is reasonable to assume that it appears as a result of the reflection of a traveling wave by the edge of a sheet of paper.
