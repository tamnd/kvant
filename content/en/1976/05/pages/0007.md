However, the integrals still could not be calculated. And it was not clear what was the matter: a lack of pro-Earl knowledge or the fact that these integrals cannot be expressed through elementary functions.

Abel approached the issue from a new point of view. He decided to find out under what conditions the integral of a given function can be expressed in terms of elementary functions.

His work apparently contained very interesting mathematical ideas; it is impossible to give an accurate assessment of it: the manuscript subsequently disappeared without a trace, and we can judge it only by the dry lines of the minutes of the academic council and hints scattered in other Abel manuscripts.

She played an important role in Abel’s life: after nine months of study (apparently, the memory of Abel’s failure with equations of the fifth degree was still alive), the professor approved this work and decided that Abel deserved mathematical support from the state. In addition, he spent his last holidays in Copenhagen and was promised to be sent abroad after graduation to continue his education.

The Copenhagen holidays (1823) were full of unforgettable impressions. Abel met with local mathematicians and thought about Fermat's Last Theorem («Kvant», 1972, no. 8 and 1974, no. 5, p. 29).

Although Abel was unable to prove this theorem (it has not been proven, and understandably!), he obtained a number of interesting results. For example, he proved that if the natural numbers a, b, c are a solution to the equation $a^n = b^n + c^n$ (n > 2), then a must have one of the formulas $\frac{1}{2} (x^n + y^n + z^n)$, $\frac{1}{2} (x^n + y^n + n^{n-1} z^n)$, where x, y, z are coprime numbers. These results were published only after Abel's death.

Professor Degen advised Abel to study the theories of so-called elliptic integrals.

Mathematicians have long divided all functions into algebraic and transcendental. Functions $y = f(x)$ that satisfy some equation of the form (1) are called algebraic and are called transcendental. These include, in particular, the exponential, logarithmic, trigonometric and inverse trigonometric functions studied in school.

When differentiating some transcendental functions, they turn into algebraic ones, for example

$$\ln x)' = \frac{1}{x}, \arcsin x)' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}.$$

The inverse of differentiation is called integration. This means that when integrated, transcendental functions are obtained from some algebraic functions. However, not for all algebraic functions their integrals are expressed through the elementary functions studied in school. For example, when calculating the length of other ellipses, an integral of the form is obtained

$$\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{(1-x^2)(1-k^2x^2)}}.$$

If $k=0$, we get an integral equal to arcsin x (the fact is that the service $k=0$ corresponds to an ellipse with equal half-symmetry, that is, a circle, and arcsin x is related to the length of other circles). For other values ​​of $k$ it was not possible to express this integral through elementary functions. It was necessary to introduce a class of transcendental functions - elliptic integrals. This is the name given to integrals containing square roots of fourth-degree polynomials. The goal of a series of replacement outputs on such integers was obtained by Euler, Gauss, and Legendre.
