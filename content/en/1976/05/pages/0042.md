With the help of a sounding tube you can perform many beautiful and honoring experiments. Let's look at one of them. Cover the table with a layer of foam rubber at least 1 cm thick, and place a sheet of thick white paper on top. In our experiments, we used a sheet with a thickness of 0.3 mm and dimensions of 200 × 290 mm. Sprinkle the sheet evenly with finely crushed crystals of potassium permanganate. Taking a glass tube 30-40 cm long in the middle, place it vertically and lightly touch its end to a sheet of paper at a distance of several centimeters from its edge. Using friction, excite vibrations of the tube. At the same time, as soon as you hear the sound, the crystals of potassium permanganate will move and come together, forming certain lines.

The experience is very beautiful and everyone who sees it for the first time is more impressed. But it must be said that successfully delivering an experience requires a certain experimental skill. The tube must be held so that it only slightly touches the paper sheet. It might be better to wrap the tube in the middle with several layers of paper and secure it in a shatee.

When oscillations are excited, you should not press hard on the tube, otherwise there will be too good an “acoustic contact” between its end and the sheet, and the experiment will fail. The pipe and the tripod must be clean. In a word, at first you will need a lot of ingenuity and authenticity to get the desired result.

And this result is worthy of study! In fact, isn’t it interesting why powder evenly poured onto paper naturally collects in certain places as soon as the tube begins to sound? Why does the figure appearing on the sheet depend on the length of the tube? Why does the picture change depending on the distance between the end of the tube and the edge of the sheet (Fig. 1)?

Next, a quick glance at a sheet with redistributed crystals of potassium permanganate allows us to suggest that the lines where the crystals gather represent a family of hyperbolas. Check if this is true. Using a sharpened pencil, mark on the sheet the lines to be examined and the position of the end of the tube. Having removed the potassium permanganate, place a sheet of paper on another, “prick” the experimental points with a needle, and use a pencil to mark the position of the edge of the sheet. By connecting the points with smooth lines, you will get a drawing similar to the one shown in figure 2. Construct point $S'$ on this drawing, symmetrically relative to the edge of the sheet and point $S$ touching the tube.

By definition, a hyperbola is the locus of points whose distance difference to two given points, called the foci of the hyperbola, is constant. If the assumption that the lines obtained in the experiment are hyperbolas is true, then it is very likely that the points $S$ and $S'$ are the foci of the family of hyperbolas. Select an arbitrary point on any of the drawing lines. Measure the distances from it to points $S$ and $S'$. Calculate the difference between these distances. On the same line, take any other point and do the same for it. You will get a result within the experimental errors that coincides with the first one. By adding a few more points on the selected line, it is not difficult to verify that this line is indeed a hyperbola.
