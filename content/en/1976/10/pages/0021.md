Diffraction of light. We could end our article here, having made the choice that the wave theory turned out to be more correct than geometric optics. But before we put an end to it, let's once again evaluate the intensity of focused solar radiation, that is, let's see what results the wave theory leads to in the case when we successfully managed without it.

Let's take the same lens ($a=2\text{ cm}, f=10\text{ cm}$), but use a new formula for the gain. Formula (8) includes the wavelength of light $\lambda$. It can be considered equal to $4500\mathring{\text{A}}=4,5\cdot 10^{-5}\text{ cm}$.

Then $K_d \approx \frac{a^4}{\lambda^2 f^2} \approx 8\cdot 10^7$. Next, remember that $I_0 \approx 0,14 \text{ bm/cm}^2$, we find the desired intensity: $I_F = I_0 K_d \approx 1,1\cdot 10^7 \text{ bm/cm}^2 = 11 \text{ Mb/m/cm}^2$.

The result was a colossal value, almost 200,000 times higher than the results of our old calculation, which is in good agreement with experiment!

Agree that there is no way to end the article at this point. It turns out that the diffraction formula also brought us a “surprise”, and we need to figure out what’s going on here.

5. Why do two correct formulas lead to different results?

It turns out that formula (4) for the gain, derived on the basis of geometric optics, well describes the focusing of solar radiation, but is not suitable for stars. Diffraction formula (8) correctly describes the focusing of starlight, but leads to incorrect results for the Sun. The solution to the “paradox” lies in the fact that using one or another physical formula is completely insufficient to correctly drive the wave theory in the case when we successfully managed without it.

How to formulate the conditions of application in this case? Analyzing the conclusions of the beam and diffraction formulas, one can notice that the difference between them arose when calculating the radius of the focal spot.

Let's rewrite the formulas for determining $r_F$ again, to remind you how they were derived.

1) “Solar” formula: $r_F = f\theta_0$. In the derivation, a source with an angular size of $\theta_0$ is considered; the diffraction development that does not take it into account is $\theta_d = 0$.

2) “Star” formula: $r_F = f\theta_d$. In the derivation, taking into account the diffraction development better at the angle $\theta_d \approx \frac{\lambda}{a}$, the angular size of the source was considered vanishingly small (parallel rays), that is, $\theta_0 = 0$.

In reality, of course, the diffraction of light is significant, and, strictly speaking, there are no “exact” sources in nature. Therefore, it would be necessary to take into account both simultaneously. Then the radius of the focal spot would be determined by the formula

$$r_F \approx f(\theta_0 + \theta_d).$$

How this correspondence is obtained is easy to understand from figure 6. Here the construction is repeated, performed on figure 2 (the source of finite angular dimensions $\theta_0$), but the diffraction of light is taken into account (blurring of the rays by the angle $\theta_d$).
