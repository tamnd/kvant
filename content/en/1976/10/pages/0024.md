The reason for this is the surface tension in the liquid. Because of it, the jet appears to be wrapped in a film (similar to a rubber tube or a stretched string). Under the influence of random external influences, this film is deformed and vibrations begin in it. The oscillations propagate over the surface, and surface waves called capillary waves appear in the jet. It is not difficult to explain their formation qualitatively.

Let the surface of the liquid be randomly curved in some place, for example, become concave (Fig. 2, a). The pressure in the liquid under the concave surface, due to surface tension forces, is less than in neighboring areas where the surface remains flat. Under the influence of the pressure difference, liquid from neighboring areas begins to flow under the concave surface until the surface becomes flat again. But the movement of the fluid will not stop and will continue by inertia. Therefore, the surface will become convex, the pressure under it will increase, and the liquid will flow out from under it (Fig. 2, b), etc. Such vibrations in the liquid will naturally cause similar vibrations in neighboring areas, that is, a wave will arise.

Generally speaking, surface waves also arise under the influence of gravity. But at small oscillation amplitudes and short wavelengths, the main role is played by surface tension forces. The waves in this case are called capillary.

You can directly verify by experiment that in a jet in the presence of an obstacle it is precisely wave surfaces that arise. We slightly bend the sharp corners of only the surface of the jet - the accordion is exactly the same as when introducing an obstacle over the entire cross-section of the jet (Fig. 3).
