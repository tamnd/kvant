The mapping $Z_0$ is said to be a “composition” of the mappings $S_x$ and $S_y$; write this fact in the form of equality

$$Z_0 = S_y \circ S_x.$$

Here the circle $\circ$ is the sign of the operation on mappings. Just as the operations of addition ("+" sign) or multiplication ("×" sign) applied to a pair of numbers $\langle a, b \rangle$ produce new numbers:

$$c = a + b, \quad d = a \times b,$$

a composition operation applied to two mappings produces a new mapping.

We will be interested in the inverse mappings of a certain set $M$ onto itself. Such mappings are called “transformations of the set $M$”. As examples, we give the movement of a plane, a ready-made one, and a similarity transformation.

Let the set $M$ be a plane. Let us consider the set $G$ of all displacements of this plane, that is, the set of all mappings $F$ of the plane $M$ onto itself, preserving distances: for any two points $P$ and $Q$ of the plane $M$

$$|F(P)F(Q)| = |PQ|.$$

All movements are inverse, and therefore, according to the above terminology, they are plane transformations.

Our set $G$ has two interesting properties:

(1) the composition of two transformations from $G$ belongs to $G$, i.e. the composition of two displacements is a displacement;

(2) along with the transformations $F$, the set $G$ always also includes an inverse transformation, that is, a transformation inverse to a displacement is also a displacement.

About the limit. The set of transformations of the set $A$, which has properties (1) and (2), is called the group of transformations of the set $A$.

In view of the above, the set of all displacements of a plane can serve as an example of a transformation group of a plane. Another example is the set of all similarity transformations.

There are, however, much simpler examples. Consider, for example, the set $G_1$ of all displacements that the equilateral triangle $ABC$ (Fig. 3) ​​maps onto itself. It is easy to indicate six such movements:

1) the same effective mapping $E$, mapping any point $P$ of the plane onto itself;

2) rotate $R_0^{120^\circ}$ around the center of triangle $O$ by $120^\circ$ counterclockwise;

3) rotate $R_0^{-120^\circ}$ around center $O$ by $120^\circ$ clockwise;

4), 5), 6) axial symmetrines $S_{OA}$, $S_{OB}$, $S_{OC}$.
