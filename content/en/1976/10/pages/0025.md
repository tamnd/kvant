⟦folio 23⟧

The volume $V$ of water flowing out of the tap during time $t$ is equal to

$$V = Svt = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}vt,$$

where $S$ is the area of ​​the jet section, $d$ is the diameter of this section, $v$ is the speed of water outflow, equal to the speed of wave propagation. From here

$$v = \frac{4V}{\pi d^2t}.$$

The volume of water can be measured with a beaker, the diameter of the stream with a ruler, and the time with a stopwatch.

The question may arise - why do we see an accordion? The amplitudes of capitular waves are very small (on the order of tenths and even hundredths of a millimeter), so it is impossible to directly distinguish between bulges and depressions on the surface of the jet. However, these neonovae, in relation to the light shining on them, behave like convex and concave lenses (they collect and scatter light). In fact, we see alternating light and dark stripes, respectively, during the bulges and vlinnes.

Note that experiments can be carried out using a variety of obstacles: plastic, metal or paper plates, and even just a water surface. The elastic properties of the barrier materials are different, but the speed and wavelength in the water jet are the same in all cases.
