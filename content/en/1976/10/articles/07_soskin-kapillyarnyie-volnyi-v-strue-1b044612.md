At the IX City Conference of Young Physicists in Kyiv (April 1975), an 8th grade student at secondary school No. 145 Stanislav Sokkin made a report on the topic “Waves in a stream in the presence of an obstacle.”
The report talked about experiments carried out with a stream of water or some other liquid.
These experiments, very simple and visual, not requiring any special equipment, make it possible to get acquainted with a rather complex physical phenomenon - the appearance of capillary waves on the surface of a liquid.
Here we publish (with some abbreviations) the text of this report.

If you look carefully, you can see many interesting phenomena even in the flow of water. Try to describe and explain one of them.

We place a plate (or even just a finger) under a thin water stream at a distance of 1-7 cm from the tap (where the flow can still be considered laminar). Immediately, part of the stream above the plate will become like an accordion (Fig. 1). What happened?

The reason for this is the surface tension in the liquid. Because of it, the jet appears to be wrapped in a film (similar to a rubber tube or a stretched string). Under the influence of random external influences, this film is deformed and vibrations begin in it. The oscillations propagate over the surface, and surface waves called capillary waves appear in the jet. It is not difficult to explain their formation qualitatively.

Let the surface of the liquid be randomly curved in some place, for example, become concave (Fig. 2, a). The pressure in the liquid under the concave surface, due to surface tension forces, is less than in neighboring areas where the surface remains flat. Under the influence of the pressure difference, liquid from neighboring areas begins to flow under the concave surface until the surface becomes flat again. But the movement of the fluid will not stop and will continue by inertia. Therefore, the surface will become convex, the pressure under it will increase, and the liquid will flow out from under it (Fig. 2, b), etc. Such vibrations in the liquid will naturally cause similar vibrations in neighboring areas, that is, a wave will arise.

Generally speaking, surface waves also arise under the influence of gravity. But at small oscillation amplitudes and short wavelengths, the main role is played by surface tension forces. The waves in this case are called capillary.

You can directly verify by experiment that in a jet in the presence of an obstacle it is precisely wave surfaces that arise. We slightly bend the sharp corners of only the surface of the jet - the accordion is exactly the same as when introducing an obstacle over the entire cross-section of the jet (Fig. 3).

Having carried out several experiments, it is possible to deduce unexplored quantitative patterns. Theoretical

The volume $V$ of water flowing out of the tap during time $t$ is equal to

$$V = Svt = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}vt,$$

where $S$ is the area of ​​the jet section, $d$ is the diameter of this section, $v$ is the speed of water outflow, equal to the speed of wave propagation. From here

$$v = \frac{4V}{\pi d^2t}.$$

The volume of water can be measured with a beaker, the diameter of the stream with a ruler, and the time with a stopwatch.

The question may arise - why do we see an accordion? The amplitudes of capitular waves are very small (on the order of tenths and even hundredths of a millimeter), so it is impossible to directly distinguish between bulges and depressions on the surface of the jet. However, these neonovae, in relation to the light shining on them, behave like convex and concave lenses (they collect and scatter light). In fact, we see alternating light and dark stripes, respectively, during the bulges and vlinnes.

Note that experiments can be carried out using a variety of obstacles: plastic, metal or paper plates, and even just a water surface. The elastic properties of the barrier materials are different, but the speed and wavelength in the water jet are the same in all cases.
