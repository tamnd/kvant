P. Blukh IN FOCUS
LENSES

“... all bodies on which the radiation falls heat up, that is, they acquire some colic energy.”
R.P.o. l.b. Optics and atomic physics

Take a magnifying glass, point it at the Sun, and place a piece of paper in focus. Probably every reader «Kvant» knows what will happen next. In the place where the blindingly bright image of the Sun appears, the temperature rises so much that the paper begins to smoke and may even catch fire. If you try to repeat the same experiment at night, directing an increase
the identifying glass on some star, then the paper, of course, will not light up, although you can see the star. This, of course, will not surprise anyone, and when asked why paper lights up from the Sun, but not from the stars, there is usually a simple answer: the whole point is that the Sun shines strongly, and the star shines weakly.

There is no mistake in such an explanation, but if you try to confirm it with calculations (without them the answer will remain just a guess and not an explanation), then it turns out that the matter is not so simple.

1. It is easy to explain why paper lights up from the Sun (the first correct formula for the lens gain)

All bodies on Earth receive some energy from the Sun. Let us define by integisity the amount of energy received by a unit of area per unit of time.

Let's try to calculate how many times the intensity of solar radiation increases as a result of focusing it with a lens. The calculation is very simple and is based on the law of conservation of energy. Obviously, the total power $P_0$ of radiation passing through the lens is equal to

$$P_0 = S_0 I_0,$$

(1)

where $S_0$ is the area of ​​the lens (this quantity is also called the aperture area), and $I_0$ is the radiation integrity before focusing. The radiation power in the focal path $P_F$ is determined in a similar way:

$$P_F = S_F I_F.$$

(2)

Here $S_F$ is the area of ​​the focal path (the image of the Sun), and $I_F$ is the integisity at the focus (Fig. 1).

The focal path receives all the power that passes through the lens aperture *). Therefore, $P_0$ and $P_F$ are equal to each other. If we now equate the right-hand sides of the equalities

*) This statement is not strict. Some of the radiation is “lost” when reflected from the surface of the lens and as a result of absorption in the glass. But we will consider the “ideal” case by transforming these examples.

(1) and (2), one can find the relation of integisities:

$$\frac{I_F}{I_0} = \frac{S_0}{S_F}.$$

This ratio is called the lens integrity gain, or, in short, simply the gain. Let's denote it by the letter $K^*$. So, the gain is equal to the ratio of the plane aperture and the source image at focus:

$$K = \frac{S_0}{S_F}.$$

(3)

In order for this formula to be practically used, it is necessary to express both areas through the main parameters of the lens - its radius $a$ and focal length $f$.

The aperture area is determined immediately: $S_0 = \pi a^2$. We will write a similar formula for the area of ​​the focal path, the radius of which will be denoted by $r_F$:

$$S_F = \pi r_F^2.$$

Now all that remains is to find $r_F$. The focal spot is an image of the Sun. Its radius can be determined by comparing two similar triangles shown in figure2:

$$r_F = f \frac{r_\odot}{R} = f \theta_0.$$

First, we express $I_0$ in terms of the total power $P$, which the Sun radiates in all directions (Fig. 3):

$$I_0 = \frac{P}{4\pi R^2}.$$

Thus, $I_0$ kills inversely proportional to $R^2$. On the other hand, the denominator of formula (4) is worth the value $\theta_0^2 = \frac{r^2}{\dot{R}^2}$. Therefore, as the distance to the Sun increases, the lens gain will increase in proportion to $R^2$, and the product $I_0 K$, that is, the intensity at the focus, will remain unchanged:

$$I_F = I_0 K = \frac{P}{4\pi R^2} \cdot \frac{a^2}{f^2 \theta_0^2} = \frac{P}{4\pi R^2} \cdot \frac{a^2}{f^2} \cdot \frac{R^2}{\dot{R}^2} = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} \cdot \frac{a^2}{f^2}.$$

But among the stars there are many that are similar to our Sun in size and emitted power. This means that all of them will create at the focus of the lens the same intensity as the Sun, at which the paper lights up and the wood chars? One feels that something is wrong here: on the one hand, the formula seems to be correct, but on the other hand, it is still not possible to light the paper from the light of a star!

First of all, the following explanation comes to mind: the paper does not light up, probably because, although the intensity in focus remains unchanged, when the source is removed, the size of the image decreases. A very small area is heated, and now all the time is allocated to the adjacent cooler parts of the paper. Indeed, the area of ​​the focal spot where the radiation energy enters kills as the source moves away in inverse proportion to $R^2$: $S_F = \pi r^2 F = \pi f^2 \theta_0^2 = \pi f^2 \frac{r^2}{\dot{R}^2}$. At the same time

The spot boundary $L$ (heat removal occurs through it) kills in inverse proportion to $R$: $L = 2\pi r_F = 2\pi f \frac{r}{\dot{R}}$. Thus, as $R$ increases, the heating of the paper decreases faster than its cooling, and a lower temperature is established at the focus.

The explanation is completely plausible, and the formula seems to have been saved. By the way, let’s determine what the size of the image will be when the Sun moves away at least to the nearest star, that is, at a distance of $R = 4 \cdot 10^{13}$ km. For the lens we have already chosen as an example, with a focal length of $f = 10$ cm, radUNC spots

$$r_F = f \frac{r}{\dot{R}} \approx 1,75 \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ см}.$$

The sun has shrunk to the size of one molecule! After this calculation, the fact that paper does not light up from starlight seems completely natural. But here a new uncertainty awaits us. The image size turns out to be approximately 250 times smaller than the wavelength of visible light ($\lambda \approx 4,5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ cm). This is completely unacceptable! After all, for a wave to exist, it is essential to have a space that covers at least several wavelengths. Otherwise, the wave simply “will not fit” there.

Does this mean that in our example the wave nature of light has been “violated”? Or, perhaps, for some reason the lens does not create any image smaller than the wavelength?

3. The thing is that light is a wave (the second correct formula for the lens gain)

The second assumption is, of course, correct, and the reason limiting images is now well known - diffraction of light.

We will be presented with the formula for the lens gain again, but this time taking into account diffraction. Take a look at figure 4. Here is a bunch of parallel rays coming from a very distant source. The rays are refracted in the glass in such a way that after passing through the lens they all converge at one point - the focus. The same focusing process can be explained in another way, considering not the intersection of rays, but the interaction of waves propagating from each point of the lens aperture. Our reasoning is based on the so-called Huygen principle. According to this principle, each point in space in which a wave propagates itself becomes a source of secondary waves. These waves diverge from the aperture in all directions. They also fall into the focus of the lens. Point $F$ (lens focus) differs from all other points in space in that the phases of waves coming from any place on the aperture - from the edges, from the middle, etc. - coincide with each other. The in-phase nature of the waves at point $F$ is achieved by a specially selected glass formula. This, in fact, is what focusing is all about: when oscillations with coinciding phases are added, they mutually reinforce each other, and the intensity of the wave at point $F$ is the highest.

It may seem that the wave explanation is no better than the beam explanation, except that it is longer and less understandable. But now we will see that it has a very important advantage. Let's laugh a little

Let's move from the focus in the direction perpendicular to the axis of the lens: let's move from $F$ to $F'$ (see Fig. 4). What happens to the intensity?

According to geometric optics, the answer is: the rays do not pass through the point $F'$, therefore, when mixing from $F$ to $F'$, the intensity will drop to zero, no matter how small the segment $FF'$ is.

The situation is completely different when we consider the interaction of waves. If the mixing $FF'$ is small, then the phases of the waves coming from different points of the aperture shift “slightly” relative to each other. The intensity will decrease somewhat, but the decrease in intensity will occur gradually, smoothly.

Let us calculate the magnitude of the mixing $FF'$, at which the waves coming from the extreme points of the aperture find themselves in antiphase. This will mean that the waves will cancel each other out and the intensity will drop significantly. The length of the segment $FF'$, at which the difference in the path of the rays $AF'$ and $BF'$ is half the length of the light wave, can be taken as the radius of the focal spot $r_F$ (the phase shift $180^\circ$ corresponds to such mixing).

So we have the conditions:

$$AF' - BF' = \frac{\lambda}{2}.$$

Consider the right triangles $ACF'$ and $BCF'$. Based on the Pythagorean theorem

$$(AF')^2 = (AC)^2 + (CF')^2,$$
$$(BF')^2 = (CF')^2 + (BC')^2,$$

or, use our old notation:

$$(AF')^2 = (a + r_F)^2 + f^2,$$
$$(BF')^2 = (a - r_F)^2 + f^2.$$

Subtract the second from the first equality and use the formula for the difference of squares, then we get:

$$(AF' - BF')(AF' + BF') = 4ar_F.$$ For small mixings $FF'$ the sum of the segments $AF' + BF'$ can be approximately replaced by $2AF$. If, in addition, the focal length $f$ is large

After these simplifications, the desired path difference $AF' - BF'$ can be written as follows:

$$AF' - BF' = \frac{4ar_F}{AF' + BF'} \approx \frac{2ar_F}{f}.$$

According to condition (5), it must be equated to $\lambda/2$, and we obtain the formula for the radius of the focal spot:

$$r_F \approx \frac{\lambda}{4a} f.$$

In view of the approximate derivation of this formula, it should be considered only as an estimate of the radius in order of magnitude. Therefore, it makes no sense to leave a numerical coefficient in it, but it is better to rewrite it in the following form:

$$r_F \approx f \frac{\lambda}{a}.$$

(6)

To visualize the result obtained, take a look at figure 5. It shows the usual construction of rays crossing after the assumption in the lens, but each ray is symmetrically “blurred” by an angle $\pm \theta_d$. Beams of rays, intersecting in the focal plane, create an image in the form of a circle with a radius

$$r_F \approx f\theta_d.$$

(7)

If during construction we choose the angle $\theta_d$ equal to $\lambda/a$, then we will get formula (6). The angle $\theta_d \approx \lambda/a$ is called the diffraction blur angle.

Now the hardest part of the calculation is behind us. Having determined the area of ​​the focal spot and used correspondence (3), we obtain a new formula for the lens gain:

$$K_d \approx \frac{a}{\lambda^2f^2}$$

(8)

(we added the index “$d$” so as not to forget that the formula was derived taking into account diffraction).

4. The stars are fine, but the new formula is no good for the Sun!

Let's return again to our thought experiment with the removal of the Sun to interstellar distances. Let's see how the focal intensity will change if we use the new formula for the gain. The same as before, $I_F = I_0K_d$, but, unlike the first formula, the value of $K_d$ does not depend on the distance to the source (see formula (8)). Therefore, the intensity at the focus will decrease with $I_0$, that is, inversely proportional to the square of the distance. This is confirmed by the corresponding formula

$$I_F = I_0K_d \approx \frac{P}{4\pi R^2} \cdot \frac{a^4}{\lambda^2f^2}.$$

It is clear that for very large $R$ the intensity will eventually become vanishingly small, no matter what the radiation power $P$ the source has. This is the explanation why the paper at the focus of the lens does not light up from the light of the stars.

The second problem - the infinite reduction in the radius of the focal spot - has now also been eliminated. Indeed, according to formula (6), the radius of the focal spot can never become less than the wavelength (the fraction $f/a$ is always greater than units). Therefore, there is no longer any need to worry about the wave “not fitting” into the focus of the lens.

So, the new calculation is free from those contradictions that we discovered earlier, which we did not yet take into account
