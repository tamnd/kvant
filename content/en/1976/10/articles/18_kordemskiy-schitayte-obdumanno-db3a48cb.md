B. Kordemsky
Count wisely
(Dialogue of the other two)

Petya. I like to count, find the numerical values ​​of algebraic expressions, perform arithmetic operations - do the actions according to the rules in the order indicated, but just be careful not to miscalculate.

Kolya. I also like to count, but I don’t want to exhaust myself to the level of arithmetic. If you need to perform several actions in a row, then spending a minute to think about the air can be simplified, I save 5 minutes, and maybe more.

Example 1. Embroider the amount 73 + 76 + 66 + 68 + 75 + 67 + 78.

Petya. I add up the units: 3 + 6 + 6 + 8 + 5 + 7 + 8 = 43. I write 3 units, and add 4 tens to the other tens...

Kolya. But I would have acted differently. I notice that all 7 terms are close to the number 70, so we need to multiply 70 by 7, and take into account the excesses and deficiencies: 70 7 + 3 + 6 - 4 - 2 + 5 - 3 + 8 = 490 + 13 = 503.

Example 2. Embroider

$$\left(\frac{72}{13}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{30}{13}\right)^2.$$

Petya. Now I also want to look for a more rational way. The numbers 72 and 30 have a common factor of 6, therefore $$\left(\frac{6}{13}\right)^2$$ can be taken out of the bracket, and then $$\left(\frac{6}{13}\right)^2 \cdot (12^2 + 5^2) = \frac{36 \cdot 169}{169} = 36.$$

It turns out that even I can “mentally” cope with such calculations.

Example 3: Divide 28,902 by 7 $\frac{1}{2}$.

Kolya. It is convenient to use this method: add one third of it to a given number and divide the result by 10 - after all, 1:7 $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{15} = \frac{4}{30} = \frac{1}{10} \left(1 + \frac{1}{3}\right)$, therefore 28,902: $7 \frac{1}{2} = (28 902 + 9 634): 10 = 3 853,6.$

Example 4. Name $a^2 + ab + b^2$ if $a = 236, b = 164$.

Petya. Amount $a + b = 400$. This is fortunate. This number is easy to square. So, I transform this expression to the form: $(a + b)^2 — ab$, and now $400^2 — 236 \cdot 164 = ...$. Storp. I seem to have actually become observed. I see that the product 236·164 is not difficult to calculate. Indeed, $236 \cdot 164 = (200 + 36) \cdot (200 - 36) = 200^2 - 36^2$. I finally get $400^2 — 200^2 + 36^2 = 600 \cdot 200 + 36^2 = 120 000 + 1296 = 121 296.$

Kolya. Well, if so, then I’ll give you one example, and you explain why this happens.

Example 5. Let's embroider $58^2$.

I'm deciding. 58 - 25 = 33, $8^2 = 64$. I add 64 to 33, I get 3364. Or like this: 25 + 8 = 33, $8^2 = 64$; answer: 3364. When else can you calculate the squares of integers like this?
