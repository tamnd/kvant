⟦folio 61⟧

This year's commission was Academician of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences G. Marx.

According to the Olympiad statute, problems are compiled on the basis of a special program. This program basically includes all those topics that are studied in secondary schools in all participating countries. But there are some additional ones. For example, the topic “Rotational motion of rigid bodies”, which is not studied by schoolchildren in our country.

This year, teams from 10 countries took part in the Olympiad: Bulgaria, Hungary, East Germany, Poland, Romania, USSR, France, Germany, Czechoslovakia and Sweden. An observer from Finland was also present at the Olympics. Each team, as always, consisted of 5 participants.

The USSR team included the winners of the All-Union Olympiad, who showed good results at the three-week training camp in Gorki Leninskiye:

Vladimir Bulatom is a graduate of physics and mathematics boarding school No. 45 at Leningrad State University;

Andrey Golubentsev - graduate of secondary school No. 13 in Saratov;

Vladimir Krivtsun - graduate of secondary school No. 27 in Kharkov;

Valery Staratsenko - graduate of secondary school No. 28 in Zaporozhye;

Ildar Khamitov is a graduate of physics and mathematics boarding school No. 45 at Leningrad State University.

Of course, during the Olympics, excursions were organized along the Danube, along Lake Balaton and to the cheerful Budapest Luna Park. But the main thing is solving problems.

What tasks were given to the Olympiad participants? All theoretical problems are placed in the “Problem «Kvant»” (see problem F428, F429 and F432 in this issue of the journal). In terms of difficulty, they are approximately the same as the problems of the final type of the All-Union Olympiad. For solving each theoretical problem, you could get a maximum of 10 points. 13 participants received the greatest number of points (9 and 10) for solving the first problem, 14 for the second, and 8 for the third.

You could get up to 20 points for completing experimental work. The task was like this:

On the desktop there are: a clock, a thermometer, a heated element at 12 °C, two test tubes with a liquid of known specific temperature $c_0 = 0,5$ cal/(2·erad) and a crystalline material $X$ with unknown tell properties. The amount of liquid in the test tubes and the mass of the crystal $X$ are known. Material $X$ does not become etched in liquid.

Investigate thermal materials $X$ in the range from room temperature to 80 °C and determine its thermal constants. Present the measurement results in the form of tables and graphs.

You can only dispose of those devices and/materials that are on the table. Damaged devices and used materials will not be replaced.

This task cannot be called difficult, but it was completed a little. If for theoretical tasks approximately 20% of participants scored 27-30 points, then for experimental work only 4 participants received 19-20 points, i.e. less than 10%.
