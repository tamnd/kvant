Using these graphs, you can determine the melting temperature of the crystal, the specific melting temperature of the crystal, and the specific melting temperature of the crystal. Since the mass of the crystal was small, it was natural to assume that when the test tube was heated, the heat conductor (taking into account the heating of the test tube itself) was the same in all cases. The influx of heat from heating cannot be changed, of course.

The melting point of the crystal is determined directly from the graph.

The specific heat of the crystal had to be calculated by drawing up a heat balance equation for a pure liquid and for a liquid with a crystal. When a pure liquid is heated in a test tube, the equality

$$c_0 m_{\text{ж}} \Delta T = \Delta W t. \tag{1}$$

Here $m_{\text{ж}}$ is the mass of the liquid, $\Delta W$ is the difference between the power of the source (heat influx) and the power of losses (solid removal), $t$ is time and $\Delta T$ is the change in temperature of the liquid during this time. Similarly when heating a liquid with a crystal

$$c_0 m_{\text{ж}} \Delta T_1 + c_{\text{ж}} m_{\text{ж}} \Delta T_2 = \Delta W t_1, \tag{2}$$

where $c_{\text{ж}}$ is the specific heat of the crystal, $m_{\text{ж}}$ is its mass, $t_1$ is the new time and $\Delta T_1$ is the new temperature change.

We denote $\Delta T/t$ and $\Delta T_1/t_1$ by $\alpha$ and $\alpha_1$ respectively. At figure $\alpha$ and $\alpha_1$ are the tangents of the slopes of the graphs of temperature in a test tube versus time (naturally, until the start of melting). Equations (1) and (2) can be rewritten as follows:

$$c_0 m_{\text{ж}} \alpha = \Delta W, \tag{1'}$$
$$c_0 m_{\text{ж}} \alpha_1 + c_{\text{ж}} m_{\text{ж}} \alpha_2 = \Delta W. \tag{2'}$$

Dividing the second equation by the first, we get

$$1 + \frac{c_{\text{ж}}}{c_0} \frac{m_{\text{ж}}}{m_{\text{ж}}} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha_1},$$

where

$$c_{\text{ж}} = c_0 \frac{m_{\text{ж}}}{m_{\text{ж}}} \frac{\alpha - \alpha_1}{\alpha_1}.$$

Having determined $\alpha$ and $\alpha_1$ from the graphs, it is not difficult to find $c_{\text{ж}}$.

The specific heat of the melt can be determined similarly.

In order to find the specific heat of melting of the crystal $\lambda$, it was necessary to create another heat balance equation:

$$\lambda m_{\text{ж}} = \Delta W t_2, \tag{3}$$

where $t_2$ is the melting time of the crystal. Dividing this equation by equation (1'), we get

$$\frac{\lambda m_{\text{ж}}}{c_0 m_{\text{ж}}} = \alpha t_2,$$

where

$$\lambda = c_0 \frac{m_{\text{ж}}}{m_{\text{ж}}} \alpha t_2.$$

All these calculations could have been carried out more accurately. Firstly, due to uneven heating, the temperature of the liquid and crystal increased slightly during melting (see figure). Therefore, the left side of equation (3) must contain terms $c_0 m_{\text{ж}} \Delta T_2$ and $c_{\text{ж}} m_{\text{ж}} \Delta T_2$ ($\Delta T_2$ - the corresponding change in temperature during time $t_2$). True, it is not entirely clear what would happen in the second term - when a crystal melts, its specific temperature changes. However, they could absolutely be complained about, which did not introduce a big mistake. Secondly, it was possible to explicitly take into account the heat of the test tube itself. To do this, it would be enough, for example, to plot the temperature change over time for a test tube with a different amount of liquid.
