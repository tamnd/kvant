Combinatorics - what is it?

Last year, a new book by the famous scientist and popularizer of science N. Ya. Vilenkin was published - “Popular Combinatorics” *).

In 1969, his book “Combinatorics” was published in the Main Editorial Office of Physics and Mathematics Literature of the Nauka Publishing House. It received worldwide recognition and was translated into many languages ​​(English, German, Spanish, Polish, Hungarian, Czech, Estonian).

The book under review adds very little to its predecessor. Six of the chapters are attributed to Zanyuvo, and the remaining three are completely revised text from the book “Combinatorics”.

“Popular Combinatorics” begins with the main story of combinatorics and its applications. Here we talk about the first magic squares, about figured numbers, about the combinatorial works of astrology involving combinations of planets, about how the scholastic Raymond Lully is trying to create a machine that makes various combinations understandable, and about combinatorial problems that arose in gambling. The author talks about how combinatorics became an independent branch of mathematics, about the works of Pascal, Fermat, Leibniz and Euler. Without a doubt, the reader will be more interested in stories about the applications of combinatorics to unraveling ancient writings, to the study of the genetic code,

The second chapter is devoted to the classification of combinatorial problems.

Following Berge's famous book, the author divides combinatorial problems as follows:

1. Find the configuration of elements that have properties.
2. Prove the existence (or absence) of a configuration with given properties.
3. Find the total number of configurations with the given properties.
4. Describe all the ways to solve this combinatorial problem, give an algorithm for listing them.
5. From all the solutions to a given combinatorial problem, select the optimal one (according to certain parameters).

Using magic squares and the queen problem as an example, he shows how configurations with given properties are sought, then talks about the general principle for solving optimization problems: if a solution is optimal as a whole, then it must be optimal in any part of it (this principle underlies the so-called diamantic programming *)). Using the example of the “game of 15”, he shows how it is possible to prove the impossibility of solving some problems.
