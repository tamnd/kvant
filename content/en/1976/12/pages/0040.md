Think of two such (everywhere defined) functions $f$ and $g$, the period of whose sum was exactly 2 times less than $T_0$.

Let the periodic function $f$ and $g$ have the smallest positive periods $T_1$ and $T_2$, corresponding, and $T_1 \neq T_2$.

Question. In what case do the functions $f$ and $g$ have a common (of course, not the smallest) period $T$?

Answer. If $f$ and $g$ are the common period $T$, then by Theorem 1 $T = mT_1 = nT_2$, where $m$ and $n$ are integers; it follows that $T_2/T_1 = m/n$ is a rational number. Conversely, if the relation $T_2/T_1$ is rational, $T_2/T_1 = m/n$, then $mT_1 = nT_2 = T$ is the common period of the functions $f$ and $g$.

All our reasoning shows the validity of this statement.

Theorem 2. If the smallest positive periods of the functions $f$ and $g$ are commensurate, that is, the relation $T_2/T_1$ is rational, then $u$ the sum of these functions $f(x) + g(x)$ is periodic.

It turns out that if the ratio of the smallest periods of the defined and continuous functions $f$ and $g$ plays the role of the smallest, then the function $f + g$ will be continuous. The same can be said about the product of periodic functions (we will limit ourselves to just giving a few relevant examples; the general proof of the stated statement is quite complicated).
