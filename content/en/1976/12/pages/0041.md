Comment. The functions from Problem 6 belong to a very important, special in physical applications, class of *almost periodic* functions. Such functions $f$ satisfy the following condition: for any (however angularly small) number $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an “almost-period” - a number $T \neq 0$ such that for an arbitrary $x$ the value $f(x+T)$ is equal to $f(x)$ up to $\varepsilon$, that is, $|f(x+T)| \leq \varepsilon$. Roughly speaking, this is explained by the fact that irrational numbers can be approximated by rational numbers with any accuracy; then, the smaller the $\varepsilon$, the longer the “almost-period” $T$ will be.

In physics, quantities are measured approximately, so there is no need to talk about their irrationality. Almost periodic processes in nature are encountered when we consider periodic phenomena, the ratio of periods of which is not a whole, but a fraction with a large value.

Example. When compiling a simultaneous calendar, it is necessary to take into account the revolution of the Earth around the Sun (with a period of 1 astronomical year) and the rotation of the Earth about its own axis (with a period of 1 day). As a matter of convenience, the calendar should be periodic, and it is desirable that its period be the common period of both rotations. The ratio of the astronomical year to the day is approximately equal to $m = 365,242199$. If we consider this value to be accurate, then the total period of the processes is considered through our influence - 1 million years! Therefore, Julius Caesar introduced the “almost-period” of 4 years, which corresponds to the value of $m \approx 365,25 = 365^{1/4}$, and Gregory XIII of Rome in 1582 legitimized the “almost-period” of the calendar of 400 years, adopted in the present day.
