Theorem 1. If $T_0$ is the smallest positive period of a Periodic function f, then both their sum $T_1 + T_2$ and difference $T_1 - T_2$ will be periods of f. (Check this out for yourself.)

From here it is immediately clear that if $T_0$ is a period of f, then all integer multiples of $T_0$—the numbers $T = nT_0$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}$—are periods of f. Now let $T_0$ be the smallest positive period of f, and $T$ be an arbitrary period of f. Let us assume that $T$ is not represented as $T = nT_0$, where $n$ is an integer. Then the number $T$ is contained strictly between y some neighboring integer multiples of the number $T_0$ (Fig. 5):

$$nT_0 < T < (n + 1)T_0.$$

Subtracting the number $nT_0$ from all parts of this inequality, we obtain: $nT_0 - nT_0 < T - nT_0 < (n + 1)T_0 - nT_0$, i.e. $0 < T' = T - nT_0 < T_0$. On the one hand, since $T_0$ is the smallest period of f, the number $T'$ between 0 and $T_0$ cannot be the period of f; on the other hand, according to the remark made above, the number $T' = T - nT_0$ is the period of f (as the difference of the periods $T$ and $nT_0$). The resulting contradiction shows that $T = nT_0$, and the positive theorem is proven.
