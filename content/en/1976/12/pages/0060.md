In addition to solving various kinds of problems, the training camp focused more on theoretical training of the participants. In past years, our team’s “Achilles heel” was problems on polynomials, since this topic is practically absent from school curricula. This year, quite a lot of time was spent on polynomial algebra, Dirichlet domains, graph theory and some other issues, which allowed our team at Optimide to successfully cope with problems of this kind.

On July 9, Soviet schoolchildren came to Vienna, from where the next day, together with teams from other countries, they went to the venue of the XVIII International Mathematical Optimization in Lienz, the capital of Eastern Tiral. The route of this excursion, about 600 km long, passed through the picturesque places of southern Australia. The romantic alpine landscapes will remain in the children’s memories for a long time (more than 50% of the territory of Eastern Tiral is located above 2000 m above sea level): meadows crossed by many bubbling nameless streams and rivulets, snow-white mountain peaks, forests.

The competitions were held on July 12 and 13. On each day, the student was offered three problems, the solution of which was given 4 hours (the texts of the problems are placed at the end of the stats; in brackets it is indicated which country proposed the problem and how many points were given for its complete solution; instructions for solving the problem are placed at the end of the magazine).

The work, as always, was checked by the team leaders, and the final assessment was made jointly with the coordinates - Austrian mathematicians. The work of the coordinators and jorri was completed by July 17th.

It should be noted that the actual difficulty of the tasks differed somewhat from George’s assessment. The fifth problem turned out to be the most difficult, for which the participants improved and collected 15% of the possible number of points. The next most difficult task was the second (26%), then the third (38%), the first (52%), the quadruple (62%) and the sixth (65%). The difficulty of individual tasks for teams from different countries also varied. Apparently, the success of schoolchildren from different countries in solving one or another problem is related to the program of their education at school. Thus, the British and French are traditionally good at solving problems involving polynomials and extrema, the Swedes are good at solving extremal problems, etc. Almost all teams successfully completed the sixth task, in which it was necessary to see a certain pattern and prove it using the method of mathematical induction; the only exceptions were those countries where this method of proving was not widely used in programs and textbooks (Finland, Holland, Greece, Cuba). The schoolchildren were not able to cope with the fifth problem (a system of linear equations) without noticing its combinatorial nature under the algebraic shell.
