The merits of D. S. Rozhdestvensky in the development of Soviet physics are far from being limited to his personal contribution to science. He was not only a very great scientist, but also an excellent teacher and organizer of science.

In his teaching activities, Rozhdestvensky highly valued and sought to emphasize independence in the work of students. In his lectures, he tried to stay primarily on fundamental problems and on what was not in the textbooks, so that the students themselves would then break down the simpler and well-known sections of science.

Boldly involving young people in independent scientific research, he created a talented school of Soviet physicists. Among his students are academicians A. A. Lebedev, I. V. Obrenmov, D. V. Skobeltsyn, A. N. Terenin, V. A. Fok, corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences E. F. Gross, S. E. Frish and many other famous scientists.

Rozhdestvensky was one of the leading Russian scientists who sought to put science at the service of the people. Therefore, he greeted the Great October Socialist Revolution with enthusiasm, realizing what enormous opportunities for the development of science it opened up for scientists. In the very first months after the revolution, he worked diligently to unify the efforts of disparate and very small groups of Petrograd optical physicists and soon submitted to the People's Commissariat of Education a detailed project for the creation of a new type of scientific center - the State Optical Institute (GOI). In it he wrote: “...with modest funds from university physics laboratories, with the weak development of the optical industry in Russia, the centralization of optics workers with broad government support is necessary both for purely scientific tasks and for the correct direction and growth of the activities of optical technology and industry. An optical institute is needed." The project also provided for the organization of two subordinate enterprises at the institute - an optical stack production plant and an optical equipment plant. The Soviet government supported this idea, and on May 6, 1919, the head of education, A.V. Lunacharsky, signed a decree establishing the State Optical Institute.

There was a war going on. The young Red Army was in dire need of biocles, stereo tubes, and optical sights for guns. And in the country there was no time to buy even simple glasses. Tsarist Russia did not have an optical industry and imported optical instruments from abroad, mainly from Germany, where the famous Zeiss factories were located. It was for them that Abbe worked, who, according to Rozhdestvensky, almost won the first world science with the optical equipment of the German army.
