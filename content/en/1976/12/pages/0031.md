M378. Prove that there are infinite multidimensional numbers that cannot be represented in the form
a) $x^3 + y^3 + z^3$, where $x, y, z$ are integers;
b) $x_1^n + x_2^n + \dots + x_n^n$, where $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are not natural numbers.
b) Prove that any rational number can be represented as $x^3 + y^3 + z^3$, where $x, y, z$ are not rational numbers.

◆

a) It is easy to check that if $x$ is an integer, then either $x^3$, or $x^3 + 1$, or $x^3 - 1$ is divisible by 9. Indeed, if $x = 3k$, then this is obvious; if $x = 3k + 1$, then $x^3 - 1 = (x - 1)^3 + 3x(x - 1) = (3k)^3 + 9kx$; if $x = 3k - 1$, then $x^3 + 1 = (x + 1)^3 + 3x(x + 1) = (3k)^3 + 9kx$. Otherwise, our statement can be formulated as follows: when dividing the cube of an integer by 9, the remainder can only be 0, 1 and -1. From here it is immediately clear that the sum of three cubes when divided by 9 can only have a remainder of 0, ±1, ±2, ±3. Therefore, all numbers $9k \neq 4$ cannot be represented as the sum of three cubes of integers.

b) Take all natural numbers less than the number $N^n$ (where $N > 1$ is some natural number). If $a = x_1^n + x_2^n + \dots + x_n^n$ (where $x_i$ are natural numbers), and $a < N^n$, then $x_i \leqslant N - 1$. From $n$ numbers $x_1, \dots, x_n$, each of which can belong to $(N - 1)$ values ​​$(1, 2, \dots, N - 1)$, one can compose $(N - 1)^n$ sets (see, for example, “Quant”, 1971, No. 1, articles by N. Ya. Vilenkin “Combinatorics”). Among the sums made up of the $n$ powers of the numbers of these sets, there will be identical ones; so the number of numbers $a$ of the form $x_1^n + x_2^n + \dots + x_n^n$, less than $N^n$, is not more than $(N - 1)^n$. Therefore, $[N^n - (N - 1)^n] = A_N$ natural numbers less than $N^n$ cannot be represented as the sum of $n$-th powers of $n$ natural numbers. The number $A_N$ can be made as a corner, since $A_N > N^{n-1}$ and $n > 1$. It follows from this that there are an infinite number of natural numbers that cannot be represented in the form $x_1^n + x_2^n + \dots + x_n^n$.

b) The proof of the statement formulated in paragraph c) was first obtained in 1825. It looks amazing: for the rational number $a$ its representation is directly written as the sum of three cubes of rational numbers:

$$a = \left( \frac{a^3 - 3^6}{3^2 a^2 + 3^4 a + 3^6} \right)^3 + \left( \frac{-a^3 + 3^6 a + 3^6}{3^2 a^2 + 3^4 a + 3^6} \right)^3 + \left( \frac{a^2 + 3^4 a}{3^2 a^2 + 3^4 a + 3^6} \right)^3.$$

It is not clear, however, how one can “come up with” such a formula (however, perhaps one of our readers has succeeded). We can only suggest that you check that this formula is correct.

However, mathematicians did not study the problem of the sum of three cubes in order to obtain formula (*); in itself, although it looked impressive, it was not very respectable. It was much more important to understand what was “hidden” behind this identity. But this is a completely specialized question; it deals with one of the most difficult branches of mathematics - algebraic geometry.

I. Glumanov
