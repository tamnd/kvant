a) It is easy to check that if $x$ is an integer, then either $x^3$, or $x^3 + 1$, or $x^3 - 1$ is divisible by 9. Indeed, if $x = 3k$, then this is obvious; if $x = 3k + 1$, then $x^3 - 1 = (x - 1)^3 + 3x(x - 1) = (3k)^3 + 9kx$; if $x = 3k - 1$, then $x^3 + 1 = (x + 1)^3 + 3x(x + 1) = (3k)^3 + 9kx$. Otherwise, our statement can be formulated as follows: when dividing the cube of an integer by 9, the remainder can only be 0, 1 and -1. From here it is immediately clear that the sum of three cubes when divided by 9 can only have a remainder of 0, ±1, ±2, ±3. Therefore, all numbers $9k \neq 4$ cannot be represented as the sum of three cubes of integers.

b) Take all natural numbers less than the number $N^n$ (where $N > 1$ is some natural number). If $a = x_1^n + x_2^n + \dots + x_n^n$ (where $x_i$ are natural numbers), and $a < N^n$, then $x_i \leqslant N - 1$. From $n$ numbers $x_1, \dots, x_n$, each of which can belong to $(N - 1)$ values ​​$(1, 2, \dots, N - 1)$, one can compose $(N - 1)^n$ sets (see, for example, “Quant”, 1971, No. 1, articles by N. Ya. Vilenkin “Combinatorics”). Among the sums made up of the $n$ powers of the numbers of these sets, there will be identical ones; so the number of numbers $a$ of the form $x_1^n + x_2^n + \dots + x_n^n$, less than $N^n$, is not more than $(N - 1)^n$. Therefore, $[N^n - (N - 1)^n] = A_N$ natural numbers less than $N^n$ cannot be represented as the sum of $n$-th powers of $n$ natural numbers. The number $A_N$ can be made as a corner, since $A_N > N^{n-1}$ and $n > 1$. It follows from this that there are an infinite number of natural numbers that cannot be represented in the form $x_1^n + x_2^n + \dots + x_n^n$.

b) The proof of the statement formulated in paragraph c) was first obtained in 1825. It looks amazing: for the rational number $a$ its representation is directly written as the sum of three cubes of rational numbers:

$$a = \left( \frac{a^3 - 3^6}{3^2 a^2 + 3^4 a + 3^6} \right)^3 + \left( \frac{-a^3 + 3^6 a + 3^6}{3^2 a^2 + 3^4 a + 3^6} \right)^3 + \left( \frac{a^2 + 3^4 a}{3^2 a^2 + 3^4 a + 3^6} \right)^3.$$

It is not clear, however, how one can “come up with” such a formula (however, perhaps one of our readers has succeeded). We can only suggest that you check that this formula is correct.

However, mathematicians did not study the problem of the sum of three cubes in order to obtain formula (*); in itself, although it looked impressive, it was not very respectable. It was much more important to understand what was “hidden” behind this identity. F385. A large thin conducting plate of area S and thickness d is placed in a uniform electric field E, redistributing the plate. How much heat will be released in the conductor if the field is turned off?

When a conducting plate is introduced into an electric field, under the influence of this field it produces a redistribution of free charges in the plate. On opposite sides of the plate, perpendicular to the direction of the external field, charges of opposite signs accumulate. Inside the plate the field strength is zero. This means that free charges distributed over the surfaces of the plate create a field, the intensity of which is equal to -E on the inside of the plates and zero on the outside.

Immediately after the disappearance of the external field of the internal ones, only the field of surface charges remains, which has the energy

$$W = \frac{E^2}{2} \varepsilon_0 Sd.$$

Under the influence of this field, it produces a “reverse” redistribution of charges throughout the entire volume of the plate. In this case, all the energy of the electric field will be released in the form of heat:

$$Q = \frac{E^2}{2} \varepsilon_0 Sd.$$

Comment. The functions from Problem 6 belong to a very important, special in physical applications, class of *almost periodic* functions. Such functions $f$ satisfy the following condition: for any (however angularly small) number $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an “almost-period” - a number $T \neq 0$ such that for an arbitrary $x$ the value $f(x+T)$ is equal to $f(x)$ up to $\varepsilon$, that is, $|f(x+T)| \leq \varepsilon$. Roughly speaking, this is explained by the fact that irrational numbers can be approximated by rational numbers with any accuracy; then, the smaller the $\varepsilon$, the longer the “almost-period” $T$ will be.

In physics, quantities are measured approximately, so there is no need to talk about their irrationality. Almost periodic processes in nature are encountered when we consider periodic phenomena, the ratio of periods of which is not a whole, but a fraction with a large value.

Example. When compiling a simultaneous calendar, it is necessary to take into account the revolution of the Earth around the Sun (with a period of 1 astronomical year) and the rotation of the Earth about its own axis (with a period of 1 day). As a matter of convenience, the calendar should be periodic, and it is desirable that its period be the common period of both rotations. The ratio of the astronomical year to the day is approximately equal to $m = 365,242199$. If we consider this value to be accurate, then the total period of the processes is considered through our influence - 1 million years! Therefore, Julius Caesar introduced the “almost-period” of 4 years, which corresponds to the value of $m \approx 365,25 = 365^{1/4}$, and Gregory XIII of Rome in 1582 legitimized the “almost-period” of the calendar of 400 years, adopted in the present day.
