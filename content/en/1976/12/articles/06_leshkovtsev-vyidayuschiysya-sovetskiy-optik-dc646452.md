Let's get to know this amazing person.

Dmitry Sergeevich Rozhdestvensky was born on April 7, 1876 in St. Petersburg, in the family of the talented Russian teacher Sergei Egorovich Rozhdestvensky, from whose Russian history textbooks several generations of high school students were taught. He received a good home upbringing, had an excellent command of a little English and French, and graduated from high school with a medal (he failed with a B in Russian language and literature at the final exams). His favorite subjects were physics, chemistry and biology, and he retained this love throughout his life.

In 1894, he entered the natural sciences department of St. Petersburg University and studied biology and chemistry for a year. But interest in physics won out, and having passed the required exams, Rozhdestvensky again entered the first year of this time in the mathematics department, where they also studied mechanics, physics and astronomy. While studying at the university, he did not attend all the lectures (at that time this was allowed to students), but he worked independently. True, he later wished that he had attended few lectures, and believed that he should have attended at least all the lectures on physics, which were accompanied by demonstration experiments. He attached great importance to these experiments and once, already a professor at the same university, canceled a lecture at the last moment when he was convinced that the demonstration experiments were poorly prepared.

Having lost a year at the beginning of his university studies, he was forced to do the same in his final year. It so happened that in 1899, just before the final exams, a student strike began at the university. Tsarist development issued an order to conduct exams under police protection

This was a rare case in Russian physics when someone managed to make a major contribution to world science while remaining at home, in Russia. Concluding the last list of D. S. How did this outstanding Soviet scientist enrich science?

He began his research by studying the anomalous dissertation of light in the vapor of the solid metal sodium. In Newton's famous experiment, light passing through a glass element was decomposed into a color spectrum. From this experiment it follows that the refractive index depends on the color (or wavelength). This dependence is called dissertation. Since the sign in Newton's experiment refracts violet rays most strongly and red rays weakest, we can argue that the shorter the wavelength, the stronger the refraction. For arbitrary bodies in the visible region of the spectrum, the dependence of the refractive index on the wavelength is described quite well by the following formula:

$$n = a + \frac{b}{\lambda^2},$$

where $a$ and $b$ are constant quantities characterizing the given substance. This dependence is called normal dissertation.

But back in 1862, the French physicist Leroux, passing light through a prism filled with iodine vapor, discovered a violation of this pattern. Iodine vapor strongly absorbs light at several specific wavelengths. Having decomposed the light that passed through these pairs into a spectrum,

we see a series of dark lines in it - they are called absorption lines. It turned out that near these lines the refractive index of longer waves is higher than that of shorter ones. A similar phenomenon is observed when light passes through sodium vapor (Fig. 1) and other substances whose absorption lines lie in the visible region of the spectrum. This phenomenon is called anomalous dispersion. The theory of anomalous dispersion was created by the German physicist Sellmeyer back in 1871, but for a long time there was no reliable experimental verification. Determining the refractive index near absorption lines has proven to be extremely difficult due to the strong absorption of light.

D. S. Rozhdestvensky developed an original method for accurately determining the refractive index not only near absorption lines, but even inside the lines themselves. The data he obtained on changes in the refractive index near the absorption lines of sodium vapor confirmed the validity of Selmeyer's theory. But this work had an even more important result. Rozhdestvensky's method makes it possible to accurately determine the intensity of double spectral lines (doublets), or, as they said at that time, the ratio of the numbers of selectors emitting each of these lines. It turned out that for many substances the ratio of the intensities of neighboring lines in dublets is expressed in small integers. Rozhdestvensky determined the values ​​of these ratios for most doublets of whole metals and showed that they do not depend on either temperature or vapor pressure, but are determined by the properties of the atoms themselves and the substances under study. In his doctoral dissertation, he wrote about it this way: “The simple relationships found must, very much so, correspond to some very simple feature in the structure of atoms and molecules. But what this simplicity consists of is still impossible to decide with modern data on atoms.” Let us remind readers that this was written at the very beginning of 1915, when the atom theory proposed by Niels Bohr was still taking its first steps.

As quantum mechanics later showed, the intensity ratio characterizes the probabilities of electron transition from one orbit of an atom to another and is a very important characteristic of an atom. True to the conditions unfavorable for the pursuit of science, and even in complete isolation from all world science, D. S. Rozhdestvensky found the basic ideas for the further development of Bohr's theory. In a scientific report on the topic “Spectral analysis and structure of the atom,” which he read in 1919, at the celebration of the first anniversary of the State Optical Institute, Rozhdestvensky clearly formulated the main principles, thanks to which Bohr’s theory was applied first to the spectra of alkali metal atoms with one electron in the outer shell, and then to the spectra of any atom.

As they later found out, the same ideas were used by Arnald Sommerfeld, Erwin Schrödinger and a number of other outstanding foreign physicists in the development and improvement of Bohr's theory.

Implementing his ideas, Rozhdestvensky carried out a classical analysis of the spectrum of nanomagnesium, the lines of which are close to those of the neutral atom of the alkali metal sodium. This work served as a model for numerous analyzes of the ion spectra of various elements carried out in subsequent years.

D. S. Rozhdestvensky made a major contribution to the theory of microcospa. Invented back in the 17th century, the microcosop soon found many applications. However, the theory of this important optical device was created only at the beginning of our century. Geometric optics allows you to calculate the magnification of a microscope. But she cannot answer the question of what are the maximum body sizes that can still be seen using a microscope. (After all, we know, for example, that an ordinary microscope does not allow us to see atoms and molecules.) It also cannot tell us anything about the characteristics and origin of diffraction and interference fringes,

The merits of D. S. Rozhdestvensky in the development of Soviet physics are far from being limited to his personal contribution to science. He was not only a very great scientist, but also an excellent teacher and organizer of science.

In his teaching activities, Rozhdestvensky highly valued and sought to emphasize independence in the work of students. In his lectures, he tried to stay primarily on fundamental problems and on what was not in the textbooks, so that the students themselves would then break down the simpler and well-known sections of science.

Boldly involving young people in independent scientific research, he created a talented school of Soviet physicists. Among his students are academicians A. A. Lebedev, I. V. Obrenmov, D. V. Skobeltsyn, A. N. Terenin, V. A. Fok, corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences E. F. Gross, S. E. Frish and many other famous scientists.

Rozhdestvensky was one of the leading Russian scientists who sought to put science at the service of the people. Therefore, he greeted the Great October Socialist Revolution with enthusiasm, realizing what enormous opportunities for the development of science it opened up for scientists. In the very first months after the revolution, he worked diligently to unify the efforts of disparate and very small groups of Petrograd optical physicists and soon submitted to the People's Commissariat of Education a detailed project for the creation of a new type of scientific center - the State Optical Institute (GOI). In it he wrote: “...with modest funds from university physics laboratories, with the weak development of the optical industry in Russia, the centralization of optics workers with broad government support is necessary both for purely scientific tasks and for the correct direction and growth of the activities of optical technology and industry. An optical institute is needed." The project also provided for the organization of two subordinate enterprises at the institute - an optical stack production plant and an optical equipment plant. The Soviet government supported this idea, and on May 6, 1919, the head of education, A.V. Lunacharsky, signed a decree establishing the State Optical Institute.

There was a war going on. The young Red Army was in dire need of biocles, stereo tubes, and optical sights for guns. And in the country there was no time to buy even simple glasses. Tsarist Russia did not have an optical industry and imported optical instruments from abroad, mainly from Germany, where the famous Zeiss factories were located. It was for them that Abbe worked, who, according to Rozhdestvensky, almost won the first world science with the optical equipment of the German army. The grandiose scientific and technological revolution taking place at the present time is extremely successful in science and production. Science now plays a huge role in the life of society, it has become an immediate productive force that is preparing a revolutionary revolution in all modern industries - in metallurgy, and in mechanical engineering, and on truss sites, and on collective and state farm fields. These changes required a radical restructuring of production, consolidation and consolidation of enterprises, the creation of firms and scientific and production associations. A. N. D. S. Rozhdestvensky attached great importance to the interaction of science with production. So, in 1936 he wrote: “We are winning one victory after another over the old world. We are taking first place in a number of industries and are continuously winning new first places... Now it’s our turn to organize our own science, to show our strength in science and, most importantly, in the scientific organization of industry. Especially in such an industry as optical industry, since it is inseparable from optical science.”

It is not surprising that the State Optical Institute was among the initiators of the creation of scientific and production associations. The Leningrad Optical Association, organized on his initiative more than 10 years ago, which, in addition to GOI, includes special design bureaus, optical factories and other divisions, is working very successfully. Recently, the largest optical telephoto lens in the world with a mirror diameter of 6 meters was created in the plant.

We would like to end this article with a bold and far-sighted dream, which D. S. Rozhdestvensky expressed back in 1919, at the end of his speech at the celebration of the first anniversary of the State Optical Institute.

“A vision of the near future appears before the eyes. Every atom is known, the possibilities of connections into molecules are explored and can be calculated at any moment. Chemistry is no longer an experimental science, but a theoretical one. Just like an architect has a method for calculating buildings in his reference book, so does an ordinary chemist, sitting in his office, use certain equations and tables to find the implementation of the most complex chemical compounds necessary for life. This is power over nature, its subjugation of man to an almost non-optical degree - it is the essence and soul of technology. It will be given by the knowledge of atoma... we cannot even predict how human life will be transformed in the coming decade, when the riddle of atoma will be solved, when thousands, ten thousand scientists will apply the will to solve, along with other problems, this, perhaps, the most important, when science will hourly approach life.”
