Static elements

Statics studies the equilibrium of solid bodies under the influence of forces. The equilibrium of a body should be understood as a state in which the body does not receive acceleration, that is, it moves uniformly and rectilinearly or, in particular, is at rest in the inertial frame of reference. (In practical problems, the system associated with Emley is considered inertial.)

What forces act on a body in equilibrium? First of all, you need to know the force of gravity. The force of gravity acting on a given solid body is an equal force of gravity acting on its particles. The line of action of gravity passes through the center of mass of the body - the center of gravity.

Further, the body is affected by the reactions of connections - forces that transform the body into moving in some direction. The direction of action of the reaction of the bonds is opposite to the direction in which the connection turns to the movement of a given body. The reactions of bonds are elastic forces and frictional forces. What is especially special about them is that their absolute value, and sometimes even their direction, are not really known and depend on the shape of the body, the state of the surface, as well as on other forces acting on the body.

Correct determination of the direction of reaction forces plays a very important role in solving statics problems.

Let us consider in detail how the direction of reaction of certain types of connections.

1. The body rests on a smooth surface or support. There is no training. When the contact of a body with a support occurs at one point, the surface reaction force is applied at the point of contact of the bodies and is directed either along the common normal to the surface of the contacting bodies at the point of their contact (Fig. 1, a), or along the normal to the surface of the body or to the surface of the support (Fig. 1, b). This reaction is called normal.

2. Communication is carried out by a flexible thread. The reaction force of the thread is always directed along the thread from the point at which the thread is attached to the body (Fig. 1, θ).

3. A hinge connection is a cylindrical hinge in which the hinge axis is perpedicular to the plane of action of the force (Fig. 1, e). The reaction of such a hinge can have any direction in the plane perpedicular to its axis (in the figure plane).

The considered types of connections are ideal, or connections without friction.

4. In the presence of friction between the body and the connection surface, in addition to the normal reaction, it gives an additional reaction - the friction force $F_{\text{тр}}$. The friction force is always directed in the direction opposite to the ability of the body to move along the surface. If the body on which the forces act ends, then the static friction force each time has the value that is necessary to prevent slipping. The maximum value of the static friction force is determined, as is known, from the condition

$$F_{\text{тр. max}} = \mu N,$$

where $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction and $N$ is the normal reaction force of the surface. Thus, depending on other forces acting on the body, the static friction force can take all values ​​from zero to $F_{\text{тр. max}}$. In this case, the total force $R = F_{\text{тр.}} + N$ of the surface reaction (Fig. 1, $\partial$) will change from the value $N$ to a certain maximum value $R_{\text{max}}$, determined by the conditions $R_{\text{max}} = N + F_{\text{тр. max}}$. The angle $\varphi$, which is made by the force $R$ with the normal to the surface, will vary from zero to a certain limiting value $\varphi_0$, specified by the conditions

$$\text{tg} \varphi_0 = \frac{F_{\text{тр. max}}}{N} = \mu$$

(this angle is called the friction angle).

In rigid body statics, two main problems are considered:

1. Determination of the conditions under which a body under the influence of forces can be in equilibrium.
2. Finding the forces acting on the body (in most cases, coupling reactions) when the body depends on being in equilibrium.

We will limit ourselves to considering only such systems in which all the forces acting on the body lie in one plane - the so-called plane systems of forces.

Any motion of a rigid body can be represented as a superposition of two types of motion - translational and rotational (around a certain axis). The body will remain at rest if there is no cause leading to translational motion or rotation.

When a body moves forward, we can consider the movements of one point of the body—its center of mass.

If the sum of the forces applied to the body is equal to zero, then the center of mass will maintain its speed unchanged, in particular, it will be at rest if it was at rest. But

this does not mean that the body will be in equilibrium.

Problem 1

A block lying on a smooth horizontal plane has two forces applied at its two points, equal in magnitude and directed in opposite directions (Fig. 2). (Such a system of forces is called a pair of forces.) Relative to what point will the block rotate?

Solution and e. Olt suggests that the block will rotate. But since the sum of the forces acting on the body is equal to zero, its center of mass will remain at rest, and a pair of forces will cause the bar to rotate around an axis passing through the center of mass and perpendicular to the plane in which the forces lie.

In the general case, when the sum of the forces applied to the body is equal to zero ($\sum F_i = 0$), and the lines along which the forces act do not intersect at one point, the center of mass maintains the state of motion unchanged, in particular, it will end, but the body itself will rotate under the force passing through the center of mass.

To characterize the rotational action of force in statics, a new concept is introduced - moment of force.

Consider a rigid body capable of rotating in a vertical plane around a horizontal axis passing through the point $O$ (Fig. 3). Let us assume that forces $F_1, F_2, F_3, F_4$ are applied to the body, the lines of action of which lie in the vertical plane. Olt shows that the rotational effect of each of these forces depends not only on the magnitude of the force, but also on the distance from the axis to the line of action of the force. This distance is called the leverage.

By introducing the moment of force, it is possible to formulate general equilibrium conditions for a plane system of forces.

For the body to be in equilibrium, it is necessary and sufficient that the vector sum of the forces applied to the body and the algebraic sum of the moments of these forces relative to any point $O$ of the plane be simultaneously equal to zero:

$$\sum_i F_i = 0,$$
$$\sum_i M_O(F_i) = 0.$$

When the first condition is met, the acceleration of the body's center of mass is zero; when the second condition is met, the angular velocity of rotation of all points of the body remains unchanged and, in particular, if the body is buried, the angular velocity of its points remains equal to zero *).

Vector equality (*) can be represented as two folding ones:

$$\sum_i F_{ix} = 0,$$
$$\sum_i F_{iy} = 0,$$

where $F_{ix}$ and $F_{iy}$ are respectively projections of forces $F_i$ on the coordinate axes $X$ and $Y$, lying in the plane of action of the forces.

When solving problems to obtain an equation in the simplest form, it is recommended to draw one of the coordinate axes perpendicular to the largest possible number of unknown forces, and to locate the moments of the forces relative to the point at which the largest possible number of unknown forces intersect.

Ten balls are strung on a weightless rod divided into 10 equal parts, the masses of which are successively equal to 1, 2, 3, ..., 9, 10 $z$ so that their centers coincide with the division points (Fig. 4). Determine where the rod should rest on the support in order to be in balance.

The considered problem is essentially the problem of determining the center of gravity for the case of a linear arrangement of exact masses.

Prove that the position of the center of gravity of a system consisting of $n$ material points, the masses of which are $m_1, m_2, \dots, m_n$, lying on the same straight line and having the coordinates of the corresponding $x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots, x_n$, is determined by the coordinate

$$x_c = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i x_i}{M},$$

where $M = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i$ is the mass of the entire system.

For bodies whose dimensions are very small compared to the radius of the Earth, the gravitational forces acting on individual parts of the body can be considered parallel to each other and retaining a constant value for each particle for any rotation of the body. The resultant of all elements of gravitational forces is the gravitational force acting on the entire body. Its absolute value is equal to

$$Mg = \Sigma m_i g,$$
and this force is applied to the center of mass, since any body falling freely (under the influence of gravity only) moves translationally. Therefore, the center of mass is called the center of gravity of the body.

So, the center of gravity of the third body is the point at which the resultant of the gravitational forces acting on parts of this body is applied.

It should be noted that the center of gravity can also lie outside the boundaries of a given body (for example, for a flask, according to a thin rod, etc.).

When starting to analyze the next problem, we indicate some additional easily provable provisions that we will use.

A force applied to a rigid body can be transferred along the line of its action, while its moment relative to a point or axis does not change.

If there is a system of forces on the body of action, the lines of action of which intersect at one point, then we can transfer the forces along the lines of their action to the point of intersection and add them, using the parallelgram rule. If the resultant force is zero and the initial velocity of the body is also zero, then the body will be at rest.

If a body is acted upon by three irregular forces lying in the same plane, and under the action of these forces the body is in equilibrium, then the lines of action of these forces intersect at one point (this position is called the three-force theorem).
