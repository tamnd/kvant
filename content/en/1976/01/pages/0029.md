However, in some other sense, almost all real numbers are transcendental; algebraic numbers constitute only an insignificant part of the real numbers!

In 1878, the German mathematician G. Cantor (1845-1918) proved that algebraic numbers can be numbered in such a way that each algebraic number will receive its own number, and under each number there will be an algebraic number (see Appendix). Let us take a segment of arbitrarily small length, for example the length $\varepsilon=10^{-15}\,см$ (recall that the radius of the electron is approximately $10^{-12}\,см$). Having depicted real numbers as points on the number line, we cover algebraic point No. 1 with a segment of length $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon$, algebraic point No. 2 with a segment of length $\frac{1}{2^2}\varepsilon$, algebraic point No. $n$ with a segment of length $\frac{1}{2^n}\varepsilon$, and so on.

The sum of the lengths of the covering segments is equal to $\varepsilon$, although they partially overlap each other and cover not only all algebraic numbers, but also some transcendental ones. The entire number axis is infinite, and the sum of the lengths of the segments covering the algebraic points is only a thousandth of the radius of the electron! This clearly shows that almost all numbers are transcendental, while algebraic numbers are rare exceptions.

Application

Let us call the rank of equation (1) the number $r$:
$$
r=n+|a_0|+|a_1|+\ldots+|a_n|; \tag{13}
$$
here $n\geq1,\ |a_i|\geq1$, therefore $r\geq2$.

For each natural number $r\geq2$, the indefinite equation (13) has a finite number of solutions in non-negative integers. Consequently, there is a finite number of equations of each rank. For example, rank 2 - two equations: $\pm x=0$; rank 3 - six equations:
$$
\pm x^2=0,\qquad \pm x\pm1=0;
$$

⟦column⟧

rank 4 - equations
$$
\pm x^3=0,\qquad \pm x^2\pm x=0,\qquad \pm x^2\pm1=0,
$$
$$
\pm x\pm2=0,\qquad \pm2x\pm1=0.
$$

Let's find the roots of these equations and write them out one after another: first, the roots of equations of rank 2, then the roots of equations of rank 3, and so on; We write out the roots of equations of the same rank in any order, and if a number is the root of several equations, then we write it out only once. We get a sequence of numbers:

$$
0,\ 1,\ -1,\ i,\ -i,\ 2,\ -2,\ \frac{1}{2},\ -\frac{1}{2},
$$

further - roots of equations of rank 5, etc.

Each algebraic number will thus be written out and receive its own serial number in the sequence. If one is only interested in real algebraic numbers, then the complex numbers are omitted.

Exercises

1. Prove that the number

$$
\alpha=c_0+\frac{c_1}{2}+\frac{c_2}{2^2}+\ldots+\frac{c_k}{2^{2k}}+\ldots,
$$

where $c_0$ is an integer and $c_1,c_2,\ldots$ takes one of the values ​​$0$ or $1$, is transcendental.

2. Prove that Theorem 2 is also true if the numbers $\{c_k\}$ take any integer values ​​bounded in the aggregate, that is, if there is a constant $B$ such that $\{c_k\}<B$ for all values ​​of the index $k$.

3. Prove that in Theorem 2 we can put

$$
\alpha=c_0+\frac{c_1}{10^{n_1}}+\frac{c_2}{10^{n_2}}+\ldots,
$$

where $\{n_k\}$ is any sequence of natural numbers subject to the condition

$$
n_{k+1}\geq\lambda_kn_k,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,
$$

and $\{\lambda_k\}$ is an arbitrary infinitely increasing sequence of real numbers, for example, $\lambda_k=\lg k$.

In the following exercises, by a complex integer we mean a number of the form $a+bi$, by a rational complex number we mean a number $(a+bi)/q$, where $a,b$ are integers, $q$ is a natural number, and $i$ is such that $i^2=-1$.

4. Prove that if the number $\alpha$ satisfies equation (1) with complex integer coefficients, then it is algebraic.

5. Prove that Theorem 1 is true if, under its conditions, $\alpha$ is a complex irrational number and $p/q$ is a complex rational number.

6. Prove that Theorem 2 and statements in exercises 1, 2, 3 are also valid when the coefficients $\{c_k\}$ take complex integer values.
