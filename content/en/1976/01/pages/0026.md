Transcendental numbers

The concept of number has been formed over the course of a millennium. And only ancient years ago - primarily in the works of L. Euler (1707-1783) - complex numbers entered mathematics, and the development of the concept of number was completed.

Let us recall the stages (non-chronological) of this development:
a) natural numbers 1, 2, 3, ...
b) integers ... -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
c) rational numbers, i.e. numbers of the form $\frac{p}{q}$, where $p$ is an integer, $q$ is a natural number;
d) complex numbers, i.e. numbers expressed in infinite decimal fractions;
e) complex numbers, i.e. numbers of the form $a+bi$, where $a$ and $b$ are real numbers, and the symbol $i$ is determined by the condition $i^2 = -1$.

In this sequence, each subsequent class contains all the numbers of the previous class and additionally “new” numbers. With each expansion of the class of numbers, we acquire the opportunity to perform in all cases certain actions that we cannot always perform in “old” numbers. So, in the class of integers, subtraction is always feasible, and in the class of rational numbers, in addition, division is always feasible; in the class of real numbers, we can always perform numbers from only four arithmetic operations, but - for positive numbers - extracting the root, and in general raising to any real power, as well as finding the logarithm. Finally, as Gauss (1777–1855) proved in 1799, algebraic equations of any degree are always solvable in complex numbers: equation
$$a_0z^n+a_1z^{n-1}+\dots+a_n=0,$$
(1)
where $n$ is a natural number and the coefficients are complex, has $n$ complex roots. Still Raving (1748) Euler constructed the theory of exponential, logarithmic, trionometric and their inverse functions of a complex argument.

Currently, we will consider another classification of numbers, namely, dividing them into algebraic and non-algebraic (initially, transcendental) *.

Determined. A number is called algebraic if there is an algebraic equation with integer coefficients of which it is the root.

Emphasizing that for a number to be algebraic, the equation (1) it satisfies must have integer coefficients (or can be reduced to such an equation).

Obviously, rational numbers are algebraic: $\frac{p}{q}$ is the root of the equation $qz-p=0$ with integer coefficients $q$ and $-p$.
