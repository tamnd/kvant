It was calculated by the value $m'$, and the magnitude of the force remained equal to the hydrostatic Archimedean force ($V\rho g$). The quantity $m'$ is called the added mass. This is the number of the formula, but it is a convenient expression of equality (7). Formula (7) is obtained from the incorrect formula (1) by adding the term $m'$ to the denominator. Note that in a similar way, formula (5) is obtained from (3) by adding the term $M$ to the denominator.

The force $F_{\text{выт}}$ with which the moving fluid acts on the rod can now be easily obtained from Newton’s second law

$$ma = F_{\text{выт}} - mg,$$

where

$$F_{\text{выт}} = m(g + a) = mg \frac{V\rho + m'}{m + m'}. \tag{8}$$

In particular, if $m \ll m'$, then $F_{\text{выт}} \approx \approx mg \frac{R^2}{r^2}$; at $R \sim r$ the magnitude force turns out to be of the order of the weight of the rod (and has no relation to the weight of the displaced water). If $m \gg m'$, then $F_{\text{выт}} \approx V\rho g$, that is, we return to Archimedes’ law in its general form.

For a ball (in particular, for a bubble), the calculation gives the following result: the kinetic energy of the liquid is equal to $\frac{1}{4} V\rho v^2$, where $V$ is the volume of the ball, $v$ is its speed. then the added mass for the bubble is $m' = \frac{1}{2} V\rho$, i.e. it is equal to half the mass of the displaced water. The bubble floats up with acceleration

$$a = \frac{V\rho - V\rho_0}{V\rho_0 + V\rho/2} g \approx 2g.$$

The buoyancy force is determined from equation (8), it is approximately equal to $F_{\text{выт}} \approx 3 mg$, i.e., triple the weight of a stationary bubble (and many times less than the weight of the displaced water).

Now let's remember about the force of resistance. For a gas bubble in a liquid, it is determined by the formula $F_c = 12\pi \eta rv$, where $r$ is the radius of the bubble, $v$ is its speed, $\eta$ is the so-called viscosity coefficient of the medium $^*$). Taking into account the resistance force, the bubble motion equation will be written as follows (see (7)):

$$(m + m') a = V\rho g - mg - F_c. \tag{9}$$

It is obvious that $F_c$ decreases (and hence the speed) of the bubble compared to that value when we do not take into account the resistance of the liquid. However, if $\frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \rho g \gg 12\pi \eta rv$, i.e. at $v \ll \frac{r^2 \rho g}{9\eta}$, the resistance force can be neglected. For example, if a child is about a bubble of radius $r = 3 \text{ mm}$ $^*$) moving in water ($\rho = 1 \text{ e/cm}^3$, $\eta = 1,0 \times 10^{-2} \text{ e/(cm} \cdot \text{сек)}$), then its speed should be much less than the value $v_0 = \frac{r^2 \rho g}{9\eta} = 10 \text{ m/сек}$.
