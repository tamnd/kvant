⟦folio 9⟧

The averages of semiregular solids, which are described in the article by V. Savchenko (see p. 2), are most interesting from the point of view of the designer: equiboundary semiregular polytopes and regular Platonic solids.

The fact is that if we continue the planes of the faces of any equi-boundary semi-regular body, then the lines of their intersection with each of the planes form systems that are congruent with each other. (For example, on the intro to the article there is a system of lines obtained in each of the planes of the faces of an equi-boundary semi-regular 120-hedron, depicted on Fig. 11, b on p. 6.) On the other hand, the extended faces of an equi-boundary polyhedron “cut down” in space a huge variety of all possible polyhedrons formed, sometimes of the most sub-unit form. And all these formations, thanks to the noted property, are conveniently defined on a system of *pseudo-edges* lying in the same plane (we will call a pseudo-edge the line of intersection of extended faces).

Let's explain this with an example. Let us take an equiboundary 24-hedron (see Fig. 11, b, p. 6). The system of its pseudo-edges belonging to the same plane is shown in figure 1. Let us mark on it the points O, O₁, O₂, O₃, ... and some angles with vertices at these points. What these points and angles are and how to find them, we will say a little later. For now, we just note that if one of the pseudo-edges passing through the same point, another at the marked angle, then it will go to another pseudo-edge passing through the same point, so that all intersection points lying on these pseudo-edges will coincide.

Using figure 1 we construct a polyhedron consisting of two intersecting irregular triangular pyramids.

First let's build these pyramids. Let's take a marked point O (see Fig. 1), passing through pseudo-edges and points S' and S'' on these pseudo-edges, which transform into each other when rotated by a marked angle around point O. Points S' and S'' are the intersection points of *two pseudo-edges*; Let's take the second pseudo-edges passing through them. Intersecting with the previously selected ones, they give us point A' (on the line OS''') and point B''' (on the line OS'). We get two triangles S'OA' and S''OB'''. Since the edge OS' is congruent with the edge OS''', then these two triangles can be “clined” to each other along these edges; Let's take them as two side faces of one of the future pyramids. Let us now note that when turning around point O by the marked angle counterclockwise, point A' goes to point A, and when turning around point B''' goes to point B. Together with point O, these two points form a triangle ABO, which is “attached” to the selected side faces along the edges OB (to the face S''''OB''': [OB'''] ≈ [OB]) and OA (to the face S'OA': [OA'] ≈ [OA]). Taking this triangle as the base of the future pyramids.
