M325. In a number triangle, the top number is 1 and the extreme numbers in each row are maybe 1, and each of the remaining numbers is not less than the sum of the two numbers above it (in part, we can conditionally satisfy the “Pascal triangle”). Let a natural number $a$, greater than 1, occur in this triangle $k$ times. Let us prove that $2^k < a^2$.

Divide the number triangle into right angles (see figure 6) and number these angles with the numbers 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ... Let $x_m$ be the number at the vertex of the $m$th angle, and $y_m$ and $z_m$ the numbers above the number $x_m$, $m \geq 1$. Then $y_m \geq x_m-1$ and $z_m \geq x_m-1$, and since $x_m \geq y_m + z_m$, then $x_m \geq 2x_m-1$, and if $m \geq 2$, then $y_m > x_m-1$, $z_m > x_m-1$, so $x_m > 2x_m-1$. Since $x_0 = 1$, then $x_m \geq 2^m$, and $x_m > 2^m$ with $m \geq 2$. Suppose

Fig. 6.

Bear in mind that $x_m$ is the largest of the numbers located at the vertices of the angles, not exceeding the number $a$, that is, $x_m \leq a$, $x_{m+1} > a$. The "sides" of each of the angles numbered 1, 2, ..., $m$ contain at most two numbers equal to $a$, and the angles numbered greater than $m$ consist of numbers greater than $a$. Therefore, $k \leq 2m$. At the same time, $a \geq x_m \geq 2^m$, which implies that $2^k \leq 2^{2m} \leq a^2$, and the equality $2^k = a^2$ is possible only for $m = 1$. But then $k \leq 2$, $2^k \leq 4$, so $a = 2$. But it is easy to verify that the number 2 can appear in a number triangle at most once, so in this case $2^k < a^2$.

F333. On a non-conducting disk of radius $R$, a wire of length $l$ is fixed along the chord. The disk is treated at a constant angular velocity $\omega$. Directed perpendicular to the disk.

Fig. 7.

There is a magnetic field with induction $B$ (Fig. 7). The maximum potential between the middle and the end of the wiring.

Let us first consider a conductor $OA$ of length $R$ attached to a disk along its radius. When the disk rotates, Lorentz forces will act on the free electrons of the conductor, as a result of which a potential difference will arise between the conductor. It can be calculated by imagining that the moving end $A$ of the conductor slides along a semi-angled contact wire connected to the end $O$ through a voltmeter (Fig. 8). Then, in a short period of time $\Delta t$, the area of ​​the circuit formed by moving and resting conductors will change by $\Delta S = R^2\omega \Delta t/2$, and the magnetic flux through the circuit will change by $\Delta \Phi = B\Delta S$. Consequently, the absolute value $\Delta c$ and the induction in the circuit, equal to the modulo potential difference at the ends of the conductor, will be equal to $|\mathcal{E}_1| = \Delta \Phi/\Delta t = BR^2\omega/2$, and the potential of the fixed end $O$ of the conductor will be higher than the potential of the end $A$.

If the length of the conductor were equal to $a$, then the potential of the end $O$ would be higher than the corresponding one by values ​​$\mathcal{E}_2 = Ba^2\omega/2$.

Let us now return to the original problem. Additionally, along the chord, two conductors of length $R$ and $a = \sqrt{R^2-l^2}/4$, connecting the center of the disk with one of the ends of the wiring and with its middle (points $A$ and $C$ on figure 9). When the disk rotates, the magnetic flux through the triangular circuit $AOCA$ does not change, therefore, the total e. d. c. induction in the circuit is zero, i.e.

$\mathcal{E}_{CA} - \mathcal{E}_1 + \mathcal{E}_2 = 0$.

This means that the required potential difference between the middle $C$ of the wiring and its end $A$ is equal to

$\mathcal{E}_{CA} = \mathcal{E}_1 - \mathcal{E}_2 = \frac{B\omega}{2}(R^2 - a^2) = Bl^2\omega/8$.
