Ultraviolet rays, obeying the general laws of absorption, reflection and refraction of electromagnetic waves, are at the same time absorbed and reflected by a number of substances differently than visible rays. Some substances have the property of absorbing ultraviolet rays, while others, on the contrary, transmit them unhindered, while remaining opaque to visible light rays. Under the influence of ultraviolet rays, many substances luminesce, that is, they emit visible light. Observing this glow is the most convenient and common way to study ultraviolet rays. When an object under study (for example, a painting or document) is irradiated with ultraviolet rays, details that are invisible under normal lighting become visible. You can also take photographs in ultraviolet rays (see Fig. 1). To do this, a layer of luminescent substance is applied to the photosensitive layer of the photographic plate, which converts invisible radiation into visible radiation. Photos obtained this way turn out to be clearer, with more detail.
