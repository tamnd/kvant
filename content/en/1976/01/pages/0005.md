If we combine the vertices of a regular cocahedra in the described way, then we get another semiregular polyhedron (see Fig. 3). It's called a *truncated icocahedron* and it resembles the image of a soccer ball you might see on a TV screen.

From other regular polyhedra one can obtain several more semiregular polyhedra. The most complex of them is called a *flat dodecahedron* and has 60 vertices, 92 faces and 150 edges.

How many semiregular polyhedra are there? For more than two thousand years it was thought that there were only thirteen (they are called *chalks of Archimedes*), not counting two endless series composed of prims and antiprisms. But quite recently, the Soviet mathematician V. G. Ashkinese found another semi-regular polyhedron. Look at figure 4: if the top of the “octagonal bowl” in this polyhedron is rotated by 45°, you will get the polyhedron shown at figure 5. This is exactly what Ashkinese discovered. The Ashkinese facet is sometimes called the “pseudarchimedean” body.

But it turns out that Archimedes' solids do not exhaust all types of semiregular polyhedra. Let's take a hexagonal feature (Fig. 6) and draw planes through the midpoints of each triple of edges emerging from one vertex.

Intersecting each other, these planes form a new polyhedron, which has the same number of faces as the recognition had vertices (in this case, 12). One of these planes - SFG - contains segment AB, belonging to the base of the feature, and the other plane - PFG - contains segment CD, belonging to the lower base of the feature.
