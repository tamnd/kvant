Since $[AB] \parallel [CD]$, then the planes $SFG$ and $PFG$ intersect along the straight line $FG$ parallel to $AB$. The resulting polyhedron is the union of two equal regular hexagonal pairs, the vertices $S$ and $P$ lying on opposite sides of their common base. A polyhedron of this type is called a **bipyramid**. The six planes passing through the point $S$ intersect with the planes passing through the point $P$, forming a regular hexagon, the midpoint of which side is the midpoint of the side edges recognized. It follows that the bipyramid obtained from the hexagonal pattern has 8 vertices and 18 edges. It contains polyhedral angles of two types: hexagonal and tetrahedral. Since all the faces of a bipyramid are equal equilateral triangles, it is classified as an equilateral semiregular polyhedron.

Similarly, one can obtain another isohedral semiregular polyhedron if we draw planes through the midpoints of each quadruple of antiprism edges emerging from one vertex. Thus, the plane $SM_1QT_1$ (Fig. 7) passes through the midpoints $M, T, G$ and $H$ of the edges $BA, BC, BD$ and $BE$. The number of faces of this polyhedron is equal to the number of vertices of the antiprism, that is, 12. Let's count the number of vertices of such a polyhedron. An edge of a new polyhedron passes through the middle of each antiprism edge, forming a spatial dodecagon. Adding the points $S$ and $P$ to its twelve vertices, we get that there are 14 vertices in total. It is easy to see that this polyhedron has 24 edges. All its faces are equal to each other and is a quadrilateral composed of two equilateral triangles with a common base (such a quadrilateral is called a **deltmoid**).
