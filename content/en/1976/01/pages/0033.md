Descent Methods

The need to find the maximum or minimum (united by the name “extremum” - “extreme value”) of a certain function arises in many problems of economics and technology. And the terrain can be considered a model of a function of two variables, say latitude and longitude. The value of the function is the height of the terrain (above sea level) at a given point, the horizontal lines are the level line, along them the function is constant. And the situation that arises when searching for an extreme point is in many ways similar to the one in which unlucky travelers find themselves. The search for an extremum is carried out as if in the dark, the only means of orientation being the function being minimized (maximized) at some point, as well as the direction of its fastest decrease or increase.

Many methods have already been invented for finding the extremum. It is no coincidence, as the reader noted, that the names of those who were shipwrecked will coincide with the names of a number of mathematicians, past and present, who solved this problem. And the path they took shows the essence of their methods of finding an extremum.

The great Frenchman P. Fermat (1601-1665) was the first to clearly understand and was able to describe the analytical properties of the extremum of a smooth continuous function: the tangent at this place must be horizontal. K. Gauss (1777-1855) and A. Seidel (1821-1896), following their method of coordinate descent, took turns descending along one or the other coordinate. We descended until the tangent to the direction of movement became horizontal. And the position of the level naturally determines the direction of the tangent.

Please note that Gauss and Seidel did not strive to descend in the direction where the circularity is greatest. O. Cauchy (1789-1857) was inspired by this idea. The vector indicating the direction (upward) with the largest coarseness is called a graden, and the Cauchy method itself is called a graden method. It was along the gradient that Cauchy would have made his way if his goal had been to find a high point in the area to inspect. But let us remember that there was pitch darkness on the island and Cauchy’s goal was water. Therefore, he set off in the direction of the fastest decreasing altitude of the area, or in the direction of the anti-gradient. To determine the antigradient of a function, approximate methods are also essential, but it is enough for us to imagine the level that we apply to the ground and look for the direction in which the air bubble quickly runs away to the edge of the scale. Of course, this method is very inaccurate, and there are a lot of hassles with it. But to determine the gradient using the mentioned analytical methods, you also have to tinker.
