As paradoxical as it may seem, water, starting to move from point $A$, always descends relative to the surface of the ventite, and as a result of this rises up to point $C$. Try to figure out how this happens. Let the section of the gutter $AE$ make an angle $\alpha$ with the “bottom” of the cylinder $AB$. Let us tilt the cylinder with the groove at an angle greater than $\alpha$. Then water will fill area $AE$ and part of area $EO$.

Let's start rotating the cylinder around its axis. All points of section $AE$ (except point $A$) will begin to rise, while all points of section $EO$ (except point $E$) will fall, and the water will gradually move from the “heated” section further along the gutter, falling down relative to it and rising relative to the surface of the earth. This will happen because during rotation, various parts of the gutter, successively replacing one another, will be in relation to the water in the same position as section $AE$; so the water will begin to descend down the chute, but in the end it turns out that it has risen from point $A$ to point $C$.

Some readers have probably already asked a logical question: “Doesn’t all these arguments contradict the law of conservation of energy, does this mechanism represent perpetual motion?” Indeed, at first glance it seems that this is so. We turn the wheel, water flows down the screw, as if along an inclined plane, under the influence of its own weight and pours out of the upper hole. All that remains is to convert the energy stored in water into the energy of rotation of the ventite itself, and the perpetual motion is ready!

If this problem were offered to you in a physics lesson, then, apparently, many of you would immediately think that such reasoning contradicts the law of conservation of energy, and some of you would go even further in reasoning and add that water rises along the chute due to energy supplied from the outside and causing the vent to rotate, therefore the potential energy of water will always be less than the work expended on its rise.

All this, of course, is true, but how does the gutter transfer energy supplied from outside to the water? In what areas does this happen? How is the force acting on the water directed? Try to answer these questions yourself. To simplify the consideration of the movement of water in the gutter, we can replace the movement of the ball along the screw channel.

A primitive model of Archimedes' ventite is easy to make at home. Take a piece of tube with a diameter of 3-5 cm and a length of 20-30 cm or a round wooden block of the same size. The role of the gutter can be performed by any rubber, polyvinyl chloride (cambric) or copper tube with a diameter of several millimeters (the larger the diameter, the better and more visual the model will be). It is very good if you have a transparent flexible tube at your disposal; then the movement of water or a ball can be traced inside the Archimedes ventite.

Make a ventite trench from this tube by wrapping it around the cylinder of your choice along a screw line, and secure the ends of the tube to the upper and lower bases of this cylinder using threads or wires. That's it, your model is ready to go. Dip one end into a vessel with water and tilt it at an angle greater than the angle of the spiral to the base of the cylinder.
