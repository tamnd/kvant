Descent Methods

The need to find the maximum or minimum (united by the name “extremum” - “extreme value”) of a certain function arises in many problems of economics and technology. And the terrain can be considered a model of a function of two variables, say latitude and longitude. The value of the function is the height of the terrain (above sea level) at a given point, the horizontal lines are the level line, along them the function is constant. And the situation that arises when searching for an extreme point is in many ways similar to the one in which unlucky travelers find themselves. The search for an extremum is carried out as if in the dark, the only means of orientation being the function being minimized (maximized) at some point, as well as the direction of its fastest decrease or increase.

Many methods have already been invented for finding the extremum. It is no coincidence, as the reader noted, that the names of those who were shipwrecked will coincide with the names of a number of mathematicians, past and present, who solved this problem. And the path they took shows the essence of their methods of finding an extremum.

The great Frenchman P. Fermat (1601-1665) was the first to clearly understand and was able to describe the analytical properties of the extremum of a smooth continuous function: the tangent at this place must be horizontal. K. Gauss (1777-1855) and A. Seidel (1821-1896), following their method of coordinate descent, took turns descending along one or the other coordinate. We descended until the tangent to the direction of movement became horizontal. And the position of the level naturally determines the direction of the tangent.

Please note that Gauss and Seidel did not strive to descend in the direction where the circularity is greatest. O. Cauchy (1789-1857) was inspired by this idea. The vector indicating the direction (upward) with the largest coarseness is called a graden, and the Cauchy method itself is called a graden method. It was along the gradient that Cauchy would have made his way if his goal had been to find a high point in the area to inspect. But let us remember that there was pitch darkness on the island and Cauchy’s goal was water. Therefore, he set off in the direction of the fastest decreasing altitude of the area, or in the direction of the anti-gradient. To determine the antigradient of a function, approximate methods are also essential, but it is enough for us to imagine the level that we apply to the ground and look for the direction in which the air bubble quickly runs away to the edge of the scale. Of course, this method is very inaccurate, and there are a lot of hassles with it. But to determine the gradient using the mentioned analytical methods, you also have to tinker.

Where does the asphalt end?

When you have to determine the extremum of a function that has many of them, the problem is a lot of conditions. In this case, it is usually necessary not only to find the extremum (say, the minimum), but also to determine the “global” extremum, or “minimum minimum”. Unfortunately, most real mathematical problems for which a minimum is sought are multiextremal. In the same way, it is difficult to find a terrain in which one peak would dominate undividedly over a very large area. Obviously listened to the tops there are tops

smaller, but there is also a relatively small area from which the climb will certainly lead to the highest peak. This area is called the *region of acceptance* of the global extreme. It is enough to go beyond the boundaries of this area or find yourself beyond it at the beginning of the period, and the achievement of a global extremum becomes difficult to guarantee. The chances of hitting the local (*local*) extreme increase.

There are many ways to combat multi-extreme behavior, but none of them guarantee success. These methods are of a non-rigorous, as mathematicians say, *heuristic* nature. Proponents of three different multi-extreme methods met in the Skeptics group. We do not cite them specifically: the front of scientific research in this area has recently expanded so much and has increasingly entered into the fight against multi-extremeness that the indicated methods are difficult to associate with someone’s specific names.

Some tried to jump out of the extremum if they were already in it, and by the success or slowness of these attempts they judged the achievement of the global extremum. Others attempted to descend from several randomly selected points. Finally, others (the height of skepticism!) came to the conclusion that the wisest thing to do was not to direct the mind, but to simply fight around the island, looking at the altimeter.

All three of these courses of action find adherents among scientists, although the third is still less common. Special programs for computers have been compiled that implement each of these methods and many others. Nevertheless, not only inveterate skeptics, but also cheerful optimists do not risk today considering the problem solved. And the first impulse when analyzing any function for an extremum is an attempt to prove its uniqueness. Often this achievement is revered no less than the correctness of the mathematical model in general. For example, the absence of local extrema in linear programming problems is an important argument for trying linear models, outweighing many of their shortcomings.

There are all kinds of sophisticated ways to combine different methods. For example, all three skeptics could be united into one team. You say there is little kinship between them? And you don’t need much. It is enough that they have little faith in their own methods.
