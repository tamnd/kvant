How to draw a five-pointed star?

A regular five-pointed star is beautiful, isn’t it? We see it on the coat of arms and flag of our Motherland, it shines on military caps and on the spiers of the Kremlin towers.

Who didn’t try to draw this star as a child? Probably everyone. But the result was something lopsided and awkward, something like figure 1.

First try
At school you were introduced to tools for geometric constructions - compasses and rulers.

Let's try to resume our attempts to draw the correct five-pointed star. First we ask ourselves, what does correct mean? Probably, it is necessary that the five vertices be connected by segments of equal length. The ruler will allow us to make the sides of the star straight, and with the help of a compass we will try to make them equal.

Let's get started. We construct an angle with vertex A. On its sides we lay equal segments AB and AC (Fig. 2). There are three peaks. Just two more to find and everything is fine. One of them (D) must be removed from point B at the same distance as point A. This means that it must lie on a circle with a center at point B of radius AB. Let's draw arc (2) of this circle. Similarly, we draw an arc (3) of a circle of the same radius with a center at point C. The second of the remaining vertices (E) should lie on it.

Let's remember what we want: the distance between vertices D and E should be equal to the distance between the other vertices. Therefore, without changing the distance of the compass, we place one of its legs at some point D of one arc (this will be even

### Second try

Where is the mistake? It is immediately visible. The sides of the star are equal, but the angles at the vertices are not. How can we make sure that all sides and all angles are equal?

Let's draw a circle. Let's divide it into 5 equal arcs, and then connect the ends of the arcs through one (Fig. 3). There is no doubt that this is the star we need! But how to divide a circle into 5 equal parts? Using a protractor? There are $360^\circ$ in the entire circle, which means there are $72^\circ$ in each part. We take the protractor... where is it? He disappeared somewhere again. Or maybe we can do without it?

Exact solution
Is it possible to immediately get an exact solution? It turns out that it is possible.

Here is one of the “sieves” of such a construction. Let $BC$ be the diameter of the circle (1), $AO$ the radius perpendicular to it (Fig. 6). Let us draw a circle (2) of radius $BO$ with center $B$. We draw a straight line through the intersection points of circles (1) and (2) and denote by $D$ the point of its intersection with the diameter. Then, with the center at point $D$ and radius $DA$, we draw another circle (3). The point of its intersection with the diameter will be denoted by $E$. Drawing circle (4) of radius $AE$ with center $A$, at the intersection with circle (1) we obtain points $M$ and $M'$.

The arc $AM$ is equal to the arc $AM'$ and is equal to the arc $1/5$ of the first circle.

To justify this, you need knowledge of high school mathematics.

You can come up with a designated construction lattice yourself if you read the article by A.D. Bendukidze “The Golden Solution” («Kvant», 1973, No. 8). It talks about the underlying geometric properties of a regular five-pointed star.

approximate construction, if there is an exact one?” But why.

First of all, what does “accurate” mean? If it is carried out with perfect instructions, then it is valid for sure. But you will draw realistically with a pencil, compass and ruler. This will obviously result in an inaccurate result.

And, secondly, the approximate method is suitable for dividing a circle into any number of equal parts, therefore, it is enough to remember only one method for this.

Further, this method is simpler than the exact one. And simplicity is often an argument. Thus, many mathematical calculations on electronic computers (computers) are carried out using approximate methods (although there are also exact ones) only because their use is simpler, and in some cases the machines may not allow the use of an accurate but cumbersome method.

And one last thing. It turns out that there are no and cannot be exact methods for dividing a circle into 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 18, 21, 22, 23, 25 and many other equal parts. The proof of this fact belongs to the remarkable German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss.
