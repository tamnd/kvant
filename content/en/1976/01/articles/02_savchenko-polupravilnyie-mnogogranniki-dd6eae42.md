## Semiregular polyhedra

Readers of the magazine «Kvant» have encountered regular polyhedra more than once. Regular polyhedra have faces that are equal regular polygons, and all polyhedral angles are equal. But there are also polyhedra in which all polyhedral angles are equal, and the faces are regular polygons of several types. They cannot be classified as regular - they are called *equiangular semiregular polyhedra*.

The simplest of the semiregular polyhedra is the regular $n$-gonal *prism* ($n=3$, 5, 6, $\ldots$), all of whose edges are equal and whose lateral faces are squares. The bases of, for example, a hexagonal prism are regular hexagons: it has 12 vertices, 8 faces and 18 edges.

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 1

Another representative of equiangular semiregular polyhedra is the so-called *antiprism*. Imagine, for example, two regular hexagons located in parallel planes, one of which is rotated about the center relative to the other by $30^\circ$. Each vertex of both the lower and upper hexagons is connected to the two closest vertices of the other. The distance between the hexagons (bases) is selected so that the side faces are regular triangles (Fig. 1) - in the case of a hexagonal antiprism, this distance (height) is $H=a\sqrt{\sqrt3-1}$ (check that this is the case). A hexagonal antiprism has 12 vertices, 14 faces and 24 edges, all edges and all tetrahedral angles are equal to each other.

Using all regular polygons for prisms and antiprisms, we obtain an infinite series of semiregular polyhedra. However, these series do not exhaust all equiangular semiregular polyhedra: there are other semiregular polyhedra. The simplest of them can be obtained by cutting off the corners of a regular tetrahedron with planes, each of which cuts off a third of the edges emerging from one vertex (Fig. 2); this polyhedron has 12 vertices, 8 faces and 18 edges, and is called a *truncated tetrahedron*. All ero trihedral angles are equal to each other - each of them contains two plane angles in $120^\circ$ and one plane angle in $60^\circ$. A truncated tetrahedron has four faces that are regular hexagons and another four that are regular triangles.

If we cut off the vertices of a regular icosahedron in the described way, we get another semiregular polyhedron (see Fig. 3). It's called a *truncated icosahedron* and resembles the image of a soccer ball that can be seen on a TV screen.

From other regular polyhedra one can obtain several more semiregular polyhedra. The most complex of them is called the *snosed dodecahedron* and has 60 vertices, 92 faces and 150 edges.

How many semiregular polyhedra are there? For more than two thousand years it was thought that there were only thirteen (they are called *Archimedes' bodies*), not counting two infinite series composed of prisms and antiprisms. But quite recently, the Soviet mathematician V. G. Ashkinuse found another semi-regular polyhedron. Look at figure 4: if in this polyhedron the upper “octagonal bowl” is rotated by $45^\circ$, you get the polyhedron shown in figure 5. This is what Ashkinuse discovered. True, this polyhedron is “not quite an Archimedean” body: it does not possess some properties that the first thirteen representatives possess (but it does have its own properties). The Ashkinuse polyhedron is sometimes called a "pseudo-Archimedean" solid.

But it turns out that Archimedes' solids do not exhaust all types of semiregular polyhedra. Let's take a hexagonal prism (Fig. 6) and draw planes through the midpoints of each triple of edges emerging from one vertex. Intersecting each other, these planes form a new polyhedron, which has the same number of faces as the prism had vertices (in this case, 12). One of these planes - $SFG$ - contains the segment $AB$, belonging to the base of the prism, and the other plane - $PFG$ - contains the segment $CD$, belonging to the lower base of the prism. Since $[AB]\parallel[CD]$, then the planes $SFG$ and $PFG$ intersect along the line $FG$ parallel to $AB$. The resulting polyhedron is a union of two equal regular hexagonal pyramids, the vertices $S$ and $P$ lying on opposite sides of their common base. A polyhedron of this type is called a *bipyramid*. Six planes passing through point $S$ intersect with planes passing through point $P$, forming a regular hexagon, the midpoints of the sides of which are the midpoints of the lateral edges of the prism. It follows that the bipyramid, obtained from a hexagonal prism, has 8 vertices and 18 edges. It contains polyhedral angles of two types: hexagonal and tetrahedral. Since all the faces of a bipyramid are equal isosceles triangles, it is classified as an *equifaceted semiregular* polyhedron.

Similarly, you can get another isohedral semiregular polyhedron if you draw planes through the midpoints of each four antiprism edges emerging from one vertex. Thus, the plane $SM_1QT_1$ (Fig. 7) passes through the midpoints $M$, $T$, $G$ and $H$ of edges $BA$, $BC$, $BD$ and $BE$. The number of faces of this polyhedron is equal to the number of vertices of the antiprism, i.e. 12. Let's count the number of vertices of such a polyhedron. An edge of a new polyhedron passes through the middle of each of the side edges of the antiprism, forming a spatial dodecagon. By adding the points $S$ and $P$ to its twelve vertices, we get that there are 14 vertices in total. It is easy to see that this polyhedron has 24 edges. All its faces are equal to each other and are quadrangles, composed of two isosceles triangles with a common base (such a quadrilateral is called *deltoid*). The polyhedron contains two hexagonal angles and twelve trihedral angles. All angles are correct. (A convex polyhedral angle is called regular if all its plane angles are equal.) On figure 8 the general view of such a polyhedron is given.

If we draw planes through the midpoints of each triple of edges emanating from one vertex of a truncated tetrahedron (Fig. 2), then, mutually intersecting, these planes form a new isohedral semiregular polyhedron. It has 12 faces, 8 vertices and 18 edges. The construction of such a polyhedron is shown in figure 9. This polyhedron has four regular trihedral and hexagonal angles, and the faces are equal isosceles triangles. The general view of the polyhedron is shown in figure 10.

There is a certain relationship between equiangular semi-regular polyhedra and the resulting equi-semi-regular polyhedra. Let us compare the number of vertices, faces and edges of each pair of polyhedra considered.

A hexagonal prism has 12 vertices, 8 faces and 18 edges, and the bipyramid obtained from it has 8 vertices, 12 faces and 18 edges. Thus, the numbers of vertices and faces of a prism and a bipyramid are, as it were, “rearranged,” but the number of edges remains unchanged. Make sure that the same relationship is observed for the remaining pairs of polyhedra considered. The established pattern follows from the *principle of duality*, which consists of the following: *for any sentence regarding points, lines and planes, you can always formulate a sentence dual to it by replacing the word “point” with the word “plane”, and the word “plane” with the word “point”, leaving the word “straight” unchanged.*

The principle of duality does not only apply to polyhedra: it extends to all figures and statements that are considered in projective geometry. This principle finds its justification there, but we will not prove it here.

It was indicated above that there are 14 equiangular semiregular polyhedra. Each of them corresponds to a certain equifaceted semiregular polyhedron dual to it. On figure 11 you see three Archimedean solids (above) depicted together with their equihedral semi-regular "doubles" (below). Ashkinuse, published in the Encyclopedia of Elementary Mathematics, book four (M.: Nauka, 1963).

### Exercises

1. What is an antiprism, the bases of which are regular triangles? Determine its dual polyhedron.
2. Calculate the volume of an antiprism with a hexagonal base (Fig. 1), assuming that the edge length is $a$.
3. The polyhedron shown in Figures 9 and 10 can be obtained in another way. It is enough to place a regular triangular pyramid on each face of a regular tetrahedron. Calculate the height of this pyramid and the plane angle at its apex if the edge of the tetrahedron is $a$.
4. Under the conditions of Exercise 3, calculate the volume of the resulting polyhedron.
5. Calculate the plane angle $\psi$ at the vertex of the face of the polyhedron shown in figure 7, and construct its development.
6. By cutting off the corners of a regular octahedron, construct a semi-regular polyhedron with 8 faces - regular hexagons and 6 faces - squares, and find its volume.
7. Determine the polyhedron dual to the truncated octahedron (Exercise 6). Find the plane angle $\phi$ at the vertex of its face.
