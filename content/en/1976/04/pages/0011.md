When the crystal is loaded to a value below the “theoretical” strength in the UCT crack (region $a$ on figure 1), in a small volume of the crystal, stresses may arise at which the bonds between the units begin to break down. This means that the crack will spread deep into the sample, penetrate it and split it into two parts. But the crystal will collapse not because all the bonds in the fracture plane are broken at the same time, but because the sequential destruction of the bond must occur and a crack will grow and dismember the crystal.

How Ioffe imagined the mechanism of crystal destruction can be clearly illustrated by a model experiment. It is simple, and its results leave no doubt. On the microsopp table there is a thin plate of plexiglass stretched, on the side end of which a sharp and shallow cut is made. Plastic models a crystal, a cut represents a crack on its surface. With special illumination (in polarized light), it is possible to distinguish stressed areas in plexiglass: the greater the stress, the darker the corresponding area. So, in the sequence of frames of the movie we have shown (Fig. 2), it is clear that in the UCT of the notch the stress is actually maximum and that the plate is destroyed due to the movement of the stressed UCT through it. Moreover, this occurs at stresses significantly lower than those necessary to destroy the plate without a cut.

In a simplified version, you can do a similar experiment yourself, without resorting to a microscope, polarized light, or film. You can simply see that it is much easier to tear a strip of paper by stretching it if you first make a small cut on it.
