Comparing this experiment with the experiment described by G.I. Pokrovsky, try to explain its result.

The proposed experiment is especially interesting because in it one can observe the very process of formation of a cumulative jet. In an experiment with a falling test tube, this is much more difficult to do: the eye does not have time to detect the phenomena that occur at the moment of formation of the jet when the test tube hits the table. However, we advise you to return once again to the experiment with the falling test tube and use the principle of jet formation in more detail. To this end, we offer you several experimental tasks.

1. Find out whether the shape of the bottom of the test tube affects the formation of the jet. Perhaps the jet will arise due to the focusing of a shock wave appearing in the water?

Solder a rigid one to a thin-walled copper tube to any shape you like (for example, flat or wavy). Experiments carried out with the resulting test tubes will show that the shape of the bottom does not affect the formation of the jet. Thus, the focusing of the shock wave cannot explain the result of the experiment.

2. Does the liquid necessarily have to be the wall of the test tube?

 Place a small piece of paraffin inside the test tube and melt it over a dry fuel flame. Rotating the test tube removed from the flame, cover it from the inside with a thin layer of paraffin and conduct the experiment of G.I. Pokrovsky. In this case, a cumulative jet is not formed. Consequently, the wettability of the walls of the test tube with liquid is an essential condition for the experiment.
