The tensile strength of a crystal is the ratio of the force at which it develops to the cross-sectional area over which rupture will occur: $\sigma_{\text{np}} = F/S$.

It is natural to assume that the force $F$ is the sum of the forces $f$ acting on each of the $n$ pairs of interacting ones: $F = nf$. If the cross-sectional area of ​​the sample at the rupture site is $S$, and the distance between them (lattice constant) is $a^*$, then $n = S/a^2$. And since the crystal is solid and the force of interaction between them is described by Coulomb’s law, then

$$f = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q^2}{a^2},$$

where $q$ is the abscile value of the ion charge.

So,

$$\sigma_{\text{np}} = \frac{F}{S} = \frac{nf}{S} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q^2}{a^4}.$$

In NaCl crystals, the charge coincides with the charge of the electron, i.e. $q = e = 1,6 \cdot 10^{-19} \text{ к}$, the value is $a = 5,3 \cdot 10^{-10} \text{ м}$, therefore $\sigma_{\text{np}} \approx 2,6 \cdot 10^9 \text{ н/m}^2$. This value is close to that following from the exact theory.

Unexpected idea

Ioffe reasons as follows: the theorist is not mistaken, but he is calculating an ideal situation when the interactions of all $n$ parons are simultaneously stopped.
