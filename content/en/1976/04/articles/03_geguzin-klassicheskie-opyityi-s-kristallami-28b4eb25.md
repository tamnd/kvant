The tensile strength of a crystal is the ratio of the force at which it develops to the cross-sectional area over which rupture will occur: $\sigma_{\text{np}} = F/S$.

It is natural to assume that the force $F$ is the sum of the forces $f$ acting on each of the $n$ pairs of interacting ones: $F = nf$. If the cross-sectional area of ​​the sample at the rupture site is $S$, and the distance between them (lattice constant) is $a^*$, then $n = S/a^2$. And since the crystal is solid and the force of interaction between them is described by Coulomb’s law, then

$$f = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q^2}{a^2},$$

where $q$ is the abscile value of the ion charge.

So,

$$\sigma_{\text{np}} = \frac{F}{S} = \frac{nf}{S} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q^2}{a^4}.$$

In NaCl crystals, the charge coincides with the charge of the electron, i.e. $q = e = 1,6 \cdot 10^{-19} \text{ к}$, the value is $a = 5,3 \cdot 10^{-10} \text{ м}$, therefore $\sigma_{\text{np}} \approx 2,6 \cdot 10^9 \text{ н/m}^2$. This value is close to that following from the exact theory.

II. LUKIRSKY EXPERIENCE

War, 1943, a large room at the Kazan University is divided into several small rooms, in each of them there is a group of physicists from the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology, who were evacuated to Kazan. In one of the improvised laboratories there are employees of Professor Pyotr Ivanovich Lukirsky. A lot of things are connected with defense work, and the professor and his employees are busy with them. But as a tribute to the natural curiosity of the rustling scientist - experiments with single crystals of rock coli. They have become classics of crystal physics, and I want to talk about them.

Both in concept and in implementation, these experiments are valid and differ only in the object of research, more precisely, in the form and shape of the sample studied.

One version of the experiment went like this. A carefully polished cylinder made from a single crystal of rock coli was subjected to long-term high-temperature annealing; The cylinder was oriented parallel to the edge of the cube of the natural bounded crystal. If before annealing the cylinder silently rolled along a slightly inclined glass surface, then after annealing the rolling surface was accompanied by a uniform movement, as if ribs appeared on the surface of the cylinder - four ribs equally spaced from one another. These ribs could be seen by examining the annealed cylinder in reflected light.

In another variant, a carefully polished single-crystal sphere was also subjected to annealing. After annealing, filament highlights could be clearly seen on its surface in reflected light (Fig. 3) *); there were none before annealing.

*) In the above photo you can see glare of various shapes. This is due to the fact that in crystals there are symmetrine axes of different orders. (Some straight line is called a $k$-th order symmetry axis for a given body if, when the body rotates around this line through an angle $360^\circ/k$, it is aligned with itself.) In particular, an elliptical block corresponds to a second-order symmetry axis, and a square block corresponds to a fourth-order symmetry axis.

The result of both experiments can be formulated as follows: crystals of rock salt, which are blown into a cylindrical or spherical shape that is not typical for them, tend to restore the shape of a cube - their natural limit, or, as crystallographers say, “natural gas”. The high temperature in these experiments is needed only to give activity to some mechanism of crystal mass transfer necessary for the formation of “natural gas”. crystals, of course, will “prefer” the one of the mechanisms that gives them the opportunity to quickly get rid of the air of a given shape and restore their natural shape.

Why does the crystal strive for natural limitation? It turns out that, among a myriad of other ideas, it is precisely this that provides the lowest surface energy of a crystal for a given volume *). Namely, the minimum energy is characteristic of the equilibrium, i.e., the most natural, state of the crystal. Mathematically, this behavior of the crystal is described by the Curie-Wolfe rule. It can be formed as follows: if a crystal has a volume of $V$, is bounded by $n$ edges, its $i$-th edge has a surface area of ​​$s_i$ and a surface energy of $\alpha_i$, then

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i \alpha_i \rightarrow \text{minimum при } V = \text{const.}$$

In this notation, the arrow indicates the tendency of the crystal to make its total surface energy minimal, provided that the volume is constant. Lukirsky's experience made visible what the symbolic arrow denotes.

The wise Curie-Wolfe rule may seem contradictory to the no less wise statement of geometry, according to which, of all bodies of a given volume, the sphere has the minimum surface. Therefore, if a spherical single crystal tends to reduce the surface energy, it would seem that it should not be limited, since in this case its surface will only increase. The surface will actually increase - the geometry is right. But the energy will decrease, because during limitation, surface areas that have a large specific surface energy (i.e., surface energy relative to a unit surface area) disappear, and surfaces presented in a natural limitation, which have a lower surface energy, develop. And this effect plays a larger role than the energy of increasing the surface. The surface area increases, but the energy decreases! For many years, photographs of crystal surfaces have hung on the wall of our laboratory. Look at the photograph of a copper crystal shown on figure 4. We call it the “staircase of the Peterf fountain”—alternating light and dark stripes are not visible on it, actually filling the staircase along which water flows in a continuous stream. Another photo of the crystal surface (Fig. 5) is called "tent city". It shows a set of pointed triangular protrusions, which are limited by the same planes as the equilibrium single crystal.

Why is a crystal, dissected along an arbitrary plane, not limited as a whole, like a sphere in Lukirsky’s opite, but the formation of a “Peterf staircase” and a “tent city” is allowed? Yes, simply because both the “staircase” and the “town” are only stages on the way to true equilibrium, stages that are achieved faster, with less mass transfer, than the true equilibrium form of the entire crystal. And on the surface of Lukirsky’s samples, intermediate forms could be observed. However, due to the fact that at high temperatures the required mass transfer occurs quickly in rock salt crystals, in Lukirsky’s process of striving for an equilibrium form went so far that one could observe glare on the sphere, and when the cylinder rolled, one could hear tapping.
