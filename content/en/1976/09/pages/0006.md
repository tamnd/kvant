Gerolamo Cardano
Cardano was born on September 24, 1501 in Pavni, into the family of a lawyer. After graduating from university, Gerolamo decides to devote himself to medicine. Since he was an illegal child, he initially had to practice for a long time in the provinces. Only in August 1539, having specially changed the rules for this purpose, Cardano was admitted to the college of doctors of Milan. Later he even became the rector of this college. Cardano was one of the most famous doctors of his time; probably second - after his friend Bezalia.

In his declining years, Cardano wrote an autobiography, “About My Life.” In it, he only mentions his studies in mathematics a few times and talks in detail about his research in medicine. Cardano claims that he described up to five thousand more teaching techniques, that the number of problems he solved reached up to forty thousand, and the number of small instructions reached two hundred thousand. Of course, these numbers should be taken with a grain of salt. Yet the words of Cardano the doctor were undeniable. Cardano claims that he suffered only three misfortunes in his medical practice.

However, medical studies did not completely absorb Cardano. In his free time from medicine, he studied “everything in the world”: philosophy, astrology, physics, mechanics, mathematics.

Cardano compiled horoscopes of both the living and the dead (Christ, Petraki, Dürer, Bezalia, Luther). The Pope used the services of a Cardan astrologer. (According to one unkind legend, Cardano committed suicide to confirm his own horoscope.)

Cardano's book On Thin Materials (1550) was translated into French and served as a popular textbook on statics and hydrostatics throughout the 17th century. Galileo, when observing the swing of the natural matrix - the chandelier in the cathedral - used Cardano's instructions to use his own pulse to measure time. Cardano wrote about the need for a perpetual motion machine. Some of his remarks can be interpreted as a principle of possible movements. Cardano experimentally established the relationship between the densities of air and water. For the royal carriage, he invented a system for connecting two currencies, which below is called cardan (gimbal, cardan shaft, cardan joint) and is widely used in automation. (However, it is fair to note that the idea of ​​such a system dates back to antiquity; in addition, on one of Leonardo da Vinci’s figure a compass with a gimbal is chosen.)

Some of Cardano's works were encyclopedias of sorts. During the Renaissance, encyclopedias were written by single people. The first encyclopedias created by collective work appeared only a century and a half later.

Cardano wrote a huge number of books (some of them were published, some remained in manuscripts, some he destroyed, awaiting arrest (see below)). However, their description alone made up an entire book, “On My Own Writings.” For many years his books on philosophy and ethics were received.
