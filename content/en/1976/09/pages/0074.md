⟦folio 72⟧

Tamuna and Georgy somehow came running up to me and interrupting each other, asking:

—Can a part be equal to the whole?
— Part to the whole? - I say. - Of course he can’t. The part is always less than the whole. By the way, the great ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, who wrote the first systematic course on geometry, took this statement as one of his axims.

“But today the brother of our classmate Kakha, he’s a student, came to see us at school and said that this is not so, that there are cases when a part is equal to the whole. “He even sets an example for us,” said Georgy.

- Do you remember this example?
“We remember, we remember very well, listen...,” they said in one voice.

- Please, I am at your service. Just let's go in order, don't interrupt each other. Well, Tamuna, tell me what it was about.

- So, so. Let's take the set of all natural numbers
{1, 2, 3, 4,...}
and in it is a subset of all even numbers
{2, 4, 6, 8,...}.

After all, the second is part of the first?
- Certainly.

- And it turns out they are equal. Look,” and she drew the following table:

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | ... | 100 | 101 | ... | 500 | 501 | ... |
| 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | ... | 200 | 202 | ... | 1000 | 1002 | ... |

In this table, under each natural number, the definition of an even number is written, and none is missing and none is repeated. It turns out that there are the same number of even numbers as there are natural numbers, i.e. these sets are equal!

- What did you say - “equal”? Don't you remember which sets are called equal?
