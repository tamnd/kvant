Mastering symmetry

A. Equilateral triangle

An equilateral triangle is a symmetrical figure: the bisector of the angle at the vertex is its axis of symmetry. In a sixth-grade geometry course, axial symmetry is used to prove the properties of an equilateral triangle regarding the angles at its base and the height drawn from the vertex.

Here's another simple property of an equilateral triangle; it will help us solve several difficult problems in the future.

If the lateral sides AB and BC of an isosceles triangle ABC contain congruous segments AM and CN, then the point of intersection of the lines CM and AN lies on the bisector BD of the triangle.

Proof. Indeed, if the bisector BD of a given isosceles triangle ABC is symmetric, vertex A goes to vertex C, side AB goes to side CB (see Fig. 1). Basic symmetry is movement; the lengths of the segments are preserved under symmetry. By condition $N \in [BC]$ and $|CN| = |AM|$, therefore, the point $M \in [AB]$ goes to the point $N$.

So, points $A$ and $C$, as well as points $M$ and $N$ are symmetrical relative to the BD axis; This means that the segments AN and CM are symmetrical to each other.

Let $K = [AN] \cap [BD]$. Since $K$ lies on the axis of symmetry, the point symmetric to it is itself. On the other hand, $K \in [AN]$; therefore, the image of the point $K$ must belong to the image of the segment $AN$, i.e., the segment CM. Thus $K = [AN] \cap [CM] \in [BD]$, which is what needed to be proved.
