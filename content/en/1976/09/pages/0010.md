Equation (2) can be solved using the substitution $x = \beta + \alpha$, but here a case may already arise when the original equation has three real roots, and the auxiliary quadratic equation has no real roots. This is the so-called “irreducible” case*). He caused a lot of trouble for the Cardans (and probably Tartaglia).

Kardanov solved equation (3), giving a very bold alienation for those times, burdening the negativity of the root. No one before him had used negative numbers so decisively, although Kardanov is still far from freely handling them: he considers equations (1) and (2) separately!

Kardanov also completely figured out the general cubic equation $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ (Tartaglia has nothing to do with this!), then, speaking in modern language, the substitution $x = y - \frac{a}{3}$ destroys the term with $x^2$.

Kardanov decides to consider not only negative numbers (he calls them “purely false”), but also complex ones (he calls them “posistibly sophistical”). He notes that if you operate with them according to some natural rules, then complex roots can be assigned to a quadratic equation that does not have real roots. Possibly to complex Cardan numbers at SEL in connection with the “irreducible” case.

"Great Art" also featured Ferrari's personal contribution - solving a fourth-degree equation.

In modern language, the Ferrari method for the equation
$$x^4 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$
(the complete equation of the fourth degree easily coincides with equation (6)) is as follows.

*) It was already written about him in «Kvant»: 1971, No. 11, p. 20 and 1973, no. 3, p. 30. See also p. 11 in this issue.

By introducing the auxiliary parameter $t$, we rewrite equation (6) in an equivalent form:
$$\left(x^2 + \frac{a}{2} + t\right)^2 = 2tx^2 - bx + \left(t^2 + at - c + \frac{a^2}{4}\right).$$

Let us now select the value of the parameter $t$ so that the square (relative to $x$) trilen on the right side of equation (7) has two coinciding roots. To do this, it is necessary that the discriminant of this triplen be equal to zero:
$$b^2 - 4 \cdot 2t \cdot \left(t^2 + at - c + \frac{a^2}{4}\right) = 0.$$

We have obtained an auxiliary cubic equation for $t$. Let us find some of its roots $t_0$ using the Cardanov formula. Equation (7) can now be rewritten as:
$$\left(x^2 + \frac{a}{2} + t_0\right)^2 = 2t_0 \left(x - \frac{b}{4t_0}\right)^2.$$

Equation (8) breaks down into a pair of quadratic equations that give the four roots we are looking for.

Thus, according to the Ferrari method, the solution to a fourth-degree equation will coincide with the solution to the auxiliary cubic equation and two quadratic equations.

The place of the “Great Art” in the history of mathematics is determined, first of all, not by specific results about equations of the third and fourth degree, but by the fact that general algebraic concepts (for example, the multiplicity of a root) and the statement (if the equation $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ has three real roots, then their sum is equal to - $a$) appear in it for the first time.

Ferrari and Tartaglia

It is not difficult to imagine what an impression the publication of “The Great Art” (1545) made on Tartaglia. In the last part of the book “Problems and Various Inventions” (1546), he publishes correspondence with Cardanov and records of conversations with him and attacks him with abuse and reproaches. Kardanov does not react to the attack.
