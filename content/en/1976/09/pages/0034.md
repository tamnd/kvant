The transmitter in Eindhoven operated for almost a whole year. The signal transmission mode changed several times. Radio echoes recorded in Oslo, having time delays ranging from 3 seconds to 3.5 minutes. On November 7, 1929, the Eindhoven experiment was terminated.

An interesting study of LDE was carried out by J. Gaulle and G. Talon in May 1929 during the work of the French expedition observing a solar eclipse in Indochina. A 500 bt transmitter installed on board the ship generated a sequence of signals at a wavelength of 25 m with an interval of 30 seconds. Long series of LDEs with variable time delays were recorded.

The observations of French research, like those of Vander Pol, Hals and Stermar, were not satisfactorily explained at that time. Indeed, the minimum delay equal to those seconds corresponds to a distance of 450,000 km from the Earth, that is, the reflecting matter should be located further outside the Earth’s atmosphere, somewhere in the region of the lunar orbit. Meanwhile, the power of the echo exceeded a third of the signal power, which did not correspond to the expected power when naturally reflected from an object located at the distance of the Moon or further. It is even more difficult to explain the change in echo delay. If it is associated with the movement of reflected matter in space, the speed of its movement should be too high. High. And it was already quite difficult to imagine how double and triple echoes arise (from the same signal). The mystery of the world echo remains unsolved.

In 1934, LDE was observed by the English researcher E. Appleton. Later, a greatly increased level of radio interference began to interfere with LDE registrations. In 1947-1949, K. Boudin and J. Yatis tried to study radio waves at a wave of 14.5 m and did not find it. The amazing phenomenon was gradually forgotten, although from time to time radio amateurs did observe echoes from their own transmissions (recording spoken speech or Morse signals repeated after a few seconds). As can be judged from these reports, the frequency regions in which LDE is observed range from 0.8 to 140 MHz.

In 1967, the study of LDE began at Stanford University (USA) under the leadership of Professor F. Crawford. The reality of the phenomenon was confirmed. True, at Stanford, unlike in the 20s, long echo sequences were not observed. Echoes with delays of approximately 2 and 8 seconds were especially common. It is found that the frequency of the echo is mixed by ten hertz compared to the frequency of the original signal, and the time interval between pulses in the echo signal is less than in the original one.

Firstly, the echoes observed in the 20s were free from time rolling and frequency (Doppler) distortions, which are caused by the Stoermer echo, which remained constant and independent of the delay time.
