$$\overline{L^2} = n(\Delta x)^2.$$

Let us denote by $t$ the duration of the experiment during which $n$ the first $\Delta x$, changed at intervals of time $\Delta t$, occurred. Then

$$n = \frac{t}{\Delta t}, \quad \overline{L^2} = \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{\Delta t} t.$$

Let us note first of all that, by participating in chaotic motion, a grain over time on average moves away from the first position by the value $L$, the square of which (and not the value $L$ itself) grows in proportion to time $t$.

We define the average displacement $L_0$ for time $t$ as $\sqrt{\overline{L^2}}$:

$$L_0 = \sqrt{\overline{L^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{(\Delta x)^2}{\Delta t}} \cdot \sqrt{t} = B \sqrt{t}.$$

The average displacement $\sqrt{\overline{L^2}}$ can have any direction in space with equal probability. We can say that a grain that was at point $A$ at the initial moment of time, after $t$ seconds will, on average, be somewhere near the surface of a ball with radius $R = B\sqrt{t}$. The radius of this ball does not grow in proportion to time $t$, but in proportion to the square root of $t$. The indicated value of the radius is determined the more accurately and by the increase, the more individual values ​​$\Delta x$ are included in the displacement $L$ or the more often the experiment is repeated to calculate the average value $\overline{L^2}$.

It is easy to see that the concept of the velocity of a particle as the ratio of the movement $L_0$ of a particle over a certain period of time to the value $t$ of this interval loses its meaning for chaotic motion. Really,

$$\frac{L_0}{t} = \frac{L_0}{\sqrt{t}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} = \frac{B}{\sqrt{t}}.$$

And only when $t$ becomes less than the time between two collisions with the molecules of the medium, the movement during this time can be considered persistent and the speed of this movement can be determined in a general way.

If we trace the entire intricate path of a particle and divide its total length by a period of time, then this is the relative speed $\overline{v}$ of the thermal motion of the particle, and the value $\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2}$ is the average kinetic energy of the molecules of the medium

(a is the radius of the grain, η is the coefficient that determines the fluidity of the medium).

The value of $\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2}$ calculated from equation (2), as the experiment shows, does not depend either on the properties of the medium, or on the choice of grains, or on other conditions of the experiment, as long as it is carried out at a constant temperature. By observing the Brownian motion of a particle at different temperatures, one can establish a direct connection between the average kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2}$ and the absolute temperature $T$:

$$\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2} = \frac{3}{2} kT,$$

where $k$ is a universal constant, the same for all substances and in all states. The constant $k$ is called Boltzmann's constant.
