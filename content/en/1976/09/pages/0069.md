World of sounds

The sound is meant to be heard. Is it possible to see him? It turns out that it is possible. This is discussed in sufficient detail in one of the chapters of G. Chadd “Sound” *).

Recently, devices have appeared that decompose any sounds, including human sounds, into their constituent elements and then register them in some way (on a photometer, on the screen of an electron tube, etc.). The result is a sound spectrograph, a kind of model of a person with a sound voice.

But what is the use of a model when modern sound recording technology makes it possible to preserve the voice of any person for many years and reproduce it at will? And yet, we cannot do without modeling if we want to learn how to analyze a person’s sound, and then synthesize it. This is necessary, for example, in order to acquire an electronic computer for human language,

which has already become a familiar attribute of our life.

Modern computers can do many things, but they cannot talk. They have to write tasks in a special code and enter them into machines using a keyboard, punch cards or magnetic tapes; They also provide the results in written form. And how much easier, more convenient and more effective it would be to talk to a machine in normal human language!

Something in this direction has already been done. Devices have appeared that can “recognize” a small number of individual monosyllabic words spoken by one person. Look how many restrictions there are: words must be monosyllabic, separated by phrases; the voice must belong to a certain person, and, most importantly, there are only a few words. In 1950, one of the first such devices was manufactured in the USA; it “recognized” with almost perfect accuracy the ten numbers that the “owners” told it over the phone. However, when all this was done in compliance with the same rules of another person, the speech continued to be improved, but the problems were still far from being solved.

The range of frequencies perceived by our ear (from approximately 20 hertz to 20 kilohertz) does not cover the entire range of acoustic colabia. Above this chaff lies the area of ​​ultrasound, below - infrasound. A lot of interesting things are said about “unsatisfactory” sounds in G. Chadd’s book.

Circus lovers - both adults and especially children - have more than once been delighted by mathematician dogs who can count, add numbers and perform other harp-metic operations. I think I won’t be discouraged from visiting the circus if one secret is revealed (however, this is no longer a secret): it is not so much the dog that has mathematical abilities, but its trainer. He gives the dog a command using an ultrasonic whistle hidden in his pocket, the sound of which lies outside the range of susceptibility of a human sound whistle, which is why it is absent.

The area of ​​application of the first ultrasonic whistle, depicted by the English Galton almost a hundred years ago, in 1883, turned out to be so unusual. Nowadays, ultrasound is used in a wide variety of branches of science and technology, as well as in medicine. Let's give just a few examples.

Ultrasound is an excellent and trouble-free dishwasher, and it not only removes fruits and slippers, but also removes technical debris from the minor parts of a watch mechanism, “refreshes” ancient coins, prepares surgical instruments for surgery, and cleans scales from foreign engines.
