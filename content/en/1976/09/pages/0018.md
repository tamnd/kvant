put $a + bi$ in the form
$$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \left( \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} + \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} i \right).$$

Then we find $\alpha$ such that
$$\cos \alpha = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}$$ and $$\sin \alpha = \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}$$ (this can be done because
$$\left( \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} \right)^2 = 1).$$
The desired cube root will be written as
$$\sqrt[6]{a^2 + b^2} \left( \cos \frac{\alpha}{3} + i \sin \frac{\alpha}{3} \right).$$

(Note that $\alpha$ is defined up to the term $2k\pi$, $\frac{\alpha}{3}$ is defined up to the term $\frac{2k\pi}{3}$, and $$\cos \frac{\alpha}{3} + i \sin \frac{\alpha}{3}$$ has three values ​​- as it should be!)
Using this method, you can approximately find the cube roots included in formula (3). However, we only need to find the active part of one of them: if one of the two roots is different $c + di$, then the other is equal to $c - di$ (prove it!) and their sum is $2c$.

But please, this method of solving a cubic equation using a table of square and cubic roots and trigonometric tables is even more cumbersome and inaccurate (after all, all table values ​​are determined only with some accuracy, and with each arithmetic operation the error increases) than the method of selection and successive approximations, which are completely conventional.

For some reason, we do not remember formula (3): it is not suitable for the practical solution of cubic equations.

7. Conclusion: why is formula (3) needed?

The significance of formula (3) is that it gives an answer to the classical question about “the solvability of equations of the third degree in radicals.” Let's explain this.

The first irrational numbers we have to deal with are the roots (the very first $-\sqrt{2}$, the diagonal of the square on the side one). Extracting roots, together with arithmetic operations, expands the stock of numbers (by adding numbers such as $\sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{\sqrt{5}/(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})}$, etc.) to rational numbers. Is this expanded reserve sufficient to solve algebraic equations with integer coefficients? Our formula shows that it is enough to solve cubic equations - in any case, if we allow not only real, but also complex numbers as radical expressions.

It turns out that equations of the fourth degree can also be solved in radicals; equations of direct degree and higher are unsolvable in radicals. It is very likely that the reader has already heard about this last theorem. Its proof is actually much simpler than is commonly believed; but this is a subject for another discussion.
