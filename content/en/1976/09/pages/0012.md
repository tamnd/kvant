⟦folio 10⟧

The further fate of the heroes

A year before Tartaglia’s death (he died in 1557), his “General Treatise on Number and Measure” began to be published, containing the results he had obtained in those years on combinatorics and probability theory. The Treatise says very little about cubic equations. The Treatise finished publishing after the author’s death.

After the dispute, Ferrari gained great fame: he gives public lectures in Rome, heads personnel management in Milan, and participates in the education of the king’s son. But he left no further traces in science! He died at 43 years old (1565).

Kardanov lived longer than them. But his life was not easy. One of his sons poisoned his wife and was executed. Another son became a bully and a limited father. In 1570, Kardanov himself was imprisoned, and his property was confiscated. (The reason for his arrest is unknown. It is only known that in anticipation of his arrest he destroyed 120 of his books.) Cardanov ended his days in Rome, in the position of a “private man” (his expression), better than a modest pension from the Pope. He dedicated the end of his life to the autobiographical book “About My Life.” The last fact mentioned in this book dates back to April 28, 1576. Kardanov died on September 21, 1576.

In his book, Kardanov recalls Tartaglia several times. He writes that Tartaglia preferred to have in him “a rival and a winner, rather than a friend and a person obliged to thank him.” Elsewhere he lists Tartaglia as one of his books that “do not go beyond the bounds of grammar.” However, on the last pages he writes: “I confess that in mathematics I borrowed something, but in fact an insignificant amount, from Brother Niccolo.”

Apparently he was restless in his soul!

* * *

The history of mathematics returned to the Tartaglia-Kardanov problem only at the beginning of the 19th century, having discovered the existence of a common Tartaglia, which by that time had been practically forgotten. The almost forgotten story became public, and not only professionals, but also amateurs were ready to suffer at Tartaglia’s expense. With repeated transmissions of history and its penetration into popular literature, Kardanov sometimes turned into an adventurer and a villain, who stole his open ones from Tartaglia and gave this discovery his name.

By the end of the 19th century, part of the discussion began to take on the character of service-based historical-mathematical research. Mathematicians realized what a big role the work of the Cardans played in 16th-century science. It became clear what Leibniz had noted earlier: “Kardanov was a Great man with all his shortcomings; without them he would be perfect.”

The greatest historian of mathematics, Moritz Cantor (1829-1920; not on the same page as the creator of the multivest theory, Georg Cantor!), expressed the assumption, already found in Ferrari, that Tartaglia did not rediscover the correct Ferro division, but learned it in its finished form at second hand.

For a century and a half, passions either subsided or flared up again. But maybe this is one of those questions to which it is no longer possible to give a definite answer?

And behind the formula for solving the cubic equation the name “Cardan’s formula” has firmly taken root.
