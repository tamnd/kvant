Cardano played chess for forty years (“I could never express in brief words how much damage, without any compensation for it, they caused my household affairs”), twenty-five played dice (“but chess damaged my bones even more”). For the sake of playing, he sometimes abandoned all his studies. A by-product of this passion was Cardano's Book of Dice, written in 1526 but not published until 1663. This book examines some issues in probability theory and combinatorics; it contains observations on the psychological players.

Cardano and Tartaglia

By 1539, Cardano completed his first book, entirely devoted to mathematics, “The Practice of General Argumetics.” According to his plan, it was supposed to replace Pacioli's book. Hearing about Tartaglia's secret, he was eager to decorate his book with it.

In January 1539, Cardano turned to Tartaglia with a request to give him the rule for solving equation (1) either for publication in his book, or under a promise to keep what was communicated secret. Tartaglia opens: “Tell his lordship that he will forgive me, but if I want to publish my discovery, I will do it in my own work, and not in the book of another.” On February 12, Cardano repeats his request. Tartaglia is relentless. On March 13, Cardano invites Tartaglia to Milan, promising to introduce him to the governor of Lombardy. Apparently this prospect presented Tartaglia: he accepts the invitation. On March 25, a decisive conversation took place at Cardano’s house.

Here is an excerpt from the recording of this conversation (it should be borne in mind that the recording was made by Tartali; a student of Cardano Ferrari claims that it is not entirely true):

“Niccolo. I tell you: I refused you not because of this chapter alone and the discovery made in it, but because of those things that can be discovered by knowing it, since this is the key that unlocks the path for the study of a large number of other sections. I would have long ago found a general rule for many other problems if I were not currently busy translating Euclid into the vernacular (I have currently completed the translation to the end of the 13th book). But when this work, which I have already begun, is completed, I am going to publish a work for practical application along with a new algebra... If I give it to some theorist (like Your Lordship), then he can easily, with the help of this explanation, find other chapters (and this explanation can easily be applied to other questions) and publish the fruits of my discovery under his own name. These will ruin all my plans.

Messer Gerolamo. I swear to you by the binding of the Lord God and not only give you the word of a pure man that I will never publish this discovery of yours if you entrust it to me, but I promise, and may my fullness of true Christian be your guarantee, to protect it so that after my death no one will be able to read what is written. If, in your opinion, I am trustworthy, then do it; if not, then let’s leave this conversation.

Niccolo. If I didn’t believe this claim of yours, then, of course, I would deserve to be considered an unbeliever myself.”

So Tartaglia allowed himself to be persuaded. From the above recording it is difficult to understand what made him change his decision. Did he need to be so shocked by the curse of Cardano? Having told the secret, Tartaglia immediately left Milan, refusing to testify with the governor, for whose sake he had undertaken this journey. Had Cardano hypnotized him?

When Tartaglia received on May 12 the freshly printed “Practice of General Argumetics” without his “lattice” (he reported his method of solving equation (1) in the form of a Latin poem: they did not know how to write formulas then), he calmed down somewhat.

Cardano received from Tartaglia a ready-made method for solving equation (1) without any hint of proof. He put a lot of effort into carefully testing and justifying the rule.
