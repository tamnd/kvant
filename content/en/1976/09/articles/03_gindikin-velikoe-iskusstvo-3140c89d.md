S. Gindikin

"Great Art"

This article is devoted to the history of the main achievement of mathematics of the 16th century - the solution of algebraic equations of the third and fourth degree. Mathematics lovers are still attracted by the dramatic events associated with this discovery, in which the fates of four scientists are intertwined - del Ferro, Tartaglia, Cardano and Ferrari. 1976 marks the 475th anniversary of the birth of Cardano, the 400th anniversary of his death and the 450th anniversary of the death of del Ferro.

In our discussion about these mathematicians and their discoveries, there are some things left unclear: there are many things we do not know about the events of four years ago.

The title of the article is part of the title of Cardano's book, Published in 1545. Felix Klein wrote about it: “This extremely valuable work contains the germ of modern algebra, going beyond the boundaries of ancient mathematics.”

The 16th century was the century of the revival of European mathematics after medieval hibernation. At first, European scientists tried to understand what was done by their ancient and eastern (Indian and Arab) predecessors. The first achievements of the mathematicians of the 16th century related to algebra. (This is due to the fact that in the 16th century algebra was just emerging, while geometry was an established science.)

The state of algebra at the turn of the 16th and 16th centuries was prepared in one of the first printed books on mathematics, “The Summa of Knowledge of Arithmetic, Geometry, Relations and Proportionality,” written in Venice in 1494. The book was written in the Inalyan language. It was one of the first scientific books not written in Latin. Its author is the monk Luca Pacioli, a friend of the great Leonardo da Vinci. At the end of the book, Pacioli writes: for solving cubic equations, “the art of algebra has not yet provided a method, just as the method of squaring a circle has not been provided.” These words were apparently taken as a statement about the impossibility of a formula for solving cubic equations.

Scippon del Ferro

There was a person who didn’t stop Pacioli for me. It was the professor of mathematics in Bologna, Scippo della Ferro (1465-1526), ​​who managed to find a way to solve the equation

$$x^3 + ax = b.$$

(1)

Since negative numbers were not yet used at that time, the letter coefficients in equation (1) (and later in this article) are assumed to be positive. Therefore, equations (1) and

$$x^3 = ax + b$$

(2)

were perceived as completely different equations! The presentation of the Ferro case itself has not reached us. Ferro communicated this to his successor in the department, Annibal della Nava, and to his student Antonio Mari Fiore. After his death, Master Fiore decided to use the secret entrusted to him to become invincible in solving problems. At the end of 1534, he summoned the mathematician from Venice Niccolò Tartaglia.

Niccolo Tartaglia

The prospect of serving Fiore's friends with a ceremonial dinner equal to the number of problems solved by the winner (that was the rule!) did not appeal to Tartaglia. He applies the conditions, and eight days before the appointed time (the deadline expired on February 12, 1535), happiness smiles on him: the desired way to find Tartaglia’s problems (strangely, he did not even cope with the problem, which could have been solved by the method of Ferro’s case).

Tartaglia soon found a way to solve equation (2).

They knew a lot about the Fiore-Tartaglia dish and the Tartaglia dinner. They turn to him with requests to reveal his secret. He opens, but there is a petitioner - Gerolamo Cardano, who persuades him.

Gerolamo Cardano
Cardano was born on September 24, 1501 in Pavni, into the family of a lawyer. After graduating from university, Gerolamo decides to devote himself to medicine. Since he was an illegal child, he initially had to practice for a long time in the provinces. Only in August 1539, having specially changed the rules for this purpose, Cardano was admitted to the college of doctors of Milan. Later he even became the rector of this college. Cardano was one of the most famous doctors of his time; probably second - after his friend Bezalia.

In his declining years, Cardano wrote an autobiography, “About My Life.” In it, he only mentions his studies in mathematics a few times and talks in detail about his research in medicine. Cardano claims that he described up to five thousand more teaching techniques, that the number of problems he solved reached up to forty thousand, and the number of small instructions reached two hundred thousand. Of course, these numbers should be taken with a grain of salt. Yet the words of Cardano the doctor were undeniable. Cardano claims that he suffered only three misfortunes in his medical practice.

However, medical studies did not completely absorb Cardano. In his free time from medicine, he studied “everything in the world”: philosophy, astrology, physics, mechanics, mathematics.

Cardano compiled horoscopes of both the living and the dead (Christ, Petraki, Dürer, Bezalia, Luther). The Pope used the services of a Cardan astrologer. (According to one unkind legend, Cardano committed suicide to confirm his own horoscope.)

Cardano's book On Thin Materials (1550) was translated into French and served as a popular textbook on statics and hydrostatics throughout the 17th century. Galileo, when observing the swing of the natural matrix - the chandelier in the cathedral - used Cardano's instructions to use his own pulse to measure time. Cardano wrote about the need for a perpetual motion machine. Some of his remarks can be interpreted as a principle of possible movements. Cardano experimentally established the relationship between the densities of air and water. For the royal carriage, he invented a system for connecting two currencies, which below is called cardan (gimbal, cardan shaft, cardan joint) and is widely used in automation. (However, it is fair to note that the idea of ​​such a system dates back to antiquity; in addition, on one of Leonardo da Vinci’s figure a compass with a gimbal is chosen.)

Some of Cardano's works were encyclopedias of sorts. During the Renaissance, encyclopedias were written by single people. The first encyclopedias created by collective work appeared only a century and a half later.

Cardano wrote a huge number of books (some of them were published, some remained in manuscripts, some he destroyed, awaiting arrest (see below)). However, their description alone made up an entire book, “On My Own Writings.” For many years his books on philosophy and ethics were received. His book On Assertion was translated into English and influenced Shakespeare. Some six-story scholars even claim that Hamlet utters his

Cardano played chess for forty years (“I could never express in brief words how much damage, without any compensation for it, they caused my household affairs”), twenty-five played dice (“but chess damaged my bones even more”). For the sake of playing, he sometimes abandoned all his studies. A by-product of this passion was Cardano's Book of Dice, written in 1526 but not published until 1663. This book examines some issues in probability theory and combinatorics; it contains observations on the psychological players.

Cardano and Tartaglia

By 1539, Cardano completed his first book, entirely devoted to mathematics, “The Practice of General Argumetics.” According to his plan, it was supposed to replace Pacioli's book. Hearing about Tartaglia's secret, he was eager to decorate his book with it.

In January 1539, Cardano turned to Tartaglia with a request to give him the rule for solving equation (1) either for publication in his book, or under a promise to keep what was communicated secret. Tartaglia opens: “Tell his lordship that he will forgive me, but if I want to publish my discovery, I will do it in my own work, and not in the book of another.” On February 12, Cardano repeats his request. Tartaglia is relentless. On March 13, Cardano invites Tartaglia to Milan, promising to introduce him to the governor of Lombardy. Apparently this prospect presented Tartaglia: he accepts the invitation. On March 25, a decisive conversation took place at Cardano’s house.

Here is an excerpt from the recording of this conversation (it should be borne in mind that the recording was made by Tartali; a student of Cardano Ferrari claims that it is not entirely true):

“Niccolo. I tell you: I refused you not because of this chapter alone and the discovery made in it, but because of those things that can be discovered by knowing it, since this is the key that unlocks the path for the study of a large number of other sections. I would have long ago found a general rule for many other problems if I were not currently busy translating Euclid into the vernacular (I have currently completed the translation to the end of the 13th book). But when this work, which I have already begun, is completed, I am going to publish a work for practical application along with a new algebra... If I give it to some theorist (like Your Lordship), then he can easily, with the help of this explanation, find other chapters (and this explanation can easily be applied to other questions) and publish the fruits of my discovery under his own name. These will ruin all my plans.

Messer Gerolamo. I swear to you by the binding of the Lord God and not only give you the word of a pure man that I will never publish this discovery of yours if you entrust it to me, but I promise, and may my fullness of true Christian be your guarantee, to protect it so that after my death no one will be able to read what is written. If, in your opinion, I am trustworthy, then do it; if not, then let’s leave this conversation.

Niccolo. If I didn’t believe this claim of yours, then, of course, I would deserve to be considered an unbeliever myself.”

So Tartaglia allowed himself to be persuaded. From the above recording it is difficult to understand what made him change his decision. Did he need to be so shocked by the curse of Cardano? Having told the secret, Tartaglia immediately left Milan, refusing to testify with the governor, for whose sake he had undertaken this journey. Had Cardano hypnotized him?

When Tartaglia received on May 12 the freshly printed “Practice of General Argumetics” without his “lattice” (he reported his method of solving equation (1) in the form of a Latin poem: they did not know how to write formulas then), he calmed down somewhat.

Cardano received from Tartaglia a ready-made method for solving equation (1) without any hint of proof. He put a lot of effort into carefully testing and justifying the rule. Luigi Ferrari
For some time now, Cardano was helped in his mathematical studies by the young Luigi Ferrari (1522-1565). In Cardano's list of his 14 students, Ferrari appears as one of the three most prominent.

In 1543, Cardano and Ferrari traveled to Bologna, where della Nave allowed them to get acquainted with the papers of the late Ferro. There they became convinced that the latter already knew Tartaglia's rule.

Apparently, contemporaries knew almost nothing about the formula of the Ferro case. It is unlikely that Cardano would have attacked Tartaglia so seriously if he had known that he could get the same information from della Nave.

Now almost all histories of mathematics agree that Ferro came up with the formula, that Fiore knew it, and that Tartaglia rediscovered it, knowing that Fiore had it. However, none of these facts has been strictly proven!

At the end of his life, Tartaglia wrote: “... I can assure that this theorem described has not yet been proven either by Euclid or anyone else, but only by Gerolamo Cardano, who we showed it... I in 1534 (elsewhere it is written that this happened on February 4, 1535 - S.G.) found in Venice the general formula for the equation...”

It's hard to make ends meet in this complicated story.

By 1545, Cardano had learned to solve not only equations (1) and (2), but also the equation
$$x^3 + b = ax,$$
(3)
as well as a "complete" cubic equation, i.e. an equation containing a term with $x^2$. By the same time, Ferrari had figured out how to solve a quadruple equation.

"Great Art"
Whether familiarity with Ferro’s case papers, strong pressure from Ferrari, or, most likely, reluctance to bury the results of many years of work led to the fact that

Cardano included everything he knew about cubic equations in his 1545 book “The Great Art or the Rules of Algebra.”

In the preface, Cardano sets out the history of the issue: “... in our time, Scippo del Ferro discovered a formula according to which the cube of an unknown plane is equal to a number. It was very beautiful and wonderful work. Since this art surpasses all human fortitude and all the clarity of mortal mind, it must be considered as a gift of heavenly origin, as well as an ability of mental power, and it is such a weak discovery that one who could achieve it can be expected to achieve everything. Competing with him, Nicollo Tartaglia from Brescia, our friend, having been called to compose with a student of del Ferro, named Antono Mario Fiore, decided, so as not to be defeated, the same problem, and after much time he woke it up to me. I was led into delusion by the words of Luca Pacioli, who says that there is no general solution to this kind of equation, and although I already had many, many discoveries made by myself, I still did not despair of finding what I did not dare to look for. However, when I received this chapter and added its solutions, I saw that there was a lot more that could be done with it; and already with increased confidence in my affairs, I, with use, discovered further, partly myself, partly with Lodovico Ferrari, my former student.”

In a modernized form, the way Cardano finds a solution to equation (1) can be stated as follows. We will look for a solution to equation (1) in the form $x = \beta - \alpha$. Then $x + \alpha = \beta$ and

$$x^3 + 3x^2\alpha + 3x\alpha^2 + \alpha^3 = \beta^3.$$ (4)

Since $3x^2\alpha + 3x\alpha^2 = 3x\alpha(x + \alpha) = 3x\alpha\beta$, equality (4) can be rewritten as

$$x^3 + 3\alpha\beta x = \beta^3 - \alpha^3.$$ (5)

Let's try one by one $\langle a, b \rangle$ so pick a pair $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle$so that (5) coincides with (1). For this it is necessary that the couple $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle$ was the system's solution

$$\begin{cases} 3\alpha\beta = a, \\ \beta^3 - \alpha^3 = b \end{cases}$$

or equivalent systems

$$\begin{cases} \beta^3 \cdot (-\alpha^3) = -\frac{a^3}{27}, \\ \beta^3 + (-\alpha^3) = b. \end{cases}$$

By Vieta's theorem, $^*)$ $\beta^3$ and $-\alpha^3$ will be the roots of the intersecting quadratic equation $y^2 - by - \frac{a^3}{27} = 0$. Since we are looking for positive roots of equation (1), $\beta > \alpha$. So, $\beta^3 = \frac{b}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{b^2}{4} + \frac{a^3}{27}}$ and $-\alpha^3 = \frac{b}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{b^2}{4} + \frac{a^3}{27}}$. Hence,

$$x = \sqrt[3]{\frac{b}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{b^2}{4} + \frac{a^3}{27}}} - \sqrt[3]{\frac{b}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{b^2}{4} + \frac{a^3}{27}}}.$$

For positive $a$ and $b$, the root $x$ is also positive.

The above calculation only in ideological terms follows the flow of Cardano’s reasoning. He himself argues in geometric language: if the cube from the side $\beta = \alpha + x$ is cut by planes parallel to the faces into a cube from the side $\alpha$ and a cube from the side $x$, it turns out, in addition to two cubes, with rectangular parallelipeds from the side $\langle \alpha, \alpha, x \rangle$ and three from the side $\langle \alpha, x, x \rangle$; respectively, between the volumes gives (4); To go to (5), parallelipeds of different types are combined in pairs.

“Since I realized that the department that Tartaglia gave me was discovered by him with the help of geometric proof, I thought that this was the royal path leading to all other departments.”

$^*)$ Viète himself (1540–1603) lived later than Cardano, but that particular case of his theorem, which in school is called Viète’s theorem, was essentially known to Cardano.

Equation (2) can be solved using the substitution $x = \beta + \alpha$, but here a case may already arise when the original equation has three real roots, and the auxiliary quadratic equation has no real roots. This is the so-called “irreducible” case*). He caused a lot of trouble for the Cardans (and probably Tartaglia).

Kardanov solved equation (3), giving a very bold alienation for those times, burdening the negativity of the root. No one before him had used negative numbers so decisively, although Kardanov is still far from freely handling them: he considers equations (1) and (2) separately!

Kardanov also completely figured out the general cubic equation $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ (Tartaglia has nothing to do with this!), then, speaking in modern language, the substitution $x = y - \frac{a}{3}$ destroys the term with $x^2$.

Kardanov decides to consider not only negative numbers (he calls them “purely false”), but also complex ones (he calls them “posistibly sophistical”). He notes that if you operate with them according to some natural rules, then complex roots can be assigned to a quadratic equation that does not have real roots. Possibly to complex Cardan numbers at SEL in connection with the “irreducible” case.

"Great Art" also featured Ferrari's personal contribution - solving a fourth-degree equation.

In modern language, the Ferrari method for the equation
$$x^4 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$
(the complete equation of the fourth degree easily coincides with equation (6)) is as follows.

By introducing the auxiliary parameter $t$, we rewrite equation (6) in an equivalent form:
$$\left(x^2 + \frac{a}{2} + t\right)^2 = 2tx^2 - bx + \left(t^2 + at - c + \frac{a^2}{4}\right).$$

Let us now select the value of the parameter $t$ so that the square (relative to $x$) trilen on the right side of equation (7) has two coinciding roots. To do this, it is necessary that the discriminant of this triplen be equal to zero:
$$b^2 - 4 \cdot 2t \cdot \left(t^2 + at - c + \frac{a^2}{4}\right) = 0.$$

We have obtained an auxiliary cubic equation for $t$. Let us find some of its roots $t_0$ using the Cardanov formula. Equation (7) can now be rewritten as:
$$\left(x^2 + \frac{a}{2} + t_0\right)^2 = 2t_0 \left(x - \frac{b}{4t_0}\right)^2.$$

Equation (8) breaks down into a pair of quadratic equations that give the four roots we are looking for.

Thus, according to the Ferrari method, the solution to a fourth-degree equation will coincide with the solution to the auxiliary cubic equation and two quadratic equations.

The place of the “Great Art” in the history of mathematics is determined, first of all, not by specific results about equations of the third and fourth degree, but by the fact that general algebraic concepts (for example, the multiplicity of a root) and the statement (if the equation $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ has three real roots, then their sum is equal to - $a$) appear in it for the first time.

Ferrari and Tartaglia

It is not difficult to imagine what an impression the publication of “The Great Art” (1545) made on Tartaglia. In the last part of the book “Problems and Various Inventions” (1546), he publishes correspondence with Cardanov and records of conversations with him and attacks him with abuse and reproaches. Kardanov does not react to the attack.

February 10, 1547 Tartaglia, together with Cardano, responds to Ferrari. He objects to Tartaglia’s reproaches, points out shortcomings in his book, and in one case reproaches him for appropriating someone else’s results and for having a bad memory (it seems that at that time this was a difficult accusation). In conclusion, Tartaglia is called to a public debate “on geometry, arithmetic or related disciplines, such as Astrology, Music, Cosmography, Perspective, Architecture, etc.”

On February 19, Tartaglia, in his response, tries to get into a squabble with Cardano himself: “I wrote there in such a heated and insulting tone in order to force his lordship (and not you) to write something of his own, or I have old scores with him.”

The discussion of the terms of the dispute is dragging on. Tartaglia begins to understand that Cardano will remain in the

rone. Then he begins to emphasize Ferrari’s lack of independence, namely his “creature of Cardano,” as he called himself in his challenge. His “Questions”, which he traditionally sent in response to a challenge, he addresses to both of them: “You, Messer Gerolamo, and you, Messer Luigi...”

Eventually Tartaglia agreed to a DISPUT with Ferrari. DISPUT took place on August 10, 1548 in Milan, in the presence of many famous persons, but in the absence of Cardano. ODISPUTE only short records of Tartaglia have been preserved, from which it is almost impossible to reconstruct the true picture. It looks like Tartaglia has lost order.

The further fate of the heroes

A year before Tartaglia’s death (he died in 1557), his “General Treatise on Number and Measure” began to be published, containing the results he had obtained in those years on combinatorics and probability theory. The Treatise says very little about cubic equations. The Treatise finished publishing after the author’s death.

After the dispute, Ferrari gained great fame: he gives public lectures in Rome, heads personnel management in Milan, and participates in the education of the king’s son. But he left no further traces in science! He died at 43 years old (1565).

Kardanov lived longer than them. But his life was not easy. One of his sons poisoned his wife and was executed. Another son became a bully and a limited father. In 1570, Kardanov himself was imprisoned, and his property was confiscated. (The reason for his arrest is unknown. It is only known that in anticipation of his arrest he destroyed 120 of his books.) Cardanov ended his days in Rome, in the position of a “private man” (his expression), better than a modest pension from the Pope. He dedicated the end of his life to the autobiographical book “About My Life.” The last fact mentioned in this book dates back to April 28, 1576. Kardanov died on September 21, 1576.

In his book, Kardanov recalls Tartaglia several times. He writes that Tartaglia preferred to have in him “a rival and a winner, rather than a friend and a person obliged to thank him.” Elsewhere he lists Tartaglia as one of his books that “do not go beyond the bounds of grammar.” However, on the last pages he writes: “I confess that in mathematics I borrowed something, but in fact an insignificant amount, from Brother Niccolo.”

Apparently he was restless in his soul!

* * *

The history of mathematics returned to the Tartaglia-Kardanov problem only at the beginning of the 19th century, having discovered the existence of a common Tartaglia, which by that time had been practically forgotten. The almost forgotten story became public, and not only professionals, but also amateurs were ready to suffer at Tartaglia’s expense. With repeated transmissions of history and its penetration into popular literature, Kardanov sometimes turned into an adventurer and a villain, who stole his open ones from Tartaglia and gave this discovery his name.

By the end of the 19th century, part of the discussion began to take on the character of service-based historical-mathematical research. Mathematicians realized what a big role the work of the Cardans played in 16th-century science. It became clear what Leibniz had noted earlier: “Kardanov was a Great man with all his shortcomings; without them he would be perfect.”

The greatest historian of mathematics, Moritz Cantor (1829-1920; not on the same page as the creator of the multivest theory, Georg Cantor!), expressed the assumption, already found in Ferrari, that Tartaglia did not rediscover the correct Ferro division, but learned it in its finished form at second hand.

For a century and a half, passions either subsided or flared up again. But maybe this is one of those questions to which it is no longer possible to give a definite answer?

And behind the formula for solving the cubic equation the name “Cardan’s formula” has firmly taken root.
