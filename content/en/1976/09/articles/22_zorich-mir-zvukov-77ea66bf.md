## World of sounds

The sound is meant to be heard. Is it possible to see him? It turns out that it is possible. This is described in sufficient detail in one of the chapters of G. Chadd’s book “Sound”.

Recently, devices have appeared that decompose any sounds, including human speech, into their component elements and then record them in some way (on photographic paper, on the screen of a cathode ray tube, etc.). The result is a sound spectrogram, a kind of model of the human voice.

But why use a model when modern recording technology makes it possible to preserve the voice of any person for many years and reproduce it at will? And yet, we cannot do without modeling if we want to learn how to analyze human speech and then synthesize it. This is necessary, for example, in order to introduce an electronic computer, which has already become a familiar attribute of our life, to the human language.

Modern computers can do many things, but they cannot talk. They have to write tasks in a special code and enter them into machines using a keyboard, punched cards or magnetic tapes; They also provide the results in written form. And how much simpler, more convenient and effective it would be to talk to a machine in normal human language!

Something in this direction has already been done. Devices have appeared that can “recognize” a small number of individual monosyllabic words spoken by one person. Look how many restrictions there are: words must be monosyllabic, separated by pauses; the voice must belong to a certain person, and, most importantly, there are only a few words. In 1950, one of the first such devices was manufactured in the USA; it “recognized” ten numbers that the “owner” told him over the phone with almost one hundred percent accuracy. However, when another person did all this in compliance with the same rules, the accuracy of the device dropped to 50%. Of course, over the past quarter century, speech recognition machines have continued to improve, but the problem is still far from being solved.

The frequency band perceived by our ear (from approximately 20 hertz to 20 kilohertz) does not cover the entire range of acoustic vibrations. Above this band lies the region of ultrasound, below - infrasound. A lot of interesting things are said about “inaudible” sounds in G. Chadd’s book.

Circus lovers - both adults and especially children - have often been amazed by mathematician dogs who can count, add numbers and perform other arithmetic operations. I think I won’t be discouraged from visiting the circus if I reveal one secret (however, this is no longer a secret): it is not so much the dog that has mathematical abilities, but its trainer. He gives the dog a command using an ultrasonic whistle hidden in his pocket, the sound of which is beyond the range of sensitivity of the human ear, but the dogs can clearly hear it. The area of ​​application of the first ultrasonic whistle, invented by the Englishman Galton almost a hundred years ago, in 1883, turned out to be so unusual. Nowadays, ultrasound is used in a wide variety of branches of science and technology, as well as in medicine. Let's give just a few examples.

Ultrasound is an excellent and trouble-free dishwasher, and it not only washes fruits and plates, but also removes technological dirt from miniature parts of a watch mechanism, “refreshes” ancient coins, prepares surgical instruments for surgery, and cleans aircraft engines of scale.

Until recently, they practically did not know how to solder aluminum parts. As you know, in air, aluminum becomes covered with a film of oxide, and no fluxes could dissolve it until they used... ultrasound, which can tear off its armor from aluminum. Like soldering, ultrasonic welding has also become indispensable in electronics, with the help of which dissimilar materials such as germanium and copper are reliably joined.

Infrasound is also of great interest for science and practice. They began to study it during the First World War, but then forgot about it for half a century. Only in the last decade has there been a renewed interest in infrasound.

Infrasound, as a rule, is an accompaniment to ordinary noise; it occurs during the operation of aircraft and ship engines, when cars move at high speeds, in metallurgical and other noisy workshops. It also occurs in nature, accompanying volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, severe storms and tornadoes. It is probably infrasound waves that warn animals about the impending danger of a natural disaster. It turns out that under the influence of large doses of infrasound, people feel pain in the ears, experience nausea, lose their ability to work, their vestibular apparatus is disrupted, and according to some data, even a disorder of mental abilities and psyche occurs.

The book “Sound” touches on another problem that cannot be ignored. The last two chapters of the book are filled with anxiety, beginning with the words:

“Ever since the technological revolution gave humanity new sources of energy of unprecedented power, people began to somehow treat their planet in an uneconomical way. The “costs” of technical progress can be seen at every step: ...dumps of rusting cars; rivers and lakes dying from industrial waste; and even the air we breathe is polluted by the smoke coming out of thousands of chimneys of factories and factories, and the exhaust gases of millions of cars... However, there is an even more sinister companion to the development of industry, which is more dangerous for people than polluting waste - this is noise.”

Noise affects a person, his performance, mood, and psyche. Now attempts are being made to systematize noise from the point of view of their impact on humans, different scales are being invented, empirical coefficients are being introduced, noise analyzers are being developed, etc. But even without mathematical formulas, it is clear that any noise must be actively combated, or better yet, not creating noise if possible. Here is a vast field for joint research by physicists, geophysicists, meteorologists, doctors, psychologists

All this is described in the book by G. Chadd. About this and much more, so much that you wonder how the author managed to fit such a huge amount of information into two hundred pages of a small format? Physical characteristics of sound and psychophysiological aspects of its perception, the architecture of concert halls and cities, stereo recording techniques and electromusical instruments, sonar and acoustic holography... Can you list everything? The presentation is brief, but by no means summary: specific devices are described, interesting facts and examples are given, and there are numerous photographs. The book is both interesting and useful to anyone who wants to get an idea of ​​the problems of modern acoustics.
