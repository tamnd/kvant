Brownian molecular motion
A. Noffe

At the very beginning of the school physics course, in the sixth grade, students are introduced to two interesting physical phenomena - Brownian motion and diffusion, which are directly related to the movement of molecules. These phenomena serve as the experimental basis of the molecular-kinetic theory. The theory of these phenomena is not studied at school. Therefore, even such a very important question remains unclear - why can molecules mix over significant distances during the process of diffusion? After all, they move completely randomly, all directions of movement are equal for them, and it would seem that over large periods of time they should actually remain in almost the same place.

The theory of Brownian motion was created by Albert Einstein. It is contained in his work “On the motion of particles suspended in a fluid at rest, required by the molecular-kinetic theory of heat,” published in 1905. Experimental studies carried out soon by the French physicist Jean Perrin completely confirmed this theory, and with it our ideas about the molecular structure of matter.

We present here a presentation of the fundamentals of the theory of Brownian motion, borrowed from the popular science book by academician Abram Fedorovich Ioffe “Basic Concepts of Perfect Physics,” published by the State Publishing House of Physics and Mathematics Literature in 1949.

There is direct evidence of the continuous thermal motion of molecules - the so-called Brownian molecular motion. Back in 1827, English Brown noticed in plant preparations under the microsomes the constant movement of small yeast particles suspended in the liquid. It subsequently turned out that this property is not limited to living nature, that any sufficiently small grain placed in an environment where it can move undergoes constant movements of the most random nature. The study of this phenomenon showed that it cannot be due to secondary causes, for example, flows that arise in a liquid or gaseous medium under the influence of uneven

It's easy to see why microsoptic grains were needed to detect thermal motion. Further, in a relatively rarefied substance, in a gas, under normal conditions there will be 2.7 1019 molecules in each cubic centimeter; all these molecules move at enormous speeds in all possible directions. Therefore, the number of shocks experienced by a body of the same size is so great that we not only do not have time to notice an individual shock, but also do not notice the random predominance of shocks in one direction over shocks in the opposite direction. The smaller the body, the fewer shocks it experiences, and for microsoptic dust grains the number of shocks it experiences is no longer so great that the predominance of shocks from one or the other random direction is constant, and the dust grain is thrown from side to side. Such a speck of dust will contain millions more molecules, so its random movement that we see is not molecular movement itself; we do not notice only a single molecule, but only the predominance of shocks in some direction; but this speck of dust is a sure indicator of the existence of molecular motion, just as the sight of a ship rocking on the sea indicates the existence of waves.

Let us note first of all that, by participating in chaotic motion, a grain over time on average moves away from the first position by the value $L$, the square of which (and not the value $L$ itself) grows in proportion to time $t$.

We define the average displacement $L_0$ for time $t$ as $\sqrt{\overline{L^2}}$:

$$L_0 = \sqrt{\overline{L^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{(\Delta x)^2}{\Delta t}} \cdot \sqrt{t} = B \sqrt{t}.$$

The average displacement $\sqrt{\overline{L^2}}$ can have any direction in space with equal probability. We can say that a grain that was at point $A$ at the initial moment of time, after $t$ seconds will, on average, be somewhere near the surface of a ball with radius $R = B\sqrt{t}$. The radius of this ball does not grow in proportion to time $t$, but in proportion to the square root of $t$. The indicated value of the radius is determined the more accurately and by the increase, the more individual values ​​$\Delta x$ are included in the displacement $L$ or the more often the experiment is repeated to calculate the average value $\overline{L^2}$.

It is easy to see that the concept of the velocity of a particle as the ratio of the movement $L_0$ of a particle over a certain period of time to the value $t$ of this interval loses its meaning for chaotic motion. Really,

$$\frac{L_0}{t} = \frac{L_0}{\sqrt{t}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} = \frac{B}{\sqrt{t}}.$$

If we trace the entire intricate path of a particle and divide its total length by a period of time, then this is the relative speed $\overline{v}$ of the thermal motion of the particle, and the value $\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2}$ is the average kinetic energy of the molecules of the medium

(a is the radius of the grain, η is the coefficient that determines the fluidity of the medium).

The value of $\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2}$ calculated from equation (2), as the experiment shows, does not depend either on the properties of the medium, or on the choice of grains, or on other conditions of the experiment, as long as it is carried out at a constant temperature. By observing the Brownian motion of a particle at different temperatures, one can establish a direct connection between the average kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2}$ and the absolute temperature $T$:

$$\frac{1}{2} \overline{mv^2} = \frac{3}{2} kT,$$

where $k$ is a universal constant, the same for all substances and in all states. The constant $k$ is called Boltzmann's constant.
