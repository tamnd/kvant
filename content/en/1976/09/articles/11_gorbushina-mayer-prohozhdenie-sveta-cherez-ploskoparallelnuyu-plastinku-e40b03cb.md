Passage of light through a plane-parallel plate

It would seem that what could be simpler than the flat-parelle design of the plate? In elementary physics textbooks, several words are devoted to it: a ray of light passing through a plate is parallel to the incident one and only slightly displaced relative to it. Meanwhile, the phenomena that can be observed when light passes through a plate are extremely interesting. They have been studied by researchers for centuries, and are now widely used in science and technology. For example, thin flat-parella plates (elephant) are used in interference filters and mirrors. An interferometer, one of the most precise optical instruments, cannot be built without plates.

To get an idea of ​​the variety and beauty of the optical phenomena occurring in a plane-parella plate, let us consider the following experiment*). Let us assume that the plate is illuminated by a diverging beam of rays. The lower surface of the plate is matte, and the exact light source producing the diverging beam is located inside the plate on this surface (Fig. 1). It is obvious that a beam of light from a source cannot completely exit the plate: rays incident on the glass-air interface at an angle equal to or greater than the limit will experience complete reflection on the upper surface of the plate and return to the lower one. Since the surface of the plate is matte, the light falling on it will be scattered - the surface will be illuminated. If, under these conditions, one looks at the plane-parella plate in the light, one will see a bright luminous point—the light source S—surrounded by a weak light ring by cutting off the inner boundary.

Let's calculate the internal diameter D of the light ring (or the external diameter of the dark ring). According to the law of refraction (see figure 1) sinα/sinβ=n2/n1, where n1, n2 are the correspondence between the abscidal refractive indices of the plate and the medium above its upper surface. The angle of incidence α is called limiting if the angle of refraction β is equal to π/2. Consequently, the limiting angle of incidence can be found from the correspondence sinαpr=n2/n1. From triangle SAB sinαpr=SB/SA=D/4/√h²+D²/16. Excluding the angle αpr from the last two formulas, we obtain

$$\frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{\sqrt{D^2 + 16 h^2}}{D} = \sqrt{1 + 16 \frac{h^2}{D^2}}.$$

If you look at the plate from above in complete darkness, you will see the bright bulb (around the exact light source) that the theory predicts.

The experience can be slightly changed. Let's make plate sides about 5 mm high on the plate along its perimeter and pour a thin layer of water on the surface of the plate. (The plate must be clean so that the water evenly covers its entire surface.) At the same time, a second light bulb appears around the glowing point, having a larger diameter than the one obtained in the absence of water. In this experiment, total reflection from the interface between glass and water media is experienced (Fig. 3). Where does the first light prick around the source come from? It is reasonable to assume that its appearance is explained by the complete reflection of light from the water-air boundaries. If this assumption is correct, then the diameter of the first light ring should depend on the thickness of the water layer above the plate. Let's check it out.

If there is no water at all, one light spike is visible (Fig. 4; unfortunately, the dark circle in the center of which the source is located is not very clearly visible; as the thickness of the plate increases, observing conditions improve). We will gradually add water to the cuvette formed by a plate with plate sides. In this case, the size of the first light ring increases, while the diameter of the second remains unchanged (Fig. 5). At first the first colute is smaller than the second (see Fig. 5a) then it is combined with the second and finally becomes larger than the second (see Fig. 56). So, the assumption made is correct.

In conclusion, let's look at this experience. On the matchbox we put a piece of paniro (or other thin white) paper, which perfectly scatters the light. Place an air-tight glass plate on it (without a matte surface). Let's turn on the light bulb. At the same time, together with a light ring with a clear inner border surrounding the source, we will see a light circle with a sharp outer border (Fig. 6)!

This somewhat unexpected result is explained by the fact that now the light source is located not inside, but outside the plate. We leave a detailed explanation of the experience for you to try on your own.
