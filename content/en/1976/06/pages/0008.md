Fig. 4. A bundle of lines parallel to $e$ is constructed from the points of line $a$.

Let $e$ be an arbitrary line, then one of the three lines, say $a$, of Theorem A5 intersects the line $e$ at the point $E$. It is obvious that through any point on the line $a$, excluding the point $E$, there passes a single straight line parallel to the line $e$ (A2), and vice versa (Fig. 4), any straight line parallel to the line $e$ intersects the line $a$ at some point (see A6). We have established a one-to-one correspondence between the line $a$ and the lines of the bundle of parallel lines, which belongs to the line $e$.

Theorem A12. Any plane of order $n$ contains $n^2$ points $n^2+n$ lines.

Any pencil of lines parallel to a given line will contain all points of the final plane: otherwise, according to Axim A2, this family could be supplemented with another line parallel to a given line and passing through a point “not covered” by the pencil. But in any such family $n$ are lines (A11), and on each line $n$ points (A9), i.e., only $n^2$ points (Fig. 5).

§ 3. About the creep of military parades when a great mathematician observes them

Leonhard Euler came up with his famous puzzle about 36 offices, apparently while bored at one of the many St. Petersburg palace parades. Here is its formulation: among the 36 offices there are an equal number of lancers, dragoons, hussars, cuirassiers, cavalry guards and grenadiers, and, in addition, an equal number of generals, colonels, majors, captains, lieutenants, and second lieutenants, and the regiment of each of the “arms” is represented by officers of all six ranks. Is it possible to arrange these offices in a square $6 \times 6$ so that in any column and any line there are officers of all regiments and all ranks? (The solution to this problem, when it is required to build a square of 25 offices according to the same rules, is depicted on the cover of the magazine.)

The astute reader has already connected Euler's puzzle with the problem of constructing a finite plane of order 6 in which the points will be offices, and the lines will unite offices of either the same regiment or the same rank. The reader is, of course, right, but with one caveat: we will show that if for some $n$ ($n > 2$) there exists a finite affinity plane of order $n$, then there also exists the required arrangement of $n^2$ offices in the square $n \times n$ (for Euler $n=6$); however, the converse is, generally speaking, not true. For example, for $n=14$ there is no plane of order 14 (see Theorem 2 below), and Euler’s problem has a solution.
