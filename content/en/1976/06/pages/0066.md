What are physicists thinking about?

“What is the size and shape of the kernel? Everyone understands what the size and shape of a solid body at rest is. But the nucleus consists of particles that perform fast and intricate movements. In this case, does it make sense to talk about its size and shape? As a useful analogy, consider the propeller of an esroplane. The rotating propeller has the appearance of a nebulous circle, and a casual observer might assume that it is, in fact, in the shape of a circle. However, a more attentive observer, armed with a speed factor, can make a factoromock that will reveal the true shape of the propeller. This “instant forturation,” according to our calculation, represents the form, while the “exposed forturation,” seen by the first observer, allows us to determine only the average form, which may have nothing in common with the true one. Let us now assume that we have made a second instant forturation of this propeller or another propeller belonging to the same esroplane. On the second image we will see the same shape as before—the same length of the blade, the same angle between them, etc. Only the orientation of the propeller in the plane of expression can be different. The propeller retains its shape and hence we can consider it to be “solid”. The situation would be different if we made instant forturations of, for example, two identical octopuses. In this case, the shapes of the octopuses on the two 摄影s would almost certainly be different—the octopus is “soft.”

The size and shape of the nuclei can also be determined using momentary forturations. Largely, we can distinguish between “hard” kernels, which retain their shape, and “soft” kernels, whose shape can change. In addition, we can make chimneys of nuclei with different exposure times. In this case, we will not get the true shape, but only a shape averaged over time, something similar to a vague circle, in the form of which a rotating propeller appears to us.

This is how one of the articles begins in the tenth issue of the popular science collection “What Physicists Are Thinking About” *).
