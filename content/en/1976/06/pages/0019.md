Liquid thermometers are thin glass tubes that end in a small ball or cylinder at the bottom. The ball and the lower part of the tube were filled with liquid (alcohol, mercury, oil). On the second page of the cover you see samples of Florentine thermometers. (Isn’t it true, these are not only devices, but also, in a way, a work of art. In general, devices were made with an “artistic approach.”)
As for thermometric cabinets, the most personal ways of constructing them were used. Each designer and manufacturer of thermometers comes up with a way to create a cabinet for them. By the end of the 18th century, there were about two dozen different thermometric cabinets in use, of which three have survived to this day (which is also too many).

In the end, the principles for constructing thermometric cabinets, proposed by the Dutch glassblower and physicist Fahrenheit and the Swedish astronomer Celsen, triumphed.
