There is a well-known story about a smart centipede that forgot how to walk while thinking about the order in which it should rearrange its many legs. Eighty to ninety years ago, something happened to mathematicians: they thought about how valid their conclusions were when proving theorems. After almost twenty years of excitement and intense work, those standards of mathematical structure were developed, according to which any “self-respecting” theory is built today, and it finally became clear that mathematics would avoid teaching the unfortunate centipede.

At the same time, the majestic and at the same time well-ordered building of Euclidean geometry was saved from destruction and restoration; since then, its eternal foundation has become 20 axioms described by David Hilbert in his “Foundations of Geometry” *). Euclidean geometry can be compared with the famous Kell Cathedral (which was built, delivered and rebuilt by many generations of builders, who permanently settled next to the cathedral square and passed on their repair work and work place to their children).

Finite geometries are structured completely differently: the minimum of building material is a finite number of points, straight lines and planes, the minimum of rules for handling this material is three or four axioms that regulate the relationships between points, straight lines and planes.
