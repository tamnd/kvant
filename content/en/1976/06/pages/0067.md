Kalocsich periodic table of elements, the cycle of radioactive isotopes and the use of neutrons for scientific research. The seventh article is devoted to the so-called nuclear breeder reactors, which not only burn off nuclear fuel, but, at the same time, produce new insulated nuclear fuel, and even in quantities greater than the burned one. It is with these reactors that the most important research in the field of nuclear energy is now associated.

This is followed by four articles about research in the field of control of thermonuclear reactions - the prospects for thermonuclear energy, the problem of confining hot plasma inside thermonuclear installations, attempts to induce a thermonuclear reaction using a laser beam, and stories about the Tokamak installations, on which Soviet physicists added results on heating and confining thermonuclear plasma. The collection concludes with two articles on new methods for accelerating charged particles. Although the articles in this collection were written in different years, they successfully complement and develop each other.

Despite the fact that all articles in this collection are assumed to be by a foreign author, the names of Soviet physicists are often found in them. Soviet physicists made an appreciable contribution both to the physics of the atomic nucleus and to the study of the control of thermonuclear reactions. The collection talks about the work of Academician N. G. Basov, who was the first to obtain thermonuclear neutrons by irradiating hydrogen plasma with a powerful laser beam. The Soviet Tokamak thermonuclear installations, created under the leadership of Academician L. A. Artsimovich, are described in detail. Much attention is paid to the criterion of thermonuclear plasma stability proposed by the Soviet physicist V.D. Sha-

Frannov and American physicist Kruskal. New methods for accelerating charged particles, proposed by academicians G.I. Buder and V.I. Veksler, are also described. However, there are also places in the collection where, unfortunately, there are no necessary references to the works of Soviet scientists. It is a pity that these problems were not corrected during the translation and editing of the collection (although the translations themselves were done very well). So, for example, at the beginning of an article on the fission of atomic nuclei, a link should be given to the work of Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Ya. I. Frenkel, who, independently of N. Bohr, created a drop model of a nucleus and applied it to the process of fission of heavy nuclei. In the article about breeder reactors, it was necessary to provide information about Soviet reactors of this type and, first of all, about a powerful industrial breeder reactor, which has long been operating on the coast of the Caspian Sea near the city of Shevchenko.

In conclusion, we would like to give a short answer from the article by V. Vol and G. Kramer, after reading which, the reader will be convinced of how lively and interesting the solution to the collection is written. But first, a few words about the method of neutron activation analysis, which this article is devoted to.

By irradiating a substance with neutrons, it is possible to make its atomic radioactive. Using the radioactive radiation emitted in this case, it is possible to establish the chemical composition of a substance and detect the impurities present in it, the atoms of which will emit radiation characteristic of them.

At the end of the article, Wohl and Kramer write:

“Perhaps the most exciting application of neutron activity analysis in recent years was proposed by two Swedish physicists, S. Forshuud and A. Wassen, in 1961, in collaboration with G. Smith of the Department of Forensic Science at the University of Glasgow. For a long time, medicine began to question the official diagnosis that Napoleon died of cancer in 1821 during his exile on St. Helena. Based on the recorded symbols, many different diagnostics were assumed: pepsin ulcer, malaria, dysentery, etc. To test the hypothesis that Napoleon was in fact poisoned, Forshuud and his colleagues broke hair that undoubtedly belonged to Napoleon. They were torn from his head the night after his death, very much in order to distribute them as souvenirs, and also to make the mask shine. Several Volozhov residents were irradiated with neutrons in a nuclear reactor. Then litermitra sections of quite a length of hair were sequentially obtained and examined for the presence of arsenic in them. The concentration of arsenic in the hair segments was much higher than normal. This indicates the possibility of chronic poisoning of Napoleon with arsenic. In addition, arsenic levels were not consistent across hair lengths. Apparently, Napoleon was not given arsenic with pervers every day. Given that the hair on the head on average decreases by 0.35 mm per day, the scientists came to the conclusion that the periods of the highest concentrations of arsenic in the hair coincide with those days when, judging by the records, Napoleon was born during captivity. Thus, based on the concentration of arsenic contained in the hair of matching healthy people, we can conclude that Napoleon most likely died from arsenic poisoning, which was given to him under the guise of medicine during his illness.”
