Task 3. Build 196 officers (14 officers of all 14 ranks from 14 regiments) in Euler square $14 \times 14$.

According to Theorem A12, a plane of order $n$ contains $n^2$ points—they will be our officers. From the $n+1$ family of parallel lines (see A10 and A11) we choose four different families (for this the inequality $n+1 \geq 4$, i.e. $n > 2$, must be satisfied). To distinguish between these families of lines, let’s assign one of them number one, the other number two, etc. We also number the lines in each of these four families in random order. All points belonging to the first line of the first family are called, the second line of this family - lancers, etc. - a total of $n$ names of regiments. We call all points from the first line of the second family generals, the second line of this family - colonels, etc. - a total of $n$ ranks.

§ 4. Red, black and blue

Euler's puzzle led us to one of the most interesting questions in the theory of finite geometries: what are the finite affinity planes? For us, novice geometricians, it would be enough to find an answer to a narrower question: for which $n$ there is a finite affinity plane of order $n$?

There is still no complete solution to this problem. All that is known is exhausted by two substituted theorems.

Theorem 1. If the number $n$ is scanned or the degree is scanned, then a plane of order $n$ exists.

We will prove this theorem in § 5 for all prime numbers $n^*$).

Theorem 2. If the number $n$ when divided by 4 gives a remainder of 1 or 2, and if in the decomposition of this number into prime factors at least one lookup number $p$ of the form $p = 4k+3$ occurs in odd powers, then there is no finite affine plane of order $n$.

The first of the numbers that satisfy the conditions of Theorem 2 is the number $n=6: 6=4+2$ and $6=2 \cdot 3$. So, a plane of 36 points does not exist!

(Euler's remarkable intuition)
