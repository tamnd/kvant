A similar situation is typical in a geometric problem. Practitioners, when starting a solution, we are not able to construct a drawing that accurately reflects all the specifics of the configuration - many of its features are revealed only in the course of reasoning. Therefore, it is important first of all to identify the geometric properties that exist in a given problem. This requires special attention and caution, since at first glance it is not always obvious which features of the configuration are significant and to what extent the discrepancy between a given configuration and the drawing is acceptable.

Of course, if during the solution process it turns out that the drawing clearly does not correspond to the problem data, it should be replaced with the correct one. For example, in the following problem, even the development of geometric imagination cannot help to immediately complete a drawing that accurately reflects the essential features of the configuration.

Problem 2 (MSU, mechanism, 1972). In the triangular paramid $SABC$, the lateral edge $SC$ is equal to the edge $AB$ and is inclined to the base plane $ABC$ at an angle $60^\circ$. It is known that the vertices $A, B, C$ of the middle of the lateral edges of the paramid are located on a sphere of radius 1. Prove that the value of the indicated sphere lies on the edge $AB$, and find the height of the paramid.

To solve the problem, let's make a traditional drawing of the paramida $SABC$ (Fig. 3), construct its height $SH$ and draw a segment $HC$. Since according to the conditions of the problem $\angle SCH = 60^\circ$, then from the triangle $CHS$ there are $SH = a\sqrt{3}/2$, $HC = a/2$, where $a$ denotes the length of the edge $SC$.

According to the conditions, the vertices $A, B, C$ and the middle $A_1, B_1, C_1$ of the corresponding lateral edges lie on the same sphere. From here, in particular, it follows that a circle passes through the points $A, A_1, B, B_1$—the section of this sphere by the plane of the face $SAB$. Since $A_1B_1 \parallel AB$, then the quadrilateral $AA_1B_1B$ is a trapezoid, and, moreover, equilateral, since it is inscribed in a circle. That's why

$$AA_1 = BB_1 = \frac{1}{2} SA = \frac{1}{2} SB.$$

Similar distributions for the quadrilateral $BB_1C_1C$ show that the paramid $SABC$ has equal lateral edges: $SA = SB = SC = AB = a$. From this we can see that the triangle $ASB$ is equilateral, and therefore the apothem $SM$ of this lateral face is equal to $a\sqrt{3}/2$, i.e. $SM = SH$.
