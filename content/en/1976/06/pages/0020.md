These were the temperatures of ice and the boiling of water at atmospheric pressure. (The melting point of any solid and the boiling point of any liquid at a given pressure are also constant, but water and ice are the most accessible.)

In 1742, Celsius proposed this method of graduation, i.e., creating thermometer scales (Fig. 2). The thermometer, whatever it may be, is brought into contact with such ice and after thermal equilibrium is established, the level of liquid in the thermometer (if it is a liquid thermometer) is displaced by a certain number. Then such ice is replaced by boiling water, and the new level of liquid in the thermometer is offset by a number that differs from the first by 100. And the difference in levels is divided into 100 equal parts - degrees. Now it seems curious that Celsius marked the level of liquid in a thermometer at the boiling temperature of water with the number zero, and its level at the temperature of the melting ice with the number 100. However, 8 years later, in 1750, the scale was inverted, and in this form it is still used today.

Even before Celsius, in 1724, Fahrenheit, also using the temperature of melting ice and boiling water as changeover points, manufactured thermometers in which the temperature of melting ice was offset by the number 32, and the temperature of boiling water by the number 212, so that the temperature interval between melting ice and boiling water was divided not into 100, but into 180 equal parts - degrees. The French scientist Romur, like Celsius, attributed the temperature of the melting ice to 0, but to Romur's thermometer water boils at a temperature of 80 degrees.

As we see, there was considerable arbitrariness in the construction of thermometric scales. The number of degrees into which it was divided was arbitrary.
