§ 6. Epilogue

Unlike novels and plays, premature revelation of a plan is not a flaw in scientific research.

J. Galbraith

So, the question of the existence of finite affine planes of a given order becomes somewhat clearer: there are forbidden values ​​(red numbers on Fig. 6), there are unexplored values ​​(black numbers there) and there are allowed values ​​- prime numbers and their powers (blue numbers), for which there is a way to construct a plane.

But is this the only way?! Are there any other planes for this order $q=p^m$?

Indeed, the Finite Field Theorem (§5) prohibits the existence of fields other than some standard fields $F_q$ (they are called Galois fields after Evariste Galois); with the simplest of these fields—fields $F_p$—you actually already met on pages «Kvant»—in the article by A. Belsky and L. Sadovsky “Rings” («Kvant», 1974, No. 2—see problems 2 and 3 on p. 7 of this issue), but does not make the existence of planes of the same order $q=p^m$ different from standard ones. It was possible to show that all planes whose order does not exceed the number 8 can only be standard, and to construct a non-standard plane of order 9.

Saying goodbye to the reader, the author of the passionate treatise recommends that he read two wonderful books by E. Artin and M. Hall*), where the subject of the treatise is covered in an equally interesting way, although from different angles.

Amazing numbers

Write four three-digit numbers:

360, 351, 362, 402.

Now take any number of January, for example, 15/1, add to the first number 15 the number written - 3, and divide the sum 15 + 3 by 7 (the number of days of the week): $(15+3):7$. The remainder you will receive is 4 - 15/1 - 76 on the 4th day of the week, i.e. Thursday.

If you take some February number, for example 15, and add the second digit from a series of three-digit numbers, divide by 7, the remainder will be 0, which indicates that February 15 falls on Sunday.

If we take 7/1V, then according to this rule we will have to add 3 to 7, then we get $(7+3):7$ - the remainder is 3, which means that 7/1V will be the third day of the week, i.e. Wednesday.

In a word, the written series of three-digit numbers is 12 constant terms (written quarterly) for all twelve months of 1976, with the help of which you can quickly determine the day of the week of any date of the year according to a very simple rule:

The number you are interested in + the constant term of the given month: 7 = quotient and remainder is the answer.

Try using the 1976 calendar to determine where these 12 digits came from, and create the same 12 digits for 1977.

P. Sorokin (Astrakhan)
