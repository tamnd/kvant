Drawing in a geometric problem

Applicants have different views on the role of a drawing in solving a geometric problem. One thought that the drawing was not needed at all, and they drew it up carelessly. Others, on the contrary, consider the drawing itself to be a sufficient argument in reasoning and do not even find it necessary to justify in any way what is “visible from the drawing.” Both of these extreme views are wrong.

Of course, no drawing, even the most accurate one, can replace a logical proof, but is only an illustration of the reasoning. Any geometric fact that we “saw” in the drawing must be strictly substantiated - only then can we assert that this fact actually takes place and is not obtained from the correct (or, what is much more dangerous, incorrect) figure.

At the same time, a visual drawing is a good assistant in solving a problem: it can suggest the idea of ​​necessary reasoning and calculations, give rise to the idea of ​​using a certain theorem, or come up with a successful additional construction. It is not for nothing that mathematicians often resort to geometric illustrations to make the ideas of a proof more understandable.

However, only a drawing that correctly reflects the essential geometric features of the configuration discussed in the conditions can help solve the problem. That is why the drawing should be treated very carefully.

Often, applicants are limited to the first more or less successfully completed figure, without being interested in how accurately the completed drawing meets the conditions of the task. Meanwhile, in many problems, it is in principle impossible to carry out a complete solution from one drawing, since the condition of the problem allows for the existence of different geometric configurations. In addition, such attachment to one “random” drawing also leads to another inconsistency: in the course of solving a problem, a contradiction may be discovered between the resulting results and the original drawing, which usually confuses those entering. However, with a correct understanding of the drawing, there is nothing wrong with this - you should simply abandon the first initial image and make a new drawing that corresponds to the geometric information that appears (of course, provided that the reasoning and calculations carried out are correct).

When constructing a drawing, it is useful not to make an approximate sketch, which gives only a general idea of ​​the geometric configuration, but to strive to follow the construction of the drawing, based on the given tasks and general geometric facts. With this approach, it is easier to “see” those ideas that can be applied in a solution.

A similar situation is typical in a geometric problem. Practitioners, when starting a solution, we are not able to construct a drawing that accurately reflects all the specifics of the configuration - many of its features are revealed only in the course of reasoning. Therefore, it is important first of all to identify the geometric properties that exist in a given problem. This requires special attention and caution, since at first glance it is not always obvious which features of the configuration are significant and to what extent the discrepancy between a given configuration and the drawing is acceptable.

Of course, if during the solution process it turns out that the drawing clearly does not correspond to the problem data, it should be replaced with the correct one. Problem 2 (MSU, mechanism, 1972). In the triangular paramid $SABC$, the lateral edge $SC$ is equal to the edge $AB$ and is inclined to the base plane $ABC$ at an angle $60^\circ$. It is known that the vertices $A, B, C$ of the middle of the lateral edges of the paramid are located on a sphere of radius 1. Prove that the value of the indicated sphere lies on the edge $AB$, and find the height of the paramid.

To solve the problem, let's make a traditional drawing of the paramida $SABC$ (Fig. 3), construct its height $SH$ and draw a segment $HC$. Since according to the conditions of the problem $\angle SCH = 60^\circ$, then from the triangle $CHS$ there are $SH = a\sqrt{3}/2$, $HC = a/2$, where $a$ denotes the length of the edge $SC$.

According to the conditions, the vertices $A, B, C$ and the middle $A_1, B_1, C_1$ of the corresponding lateral edges lie on the same sphere. From here, in particular, it follows that a circle passes through the points $A, A_1, B, B_1$—the section of this sphere by the plane of the face $SAB$. Since $A_1B_1 \parallel AB$, then the quadrilateral $AA_1B_1B$ is a trapezoid, and, moreover, equilateral, since it is inscribed in a circle. That's why

$$AA_1 = BB_1 = \frac{1}{2} SA = \frac{1}{2} SB.$$

Similar distributions for the quadrilateral $BB_1C_1C$ show that the paramid $SABC$ has equal lateral edges: $SA = SB = SC = AB = a$. From this we can see that the triangle $ASB$ is equilateral, and therefore the apothem $SM$ of this lateral face is equal to $a\sqrt{3}/2$, i.e. $SM = SH$.

Problem 3 (MSU, Faculty of Soil Science, 1975). Two circles lie in an equal boundary. One of them, of radius 1, is described in the boundary, and the second touches two lines of the boundary and the first circle. The distance from the apex of the angle formed by the two lines of the border touching the second circle, to the point of contact of the circles is twice the diameter of the second circle. Find the area of ​​the border.

It is unclear from the conditions of the problem in which of the angles of the trapezoid - obtuse or acute - the second circle is described. Therefore, we must consider both options (Fig. 5) and try to find out which one is consistent with the specific numerical ratios given in the conditions.

Let $O$ be the center of a circle circumscribed by the trapezoid $ABCD$, $O_1$, $O_2$ the centers of the second circle (two options!), $P_1$, $P_2$, $M_1$, $M_2$, $N_1$, $N_2$ - points of tangency. We denote the radii of the second circle by $r_1$, $r_2$. From the similarity of triangles $OM_1C$ and $O_1N_1C$ in the first case and $OM_2D$ and $O_2N_2D$ in the second we have

$$\frac{OM_1}{O_1N_1} = \frac{OC}{O_1C}, \frac{OM_2}{O_2N_2} = \frac{OD}{O_2D},$$

and, since $OC = OP_1 + P_1C = 1 + 4r_1$, $OD = OP_2 + P_2D = 1 + 4r_2$, then in general cases

$$\frac{1}{2r} = \frac{1 + 4r}{3r},$$

whence $r = 1/2$. By the Pythagorean theorem, from the triangles $OM_1C$ and $OM_2D$, respectively, we now obtain that

$$M_1C = 2\sqrt{2}, M_2D = 2\sqrt{2}.$$

So, if the second circle is circumscribed by an obtuse angle of a trapezoid, then the smaller base of the trapezoid is equal to $4\sqrt{2}$; if the second circle is circumscribed into an acute angle of a trapezoid, then the larger base of the trapezoid is equal to $4\sqrt{2}$. Meanwhile, if a circle of diameter $d$ is described in an equal trapezoid with bases $a$ and $b$, where $a < b$, then

the inequality $a < d < b$ follows, for example, from the easily provable and very useful correspondence $d = \sqrt{ab}$. Therefore, in our problem, the base of the trapezoid must be less than 2, so the circle with center $O_1$ does not satisfy the conditions of the problem, and only the circle with center $O_2$ should be considered. Now it’s easy to find the area of ​​the $S$ trapezoid. Using the correspondence between the bases of the trapezoid and the diameter of the circle $d = \sqrt{ab}$ inscribed in it, we obtain the equality

$$2 = \sqrt{AD \cdot BC} = \sqrt{4 \sqrt{2} \cdot BC},$$

whence $BC = \sqrt{2}/2$ and $S = \frac{1}{2}(AD + BC) \cdot 2 = 9 \sqrt{2}/2$.

In the problem analyzed, the possibility of the existence of two fundamentally different geometric configurations was completely obvious. However, this is not always the case, and you need to have a good geometric imagination and be careful enough to “see” all the configurations that should be considered in the solution when making a drawing.
