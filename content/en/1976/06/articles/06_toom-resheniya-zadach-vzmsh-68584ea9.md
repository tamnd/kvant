Task 2. Three straight lines intersect at one point so that every two of them form an angle of 60°. Point M is located at a distance of 3 cm from one straight line and at a distance of 5 cm from the other. At what distance from a straight line can point M be located?

The solution to the problem is based on the following fact, which promises all points on the plane. For any point, the distance to one of the lines specified in the conditions is equal to the sum of the distances to the other two. Therefore, the required distance can be equal to either 5 + 3 = 8 cm, or 5-3 = 2 cm. It is easy to check that these values ​​are really possible.

Problem 7. Three concordant segments are drawn on a plane. How many axes of symmetry can this set have (the combination of these three segments)?

figure 4 shows sets having 0,1, 2, 3, 4, 6 axes of symmetry, respectively. Let us prove that a figure cannot have any other number of axes of symmetry.

Make sure for yourself that if there are less than three non-coinciding segments, then there are no more than four axes of symmetry.

Let our set consist of three non-coinciding segments. It is easy to understand that the symmetry of the figure translates one of the segments into itself.

For each segment there are only two symmetries that transform it into itself (their axes are the straight line on which the segment lies and its perpendicular bisector). This means that there are no more than six axes of symmetry in total.

It remains to prove that there cannot be a heel of symmetry axes. To do this, we will use the following fact. If straight lines I and II are axes of symmetry of the figure Ф, then straight line III, symmetrical to straight line II relative to straight line I, is also an axis of symmetry for Ф. (See Fig. 5, which shows how to obtain the point $A_4$, symmetrical $A_1$ relative to III.) Let the axes of symmetry be heels. Then the set of axes includes straight line I, containing a certain segment, and the perpendicular bisector to it - otherwise there would be fewer than four axes. But then the axes are an even number; can be combined into pairs of axes that are symmetrical with respect to I, but there are no axes perpendicular to I, except for axis II - otherwise there would be an infinite number of them (see Fig. 6).
