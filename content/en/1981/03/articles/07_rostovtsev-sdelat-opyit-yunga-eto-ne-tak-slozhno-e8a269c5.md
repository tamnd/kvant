N. Rostovtsev

Do Jung's experiment?

It's not that hard

At a sufficiently large distance from the screen for what is being observed, two identical light bulbs are located side by side, for example, light bulbs for a flashlight. Light one of them first, and then both at the same time. You will see that anywhere on the screen the illumination is greater with two lamps on than with one.

However, this is not always the case. If there are two beams of light, it may turn out that in one place in space they coax each other, in another they weaken or even extinguish each other. This phenomenon is called interference of light, and the light rays that give such a picture are called co-ferential.

The first experimental study of the interference of light was carried out by the English scientist Thomas Young at the very beginning of the 19th century. A diagram of the experiment bearing his name is shown on figure 1,a. Light from a brightly illuminated Sun-lit small response (or narrow slit) A fell on two small responses (or narrow slits) B and C located close to each other (| BC| ≈ 2 mm). On a remote screen (l≈2 m) in the place where the light beams coming out from B and C overlapped, luminous and dark stripes were observed alternating with each other (Fig. 1, b). If one of the slits B or C was blocked, the dark stripes disappeared and only a light spot remained on the screen.

How can we explain the results of Jung's experiment? According to the Huygen-Fresnel principle, the possible responses B and C, which must be reached from response A, become the exact sources of secondary waves. Due to the common origin, these waves are coherent and when superimposed they interfere. In those places where the waves arrive in the same phases, they reinforce each other and form minimum illumination. Where the waves arrive in opposite phases, they weaken each other and form minimum illumination. Since the oscillations in responses B and C are in phase, it is clear that the result of interference depends entirely on the path difference Δ of the interfering waves to the point in question.

At point O, equidistant from the responses, the waves arrive in identical phases and cancel each other out - a central maximum of illumination is observed there. When moving away from point O up or down, the wave path difference Δ=|BD−CD| gradually increases and successively takes on the values ​​λ/2, λ, 3/2λ, 2λ, ..., where λ is the wavelength. Maximum illumination is observed in those places for which the condition is met
Δ=0, λ, 2λ,...

At points for which
Δ=λ/2, 3/2λ, 5/2λ,...,

Minimum illumination levels are observed.

Is it possible to reproduce Jung's experience yourself? It turns out that doing this, albeit in a slightly different version, is quite simple.

Prepare one or two identical-handled sewing needles with a diameter of 0.6-0.8 mm and a sheet of paper. It is better to use black paper used for packaging photographic film or photo paper. The role of an accurate light source can be played by a flashlight bulb (if observed at a distance of at least 2-3 m from it) or a small hole (2-3 mm in diameter) in a piece of cardboard installed directly in front of the flashlight so that the filament is visible through the hole. In the evening, you can use the light from a remote lantern. The best light source is the image of the Sun in a steel ball (10-20 mm in diameter).

If you have everything ready, attend the experiments.

1. Cut a strip of paper 1-2 cm wide and use a needle to pierce holes of ever-decreasing diameters in it, starting with a needle diameter of 0.6-0.8 mm. To make the holes round, rotate the strip of paper around the needle or rotate the needle around its axis when piercing.

After rubbing at the exact source of the sequential through each hole, placing it directly in front of the eye. You will see that through the largest hole the source will look virtually the same as without the hole. With a smaller hole diameter, the picture will be different: a light round spot will be surrounded by dark and light pegs (Fig. 2). As the diameter of the hole decreases, the diameter of the central bright spot and the wheels surrounding it will increase and their intensity will decrease.

2. Now make a so-called double hole to carry out Jung's experiment directly. Take the same needles on your right and left hands. On the

On a piece of paper 1-2 cm wide, place sharp needles on one, and at a distance of a fraction of a millimeter from it, place sharp needles on the other. The needles should make a slight angle with the vertical. Try to press both needles at the same time and equally hard - you will get a double hole. (Of course, a double hole can be made with one needle, but, as practice shows, its quality will be worse.) It is good if the diameters of the holes are about 0.2-0.3 mm, and the distance between them is about 0.4-0.6 mm.

Afterwards, rub through these holes onto a precise light source. You see that the central light spot obtained from the previous observation will be divided by dark stripes into a number of light stripes (Fig. 3). Dark stripes (minutes of illumination) are those places where the light from one hole is extinguished by the light from another hole. If the holes are round and of the same diameter, the larvae of the minima are clearly visible not only on the central light spot, but also on the light pegs surrounding it.

A light stripe is always visible in the center of the interference pattern. The number of light stripes placed on the central spot can be equal to 3,5,7,... This depends on the ratio of the distance between the centers of the holes to their diameter. If the diameter of the hole is left unchanged and the distance between the holes is reduced, the number of light stripes also decreases, and their width correspondingly increases.

Turn the strip of paper with a double hole around the game and the eyes, and the system of light and dark stripes will also turn.
