What will the diameter show?

A diameter meter rests on a smooth horizontal surface. A force $F_1=5\text{H}$ is applied to one end of the diameter meter, and a force $F_2=8\text{H}$ (Fig. 1) is applied to the other. What will the diameter show?

Problems of similar content can be found in problem books and textbooks on physics. The answer, as a rule, is given as follows: the diameter will show 5\text{H}. This can be confirmed by the following reasoning. If forces equal to 5\text{H} are applied to both ends of the diameter meter, then the diameter meter shows 5\text{H}. If one of the forces is increased by 3\text{H}, then the balance is disturbed, and the diameter meter, showing 5\text{H}, begins to wonder in the direction of action of the larger force with acceleration $a=(F_2-F_1)/m$, where $m$ is the mass of the diameter meter.

It would seem that everything is obvious. However...

Let's look at this issue in detail. Let's start with this simple task:

A spring with stiffness $k$ lies on a smooth horizontal table. A force $F_0$ is applied to one end of the spring, directed

$$m_x = \frac{m_0}{l_0} x,$$

where $m_0$ is the mass of the spring, $l_0$ is the length of the spring in an unstretched state. The acceleration with which all springs will be surprised under the influence of force $F_0$ is equal to

$$a = \frac{F_0}{m_0}.$$

The section of the spring we are considering with length $x$ will be amazed at the same acceleration. This section is subject to a force from the rest of the spring equal to the mass of the spring $F_x$, arising in the flow at a distance $x$ from the free end of the spring. Therefore, the value of the spring force $F_x$ is equal to

$$F_x = m_x a = \frac{F_0 x}{l_0}.$$

We got an interesting result. Firstly, the spring force that arises in this case is equal to its coordinate with an unstretched spring, or the length of the section cut off by this section. Secondly, the value of this force does not depend on the mass of the spring. The spring mass can take values ​​other than zero. In the case of $m_0=0$ the acceleration acquired by the spring goes to infinity.

The resulting unification of the spring, the spring force, is proportional to this unification: $F=k\Delta l$, where $k$ is the spring stiffness. However, it is possible to calculate the diameter readings using this formula only when in any section of the spring

In our case of accelerated motion, the arrivals of the elastic force arising in different sections of the arrivals are different. This means that the elongation of individual sections and the arrivals, as a result of which the corresponding elastic forces arise, are different.

To calculate the extension of all arrivals, the following reasoning is used. We will divide the undistributed income into $N$ equal parts. Let's consider a piece of the parish separated from its free end $i$ by pieces (Fig. 3). The force $T_i$ with which the selected area acts on these $i$ pieces with mass $\frac{m_0}{N} i$ imparts to them an acceleration $a = F_0/m_0$; hence,

$$T_i = \frac{i}{N} m_0 a = \frac{i}{N} F_0.$$

The same absolute force acts on the selected area of ​​the parish. Let the force $T_{i+1}$ act on this section from the right. Then

$$T_{i+1} - T_i = m_i a = \frac{F_0}{N}.$$

As $N$ increases, the value $F_0/N$ decreases, and for sufficiently large $N$ we can assume that $T_{i+1} - T_i \approx 0$, that is, $T_{i+1} \approx T_i = \frac{i}{N} F_0$. In other words, for large $N$ we can assume that

$$T_i = \frac{F_1 - F_2}{N} i + F_2 = kN \cdot \Delta l_i.$$

Now we will move from a simple spring to a diameter gauge. Let's remember what a simple school diameter meter is. This is a removable spring with a cry, attached to a body with a scale printed on it (Fig. 5). So the conditions of our very first task can be formulated as follows:

The diameter meter lies on the main horizontal surface. A force $F_1$ is applied to its body, and a force $F_2$ is applied to the spring (to the shout). What will the diameter show?

To represent the readings of a diameter meter, let us imagine it in the form of a system such as in figure 6. The cube on the left is the body of the diameter meter; its mass is $m_1$. The spring on the right is a diameter spring; its mass is $m_2$. Let us write down the equations of motion of the body and spring:

$$T - F_1 = m_1 a,$$

$$F_2 - T = m_2 a,$$

where $a$ is the acceleration with which the diameter meter moves.

From these equations one finds

$$T = F_2 - \frac{(F_2 - F_1) m_2}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

Two forces act on the outer diameter of the diameter - $F_2$ and $T$, with $T < F_2$. And the extension of the spring in this case, as we found out, is equal to $\Delta l = \frac{T + F_2}{2k}$. Substituting the found value $T$, we get

$$\Delta l = \frac{F_2}{k} - \frac{(F_2 - F_1) m_2}{2k(m_1 + m_2)}.$$

From this expression it is clear that at $m_2 = 0$ (that is, in the case when the mass of the spring can be neglected) the extension of the spring is equal to $\Delta l = F_2/k$.

So, the result of our deliberations is as follows: the extension of the spring, and therefore the readings of the diameter meter, depends not only on the magnitude of the forces acting on the body and the spring, but also on the mass of the body and the diameter spring. That's not enough. The indication depends on what (to the body or to the spring) the forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ are applied. If we place the forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ in places in our consideration of the problem, the reading changes. (Check for yourself that this is indeed the case.)

We come to the conclusion that the problem formed at the beginning of the article cannot be solved strictly until the masses of the body and the diameter springs are specified. And the answer we gave at the very beginning is not correct. To verify this once again, solve the problem using real data typical of the school diameter meter you are familiar with.
