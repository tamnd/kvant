About snowballs, nuts, bubbles and...
liquid helium

Although helium is located almost at the very beginning of the periodic table, since its discovery it has caused physicists a lot of trouble due to its unusual properties. True, the efforts of the liquid helium pay off with the beauty and unique physical phenomena produced in liquid helium, as well as the use of multi-hat properties in modern physical laboratories. This includes daily obtaining low temperatures (the boiling point of liquid gel at atmospheric pressure is 4.2 K), and the study of the phenomenon of superfluidity, discovered in liquid helium by P. L. Kapitsa in 1936, and obtaining ultra-low temperatures (down to 0.001 K), and much more.

One of the replacement properties of a liquid gel is the unusual mechanism of charge transfer in it.

Charge carriers in liquids, as a rule, are electrons and ions. One can also imagine a process in which an electron “jumps” from its own atom to one that happens to be nearby, an electron from a neighboring atom “jumps” to its vacated place, a third electron from the next atom takes its place, and so on. From the outside, such a leapfrog of electrons looks like a movement of a positive charge in the opposite direction. And since in reality this positive charge does not exist, but there is simply the absence of an electron at its “working place,” such an object is called a “hole.” The movement of both electrons and holes produces very complex fields of a large number of atoms. At one time, problems of this kind - about the movement of electrons in a solid or liquid - were delivered to physicists of multi-hat particles. It turns out that in such a system the particles lose their individual qualities to a large extent. Further, the understandable masses become horizons more complex than the property of inertia that has been ingrained in us. Under these conditions, the particle is assigned the so-called effective mass, which is an important characteristic of the particle that takes into account its interaction with the atoms of the environment. If you mentally “turn off” all interactions, then the effective mass of the particle turns into its ordinary mass.

It was hypothesized that the charge in liquid helium passes between electrons and holes. Their movement should be almost free due to the low density of the gel and the weak interaction of its atoms with charged particles. Therefore, the effective masses of such charged particles should be close to the mass of a free electron.

To change the masses of positive and negative charge carriers, their trajectories in liquid helium were studied under the application of a uniform magnetic field. Knowing the speed of the particle and the magnitude of the magnetic field, changing the radius of the circular trajectory of the particle, it is easy to determine its mass. The results of these experiments turned out to be unexpected: the masses of positive and negative charge carriers were tens of thousands of times greater than the mass of a free electron!

An explanation for the structure of a positive charge carrier was proposed by the American physicist Atkins. It turned out that the whole “trick” lies in the relatively low solidification pressure of the liquid gel: at fairly low temperatures $p_{\text{TB}}=25$ atm. It is this fact that leads to the unusual structure of positive charge carriers in liquid helium.

It is known that liquid helium contains positive ions that retain their charge quite well. The interaction of the positive ion with the molecule $\text{He}_2$ leads to the fact that its electrons slightly change their orbits, being attracted to the positive ion, and the positively charged nuclei of atoms, on the contrary, are repelled from it. As a result, the centers of negative and positive charge in the molecule are separated by some distance. Thus, the presence of positive ions in the thickness of the liquid gel leads to the polarization of its molecules. Polarized molecules are attracted to the positive ion. This attraction leads to an increase in the concentration of molecules (that is, to the appearance of excess density), and therefore to an increase in pressure as the ion approaches. Graphic dependences of excess pressure on the distance $r$ to the ion are shown in figure 1.

As we have already said, at a pressure of 25 atm the liquid gel hardens. Therefore, as soon as the pressure in the nearly positive ion reaches this value, the corresponding volume of the liquid gel will solidify. As can be seen from figure 1, at low external pressure solidification occurs at a distance $r_0=7\text{Å}$ from the ion. Thus, the positive ion appears to be “frozen” into a “snowball” of solid gel. Now we can estimate the mass of the positive charge moving in liquid helium. The mass of the snowball itself is equal to $\frac{4}{3}\pi q r_0^3=32m_0$, where $m_0$ is the mass of the gel atom (density of the solid gel). The mass of the “tail” of excess density, which stretches behind the snowball, can be estimated as follows: knowing the dependence of pressure on the distance to the center of the snowball (that is, to the ion itself), you can easily find how the excess density of the gel changes depending on this distance; Next, we mentally divide the liquid around the snowball into a spherical layer of small thickness (such that the density can be considered constant at the thickness of the layer) and calculate the mass of each such layer; sum up all these masses, we find the total mass of the “tail”, which turns out to be equal to 28 $m_0$.

In addition, when a body moves in a liquid, its layers move. This causes the body to move as if its mass were some amount greater than its true mass. This additional mass associated with the movement of fluid is called added mass (see, for example, G. Kotkin

So, for the total mass of the positive charge moving in liquid helium, the calculation gives a value of 75 $m_0$. This value already agrees well with the value found experimentally.

The situation is completely different with negative charge carriers. It turns out that negatively charged helium ions do not exist, and therefore, it is impossible to build a “snowball” with a negative ion inside. Therefore, the only candidate for a negative charge carrier in liquid helium is the electron.

It is known from experiment that an electron cannot continuously enter liquid helium through its surface. This fact is not qualitatively difficult to understand. As you know from the chemistry course, helium is an inert gas, therefore its first electron shell is completely built up, and the next one is built up again - the nuclear charge is only $2e^+$, and the appearance of a third electron in the energy shell is impossible. Therefore, an electron cannot approach a helium atom with a slick (the same is the case with a helium molecule) - at close distances it is repelled. Thus, in order to drive an electron through the surface into the thickness of liquid helium, it is necessary to perform some “entry work”. Italian physicists Carreri, Fasoli and Gaeta proposed that it is thanks to this energy that an electron, arbitrary into the thickness of helium through the surface, “melts” helium molecules, to which it has no right to approach closely, and forms a spherical-symmetrical cavity around itself - a free-flowing bubble (Fig. 2).

The size of such a bubble can be estimated by considering the equilibrium conditions of its boundary. Although at close distances the electron is repelled from helium molecules, with sufficiently distant molecules it behaves in exactly the same way as a positive electron.

What balances these forces? It turns out that the electron itself creates counteraction to them. Being inside the bubble, the electron seems to “rush” around it, reflecting from the boundaries. As a result of the connection with the walls, a certain pressure is created, which compensates for the total pressure from the outside. In other words, the electron inside the bubble “behaves” like a gas in a closed vessel. Calculations show that at low external pressure the radius $R_0$ of such a bubble is about 20 Å.

How does the bubble behave as the external pressure increases? For the time being, like any bubble in a liquid, when external pressure increases, it simply disappears. Its radius $R$ decreases, as shown in figure 3. But at a pressure $r(p_0)=20$ atm, the graphs $r(p_0)$ and $R(p_0)$ intersect - the sizes of the bubble and the “snowball” become the same and equal to 12 Å. We know what the “snowball” will do with a further increase in pressure - it will rapidly increase its size due to the gel hardening on its surface. How should the bubble behave - to skim further (dotted line at figure 3)? But this is where the bubble shows its temper - with a further increase in pressure, it, like a “snowball”, begins to be treated with an ice crust of solid gel. Indeed, as can be seen from figure 1, at an external pressure of 20 atm, the total pressure at the boundary of the bubble (12 Å from its center) becomes equal to 25 atm, that is, it reaches the solidification pressure of the liquid gel. In this case, the bubble is “dressed in an ice shell”, the inner radius of which remains approximately constant with a further increase in pressure, and the outer radius is exactly equal to the size of the “snowball” at the corresponding pressure.

So, at external pressures greater than $p_0^*=20$ atm, the bubbles turn into ice shells and thus turn into some kind of nuts. However, the nucleolus of these “nuts” is very different - it is an electron that “throws around” inside the shell of a solid gel.

It remains to add that at $p_0\rightarrow 25$ atm the outer radius of the “nut”, like the “snowball”, tends to infinity, which corresponds to the complete solidification of the liquid gel throughout the entire volume. In solid helium, negative charge carriers turn out to be frozen bubbles with dimensions equal to the inner radius of the former “nut” - approximately 12 Å. Positive charge is not so easy to transfer charges - after all, they are in a solid substance. Therefore, the mobility of these city carriers is lower than that of “snowballs” and bubbles in liquid helium.
