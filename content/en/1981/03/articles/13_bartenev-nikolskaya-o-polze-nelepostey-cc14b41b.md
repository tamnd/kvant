One day, mom and Katya went to visit, and Petya, anticipating a pleasant evening, climbed onto the top shelf for a volume of fascinating stories about Sherlock Holmes. While taking out a book, he accidentally knocked a vase off the shelf, which broke into pieces. Petya's good mood was somewhat dampened, but, deciding not to get upset in advance, he swept the shards into a corner and settled comfortably with a book on the sofa. Buddy sat down next to him. As soon as he opened the book, Petya immediately forgot about everything in the world and plunged headlong into the world of mysterious ideas that Sherlock Holmes so deftly unraveled with the help of his detective method. Katya’s indignant exclamation brought him back to reality:

- Mom, look, Petka broke the vase that I gave you!

- Did you see it? - Petya asked out of habit. - Proof that it's me!

- What is there to prove here? - Katya shrugged. - Only you and Buddy were at home. Let's say that it wasn't you who broke the vase; That means Druzhok broke it. But you can’t say that Druzhok was able to get to the top shelf. It would be absurd: Buddy is still a dog, not a cat. So, you broke the vase, there is no one else.

“Yes,” thought Petya, “you can’t argue with her, her logic is like Sherlock Holmes’s; and there’s no point in arguing - I really did break the vase. I’ll go to Mitya and ask him what the math assignment is.

He learned from Mitya that in geometrics there was a theorem: Two straight lines have no more than one common point, the proof of which Mitya had already analyzed and calculated.

“Stories,” asked Petya (reading a geometry textbook, which they had just begun to study, seemed to him difficult and boring).

“You’re welcome,” Mitya agreed. — Let us assume that the statement of the theorem is false; Then...

“Wait, wait,” Petya said first, “I’ll tell you further.” If it is not true that two lines have at most one common point, then they have more than one common point, that is, two or three, or maybe more. But then it turns out that two points connect two straight lines. But this cannot be - we already know that there is only one straight line passing through two points. This means that two straight lines cannot have more than one common point, that is, they have no more than one common point.

“Well, you’re great,” Mitya was surprised. -Where did you get so crazy?

“I just got really crazy,” Petya laughed. “Katka just proved in the same way that it was I, not the dog, who broke the little cat.”

- In what way? - Mitya became interested. - What does it consist of? And in general, what does the proof of a geometric theorem have in common with the investigation into the case of the broken little vase?

Petya was a little confused, but only for a second. Inspired by the success and the greeting of his friend, he began to think dryly:

- In both cases I prove some statement $P$ (in the first case - Petya broke Vasochka, in the second - Two straight lines have at most one common point);

- the proof began with the assumption that not $P$ (the negation of the sentence $P$) was true;

- the assumption of truth not $P$ in both cases led to disobedience (in the first - to the fact that a dog can climb onto the top shelf, and this contradicts our ideas about the abilities and habits of dogs; in the second - to the fact that two straight lines can be drawn through two points, and this contradicts the well-known axiom).

- So what? - asked Mitya. - Why does this prove $P$?

- Here’s why: since the assumption that not $P$ is true led to disobedience, it means that not $P$ cannot be true. But if not $P$ is false, then $P$ is true!

“Everything is correct,” said Mitya. — If the negation of the sentence $P$ is false, then the sentence itself $P$ is islada.

Tinino; Do you remember we just talked about this?*). Therefore, instead of proving the truth of $P$, it is possible to prove the falsity not of $P$, but from this we can draw a conclusion about the truth of $P$.

“It’s possible,” Petya remarked, “but is it necessary?” Isn't it easier to take the direct path? This is how, for example, Katya and I took a test on algebra to prove that the number 13 is not the root of the equation $x^3-17x^2+119x-1000=0$: we calculated the value of the expression on the left side of the equation at $x=13$ and made sure that it is not equal to 0.

- And weren’t you too lazy to multiply and add? - Mitya grinned. — After all, one had only to assume that 13 is the root of this equation, and one would immediately get an incongruous conclusion: 1000 is divisible by 13 ($13^3-17 \cdot 13^2+119 \cdot 13=1000$). Therefore, 13 is not the root of this equation.

“So,” Petya inquired, “is this way of proof always shorter than the direct one?”

“No, not always,” answered Mitya, “but quite often.” Try, prove, for example, that in Moscow there are people with the same amount of hair on their heads (bald heads do not count). You’re not going to take everyone’s hair, hair by hair. But if we suggest that there are no such people, then, taking into account that about 8 million people live in Moscow, and the number of hairs on a person’s head does not exceed 500,000, we will come to the awkward conclusion: 500,000 $\geq$ 8 $\cdot$ 10^6. Consequently, in Moscow there are residents with the same number of hairs on their heads.

Proving the proposition $P$ by proving the falsity of its negation not $P$ is called indirect evidence; It is also called proof by contradiction. Now try, prove by contradiction that there is no most even number.

“Well,” said Petya, “it’s not difficult.” Let's assume that such a number $n$ exists. Then every other even number must be less than $n$. However, adding two to the number $n$, we get the even number $n+2$, greater than $n$. The resulting contradiction proves that the assumption of the existence of the most even number is false; This means that there is no most even number.

“You know,” said Mitya, “back in the third century BC, Euclid proved that the simplest number does not exist.” Assuming that such a number $p_n$ exists, he constructed the number $p=p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdot \dots \cdot p_n+1$, where $p_1, p_2, \dots$ are prime numbers, a contradiction of $p_n$; $p$ is not divisible by any of the numbers $p_1, p_2, \dots p_n$ (the remainder will always be 1), which means that either it itself is prime, or it is divisible by the prime number $q$, greater than $p_n$. The resulting contradiction ($p_n$ is the simplest number and, at the same time, either $p$ or $q$ is a prime number greater than $p_n$) proves the falsity of the assumption made and, thus, the truth of the statement The greatest prime number does not exist.

“It happens,” Mitya continued, “that the falsity of $P$ has not stood for a very long time, that one can immediately draw a conclusion about the truth of $P$.” Do you remember in fourth grade they told us that the empty set is a subset of any set? It sounded somehow strange, unconvincing. Let's try to prove this statement by contradiction. Recall that the set $M$ is called a subset of the set $N$ if every element of the set $M$ belongs to the set $N$. Let us assume that the empty set $M$ is not a subset of the set $N$, that is, there is an element of the set $M$ that does not belong to $N$. But this is a very bad thing, since in the empty set, according to its definition, there are no elements at all. Therefore, the empty set is a subset of the set $N$.

“And really convincing,” says Petya.

The friends parted, smitten with each other.

Soon, in a physics lesson, the teacher told the children how Galileo logically proved his hypothesis that in a vacuum all bodies fall equally quickly, and thereby refuted the hypothesis of Aristotle, who believed that a body falls the faster the heavier it is.

“Suppose,” said Galileo, “that a heavy cannonball falls faster than a light one.” What happens if you link these two nuclei together? On the one hand, a light nucleus should slow down the fall of a heavy one, on the other hand, the speed of fall of bound nuclei should be greater than the speed of fall of a heavy nucleus. The resulting contradiction proves that the assumption about the difference in the fall velocities of heavy and light nuclei is false. Therefore, the cores must fire at the same speed.

“This means that the reduction to a contradiction was not in physics,” Petya noted to himself.

During one of the first geometry lessons in my high school class, the theorem was explained: If two lines are perpendicular to the same line, then they are parallel. To prove this theorem this way: Suppose that lines a and b, perpendicular to line p, intersect at point M. Then two perpendiculars to line p pass through point M, which contradicts the theorem “Through any point there passes one and only one perpendicular to a given line.” The teacher said that this was proof by contradiction.

“Aha,” thought Petya, remembering last year’s conversation with Mitya, “this theorem is formulated in the form of a conditional sentence: If A, then B, and the negation of such a sentence is the sentence A and (not B), that is, the right a and b are perpendicular to the line p and intersect. We have proven that the statement A and (not B) is false, since it contradicts the previously proven theorem. So, If A, then B is true. Everything is right.

In an algebra lesson, it was necessary to prove the statement: If the natural number n is less than 1, then $\frac{1}{n} > n$.

The teacher called this reasoning proof by contradiction: let’s assume that $\frac{1}{n} \leq n$; then $\frac{1}{n} - n \leq 0$; from this it follows that $\frac{1 - n^2}{n} \leq 0$, whence $1 - n^2 \leq 0$, $1 \leq n^2$ and $n \geq 1$. It seemed to Petya that this was not quite what he had been taught to consider as proof by contradiction, and he decided to discuss this issue with Mitya. He cleared away his doubts.

“Look,” he said, “we assumed that the negation of the original statement is true: The natural number n is less than 1 and $\frac{1}{n} \leq n$.”

From this sentence follows a contradiction that refutes it: $n < 1$ and $n \geq 1$ at the same time. Therefore, the original sentence is true. As you can see, this is real proof by contradiction.
