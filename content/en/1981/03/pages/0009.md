A helium com is bubbles with an electron inside. The mass of such carriers is calculated in the same way as the mass of “snowballs”. However, now there is practically no own mass of the bubble - it is equal to the mass of the electron and is negligibly small compared to the mass of the entrained liquid. A calculation method similar to the above gives a value of 245 $m_0$ for the total mass of the negative charge carrier.

Now let's see how the increase in external pressure $p_0$ affects charge carriers. figure 1 shows the dependence of the total pressure in helium (taking into account external pressure) on the distance to the ion for $p_0^*=20$ atm. This dependence for an arbitrary external pressure $p_0<25$ atm is obtained by transferring the dependence for $p_0=0$ along the ordinat axis. As can be seen from figure 1, the greater the external pressure, the greater the distance from the ion, the total pressure becomes equal to 25 atm. Therefore, with increasing external pressure, the “snowball” behaves like a snowball: it rapidly turns into “snow” - solid helium, and becomes larger and larger in size. The dependence $r(p_0)$ of placing the “snowball” on external pressure is shown in figure 3.

How does the bubble behave as the external pressure increases? For the time being, like any bubble in a liquid, when external pressure increases, it simply disappears. Its radius $R$ decreases, as shown in figure 3. But at a pressure $r(p_0)=20$ atm, the graphs $r(p_0)$ and $R(p_0)$ intersect - the sizes of the bubble and the “snowball” become the same and equal to 12 Å. We know what the “snowball” will do with a further increase in pressure - it will rapidly increase its size due to the gel hardening on its surface. How should the bubble behave - to skim further (dotted line at figure 3)? But this is where the bubble shows its temper - with a further increase in pressure, it, like a “snowball”, begins to be treated with an ice crust of solid gel. Indeed, as can be seen from figure 1, at an external pressure of 20 atm, the total pressure at the boundary of the bubble (12 Å from its center) becomes equal to 25 atm, that is, it reaches the solidification pressure of the liquid gel. In this case, the bubble is “dressed in an ice shell”, the inner radius of which remains approximately constant with a further increase in pressure, and the outer radius is exactly equal to the size of the “snowball” at the corresponding pressure.

So, at external pressures greater than $p_0^*=20$ atm, the bubbles turn into ice shells and thus turn into some kind of nuts. However, the nucleolus of these “nuts” is very different - it is an electron that “throws around” inside the shell of a solid gel.

It remains to add that at $p_0\rightarrow 25$ atm the outer radius of the “nut”, like the “snowball”, tends to infinity, which corresponds to the complete solidification of the liquid gel throughout the entire volume. In solid helium, negative charge carriers turn out to be frozen bubbles with dimensions equal to the inner radius of the former “nut” - approximately 12 Å. Positive charge is not so easy to transfer charges - after all, they are in a solid substance. Therefore, the mobility of these city carriers is lower than that of “snowballs” and bubbles in liquid helium.
