About snowballs, nuts, bubbles and...
liquid helium

Although helium is located almost at the very beginning of the periodic table, since its discovery it has caused physicists a lot of trouble due to its unusual properties. True, the efforts of the liquid helium pay off with the beauty and unique physical phenomena produced in liquid helium, as well as the use of multi-hat properties in modern physical laboratories. This includes daily obtaining low temperatures (the boiling point of liquid gel at atmospheric pressure is 4.2 K), and the study of the phenomenon of superfluidity, discovered in liquid helium by P. L. Kapitsa in 1936, and obtaining ultra-low temperatures (down to 0.001 K), and much more.

One of the replacement properties of a liquid gel is the unusual mechanism of charge transfer in it.

Charge carriers in liquids, as a rule, are electrons and ions. One can also imagine a process in which an electron “jumps” from its own atom to one that happens to be nearby, an electron from a neighboring atom “jumps” to its vacated place, a third electron from the next atom takes its place, and so on. From the outside, such a leapfrog of electrons looks like a movement of a positive charge in the opposite direction. And since in reality this positive charge does not exist, but there is simply the absence of an electron at its “working place,” such an object is called a “hole.” The movement of both electrons and holes produces very complex fields of a large number of atoms. At one time, problems of this kind - about the movement of electrons in a solid or liquid - were delivered to physicists of multi-hat particles. It turns out that in such a system the particles lose their individual qualities to a large extent. Further, the understandable masses become horizons more complex than the property of inertia that has been ingrained in us. Under these conditions, the particle is assigned the so-called effective mass, which is an important characteristic of the particle that takes into account its interaction with the atoms of the environment. If you mentally “turn off” all interactions, then the effective mass of the particle turns into its ordinary mass.

It was hypothesized that the charge in liquid helium passes between electrons and holes. Their movement should be almost free due to the low density of the gel and the weak interaction of its atoms with charged particles. Therefore, the effective masses of such charged particles should be close to the mass of a free electron.

To change the masses of positive and negative charge carriers, their trajectories in liquid helium were studied under the application of a uniform magnetic field. Knowing the speed of the particle and the magnitude of the magnetic field, changing the radius of the circular trajectory of the particle, it is easy to determine its mass. The results of these experiments turned out to be unexpected: the masses of positive and negative charge carriers were tens of thousands of times greater than the mass of a free electron!
