Now we will move from a simple spring to a diameter gauge. Let's remember what a simple school diameter meter is. This is a removable spring with a cry, attached to a body with a scale printed on it (Fig. 5). So the conditions of our very first task can be formulated as follows:

The diameter meter lies on the main horizontal surface. A force $F_1$ is applied to its body, and a force $F_2$ is applied to the spring (to the shout). What will the diameter show?

To represent the readings of a diameter meter, let us imagine it in the form of a system such as in figure 6. The cube on the left is the body of the diameter meter; its mass is $m_1$. The spring on the right is a diameter spring; its mass is $m_2$. Let us write down the equations of motion of the body and spring:

$$T - F_1 = m_1 a,$$

$$F_2 - T = m_2 a,$$

where $a$ is the acceleration with which the diameter meter moves.

From these equations one finds

$$T = F_2 - \frac{(F_2 - F_1) m_2}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

Two forces act on the outer diameter of the diameter - $F_2$ and $T$, with $T < F_2$. And the extension of the spring in this case, as we found out, is equal to $\Delta l = \frac{T + F_2}{2k}$. Substituting the found value $T$, we get

$$\Delta l = \frac{F_2}{k} - \frac{(F_2 - F_1) m_2}{2k(m_1 + m_2)}.$$

From this expression it is clear that at $m_2 = 0$ (that is, in the case when the mass of the spring can be neglected) the extension of the spring is equal to $\Delta l = F_2/k$.

So, the result of our deliberations is as follows: the extension of the spring, and therefore the readings of the diameter meter, depends not only on the magnitude of the forces acting on the body and the spring, but also on the mass of the body and the diameter spring. That's not enough. The indication depends on what (to the body or to the spring) the forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ are applied. If we place the forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ in places in our consideration of the problem, the reading changes. (Check for yourself that this is indeed the case.)

We come to the conclusion that the problem formed at the beginning of the article cannot be solved strictly until the masses of the body and the diameter springs are specified. And the answer we gave at the very beginning is not correct. To verify this once again, solve the problem using real data typical of the school diameter meter you are familiar with.
