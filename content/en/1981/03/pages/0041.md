⟦folio 39⟧

“Wait, wait,” Petya said first, “I’ll tell you further.” If it is not true that two lines have at most one common point, then they have more than one common point, that is, two or three, or maybe more. But then it turns out that two points connect two straight lines. But this cannot be - we already know that there is only one straight line passing through two points. This means that two straight lines cannot have more than one common point, that is, they have no more than one common point.

“Well, you’re great,” Mitya was surprised. -Where did you get so crazy?

“I just got really crazy,” Petya laughed. “Katka just proved in the same way that it was I, not the dog, who broke the little cat.”

- In what way? - Mitya became interested. - What does it consist of? And in general, what does the proof of a geometric theorem have in common with the investigation into the case of the broken little vase?

Petya was a little confused, but only for a second. Inspired by the success and the greeting of his friend, he began to think dryly:

- In both cases I prove some statement $P$ (in the first case - Petya broke Vasochka, in the second - Two straight lines have at most one common point);

- the proof began with the assumption that not $P$ (the negation of the sentence $P$) was true;

- the assumption of truth not $P$ in both cases led to disobedience (in the first - to the fact that a dog can climb onto the top shelf, and this contradicts our ideas about the abilities and habits of dogs; in the second - to the fact that two straight lines can be drawn through two points, and this contradicts the well-known axiom).

- So what? - asked Mitya. - Why does this prove $P$?

- Here’s why: since the assumption that not $P$ is true led to disobedience, it means that not $P$ cannot be true. But if not $P$ is false, then $P$ is true!

“Everything is correct,” said Mitya. — If the negation of the sentence $P$ is false, then the sentence itself $P$ is islada.

Tinino; Do you remember we just talked about this?*). Therefore, instead of proving the truth of $P$, it is possible to prove the falsity not of $P$, but from this we can draw a conclusion about the truth of $P$.

“It’s possible,” Petya remarked, “but is it necessary?” Isn't it easier to take the direct path? This is how, for example, Katya and I took a test on algebra to prove that the number 13 is not the root of the equation $x^3-17x^2+119x-1000=0$: we calculated the value of the expression on the left side of the equation at $x=13$ and made sure that it is not equal to 0.

- And weren’t you too lazy to multiply and add? - Mitya grinned. — After all, one had only to assume that 13 is the root of this equation, and one would immediately get an incongruous conclusion: 1000 is divisible by 13 ($13^3-17 \cdot 13^2+119 \cdot 13=1000$). Therefore, 13 is not the root of this equation.

“So,” Petya inquired, “is this way of proof always shorter than the direct one?”

“No, not always,” answered Mitya, “but quite often.” Try, prove, for example, that in Moscow there are people with the same amount of hair on their heads (bald heads do not count). You’re not going to take everyone’s hair, hair by hair. But if we suggest that there are no such people, then, taking into account that about 8 million people live in Moscow, and the number of hairs on a person’s head does not exceed 500,000, we will come to the awkward conclusion: 500,000 $\geq$ 8 $\cdot$ 10^6. Consequently, in Moscow there are residents with the same number of hairs on their heads.

Proving the proposition $P$ by proving the falsity of its negation not $P$ is called indirect evidence; It is also called proof by contradiction. Now try, prove by contradiction that there is no most even number.

*) See the article “It is not true that... - how is this clear?” («Kvant», 1980, no. 9).
