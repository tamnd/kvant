it was necessary to propose some other mechanism, not the same as that of an ordinary liquid, for re-installing helium in liquid. An explanation for the structure of a positive charge carrier was proposed by the American physicist Atkins. It turned out that the whole “trick” lies in the relatively low solidification pressure of the liquid gel: at fairly low temperatures $p_{\text{TB}}=25$ atm. It is this fact that leads to the unusual structure of positive charge carriers in liquid helium.

It is known that liquid helium contains positive ions that retain their charge quite well. The interaction of the positive ion with the molecule $\text{He}_2$ leads to the fact that its electrons slightly change their orbits, being attracted to the positive ion, and the positively charged nuclei of atoms, on the contrary, are repelled from it. As a result, the centers of negative and positive charge in the molecule are separated by some distance. Thus, the presence of positive ions in the thickness of the liquid gel leads to the polarization of its molecules. Polarized molecules are attracted to the positive ion. This attraction leads to an increase in the concentration of molecules (that is, to the appearance of excess density), and therefore to an increase in pressure as the ion approaches. Graphic dependences of excess pressure on the distance $r$ to the ion are shown in figure 1.

As we have already said, at a pressure of 25 atm the liquid gel hardens. Therefore, as soon as the pressure in the nearly positive ion reaches this value, the corresponding volume of the liquid gel will solidify. As can be seen from figure 1, at low external pressure solidification occurs at a distance $r_0=7\text{Å}$ from the ion. Thus, the positive ion appears to be “frozen” into a “snowball” of solid gel. Since the center of this snowball is positively charged, when it moves in liquid helium

behind it there is a “tail” of excess density.

Now we can estimate the mass of the positive charge moving in liquid helium. The mass of the snowball itself is equal to $\frac{4}{3}\pi q r_0^3=32m_0$, where $m_0$ is the mass of the gel atom (density of the solid gel). The mass of the “tail” of excess density, which stretches behind the snowball, can be estimated as follows: knowing the dependence of pressure on the distance to the center of the snowball (that is, to the ion itself), you can easily find how the excess density of the gel changes depending on this distance; Next, we mentally divide the liquid around the snowball into a spherical layer of small thickness (such that the density can be considered constant at the thickness of the layer) and calculate the mass of each such layer; sum up all these masses, we find the total mass of the “tail”, which turns out to be equal to 28 $m_0$.
