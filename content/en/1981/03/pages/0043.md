To prove this theorem this way: Suppose that lines a and b, perpendicular to line p, intersect at point M. Then two perpendiculars to line p pass through point M, which contradicts the theorem “Through any point there passes one and only one perpendicular to a given line.” The teacher said that this was proof by contradiction.

“Aha,” thought Petya, remembering last year’s conversation with Mitya, “this theorem is formulated in the form of a conditional sentence: If A, then B, and the negation of such a sentence is the sentence A and (not B), that is, the right a and b are perpendicular to the line p and intersect. We have proven that the statement A and (not B) is false, since it contradicts the previously proven theorem. So, If A, then B is true. Everything is right.

In an algebra lesson, it was necessary to prove the statement: If the natural number n is less than 1, then $\frac{1}{n} > n$.

The teacher called this reasoning proof by contradiction: let’s assume that $\frac{1}{n} \leq n$; then $\frac{1}{n} - n \leq 0$; from this it follows that $\frac{1 - n^2}{n} \leq 0$, whence $1 - n^2 \leq 0$, $1 \leq n^2$ and $n \geq 1$. It seemed to Petya that this was not quite what he had been taught to consider as proof by contradiction, and he decided to discuss this issue with Mitya. He cleared away his doubts.

“Look,” he said, “we assumed that the negation of the original statement is true: The natural number n is less than 1 and $\frac{1}{n} \leq n$.”

From this sentence follows a contradiction that refutes it: $n < 1$ and $n \geq 1$ at the same time. Therefore, the original sentence is true. As you can see, this is real proof by contradiction.
