⟦folio 16⟧

N. Rostovtsev

Do Jung's experiment?

It's not that hard

At a sufficiently large distance from the screen for what is being observed, two identical light bulbs are located side by side, for example, light bulbs for a flashlight. Light one of them first, and then both at the same time. You will see that anywhere on the screen the illumination is greater with two lamps on than with one.

However, this is not always the case. If there are two beams of light, it may turn out that in one place in space they coax each other, in another they weaken or even extinguish each other. This phenomenon is called interference of light, and the light rays that give such a picture are called co-ferential.

The first experimental study of the interference of light was carried out by the English scientist Thomas Young at the very beginning of the 19th century. A diagram of the experiment bearing his name is shown on figure 1,a. Light from a brightly illuminated Sun-lit small response (or narrow slit) A fell on two small responses (or narrow slits) B and C located close to each other (| BC| ≈ 2 mm). On a remote screen (l≈2 m) in the place where the light beams coming out from B and C overlapped, luminous and dark stripes were observed alternating with each other (Fig. 1, b). If one of the slits B or C was blocked, the dark stripes disappeared and only a light spot remained on the screen.

How can we explain the results of Jung's experiment? According to the Huygen-Fresnel principle, the possible responses B and C, which must be reached from response A, become the exact sources of secondary waves. Due to the common origin, these waves are coherent and when superimposed they interfere. In those places where the waves arrive in the same phases, they reinforce each other and form minimum illumination. Where the waves arrive in opposite phases, they weaken each other and form minimum illumination. Since the oscillations in responses B and C are in phase, it is clear that the result of interference depends entirely on the path difference Δ of the interfering waves to the point in question.

At point O, equidistant from the responses, the waves arrive in identical phases and cancel each other out - a central maximum of illumination is observed there. When moving away from point O up or down, the wave path difference Δ=|BD−CD| gradually increases and successively takes on the values ​​λ/2, λ, 3/2λ, 2λ, ..., where λ is the wavelength. Maximum illumination is observed in those places for which the condition is met
Δ=0, λ, 2λ,...

At points for which
Δ=λ/2, 3/2λ, 5/2λ,...,

Minimum illumination levels are observed.
