Interference of light

We have all been familiar with interference patterns since childhood. These are rainbow spots from spilled gasoline on puddles, bright flowers of soap bubbles. The mechanism for the formation of a multi-colored interference pattern is quite complex. Let us limit our consideration of interference to simpler examples.

In the physical encyclopedic dictionary we read: “Interference is the successful or weakening of the amplitude of the resulting wave, depending on the correspondence between the phases of two (or several) waves with the same periods that add up in space. Interference occurs for all waves, regardless of their nature.” To this we can add that the interference pattern will be stable if the folding waves have not only identical periods, but also a constant (independent of time) phase shift. Such waves are called coherent.

Let us remember how an interference pattern arises when oscillations from two precise coherent light sources are added. The direction vectors $\vec{E}$ of the electron field (the so-called light vectors) in electron waves emitted by sources can be written in the form

$$E_1 = A_1 \cos \omega t, \\
E_2 = A_2 \cos (\omega t + \varphi_0).$$

For simplicity, let us consider the case when the light sources are in phase (phase shift $\varphi_0 = 0$) and the oscillation amplitudes are the same ($A_1 = A_2 = A$).

Let the distances from the sources $S_1$ and $S_2$ to the observation point $C$ in the plane $P$ be $r_1$ and $r_2$, respectively (Fig. 1). On the way from the sources to the observation point, the waves will acquire a path difference of $\Delta r = r_1 - r_2$. At those points of the $P$ plane where the waves arrive in the same phases (that is, with a path difference $\Delta r = k\lambda$, where $k$ is any integer), they will sleep each other, and as a result, the light vector $\vec{E}$ will oscillate with an amplitude $2A$. At these points there is an interference maxima. Where two waves of equal amplitude arrive in opposite phases (that is, with a path difference equal to an odd number of half-waves: $\Delta r = (2k + 1)\lambda/2$), they will completely cancel each other out. At these points there are interference minima, where there are no oscillations: $E = 0$ at any time.

The eye observes the pattern over time (at frequencies corresponding to light fluctuations, the eye “does not notice” the fluctuations) and reacts to the light intensity in proportion to the average value of the square of the resulting light vector ($E^2$).
