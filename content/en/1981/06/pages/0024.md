There are 29.53 days between two nawolliyas; During this time, at its maximum speed, the Sun will travel 30°, that is, less than the minimum area of ​​eclipses. Consequently, it will not have time to continuously cover the areas of eclipses between two navollias, and at least one of them will overshadow the Sun inside this zone. If this happens close to the node, the eclipse will be of great phase, even total or collegiate. If the first navollia occurs at the very beginning of the Sun’s entry into the eclipse zone, then the next navollia will coincide even before the Sun leaves the danger zone, so that two navollias in a row will be accompanied by eclipses, but in parts and of a very small phase (which may go unnoticed by the general public). After this, almost half a year later, the same phenomenon is repeated in the vicinity of another node.

Thus, there are two epochs every day when solar eclipses occur. In each epoch there is either one eclipse of a major phase, or two of a minor phase, and during the course of a year there can be two eclipses of a major phase or four of a minor phase. Of course, intermediate comparisons are also possible - one eclipse of a major phase and two minor ones, or two major and one minor. But if the first eclipse occurs at the beginning of January, then even before the end of the year (at the end of December), the Sun will again enter the danger zone and another eclipse of the minor phase may occur. (This is also facilitated by the obstruction that the radial nodes do not remain motionless on the ecliptic, but move towards the Sun, so that the time between the passage of the Sun through a given node is 19 days shorter than the calendar year.) In this case, within one Five eclipses are possible per year. This was the case, for example, in 1935, when partial eclipses were observed on January 5, February 3, June 30, July 30 and December 25.

If we talk about the Earth in general, then solar eclipses are not at all rare phenomena. Over the course of a hundred years, there were an average of 84 partial, 77 collegiate, 11 collegiate and 66 total eclipses, for a total of 238 eclipses. However, each partial eclipse is visible not on the entire Earth, but on a limited, albeit large, part of it. The lunar penumbra (Fig. 3) usually has a radius of about 3500 km and slides across the Earth in the direction of the Moon's movement - in an easterly direction. In the center of the penumbra there is a shadow spot, the radius of which can reach 135 km. But it may also be that the top of the shadow cone does not reach the Earth, then the expanding cavity of the cone falls on its surface, producing a colle-shaped eclipse. In the case of a total eclipse, the shadow spot is carried away by the Moon at a speed of about one kilometer per second, so that the longest duration of a total eclipse in a given location does not exceed 7 minutes.
