The interference pattern is a system of swirling light and dark stripes. By placing the observation plane perpendicular to the line $S_1S_2$, one can see the interference pattern in the form of concentric wheels.

Let's return to figure 1. The wave arrives at point O with a path difference $\Delta r = 0$. The angle $\Omega = d/L$ at which sources are visible from point O (and at $d/L \ll 1$ from any observation point) is called the angle of convergence of interference rays, or interference angle. The distance between the centers of adjacent light (or dark) stripes is called the width of the interference fringe. Let's see what it depends on.

Problem 1. Two plane waves interfere. Calculate the width x of the interference fringe if the angle of convergence of the waves on the screen is $\Omega$ and the wavelength is $\lambda$.

figure 2 shows the positions of the wave fronts at some point in time. Blue (and red) solid lines connect the points where, at this moment, the direction of the electric field takes on an amplitude value of $E = +A$. The distances between adjacent solid lines are $\lambda$. Dashed lines connect the points where the direction of the field is $E = -A$.

At points O, M, N, $M', N'$, etc., the two waves meet in equal phases. Since the phases of the oscillations of both waves change equally with time ($\varphi = \omega t + \varphi_0$), at any given time the waves will arrive at these points in the same phases and, therefore, reinforce each other. Points O, M, N, $M', N'$ correspond to interference maxima.

From figure 2 it is clear that $NOC = \Omega/2$, $|ON| = 2x$ and $|CN| = \lambda$. From compliance

$$\frac{\lambda}{2x} = \sin \frac{\Omega}{2} \approx \frac{\Omega}{2}$$

we'll find

$$x = \frac{\lambda}{\Omega}$$

— the width of the interference fringe depends on the wavelength of the sources and the angle of convergence. This result is valid for any interference scheme with a small angle of convergence of the rays (at a sufficient distance from the sources, the waves can always be considered flat). Particularly for precision sources (see Fig. 1)

$$\Omega = \frac{d}{L} \text{ и } x = \frac{\lambda}{\Omega} = \frac{\lambda L}{d}$$.

In red light ($\lambda \approx 7 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m) the fringes are wider than in green light ($\lambda \approx 5,5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m); as the screen moves away from the sources, the stripes expand ($x \sim L$); merging of sources also leads to broadening of the bands ($x \sim 1/d$).

Note that two common independent sources are not correct, because the phase difference of the waves coming from them is not constant. To obtain a clear interference pattern, light from one source is divided into two beams. This can be done, for example, using one of the circuits presented at figure 3. By placing the screen where the light beams emanating from two sources (or their images) $S_1$ and $S_2$ intersect, interference fringes can be observed.
