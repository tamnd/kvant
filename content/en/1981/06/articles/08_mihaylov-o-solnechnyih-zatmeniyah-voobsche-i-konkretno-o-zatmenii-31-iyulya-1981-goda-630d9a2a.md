About solar eclipses in general and specifically about eclipses
July 31, 1981

On the morning of July 31, 1981, an interesting astronomical phenomenon will occur - a solar eclipse, visible as a partial eclipse throughout the entire territory of the Soviet Union and as a total eclipse on a narrow but long strip from the shores of the Black Sea through the Caucasus, Kazakhstan, Siberia, the Far East and the Sea of ​​Okhotsk to the simple Pacific Ocean. But before we describe this eclipse, let's look at some general questions about solar eclipses.

These phenomena are caused by the Moon when it partially or completely covers the solar disk. The Moon is almost 400 times smaller in diameter than the Sun, but it is approximately the same number of times closer to the Earth, as a result of which the apparent sizes of the Sun and Moon for an earthly observer are practically the same. However, small deviations of the earth's orbit from circular cause small changes in the distances to the Sun, and at the same time in the angular diameter of the Sun. The same thing happens to the Moon as it moves around the Earth. Therefore, the lunar disk is quickly visible to us for a little more time than the solar one, and a little less time. In the first case, the Moon can completely cover the entire Sun and produce a total solar eclipse, and in the second case, it can leave the edges of the Sun visible in the form of a narrow ring surrounding the dark disk of the Moon, and produce a pincer-shaped eclipse. Every year in winter, when the Earth is close to the Sun and therefore the Sun is visible to us at a greater angle, predominantly collateral eclipses occur, and in summer, when the Earth is farther from the Sun, total eclipses predominate.

without falling victim to the dragon. The Sun travels an average of 57′ per day, and in winter, with its fastest movement, 61′. There are 29.53 days between two nawolliyas; During this time, at its maximum speed, the Sun will travel 30°, that is, less than the minimum area of ​​eclipses. Consequently, it will not have time to continuously cover the areas of eclipses between two navollias, and at least one of them will overshadow the Sun inside this zone. If this happens close to the node, the eclipse will be of great phase, even total or collegiate. If the first navollia occurs at the very beginning of the Sun’s entry into the eclipse zone, then the next navollia will coincide even before the Sun leaves the danger zone, so that two navollias in a row will be accompanied by eclipses, but in parts and of a very small phase (which may go unnoticed by the general public). After this, almost half a year later, the same phenomenon is repeated in the vicinity of another node.

Thus, there are two epochs every day when solar eclipses occur. In each epoch there is either one eclipse of a major phase, or two of a minor phase, and during the course of a year there can be two eclipses of a major phase or four of a minor phase. Of course, intermediate comparisons are also possible - one eclipse of a major phase and two minor ones, or two major and one minor. But if the first eclipse occurs at the beginning of January, then even before the end of the year (at the end of December), the Sun will again enter the danger zone and another eclipse of the minor phase may occur. (This is also facilitated by the obstruction that the radial nodes do not remain motionless on the ecliptic, but move towards the Sun, so that the time between the passage of the Sun through a given node is 19 days shorter than the calendar year.) In this case, within one Five eclipses are possible per year. This was the case, for example, in 1935, when partial eclipses were observed on January 5, February 3, June 30, July 30 and December 25.

If we talk about the Earth in general, then solar eclipses are not at all rare phenomena. Over the course of a hundred years, there were an average of 84 partial, 77 collegiate, 11 collegiate and 66 total eclipses, for a total of 238 eclipses. However, each partial eclipse is visible not on the entire Earth, but on a limited, albeit large, part of it. The lunar penumbra (Fig. 3) usually has a radius of about 3500 km and slides across the Earth in the direction of the Moon's movement - in an easterly direction. In the center of the penumbra there is a shadow spot, the radius of which can reach 135 km. But it may also be that the top of the shadow cone does not reach the Earth, then the expanding cavity of the cone falls on its surface, producing a colle-shaped eclipse. In the case of a total eclipse, the shadow spot is carried away by the Moon at a speed of about one kilometer per second, so that the longest duration of a total eclipse in a given location does not exceed 7 minutes.

This period, rounded to 6585 1/3 days, or 18 years and 10 1/3 or 11 1/3 days, depending on the number of leap years in this period, is called karos. According to Herodotus, it was known back in the sixth century BC. e. During one karos, 15 partial, 14 collegiate, 2 collegiate-total and 12 total eclipses will occur, and in total there will be 43 solar eclipses, which after the expiration of the karos alternate in the same order. However, such a number of days in Karos leads to the fact that at the end of Karos the Earth turns an additional 1/3 of a revolution, so that eclipses each time move 120° to the west. Therefore, with the help of carosa, one can predict only the day and the general nature of the future eclipse, but without detailed indication of the places of its visibility.

Now eclipses affect many years in advance with an accuracy of a few seconds in time and up to two kilometers in the place of its visibility, which is the result of a huge and long-term work on studying the movement of the Sun and Moon across the starry sky.

The year 1981 was marked by two eclipses - a collegiate eclipse, which occurs on February 5 in the South Pacific, and a total eclipse of interest, which occurs on July 31. It begins in the Caucasus, when a strip of total eclipse 65 km wide enters the Black Sea coast near Ochamchir and from here passes in a north-easterly direction through Nalchik and Mozdok, crosses the northern part of the Caspian Sea, affects the Aktibinsk region (Berchogur station on the Tashkent railway),

In conclusion, let us remind you that during a partial eclipse, in order to avoid server damage to vision, you cannot look at the Sun without protected dark glass, since the uncovered part of the solar disk has the same brightness as outside the eclipse. Only when the total phase of the eclipse is present can you admire the beautiful spectacle of the chromosphere and corona with the naked eye or through binoculars.
