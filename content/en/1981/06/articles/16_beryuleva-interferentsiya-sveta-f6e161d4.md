Interference of light

We have all been familiar with interference patterns since childhood. These are rainbow spots from spilled gasoline on puddles, bright flowers of soap bubbles. The mechanism for the formation of a multi-colored interference pattern is quite complex. Let us limit our consideration of interference to simpler examples.

In the physical encyclopedic dictionary we read: “Interference is the successful or weakening of the amplitude of the resulting wave, depending on the correspondence between the phases of two (or several) waves with the same periods that add up in space. Interference occurs for all waves, regardless of their nature.” To this we can add that the interference pattern will be stable if the folding waves have not only identical periods, but also a constant (independent of time) phase shift. Such waves are called coherent.

Let us remember how an interference pattern arises when oscillations from two precise coherent light sources are added. The direction vectors $\vec{E}$ of the electron field (the so-called light vectors) in electron waves emitted by sources can be written in the form

$$E_1 = A_1 \cos \omega t, \\
E_2 = A_2 \cos (\omega t + \varphi_0).$$

For simplicity, let us consider the case when the light sources are in phase (phase shift $\varphi_0 = 0$) and the oscillation amplitudes are the same ($A_1 = A_2 = A$).

Let the distances from the sources $S_1$ and $S_2$ to the observation point $C$ in the plane $P$ be $r_1$ and $r_2$, respectively (Fig. 1). On the way from the sources to the observation point, the waves will acquire a path difference of $\Delta r = r_1 - r_2$. At those points of the $P$ plane where the waves arrive in the same phases (that is, with a path difference $\Delta r = k\lambda$, where $k$ is any integer), they will sleep each other, and as a result, the light vector $\vec{E}$ will oscillate with an amplitude $2A$. At these points there is an interference maxima. Where two waves of equal amplitude arrive in opposite phases (that is, with a path difference equal to an odd number of half-waves: $\Delta r = (2k + 1)\lambda/2$), they will completely cancel each other out. At these points there are interference minima, where there are no oscillations: $E = 0$ at any time.

The eye observes the pattern over time (at frequencies corresponding to light fluctuations, the eye “does not notice” the fluctuations) and reacts to the light intensity in proportion to the average value of the square of the resulting light vector ($E^2$).

The distance between the centers of adjacent light (or dark) stripes is called the width of the interference fringe. Let's see what it depends on.

Problem 1. Two plane waves interfere. Calculate the width x of the interference fringe if the angle of convergence of the waves on the screen is $\Omega$ and the wavelength is $\lambda$.

figure 2 shows the positions of the wave fronts at some point in time. Blue (and red) solid lines connect the points where, at this moment, the direction of the electric field takes on an amplitude value of $E = +A$. The distances between adjacent solid lines are $\lambda$. Dashed lines connect the points where the direction of the field is $E = -A$.

At points O, M, N, $M', N'$, etc., the two waves meet in equal phases. Since the phases of the oscillations of both waves change equally with time ($\varphi = \omega t + \varphi_0$), at any given time the waves will arrive at these points in the same phases and, therefore, reinforce each other. Points O, M, N, $M', N'$ correspond to interference maxima.

From figure 2 it is clear that $NOC = \Omega/2$, $|ON| = 2x$ and $|CN| = \lambda$. From compliance

$$\frac{\lambda}{2x} = \sin \frac{\Omega}{2} \approx \frac{\Omega}{2}$$

we'll find

$$x = \frac{\lambda}{\Omega}$$

— the width of the interference fringe depends on the wavelength of the sources and the angle of convergence. This result is valid for any interference scheme with a small angle of convergence of the rays (at a sufficient distance from the sources, the waves can always be considered flat). Particularly for precision sources (see Fig. 1)

$$\Omega = \frac{d}{L} \text{ и } x = \frac{\lambda}{\Omega} = \frac{\lambda L}{d}$$.

In red light ($\lambda \approx 7 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m) the fringes are wider than in green light ($\lambda \approx 5,5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m); as the screen moves away from the sources, the stripes expand ($x \sim L$); merging of sources also leads to broadening of the bands ($x \sim 1/d$).

Note that two common independent sources are not correct, because the phase difference of the waves coming from them is not constant. From figure 5 it is clear that in this case the area of ​​overlap of the rays is limited and lies between the lens and the intersection point of the rays $AS_1$ and $BS_2$. At a distance $l$ from the lens, the size $|CE|$ of the overlap area is maximum. Here you can observe $N = \frac{|CE|}{x}$ bands, where $x = \frac{\lambda}{\Omega}$ is the width of the band. The convergence angle is $\Omega = \frac{|S_1S_2|}{f-l}$, therefore

$$N = \frac{|CE|}{\lambda} \frac{|S_1S_2|}{f-l}.$$

From the similarity of triangles $F_2F_1O$ and CEO we find

$$|CE| = |F_2F_1| \frac{l}{F} = \frac{al}{F},$$

and from the similarity of triangles $AOC$ and $S_1S_2C$ -

$$|S_2S_1| = |AO| \frac{f-l}{l} = \frac{D-a}{2} \frac{f-l}{l}.$$

Then finally

$$N = \frac{|CE|}{\lambda} \frac{|S_1S_2|}{f-l} = \frac{a(D-a)}{2F\lambda} = 450 \text{ полос.}$$

As the observation plane moves away from the lens, the stripes become narrower ($\Omega$ increases). The width of the overlap region up to plane $CE$ increases and then decreases, and moreover, faster than the width of the strip. Therefore, the maximum number of stripes is observed in the $CE$ plane – the widest section of the overlap area.

When the source $B$ moves away, the first darkening at point $O$ will occur provided that the difference in the wave paths from $B$ and $A$ is equal to 1/2 the wavelength:

$$\Delta r = |BO| - |AO| = \frac{\lambda}{2},$$

or

$$\sqrt{L^2 + d^2} - L = \frac{\lambda}{2}. \tag{1}$$

If the screen is brought closer to the sources at a distance $l$, the minimum at point $O_1$ will correspond to a path difference of 3/2 wavelength:

$$\Delta r_1 = |BO_1| - |AO_1| = \frac{3\lambda}{2},$$

or

$$\sqrt{(L-l)^2 + d^2} - (L-l) = \frac{3\lambda}{2}. \tag{2}$$

Let us transform expressions (1) and (2) and use the approximate formula $(1+x)^{1/2} = 1 + \frac{x}{2}$ for $x \ll 1$:

$$\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{d}{L}\right)^2} - 1 = \frac{\lambda}{2L},$$

$$\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{d}{L-l}\right)^2} - 1 = \frac{3\lambda}{2(L-l)},$$

or

$$d^2 = \lambda L,$$

$$d^2 = 3\lambda(L-l).$$

From here

$$l = \frac{2}{3} L = 6 \text{ м.}$$
