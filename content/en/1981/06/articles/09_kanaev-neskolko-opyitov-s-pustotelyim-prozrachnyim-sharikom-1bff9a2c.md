Several experiments with a hollow air-filled ball

Sporting goods stores sell a simple item for anglers - a “floating weight.” This is a hollow plastic ball with two ears and a small, slightly elongated hole (Fig. 1). With it you can test a number of interesting physical experiments.

Before you begin the experiment, learn how to quickly fill a balloon with water (or some other liquid). A syringe without a needle or a rubber bulb, in which an empty tube from a ballpoint pen is inserted, can help you with this.

1. Dissolve a few crystals of potassium permanganate (potassium permanganate) in a small amount of hot water until you obtain a dark crimson-colored solution. Fill the ball with this solution and lower it on a thread into a glass vessel with water at room temperature. (To stabilize the ball, tie a small weight to its lower ear.)

Immediately, a colored jet with a diameter equal to the diameter of the hole (Fig. 2) begins to rise from the hole. The jet reaches the “ceiling” (the surface layer), from where separated streams flow down. This process continues for quite a long time.

Some vision experience. Prepare a solution of potassium permanganate at room temperature and immerse the ball in water at the same temperature - no colored stream will appear from the hole.

Now add a few drops of ethyl alcohol to the solution and repeat the experiment - the colored stream rushes upward and dries for a long time.

Why does a colored jet appear in the first and last cases and not in the second case?

The reason for everything is the movement of molecules. In the first experiment, the speed of movement of the molecules of the hot liquid is greater than the speed of movement of the molecules of cold water in the vessel - the warm stream rises upward. In the second experiment, the temperatures of the solution and water are the same, and the speeds of movement of the molecules are also the same. In the third experiment, the temperatures are also the same, but the movement of the alcohol molecules is more intense, thanks to which the solution is forced out of the hole.

2. Fill the balloon with water to the brim. Then use a pipette to remove some of the water (about three pipettes full) and add the same amount of ethyl alcohol. After this, cover the answer with plasticine.

After about 20 minutes, an air bubble the size of a pea will appear at the top of the ball.

The same is observed in a mixture of water with boric or acetic acid, but in a mixture with glycerol, skatium does not occur (obviously, in this case the gaps between the molecules are not filled).

3. Take the ball and touch the surface of the water with the edges of its hole. The hole will be covered with film, but after 30-40 seconds the film will burst (you will hear a slight click), and a drop of water will form inside the ball at the edge of the hole.

Once again, tighten the hole of the ball with film, but now pierce the film in the middle with a needle. The film remains intact for a very long time.

Why is film so short-lived in the first layer and durable in the second?

The fact is that the water film formed on the hole is only at the edges than in the middle (due to wetting). Therefore, if the plane of the film is not strictly horizontal (which is very likely), the water will shift along the slope, and the film will thin out. At some point the film will burst and the water will collect in a drop.

The durability of the film in the second layer is explained by the fact that the needle is well wetted with water and the water is drawn from the edges to the point of contact with the needle. The film only becomes stronger.

4. Prepare two glass jars with a capacity of 0.5 liters each. Fill both jars to the top with water at room temperature. Drop a little shampoo diluted in water (1:4 ratio) into one of them from a pipette.

Fill the ball with clean water and carefully lower it into the jar of shampoo solution. The ball will immediately sink to the bottom.

You can place the surface of the ball under the tap and just as carefully lower it into a jar of clean water. Almost completely immersed in water, the ball does not sink, although the force of gravity slightly exceeds the buoyancy force.

Why does a ball of water sink in one layer, but not in another? Will the ball sink in weak dissolved sugar in water?

It turns out that everything depends on the surface tension of the liquid and whether or not this liquid wets the surface of the ball. Thus, the surface tension coefficient of pure water is almost twice as large as that of a soap solution, and plastic is poorly wetted with water. That's why the ball doesn't sink in water.

Sugar will also increase the surface tension of water, so that in a weak solution of sugar the ball will not sink either.

5. Take a wire with a diameter of 2-3 millimeters and a length of about a meter. File one end of the wire and, at a right angle, insert it into the eye of the ball closest to the hole. Slowly lower the ball into a tank or bucket of water and just as slowly lift it to the surface. You will see that when you lift the ball, air bubbles will fly out of its hole. The deeper the ball sinks, the more bubbles fly out.

How to ensure the formation and separation of air bubbles when lifting the ball?

When the ball lowers, the air inside it is removed and a certain amount of water enters the ball. As it rises, the external pressure decreases, the air in the ball expands and a bubble forms at the edges of the hole. The size of the bubble gradually increases, and a cervix is ​​formed, which dries out. In this case, the buoyant force directed upward increases, and the force of the tension surface holding the bubble decreases. At some point, these forces are equalized, the bubble breaks off and takes on a spherical shape.
