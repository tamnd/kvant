Feathered chess king
So, our chess page begins the second year of its existence. During this time, the editor of the magazine received many letters from readers, and we were convinced that chess was very popular among children interested in science. We strived to ensure that the materials on our pages were varied - tasks and stages, interesting combinations and games of outstanding chess players were accompanied by information about events, origins in the chess world, globe puzzles and chess problems with a geometric plot. We will continue to introduce you to various aspects of the game of chess and the art of chess.

Let us remind you that this summer the 30th match of the world chess championship took place. Therefore, a lot of the attention on our pages will be removed by the match for the chess crown. We will at least briefly talk about each match and present the most striking and interesting fragments of the fight. In the chess competition, combinations belonging to world champions and their opponents will be presented for solution.

The history of chess is full of “uncrowned queens” - the strongest chess players of their era, unofficial world champions: Greco, Philidor, Labourdonnais, Statunton, Andersen, Morphy. In the 80s, centuries passed, the chess world decided that the chess had a “real” king, and in 1886, after defeating I. Zukertort, Wilhelm Steinitz was declared the first official world champion. Steinitz was unofficially recognized as the best chess player in the world back in 1866 after winning a match against Andersen. So, twenty years later, he legitimized his title.

The match took place in several US cities. After five games, Steinitz lost to his outstanding contemporary with a score of 1:4, but later managed to turn the tide of events and won nine victories (the match lasted until ten victories). Here's the ending of the penultimate game of the match.

From the obtained equalities we find the friction coefficient

$$\mu=\frac{kl_0(1/cos\alpha-1)sin\alpha}{mg-kl_0(1-cos\alpha)}=0,2.$$

2. Let us denote by $\vec{v}$ the speed of the puck at point $C$ (see Fig. 3 in the article). It can be found from the law of conservation of energy

$$\frac{m|\vec{v}|^2}{2}=\frac{m|\vec{v}|^2}{2}+mgR(1-cos\alpha).$$

The time of the block in flight from point $C$ to point $D$ is determined by the formula

$$t=2|\vec{v}|sin\alpha/g.$$

The flight range of the block is

$$l=|\vec{v}|cos\alpha\cdot t=|\vec{v}|^2sin2\alpha/g=(\|\vec{v}\|^2-2gR(1-cos\alpha))sin2\alpha/g=6,9\text{ м}.$$

3. Let us record the acceleration of the equilibrium of the pistons after heating the gas and moving the point $O$:

$$kx+pS_1-p_0S_1-|\vec{F}|=0,$$

$$p_0S_2+|\vec{F}|-pS_2=0.$$

Here $\vec{F}$ is the tension force of the rod, $p$ is the new gas pressure, which can be found from the acceleration of the constant gas volume:

$$\frac{p}{p_0}=\frac{T}{T_0}=\frac{T_0+\Delta T}{T_0}.$$

Eliminating $|\vec{F}|$ and $p$ from the obtained equalities, we find the required distance

$$x=\frac{p_0(S_2-S_1)}{k}\frac{\Delta T}{T_0}=0,25\text{ м}.$$

where $p_2$ is the pressure of the rock state of the gas, and $p_1$ is the pressure of the rarefied gas. The pressures $p_1$ and $p_2$ can be found from the acceleration that the gas temperature remains unchanged:

6. Distance $l$ from the source to the mirror and distance $l'$ from the mirror to the image of the connection between the formulas of a spherical mirror

$$\frac{1}{l} + \frac{1}{l'} = \frac{2}{R}.$$

The radius $r$ of the circle along which the source moves and the radius $r'$ of the circle along which the image moves are related by the relation

$$\frac{r}{r'} = \frac{l}{l'}$$

$$r' = r \frac{l'}{l} = \frac{r R}{2l - R}.$$
