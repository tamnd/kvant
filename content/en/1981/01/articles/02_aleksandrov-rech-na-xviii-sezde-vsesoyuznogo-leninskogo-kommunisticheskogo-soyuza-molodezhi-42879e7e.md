Scientists educate the youth

President of the USSR Academy of Sciences
Academician A. P. ALEXANDROV

Speech at the XVIII Congress
Vesoyuzny Leninsky
Communist Youth Union

Dear comrades! Let me first of all convey to you heartfelt greetings from the headquarters of Soviet science - the Academy of Sciences of the USSR. In his speech to the Komsomol congress, Comrade Leonid Ilyich Brezhnev said that, in general, communists of the older generation can be satisfied with the Soviet youth of our days. These, comrades, are very meaningful words. We are really pleased with our Soviet youth.

I've been around for many years now - 75 minutes. I had to work with young people in a wide variety of activities and in a variety of conditions. This happened in peacetime, this happened during war, and this happened during the most difficult consecutive period when work began in the field of atomic technology and nuclear energy. In all these cases, the older generation was helped wholeheartedly by the youth. They, young scientists, bore the greatest burden of all this work. Young people eagerly went to the most difficult, difficult areas. And she selflessly did what needed to be done. This is why I believe that we can be absolutely satisfied with our youth. And we, scientists, are very pleased with the XVIII Komsomol Congress.

Comrade! We have lived for 33 years without war, and this is your great happiness, and for this you, just like us, older people, should be deeply recognized by our Communist Party, which is directing enormous efforts towards the noble cause of peace.

I must say that Leonid Ilyich, who spent the entire war in its most difficult places, who saw and experienced everything that the war brought to our people, especially clearly feels the need to preserve peace, he makes especially great efforts so that our country can further develop in peaceful conditions. And this, comrades, is not easy. After all, after the October Revolution, and especially after the end of the Second World War, the confrontation between the countries with socialist content and the capitalist camp is intensifying. As soon as we find some weak point, our enemies immediately pay attention to it. Of course, all this places an enormous responsibility on us and you, the young people. We must not allow any weak spots in our united front that could be used for ideological sabotage against us, for effort-oriented efforts and military confrontation.

Comrade! You know that the use of nuclear weapons in Hiroshima and Nagasaki was not forced by military circumstances. This was a demonstration of the so-called “position of force” policy in order to force our country to one way or another capitulate to this new type of weapon. You know that soon after the end of the war they began to create military bases around the countries. I remember the period when maps were drawn abroad that showed planes taking off from military bases, their routes lay towards our cities, they were carrying nuclear bombs. And then, comrades, our youth, together with our scientists, who were led by our greatest scientist and patriot, a wonderful, selfless and devoted person to the Motherland, Academician Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov, solved the most difficult scientific and technical problem - they created our nuclear shield. Under his leadership, work on the creation of nuclear technology was launched, and our country solved this most difficult task in the same time frame required by the United States, which attracted the world's largest scientists to this work.

Many young men and women who had just returned from the war took part in this important work. Let me tell you that at that time these teams did not feel that the war was ending, the focus of the work was so great. Everyone understood that the timely completion of this work could save the world from nuclear war. And then the youth showed themselves in this new direction for them. After all, I had to learn everything that had not been taught before—nuclear physics, and all the most complex issues of atomic technology. These sciences simply did not exist at that time. Everything had to be created and discovered, studied and taught. And these tasks, comrades, with great help, with the great participation of our youth, were solved on time.

At that time, in the West they wrote that the Soviet Union could create nuclear weapons, it is believed that by 1954, and around 1952, a nuclear war against our country was planned, but we created nuclear weapons already in 1949 thanks to this selfless work. Let me remind you, comrades: Igor Vasilievich Kurchatov then reported at the session of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR regarding the participation of youth in these works. This was truly a heroic feat of our scientific youth.

Comrade! We live in a tense environment. We, the people of the Earth, are beginning to feel that the resources of our planet are not limitless,

Readability problem

figure 2 shows a solenoid whose turns are made from metal tape (we will soon learn why they are made from tape and not from round wire). The forces acting from the magnetic field tend to tear the solenoid in the plane of the turns and, in addition, press the outer turns towards the middle ones. To combat tearing forces, the solenoid is placed in a durable outer shell, which absorbs the tearing forces. To combat skimming forces, insulation is laid between the turns, having high strength on the roll, for example mica or ceramics. But both the insulation strength and the strength of the shell are not unlimited. Another way to combat destruction in large fields is the so-called “low-power” solenodes. The turns in such solenodes are arranged so that the angle between the vector $\vec{B}$ and the direction of the current is as small as possible - then the forces decrease (see (1)). However, it is impossible to make a completely “powerless” solenoid.

The forces acting from the magnetic field limit the possibility of the magnitude of the field induction. Thus, in a volume of ~100 cm³, it is practically impossible to obtain a field greater than 20 Tesla using a copper solenode. If the winding is made of high-strength copper alloys (copper + zirconium, copper + cadmium, etc.), the field induction in a volume of 100 cm³ can reach 40 Tesla. However, copper alloys have higher (1.5-2 times) resistance than copper. And these are the conditions (see (2)) for the already difficult problem of heat transfer.

Heatsink

Of all the liquids that can be used for cooling, water is the most heat-resistant; therefore, as a rule, it is used for cooling. It would be easiest to wind the solenoid with a copper tube together with a copper wire and pass water through the inner tube for cooling, and current through the tube itself. This is what they did once upon a time; This is still done now, but only in cases where the heat that needs to be removed from the solenoid is small. A long and thin tube creates a lot of resistance to flowing water, and it is impossible to pump a large amount of water through such a solenoid.

Meanwhile, in order to respond to a thermal power of, say, 5 MBt from the solenoid using water from the water main (water temperature 10÷15°C), you need to pump 1000 liters of water per minute through the solenoid, and this water will come out of the heating solenoid almost to a boil. The water flow should not weaken even for a moment - otherwise the water temperature will reach 100°C, the water will boil, the heat transfer will deteriorate (the thermal conductivity of the steam is low) and the solenoid will collapse. Therefore, the path for water should be shorter and wider to resist the flow

So cryogenic solenodes have an important drawback - higher consumption of relatively expensive refrigerant.

As a result, cryogenic solenodes are not widely used, especially since they have even colder competitors:

Superconducting solenodes

For many metals and alloys, when cooled to a certain temperature (critical), the resistance becomes zero. In this state, these metals and alloys are called superconductors. If you make a solenoid winding from such a material and cool the solenoid to a temperature below critical, then its resistance becomes zero. This solves the heat sink problem - after all, if the resistance is zero, then the higher power is also zero. The problem of readability remains, so superconducting solenodes do not create “record” fields. But they are lighter and smaller in size than water-cooled copper ones. Compared to cryogenic superconducting solenodes, they require hundreds of times less refrigerant consumption. (In general, superconducting solenodes could also be called cryogenic - after all, they are cooled by the same liquid gases, but according to tradition, only non-superconducting solenodes are called cryogenic).

It would seem that with the help of superconducting solenodes it is possible to obtain enormous fields. However, this is not so - the field induction in a superconducting solenode is limited to a certain critical value $B_{\kappa p}$. At this critical field value, superconductivity disappears. The values ​​of $B_{\kappa p}$ are different for different metals and alloys. The content of $Nb_3Sn$ is now more widely used as a material for superconducting solenodes. For him $B_{\kappa p} = 30$ T (at 4.2 K). But such a field could not be obtained using a superconducting solenode from $Nb_3Sn$. Practitioners using solenodes from $Nb_3Sn$ still obtain fields $B \leqslant 20$ T.

An important contribution made to the technology of strong magnetic fields by superconducting solenodes is that $B = 10 \div 15$T fields have become available to most physics laboratories. For example, the superconducting solenoid, which was just discussed, takes up several square meters of space with all the equipment attached to it and consumes ≈ 10 liters of liquid gel per day, and the corresponding copper water-cooled solenoid requires the construction of a special pumping station.

The areas of application of superconducting solenodes will expand if superconducting compounds with large values ​​of $B_{\kappa p}$ are found.

And in conclusion, let us mention one more possibility of obtaining strong magnetic fields. Let us remember what limits the available field induction values: overheating of the solenoid and its mechanical destruction. However, both heating to melting and destruction do not. They will happen instantly (you can run on ice, even when it is so thin that it will break if you stop). Therefore, for a short period of time, such a greater current can be passed through the solenoid that it “could not withstand” during long-term operation. In such a pulsed mode, hotter magnetic fields can be obtained for a short period of time than when operating in a stationary mode.
