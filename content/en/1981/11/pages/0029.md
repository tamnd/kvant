The movement of the puck relative to a stationary observer can be represented at any moment of time as a superposition of two movements: movement relative to the center of the hoop with a speed $v_k$, directed tangentially to the hoop, and movement together with the hoop with a speed $u$, directed horizontally (Fig. 2). As can be seen from figure 2,

$$\frac{|v_y|}{|v_x| + |u|} = tg \varphi.$$ (3)

Solving equations (1) - (3) together, we find the value of the speed of the center of the hoop at the moment when the radius vector of the point at which the puck is located makes an angle $\varphi$ with the vertical:

$$|\vec{u}| = m \cos \varphi \sqrt{\frac{2Rg(1+\cos \varphi)}{(M+m)(M+m\sin^2\varphi)}}.$$

S. Krotov

F687. A steel ball located at point A of the base AB of an equilateral rectangular wedge is given a speed $v_0$ in the direction of side AO (see figure). At what values ​​of $v_0$ does the ball go from point A to point B? Wedge edge length $l$.

In the general case, the trajectory of the ball after opening from the wedge at point O will be as shown in figure - it will consist of sections of parabolas. Projections onto the OX axis of the movements of the ball over successive equal time intervals $t_1, t_2, t_3, \dots, t_n$ are related as

$$s_1: s_2: s_3: \dots: s_n = 1: 3: 5: \dots: (2n-1)$$

(since at the moment of “separation” of the ball at point O, the projection of its velocity $\vec{v}_1$ onto the OX axis is equal to zero). If during the $n$th collision with edge OB the ball hits point B, then

$$s_1 + s_2 + s_3 + \dots + s_n = s_1(1+3+5+\dots+(2n-1)) = s_1n^2 = l.$$

Let's find how $s_1$ and $v_1$ are related. The projections of the ball acceleration on the OX and OY axes are equal to $a_x = a_y = g\sqrt{2}/2$, and

$$s_1 = a_x \frac{t_1^2}{2} = g \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4} t_1^2.$$

We find the time $t_1$ of the ball’s movement before the first impact on the wedge from condition $v_1 - a_y \frac{t_1}{2} = 0$:

$$t_1 = \frac{2v_1}{a_y} = \frac{4v_1}{g\sqrt{2}}.$$

Thus,

$$s_1 = \frac{2v_1^2\sqrt{2}}{g},$$ or $v_1^2 = \frac{gl\sqrt{2}}{4n^2}$.

We find the connection between $v_1$ and $v_0$ from the law of conservation of energy:

$$\frac{mv_0^2}{2} = \frac{mv_1^2}{2} + mgl \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \Rightarrow v_0 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + gl\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2n} \sqrt{gl\sqrt{2}(4n^2 + 1)}$$

So, the ball hits point B if at point A it is given speed $|\vec{v}_0| = \frac{1}{2n} \sqrt{lg\sqrt{2}(4n^2 + 1)}$, where $n$ is a natural number. Note that

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} v_0 = \sqrt{gl\sqrt{2}}.$$

The same result from the law of conservation of energy: at $n \to \infty v_1 \to 0$, and

$$\frac{mv_0^2}{2} = mgl \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \Rightarrow v_0 = \sqrt{gl\sqrt{2}}.$$

E. Rabkin
