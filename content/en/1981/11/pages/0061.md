Illumination of the plane with projectors

1. Proof of the statement.
1. Without loss of generality, we can assume that there is at least one projector located on each side of a given angle $A$.
Let $a < \pi$. We carry out the proof by induction on $k$.
For $k=1$ the statement is obvious, and let it be true for any $j < k$. Let's prove it for $j=k$. Divide the angle $A$ and the angle symmetrical to it relative to the vertex into $k$ angles of $\frac{2\pi}{n}$ each - for convenience we call these small angles “sectors”. Let us denote the number of projectors in the $i$-th sector by $s_i(i=1, 2, ..., k)$.
All numbers $s_i$ are non-negative integers, $s_1 \geqslant 1$, $s_k \geqslant 1$ and $s_1+s_2+...+s_k=k$.
Let us take the first $m$ sectors, where $m$ is chosen according to the lemma. Their union is the angle containing $m$ projectors. They, according to the assumption of induction, can illuminate the angle symmetrical to it, and the remaining $k - m$ projectors can illuminate the residual angle. Consequently, everything symmetrical to angle $A$ will be completely illuminated.
Let now $a=\pi$ and all projectors be in the upper half-plane. Without loss of generality, we can assume that the projector $A$ is located on the boundary of the half-plane (A is the vertex of an angle of 180°). If there are no projectors in the first sector, relative the projector $A$ to the second sector, reducing the problem to the case $a < \pi$. If there are projectors, then projector $A$ is assigned to sector $k$. Then in the first and $k$th sectors the numbers of projectors are $s_1 \geqslant 1$ and $s_k \geqslant 1$, and the equality $s_1+s_2+...+s_k=k$ is satisfied, that is, the conditions of Exercise 2 are satisfied. Further we reason similarly to the previous one.
Proof of the lemma.
If $s_1=1$, then $m=1$ - otherwise $s_1>1$. If, further, $s_1+s_2=2$ and $s_1>1$, then $s_2=0$ and then $m=2$ - otherwise $s_1+s_2>2$. And so on: if at the $l$th step the equality $s_1+\dots+s_m=l$ arises, then $m=l$ and $s_m=0$, but if this equality did not arise, then $s_l>0$. However, if equality did not arise, then it necessarily arises (by condition) at the $k$th step, that is, at $m=k$, and then $s_k=0$, which contradicts the condition $s_k \geqslant 1$. This means that equality arose equal, and $m<k$.

1. From the laws of conservation of momentum and energy we obtain
$\frac{v_n}{v_1} = \left(\frac{2k}{k+1}\right)^{n-1}$ and efficiency $= \left(\frac{4k}{(k+1)^2}\right)^{n-1}$.
From this it can be seen that for a fixed $n$ the ratio $v_n/v_1$ with increasing $k$ first grows quickly and then reaches saturation; the efficiency already decreases without saturation. Therefore, there is an optimal value $k$ for every $n$, and therefore for every relation $v_n/v_1$.

To restore the thermometer, you need to create the opposite conditions - place the upper part of the thermometer above the heated one. 3. Let there be sound in one of the rooms. It is clear that through a hole in the partition, a portion of the sound power incident on the partition will be transmitted to the next room, equal to the ratio of the area of ​​the hole to the area of ​​the partition, that is, $S_\ell/S_\pi$. If the upholstery is not ideal (it allows a hundredth of the sound power to pass through), then the sound passes into the next room not only through the hole, but also through the frame itself. Thus, the ratio of sound powers projected into the next room in the second and first hearings is equal to
$$k = \frac{S_\ell/S_\pi + 0,01}{S_\pi/S_\pi} = 1 + \frac{10^{-2}}{4 \cdot 10^{-4}/4} = 101.$$

So, it is better if there is a hole in the partition than if the partition is flattened, but thin.
