Maxwell's theory of electricity and magnetism must be considered one of the most difficult physical theories. To his contemporary it seemed no less difficult than quantum mechanics - the physicist of our century. The main difficulty is that Maxwell needed a field carrier - ether.

Following Faraday, Maxwell believed that the space surrounding a charge or current has properties different from the properties of empty space, space without matter. But, like all physicists over the centuries, he was very upset that empty space has no properties, especially since empty space cannot change a property. Therefore, physicists believed that space was filled with a certain substance - ether, the properties of which differ from the properties of known substances *). The ether changes under the influence of current; the vibrations of the ether underlie the propagation of light. Reflecting on the properties of the ether, Maxwell discussed the possibility of changing the influence of the Earth's motion on the speed of light (in 1881, the experiment was carried out by Michels). Ether remained in physics for a long time. Only Einstein finally understood that the electromagnetic field does not need any special material carrier, that it itself has a property characteristic of materials - it has energy, momentum.

Thus ended another story. Maxwell's theory has not yet revealed any limits of applicability. When Einstein constructed the theory of relative theory, Maxwell's equations were included in it almost unchanged, while Newton's equations of mechanics turned out to be only approximate. Maxwell's equations included corrections originating from quantum mechanics. They describe phenomena that occur both on the scale of the Universe (distances $\sim 10^{28}$ cm) and in the microcosm (distances $\sim 10^{-16}$ cm).

So, about the history of kinetic theory. Physicists have long tried to derive formulas that describe the pressure of a gas on the walls of a vessel. It turned out that this is not so easy to do. Since the time of Bernoulli, to describe the gas pressure on the walls of a vessel, a model has been used in which gas particles do not collide with each other, but fly from wall to wall with the same (in magnitude) speed, without changing it even when moving away from the wall. This, of course, did not correspond to the real state of affairs. In the middle of the 19th century, Clausius began to consider mutual collisions of molecules. He introduced the concept of path length $\lambda$ - the distance traveled by molecules between two collisions. Clausius considered this quantity to be a unique characteristic of gas. Collisions of molecules occur frequently, and the molecule cannot fly throughout the entire vessel, but remains, as it were, locked in a small volume, the size of which is approximately $\lambda^3$.
