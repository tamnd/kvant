CHESS COMPETITION

COMPUTER AND CHESS CODE

When mathematicians (the creators of Kansa) taught computers to study the endgame, they analyzed some well-known positions. And this is what became clear.

One of the most difficult endings is "queen with knight's pawn versus queen." The machine examined positions with a white pawn on the seventh rank and now can accurately say for each of them whether White will win or not, and if they win, then in how many moves. Here is one of two positions that deserved their special attention.

Black's move. White will win

White really wins here, but... in the chess code there are the following rules: the game ends in a draw if both sides made 50 moves, during which one piece was not captured and not a single pawn made a move. To this point in the latest edition of the code there is the following interpretation: “For positions “king and two knights against a king and pawn” the number of 50 moves increases to 75 moves. It may be increased for other specific positions only on the condition that this number and these positions are clearly indicated in the tournament or match regulations.”

In the above position, with both sides playing for 58 moves, there is maneuvering and only on the 59th move are pawns captured or promoted.

So, the positions discovered by the computer show that the number of 50 moves should be increased in the code and for the endgame "king, queen and pawn against king and queen". We can say that this is the first time in history when a computer is combined into a chess code! (The endgame “king and two knights against a king and pawn” was studied many years ago and without the help of a computer).

In practice, a significant number of queen endings are found in rook endings. They are one of the common types of such endings - “rook with pawn against rook” - and were also commissioned to study the computer. Having spent 60 hours of computer time, she quickly completed her task and can now evaluate any position of this type, regardless of the position of the pawn. In this case, the machine can be established by any facts. For example, she discovered a position that would be won no earlier than the 60th move: White - Kc3, Rlc4, pb2; Black - Kc4, Rd1 (Black to move). By the way, the pawn here can be removed from its place only on the 32nd move after long and difficult maneuvering.

To quickly assess the emerging endgame positions, it is useful to have a schematic and winning zone for the location of any one piece with a fixed location of the remaining pieces. The computer also helps here.

In this “position” it is Black’s move (the black king can stand on any square of the board available to him). In some positions of Black's king, White will win, in others - not. The results of the analysis carried out by the computer are shown directly on figure. If there is no number on the square for the king, then the position is worthless; if the number n is written on this field, then White will win in n moves. We see that if the black king is cut off along the “f” line, then the only saving square for him is g4 (not counting the squares on which the king simply takes the white rook).
