Maxwell's pendulum

“An important part of our responsibility is to set up creative experiments, to encourage others to set them up and to develop in every possible way the ideas they illuminate. The simpler the material of illustrative experience and the more instilled in the student they are, the more clearly he will understand the idea that this experience should illustrate. The educational value of such experiments is often inversely proportional to the complexity of the device."
These words of James Clerk Maxwell, the great English physicist, whose tenth birthday is celebrated this year, seem to be especially intended for Laboratory «Kvant». In this article we want to turn to the device that bears his name - the so-called Maxwell matrix. Easy to make, it is often demonstrated in lectures on mechanics and allows one to identify a number of interesting patterns of motion of a rigid body.

Look at the photo of such a matrix (Fig. 1). This is a disk mounted on an axis to which two threads are tied (their upper ends are fixed). Twist the threads around the axis and the disk will rise. Now release the matrifik - and it begins to make a periodic movement: first the disk lowers, the threads unwind, the disk rotates faster and faster; having reached the lowest point and continuing to heal by inertia, the disk changes the direction of its movement and rises into the axis, the movement of the disk slows down; at the highest point the matrific stops for a moment and again begins its downward movement, etc.

We would like to immediately warn readers that the matrix will fluctuate steadily and for quite a long time if several conditions are met. The disk itself should be relatively heavy (m>100 g) and large (dynamir 5-8 cm), the axis should be thin (dynamir 4-5 mm) and “weightless”, and the threads should be strong and sufficiently long (~0.5 m). Make sure that the disk is strictly in the middle of the axis and is perpendicular to it, and that the threads are of the same length.

So, let's begin our experiments. Observations of the oscillations of various magnets give the first result - the period of oscillation, that is, the total time of descent and ascent of the magnet, does not depend on the mass of the disk, but depends on its radius, and this dependence is almost directly proportional.

Further, it is easy to see that over time the oscillations fade quite noticeably, and the smaller their amplitude becomes (the maximum height of rise) and the lower the speed of rotation of the disk, the less stable the magnet behaves. It is also interesting to do the following experiment: by setting some possibilities (that is, removing the magnet from the source state), monitor their attenuation as the magnet moves. If, for example, at the moment of launch, you slightly rotate the disk in the horizontal plane, then when it falls, it will quickly return to a relatively stable position. But if you deflect the magnet in the plane of its rotation, then practically until the very end of the movement both oscillations are maintained: up and down and in the plane of rotation.

Let us now try to explain some of our experimental results. For simplicity, let us assume that the entire mass of the disk in question is concentrated on its surface, that is, that the magnet is not a disk, but a hoop.

Let's denote the radius of the hoop by $R$, and the radius of the axis by $r$ (Fig. 3). Let's look at the magnet at the moment when it has dropped to a distance $h$ from the top position. Each point of the hoop, for example point $A$, simultaneously participates in two movements: in a post-rotation movement together with the axis $O$ down at a speed $\vec{v}_0 = \vec{v}_{\text{пост}}$ and a rotational one at a speed $\vec{v}_1 = \vec{v}_{\text{пост}} + \vec{v}_{\text{пост}}$.

The thread erodes without being pierced, which means that the instantaneous speed of point $B$ is zero. Therefore, the movement of the magnet can also be represented as a series of successive rotations around the instantaneous center of rotation $B$. Then the equality is true

$$\frac{v_0}{v_A} = \frac{r}{R-r}, \text{ или } \frac{v_{\text{пост}}}{v_{\text{bp}} - v_{\text{пост}}} = \frac{r}{R-r} \tag{1}$$

(we denote the module of a particular speed with the letter $v$). From here

$$\frac{v_{\text{пост}}}{v_{\text{bp}}} = \frac{r}{R}.$$

Now let's use the law of conservation of energy:

$$mgh = \frac{mv_{\text{пост}}^2}{2} + \frac{mv_{\text{bp}}^2}{2} \tag{2}$$

We presented kinetic energy (the right side of equality (2)) in the form of two terms - the energy of translational motion and the energy of rotational motion of the gravity vector*).

From kinematics it is known that

$$v_{\text{пост}}^2 = 2ah, \tag{3}$$

where $a$ is the acceleration module of the translational motion of the magnet.

*) The possibility of such a partition can be proven strictly, but intuitively this is clear.

Thus, from equation (1) - (3) we find:

$$a = \frac{g}{1 + (R/r)^2}.$$

In our case $R/r \gg 1$ (under this condition the requirement of “weightlessness” of the axis is met), therefore we can assume that

$$a = g \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2.$$

If the length of the unwinding thread is $l$, then $l = at^2/2$ and the oscillation period of the matrix is ​​equal to

$$T = 2t = 2\sqrt{\frac{2l}{a}} = 2\frac{R}{r}\sqrt{\frac{2l}{g}}.$$

For disk, formulas (4) and (5) are transformed accordingly (see “Additional”):

$$a = 2g \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2,$$

$$T = 2\frac{R}{r}\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}.$$

Now we can compare the results of calculations and experiments. From (4') and (5') it is clear that both the acceleration $a$ and the period $T$ do not depend on the mass of the disk, but depend on the ratio of the radii $R/r$: the larger $R$ (compared to $r$), the smaller the acceleration and, accordingly, the longer the oscillation period. This reality is consistent with experiment. But if you carried out the experiments carefully, you will notice that the calculated period (5') is less than the experimental one. This, as already mentioned, is due to attenuation. What do you think - where exactly does the most detailed error, the greatest error occur?

Finally, one can continue with another very effective experiment with the Maxwell matrix, but for this it is necessary to call everything (Fig. 4). First, with the matrix stationary, bring everything into balance, and then release the matrix. The balance will be disrupted: the matrix will seem to become lighter. Why? Explain it yourself.

Additional

For those who are familiar with integral calculus, we present the derivation of the formula for the kinetic energy of a rotating disk, from which it will be easy to obtain formulas (4') and (5').

Divide a disk with a radius of $R$ into $n$ thin wheels with a width of $\Delta x$ each (Fig. 5). The kinetic energy of one wheel with radius $x$ and width $\Delta x$ is equal to

$$\Delta E_k = \left( \varrho 2\pi xb \Delta x \right) \frac{\omega^2 x^2}{2},$$

where $\varrho$ is the density of the disk material, $b$ is its thickness, $\omega$ is the angular velocity of rotation, and the expression in parentheses is the mass of the selected ring.

Fig. 5.

The kinetic energy of the entire disk is approximately equal to the sum of the kinetic energies of the individual wheels:

$$E_k \approx \sum \Delta E_k.$$

If we go to the limit as $n$ tends to infinity, the approximate equality becomes exact:

$$E_k = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum \Delta E_k =$$

$$= \int_0^R \varrho 2\pi xb \frac{\omega^2 x^2}{2} dx =$$

$$= \frac{\varrho \pi b \omega^2 R^4}{4} = \frac{\varrho \pi R^2 b \omega^2 R^2}{4} = \frac{m \omega^2 R^2}{4}.$$

The kinetic energy of a rotating hoop of the same mass is $mv^2/2 = m\omega^2 R^2/2$. Consequently, the kinetic energy of the disk is two times less.
