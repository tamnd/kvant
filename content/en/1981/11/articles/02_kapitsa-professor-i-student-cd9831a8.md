P. Kapitsa

Professor and student

Our traditional evening, where those who have graduated from the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology and those who plan to graduate from it gather, is divided into two parts. The first part is called trade and is devoted to the life and activities of our institute. In the second part we look at our wonderful independence, meet old friends and have fun. I have to take part in the first part of this program, which is less attractive than the second, but we must remember, comrades, that a good lunch always consists of a hearty roast, and only after it has been served does it give us comfort, and this roast cannot be neglected, we must treat it with all seriousness. Of course, for me, as a cook, this is not such an easy task - to make you a delicious roast in the 15-20 minutes that are given for the performance, and to talk about our affairs so that it is heartfelt and so that you do not deserve it. We have, however, a number of questions related to the work of our institute that should be of interest to all of us. These are the questions I would like to talk to you about.

You all know well that fiseh was created about twenty years ago. The main idea behind the creation of this institute was simple and very visible. Science has developed extremely quickly both here in the Soviet Union and in other countries; Many scientific institutes were created, these institutes accepted the best scientists, and all “big science” was concentrated in these institutes. The universities were destitute: they lost teaching staff, professors, as well as the equipment on which students studied. Therefore, students did not have the opportunity to attend scientific work at the university, and they had to retrain at other institutes before being prepared for scientific work.

Academician Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa - twice Hero of Social Labor, Nobel and State Prize laureate, director of the Institute of Physical Problems named after. S.I. Vavilova Academy of Sciences of the USSR.

The article, which is a speech by P. L. Kapitsa at the evening of graduates of the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology in 1963, is reprinted with slight abbreviations from the collection “Lenin. Science. Youth", published in 1980 by the Nauka publishing house.

And yet such a system, of course, is not lazy. Just imagine that at the institute, together with the professors, there is one movie camera and only students and film fans go there. It will be an exceptionally boring and dark ending that you won't treat like your alma mater. However, this is not the point. They say that sooner or later students will somehow adapt to this, somehow survive it. The teachers themselves will react much worse to this change. The fact is that they completely forget about another function of higher educational completion - to teach not only students, but to teach professors and teachers themselves.

A good scientist always learns himself when he teaches. Firstly, he tests his knowledge, because only by clearly obliging the other person can he be sure that he himself understands the question. Secondly, when you look for a form of clear description of a particular issue, new ideas often come up. Thirdly, those often ridiculous questions that students ask after a lecture exclusively stimulate thought and force them to look at a phenomenon that was approached in a standard way from a completely new point of view, and this also helps creative thinkers.

And finally, students know better and have a broader knowledge of physics issues than the teacher. The teacher, as a specialist, has a narrow approach; he does not have a broad approach. Students have a much broader approach. And when a student talks to a teacher, the teacher learns a lot from the student.

This is why young scientists need to engage in teaching activities. A good university is one that makes it possible to develop the talents of teachers as easily as the talents of their students.

To show that these are not general phrases, I will give you a number of examples of how teaching led to great discoveries. These examples are so striking that they, it seems to me, strongly confirm this idea.

One of the most classic examples is well known - this is D.I. Mendeleev and his periodic table. Mendeleev was looking for a way to more easily teach students the properties of elements, so that these properties could be perceived according to a certain system. He distributed the elements into cards, folded these cards in different orders, and finally found that the cards, laid out in the form of a periodic table, represented a regular system. On March 1, 1869, the table was published in a separate edition and a little later included as an appendix in the second issue of “Fundamentals of Chemistry.” Thus, the periodic system of elements basically arose from the pedagogical activities of Mendeleev as a professor at St. Petersburg University.

The second case, a little earlier, relates to mathematics. At the beginning of the 19th century. The Russian government decided that all officials should have a secondary education. Those officials who did not have a certificate of maturity were required to obtain one. To make this easier for them, courses were created that prepared them for the matriculation exams. One of the geometry teachers of such courses was N.I. Lobachevsky. He was then 24-25 years old. He was very young, and he was taught principles in geometry included by the officials he represented. And they could not understand where the axiom about the disjointness of two parallel lines comes from.

Lobachevsky struggled for a long time to give suitable obligations, but became convinced that such obligations did not exist. He realized that it was possible to construct a geometry in which the lines always intersect. This is how its non-Euclidean geometry was created. Thus

So, he found a new field of mathematics, which, as you know, existed and was to play a fundamental role in modern physics.

I can give another example that the famous physicist Debye told me about. Debye was a teacher, a professor in Zurich. He had a student, also a teacher, Schrödinger, then a completely unknown young man. Debye became acquainted with the work of de Broglie, in which de Broglie, who, as you know, put forward the hypothesis about the existence of the wave structure of the electron, showed that under certain interference conditions it is possible to replace the motion of the electron with wave motion. The idea of ​​the equivalence of wave motion and quantum processes, wave motion and corpuscular motion was perceived very negatively by a number of physicists. Schrödinger also reacted negatively to her. When Debye asked him to tell young people about de Broglie's work, Schrödinger initially refused. Then, when Debye, taking advantage of his position as a professor, again allowed him to do this, Schrödinger agreed and began to look for how de Broglie's ideas could be explained in the most complete and accurate mathematical form. And when he spoke about the work of de Broglie in the presentation that he considered the most accurate, Debye told him: “Listen, you have found a new wonderful type of equation, which is fundamental in modern physics!” Thus, as a result of pedagogical activity, the wave equation was found - the basic equation of modern physics.

Many more similar examples could be given, but it seems to me quite obvious that if educational activity is fruitful in such serious fundamental issues, then it is undoubtedly fruitful in simpler things; it often has a fruitful influence on modern science and on modern scientists. Therefore, higher educational institutions should be considered not only as institutions in which young scientists are trained, but also as a place where scientific talents and already formed scientists are developed. Educational institutions should be so organized that this opportunity is widely available to teaching staff.

We don't always recognize this. Until then, for example, in universities and other higher educational institutions it was considered reasonable for part of the staff to be engaged in scientific activities, and part of the staff to be engaged in teaching. It is precisely in higher educational institutions that there should be such a system that it is based on scientists who spend most of their time engaged in teaching activities. Only then will the educational institution fulfill all its functions - teach students and teach teaching staff.

Therefore, replacing professors with film cameras was completely inconvenient; it made impossible the second part of the activities of a higher educational institution, which, undoubtedly, will develop in the near future and which we will pay attention to. Should pay more attention.

I told you about this because all of you, young scientists, will soon be leaving physics and working in research institutes in one capacity or another. If you want to continue to grow as scientists, not grow old and improve your knowledge, you need to not lose contact with the next younger generation, teach this younger generation and learn from them, develop your knowledge. If you take a break from teaching young people, you will immediately begin to grow old and immediately begin to lag behind science.

I want to convey this little testament to you on my own behalf, because I consider it very important.
