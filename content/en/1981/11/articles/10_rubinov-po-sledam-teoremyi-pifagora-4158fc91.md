Following the Pythagorean Theorem

In the current geometry textbook, the Pythagorean theorem “The square of the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle is equal to the sum of the squares of its legs” is proved “algebraically” by calculation (“Geometry 6-8”, paragraph 67).

Euclid proves it "geometric". He constructed squares on the sides of a right triangle and proved that the sum of the areas of the squares on the sides is equal to the area of ​​the square on the hypotenuse. We will now present two proofs of the Pythagorean theorem that use this idea, and then present many other figures for which the same “Pythagorean correspondence” holds as for squares.

Let's generalize the Pythagorean theorem

So, in a right triangle $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$,

where $a, b, c$ are its quadets and hypotenuse. Note now that if we have figures $A, B$ and $C$ whose areas $S_A, S_B$ and $S_C$ are $ka^2, kb^2$ and $kc^2$, respectively, then $S_A + S_B = S_C$. In particular, the “Pythagorean correspondence” is satisfied for areas of similar figures constructed on the sides of a right triangle*). (This note may be useful for calculating the area of ​​the figure that we will draw below.)
