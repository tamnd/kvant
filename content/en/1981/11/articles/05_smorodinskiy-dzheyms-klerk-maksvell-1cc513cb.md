The life of Maxwell, probably the greatest physicist of the past century, appears as an interweaving of occupied stories. Each story has had a profound impact on our understanding of nature.

If we are now talking about a physical field (electromagnetic, gravitational or some other), then the meaning of this concept was introduced by Maxwell. Molecular (or statistical) physics acquired a clear meaning after the work of Maxwell. The first theoretical work on cybernetics, “On the Governor” (as Maxwell calls the regulator of machines), belongs to Maxwell. The color theory of existence is closely related to his work. One of the very old problems about the nature of the rings of Saturn was solved by Maxwell.

The story of the stakes of Saturn is the simplest. That's where we'll start.

In 1609, Galileo made the first telephone using a Dutch smoking pipe he saw in Venice. Having built a pipe (he called it a “perspective”), Galileo looked at him through it. What he saw there shocked him. Jupiter had four Moons, mountains appeared on the Moon, and spots were found on the Sun. But the most amazing things were the visible shoots. After some time, the shoots disappeared, and Galileo could not unravel their nature. Only in 1659 did Huygens realize that Saturn was surrounded by rings.

Another 100 years have passed. Astronomers began to think: why don’t the rings fall on Saturn, why don’t they destroy under the influence of their own gravity?

At the end of the 18th century, Laplace took up this task. The conclusion he came to was of little comfort: in order for the rings to rotate around Saturn like solid solid structures and at the same time not collapse

Theoretical concepts turned out to be insufficient to describe the new observations. In 1855, a competition (Adams Prize*) was announced in Cambridge to solve the riddle of Saturn's wheels. Maxwell, who at that time held a chair in Aberdeen (in Scotland), began a systematic study of this problem. He consistently examined three cases: 1) - liquid rings; 2) - solid rings; 3) - the rings consist of separate pieces.

The conclusions reached by Maxwell are as follows. Dense rings, even if they satisfy Laplace's conditions, will be destroyed by waves propagating along the circumference of the ring. No rigid or deformable structure (liquid rings) can rotate stably around Saturn.

The only possibility remains a ring consisting of separate relatively small pieces, each of which rotates at the first cosmic (Saturnian) speed - along the orbit of the satellite, as we would say now.

This was the solution to the problem. The Adams Prize was awarded to Maxwell.

“Work, finish, publish” was Maxwell’s motto. In 1855 he began work on the problem of the rings of Saturn, and in 1859 a complete study of the problem was published in Cambridge.

As was said, different stories from Maxwell’s life will intertwine with each other. In the story of color vision, the characteristic features of Maxwell's scientific method are clearly visible. Almost all of his research contained mandatory stages: the creation of a visual geometric model and the use of simple analogies.

Here are the words of Maxwell about what a physical analogy means, what it means to build a model: the task is to “find a physical analogy that would allow the mind to embrace the results of previous research, or the latest of any theory based on the physical principles of the science from which the analogy was borrowed, so as not to be distracted from the very subject of the study, breaking down the analytical subtleties, and not to be clear of the truth because the hypothesis you like.”

Such a formula, full of only warnings, is completely equivalent in the Japanese solutions to Maxwell's main problem - the theory of the electromagnetic field. It was Maxwell who managed to see in Faraday’s works what no one else could see. In Faraday's power lines, he saw a new physical object - a field. But for this it was necessary to go through a difficult stage of research - the stage of a primitive model.

Again the story begins with the works of the Great Physicist. Faraday suggested that the action at a distance between charges and currents is transmitted using lines of force connecting different charges (electric lines) or rings surrounding points (magnetic lines). These lines tend to move along their length (attraction) and push away from their neighbors. By the movement of such lines, Faraday was able to explain the results of a huge number of his own and other people’s experiments.

Maxwell really liked Faraday's idea: the visual picture of elastic threads turned out to be a successful analogy. It was necessary to find a mathematical apparatus to describe such a mechanical model. Maxwell recalls the work of Thomson (future Lord Kelvin) on how to fit together the equation for the gravitational potential and the equation for heat distribution. Both phenomena can be described using a system of surfaces on which there is potential or temperature. Thus, the temperature in the space around a heated ball drops according to the same law as the potential of the gravitational field of a massive ball.

Based on a similar analogy, Maxwell begins by describing the electric and magnetic fields using a system of lines, arbitrary at each point in space and indicating the direction of action of forces. In order to describe the magnitude of the forces, Maxwell takes one more step, which must be indicated, like in a chess game, with the sign “!!”. He replaces drain lines with tubes, and in order to make the tubes elastic, he assumes that liquid is tapping along them.

The concept of electric and magnetic fluids was not new. Thus, even before Faraday, he tried to describe the phenomena occurring with charges and magnets. But no one tried to treat them as objects worthy of mathematical description, and no one combined them with Faraday force lines. Faraday realized that in the presence of fields, the body goes into some “electric state” (this is how he imagined the action of the field). Maxwell tried to find mathematical laws that describe this state. In his first work on electromagnetism, “On Faradian Lines of Force” (made in 1855-1856, and published in 1861), he still sadly writes: “... I still hope, when visiting the properties of elastic bodies and the movement of viscous liquids, to find a method that would allow us to give for the electrical state a certain mechanical image that can lead to general ossification.” The next work “On physics”

With unimaginative skill, Maxwell extracts from the model only what is needed for the future theory, and does not in any way use the specific properties of the mechanisms that he filled the space with (this is the meaning of the prevention that he wrote about). “The theory that I will propose can be called the theory of the electromagnetic field, because it deals with the space surrounding electric and magnetic bodies, and it can also be called the dynamic theory, since it admits that in this space there is matter in motion, through which the observed electromagnetic phenomena are produced. An electromagnetic field is a part of space that contains and surrounds bodies, which is in an electric or magnetic state.”

Practical electrodynamics was built. It was finally formalized in the famous two-volume Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism, published in 1873.

Only the first of the work on electrodynamics was done in Aberdeen. In 1860, due to the reorganization of the University (two colleges were merged into one), Maxwell was forced to leave Aberdeen. He accepted the invitation and took a chair at King's College in London, where he remained for five years, after which he did not engage in teaching activities until 1871.

Taking over the chair in London, Maxwell addressed the students with an introductory lecture in which he talked about how the separate branches of physics are intertwined with each other and how important it is that the doctrines of tellite and electricity reach the same degree of completion as mechanics has achieved.

“In our country,” said Maxwell, “natural philosophy is a collection of sciences consisting of two main groups (Newton’s classic work is called “The Mathematical Science of Natural Philosophy” - Ya. S.). The first group includes mechanics and covers the general theory of motion and equilibrium, as well as applications of the principles of mechanics to the study of natural phenomena.

The second group is usually called physics. It now includes the study of light, heat, electricity and magnetism and all such phenomena which we associate with more general principles, although we cannot yet reduce them to the simple result of known mechanical actions.”

Maxwell's depth of application to the laws of nature was developed. In 1868, he published the work “On the method of direct comparison of electrostatic and electromagnetic forces; with a substitution about the electromagnetic theory of light."

The reasoning in this work is perceived as completely modern. If you do not strive to convey them completely accurately, then Maxwell’s idea can be stated simply.

The interaction of electric charges manifests itself in two ways. Two stationary charges act on each other according to Coulomb's law. On the other hand, the current in a conductor, which is nothing more than a flow of charges, creates a magnetic field. This magnetic field can act on another current-carrying conductor. As a result, as is well known, two straight conductors with currents flowing parallel to each other become adjacent. Since the conductors are not charged, there is no electrical force between them.

Formulas describing the interaction of charges and currents have been known for a long time. Two charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ at a distance $r$ from each other interact according to Coulomb’s law:

$$F_q = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2},$$

where $k$ is a constant coefficient (equal to $9 \cdot 10^9 \frac{H M^2}{K \pi}$ in SI). The force with which a straight-line current $I_1$ acts on a parallel current $I_2$ flowing along a segment of conductor of length $l$, located at a distance $r$, is equal

$$F_l = a \frac{2 I_1 I_2}{r l},$$

where $a$ is a constant coefficient (equal to $10^{-7} \frac{H c^2}{K \pi^2}$ in SI).

In Maxwell's time, physicists used two systems of units to measure electrical quantities. The unit for the charge was chosen so that two charges $q_1 = q_2 = 1$ repel at a distance $r = 1$ (1 cm) with a force $F_q = 1$ (1 dyne = $10^{-5}$ H). Such a system was called electrical (we will denote it E). In another system, a unit also two currents $I_1 = I_2 = 1$ were pressed at a distance $r = 1$ (1 cm) with a force ($F_l = 2$ (2 dynes). This system was called electromagnetic (we will denote it M). In the system $E = k = 1$, in the system $M = 1$. It is not difficult to understand that it is impossible to come up with a system of units in which both conditions ($k = 1, a = 1$) would be simultaneously satisfied. The laws of interaction between currents and stationary charges are different, and they cannot be changed by choosing a system of units.

But current $I$ and charge $q$ can be connected to each other. The current is equal to the charge passing through the cross section of the conductor per unit time. In the system $E$ the quantity $q^2$ has dimension $[F] [L^2]$ ($[L]$ is the dimension of distance). The squared current $I^2$ in this system will have a dimension $[F] [L^2] [T^{-2}] [T]$—the dimension of time. In the system $M$ the dimension is $I^2 - [F] [\frac{1}{a}]$. From the last two correspondences it is clear that the coefficient $a$ in the formula for $F_M$ is dimensional and its dimension is $[L^{-2} T^2]$. In other words, the quantity $a$ has the dimension of the square of the speed (Maxwell began his work with these remarks).

In order to bring the numerical values ​​of charge units into contact in the system $E$ and $M$, you can, for example, charge two capacitors with charges $q_1 = q_2 = 1$ ($q = 1$ - in the system $E$; you can take any known charge in the experiment) and discharge these capacitors through two parallel conductors, changing the charging time (this makes it possible to determine the current). The magnitude of the interaction force between the conductors, very/no, determines how many times the charge $q_E = 1$ in the system $E$ is less than the charge $q_M = 1$ in the system $M$.

The ratio $q_E:q_M$ was changed back in 1856 by Weber and Kohlrausch, obtaining $(3,107 \cdot 10^8 \text{ m/c})^2$, a value close to the square of the speed of light. Is this a coincidence? If not, what is the relationship between the correspondence of the units $q_E$ and $q_M$ and the speed of light? There were no answers to these questions before Maxwell.

Maxwell's theory of electricity and magnetism must be considered one of the most difficult physical theories. To his contemporary it seemed no less difficult than quantum mechanics - the physicist of our century. The main difficulty is that Maxwell needed a field carrier - ether.

Following Faraday, Maxwell believed that the space surrounding a charge or current has properties different from the properties of empty space, space without matter. But, like all physicists over the centuries, he was very upset that empty space has no properties, especially since empty space cannot change a property. Therefore, physicists believed that space was filled with a certain substance - ether, the properties of which differ from the properties of known substances *). The ether changes under the influence of current; the vibrations of the ether underlie the propagation of light. Reflecting on the properties of the ether, Maxwell discussed the possibility of changing the influence of the Earth's motion on the speed of light (in 1881, the experiment was carried out by Michels). Ether remained in physics for a long time. Only Einstein finally understood that the electromagnetic field does not need any special material carrier, that it itself has a property characteristic of materials - it has energy, momentum.

Thus ended another story. Maxwell's theory has not yet revealed any limits of applicability. When Einstein constructed the theory of relative theory, Maxwell's equations were included in it almost unchanged, while Newton's equations of mechanics turned out to be only approximate. Maxwell's equations included corrections originating from quantum mechanics. They describe phenomena that occur both on the scale of the Universe (distances $\sim 10^{28}$ cm) and in the microcosm (distances $\sim 10^{-16}$ cm).

There are also poems by Maxwell, serveral and funny - he loved to joke. We will ignore the work on the movement of the Earth’s rotation axis and various other “little things” that can in themselves ensure fame.

So, about the history of kinetic theory. Physicists have long tried to derive formulas that describe the pressure of a gas on the walls of a vessel. It turned out that this is not so easy to do. Since the time of Bernoulli, to describe the gas pressure on the walls of a vessel, a model has been used in which gas particles do not collide with each other, but fly from wall to wall with the same (in magnitude) speed, without changing it even when moving away from the wall. This, of course, did not correspond to the real state of affairs. In the middle of the 19th century, Clausius began to consider mutual collisions of molecules. He introduced the concept of path length $\lambda$ - the distance traveled by molecules between two collisions. Clausius considered this quantity to be a unique characteristic of gas. Collisions of molecules occur frequently, and the molecule cannot fly throughout the entire vessel, but remains, as it were, locked in a small volume, the size of which is approximately $\lambda^3$. Maxwell tried to correct the imperfect gas model. First of all, he considered the problem of the velocity distribution of molecules. After this, he showed that in a gas, during collisions, momentum is transferred from molecule to molecule, just as iron is transferred in a race. Although the molecules themselves do not, as a rule, go far from their place of interruption (just as a runner does not leave his stage), during collisions a flow of momentum arises, which reaches the walls. Heat transfer in an unevenly heated gas or equalization of the concentration of a mixture occurs in the same way - the process of diffusion. The theory of transport processes is a very important work of Maxwell, which had a great influence on the development of the kinetic theory of gases.

For detailed calculations of the properties of gases, Maxwell proposed that forces act between molecules, which decrease as the fifth power of the distance between molecules. Such a relatively simple model allowed Maxwell to calculate (of course, not very accurately) many characteristics of the gas.

Maxwell brought the ability to use models to perfection. And he always said that “an analogy should help the imagination, but not replace a physical phenomenon.”

In 1860, Maxwell published the work “Illustrations to the Dynamic Theory of Gases,” in which he gives a formula for the distribution of molecular velocities. This is the famous formula of the Maxwell distribution, which occupies a central place in molecular physics. The main thing that Maxwell understood is that in systems such as gas, the distribution of particles by speed (or, more precisely, by the magnitude of kinetic energy) depends only on temperature. Breaking down the question of collisions of molecules, Maxwell showed that friction in gases does not depend on pressure. This conclusion was received with great distrust, and Maxwell, together with his wife, tested it experimentally.

Maxwell studied kinetic theory until the end of his days. He died on November 5, 1879, aged 48. He spent the last year in Cambridge, was the first director of the famous Cavendish LAB, named after the 18th century English physicist Cavendish, whom Maxwell highly valued and whose collected works he prepared for publication.

Maxwell's life is an example of a striving movement towards victory, a life lived under the motto “Work, finish, publish.”
