M672. Let $a$ be a natural number such that $2^a - 2$ is divisible by $a$ (for example, $a = 3$). Let us define the sequence $(x_n)$ by the conditions
$$x_1 = a, x_{k+1} = 2^{xk} - 1.$$
Prove that $2^{xk} - 2$ is divisible by $x_k$ for any $k$.

It is easy to notice that triangles $ABC$ and $ADC$ are equal in size: $S_{ABC} = S_{ADC}$ - they have a common base $AC$ and heights equal in length, lowered onto this base. Therefore $\frac{1}{2} ab \sin \widehat{B} = \frac{1}{2} cd \sin (180^\circ - \widehat{B})$, that is $ab = cd$. Adding (1) and (2), we obtain what is required.

R. Mazov

Let us prove this statement using the method of mathematical induction.
For $k = 1$ it is true according to the conditions. Let us assume that $2^{xk} - 2$ is divisible by $x_k$ and prove that $2^{xk+1} - 2$ is divisible by $x_{k+1}$.
Let $x_{k+1} - 1 = 2^{xk} - 2 = mx_k$, where $m > 1$ is a natural number. Then
$$2^{xk+1} - 2 = 2(2^{xk+1} - 1) = 2(2^{mx_k} - 1) = 2(2^{xk} - 1)(2^{(m-1)x_k} + 2^{(m-2)x_k} + \dots + 1) = Mx_k + 1$$
where $M$ is a natural number, which is what we needed to prove.

V. Janous

F685. Between the wall and the cube of mass $M=10$, a ball of mass $m=0,1$ g flies on a smooth control table. This speed at the beginning, when the cube accumulated, was $v_0=100$ m/s. Find the speed of the cube at the moment when it is 2 times farther from the wall than at the beginning.

We will assume that with each impact of the ball on the cube, the speed of the cube changes by a very small amount and remains many times less than the speed of the ball.

Let $x$ be the distance from the cube to the wall, $u$ the speed of the cube, $v$ the speed of the ball. Then the time between two successive collisions is $\tau=2x/v$. With each collision, the cube receives an impulse of $\Delta p=2mv$ (we taught that $u\ll v$). The speed of the ball per blow changes by $\Delta v=-2u$ (this is very/no, if we consider the movement in the frame of reference associated with the cube). To simplify the calculations, we assume that as a result of collisions, the velocities $u$ and $v$ change not jumpwise, but smoothly. Then the “average” acceleration of the ball is $v'=\frac{\Delta v}{\tau}=-\frac{uv}{x}$. Considering that $u=x'$, we rewrite this equality:

$$v'x+x'v=0.$$

Note that the following expression is equal to the product of the product $vx$; the fact that this product is zero means that the quantity $vx$ remains during construction. Hence,

$$vx=v_0x_0,$$

where $x_0$ is the distance between the cube and the wall at the beginning of the process. At $x=2x_0$ the speed of the ball is $v_0/2$. We find the speed of the cube at this moment from the law of conservation of energy:

$$\frac{mv_0^2}{2}=\frac{m(v_0/2)^2}{2}+\frac{Mu^2}{2} \Rightarrow u=\frac{v_0}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3m}{M}} \approx 0,27 \text{ м/с.}$$

F686. Thin hoops of mass $M$ and radius $R$ are placed on a horizontal plane. Along a smooth channel passing inside the hoop, a small puck of mass $m$ slides from the top point without an initial speed. Determine the speed of the center of the hoop at the moment when the puck is at the point of the hoop $A$, the radius vector of which makes an angle $\varphi$ with vertical. At the initial moment, the hoops will accumulate. There is no friction between the hoops and the plane.

The forces acting on the hoop-puck system are the gravity force and the normal reaction force from the plane. Both of these forces are directed along the vertical. Consequently, the center of mass of the system does not move in the horizontal direction.

Since there is no connection between the hoop and the plane, the hoop moves forward. According to the law of conservation of momentum, at any moment in time

$$M|u|=m|v_x|,$$

where $u$ and $v_x$ are horizontal projections of the velocities of the hoop and puck (and $u=|\vec{u}|$). Since $v_x$ periodically changes sign, then $u$ “synchronously” changes sign. The general nature of the movement of the hoop is as follows: the puck in sections $BC$ and $BE$ (Fig. 1) - the center of the hoop moves to the right; the puck on sections $CD$ and $DE$ - the center of the hoop moves to the left.

The speeds of the puck $\vec{v}$ and the hoop $\vec{u}$ are related by the law of conservation of energy:

$$mgR(1+\cos \varphi)=\frac{mv^2}{2}+\frac{Mu^2}{2},$$

where $\varphi$ is the angle that the radius vector of the point at which the puck is located at a given moment (for given values ​​$\vec{v}$ and $\vec{u}$) makes with the vertical.

The movement of the puck relative to a stationary observer can be represented at any moment of time as a superposition of two movements: movement relative to the center of the hoop with a speed $v_k$, directed tangentially to the hoop, and movement together with the hoop with a speed $u$, directed horizontally (Fig. 2). As can be seen from figure 2,

$$\frac{|v_y|}{|v_x| + |u|} = tg \varphi.$$ (3)

Solving equations (1) - (3) together, we find the value of the speed of the center of the hoop at the moment when the radius vector of the point at which the puck is located makes an angle $\varphi$ with the vertical:

$$|\vec{u}| = m \cos \varphi \sqrt{\frac{2Rg(1+\cos \varphi)}{(M+m)(M+m\sin^2\varphi)}}.$$

F687. A steel ball located at point A of the base AB of an equilateral rectangular wedge is given a speed $v_0$ in the direction of side AO (see figure). At what values ​​of $v_0$ does the ball go from point A to point B? Wedge edge length $l$.

In the general case, the trajectory of the ball after opening from the wedge at point O will be as shown in figure - it will consist of sections of parabolas. Projections onto the OX axis of the movements of the ball over successive equal time intervals $t_1, t_2, t_3, \dots, t_n$ are related as

$$s_1: s_2: s_3: \dots: s_n = 1: 3: 5: \dots: (2n-1)$$

(since at the moment of “separation” of the ball at point O, the projection of its velocity $\vec{v}_1$ onto the OX axis is equal to zero). If during the $n$th collision with edge OB the ball hits point B, then

$$s_1 + s_2 + s_3 + \dots + s_n = s_1(1+3+5+\dots+(2n-1)) = s_1n^2 = l.$$

Let's find how $s_1$ and $v_1$ are related. The projections of the ball acceleration on the OX and OY axes are equal to $a_x = a_y = g\sqrt{2}/2$, and

$$s_1 = a_x \frac{t_1^2}{2} = g \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4} t_1^2.$$

We find the time $t_1$ of the ball’s movement before the first impact on the wedge from condition $v_1 - a_y \frac{t_1}{2} = 0$:

$$t_1 = \frac{2v_1}{a_y} = \frac{4v_1}{g\sqrt{2}}.$$

Thus,

$$s_1 = \frac{2v_1^2\sqrt{2}}{g},$$ or $v_1^2 = \frac{gl\sqrt{2}}{4n^2}$.

We find the connection between $v_1$ and $v_0$ from the law of conservation of energy:

$$\frac{mv_0^2}{2} = \frac{mv_1^2}{2} + mgl \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \Rightarrow v_0 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + gl\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2n} \sqrt{gl\sqrt{2}(4n^2 + 1)}$$

So, the ball hits point B if at point A it is given speed $|\vec{v}_0| = \frac{1}{2n} \sqrt{lg\sqrt{2}(4n^2 + 1)}$, where $n$ is a natural number. Note that

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} v_0 = \sqrt{gl\sqrt{2}}.$$

The same result from the law of conservation of energy: at $n \to \infty v_1 \to 0$, and

$$\frac{mv_0^2}{2} = mgl \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \Rightarrow v_0 = \sqrt{gl\sqrt{2}}.$$
