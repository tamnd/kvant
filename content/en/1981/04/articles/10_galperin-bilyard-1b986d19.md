Billiards

While playing billiards, I simultaneously take physics and geometry lessons.

S. M. Budyonny

Have you ever played billiards?
If you haven’t, you’ve probably had to watch others play.

Let's follow the moving ball. Until it collides with a wall or another ball, it moves in a straight line. When hitting a wall, the ball is reflected from it in almost the same way as a ray of light is reflected from a flat mirror: the angle of reflection is equal to the angle of reflection (Fig. 1).

In real billiards, there is friction; when a ball hits, it loses some of its mechanical energy and can begin to spin due to an off-center impact of the cue or collision with other balls. The theory of the motion of a ball in billiards was described by the French physicist Corilis in his book “The Mathematical Theory of the Billiard Game.”

Here we will consider ideal billiards, that is, we will assume that the balls are points, move without friction and when colliding (with a wall or with other balls) do not lose mechanical energy.

Consider the following problem: there is a ball on the path on the billiard table; At what angle should it be directed from point $A$ so that after a given number of reflections it ends up at point $B$ (in part, at Louise)?

When solving this and many other similar billiard problems, the main role is played by the process of “mirror reflection” or “straightening the billiard trajectory”: let us symmetrically reflect the rectangle (the original billiard) relative to all its sides, and so on ad infinitum (at figure 3 we also show images of the point $B$ for these symmetries); as a result of these reflections, any trajectory “straightens out” (for example, at figure 3, the trajectory $AC_1C_2C_3B$ successively turns into $AC_1D_1D_2B'$, $AC_1D_1E_1B''$, $AC_1D_1E_1B'''$). If the resulting “straightened trajectory” passes through the image of the point $B$ in one of the rectangles, then, obviously, the trajectory of the ball in the original rectangle passes through $B$. Therefore, in order to loop the ball from point $A$ so that after a given number of reflections on the billiard wall it ends up at point $B$, you need to draw such a segment with the beginning at point $A$ and the end in one of the images of point $B$ so that it intersects the same number of times the grid line of the “checkered plane”. Having performed the reverse procedure of “collapsing” the drawn segment, we turn it into the original trajectory in the original billiards (Fig. 3).

In addition to the general - rectangular - billiards, you can consider billiards in an equilateral triangle. Since the entire plane can be paved with identical equilateral triangles without gaps and covered, the procedure of “straightening the billiard trajectory” (Fig. 4) is also applied here.

It turns out that the trajectory of a billiard ball solves the following well-known problem: what is the shortest path along which a bee must crawl from point $A$ to point $B$ inside an equilateral triangle in order to first enjoy honey on one side of the triangle, then sugar on the other side, and finally jam on the third? Curious trajectories on a round billiards (Fig. 6,a). Firstly, the trajectory (after the first reflection) consists of links of the same length, which easily follows from the congruence of triangles $OAB$ and $OCB$ (Fig. 6, b). The midpoints of all links of the trajectory are located at the same distance from the center of the billiard and, thus, are located on a circle with the same center. Thus, the entire billiard trajectory is located in the ring (Fig. 6,c).

This trajectory may turn out to be closed, and then it forms either a regular polygon inscribed in a circle, or a self-intersecting star (Fig. 7). Such trajectories are called *nepiodical*.

It is clear that the question “How to send a ball from point $A$ on a circle so that after $n$ reflections it ends up at the original point?” is equivalent to the question “How to construct a regular $(n+1)$-gon with vertex at $A$?” The search for an answer to the last question must have taken two Peregreek mathematicians time, but the answer was never found. Only by the beginning of the 19th century. the great German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss showed in which cases the desired construction is possible with the help of a compass and ruler, and in which it is impossible («Kvant», 1977, No. 8, p. 5).

If the trajectory is not closed, then it fills almost the entire ring, namely *passes the ring enlarged* to any point of the ring.

Let's prove this statement. We fix one of the reflection points on the circle and denote it by $A$. Moving from point $A$, we will successively obtain new reflection points on the circle at the same (arc) distance $a$ from each other until we intersect point $A$. The closest of the two points $A$ - before the jump and after the jump over it - will be denoted by $B$. Length $B$ of arc $AB$ is not more than $\frac{\alpha}{2}$:

$$\beta \leq \frac{\alpha}{2}.$$

Starting to move from the point $B$, after a certain number of balls we will get to a point on the circle located at a distance $\beta$ from the point $B$; then we will get to the point located from $B$ to $2\beta$, etc. (Fig. 8). We can assume that we are now moving around the circle in steps of size $\beta$, at least half as large as the step size during the initial movement from point $A$. After determining the number of balls, we will cross the point $B$ for the first time and then select the point $C$ closest to it (from two points - before and after the jump). Silliards of other shapes can also be considered; their common property is the fulfillment of Silliard's law: the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.

The Eden silliard game is often used in various areas of modern mathematics, sometimes unexpected (for example, in number theory), and the silliard game itself serves as a good model for the implementation of physical processes, since, for example, gas molecules, when interacting with the boundary of a vessel and among themselves, behave in the same way as balls on a green silliard branch.

You can read about deeper connections between silliard and mathematics in two articles by A. Zemlyakov - «Kvant», 1976, No. 5 and 1979, No. 9.
