Reader advise

In the general editorial mail «Kvant» there are quite a lot of letters and notes from our readers - students, teachers, just a mathematics lover, which relate to the topic of the “Applicant's Practicum” section.
In cases where private issues are at issue, the editors respond to the authors personally. But a number of letters and notes contain useful comments of a theoretical nature, outline methods for solving certain types of problems, express interesting messages, provide methodological advice, and suggest independently composing exercises. Although these messages and tasks are not always new and original, some of them, in our opinion, are advisable for those who are preparing for university entrance exams.
Not considering it possible to publish such letters and notes in their entirety, the editors decided to prepare another collection of materials, compiled from letters from readers, with the necessary additional comments.

The experience of admission exams shows that geometry problems cause particular difficulties for seniors. Although the problems offered to applicants for their solution require only facts and formulas known from the school course, not everyone can see in a specific drawing for a specific problem which theorems need to be used and where. This can only be learned in one way - enough practice in solving the problem.
It is important to note that when solving problems, memory is automatically enriched with various ideas, connections, techniques, facts - subsequently it is precisely this additional information

The formation will help you cope with new tasks faster.

One of the “least favorite” figures to enter is the trapezoid; traditional trapezoid problems are considered difficult. Our reader V. Varvarkin (Alexandria) suggests reminding applicants of several useful facts regarding the trapezoid.

Let the trapezoid ABCD with bases BC and AD have diagonals intersecting at point O. Then the following statement is true:

— triangle AOB and DOC are equal;

— the product of the areas of triangles AOD and BOC is equal to the product of the areas of triangles AOB and DOC;

- a segment connecting the same bases BC and AD passes through point O;

— point O is the midpoint of a line segment contained inside a trapezoid, passing through the point and parallel to the bases of the trapezoid.

Let us give another useful proposal: if a circle can be written in an isosceles trapezoid, then its diameter is equal to the height of the trapezoid and is the geometric mean of the lengths of the bases of the trapezoid.

We invite the reader to independently list the properties of a trapezoid.

Exercises

1. Through the point of intersection of the diagonals of a trapezoid, a straight line is drawn parallel to its bases. Find the length of the segment of this line enclosed between the sides of the trapezoid if the lengths of its bases are equal to a and b.

2. Let O be the point of intersection of the diagonals of the trapezoid ABCD with the bases BC and AD. Prove that
$$S_{ABCD} = (\sqrt{S_{AOD}} + \sqrt{S_{BOC}})^2.$$

3. Let points E and F be the midpoints of the base BC and AD of the trapezoid ABCD, P the intersection point of the segments BF and AF, Q the intersection point of the segments ED and CF. Prove that the line PQ is parallel to the bases of the trapezoid.

Example 1 (KSU, Mechanics and Mathematics, 1979). Solve the equation

$$\frac{2 \mathrm{tg} \frac{x}{2}}{1 + \mathrm{tg}^2 \frac{x}{2}} = y^2 - 4y + 5.$$ (1)

The solution to this problem, published in «Kvant», 1980, No. 6, is based on some estimates of the left and right sides of equation (1). Meanwhile, another way of reasoning quickly leads to the goal.

Since the left side of equation (1) at $x \neq \pi + 2\pi k$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$) is equal to $\sin x$, this equation can be rewritten as

$$y^2 - 4y + (5 - \sin x) = 0$$

and treat it as square with respect to $y$. This equation has roots for those and only those values ​​of $x$ for which the discriminant is $D = \sin x - 1 \geq 0$. It follows that $\sin x = 1$. The following solution is very useful.

Example 2 (KSU, 1975). The lateral edges of a triangular paramid have the same length $l$. Two flat angles at the vertex of the para-

the first ones have magnitudes $a$, and the third one has magnitudes $b$. Find the volume of the paramid.

Let $SABC$ be a given paramid in which $A\widehat{SC} = B\widehat{SC} = a$, $A\widehat{SB} = b$; it is easy to show that $|AC| = |BC|$. If we use the “standard drawing” (S is the top of the paramid, $ABC$ is its base), then to solve it we need to separately consider three different cases: $A\widehat{CB} < 90^\circ$, $A\widehat{CB} = 90^\circ$, $A\widehat{CB} > 90^\circ$.

However, nothing prevents us from “putting the paramid on the other face” - considering the point $C$ as the vertex, and $ASB$ as the base (Fig. 1). Since the plane angles $ASC$ and $BSC$ are congestive, the vertex $C$ projects to some point $P$ lying on the bissertrix $SM$ of the angle $ASB$ (prove it!). Using the formula from §37 of the textbook “Geometry 9-10”, we can write that

$$\cos P\widehat{SC} = \frac{\cos B\widehat{SC}}{\cos B\widehat{SM}} = \frac{\cos a}{\cos \beta}.$$

To complete the solution, all that remains is to calculate the length of the height CP of the paramid (from the triangle $PSC$) and calculate the area of ​​the base $ASB$ (carry out the necessary calculations).

Example 3 (MSU, Faculty of Economics, 1978). In triangle KLM, point A is taken on side KL so that $|KA|:|AL| = 1:3$; on the LM side point B is taken so that $|LB|:|BM| = 4:1$. Let C be the intersection point of lines KB and MA. It is known that the area of ​​triangle KLC is 2. Find the area of ​​triangle KLM.

This problem can be solved using only the fact that the area of ​​triangles with identical bases is the same as their heights. The stated fact itself is, of course, well known - the main difficulty is to see in a specific configuration those triangles to which it is convenient and expedient to apply it.

Looking at figure 2 externally, we can see that triangles KCL (its area is known to us) and KCM have a common base KC. Therefore, checking the heights LP and MQ, we write

$$S_{KCL} : S_{KCM} = |LP|:|MQ|.$$

From the similarity of triangles LPB and MQB we obtain

$$|LP|:|MQ| = |LB|:|MB| = 4:1.$$

Thus, $S_{KCM} = 1/2$. Similar reasoning (do it!) shows that $S_{MCL} = 3/2$ and, therefore,

$$S_{KLM} = S_{KCM} + S_{KCM} + S_{MCL} = 4.$$

Example 4 (MIPT, 1970). In triangle ABC, the bisector AD divides side BC in the ratio $|BD|:|CD| = 2:1$. In what ratio does the median CE divide this bisector?

The solution to the problem is quite simple if you draw a segment EK parallel to the bisector AD (Fig. 3). But the construction of the segment DM parallel to the median CE leads to the goal even faster. Then (readers are left to justify these equalities on their own)

$$\frac{|AQ|}{|QD|} = \frac{|AE|}{|EM|} = \frac{|EB|}{|EM|} = \frac{|EM|+|BM|}{|EM|} =$$

$$= 1 + \frac{|BM|}{|EM|} = 1 + \frac{|BD|}{|CD|} = 3.$$

In passing, we note that in the solution it is not used anywhere that $[AD]$ is a bisector; the solution will not change if $[AD]$ is a segment connecting vertex A with point D dividing side BC in a given ratio.

Example 5. Prove that if $\widehat{A}, \widehat{B}, \widehat{C}$ are the values ​​of the angles of a triangle, then

$$\sin \frac{\widehat{A}}{2} \cdot \sin \frac{\widehat{B}}{2} \cdot \sin \frac{\widehat{C}}{2} \leq \frac{1}{8}.$$ (2)

The solution to this problem, based on the transformation of the left side of equation (2) using trigonometric formulas, can be found, for example, in the book by V. B. Liskiy, L. V. Ovsyannikov, A. N. Tulaikov and M. I. Shabunn “Problems in Elementary Mathematics” (M., “Nauka”, 1973). 10th grade student Vyacheslav Melnik (Gaysin) offers a solution that does not require cumbersome calculations.

From the cosine theorem

$$\cos \widehat{A} = \frac{b^2 + c^2 - a^2}{2bc}$$

(as always, small letters indicate the lengths of the sides of the triangle opposite the corresponding angles). Then (given that $\sin \frac{\widehat{A}}{2} > 0$)

$$\sin \frac{\hat{A}}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1-\cos \hat{A}}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} \left( 1 - \frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc} \right)} = \sqrt{\frac{a^2-(b-c)^2}{4bc}} \leqslant \frac{a}{2\sqrt{bc}}.$$

In exactly the same way we are convinced that

$$\sin \frac{B}{2} \leqslant \frac{b}{2\sqrt{ac}}, \sin \frac{\hat{C}}{2} \leqslant \frac{c}{2\sqrt{ab}}.$$

To prove inequality (2), it is enough to use the variable $t = \sqrt{x+4}$ term by term. It is clear that $t \geqslant 0$. After the indicated substitution, equation (3) turns into the equation

$$\sqrt{2t^2-14} + t = 5,$$

in which there is only one radical. Solve the last equation by “squaring” and checking the pooled values ​​of the variable, we find the only root $x = 5$.

Example 7. A triangle ABC is fixed, in which
$$|AC|=b, |AB|=c, \widehat{AC}=\alpha,$$
and $\alpha<\pi/2$. In the plane of this triangle, all possible rectangles ALMN are considered, so that vertex B of the triangle lies on the side LM of the rectangle, and vertex C of the triangle lies on the side MN of the rectangle. What is the maximum area of ​​a rectangle that has the indicated property?

From figure 4, which shows rectangles “circumscribed” around triangle ABC, it is clear that as a parameter characterizing the choice of a rectangle, we can take the values ​​of the angle $x$ between the sides AL of the rectangle and the sides AB of the triangle. Then it is obvious that the area of ​​the rectangle ALMN $S=|AL|\cdot|AN|=$
$$=c \cos x \cdot b \cos \left( \frac{\pi}{2} - x - \alpha \right)=$$
$$=bc \cos x \sin(x+\alpha).$$

However, it is also necessary to indicate, based on the geometric meaning of the problem, the domain of definition of the functions $S=S(x)$. For brevity, let's put ABC=$\beta$, $\widehat{ACB}=\gamma$.

Let ALMN be some “circumscribed” rectangle; then it is not difficult to check (make sure of this) that the following relationship holds:
$$x \geqslant \begin{cases} 0, & \text{ если } \beta < \pi/2, \\ \beta - \pi/2, & \text{ если } \beta \geqslant \pi/2, \end{cases} \quad (4)$$
$$x + \alpha \leqslant \begin{cases} \pi/2, & \text{ если } \gamma < \pi/2, \\ \pi - \gamma, & \text{ если } \gamma \geqslant \pi/2, \end{cases} \quad (5)$$
$$x \leqslant \beta, \pi/2 - (x+\alpha) \leqslant \gamma. \quad (6)$$

It is very important that the converse statement is also true: if $x$ simultaneously satisfies all inequalities (4) - (6), then the rectangle corresponding to this

value of the angle $x$, has the connection specified in the conditions.

Now you can finish solving the problem. To determine this, let us assume that in this triangle the angle ABC is acute (that is, $\beta < \pi/2$; other possibilities are left to the reader to consider). In this case, the task is obviously to find the most important functions
$$S=bc \cos x \sin(x+\alpha)$$
at $0 \leqslant x \leqslant \min(\beta, \pi/2-\alpha)$. (7)

Since (check!) $S'(x) = bs \cos(2x+\alpha)$, the equation $S'(x) = 0$ has root $x_0=\pi/4-\alpha/2$ (we cannot be interested in the other roots). The specification form of the domain of definition of functions (7) leads to the following final result:
$$\alpha+2\beta > \pi/2, \text{ то } x_0 \text{ является критической точкой функции (7)},$$
and it reaches its greatest significance there:
$$S_{\max} = bc \cos^2(\pi/4-\alpha/2) = \frac{1}{2} bc(1+\sin \alpha);$$
— if $\alpha+2\beta \leqslant \pi/2$, then function (7) has no critical points and is recurrent, taking on the most values ​​at $x=\beta$:
$$S_{\max} = bc \cos \beta \sin(\alpha+\beta) = \frac{1}{2} c^2 \sin 2\beta.$$
