About the specific power of man and the Sun
(or why a mosquito has “cold blood”)

The flow of energy from the Sun is colossal. From geophysical measurements it follows that even in the upper layers of the Earth’s atmosphere, at a huge distance of almost 150 million kilometers from the Sun, every square meter located perpendicular to the solar rays receives 1.4 kJ from the Sun every second. This value is called the solar constant and is usually denoted by the letter I. Knowing that I = 1.4 kJ/(m²·s) = 1.4 kW/m², it is not difficult to find the total solar radiation power $P_1^*$). To do this, it is enough to multiply the solar constant by the area of ​​a sphere described around the Sun with a radius of $R = 150 000 000$ km:

$$P_1 = I \cdot 4\pi R^2 \approx 4 \cdot 10^{26} \text{Bt.}$$

Of course, human energy capabilities are many times more modest. The average power $P_2$ of a developing person can be quite accurately estimated by the amount of food he consumes per day. As is known, persons who do not engage in heavy physical labor should receive approximately 12 MJ from food daily. Almost all the energy goes to maintaining a constant temperature of the human body and is ultimately generated by the person in the surrounding space. (Only a very small portion of the 12 MJ a person receives)

Thus, as an energy generator, the Sun is approximately $3 \cdot 10^{24}$ times more powerful than a person. All the more unexpected is the result of comparing their specific (that is, per unit mass) powers. The mass of $M$ the Sun is about $2 \cdot 10^{30}$ kg, the mass of $m$ man is assumed to be 80 kg. Then
$$P_1:M=2 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ Вт/кг},$$
$$P_2:m=1,75 \text{ Вт/кг}$$

— the specific power of a person turns out to be almost 10,000 times greater than that of the Sun!

The result we arrived at seems completely implausible at first glance. However, this is actually true.

How is this “paradox” explained? Why is the Sun - this hypothetical thermonuclear reactor - inferior in terms of specific power to humans, whose energy is supplied by chemical reactions that are much more “weak” than nuclear ones?

The answer to this question is not difficult to obtain if we accept that the thermomaterial body and the Sun are released more or less evenly throughout the entire volume. Consequently, the rate of release of a body is directly proportional to the volume of the body, in other words, to a cube of linear size. The rate of heat transfer is proportional to the surface area of ​​the body, that is, the square of the linear size. It would become that the larger the body, the lower the rate of release of the body required to maintain a certain given temperature.

The volume of the Sun is about $10^{27}$ m$^3$, its surface area is ~ $10^{18}$ m$^2$. The corresponding parameters of the human body are ~ $10^{-1}$ m$^3$ and ~ $1$ m$^2$. Thus, the ratio of the volumes of the Sun and man is ~ $10^{28}$, and the ratio of the surface areas of these bodies is of the order of $10^{18}$. Figuratively speaking, per unit volume of the Sun there is approximately 10 billion times less free surface than that of a person. Therefore, it is not surprising that, despite the fact that solar “metabolism” proceeds at a rate of only 0.2 mW/kg, the temperature on the surface of the Sun reaches 6000 degrees.

Let us illustrate the connections between size, rate of energy release and body temperature with the following examples from the life of the animal world.

The body temperatures of mammals do not differ much. In particular, they are approximately the same in both an elephant and a small useful mouse. However, the rate of body excretion in the elephant's body is approximately 30 times less. If heat was released inside the elephant’s body at the same speed as in a mouse, then the heat released would not have time to leave the elephant’s body quickly enough to maintain a normal temperature, and the elephant would “roast” in its own skin.

The smaller the warm-blooded animal, the greater the specific (that is, per unit mass) rate of body excretion must be in order to compensate for the loss and maintain the body temperature that ensures the normal vitality of the organism, the more food (again per unit mass) it must absorb. The little boy from the well-known children's fairy tale must be extremely greedy: with the same proportions as a normal person, he would need 20 times more food per 1 kg of mass.

The smallest mammals on Earth, the Eto-Russian mice, weigh only 1.5 g, but eat twice as much per day. If this mouse is left without food for even a few hours, it will die. Almost all periods are occupied by hummingbirds (tiny birds weighing about 2 g, living in South America) searching for and absorbing food. Hummingbirds can tolerate a long restful break in this activity only because their body temperature drops sharply during this time.
