Ask - we answer

«Kvant» does a lot to improve students’ mathematical knowledge, and provides great support to teachers in teaching schoolchildren. But issues of writing solutions to problems and proving theorems, especially using modern symbols, are still awaiting consideration on the pages of the journal.

...In this regard, the magazine could help both students and teachers. For example, in parallel with the use of theoretical material and the publication of exam variants of the problem, it would be worthwhile to provide examples of written solutions to the problem and proofs of theorems, to show how applicants should format their work for advanced university exams.

V. P. Danish uuk, mathematics teacher at vocational school (Otanya village, Ivano-Frankivsk region)

Letters from multi-readers in «Kvant» in one form or another contain the same question: how should one format written work for an advanced university exam in mathematics? Schoolchildren, applicants, and teachers ask the editors to publish “samples of exam solutions”, to explain how to solve specific problems and carry out proofs, and to correctly express their reasoning in mathematics. No, and there cannot be any special canons regulating the formal order of recording decisions, reasoning or calculations in an examination paper, requiring the mandatory use of special “modern symbols”, etc.

Of course, this does not mean that it is permissible to write haphazardly in an exam paper. You should adhere to natural universal norms - format your work neatly so that it is easy to read. Too often we see carelessly completed works in which formulas are written down free of charge, without obligatory ones, the text is written so poorly that they then have difficulty deciphering it themselves! All this testifies to the applicant’s unserving attitude to work, his inability to work, and his lack of respect for his own work. And the result of such “work” is usually sad, since few people manage to accurately navigate their own records in such chaos and avoid making mistakes.

Readers often ask how detailed decisions need to be presented. It is difficult to give detailed recommendations here, but one thing is clear: one should strive to comprehensively and logically correctly explain all stages of the necessary reasoning. So, in an algebraic example, it is useful to understand what comes from what and how it is obtained during the transformation, to indicate the resulting restrictions on the values ​​of the variables; in a geometric problem, it is advisable to name the application of the theorem along the way, prove the intermediate geometric facts used, if necessary, make auxiliary drawings, etc.

Sometimes schoolchildren are taught certain formatted “schemes for recording decisions”, from which they are not allowed to deviate. Here is just one (quite common) example of such formatism: before solving an equation, it is imperative to fulfill the range of permissible values ​​of the variable, regardless of whether this occurs during the solution or not. It is clear that such recommendations are not discussed either by common sense messages (sometimes an exhaustive solution of an equation can be much simpler than finding its OD), or by the requirements of mathematical rigor.

And the last question that interests many: if a problem allows for several different solutions, then which one is the best one? For example, ninth-grader E. Serebryannik (Moscow) added four solutions to one geometric problem to the editor and asked “I would like to know... which method is most suitable in the ninth grade, and which in the tenth?” In both the ninth and tenth grades and the advanced exams, a solution is good, as long as it is correct. We emphasize that solving the problems of the entrance exams does not require the use of any information that goes beyond the scope of the school curriculum.

H. Rozov

The dimensional method helps solve problems (see “Quant” No. 6)
1. The minimum rotation period corresponds to ω₀ = 2π/T₀. The connection between ω₀, M, R and the gravitational constant γ is found unambiguously from the dimension messages and has the form ω₀² ~ γM/R³ (the dimension γ is easily found from the law of universal gravitation). Numerical estimates for plans with Earth parameters are obtained by taking into account that γ ≈ 6.7 · 10⁻¹¹ H · m²/kg², M ≈ 6 · 10²⁴ kg, R ≈ 6.4 · 10³ km. The reader is invited to calculate for himself how much greener our Earth is than it could be, and to think about the question of what would happen if we managed to spin the Earth with ω₀.

2. In addition to the geometric similarity of chamberts, we must take into account that they are made from the same matrix, and therefore their elastic characteristics and density $Q$ are the same. The natural elastic characteristic is the Young model $E$. If $L$ is the length of the Cameron leg, then from the dimension messages we obtain an unambiguous formula for the frequency: $\omega \sim \frac{1}{L} \left( \frac{E}{\varrho} \right)^{1/2}$. The frequency ratio of geometric similar chamberts is therefore inversely proportional to the ratio of their length characteristics and is equal to 1:3. It can also be noted that the ratio $E/\varrho$ is the square of the speed of sound in a solid. then the previous formula becomes completely clear. The period of oscillation of the Cameron is $T \sim \omega^{-1}$, that is, it is determined simply by the time of passage of the sound wave in the Cameron leg.

Now think about why the geometric similarity of the chambers is emphasized in the problem conditions. can anything change if the cross sections of the Cameron legs are not geometrically similar?

3. We will assume that the period $T$ is determined by the pressure $p$, the energy released during the explosion $E$ and the density of water $Q$. Density plays the role of a mass characteristic here. Writing $T \sim p^x \varrho^y E^z$ and comparing the dimensions of the left and right sides of this formula, we get $T \sim p^{-5/6} \varrho^{1/2} E^{1/3}$. If we take into account that the water pressure $p$ is related to the depth $H$ by correspondence $p = \varrho g H$, we obtain the formula $T \sim \left( g H \right)^{-5/6} \left( \frac{E}{\varrho} \right)^{1/3}$.

The gas pulsar formed during the explosion will expand until the internal pressure in it becomes equal to the pressure of the surrounding water at a depth of $H$. We can obtain a rough but reasonable estimate for the internal pressure again from the dimension messages by dividing the energy $E$ by the volume of the pulsra: $p_{BH} \sim E/r^3$. Let us write the condition of equality (in order of magnitude) $p_{BH}$ to external pressure $p = \varrho g H$: $(E/r^3) \sim \varrho g H$. Hence $r^3 \sim E/\varrho g H$. The size of the gas pulse at a given value $E$ is proportional to $H^{-1/3}$. We, of course, neglect for such estimates the change in water density $Q$ with depth.

4. The pressure at the center of a star with mass $M$ and radius $R$ is expressed by the formula $p \sim \gamma M^2/R^4$, which is unambiguously obtained from the dimension messages.

We invite the reader to make numerical estimates in this problem and in Problem 5 themselves.
