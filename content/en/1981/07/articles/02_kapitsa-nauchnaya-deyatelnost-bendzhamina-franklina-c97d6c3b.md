Benjamin Franklin's scientific activities

Franklin was born in America, in the city of Boston, in 1706. He died when he was 84 years old. Its activities span the entire 18th century and are closely related to the rapid development of the natural and social sciences that took place at that time. This is the era of the Enlightenment, the era preceding the period of radical social upheavals in Europe.

The name of Franklin has gone down in the history of world culture not only as the name of the greatest scientist, one of the founders of the doctrine of electricity, but also as the name of the largest programmatic state and social activist in America, taking an active part in the struggle for its liberation from the colonial position.

Contemporaries unanimously describe Franklin as an exceptionally committed person, comprehensively educated, with concentrated and broad views, an interesting and witty interlocutor. Franklin traveled frequently and lived abroad for many years, mainly in England and France. Here he communicated with the leading people of his time and by the end of his life he became the most popular person in Europe. In his homeland, America, Franklin is today one of the most revered people in US history.

Franklin's main scientific discoveries in the field of electricity were made by him in the 50s of the 18th century, before the work of Galvani and Volta, that is, before the era of galvanic current, and belongs to the initial period of the conquest of this powerful force of nature by science. In the more than 200 years that have passed since Franklin's work, the teaching of electricity has advanced so much that Franklin's work is now taught in high schools, in classes where they are just beginning to learn physics. From our youth we have known the basics of the doctrine of static electricity, although perhaps some of us who are not new may have forgotten what Franklin actually did here. For example, do all of us remember that the name of positive and negative positions was first taught by Franklin?

A detailed description of Franklin’s scientific works is hardly interesting now, but the very history of the development of Franklin’s work in the field of electricity, I think, is not only interesting, but also useful for a modern scientist. This follows from the fact that the path of development of science, that is, the path of knowledge of nature along which mankind follows, is the same. In our search for scientific truths, we often go astray from the right path and then time is lost. Therefore, the less we deviate from the right path, the faster and economically our knowledge and conquests of the forces of nature will develop. Studying the history of science, we find those factors that contribute to the rapid development of science. From this point of view, the history of Franklin's scientific work is of exceptional interest.

Franklin's work on electricity was completed in a short period of time, in just 7 years, from 1747 to 1753. During this short period of time, Franklin was recognized as the leading scientist of his time. Most major scientific societies or academies recognized Franklin's scientific achievements by electing him as a member, and a number of universities awarded him honorary doctorates.

A natural question will arise: how could it be that Franklin, who had never studied physics, in a small town in America, far from the centers of world science, being already a man of mature age, was able to lead the development of an entire scientific discipline in just a few years of work?

And this happened in the middle of the 18th century, when science was carried out by people at the level of knowledge of such scientists as Newton, Huygens, Euler? There is no need to talk about dialetanthym here. How could Franklin

access results that remain inaccessible to professional scientists?

I think that we must look for an explanation in the fact that Franklin was the first to correctly understand the essence of electrical phenomena and therefore correctly opened the way for further research in this area.

Before Franklin’s work, a large amount of material had already been accumulated, but the facts were scattered, and the hypothesis he put forward not only united these facts into a coherent picture, but also pointed the right way for further research.

Franklin outlined his basic hypothesis in a letter to Peter Collinson in 1749. It gives a clear picture of the processes that occurred during the electrification of bodies. This picture still remains largely correct. Here is an excerpt from this letter: “Electric materia consists of particles of extremely small size, since they can arbitrate general substances as dense as metal with such ease and freedom that they do not experience noticeable resistance.” These days we call these “extremely small particles” electrons. Further, Franklin considered any body as a sponge, saturated with these particles of electricity. Electrification of the body consists in the fact that a body that has an excess of electrical particles is positively charged; if a body lacks these particles, it is negatively charged.

Of course, Franklin did not then have the opportunity to experimentally perceive materials of the new nature of electricity and therefore did not have the opportunity to determine who actually receives electrical matter and, therefore, is positively charged, and who loses it, that is, negatively charged. Therefore, he randomly accepted that he called it positively charged, perhaps thinking that the woolly material, when rubbing against the glass, rubs electricity into it. Only at the end of the century, after the discovery of some electricity -

Electrons, it became known that a non-positive electrode, as Franklin thought, accumulates electrical particles, but a negative one. In order not to change the usual designations of positive and negative polarity, the electron was assigned a negative charge.

Franklin extended the property of mutual repulsion of identically charged bodies to charges located on metal conductors. He believed that the charges, repelling each other, would tend to the damaged part of the charged metal body. He proved his assumptions as follows by experiment.

The metal kettle was placed on an insulator and electrified. It was necessary to find an experiment that would prove that the charge is distributed over the broken surface of the teapot. To do this, a chain was placed inside the kettle, which could be gradually removed from the kettle using an insulated handle. The degree of electrification of the teapot was determined by the repulsion of two balls suspended from it by strings. The experiment consisted of lifting a chain from a kettle using an insulated hand and observing how, as it was pulled out, the degree of electrification of the kettle decreased.

Franklin reasoned this way: while the chain is inside the kettle, its surface mutilates the inner surface of the kettle; when the chain is pulled outward, it damages the outer surface of the kettle [the chain “at the exit” touches the kettle - *approx. ped.*]. Franklin concludes: if the charge spreads only over the damaged surface of the kettle's surface, then only when it is mutilated will the lectification decrease. This is what is actually observed when the experiment is carried out.

Franklin gives a description of all his works in letters to his friend Colinson in England. These letters describe a large number of different experiments that have become classical: the production of electric wind, the properties of charges flowing from the tip, etc. In these same letters, Franklin, from the point of view of his hypothesis, gives the correct explanation of a number of already known electrical phenomena, for example, the pattern of accumulation of electric charges in a Leyden jar, and on this basis he makes a flat capacitor. Collinson reports on Franklin's work to the Royal Society ([English Academy of Sciences - *note ped.*]). Then he published them as a separate book, which became Franklin’s main scientific work. This book went through a number of editions and was translated into many languages.

The clarity and correctness of Franklin's understanding is electrifying for him to find an experiment that for the first time convincingly reports the electrical nature of gas discharges. The idea behind Franklin's experiment was as follows.

Let’s assume that a vertical metal rod insulated from the ground is placed between a thundercloud and the ground. If a thundercloud has an electric charge, then a charge of the opposite sign is located at the top of the rod. If a point is made at this upper end of the rod, then the induced charge flows off and the rod charges with an electric current of the same sign as the cloud.

Franklin believed that the presence of this charge could be detected by a spark that would occur if brought to a conductor by the free end of a grounded wire. Franklin assumed, as it later turned out, wrongly, that for this experiment to be successful, the rod must be placed on a hill so that it would be closer to the cloud.

Since there was no such elevation near his home, he thought that he had failed in this experiment. He described in detail how it should be done and suggested that others do it. He himself decided to continue a similar experience, but in a slightly different way, which did not require elevation.

For this experiment, he decided to use a string together with a metal rod, lifting it up like a kite. Since there is always wind during a thunderstorm, kites can be launched, and since it also rains, the rope, when wet, will become conductive and can replace the metal rod. To make the string easier to charge, it was possible to allow the induced charges to flow off at the upper end of the string. To do this, Franklin placed points at the corners of the kite frame. In order to isolate the string from the ground, a silk ribbon was tied to it below, which was protected from rain. A metal key was suspended from the end of the string near the ground, from which Franklin extracted sparks during a thunderstorm. In this way, in the presence of his friends and acquaintances, he proved the electrical nature of a lightning discharge. Franklin's experiment with the kite was made on April 12, 1753, when he first discovered that thunderclouds, as a rule, were negatively charged.

In addition to purely scientific works, Franklin has another generally recognized achievement: his invention - the lightning rod. In the history of the implementation of this invention, there is also a lot of honorable things. This is a long story, many research works are devoted to it. Therefore, I can only tell very briefly how Franklin invented and introduced the lightning rod.

After the existing lightning discharge was revealed, the question naturally arose of how one can rationally deal with the destruction and fires caused by lightning. It became clear that when lightning strikes a building, ship or any other towering object, the damage is caused by the fact that a powerful electric current, passing through a poorly conducting medium, causes destruction and ignition. Therefore, if, when a lightning strikes a building, the electrical discharge is allowed to pass through a highly conductive medium such as metal, there will be no damage.

Undoubtedly, Franklin, with his keen practical mind, saw before others the possibility of finding protection against lightning by draining current. But it was much more difficult for him to find the most rational form of a lightning rod and force public opinion to recognize it as an effective means of combating the destruction caused by thunderstorms. Franklin quickly coped with this task, and his activities in this direction can still serve as an example of how new technical ideas should be implemented in life.

Franklin not only did not take patent for his lightning rod, but gave the opportunity to use it free of charge to anyone who wanted it. In addition, he carried out extensive and skillful propaganda work to implement it. Without enough time, it is impossible to tell the full story of the introduction of the lightning rod, so I focused on the brightest moments observed.

It is quite possible that no invention has caused such a storm of various objections as was caused 200 years ago by that small metal rod that today crowns almost every structure and is a standard element of its design.

Both scientific and political objections arose against the lightning rod. When Franklin gave a description of the action of a lightning rod, in addition to its next function - to provide an uninterrupted path for electric current through a metal rod into the ground, he also pointed out the possibility of the existence of another process.

Franklin believed that if there is a thunderstorm above the structure and if the lightning rod is equipped with a spike, then a slow flow of electrical charge can occur from it. We now call this phenomenon a silent discharge. It will neutralize the charge of the cloud and discharge it. Therefore, Franklin admitted that a lightning rod not only protects a building, but, in general, can prevent lightning discharges. Scientific opponents

Franklin believed, for their part, that the flow of charge from the tip would not only not neutralize the charge of the cloud, but would create more favorable conditions for the occurrence of lightning. Therefore, a lightning rod is rather harmful, since it makes it possible for lightning discharges to occur, which without it would not have occurred at all.

Scientists who took this point of view considered its proximity to others equipped with a lightning rod to be especially harmful and dangerous for the building.

The interest of public opinion in these questions was very great, and this is well illustrated by a well-known case: when in Center-Omer, in France, M. de Vissery installed a lightning rod on his house, his neighbors were so frightened by this and arose that they sued him. The process created a lot of noise and lasted several years between 1780 and 1784. It is interesting that on the side of the defense of the lightning rod was a young lawyer.
card Maximilien Robespierre, and this high-profile case marked the beginning of his fame. It is also curious that one of the experts on the plaintiff’s side was Marat, who considered the lightning rod dangerous and was against its installation. After a long struggle and appeal, de Vissery won the case.

Franklin's tactics in this whole fight for the lightning rod are interesting. He did not usually speak publicly, but through conversations and through his enormous correspondence he continually influenced leading scientists and social activists. With such propaganda, he created for himself a powerful army of the leading people of that time, which fought to implement his brainchild - the lightning rod.

In England, the struggle against the lightning rod acquired a sharply political character. The English scientist Wilson tried to prove that the known harmful effects of a lightning rod can be achieved if its end is made blunt and this allows the charge to flow off. Since the time of this dispute coincided with the era of America's liberation from colonial rule and Franklin became a major political figure in young America and one of the most active fighters for freedom, any citizen of England who supplied his lightning rod with an edge, and not a blunt end, was considered politically unprofitable.

King George III of England demanded that the Royal Society abandon its decision to use the Franklin lightning rod. To this demand of the king, the resident of the Royal Society, John Prigle, the king’s physician and personal friend of Franklin, gave the following famous answer: “Both in his duty and in his inclinations, he will always fulfill the desires of his majesty to the best of his ability, but he is not able to change the laws of nature, nor change the operation of their forces.” For these words, he was fired from his position as royal physician and removed from residency at the Royal Society.

During the struggle over the issue of the lightning rod, all methods were used, slander, insinuation, both personally against Franklin and against his friends. Franklin remained very supportive, regardless of personal attacks, and invariably said that in matters of science, the truth is revealed only by experience. Indeed, experience resolved this dispute, but many tens of years later, when the doctrine of lightning discharges and the electric field reached the modern level. Now we see that this whole dispute had no basis, since for a general lightning rod it does not matter how it ends, with a point or a blunt end. At a short distance from the ground, the geometric shape of the end of the lightning rod cannot significantly influence the distribution of the electric field above the ground.

But one of the leading specialists in lightning discharges, Dr. Schonland, points out that the process of neutralizing the charge of a cloud through a quiet discharge, predicted by Franklin, can still be carried out, but only when the tip of the lightning rod is at such a great distance from the ground that it is comparable to the height of the cloud. This is the case for lightning rods placed on the tallest American skyscrapers; then it is actually possible to observe from the tip of the rod a quiet discharge that does not turn into lightning. Schonland adds that it must certainly have been a feeling of just satisfaction for Franklin if he could have known it.

Franklin is engaged in geophysics, gave a map of the Gulf Stream test, invented a musical instrument with glass balls, an economic stove, which is still distributed in America and France, street lamps, double glasses for senile distant people, and much more. In addition, thanks to his public character and lively mind, Franklin advised a lot and contributed to the development of science. Of course, information about most of these consultations has sunk into eternity, but some have reached us.

For example, Louis XVI asked Franklin to be a member of the commission on the value of the method of treatment proposed by Dr. Mesmer, who used the so-called “animal magnetism.” Interestingly, Dr. Guillotin, the inventor of the guillotine, participated in the same commission. Franklin denied the existence of animal magnetism, but believed that it was not a harmful method of treatment, since it entertained wealthy people without causing harm, which cannot always be said about other unsubstantiated medicinal methods of treatment.

Franklin was very approving of the flights of the Montgolf brothers.

Franklin believed that scientific achievements are the property of all people and the concern for the development of world science should stand outside of political and military contradictions.
