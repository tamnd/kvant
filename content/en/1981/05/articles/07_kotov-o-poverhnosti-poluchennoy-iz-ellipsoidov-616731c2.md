An easy solution to a difficult problem

At the end of the century, the English mathematician J. Sylvester posed the following problems:

Let $S$ be a finite set of points on the plane such that not all its points lie on the same line. Is there necessarily a line on which the set $S$ is a Lyatrian two points?

For more than forty years, Sylvester's problem remained unsolved, and then it was considered a very difficult task. But suddenly the next problem arose - an idea.

Let us draw all possible lines through pairs of points of the set $S$ (perhaps some of them contain more than two points). Let us consider all possible distances from the point set $S$ to the drawn lines. There are a finite number of them. Let $r$ be the smallest of these distances ($r > 0$). Let's take that point $P$ and that line $l$, the distance between them is $r$. Let us prove that there are two points on the line $l$ and the set $S$.

Suppose that there are three or more points of the set $S$ on it. Let us drop the repredcular from the point $P$ to the line $l$; let $A$ be the base of this redistribution (see figure). It is easy to see that $A \notin S$. This means that on the line $l$, at least on one side of the point $A$, there are at least two points the set $S$; we denote two of them by $X$ and $Y$, and let $X$ lie closer to $A$ than $Y$. Let us now draw a straight line through the points $P$ and $Y$. Obviously, the distance from point $X$ to this line is less than the distance from point $P$ to line $l$, which contradicts the choice of $P$ and $l$.

Consequently, the line $l$ contains only two points, the set $S$.

Our cover

The bizarrely shaped surface drawn on the first page of the cover was obtained using a technique that was already described on the pages of our magazine («Kvant», 1980, No. 10, p. 62): the left sides of the equation of three ellipsons.

The ES-1030 computer, performing a drawing according to the equation, showed two families of lines on the surface: they are cut off by planes located at intervals parallel to the coordinate planes $Oxz$ and $Oyz$. The machine painted the resulting pieces of surface in a checkerboard pattern.

The machine considered the surface opaque. At figure we thus see the “outer part” of the surface. Think about it - what’s “inside” her?

Fig. 1. Beam path during dashed scanning: 1-2 - beginning of the odd half-frame; 5—6—beginning of an even half-frame; 2-3, 4-5, 6-7 and 8-1 - reverse beam stroke.

You cannot shade the electron beam in the middle of the frame, since in this case part of the image will simply disappear. The creators of television found an original solution: the frame is not transmitted all at once, but in half-frames, so that the electron beam will first draw odd and then even lines of the image. In this case, half-frames change with a frequency of 50 Hz, and the eye perceives the image of movement as continuous. This method of forming a frame on a television screen is called dash scanning (Fig. 1).

With a TV at home, you can conduct a number of experiments demonstrating the stroopoptic effect.

In your first experiment, try to look at the TV screen through a “classic” stroscope - a rotating disk with answers. It is easy to make from a piece of cardboard mounted on the microelectric motor shaft (Fig. 2). To regulate the engine speed, it must be powered from a coin cell battery (with an emf of 4.5 V) through a rheostat (with a total resistance of 100 Ohms). The distance from the stroscope to the TV screen should be such that the entire screen is visible through the return lights.

The observed picture can be analyzed using the graphs shown on figure 3. The first graph (Fig. 3, a) demonstrates how the image fills the screen from top to bottom (the position of the line in the frame is plotted along the ordinat axis, time is plotted along the abscissa axis). The dotted line shows the reverse path of the beam (the beam is extinguished at this moment). The graph of the “visibility” of the stroscope (Fig. 3, b) represents straight line segments, the top of which corresponds to the position “visible”, the bottom - “not visible”. The third and fourth graphs (Fig. 3, c and 2) were obtained by superimposing the first two with different phase shifts between them. They explain why we see (if the frequencies coincide, of course) only half of the screen.

With a slight change in the disk rotation frequency, the dark shelf will run up or down the screen, depending on our desire.

Try to explain on your own at what frequencies the shelf will run up and at what frequencies it will go down. What will the picture look like at a stroscope frequency of 100 Hz? 150 Hz? Why does a dark shelf run across the TV screen, filmed on film? Why sometimes on a movie or television screen a car moving forward

Try another experiment. To do this, you will need a string 0.3-0.5 m long (you can use an elastic band for model airplanes). What will a vertically positioned vibrating string look like against the background of a TV screen?

Let the string be in an equilibrium position at the moment the half-frame begins. While the beam passes the string, the string will move and meet the beam in a new place. In the next line, the “meeting place” will shift again, and so on. If the period of oscillation of the string is 20 m (frequency 50 Hz), then during this time the entire half-frame is filled, and the string will return to its original position, having completed a full oscillation. Repeating this situation many times will lead to the fact that the tengь from the string, which we will see against the background of the TV screen, will have the shape of a sine wave (Fig. 4).

What will you see if the string's vibration frequency doubles? triple? What will you see if the string is placed diagonally across the screen? horizontally? Carry out these experiments and try to explain the results. Once you have gained experience, try to tell how a finger rotating around its transverse axis will look like against the background of the screen. Test your ideas experimentally.
