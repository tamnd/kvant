2. Why do metals conduct current?

It has long been noted that elements such as copper, gold, silver, iron, lead, tin, both in pure form and when alloyed with each other, form metals. Others, for example phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, nitrogen, oxygen, not only are not metallines themselves, but also combine with metals and transform them into dielectrics. An example of this is the common salt NaCl. Therefore, in chemistry the division of elements into metals and non-metals appeared.

Such a classification, alone, is nothing more than a statement of facts, although at first glance it claims to explain the properties of substances based only on the structure of atoms. In fact, let's look at the periodic table. Elements located in the same column are very similar in their chemical properties. But will the crystals or alloys made from them conduct electric current? Looking at the table, it is impossible to answer this question. Thus, all elements of the first group form metals, with the exception of the first - hydrogen. But a law that someone is allowed to break is no longer a law. True, in the second group the situation is better: here all the elements are grafted metals; and in the third group there is a failure again: boron is a semiconductor, and aluminum is a beautiful metal. It's even worse. The first element of the fourth group is carbon; We have already mentioned that the graph - the so-called carbon crystal - is a metal. But diamond is also a crystal made up of carbon atoms, but arranged differently than in graphite—an insulator. Krementium and germanium are classical semiconductors. Tin is a seemingly typical metal. However... If the familiar white shiny tin is kept at a temperature of -30°G, then its crystal structure will change, and it will turn gray in appearance. And this tin - it is called “gray tin” - is a semiconductor! And lead is always metal.

If you start mixing different elements, then the picture is completely conditional.

threads. Let's take, for example, and alloy two metals indium and antimony - in a one-to-one ratio. We obtain the semiconductor InSb, which is widely used in technology. On the other hand, we have already said that molybdenum dioxide MoO₂ at T ≈ 0 K conducts current, that is, MoO₂ is a metal. (And WO₂, and Re₂O₃ and some other oxides are also metals.) And if the resulting crystals from atoms are strongly rolled and squeezed, then it turns out that almost all substances become metallic, even such typical metallic substances as sulfur. True, for it the pressure of transition to the metallic state is very high - several hundred thousand atmospheres (and for hydrogen even more).

It seems that separating elements into metals and non-metals is not such a simple task. In any case, it is clear that when considering individual atoms, we cannot say whether a substance composed of these atoms will conduct current at T ≈ 0K, because the way the atoms are located relative to each other plays a huge role. Therefore, to answer the question “why do metals conduct current?” we need to study how atoms interact with each other to form a solid.

Let's see how things stand with the simplest of the elements that form metals—lithium. The serial number of Li is three. This means that the nucleus of Li atoms contains three protons and the positive charge of the nucleus is offset by three electrons. Two of them form a filled s-shell close to the nucleus and are strongly bound to the nucleus. The remaining electron is located in the second s-shell. It could accommodate one more electron, but lithium does not have one. All other allowed energy states are free, and electrons fall on them only when atoms arise (order, with strong heating of lithium vapor). The level diagram in a lithium atom is shown in figure 3.
