The art of constructing geometric figures using compasses and rulers was highly developed in Ancient Greece. However, one of the most difficult construction tasks that could be performed at that time was the task of constructing a circle tangent to three given circles. This problem is called the Apollonius problem after the outstanding Greek geometer Apollonius of Perga (about 260-170 BC).

However, the ancient Greek geometrician failed to perform some constructions using straightedge and ruler, and constructions made using other tools were not considered geometric. Such constructions include the construction of a square equal in size to a given circle (“quadrature of the circle”); dividing an arbitrary angle into three equal parts (“trisection of an angle”) and constructing the sides of a cube whose volume is twice the volume of a given cube (“doubling the cube”).

These three significant problems attracted the attention of outstanding mathematicians for many centuries, but the final solution was received only in the middle of the last century, when their unsolvability was proven, that is, the impossibility of these constructions only with the help of a compass and a ruler. These results were obtained not by geometry, but by algebra, which once again emphasized the unity of mathematics.

Another interesting construction task using a compass and ruler is the problem of constructing a regular polygon with a given number of sides. The ancient Greeks knew how to build a regular triangle, a square, a regular pentagon and a fifteen-sided triangle, as well as all the polygons that were obtained from them by doubling the number of sides - and only them.
