⟦folio 14⟧

About one task
Kolmogorov

From the editor

Ten years ago in «Kvant» they talked about the problem that Academician A. N. Kolmogorov posed at a lecture for schoolchildren. Here is its wording:

We fix an angle and a point O inside it on the plane (Fig. 1.). Are there figures other than a circle that can be doctored inside a given corner so that the boundary of the figure always touches the side of the corner and passes through point O?

A. N. Kolmogorov noted that the solution to this problem also has practical significance: “In some factories, cylindrical parts are checked as follows: the part is placed in a tray and treated so that it touches the side of the tray, then a “shup” (a rod that can carry out continuous movement) is brought in from above until it comes into contact with the part. If the screw moves during rotation, the part is rejected. Thus, if there are figures other than a circle that satisfy the conditions of the problem, then cylinders having the omnisection of a point figure will pass perfectly through the OTC” («Kvant», 1971, No. 3, p. 20).

The article provides a partial solution to Kolmogorov's problem.

Fig. 1.

For some types of angles and positions of the point O inside the angle, I managed to find figures other than a circle and satisfying the conditions formulated by A. N. Kolmogorov. The construction of such figures can be done as follows:

Consider a regular polygon; Let's continue its two nonpalatal sides until they intersect. We take the resulting angle as the given angle. As a point O, we take one of the vertices of the polygon, for example, as was done for the pentagon at figure 2, a. Now we will begin to rotate the polygon so that it touches both sides of the angle with some of its vertices. Our task is to deform the polygon into a figure such that, when rotated inside the corner, point O would remain on its boundary all the time; in this case, of course, it is reasonable to “preserve the vertices” of the polygon, since it touches the sides of the angle with it.

We will rotate the polygon until side BC returns to the original side of the angle (the beginning of this rotation, see Figures 2, a - e). At the same time, we will place a pencil at point O obstrike and watch what curves this pencil will draw when we rotate it.

A complete rotation (by an angle from 0 to 2π) of the pentagon naturally develops into a fifth of stages: rotation by an angle from 0 to 2π/5, from 2π/5 to 4π/5, etc. After the first stage, the side AB of the pentagon appears on the initial side of the angle on the BC side, after the second stage - the adjacent side AE, etc. In each of these stages, our angle rests on one of the diagonals pentagon: first - on the diagonal BE, then - on AD, then on EC, on DB and, finally, on CA. Point O is always located on the bisectrix of our angle.
