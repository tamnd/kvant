The current temperature difference $T_1 - T_0$ will be
$$\mathcal{E} = \left( \alpha_1 + \alpha_{11} \right) \left( T_1 - T_0 \right).$$

So $I$, which appears in a closed circuit consisting of the internal resistance $R_0$ of the thermoelement branches and the external resistance $R_0$ of the receiver, will be equal to
$$I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + R_0}.$$

The useful power $P$ that we obtain in the application can be expressed as
$$P = UI,$$

where $U$ is the potential difference at the ends of the external resistance $R$. Since the potential drops in a direct current circuit, with which we are dealing here, in proportion to the resistance, then
$$\frac{U}{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{R}{R + R_0}, \quad U = \frac{\mathcal{E} R}{R + R_0},$$

A
$$P = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2 R}{(R + R_0)^2}.$$

Substituting $\mathcal{E}$ together with its value, we obtain a general expression for the useful power $P$ delivered by the thermoelement:
$$P = \left( \alpha_1 + \alpha_{11} \right)^2 \left( T_1 - T_0 \right)^2 \frac{R}{(R + R_0)^2}.$$

$P$ reaches a nanoscale value when $R = R_0$ (prove this yourself - editor's note); The maximum power that a thermoelement can create in external applications is
$$P_{\max} = \frac{\left( \alpha_1 + \alpha_{11} \right)^2 \left( T_1 - T_0 \right)^2}{4R}.$$

The entire electric power developed by the thermoelement will be
$$\mathcal{E} I = \frac{\left( \alpha_1 + \alpha_{11} \right)^2 \left( T_1 - T_0 \right)^2}{2R}.$$

In order to maintain the temperature difference, it is necessary to continuously transfer thermal energy to the hot junction and relatively from the cold ends. Means,

The main part of the supplied warm UX doit by thermal conduction of both branches to the cold ends. Part of the electricity generated by the thermoelement is wasted on heating both branches. These irreversible processes sharply reduce the efficiency of the thermoelement. If it were possible to completely avoid them (and this is not feasible, since any semiconductor has thermal conductivity and electronic resistance), then the thermoelement would give the most permissible efficiency coefficient according to the laws of thermodynamics

$$\eta_{\max} = \frac{T_1 - T_0}{T_1}.$$

In the example of thermometers for a radio receiver, we can take $T_1 = (350 + 273) \text{K} = 623 \text{K}$ and $T_0 = (50 + 273) \text{K} = 323 \text{K}$, from where
$$\eta_{\max} = \frac{623 - 323}{623} = 0.48 = 48\%.$$

In reality, the efficiency coefficient $\eta$ thermobaterene varies slightly by percentage. This example shows the enormous role played by the processes of thermal conductivity and the release of Joylean heat by current.

If we are interested in obtaining the possible efficiency $\eta$, and not the maximum power $P$, the correspondence between the external resistance $R$ and the internal $R_0$ should be chosen differently: $R$ should be greater than $R_0$. In this case, of all the power $\mathcal{E} I$ developed by the thermoelement, more than half of the power $P$ is used.

Analysis of the thermoelement shows that a sharp decrease in the efficiency coefficient $\eta$ many times compared to the thermal range possible value $\eta_{\max}$ is caused mainly by the irreversible process of thermal conduction.
