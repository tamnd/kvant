The thermal conductivity coefficients $x_1$ and $x_{11}$ of the thermoelement branches play an important role in determining its efficiency; Along with this, thermoelectric forces $a_1$ and $a_{11}$ and resistivities $q_1$ and $q_{11}$ of both branches are important. As calculations show, at given temperatures $T_1$ of the hot and $T_0$ cold ends, the efficiency coefficient of the thermoelement is determined by one value $z$, depending on the listed characteristics of the thermoelement materials:

$$z = \frac{(\alpha_1 + \alpha_{11})^2}{(\sqrt{x_1 q_1} + \sqrt{x_2 q_2})^2}.$$

The larger $z$, the higher the efficiency; for a given value $z$ it is always the same, whatever the values ​​of $a$, $x$ and $q$ separately. The calculation also shows that the efficiency does not depend on the size and shape of the thermoelement branches, but depends only on $T_1$, $T_0$ and $z$. The thermal element efficiency can be expressed as follows:

$$\eta = \frac{T_1 - T_0}{T_1} \frac{\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{2} (T_1 + T_0) z} - 1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{2} (T_1 + T_0) z} + \frac{T_0}{T_1}}.$$

For metals $z$ has values ​​of the order of several units multiplied by $10^{-5}$, for semiconductors $10^{-3}$ and higher, that is, several tens of times more. Accordingly, with such values ​​of $z$, the efficiency of semiconductor thermoelements is tens of times higher than that of metal thermoelements.

Thermoelectric batteries are made from thermoelements. If it is necessary to obtain significant voltages, a large number of thermoelements come in succession. Thus, with thermoelectric force $a_1 + a_{11} = 4 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{B/°C}$ and temperature difference $T_1 - T_0 = 30^\circ \text{C}$, the electron-motive force of one element is $\mathcal{E} = 0,12 \text{B}$. In order to create an electromotive force of 120 volts, you need to connect 1000 such elements in series. If there is a requirement to obtain high currents, the elements are connected in parallel.

The more heat enters through each square centimeter of the hot junction, the smaller the size of the thermowell that emits this electrical energy. These messages determine the choice of one or another thermowell design. The efficiency of thermobater does not depend either on the method of connecting the elements or on its shape.

Refrigerators

Soon after Seebeck's discovery, Peltier observed a phenomenon in 1834 that turned out to be closely related to thermoelectric (which, however, Peltier himself did not suspect). Peltier wanted to prove that heating conductors with weak currents does not obey the Joule-Lenz law common to all conductors and reveals individual characteristics in different metals. For this purpose, he composed chains of different metals. Although it was not true that Peltier expected changes in the heat generated in them by the current, he noticed figurative thermal effects on the edges of two different metals, which he described. The St. Petersburg academician Lenz showed in 1838 with an elegant experiment that at the interface of various conductors, electric current either releases or absorbs a certain amount of heat. Lenz placed a drop of water at the junction of two metals and froze it by passing an electric current.

Now we know that the amount of heat $Q$ released or absorbed at the boundary of two conductors is proportional to the current strength $I$ and the absolute temperature $T$ of the junction of the conductors:

$$Q = A I T.$$
