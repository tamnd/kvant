On the chess page of this issue you got acquainted with the dramatic end of the second world championship match between V. Stepinets and the founder of the national chess school M. Chigorin. And although Chigorin was brighter than Stepinets, superior to him in understanding the art of chess, but when it came to sports stability and composure, at critical moments of the battle Chigorin was noticeably inferior to the first chess king. Only character flaws can explain the fact that Chigorin did not become world champion.

Before offering you a few of Chigorin's combinations, let's give you another editorial example of "chess blindness", taken from nothing less than the 1971 challengers' match (hidden game).

When analyzing an excellent game, this position was on the board of the Soviet “brigade” consisting of M. Taimanov and his three coaches V. Balashov, E. Vasyukov and A. Kotov. All four grandmasters unanimously decided that Fischer could not go for it, since he would lose a pawn after R:f6. When the American grandmaster had already chosen this option, the surprised Taimanov, after some deliberation, took the pawn - 1.R:f6. Shakhferem 1...Qd4+ was like a bolt from the blue for him. It turned out that the four grandmasters simply blundered a rook. After 2.Llf2 Ra1+ White settled. Such chickens show that you don’t need to lose your sense of imor when you blunder a checkmate or a rook - in this respect you are no different from grandmasters...
