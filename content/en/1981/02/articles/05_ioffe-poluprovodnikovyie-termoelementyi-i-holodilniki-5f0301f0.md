The thermal element could be considered as a thermoelectric machine, which, without any moving mechanisms, converts part of the thermal energy heating the hot junction into electrical energy; while the rest of the heat is separated by the cold junction into the environment. But at the same time, a greater flow of heat passes from the hot junction of the thermoelement to the cold junction of thermal conductivity, and some of the electrical energy created by the thermoelement turns into heat of the internal thermoelement and cannot be used. The first practical use of thermozements was carried out in the Soviet Union for the purpose of radiofication of areas without electrical energy. The source of heat here is the hot gases coming out of the glass of the kerosene lamp; they pass inside a vertical tube placed above the glass and heat the internal span of the thermocouples located along the radii of the tube. External spans are cooled with room air; To enhance cooling, these spans are contained with metal radiator plates. In such a device, the internal junctions are heated to 300-350°C, while the temperature of the external junctions does not exceed 60°C. A temperature difference of 250-300°C, maintained by the heat supplied by a kerosene lamp, is sufficient to create the electricity needed to power the radio.

Electrons, which have higher kinetic energy at the hot end, move to the cold end, where they give up excess energy to the atoms, slowing down their thermal motion and increasing the temperature. Electrons with lower kinetic energy located at the cold end, moving to the hot end, weaken the thermal movement and lower the temperature. The thermal conductivity of materials is characterized by a certain coefficient $x$, which can be determined as follows. If we take a cylindrical rod of a given substance with a unit cross section, in which for each unit of length the temperature changes by one degree, then per unit of time an amount of heat equal to $x$ is transferred in the direction from the hot to the cold end.

If the cross-sectional area of ​​the rod is $S$, its length is $L$, the temperature difference at its ends is $\Delta T$, then in time $t$ the amount of heat will transfer from the hot to the cold end by thermal conduction

$$Q = \frac{\Delta T}{L} St.$$

On the other hand, the electric current caused by the same electrons in a given substance is determined by its specific electrical conductivity $\sigma$. In the same rod with length $L$ and cross section $S$ with a potential difference $\Delta U$ between its ends in time $t$ the amount of electricity will pass

$$q = \frac{\Delta U}{L} St.$$

The connection between thermal conductivity $x$ and electrical conductivity $\sigma$ for various metals was established purely empirically by Wiedemann and Franz. Namely: experience has shown that

$$\frac{x}{\sigma} = A_M T,$$

where $T$ is the absolute temperature and $A_M$ is the same factor for all metals. Quantum semiconductor theory also leads to the correspondence $\frac{x}{\sigma} = A_\pi T$, but the coefficient $A_\pi$ has a different meaning.

Such a correspondence between $x$ and $\sigma$ makes it fundamentally impossible to implement reverse thermocouple. For thermal conductivity, it is necessary that $x=0$; To create Joule heat, it is necessary that the resistivity of the thermal cement branches be equal to zero, and the reciprocal value $x$ - infinity. But the requirement that there be $x=0$ and $\sigma \rightarrow \infty$ at the same time is incompatible with the Wiedemann-Franz law.

Let's move on to consider the second type of thermal conductivity. In addition to the transfer of heat by electrons, thermal conductivity in semiconductors is also carried out by the thermal movement of atoms of the crystal lattice. The mechanism of this process can be imagined as follows. Chaotic thermal conductivity of their most diverse vibrations. This includes vibrations of individual atoms one in relation to another, and their vibrations in pairs, threes, etc., up to vibrations of the entire body as a whole. The higher the temperature, the more intense the fluctuations. Each of these numerous vibrations propagates in the body in the form of elastic waves, transferring its energy.

The thermal conductivity coefficient $x$ of a semiconductor thus consists of two parts: thermal conductivity $x_{эл}$, due to electrons, and thermal conductivity $\cdot x_\phi$, created by the thermal movement of the atoms of the body. The value $x_{эл}$ is proportional to the specific electrical conductivity $\sigma$. Therefore, with a change in the concentration of free electrons in a semiconductor, the coefficient $x_{эл}$ changes in proportion to $\sigma$, while $x_\phi$ remains almost unchanged. The overall thermal conductivity $x$ can be expressed as

$$x = A_\pi T \sigma + x_\phi.$$

Our experiments with the real confirmed such a dependence of $x$ on $\sigma$.

The thermal conductivity coefficients $x_1$ and $x_{11}$ of the thermoelement branches play an important role in determining its efficiency; Along with this, thermoelectric forces $a_1$ and $a_{11}$ and resistivities $q_1$ and $q_{11}$ of both branches are important. As calculations show, at given temperatures $T_1$ of the hot and $T_0$ cold ends, the efficiency coefficient of the thermoelement is determined by one value $z$, depending on the listed characteristics of the thermoelement materials:

$$z = \frac{(\alpha_1 + \alpha_{11})^2}{(\sqrt{x_1 q_1} + \sqrt{x_2 q_2})^2}.$$

The larger $z$, the higher the efficiency; for a given value $z$ it is always the same, whatever the values ​​of $a$, $x$ and $q$ separately. The calculation also shows that the efficiency does not depend on the size and shape of the thermoelement branches, but depends only on $T_1$, $T_0$ and $z$. The thermal element efficiency can be expressed as follows:

$$\eta = \frac{T_1 - T_0}{T_1} \frac{\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{2} (T_1 + T_0) z} - 1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{2} (T_1 + T_0) z} + \frac{T_0}{T_1}}.$$

For metals $z$ has values ​​of the order of several units multiplied by $10^{-5}$, for semiconductors $10^{-3}$ and higher, that is, several tens of times more. Accordingly, with such values ​​of $z$, the efficiency of semiconductor thermoelements is tens of times higher than that of metal thermoelements.

Thermoelectric batteries are made from thermoelements. If it is necessary to obtain significant voltages, a large number of thermoelements come in succession. Thus, with thermoelectric force $a_1 + a_{11} = 4 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{B/°C}$ and temperature difference $T_1 - T_0 = 30^\circ \text{C}$, the electron-motive force of one element is $\mathcal{E} = 0,12 \text{B}$. In order to create an electromotive force of 120 volts, you need to connect 1000 such elements in series. If there is a requirement to obtain high currents, the elements are connected in parallel.

The more heat enters through each square centimeter of the hot junction, the smaller the size of the thermowell that emits this electrical energy. These messages determine the choice of one or another thermowell design. The efficiency of thermobater does not depend either on the method of connecting the elements or on its shape.

Refrigerators

Soon after Seebeck's discovery, Peltier observed a phenomenon in 1834 that turned out to be closely related to thermoelectric (which, however, Peltier himself did not suspect). Peltier wanted to prove that heating conductors with weak currents does not obey the Joule-Lenz law common to all conductors and reveals individual characteristics in different metals. For this purpose, he composed chains of different metals. Although it was not true that Peltier expected changes in the heat generated in them by the current, he noticed figurative thermal effects on the edges of two different metals, which he described. The St. Petersburg academician Lenz showed in 1838 with an elegant experiment that at the interface of various conductors, electric current either releases or absorbs a certain amount of heat. Lenz placed a drop of water at the junction of two metals and froze it by passing an electric current.

Now we know that the amount of heat $Q$ released or absorbed at the boundary of two conductors is proportional to the current strength $I$ and the absolute temperature $T$ of the junction of the conductors:

$$Q = A I T.$$

The ranks $Q$ (that is, the gain or loss of heat at the joint) depends on the sign of $I$, that is, the direction of the current.

The current we considered earlier, which arose in the closed circuit of the thermoelement, cools the hot junction and, conversely, heats up the cold junction, releasing heat here. If, using an external source, a reverse current is passed through the thermoelement, it will generate heat at the hot junction and relatively heat from the cold junction. The same junction of two conductors heats up in one direction of current, and cools in the other.

The calculation shows that the temperature difference that can be created in this way, as well as the amount of heat that the current takes away from the junction it cools and transfers to the heated junction, is determined by the same value $z$ as the efficiency of the thermoelement.

The same properties of materials provide certain values ​​of $a$, $x$ and $q$, which characterize the product of electrical energy in a thermoelectric generator and the cooling capacity in a refrigerator.

By maintaining the temperature of the heated junction close to room temperature and continuously removing the heat generated here from it into the environment, it is possible to significantly cool the other junction and, through it, the surrounding air. Thus, with the help of semiconductors characterized by a sufficient value of $z$, it is possible to obtain the required low temperatures in the refrigerator. Home refrigerators can be built on this principle.

On the other hand, you can use the Peltier phenomenon to heat a room, for example, to heat buildings or to heat food. Such problems have long been solved in a simpler way - by releasing Joule heat in a rheostat or a heated plate. The amount of thermal energy released in this case is equal to the amount of electrical energy expended.

Thermoelectric heating of a room differs significantly from the action of a heating tile. By passing electric current through a thermoelectric circuit, in addition to the usual heating of the entire conductor, we cool one junction and heat the other. A certain amount of thermal energy $Q_0$ is optimized from the cold junction, which can be expressed as follows:

$$Q_0 = \alpha T_0 I t,$$

where $\alpha$ is the thermoelectric driving force, $T_0$ is the absolute temperature of the cold junction, $I$ is the current strength and $t$ is the duration of current passage. Correspondence in a warm junction, the absolute temperature of which is denoted by $T_1$, thermal energy is released

$$Q_1 = \alpha T_1 I t,$$

which is greater than heat $Q_0$ in relation to

$$\frac{Q_1}{Q_0} = \frac{T_1}{T_0}.$$

If we limit ourselves to considering the processes at both junctions, they can be described as follows. The electric current extracts heat $Q_0$ from the cold junction and transfers more of its energy $Q_1 - Q_0$ to the warm junction in the form of electrical energy $W$. To the heat $Q_0$ optimized from the cold junction, energy $W$ is added, and their sum $Q_0 + W = Q_1$ is released at the warm junction.

From the given data on the values ​​of $Q_0$ and $Q_1$ it is clear that the ratio of the expended electrical energy $W$ to the heat $Q_1$, which is released at the warm junction, is equal to

$$\frac{W}{Q_1} = \frac{Q_1 - Q_0}{Q_1} = \frac{T_1 - T_0}{T_1}.$$

If, for example, the absolute temperature of a warm junction is $T_1 = 300$ K, which corresponds to $+27^\circ$ C, and the temperature is $T_0 = 270$ K, or $-3^\circ$ C, then

$$\frac{W}{Q_1} = \frac{30}{300} = 0,1.$$
