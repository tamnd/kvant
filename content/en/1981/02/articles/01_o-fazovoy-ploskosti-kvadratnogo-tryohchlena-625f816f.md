Let us correspond to each square trinomial $x^2 + p_0x + q_0$ with a point $(p_0; q_0)$ on the plane $(p, q)$; this correspondence is one-to-one. The parabola $q = \frac{p^2}{4}$ by figure separates the points corresponding to trinomials with two roots from the points corresponding to trinomials without roots. Each line $c^2 + pc + q = 0$ consists of points corresponding to trinomials, one of whose roots is equal to $c$. All these root lines touch the “discriminant parabola” (at figure such lines for $c = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm \frac{2}{3}, \pm \frac{1}{2}$ are depicted).

Each of the regions into which the plane is divided by root lines consists of points corresponding to trinomials, the roots of which are located in a certain way. For example, the points of the yellow quadrilateral correspond to trinomials whose roots $x_1, x_2$ are $-\frac{3}{2} < x_1 < -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2} < x_2 < \frac{2}{3}$.

Consider what can be said about the roots of the trinomials $x^2 + px + q$ if the point $(p, q)$ belongs to any of the shaded regions. The connection between the plane $(p, q)$ and the trinomials $x^2 + px + q$ is described in more detail in the article by V. Vavilov “Grid nomograms” («Kvant», 1978, No. 9).
