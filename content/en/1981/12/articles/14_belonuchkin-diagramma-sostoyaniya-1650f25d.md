State diagram

As is known, any substance, depending on external conditions, can be in a solid, liquid or gaseous state.

If the pressure is low and the temperature is high, the substance usually exists as a gas. At low temperature and high pressure, all substances turn into solids. At some intermediate values ​​of the parameters, liquid exists. This is shown schematically in figure 1. It remains to check the boundaries between the regions of different states - and we will obtain the so-called state diagram of matter in $p - T$ coordinates. Let us recall a few well-known facts that will help us in this matter.

1) Saturated vapor pressure increases with temperature; Accordingly, the boiling point increases with increasing pressure. This means that the liquid-gas (L-G) boundary goes in the following direction - from bottom to right - to top.

2) The melting point of weakness from pressure - the solid-liquid boundary (S - L) goes almost vertically.

3) There is a special point - critical ($T_{кр}, p_{кр}$). As you approach it along the L-G boundary, the properties of the liquid and gas (primarily the densities) differ less and less; at the critical point the differences disappear. the boundary W - G ends at this point.

4) For all substances (with the exception of helium) there is a point - a set of parameters ($T_{тр}, p_{тр}$) at which all three phases (liquid, solid and gaseous) exist. If $T < T_{тр}, p < p_{тр}$, then the liquid state is impossible, only the solid and gaseous states are possible. Consequently, there is a solid-gas boundary ($T - \Gamma$). And since at low temperatures, far from the critical temperature ($T \ll T_{кр}$), the distribution between the liquid and solid state of the city is less than between it and the gas, the behavior of the T - G boundary is similar to the behavior of the F - G boundary.

The qualitative dependence of the phase transition temperature (the so-called transition of a substance from one state - solid, liquid or gaseous - to another) on pressure can be obtained based on one of the most general laws of nature - the Le Chatelier-Brown principle *). Somewhat simplified, it can be formulated as follows:

Any external influence causes changes in the system that reduce the effectiveness of this influence.

Thus, if saturated vapors are in equilibrium with a liquid, as the volume occupied by the system decreases, the vapor pressure begins to increase. The density of liquid is greater than the density of vapor. If the vapor condenses, the density, and therefore the pressure of the remaining vapor, will decrease. The effect of external influence is partially (and if the temperature is maintained constant, then completely) compensated. Consequently, this compensating process - vapor condensation - begins immediately. To prevent condensation, that is, to still increase the vapor pressure, very much so, it is necessary to increase the temperature of the system. Thus, relying (except on the Le Chatelier-Brown principle) only on the fact that the density of the liquid is greater than the density of the vapor, we came to the conclusion that a higher temperature corresponds to a higher saturated vapor pressure.

Most often we are interested in the liquid-gas transition; This is what we will mainly be doing.

Discussion of some constants of the behavior of saturated steam. Let us note, in particular, that the density and pressure (sometimes somewhat old-fashioned saturated control) of saturated vapors are not directly proportional.

above the surface of boiling water in an open pan?

In an open pan, water boils under a pressure of $p_0 = 1$ atm at a temperature of $t_0 = 100^\circ C$. From the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation $pV = \frac{m}{\mu}RT$ we obtain the expression for density:

$$\varrho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{\mu p}{RT}.$$

For the density ratio we have

$$\frac{\varrho_1}{\varrho_0} = \frac{p_1}{p_0} \cdot \frac{T_0}{T_1} = \frac{2 \cdot 373}{1 \cdot 393} \approx 1,9.$$

The result is clear: the pressure should increase by 2 times, but the temperature has increased slightly, which means (this is also clear from the formula $p = nkT$) the density has increased less than twice.

We built a state diagram for the case when the system consists only of the substance whose state we are analyzing. How does the presence of built substances affect the condition? To understand this, let’s solve the following problem.

Problem 2. 18 g of water was poured into an exchange vessel $V = 22,4$ liters at normal air pressure and temperature $t_0 = 0^\circ C$. Then the vessel was closed with an airtight lid and heated to a temperature of $t_1 = 100^\circ C$. Determine the pressure in the vessel.

Unfortunately, you can often hear from schoolchildren two different, but equally incorrect answers.

1) The air pressure in the vessel is $p_0 = 1$ atm, and when heated to a temperature of $T_1$ it returns to a value of $p_1 = p_0 \frac{T_1}{T_0} = 1 \cdot \frac{373}{273} \approx 1,3$ atm. This is greater than the saturated vapor pressure of water at $100^\circ C$; This means that the water will not evaporate, and the total pressure in the vessel will be equal to $p = p_1 = 1,3$ atm.

2) $100^\circ C$ is the boiling point of water. This means that all the water in the vessel will evaporate; the pressure of the resulting steam (in the amount of 1 mol - after all, there was 18 g of water) is not difficult to calculate (at $T_0 = 273$ K 1 mol in a volume of 22.4 liters it creates a pressure of $p_0 = 1$ atm, and at $T_1 = 373$ K the steam pressure will be $p_{п1} \approx 1,3$ atm); the air pressure in the vessel will increase when heated to $p_{в1} \approx 1,3$ atm; the total pressure becomes equal to $p_1 = p_{п1} + p_{в1} \approx 2,6$ atm.

The authors of the first answer forget that the liquid must be in equilibrium with its vapor; this means that at $t_1 = 100^\circ\text{C}$ water will evaporate until its vapor pressure reaches 1 atm.

The second answer is also incorrect, of course, since the pressure of water vapor at $100^\circ\text{C}$ cannot rise above 1 atm.

So, in the vessel the air pressure will be $p_{\text{в1}} \approx 1,3$ atm, the water vapor pressure will be $p_{\text{п1}} = 1$ atm (we have made sure that the mass of water is sufficient to create such pressure), and the total pressure in the vessel will be only $p_1 = p_{\text{п1}} + p_{\text{в1}} = 2,3$ atm.

By the way, the water will not boil. After all, boiling is a process of vaporization not only on the surface of a liquid, but throughout its entire volume. If the temperature is such that the corresponding saturated vapor pressure is equal to the total external pressure, then vapor bubbles are formed inside the liquid and do not collapse under external pressure; Further evaporation of the inside of the bubbles increases their volume, the bubbles float to the surface, burst on the surface - the liquid boils.

Let us emphasize again: evaporation always occurs when the vapor pressure above the surface of the liquid is less than the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature; boiling occurs only when the total pressure (and the internal liquid) does not exceed the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature.

By the way, the Earth is full of water; and relative humidity equal to 100%, that is, saturation of the air with water vapor, is rare. Why, when there is an excess of water, is not the entire atmosphere saturated with water vapor? Maybe enough water simply did not have time to evaporate? Let's estimate the rate of water evaporation.

the amount of water that evaporates at room temperature per unit surface area in an open glass (as well as in a river, stream, or ocean). The saturated vapor pressure of water at temperature $T = 300\text{K}$ is equal to $p_{\text{н}} = 3,5\text{кПа}$.

When water is in equilibrium with its saturated steam, then, strictly speaking, it evaporates. Moreover, the rate of evaporation does not depend on whether there is steam above the surface of the water. It is determined only by how many water molecules have the speed reached to leave the liquid (and the appropriate direction of speed). It’s just that exactly the same number of vapor molecules enters the liquid - the rate of evaporation is equal to the rate of condensation, there is a dynamic equilibrium. And calculating the amount of condensing steam is not so difficult.

The number of vapor molecules falling per unit time (1 s) onto a unit area (1 m²) of the water surface is equal to

$$z = \frac{n |v_x|}{2} \approx \frac{p_{\text{н}}}{2kT} \sqrt{\frac{RT}{M}} \left( \frac{1}{M^2c} \right)$$

(since the calculation is approximate, we do not take into account the differences between the average absolute value $v_x(|v_x|)$ and the root mean square value $v_x(\sqrt{v_x^2})$). The mass of steam condensing on a unit surface area per unit time is obviously equal to

$$m = z \frac{M}{N_{\text{A}}} = \frac{p_{\text{н}}}{2} \sqrt{\frac{M}{RT}} \left( \frac{\kappa r}{M^2c} \right)$$

($N_{\text{A}}$ is Avogadro’s number).

As we agreed, such a mass of water evaporates regardless of air humidity, that is, regardless of the water vapor content in the air (which is characterized by the value $n$). If replenishment of evaporating water does not occur (humidity is very low), then exactly this amount of water will evaporate and disappear from a unit of surface in seconds. Let's calculate the drop in water level in seconds:

$$h = \frac{p_{\text{н}}}{2Q} \sqrt{\frac{M}{RT}} = \frac{3,5 \cdot 10^3}{2 \cdot 10^3} \sqrt{\frac{18 \cdot 10^{-3}}{8,3 \cdot 300}} \approx 4,7 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ м/c} = 4,7 \text{ мм/c}$$

At first glance, there is nothing terrible about this result. But remember that the average depth of the oceans is about 4 km; This means that the time for their complete evaporation is about $10^6$ s, which is a little more than 10 days!

What's the matter? Why do oceans exist?

There are several circumstances that prevent their disappearance.

Let's start with the less important ones. First, relative humidity is not zero. But even if we take it to be 90%, this will only lower the evaporation rate by an order of magnitude. Secondly, not all vapor molecules that strike the surface of water remain in the liquid—those that do not bounce off and are reflected. Consequently, we overestimated the rate of condensation, and therefore the equal rate of evaporation. Let a tenth of the molecules “float” (a number close to the real one, but already underestimated). Let's add some more order. We have extended the life of the oceans to 100 days, to 1001 days. For Scheherezda (and world literature) this is an era, but for the ocean with its billion-year history, three years is a moment.

But let's return to our glass. Further, with the correction we introduced by 100 times, water from a full glass should evaporate completely in less than half an hour. The figure is clearly unrealistic. In reality, a layer of saturated vapor quickly forms above the surface of the water, or more precisely, a layer of air with a relative humidity of 100%. And only before the formation of this layer does the water level drop at a rate of 4.7 mm/s. Once the layer has formed, the rate of condensation becomes almost equal to the rate of evaporation, and a further decrease in the water level is determined by the slow process of diffusion of vapor from the surface of the liquid into the surrounding space.

However, it is not these factors that replace evaporation that play the main role in the fate of the oceans; the oceans would have enough time to completely evaporate. The fact is that we considered equilibrium or almost equilibrium (quasi-equilibrium) systems. And the earth’s atmosphere is an essentially nonequilibrium system. Warm, moist air rises into the cold layers of the atmosphere, where water condenses, and on the way to the Earth's surface the droplets do not have time to evaporate. Ocean waters are replenished, the water cycle in nature continues.
