What can holography do?

Today's schoolchildren, from first-graders to graduates, were born and live in the era of satellites and space flights, color televisions and portable tape recorders, lasers and supersonic aircraft - all this is now instilled and natural. But some three decades ago, in the era of their parents’ childhood, not a color, but a black-and-white television with a minator screen was considered a miracle of technology, and neighbors and friends flocked to it to enjoy the unprecedented spectacle.

At one time, study and delight, a real shock throughout the world, was produced by the launch of the first Soviet artificial Earth satellite, the first orbital flight of Yuri Gagarin. And now, 20-25 years later (a tiny period in the scale of history and not only so much in a person’s life!) we are able to perceive not daily messages about the launch of new satellites, inoculations into the months-long everyday life of astronauts at orbital stations (although, of course, we were worried about them), we are not surprised that man has set foot on the surface of the Moon, that spacecraft are exploring Mars, Venus, Jupiter, produced from the rings of Saturn...

It’s a pity that we get used to it, stop surprising, and take it for granted. After all, if you think about it, these are amazing, fantastic achievements of a man of reason, of human activity. Miracles that have become reality. There is no doubt that similar miracles await us in the future. Many of those who are now reading this issue «Kvant» may themselves be involved in their collaboration. And it is very important, even the areas of “miracles” - the subject of the book by the young English physicist M. Wynion “Acquaintance with Holography” (M., “Mir”, 1980). It was no coincidence that I began the conversation about this book with seemingly constant arguments, although it would have been easier, as they say, to take the bull by the horns and immediately begin to present the essence of the problem or retell the contents of the book. The fact is that the book itself, as the editor of the Russian edition, Doctor of Physics and Mathematical Sciences A.I. Larkin rightly notes, was written by a romantic and enthusiastic person, and passionate not only about the external manifestations and wide-type of holography, but also about the physical study of the method.

However, it is difficult to remain equidistant when doing holography or once coming into contact with it. I remember when I had the opportunity to see a holgram for the first time - a sculpture from the Herritage, three-dimensional, completely real, which could be walked around and examined from all sides and I really wanted to walk, I was just as fascinated as, probably, inexperienced spectators were at the zarekinmatograf, then also called an illusion.

So what is holographics? The author defines it as follows: “Probably, holographics can most easily be described as three-dimensional photography using a laser.” And then he makes a reservation: “This is not a completely satisfactory definition, but there are many other types of three-dimensional photography, but it reflects many of the existence of the moment: holography is a technical method that allows you to arbitrarily “record” the appearance of an object; it creates a three-dimensional image that looks the same material as the real object; the use of lasers was crucial to its development.”

Before presenting the physical principles on which holography is based, the author introduces the reader to the wave theory of light, with concepts such as wavelength, amplitude, phase, wave front, and the phenomenon of integerity. Particular attention is paid to a characteristic that is extremely important for holography—radiation coherence—and it is emphasized that only a laser provides a high degree of coherence, without which it is impossible to produce holography. The first book is devoted to these questions.

It is curious that holography was theoretically discovered (“at the tip of a pen”) and predicted back in 1948, but only 23 years later, in 1971, its “inventor” Denis Gabor was awarded the Nobel Prize. Solsha gained international recognition due to the fact that only in 1960, after the creation of the first laser, did it become possible to implement Gabor’s ideas and become convinced of their practical value.

It is not surprising to Pozny that the purpose of two chapters - the second and third - in Wignon's generally gentle and concise book is devoted to the laser. If in the first chapter I decided to talk about one side of the “two-faced Janus” - light, namely its wave nature, then here light is considered as a flow of quanta. Talks about how a laser “works,” about different types of lasers, their use in medicine, technology, astronomy, communications, metrology, and mentions the prospects for using lasers to create thrust in future package engines.

The fourth chapter, “Additions of Holography,” is the main and, perhaps, most complex chapter in the book. Here the author again turns to the wave properties of light and talks about the phenomenon of a diffraction grating, and acts similarly, transforming the wavefront of light incident on it. 3. The recorded signal is determined by the interface of two beams: direct beam $I$ and beam 2 reflected from the sea surface (Fig. 3). The path difference between these rays $\Delta$ is determined by the formula

$$\Delta = \left( \frac{\lambda}{2} \right) + \left( \frac{h}{\sin \theta} \right) - \left( \frac{h}{\sin \theta} \right) \cos 2\theta = \left( \frac{\lambda}{2} \right) + 2h \sin \theta$$

(the term $\lambda/2$ was added because beam 2 is reflected from an optically denser medium; in this case, the phase of oscillations changes to the opposite). The maxima correspond to a path difference equal to an integer number of wavelengths $k\lambda$, whence

$$\sin \theta_{k \text{ max}} = \left( k - \frac{1}{2} \right) \left( \frac{\lambda}{2h} \right); \quad k = 1, 2, \dots,$$

and the minimums are the path difference equal to an odd number of half-waves $(2k + 1)\lambda/2$, whence

$$\sin \theta_{k \text{ min}} = \frac{k\lambda}{2h}; \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Directly when the star $\theta = 0$ rises, the minimum condition is satisfied. Therefore, at first the integratedness of the received signal will decrease.

Adding the amplitudes of the interfacing waves at the maxima and taking into account the law of amplitude change upon reflection, specified in the conditions, for the resulting field $E_k \text{ max}$ we obtain

$$E_k \text{ max} = E_{\text{пал}} \frac{2n}{n + \sin \theta_{k \text{ max}}}$$

For minima, the resulting wave has an amplitude equal to the difference between the amplitudes of the incident and reflected waves:

$$E_k \text{ min} = E_{\text{пал}} \frac{2 \sin \theta_{k \text{ min}}}{n + \sin \theta_{k \text{ min}}}.$$

The integration ratio in adjacent maxima and minima is equal to

$$p_k = \frac{4n^2 h^2}{k^2 \lambda^2} \left( \frac{n + (k\lambda/2h)}{n + (k-1/2)(\lambda/2h)} \right)^2.$$

For $k = 1$ we have $p_1 \approx 30000$. As $k$ increases (as the star rises above the horizon), the magnitude $p_k$ decreases.
