It is well known that the light source that remained dominant until his time - the electronic incandescent lamp - was preceded by many thousands of years of development and improvement. And yet, from a fundamental point of view, an incandescent lamp differs little from a primitive fire. At the common ends of the multi-year evolutionary ladder we find a heated creative body as an emitter. Initially

Let us first examine the usual sources of “solid light”. We flash the match, giving a yellowish flame and light for a short time. The flame temperature is very high, above 1500°C. At the same time, if you monitor the burning of a match more carefully, at first (at least in some types of matches) a peculiar sharp yellow glow is clearly noticeable, the same as when introducing table salt into a seedless gas flame. When the flame is established, part of it, near the barrel of the match, has a faint blue color. However, the main part of the flame glows with the familiar yellow-orange, “body” light, depending on the incandescence of the sahka dust particles in the flame. In a stearin candle we encounter the same picture - a yellow-orange flame and a faintly glowing blue part at the wick; and here the light comes mainly from a part of the coal concentrated in the flame and heated

The first models of Lodygin and Edison lamps used a carbon filament heated by current. In a coal lamp, the filament temperature is approximately 100-200 degrees higher than in candles and fires. In modern tungsten lamps, the filament is heated to approximately 2700°C. In terms of the quality of the light emitted, these lamps are still closer to a fire than to the Sun. The obstacle is the melting of metals and their evaporation. Tungsten is most suitable in this regard (melting point 3380°C and low evaporation).

Further, upon superficial observation, some common features are striking in a wide variety of warm light sources - matches, candles, incandescent lamps, the Sun. One of these features is as follows. A source at the same temperature shines brighter the darker, blacker the luminous body. For example, at the same temperature, quartz glass almost does not shine, but a piece of black coal emits very strongly. Blackness, like whiteness, of course, cannot increase indefinitely; for it there is a limit when all the light, of any color, falling on a body is absorbed by it without a trace. A body with this property is called an “absolutely black body.” It is not very difficult to realize such a body with a greater approximation to the required conditions. Everyone knows that if on a bright sunny day, when everything around is glowing and glittering, you look at a product through an open window or door into the interior of a closed room, then the internal parts of each will be coal black, despite the fact that the rooms may be whitewashed or covered with light wallpaper. This happens because the rays of light, having entered the inner light black black window, are inevitably absorbed there due to multiple reflections and reflections and do not exit.

From here it becomes clear how to build an absolutely black body: it can be made from any material - coal, iron, white porcelain, etc., if you arrange some closed space with a small hole.

An absolute black body can be heated to a high temperature, for example, incandescent, and the heating can be kept constant. Inside the post-body, light will be emitted from the walls, which must be reabsorbed at the walls. Our body is completely black, so its absorption, according to the conditions, is maximum, that is, complete. But at the same time, we have taken measures to ensure that the body temperature is constant, so it is possible to absorb more power from the maximum radiation, otherwise the temperature balance will be disrupted and the body will heat up.

Thus, we come to the conclusion that not only absorption, but also emission of an absolute black body should be maximum. At the same time, from the same reasoning it follows that radiation, since it is maximum, as well as absorption, cannot depend on the material from which the body is made. This conclusion has enormous theoretical significance. This is the reason for the great similarity between a wide variety of thermal light sources. A fire, a match, the Sun, an incandescent light bulb are quite “black” in their properties, they are close to an absolutely black body. The difference is largely due to the fact that the temperature of the Sun is approximately 6000°C, and that of matches is about 1500°C. If the temperature is low, the body cannot glow by heating with dark visible light, it will emit mainly invisible wavelengths of infrared rays. As the body heat increases, it begins to emit a dark red color. As the temperature increases further, the radiation becomes orange, yellow and finally white. Thus, based on the most general ideas about bodies (namely, assuming that they can absorb light to one degree or another and be at a constant temperature), we come to the conclusion that any body - solid, liquid, gaseous - must necessarily emit thermal radiation, if only it is in thermal equilibrium at a temperature above absolute zero. Depending on the degree of heating, this light will be visible or invisible.

As already mentioned above, it can be easily proven that there cannot be a body whose radiation would exceed the radiation of an absolute black body at the same temperature.

In this sense, an absolute black body is the best, most perfect thermal emitter, and Faraday, in the words quoted at the beginning of the chapter, had reason to be surprised at the general candle, the properties of which are quite close to the properties of an absolute black body, not to mention its practical applications.

Such “perfection” of an absolutely black body and candles and incandescent lamps close to it, however, is very relative and conditional. The fact is that all “thermal emitters” are extremely uneconomical in the usual technical sense of the word; they convert only a tiny fraction of the energy they consume into visible light. For a completely black body located at 2000°C, only 0.3% of the energy turns into visible light; at a temperature of 3000°C, the efficiency rises to 3%. The most advantageous temperature for a completely black body should be around 6000°C, that is, at the temperature of the Sun. The reason for this, at first glance, surprising coincidence is that the human eye, biologically, in the process of development, has become accustomed to sunlight, and therefore light from a thermal emitter with solar temperature is the most economical. However, the economy of even this highest absolute black body is not great; The useful coefficient of the contract is approximately 13%, that is, 87% of the total energy consumed by the source is useless for the eye. For the most economical modern incandescent lamps with a power of 100 W, the useful candle contract coefficient is 2-3%.

Natural impact question: why can’t all the energy imparted to an absolute black body, or at least a significant part of it, be converted into visible light?

It was not easy for physicists to obtain an answer to this question; To do this, it was necessary to carry out, in essence, a whole revolution in science; it was necessary to put aside much that was generally acceptable, which was considered an immutable truth.

If a completely black body remains constant at the same temperature and at the same time emits light, then, consequently, all the energy intermittently supplied to the body is converted into radiation. Only a tiny fraction of this radiation is visible; the rest is concentrated mainly in the region of invisible infrared rays with lower frequencies (longer wavelengths) than visible light. The supplied energy is converted into light and distributed among different waves, creating a continuous spectrum of thermal radiation. The manufacturing process of converting mechanical or electrical energy into heat. The number of molecules in a given volume is finite, and, using the usual rules of statistical calculation, it is possible to determine the most probable average energy of the molecules and the law of energy distribution among the molecules.

Until the end of the 19th century, it was generally accepted in science that light is a wave movement of a medium that fills space. But if light is really like this, then, unlike particles of matter, the number of which in a limited volume is finite, there should be an infinite number of possibilities for the distribution of light energy over light waves, subject to equilibrium in thermal radiation.

In fact, since the radiation of an absolutely black body for all waves cannot depend on the nature of the substance from which the body is made, there cannot be any characteristic lines or bands in its spectrum; all parts of only oscillations must be present, from very small to very large in a continuous continuum. Moreover, from the point of view of the old, classical wave theory of light, all parts are valid and do not have any advantage over another. And this means that for each infinitely small portion of the spectrum, on average, under equilibrium conditions, there is the same infinitesimal share of energy.

It is clear, on the other hand, that as we move from small parts from to large, accepting to infinitely large, we will encounter an increasing number of infinitesimal sections of the part from, each of which accounts for the same infinitesimal fraction of energy. In other words, from the point of view of the classical wave theory of light under conditions of thermal equilibrium, the radiation energy must necessarily increase continuously as it moves across the spectrum from the red to the violet part. This conclusion, however, most decisively diverges from experiment. In fact, in all heat sources, starting with an absolute black body, there is a maximum radiation, which in the case of temperatures below 4000 ° C is in the infrared region of the spectrum and only with further heating it gradually moves to the visible (yellow, green, etc.) part. In the physical region, consistent with the theoretical conclusion, the energy at the temperatures we are accustomed to does not increase, but, on the contrary, drops sharply. Such a contradiction of experience with the very general ideas about matter and light was rightly called upon physics in its time by the “violet catastrophe”; it really signifies the need for a complete rejection of many established views, and outlined the goal of a revolution in natural science.

In the experiment, it turned out that energy is distributed among light frequencies in approximately the same way as thermal energy is distributed among molecules, that is, it has no maximum. The only possible explanation of the experiment (to a certain extent suggested by analogies with molecules) is the following. Contrary to all views and the undoubted wave characteristic of light in space, we have to accept that its energy is concentrated in certain centers, that light is absorbed and emitted by molecules only in the form of whole portions of energy, called quanta. The amount of quantum energy is not constant; it is proportional to the frequency of light vibrations v. The proportionality factor h is always the same; it amounts to a small, but now precisely known value, equal to 6.62 × 10⁻²⁷ erg s.

The quantum energy hν is completely negligible for radio waves, where therefore the primary structure of the radiation is practically not replaced; but the quantum acquires increasingly tangible meanings as it passes through visible light to X-rays. Here the “grain” of light energy manifests itself completely clearly and sharply.
