Pentamino
on the plane
and in space

A pentomino is a figure that is a polygon made up of five identical squares *); the complete pentomino collection is depicted at figure before the title. Of course, in all the problems discussed below, pentominoes can be inverted.

Pentominoes make wonderful puzzles. So, from twelve pentominoes you can form a rectangle $4 \times 15$, $5 \times 12$, $6 \times 10$,

*) Such polygons, composed not only of five squares, but also of one, two, three, four, six, etc. squares, were introduced into mathematical use by the American mathematician S. V. Golom b. He proposed a common name for them - polyominoes. You can read about them in his book “Polimino” (M., “Mir”, 1975). See also «Kvant», 1977, No. 2, 3 p. covers and 1979, No. 11, p. 22.

and the latter - 2389 in fundamentally different ways (two solutions obtained from each other by rotation or reflection are considered the same); Of course, the computer helped find these 2389 solutions. But the rectangle $3 \times 20$ of twelve pentominoes can be folded only in two different ways; will find them. And two rectangles $5 \times 6$ can be folded so that you get either a rectangle $5 \times 12$ or $6 \times 10$ (check it!).

A regular chessboard with four open squares can also be made from pentominoes in many ways. This “incomplete” board looks quite elegant (three assembly options are shown at figure 1).

But the figure depicted on figure 2 cannot be folded from pentominoes: at the edges it is limited by 22 squares, and if you count how many squares of each pentomino can be on the edge of this figure, then the total for all pentominoes will be 21. The figures depicted on figure 3 cannot be folded from pentominoes, but a short and elegant proof of this fact has not yet been found.

Now imagine that pentominoes can be curved along the sides of the squares. Then, using all the pentominoes, you can paste over the surface of the figure depicted at figure 4. Of course, you will have to strain your imagination and show ingenuity. You can first construct a net of the figure and fill it with pentominoes. But we think the following method is more successful.

Let's look at figure 4. In our field of vision there are the front, top and right squares of the surface of our figure. Let's remove these squares and draw the remaining part of the surface, that is, the back, bottom and left squares (Fig. 5). Now, having designated each pentomino with its own symbol, we write on each heel square of the surface the symbol corresponding to the given pentomino.

One of the possible pastings for the figure figure 4 is shown at figure 6.

“Flexible” pentominoes can be used to cover a large number of polyhedra with a surface of 60 squares. Try to come up with such polyhedra. Oddly enough, the middle ones do not have a single straight parallelepiped. We are waiting for your suggestions - we will publish the best of them.

As just said, one parallelepiped cannot be all pentominoes. But two identical parallelepipeds 1×3×3 (on the surface of each of them there are 30 squares) can be pasted over like this. You can also cover six 1×1×2 parallelepipeds. Do it! Try to list all possible sets of parallelepipeds (not necessarily identical) pasted over with a set of pentominoes. (Of course, the number of squares on the surface of each such parallelepiped must be a multiple of a heel.)

Finally, cover two identical “cross-shaped” polyhedrons shown in figure 7 with a set of pentominoes.
