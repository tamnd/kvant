Let us first note that if we add two consecutive digits of the same parity to any place of a (2m-2) digit valid number or add a digit of the same parity to its beginning and end, then the resulting 2m-digit number will remain valid.

Prove that in any $(2m+1)$-digit number you can cross out one of the digits so that the resulting 2-digit number is fair.

Now we prove the required statement by indication of $m$. For $m=1$ it is true, since in a three-digit number you can always cross out a digit so that the remaining two have the same parity. Let us assume that the statement of the problem is true for some $m$, and prove that it is true for $m+1$.

In any $(2m+3)$-valued number, it will always find two digits of the same parity, either next to each other, or one at the beginning and the other at the end of the number. Crossing out these two digits for a while, we get a $(2m+1)$-digit number, in which, according to the indication assumption, one can cross out one digit so that the resulting 2-digit number will be valid. Having restored two deleted digits of the same parity in it, we obtain, according to the remark made, a fair $(2m+2)$-digit number.

F689. On the main horizontal table there is a stack of paper containing 500 sheets. The hundredth sheet (counting spiza) is larger than the others. Let us find the kinetic energy of a hoop rotating with angular velocity ω around the $O'O''$ axis in the position as indicated in the conditions.

The linear velocities of different points on the hoop are different; Accordingly, the contributions made by individual sections of the hoop to the total kinetic energy are different. Let us find the kinetic energy of a pair of small sections $A_1$ and $A_2$ of the hoop, located so that $A_1OA_2 = \pi/2$ (Fig. 2). The size of each section is $R \cdot \Delta\varphi$, the mass is $m = \frac{M}{2\pi R} R \cdot \Delta\varphi = \frac{M}{2\pi} \cdot \Delta\varphi$ (the size of $R \cdot \Delta\varphi$ of the present is small, which varies in the radii of the circles along which the points of the sections rotate, you can run). $$v_1 = \omega |O_1A_1|, \quad v_2 = \omega |O_2A_2|,$$

where $|O_1A_1|, |O_2A_2|$ are the radii of the circles along which the points of sections $A_1$ and $A_2$ rotate around the axis $O'O''$. As can be seen from figure 2,

$$|O_1A_1| = \sqrt{|OA_1|^2 - |OO_1|^2} = R \sqrt{1 - \cos^2 \alpha \cdot \cos^2 \varphi},$$

$$|O_2A_2| = \sqrt{|OA_2|^2 - |OO_2|^2} = R \sqrt{1 - \cos^2 \alpha \cdot \sin^2 \varphi}.$$

$$E_n = \frac{1}{2} m (v_1^2 + v_2^2) = \frac{M}{4\pi} \cdot \Delta\varphi \cdot \omega^2 R^2 (1 + \sin^2 \alpha).$$

Considering any pair of sections whose radius vectors form an angle π/2, we obtain the same expression for their kinetic energy. The number of such pairs is obviously equal to π/Δφ. Therefore, the total energy of the spinning hoop is

$$E = \frac{\pi}{\Delta\varphi} E_n = \frac{M}{4} \omega^2 R^2 (1 + \sin^2 \alpha).$$

According to the law of conservation of energy, the work that must be done to spin the hoop to angular velocity ω is equal to

$$A = \frac{M}{4} \omega^2 R^2 (1 + \sin^2 \alpha).$$

For the special case α = π/2 (the hoop rotates in a horizontal plane around a vertical axis)

$$E_r = \frac{M}{2} \omega^2 R^2.$$

For α = 0 (the hoop rotates around the vertical diameter)

F690. Between points $A$ and $B$ on the surface of the Moon, located at an angular distance $90^\circ$, there is a straight channel (Fig. 1), filled with air at normal temperature. Air pressure in the middle of the channel $p_C = 10^5$ Pa. Find the air pressure in the channel near the surface of the Moon. The moon is considered to be a homogeneous sphere with a diameter of $D = 3480$ km. The acceleration $g_L$ of free fall on the surface of the Moon is six times less than on Earth.

Let us consider a low-altitude air station $\Delta y$, located at a distance $y$ from the center of the Moon, and write the condition:

$$\varrho g \Delta y = -\Delta p,$$

(*)

where $\varrho$ is the air density, $g$ is the rooting of free fall at a given depth, $\Delta p$ is the pressure difference at heights $y + \Delta y$ and $y$.

We find the air density from the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation:

$$pV = \frac{m}{\mu} RT, \quad \varrho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{\mu}{RT} p,$$

where $p$ is the air pressure at the selected depth.

Now let’s find out how the free fall root changes as we “deepen” into the Moon. Let us select the inner Lun - a ball of radius $a$ - a ball of radius $y < a$ (Fig. 2). If a material point of mass $m_0$ is placed on the surface of this smaller ball, then in reality it will only take on the smaller ball. The total effect of gravitational forces between our/material point and all parts in the protected area (see Fig. 2) is zero. Then the rooting of $g$ at point $C$ (with coordinate $y$) is determined by the formula

$$G \frac{m_0 m_y}{y^2} = m_0 g,$$

and rooting $g_L$ at point $K$ by the formula

$$G \frac{m_0 M}{a^2} = m_0 g_L,$$

where $m_y$ and $M$ are the masses of smaller and larger balls, respectively. Dividing the formulas term by term and taking into account that the masses of the balls are proportional to their volumes (and therefore to the cubes of their radii), we obtain:

$$g = g_L \frac{y}{a}.$$

Let us substitute the resulting expressions for $q$ and $g$ into equation (*):

$$\frac{\mu p}{RT} \frac{g_L y}{a} \Delta y = -\Delta p,$$

or

$$\frac{\Delta p}{p} = -\frac{\mu g_L}{RTa} y \Delta y.$$

If the finite increments $\Delta p$ and $\Delta y$ are replaced by the infinitesimal increments $dp$ and $dy$, we obtain the differential equation

$$\frac{dp}{p} = -\frac{\mu g_L}{RTa} y dy.$$

The solution to this equation is found by integration:
