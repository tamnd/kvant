The study of the Milky Way became one of the main tasks of astronomy in the 18th and 19th centuries, and we now know that the stars of the Milky Way belong to the Hypanthus star system, which contains more than one hundred billion stars, including the star closest to us - the Sun. The general features of the structure and dynamics of this system, called the Galaxy*), have been reliably elucidated only in the last 30-40 years. At the same time, it became known that there are other systems of this kind in the Universe - galaxies.

The movement of stars in galaxies is determined by gravity. The law of mutual gravitation of bodies, established in studies of gravity, in observations of the movements of bodies in the Solar System, is also valid in star systems that are millions and billions of times larger than the Solar System; This reveals the universal, universal nature of gravity.

Galaxy and galaxies

In the Galaxy, there are two main structural elements: the disk and the spherical component. The disk is formed by young and bright stars, it has an angle in the middle, so that it looks like a chechenutsu or lens (Fig. 1). The diameter of the disk is approximately 75 thousand light years, its transverse size is ten times smaller; The Sun is located in the disk at a distance of two thirds of the radius from the center. The disk of the Galaxy visible from the inside appears to us as a strip of the Milky Way.

In addition to the disk stars, there are other, less bright stars in the Galaxy; they fill, converging towards the center, a spherical volume with a diameter close to the diameter of the disk. The densest central part of the Galaxy is its core (see Fig. 1).

One of the most impressive astronomical phenomena is the spiral pattern of galaxies (Fig. 2). It is formed by the youngest, brightest stars of the disk component. Our Galaxy and other gases

Movement of stars in the galactic disk

The stars of the disk component of the Galaxy move in almost circular orbits. Their movement is similar to the image of planets around the Sun, although in the Galaxy there is no central body as overwhelming in mass as the Sun in the Solar System, which alone would create the attraction of all bodies to the center. The core of our Galaxy has a small mass and is not what holds the stars in their orbits. The force of attraction towards the center is created in the Galaxy by the joint gravitations of the stars themselves.

A star moving in a circular orbit of radius $r$ is affected by the attraction of stars located inside this orbit. The gravitational force created by the external stars is directed in different directions and balances each other *). The stars “do not notice” the external gravitational masses, and the mass of all internal stars acts as if it were entirely concentrated in the center. In accordance with this and on the basis

According to the law of universal gravitation, the force of attraction of a star to the center is given by the formula

$$F = G \frac{m \cdot M(r)}{r^2}.$$  (1)

Here $m$ is the mass of the star, $M(r)$ is the total mass inside the orbit of radius $r$, $G = 6,67 \cdot 10^{-11} \text{ m}^3 (\text{кг} \cdot \text{с}^2)$ is the gravitational constant. The force $F$ is directed towards the center of the Galaxy. At a star speed of $v$, the gravitational force creates centripetal acceleration

$$\frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{F}{m}.$$  (2)

From equations (1) and (2) the relationship between the speed of the star, the radius of the orbit and the mass of the star follows:

$$v^2 = \frac{G \cdot M(r)}{r}.$$  (3)

The same formula describes the circular motions of the planets and the formation of a satellite around the Earth.

Formula (3) allows us to estimate the velocities of stars in the Galactic disk from known values ​​of the orbital radius and mass inside it. If the orbital radius is comparable in magnitude to the total radius of the Galaxy, that is, $r \sim R \sim 10^{20} \text{ m}$, then by mass $M(r)$ we need to understand values ​​comparable to the mass of the entire Galaxy: $M(r) \sim M \sim 10^{41} \text{ кг}$. (The mass of the Galaxy is found by multiplying the average mass of stars $m \sim 10^{30} \text{ кг}$ by the total number of stars in the Galaxy $N \sim 10^{11}$.) For these values ​​$r$ and $M(r)$ one finds

Dynamics of the spherical component

The motion of stars in the spherical component of the Galaxy is different; their orbits are not circular, but highly elongated. Some orbits are very close to straight lines, and in principle, purely rectilinear, radial orbits are possible.

In order to understand the peculiarities of such a movement, it is worth remembering first one question that, it seems, they like to ask at university entrance exams: how will a stone move when thrown into a shaft drilled into the Earth of Freedom through the center? Obviously, the stone will first fall with acceleration towards the center along the radius; then, having passed the center, it begins to slow down; then it will stop for a moment and fly back, increasing its speed; but having flown past the center, it again begins to slow down, stop, fly back to the center, etc. This will be a periodic oscillatory movement from one aposterium, that is, the point of maximum distance from the center, to another.

Similar oscillatory movements around the Galaxy are performed by the stars of its spherical component. However, the fact that their orbits are slightly different from purely radial ones changes little in this picture. The behavior of the speed of radial motion of stars to help the quantities of interest to us, we will use the method of dimension $M, R, G$:

$$[M] = \kappa r, [R] = m, [G] = \frac{M^3}{k\Gamma \cdot c^2}.$$

The mean variable speed $\vec{v}^2$ that interests us has the dimension

$$[\vec{v}^2] = \frac{M^2}{c^2}.$$

The method of dimensional analysis and order-of-magnitude estimates is indispensable when an accurate theory is either too complex mathematically or is impossible due to uncertainty or lack of available observed or experimental data. Formally, it seems simple, but essentially it assumes that first there is a fairly general qualitative understanding of what is happening. Both in circular and radial (or close to them) orbits, stars perform finite motion, that is, during their movements they do not go beyond a certain limited volume: their connection in the stellar system is their common gravitational field. The bound state of stars ensures a time-invariant, stationary state of the system as a whole.

Galaxy masses

There is only one way to determine the masses of stellar systems - by the gravitational force created by them. Gravity manifests itself in the movements of stars,

and if it is possible to change their speed characteristics and the size of the system, then the mass can be found using formulas (3) or (4).

The first is applied to spiral galaxies with disks, and the second is applied to spherical component galaxies and to elliptical galaxies, which are dynamically similar to them.

In both cases, we are most often talking about an approximate estimate, and therefore, by omitting the uncertain factor in (4), we can write a general estimate formula for the mass of the galaxy:

$$M \sim \frac{\overline{v^2} R}{G}.$$ (5)

The masses of spiral galaxies found in this way range from $3 \cdot 10^{-8} M_\odot$ to $3 \cdot 10^{11} M_\odot$, where $M_\odot$ is the mass of the Sun; the range of masses of elliptical galaxies is wider - from $3 \cdot 10^6 M_\odot$ to $3 \cdot 10^{12} M_\odot$; The masses of irregular galaxies are relatively small - from $10^6 M_\odot$ to $10^9 - 10^{10} M_\odot$.

Many round galaxies, including our Galaxy, usually have several small satellite galaxies. These satellites orbit around the central galaxy, and by analyzing their movements it is also possible to obtain a dynamic estimate of the masses of the round galaxy. If the central galaxy lies entirely within the orbits of its satellites, and this is precisely the picture observed in photographs, then the gravitational forces acting on all satellites are due to the same gravitational mass.

Taking formula (3) in this case, we find that the speed $v_1$ of the expression of a close satellite with an orbital radius $r_1$ is greater than the speed $v_2$ of a more distant satellite with an orbital radius $r_2$:

$$\frac{v_1}{v_2} = \left( \frac{r_2}{r_1} \right)^{1/2},$$

and the speed decreases with the radius of the orbit according to the law

$$v(r) \sim r^{-1/2}$$ (6)

(the planets of the solar system also obey this law).

I am concerned that in the families of satellites of our Galaxy, the Andromeda Galaxy and some other massive galaxies, law (6) is not satisfied. In observations, the velocities of satellites located at distances of up to ten radii of the central galaxy changed, and each time it turned out that the velocities did not decrease with the orbital radius, but remained (with a certain accuracy) constant. This circumstance is reflected by the dotted line on the rotation curve (see Fig. 3).

In search of an explanation, Soviet astronomers from the Tartu Observatory and American astronomers from Princeton University suggested discussing one further possibility. What if the galaxy's mass is actually not limited to what lies within the volume visible in photographs?
Could it be that, in addition to stars, there could be some other gravitating masses distributed over a much larger volume around galaxies?
This is how the hypothesis about the invisible, “hidden” masses of galaxies arose.

It is assumed that this additional mass forms the corona of the galaxy, which extends over long distances and reaches the most distant orbits of satellite galaxies. If so, then the satellites do not move in emptiness, but among invisible, “hidden” masses. Then the gravitating mass influencing the motion of the satellite consists of the massive stars of the galaxy and the “hidden” mass located inside the satellite’s orbit. As a result, the total gravitating mass increases with increasing orbital radius; because of this, the velocities of satellite galaxies do not obey law (6). If the velocities are the same in orbits of different radii, then, as can be seen from formulas (3) and (5), with increasing $r$ the gravitating mass should increase according to the law

$$M \sim r.$$ (7)

It follows that the total mass of the galaxy may be many times greater than the mass of visible stars. If a family of satellite galaxies moving at the same speeds is located at a distance, say, $3R$ from the center of the galaxy, then the total mass of the entire system is three times the mass of the visible stars, that is, the visible body of the galaxy.

As the mass increases according to law (7), the density of matter in the galaxy decreases from the center outwards as $r^{-2}$ (we will show this in Appendix II). At a sufficient distance from the center, the density drops faster and disappears.

* * *

The “hidden” mass hypothesis explains the dynamics of satellite galaxies, but at the same time raises new difficult questions. First of all, what is this mass? The answer is unknown. It is believed that it can consist of masses of small and very faint stars. If so, then there are more stars in the City Galaxy than can be seen even with the best telescope.

But this is not the only possibility. Recently, astronomers and physicists have been carefully discussing the idea that the “hidden” mass is a gas of elementary particles - neutrinos. These particles have been known in physics for almost half a century, but it is still unclear whether they have a rest mass (like a proton, electron and other particles) or their rest mass is zero (like a photon). In the presence of a rest mass, even if much smaller than that of an electron (the lightest of particles, for which it is precisely known that it has a rest mass), a neutrino could actually create extended clouds around massive galaxies, invisible, but gravitating.

In recent experiments by Moscow physicists, carried out not with cosmic, but with laborator neutrinos, errors occurred during radioactive decay, and signs were discovered that neutrinos may actually have a rest mass. It is reported to be approximately ten thousand times less than the rest mass of an electron. This is a preliminary result, but if it is confirmed, then it will be necessary to assume that cosmic neutrinos also have rest mass. Then the idea of ​​a neutrino “hidden” mass will receive a reliable physical justification.

Let us prove that if the total mass $M$, contained inside a sphere of radius $r$, returns proportionally to $r$, then the density $\rho(r)$ (spherical symmetric distribution) according to the law $\rho \sim r^{-2}$.

Let us select a thin spherical layer of thickness $l$ with an outer radius $r$. The mass of such a layer is equal to $\Delta M = M(r) - M(r-l) = Kr - K(r-l) = Kl$, where $K$ is the proportionality coefficient. The volume of such a layer is equal to $\Delta V = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 - \frac{4}{3} \pi \cdot (r-l)^3 = \frac{4}{3} \pi (3r^2 l - 3rl^2 + l^3)$ (the volume of a sphere of radius $r$ is equal to $\frac{4}{3} \pi r^3$). The average density in the layer is
$$\rho_{cp} = \frac{\Delta M}{\Delta V} = \frac{3Kl}{4\pi(3r^2 l - 3rl^2 + l^3)} = \frac{K}{4\pi r^2} \sim r^{-2},$$
