Galaxies that lack a spiral pattern generally do not have a disk component. Their spherical component (in reality, it is always solid to one degree or another) is represented by an ellipse; Therefore, such galaxies are called elliptical. There are also structureless-looking formations - irregular galaxies that look like clouds of stars. Finally, there are now very concentrated star systems, reminiscent of the dense central regions, cores of spiral and elliptical galaxies, only they are even denser and much more massive.

Movement of stars in the galactic disk

The stars of the disk component of the Galaxy move in almost circular orbits. Their movement is similar to the image of planets around the Sun, although in the Galaxy there is no central body as overwhelming in mass as the Sun in the Solar System, which alone would create the attraction of all bodies to the center. The core of our Galaxy has a small mass and is not what holds the stars in their orbits. The force of attraction towards the center is created in the Galaxy by the joint gravitations of the stars themselves.

A star moving in a circular orbit of radius $r$ is affected by the attraction of stars located inside this orbit. The gravitational force created by the external stars is directed in different directions and balances each other *). The stars “do not notice” the external gravitational masses, and the mass of all internal stars acts as if it were entirely concentrated in the center. In accordance with this and on the basis

According to the law of universal gravitation, the force of attraction of a star to the center is given by the formula

$$F = G \frac{m \cdot M(r)}{r^2}.$$  (1)

Here $m$ is the mass of the star, $M(r)$ is the total mass inside the orbit of radius $r$, $G = 6,67 \cdot 10^{-11} \text{ m}^3 (\text{кг} \cdot \text{с}^2)$ is the gravitational constant. The force $F$ is directed towards the center of the Galaxy. At a star speed of $v$, the gravitational force creates centripetal acceleration

$$\frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{F}{m}.$$  (2)

From equations (1) and (2) the relationship between the speed of the star, the radius of the orbit and the mass of the star follows:

$$v^2 = \frac{G \cdot M(r)}{r}.$$  (3)

The same formula describes the circular motions of the planets and the formation of a satellite around the Earth.

Formula (3) allows us to estimate the velocities of stars in the Galactic disk from known values ​​of the orbital radius and mass inside it. If the orbital radius is comparable in magnitude to the total radius of the Galaxy, that is, $r \sim R \sim 10^{20} \text{ m}$, then by mass $M(r)$ we need to understand values ​​comparable to the mass of the entire Galaxy: $M(r) \sim M \sim 10^{41} \text{ кг}$. (The mass of the Galaxy is found by multiplying the average mass of stars $m \sim 10^{30} \text{ кг}$ by the total number of stars in the Galaxy $N \sim 10^{11}$.)
