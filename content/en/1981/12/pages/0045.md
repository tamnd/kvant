The qualitative dependence of the phase transition temperature (the so-called transition of a substance from one state - solid, liquid or gaseous - to another) on pressure can be obtained based on one of the most general laws of nature - the Le Chatelier-Brown principle *). Somewhat simplified, it can be formulated as follows:

Any external influence causes changes in the system that reduce the effectiveness of this influence.

Thus, if saturated vapors are in equilibrium with a liquid, as the volume occupied by the system decreases, the vapor pressure begins to increase. The density of liquid is greater than the density of vapor. If the vapor condenses, the density, and therefore the pressure of the remaining vapor, will decrease. The effect of external influence is partially (and if the temperature is maintained constant, then completely) compensated. Consequently, this compensating process - vapor condensation - begins immediately. To prevent condensation, that is, to still increase the vapor pressure, very much so, it is necessary to increase the temperature of the system. Thus, relying (except on the Le Chatelier-Brown principle) only on the fact that the density of the liquid is greater than the density of the vapor, we came to the conclusion that a higher temperature corresponds to a higher saturated vapor pressure.

Most often we are interested in the liquid-gas transition; This is what we will mainly be doing.

In an open pan, water boils under a pressure of $p_0 = 1$ atm at a temperature of $t_0 = 100^\circ C$. From the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation $pV = \frac{m}{\mu}RT$ we obtain the expression for density:

$$\varrho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{\mu p}{RT}.$$

For the density ratio we have

$$\frac{\varrho_1}{\varrho_0} = \frac{p_1}{p_0} \cdot \frac{T_0}{T_1} = \frac{2 \cdot 373}{1 \cdot 393} \approx 1,9.$$

The result is clear: the pressure should increase by 2 times, but the temperature has increased slightly, which means (this is also clear from the formula $p = nkT$) the density has increased less than twice.

We built a state diagram for the case when the system consists only of the substance whose state we are analyzing. How does the presence of built substances affect the condition? To understand this, let’s solve the following problem.

Problem 2. 18 g of water was poured into an exchange vessel $V = 22,4$ liters at normal air pressure and temperature $t_0 = 0^\circ C$. Then the vessel was closed with an airtight lid and heated to a temperature of $t_1 = 100^\circ C$. Determine the pressure in the vessel.

Unfortunately, you can often hear from schoolchildren two different, but equally incorrect answers.

1) The air pressure in the vessel is $p_0 = 1$ atm, and when heated to a temperature of $T_1$ it returns to a value of $p_1 = p_0 \frac{T_1}{T_0} = 1 \cdot \frac{373}{273} \approx 1,3$ atm. This is greater than the saturated vapor pressure of water at $100^\circ C$; This means that the water will not evaporate, and the total pressure in the vessel will be equal to $p = p_1 = 1,3$ atm.

2) $100^\circ C$ is the boiling point of water. This means that all the water in the vessel will evaporate; the pressure of the resulting steam (in the amount of 1 mol - after all, there was 18 g of water) is not difficult to calculate (at $T_0 = 273$ K 1 mol in a volume of 22.4 liters it creates a pressure of $p_0 = 1$ atm, and at $T_1 = 373$ K the steam pressure will be $p_{п1} \approx 1,3$ atm); the air pressure in the vessel will increase when heated to $p_{в1} \approx 1,3$ atm; the total pressure becomes equal to $p_1 = p_{п1} + p_{в1} \approx 2,6$ atm.
