⟦folio 9⟧

I am concerned that in the families of satellites of our Galaxy, the Andromeda Galaxy and some other massive galaxies, law (6) is not satisfied. In observations, the velocities of satellites located at distances of up to ten radii of the central galaxy changed, and each time it turned out that the velocities did not decrease with the orbital radius, but remained (with a certain accuracy) constant. This circumstance is reflected by the dotted line on the rotation curve (see Fig. 3).

In search of an explanation, Soviet astronomers from the Tartu Observatory and American astronomers from Princeton University suggested discussing one further possibility. What if the galaxy's mass is actually not limited to what lies within the volume visible in photographs?
Could it be that, in addition to stars, there could be some other gravitating masses distributed over a much larger volume around galaxies?
This is how the hypothesis about the invisible, “hidden” masses of galaxies arose.

It is assumed that this additional mass forms the corona of the galaxy, which extends over long distances and reaches the most distant orbits of satellite galaxies. If so, then the satellites do not move in emptiness, but among invisible, “hidden” masses. Then the gravitating mass influencing the motion of the satellite consists of the massive stars of the galaxy and the “hidden” mass located inside the satellite’s orbit. As a result, the total gravitating mass increases with increasing orbital radius; because of this, the velocities of satellite galaxies do not obey law (6). If the velocities are the same in orbits of different radii, then, as can be seen from formulas (3) and (5), with increasing $r$ the gravitating mass should increase according to the law

$$M \sim r.$$ (7)

It follows that the total mass of the galaxy may be many times greater than the mass of visible stars. If a family of satellite galaxies moving at the same speeds is located at a distance, say, $3R$ from the center of the galaxy, then the total mass of the entire system is three times the mass of the visible stars, that is, the visible body of the galaxy.

As the mass increases according to law (7), the density of matter in the galaxy decreases from the center outwards as $r^{-2}$ (we will show this in Appendix II). At a sufficient distance from the center, the density drops faster and disappears.

* * *

The “hidden” mass hypothesis explains the dynamics of satellite galaxies, but at the same time raises new difficult questions. First of all, what is this mass? The answer is unknown. It is believed that it can consist of masses of small and very faint stars. If so, then there are more stars in the City Galaxy than can be seen even with the best telescope.

But this is not the only possibility. Recently, astronomers and physicists have been carefully discussing the idea that the “hidden” mass is a gas of elementary particles - neutrinos. These particles have been known in physics for almost half a century, but it is still unclear whether they have a rest mass (like a proton, electron and other particles) or their rest mass is zero (like a photon). In the presence of a rest mass, even if much smaller than that of an electron (the lightest of particles, for which it is precisely known that it has a rest mass), a neutrino could actually create extended clouds around massive galaxies, invisible, but gravitating.

In recent experiments by Moscow physicists, carried out not with cosmic, but with laborator neutrinos, errors occurred during radioactive decay, and signs were discovered that neutrinos may actually have a rest mass. It is reported to be approximately ten thousand times less than the rest mass of an electron. This is a preliminary result, but if it is confirmed, then it will be necessary to assume that cosmic neutrinos also have rest mass. Then the idea of ​​a neutrino “hidden” mass will receive a reliable physical justification.
