In other words, the written law can be expressed as follows: the ratio of the thermal emissivity of any body to its absorption capacity (for data $\lambda$ and $T$) is equal to the emissivity of an absolute black body (for the same $\lambda$ and $T$).

Thus, based on the most general ideas about bodies (namely, assuming that they can absorb light to one degree or another and be at a constant temperature), we come to the conclusion that any body - solid, liquid, gaseous - must necessarily emit thermal radiation, if only it is in thermal equilibrium at a temperature above absolute zero. Depending on the degree of heating, this light will be visible or invisible.

As already mentioned above, it can be easily proven that there cannot be a body whose radiation would exceed the radiation of an absolute black body at the same temperature.

In this sense, an absolute black body is the best, most perfect thermal emitter, and Faraday, in the words quoted at the beginning of the chapter, had reason to be surprised at the general candle, the properties of which are quite close to the properties of an absolute black body, not to mention its practical applications.

Such “perfection” of an absolutely black body and candles and incandescent lamps close to it, however, is very relative and conditional. The fact is that all “thermal emitters” are extremely uneconomical in the usual technical sense of the word; they convert only a tiny fraction of the energy they consume into visible light. For a completely black body located at 2000°C, only 0.3% of the energy turns into visible light; at a temperature of 3000°C, the efficiency rises to 3%. The most advantageous temperature for a completely black body should be around 6000°C, that is, at the temperature of the Sun. The reason for this, at first glance, surprising coincidence is that the human eye, biologically, in the process of development, has become accustomed to sunlight, and therefore light from a thermal emitter with solar temperature is the most economical. However, the economy of even this highest absolute black body is not great; The useful coefficient of the contract is approximately 13%, that is, 87% of the total energy consumed by the source is useless for the eye. For the most economical modern incandescent lamps with a power of 100 W, the useful candle contract coefficient is 2-3%.

Natural impact question: why can’t all the energy imparted to an absolute black body, or at least a significant part of it, be converted into visible light?

It was not easy for physicists to obtain an answer to this question; To do this, it was necessary to carry out, in essence, a whole revolution in science; it was necessary to put aside much that was generally acceptable, which was considered an immutable truth.

Only a tiny fraction of this radiation is visible; the rest is concentrated mainly in the region of invisible infrared rays with lower frequencies (longer wavelengths) than visible light.
