⟦folio 39⟧

Now imagine that pentominoes can be curved along the sides of the squares. Then, using all the pentominoes, you can paste over the surface of the figure depicted at figure 4. Of course, you will have to strain your imagination and show ingenuity. You can first construct a net of the figure and fill it with pentominoes. But we think the following method is more successful.

Let's look at figure 4. In our field of vision there are the front, top and right squares of the surface of our figure. Let's remove these squares and draw the remaining part of the surface, that is, the back, bottom and left squares (Fig. 5). Now, having designated each pentomino with its own symbol, we write on each heel square of the surface the symbol corresponding to the given pentomino.

One of the possible pastings for the figure figure 4 is shown at figure 6.

“Flexible” pentominoes can be used to cover a large number of polyhedra with a surface of 60 squares. Try to come up with such polyhedra. Oddly enough, the middle ones do not have a single straight parallelepiped. We are waiting for your suggestions - we will publish the best of them.

As just said, one parallelepiped cannot be all pentominoes. But two identical parallelepipeds 1×3×3 (on the surface of each of them there are 30 squares) can be pasted over like this. You can also cover six 1×1×2 parallelepipeds. Do it! Try to list all possible sets of parallelepipeds (not necessarily identical) pasted over with a set of pentominoes. (Of course, the number of squares on the surface of each such parallelepiped must be a multiple of a heel.)

Finally, cover two identical “cross-shaped” polyhedrons shown in figure 7 with a set of pentominoes.
