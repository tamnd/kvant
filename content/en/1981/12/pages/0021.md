However, one important remark must be made. A body with a small hole completely absorbs all kinds of rays, of any color, with any wavelength. But there are bodies (of course, not having the form of a cavity) that completely absorb only rays of a certain color, with a certain wavelength. Such bodies can also be called absolute black, but only with respect to certain wavelengths. Distance, such as alcohol flame or gas flame in oxygen. They are light, although they have a high temperature. Let's add a little objective table salt to such a flame. The flame acquires a sharp yellow color, the same as what some flames have, as already mentioned, at the very beginning of combustion. On average, the flame remains transparent; at the same time, it begins to absorb light very strongly in a narrow region of light waves, in the yellow part of the spectrum; here it becomes completely black. That's why it glows.

To further understand this significant circumstance, distance is another interesting experiment. Chemical elements called rare earths, such as neodymium, rhaseodymium, samarium, etc., when introduced into glass or quartz, give very thin strong absorption bands in the spectrum. You can prepare a thin stick or string of glass or quartz with a few rare earths. If you accumulate such a stick and look at it through a spectroscope, it turns out that only a few thin lines glow, precisely corresponding to the absorption of rare earths; the remaining parts of the spectrum are dark, since glass or quartz itself does not absorb light in the visible part and therefore does not glow when accumulated. The described glass rod became “black” only in certain narrow spectral regions.
