At $n=3$, the second player obviously wins.

Let us prove that for $n>3$ the first player wins.

If the first player connects somehow two nodes $A$ and $B$ with his first move, and the second player connects two other nodes $C$ and $D$ (Fig. 1), then the first player wins by applying the second player’s strategy for the case $n=4k+1$. If the second player connects node $A$ with the third node $C$, then with the second move the first player replaces the triangle, connecting nodes $C$ and $B$ (Fig. 2). The second player creates an even component with any response move. After this, the first player is added so that after each of his moves there are at least two even components. Among the numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3$, as in the case of $n=4k+1$, there will be two even ones, which in this case ensures the victory of the first player.

c) This point is similar to the previous one. In any game of opponents, there will come a moment when the set of nodes will split into two components $A_1$ and $A_2$ by $a_1$ and $a_2$ nodes. The player who connects the components $A_1$ and $A_2$ loses. Reasoning in the same way as before, we get that $N = \frac{a_1^2 + a_2^2 - n}{2}$ moves will be made before the critical moment.

Let's look at the same four cases again.

$1^\circ$. $n=4k$. The second player wins by adding that after each of his moves there are two even components (with $N$ being even).

$2^\circ$. $n=4k+1$. The second player wins because, regardless of the game's progress, one of the numbers $a_1$ or $a_2$ will be odd and $N$ will be even.

$3^\circ$. $n=4k+2$. The first player at $k \geqslant 1$ is added to victory in the same way as in the case of $4^\circ$ in paragraph 6): it is enough for him to ensure that after each of his moves, except perhaps the second, there are at least two even components, which is what he is added (see figure 1, 2).

$4^\circ$. $n=4k+3$. The first player wins, regardless of the game's progress (prove it!).

d) Let us isose a connected group of nodes $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_m$ by a cycle if node $B_1$ is connected to node $B_2$, node $B_2$ is connected to node $B_3$, ..., and node $B_m$ is connected to node $B_1$. It is clear that if there are cycles in connection with a system of nodes, then the player who makes the next move does not lose: he can break any of the connections in any cycle - the system remains connected.

Thus, whichever player wins, after which there are no more cycles in the system. Let us prove that in connection with a system of $n$ nodes that does not contain cycles (such a system in graph theory is called a *tree*), there are exactly $n-1$ connections. To do this, fix some node $B$ and note that there is a single path leading from $B$ to any other node. Along all paths leaving $B$, we will place arrows showing the direction from $B$. Each of the nodes, except $B$, contains exactly one arrow and, therefore, the total number of arrows is $n-1$.

Thus, the tree is formed after

$$N = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} - (n-1) = \frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}$$

moves. Therefore, for $n=4k$ and $n=4k+3$ the first player wins ($N$ is odd), and for $n=4k+1$ and $n=4k+2$ the second player wins ($N$ is even).
