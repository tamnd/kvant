The authors of the first answer forget that the liquid must be in equilibrium with its vapor; this means that at $t_1 = 100^\circ\text{C}$ water will evaporate until its vapor pressure reaches 1 atm.

The second answer is also incorrect, of course, since the pressure of water vapor at $100^\circ\text{C}$ cannot rise above 1 atm.

So, in the vessel the air pressure will be $p_{\text{в1}} \approx 1,3$ atm, the water vapor pressure will be $p_{\text{п1}} = 1$ atm (we have made sure that the mass of water is sufficient to create such pressure), and the total pressure in the vessel will be only $p_1 = p_{\text{п1}} + p_{\text{в1}} = 2,3$ atm.

By the way, the water will not boil. After all, boiling is a process of vaporization not only on the surface of a liquid, but throughout its entire volume. If the temperature is such that the corresponding saturated vapor pressure is equal to the total external pressure, then vapor bubbles are formed inside the liquid and do not collapse under external pressure; Further evaporation of the inside of the bubbles increases their volume, the bubbles float to the surface, burst on the surface - the liquid boils.

Let us emphasize again: evaporation always occurs when the vapor pressure above the surface of the liquid is less than the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature; boiling occurs only when the total pressure (and the internal liquid) does not exceed the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature.

By the way, the Earth is full of water; and relative humidity equal to 100%, that is, saturation of the air with water vapor, is rare. Why, when there is an excess of water, is not the entire atmosphere saturated with water vapor? Maybe enough water simply did not have time to evaporate? Let's estimate the rate of water evaporation.

Task 3.*) Estimate the quantity

the amount of water that evaporates at room temperature per unit surface area in an open glass (as well as in a river, stream, or ocean). The saturated vapor pressure of water at temperature $T = 300\text{K}$ is equal to $p_{\text{н}} = 3,5\text{кПа}$.

When water is in equilibrium with its saturated steam, then, strictly speaking, it evaporates. Moreover, the rate of evaporation does not depend on whether there is steam above the surface of the water. It is determined only by how many water molecules have the speed reached to leave the liquid (and the appropriate direction of speed). It’s just that exactly the same number of vapor molecules enters the liquid - the rate of evaporation is equal to the rate of condensation, there is a dynamic equilibrium. And calculating the amount of condensing steam is not so difficult.

The number of vapor molecules falling per unit time (1 s) onto a unit area (1 m²) of the water surface is equal to

$$z = \frac{n |v_x|}{2} \approx \frac{p_{\text{н}}}{2kT} \sqrt{\frac{RT}{M}} \left( \frac{1}{M^2c} \right)$$

(since the calculation is approximate, we do not take into account the differences between the average absolute value $v_x(|v_x|)$ and the root mean square value $v_x(\sqrt{v_x^2})$). The mass of steam condensing on a unit surface area per unit time is obviously equal to

$$m = z \frac{M}{N_{\text{A}}} = \frac{p_{\text{н}}}{2} \sqrt{\frac{M}{RT}} \left( \frac{\kappa r}{M^2c} \right)$$

($N_{\text{A}}$ is Avogadro’s number).

As we agreed, such a mass of water evaporates regardless of air humidity, that is, regardless of the water vapor content in the air (which is characterized by the value $n$). If replenishment of evaporating water does not occur (humidity is very low), then exactly this amount of water will evaporate and disappear from a unit of surface in seconds. Let's calculate the drop in water level in seconds:

$$h = \frac{p_{\text{н}}}{2Q} \sqrt{\frac{M}{RT}} = \frac{3,5 \cdot 10^3}{2 \cdot 10^3} \sqrt{\frac{18 \cdot 10^{-3}}{8,3 \cdot 300}} \approx 4,7 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ м/c} = 4,7 \text{ мм/c}$$
