Commentary on the work
G. Cavendish

The method used by Cavendish to determine the dependence of the force of interaction between electric charges on distance is based on the fact that if the electric field inside a uniformly charged sphere is zero, then the force of interaction is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between the charges. Let's prove it.

Consider an arbitrary point $A$ inside a uniformly charged sphere $S$ (see Fig.nok). Let's form a mental cone with the vertex at point $A$. The cone will cut out a small area of ​​area $\Delta S_1$ on the surface of the sphere. Another cone, symmetrical to the first with respect to the point $A$, will cut a flat $\Delta S_2$ on the surface of the sphere. Let the distances from $A$ to the corresponding planes be $r_1$ and $r_2$. Since the angles between the axis of the cones and the normals to the planes are equal,

$$\frac{\Delta S_1}{\Delta S_2} = \frac{r_1^2}{r_2^2}.$$

Let us now assume that the forces of interaction between charges are central and the directional field created by the charge $q$ at some point in space is inversely proportional to the $n$th power of the distance from the point to the charge, that is

$$E = k \frac{q}{r^n},$$ where $k$ = const.

Since the sphere is uniformly charged, the central charge on the area $\Delta S$ is proportional to this area. Consequently, the ratio of the field strength models created at point $A$ by charges, located at sites $\Delta S_1$ and $\Delta S_2$, is equal

$$\frac{\Delta E_1}{\Delta E_2} = \frac{\Delta S_1 r_2^n}{\Delta S_2 r_1^n}.$$

Vectors $\vec{\Delta E}_1$ and $\vec{\Delta E}_2$ are directed in opposite directions and can either completely or partially cancel each other out. To determine the voltage at point $A$ created by all the charges on the sphere, it is necessary to consider all possible orientations of the cone. It is clear that the resulting voltage can be equal to zero only under the condition $\vec{\Delta E}_1 = -\vec{\Delta E}_2$. The latter is possible only in the case when $n=2$, which is what needed to be proven.

It should be noted that the idea of ​​the experiment carried out by Cavendish was not accepted by him, but by another English scientist of the 18th century, Joseph Priestley, who, however, did not carry out the experiment himself.

Despite Cavendish’s rapid experimental art, his experiments still had one significant drawback: from them it was impossible to obtain interactions from the magnitude of charges or, in Cavendish’s language, “from the amount of electric fluid.” In its legal form, the law of interaction of stationary electric charges was established in 1785 by the French physicist Charles Augustin Coulomb, who used round scales and a method similar to that used by Cavendish to determine the average density of the Earth. Therefore, the basic law of electricity is rightly called Coulomb's law.
