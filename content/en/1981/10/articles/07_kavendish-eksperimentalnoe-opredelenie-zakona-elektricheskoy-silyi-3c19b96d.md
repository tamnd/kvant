Interest in the scientist’s scientific heritage did not wane. So, admiration and bewilderment are the feelings that arise when getting acquainted with the scientific work of Henry Cavendish. And, despite the fact that many of his results were obtained anew by other scientists who are rightfully considered the authors of the discoveries, the value of Cavendish’s works is enduring. And, above all, it is in the scientific depth with which Cavendish thinks through his experiments, and in the scrupulous care with which he carried them out. It is these requirements that are fundamental in modern experimental physics.

Experimental determination of the law of electric force

The best way to get acquainted with the work of an outstanding scientist is by reading his works. Celebrating Cavendish's anniversary, we are publishing an abridged translation of his work devoted to establishing the dependence of the forces of electrical attraction and repulsion on distance. Cavendish's work on electrical problems appears to have begun in 1771. The results he obtained represent a very complete and, most importantly, accurate solution to the problem posed. In his research, the scientist identified Coulomb for more than a decade, who published the results of his experiments in 1785. It is interesting that Cavendish was able to answer the question posed, despite the fact that he held misconceptions about the nature of electricity. He believed that there was a certain electrical fluid, the excess of which manifests itself in positive electrification of the body, and the deficiency - in negative. To facilitate understanding of the physical meaning of Cavendish's experiments in the translation, non-rotoric device terms are replaced by corresponding modern expressions; Where necessary, supporting comments are provided.

An electrometer was connected to the primary conductor through which the Leyden jar was charged, and the hemispheres were always charged equally.

The reasons why I used the jar are as follows. Without it, it would be difficult both to establish to what extent the hemispheres have been lectized, and to maintain electricity of the same strength*) for a second or two; I was afraid that if the wire lay on the hemispheres during the electrification of the jar, then the electricity might gradually melt on its own along the glass rods that hold the ball and hemispheres, and this would introduce some errors into the results of the experiment.

The result of the experiments, which I repeated several times, was always the same: I did not notice the divergence of the balls or any signs of electricity.

That it is possible to consume less electricity, I conducted the experiment in other ways. Before the hemispheres were charged, I electrified the cork balls positively, causing them to move apart about an inch. Then, as soon as the hemisphere was separated and the staniols were brought into contact with the ball, the electricity of the cork balls was naturally transferred to the ball; however, they continued to disperse, although barely noticeably. Then I repeated the experiment in almost the same way, but first electrified the balls negatively, and to the same extent as in the first case. After being brought into contact with the ball, they diverged in the same way as with positive electrification.

It should be noted that if the ball had an additional positive charge, the cork balls should diverge more when they were previously positively electrified than when they were given a negative charge.

Using this technique, the low electrification of the ball can be replaced.
