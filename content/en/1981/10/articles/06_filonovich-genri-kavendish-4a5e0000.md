Henry Cavendish

This year marks the 250th anniversary of the birth of Henry Cavendish. The name of Cavendish in the history of science is associated with many discoveries that humanity learned about many years after they were made. The personality of this man, dedicated to everything natural in life, has attracted the attention of physicists, historians of science, and psychologists for many years.

Henry Cavendish, who belonged to the famous year of the Dukes of Devonshire, was born on October 10, 1731 in Niszcz, where, on the advice of doctors, his mother lived at that time. She was not generally in good health, and the birth of children completely weakened it, and Cavendish died soon after the birth of her second son, when Henry was about two years old. At the age of 11, the boy was sent to one of the best private schools in London, and in 1749 he entered the University of Cambridge, which he left in 1753 without receiving an academic degree. I suggest that this circumstance was due to Cavendish’s painful shyness and his fear of the exam.

After leaving university, Henry traveled for some time with his younger brother around Europe, and then settled in his father's house in London. Cavendish's father, Sir Charles, deserves special mention. A noble, although not very rich, man, he was more interested in natural things than anything else in the world. Sir Charles was for many years a member of the Royal Society of London, and for a time even its vice-president. His scientific interests related to the fashionable field of electricity at that time. The famous American naturalist B. Franklin wrote about Cavendish the father: “I would like such a respected scientist to tell the world more about the many experiments he carried out with great care.” It is possible that the Cavendish father and son carried out some experiments together.

So, Henry's interest in natural sciences was born under the influence of his busy father. However, judging by the surviving records, Henry conducted more of the most important foreign experiments on his own.

What is the place of Cavendish in the science of the second half of the 18th century? What problems interested him?

Cavendish's range of scientific interests was so wide that the definition of “natural natural” best suited him. During his lifetime he became famous as a chemist. For his pioneering studies of gases, Cavendish is sometimes called the “father of pneumatic chemistry.” He was the first to determine the nature of hydrogen as a special gas, confirmed that air is a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen, and that water is formed from air and hydrogen. Cavendish's research on topical chemistry coincided with the work of many other scientists of that time: Lavouzier, Watt, Priestley. However, Cavendish's research method was original: it is characterized by a clearly expressed desire for precise quantitative experimentation. Cavendish, for example, not only proved the existence of hydrogen, but also carried out calculations to find that this gas is 11 times lighter than air*). Using a specially acquired device, the eudiometer, the scientists studied the volumetric correspondences between air, hydrogen and water formed during the explosion of the mixture.

There is an opinion that the scientist spent his entire life conducting experiments without publishing their results at all. This is a somewhat exaggerated idea. Indeed, Cavendish, for reasons that are not very clear, rarely published his scientific results. Significantly, publications were divided much later than the wholesales, and this led to acute priority disputes. Nevertheless, one can count more than a dozen of his works on physics and chemistry published in the journal Philosophical Transactions, published by the Royal Society of London. The scientist's experiments were highly appreciated by his contemporaries. In 1820, the outstanding French mechanic and mathematician Laplace wrote: “Having scrupulously studied Cavendish’s apparatus and all the experiments carried out with the accuracy and insight that are characteristic of this outstanding physicist, I could not discern a single objection to the value he gave for the average density of the Earth, 5.48.”

suggested the existence of latent heat of fusion. Cavendish's experiments carried out to study electrical and magnetic phenomena are very interesting. Some of them became known only after the publication of Cavendish's works on electricity by the outstanding English physicist Maxwell. The history of this publication is interesting. In 1861, William Cavendish, Duke of Devonshire, who himself had once graduated from this university and showed aptitude for mathematics, was elected Chancellor of the University of Cambridge. In 1870, he expressed a desire for a physical laboratory to be built at the university, and a fund to finance construction was created. In 1871, the Cavendish Department was established, to which, on the recommendation of such English physicists as Stoks, Rayleigh, W. Thomson, Maxwell was invited. He accepted this offer and actively began building the laboratorium. Three years later, construction was completed. At the opening ceremony of the Cavendish Laboratory, which played an outstanding role in the formation and development of modern physics, the Duke presented Maxwell with the Cavendish manuscripts, which were kept in the family archives. Maxwell agreed to look at them. Already his first acquaintance with Cavendish's heritage amazed him. He wrote to one of his friends: “In his manuscripts, he [Cavendish - S.F.] will reveal familiarity with the laws of parallel and series connection of conductors... He conducted very general research in the field of conductivity of salt solutions in pipes, which can be used for wiring made of different metals. The impression is created that he is worthy of even greater honors, since he surpassed Om long before permanent points were discovered. His capacitance measurements will have us sweating in the Cavendish laboratories before we reach the point where he left off...”

During the last years of his life, Maxwell devoted exceptional attention to preparing for the publication of Cavendish's manuscripts describing electrical research. Cavendish's works were published in 1879, a few months after Maxwell's death.

What new has the scientific world learned about Cavendish’s work? A simpler listing of the results speaks of a polyatom: he was the first to clearly define the concept of capacity and use the capacity of a ball of given dimensions as a reference; studied the dependence of the conductivity of aqueous salt solutions on its concentration and solution temperature; 14 years before Coulomb’s work, he established the dependence of the force of electric repulsion and attraction on distance; long before Ohm, he predicted the laws of the constructed current.

And all these results remained unpublished!
