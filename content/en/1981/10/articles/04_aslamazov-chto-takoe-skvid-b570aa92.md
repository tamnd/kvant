What is SQUID?

Probably everyone knows about superconductivity these days. This phenomenon consists in the fact that at low temperatures the electrical resistance of a number of pure metals and metals becomes equal to zero and the electrical current is generated without any losses.

The temperatures at which resistance disappears are very low - they are close to absolute zero (−273°C). To cool a substance to this temperature, it is placed in a special environment - liquid helium. At normal pressure, helium becomes liquid at a temperature of 4.2 K and does not freeze at the lowest temperatures - down to absolute zero.

Cooling to liquid gel temperatures is quite a challenge. This is why superconductivity, which is promising for use in technology, has not yet found widespread practical application. And, it would seem, how tempting, for example, to transmit electrical energy over long distances via superconducting wires - no losses! However, cooling the wire is still quite expensive. And although superconducting transmission lines several kilometers long have already been created, as well as superconducting motors and generators, and even high-speed trains on the so-called magnetic levitation (which also uses super-fast
fluidity) - such devices are still not launched.

In laboratories around the world, physicists and chemists are looking for substances that will be superconducting at temperatures that are not too low (for example, so that comparatively cheap and accessible liquid air can be used to cool them). Significant progress has already been made in this direction - more and more “high-temperature” superconductors are being discovered every year. But even today’s record holder - the alloy of nubium with germanium Nb₃Ge - becomes a superconductor only at a temperature of 22.4 K.

And yet, superconductivity is already “working.” In some cases, the possibility of superconductivity is unique to the table, which means you have to put up with cooling costs. Thus, superconducting magnets are widely used to create strong magnetic fields (several tens of tesla); superconductivity is used to measure very weak magnetic fields (with an accuracy of 10⁻¹⁸ Tesla). Sensitive voltmeters, detectors and amplifiers of electromagnetic radiation operate on the basis of superconductivity. There is a whole field of electronics - cryoelectronics (from the Greek word "krios" - cold), based on the use of the phenomenon of superconductivity.

Some practical applications of superconductivity will be discussed in this article. However, the story is told from afar. Let's first get acquainted with one beautiful physical phenomenon that underlies the design of many superconducting devices.

Magnetic flux quantization

As is known, in the microcosm - the world of molecules, atoms, elementary particles - only certain discrete values ​​can be essentially applied - as physicists say, quantities

quantized. (A special science has been created to describe the microcosm - quantum mechanics.) In large groups of particles - macroscopic bodies - quantum effects are usually noticeable, since due to chaotic thermal motion the values ​​are averaged over a large number of their different values ​​and quantum jumps are “blurred out”.

What happens if the body is cooled to very low temperatures? It turns out that in this case, quantization can also manifest itself on a macroscopic scale. An example of this is provided by a remarkable phenomenon - the quantization of magnetic flux in a superconductor.

Everyone who has studied the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction knows what magnetic flux F is:

$$\Phi = BS,$$

where B is the model of the magnetic induction vector, and S is the surface area covered by the circuit (for simplicity, we will assume that the induction is directed normal to the surface). However, we have discovered that the magnetic flux created by a superconducting current flowing along the ring can only take certain discrete values. Let's try to understand this phenomenon at least in a simplified way.

The movement of superconducting electrons in the ring (Fig. 1) is similar to the movement of electrons in an atom: the electrons in the ring seem to move along giant orbits of radius R without any collisions. Therefore, it is natural to assume that their movement is subject to the same quantization rules as the movement of electrons in an atom. According to Bohr's theory, in an atom only certain electron orbits are stationary and stable. They are selected using the following quantization rule: the product of the electron momentum model mv and the orbital radius R (the quantity mvR is called the angular momentum of the electron) can only take discrete values, that is

$$mvR = n\hbar.$$

Here n is a positive integer, and the value $\hbar$, which determines the smallest possible change (quantum) of angular momentum, is Planck’s constant*). It turns out that the quantization of all physical quantities is determined precisely by this universal constant. It is very small:

$$\hbar = 1.05 \cdot 10^{-34} \text{ Дж} \cdot \text{с},$$

In general, quantum effects appear either when the values ​​of physical quantities are very small (in the microcosm), or when there are many microparticles moving in exactly the same way. In the latter case, they talk about macroscopic quantum phenomena, which include the quantization of a magnetic flux in a ring with a superconducting current.

Let's find the magnitude of the magnetic flux quantum. The magnetic flux F through the ring is connected with the current I of the corresponding ring. The current I can be expressed in terms of the speed of electrons v and their number N. Indeed, during the time t = 2πR/v the electrons make a full revolution; therefore, during this time, all electrons will pass through any cross section of the ring, recharging the charge q = eN (where e is the charge of one electron). Posny current in the ring

$$I = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{eNv}{2\pi R}.$$

Another relationship connecting

$$N \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2} L I^2.$$

Now the expression for the magnetic flux can be represented as

$$\Phi = L I = \frac{L I^2}{2} \frac{2}{I} = N \frac{mv^2}{2} \frac{4\pi R}{e N v} = \frac{2\pi}{e} mvR,$$

whence it follows that the magnetic flux is proportional to the angular momentum of the electrons. But, according to Bohr's quantization rules, angular momentum can only take discrete values, therefore the magnetic flux through the ring through which the superconducting current flows is also quantized:

$$mvR = n \hbar$$

The magnetic flux quantum, which can be found from the previous formula, is, of course, very large ($\sim 10^{-15}$ Wb), but nevertheless, modern instruments make it possible to observe the quantization of the magnetic flux. Such an experiment was carried out by the American scientist Deaver and Fairbank in 1961, only together they used rings to obtain a superconducting tube through which circular superconducting currents circulated. In the experiment, it was discovered that the magnetic flux through the cross-sectional area of ​​the tube actually changed abruptly, but the value of the flux quantum turned out to be half that given above. The explanation for this is given by the modern theory of superconductivity. The fact is that in the superconducting state, electrons combine into pairs and it is the movement of pairs having a charge $2e$ that creates a superconducting current. Therefore, the rule of the value $\Phi_0$ of the quantum flux is obtained if we substitute the double charge of the electron into the flux quantization formula:

$$\Phi_0 = \frac{2\pi \hbar}{2e} = 2,07 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{ Bb}.$$

This is how the deuce was found, lost not only by us, but also by the English scientist London, who theoretically predicted the quantization of magnetic flux back in 1950 - long before the nature of the superconducting state was understood.

I would like to emphasize that the derivation of magnetic flux quantization given here, although it correctly reflects the physical essence of this phenomenon, is too simplified. Further, it is surprising that in this way it is possible to obtain the correct value of the magnetic flux quantum. In reality, superconductivity is a complex quantum phenomenon. Those who want to truly understand it have a long way to go from school physics to modern solid state theory - a path that will require many years of hard but exciting work.

Josephson effect

Let's look at another superconducting quantum phenomenon, which served as the basis for the creation of a number of unique transformative devices. We are talking about the Josephson effect, discovered relatively recently - in 1962, but for which Josephson, the English scientist who theoretically predicted this effect, has already been awarded the Nobel Prize.

Imagine that a superconductor film is deposited on a glass plate (as they say, on the substrate) (usually it is obtained by depositing metal in a vacuum). Then it was oxidized, creating on the surface a layer of dielectric (oxide) only a few angstroms thick*). And again they covered it with a superconductor film. The result was a so-called sandwich (literally, this English word refers to two pieces of bread with cheese or something else tasty between them). The Josephson effect is observed in sandwiches (for ease of measurement, the main sandwich is made in the form of a cross, as shown in figure 2).

Let's first discuss the case where metal films are in a normal (non-superconducting) state. Can electrons from one metal film (Fig. 3,a) move to another? It would seem not - the dielectric layer interferes with them. In order to leave the metal, an electron must have an energy greater than the work function, and at low temperatures there are practically no electrons with such energy. At figure 3.6 there is a graph of the dependence of the electron potential energy W on the x coordinate (the X axis is perpendicular to the sandwich plane). In a metal, an electron moves freely and its potential energy is zero. To enter the dielectric, it is necessary to perform the work function W_B, which is greater than the kinetic, and therefore total, energy of the electron W_9*). Therefore, they say that electrons in metal films share a potential barrier, the height of which is W_B-W_9.

If electrons obeyed the laws of classical mechanics, then such a barrier would be insurmountable for them. But electrons are microparticles, and in the microworld there are special laws that allow much that is inaccessible to large bodies. For example, a person with such energy cannot climb a hill, but an electron can pass through it! It is as if he is growing a tunnel under the hill and pierces, even if his energy is not enough to climb the hill. Of course, all this should not be taken literally. The tunnel effect (as this phenomenon is called) is explained by the wave properties of microparticles, and you can truly understand it only by studying quantum mechanics. But the fact remains: with some certainty, electrons can penetrate the dielectric from one metal film to another. This confidence is greater, the smaller the height W_B-W_9 of the barer and the smaller its width a.

So, a thin film of dielectric turns out to be permeable to electrons - the so-called tunnel current can flow through it. However, the total tunnel current is zero: how many electrons move from the lower metal film to the upper one, and how many, on average, move, on the contrary, from the upper film to the lower one.

How can we make the tunnel current different from zero? To do this, it is necessary to break the symmetry, for example, to connect metal films to a current source with voltage U (Fig. 4,a). Then the films will play the role of capacitor plates, and in the case of a dielectric, an electric field with intense E=U/a will arise. The work done to move a charge e a distance x along the direction of the field is A=Fx=eEx=eUx/a, and therefore the graph of the electron's potential energy takes on the form shown in figure 4.6.

They acquire this energy by being accelerated by the electric field in the dielectric layer through which they tunnel. Once in the lower film, they must shed excess energy and enter an equilibrium state. You noticed that the electron charge is directed to the right - we must remember that it is not individual electrons that tunnel, but superconducting pairs.

Here's an amazing effect presented by Josephson: a constant voltage applied to a superconducting tunnel junction (also called a Josephson element) leads to the generation of electromagnetic radiation. This effect was first discovered experimentally at the Kharkov Institute of Low Temperature Physics and Technology by scientists I.M. Dmitrienko, V.M. Svistinov and I.K. Yanson in 1965.

The first thing that comes to mind when talking about the practical use of Josephson energy is the creation of a generator of electromagnetic radiation. But in reality, everything is not so simple: the radiation is quite difficult to remove from the narrow gap between the superconducting films where it is generated (which is why the experimental detection of the Josephson energy was not an easy task), and the radiation power is very low. Therefore, Josephson elements are now used primarily as detectors of electromagnetic radiation, but are sensitive to light in some frequency ranges.

This application is based on the phenomenon of resonance between external electromagnetic oscillations (in the registration wave) and natural oscillations that arise in the Josephson element when a constant voltage is applied to it. As a matter of fact, resonance underlies the operation of multi-receivers: a wave can be “caught” when its frequency coincides with the frequency

Based on this principle, the most sensitive receivers of electromagnetic radiation have already been created, which are used to harness the radiation of the Universe.

Quantum magnetometer

The Josephson effect, combined with the phenomenon of magnetic flux quantization, became the basis for the creation of a whole family of ultrasensitive alternating devices. They are called “superconducting quantum interference devices” or “SQUIDs” (from the combination of the first letters of the corresponding English words Superconducting Quantum Interference Devices *). One of them - a quantum magnetometer (a device for measuring weak magnetic fields) - will be discussed in this chapter.

The simplest quantum magnetometer consists of a superconducting bulb, the target of which includes a Josephson element (Fig. 5). As you already know, in order for current to occur in a normal tunnel contact, at least a small voltage must be applied to it. But in a superconducting tunnel contact this is not necessary. If a superconducting current is created in the bulb, then it can also flow through the Josephson element: superconducting pairs will tunnel through a thin layer of dielectric. This phenomenon is called the Stationary (constant in time) Josephson effect, in contrast to the Stationary effect accompanied by radiation (it was discussed in the previous chapter). Exists,

So, when a Josephson element is included in a superconducting circuit, the superconductivity is not completely destroyed, but a place appears in the circuit in which the superconductivity is weakened (as they say, a weak coupling will arise). This is precisely the basis for the use of such a circuit for accurate measurement of magnetic fields. Let's try to understand how this is done.

If the circuit were completely superconducting (did not contain weak coupling), then the magnetic flux through its area would be strictly constant. Indeed, according to the law of electromagnetic induction, any change in the external magnetic field leads to the appearance of an inductive emf $\mathcal{E}_i = -\Delta \Phi_{\text{внеш}} / \Delta t$, and therefore to a change in the current in the circuit. But the changing current in turn generates an EMF

$$\varepsilon_i + \varepsilon_{is} = 0,$$

It follows that when the external magnetic flux changes, the superconducting current in the circuit changes so that the change in the magnetic flux created by the current compensates for the change in the external flux (Lenz's rule). In this case, the total magnetic flux through the circuit remains constant: $\Phi = \text{const}$. It is impossible to change it without transferring the circuit to a normal state (they say that in a superconducting circuit the magnetic flux is “frozen”).

What happens if the superconducting circuit contains a weak connection? It turns out that the magnetic flux through such a circuit can change - flux quanta penetrate into the circuit through a weak connection (as you already know, the magnetic flux of superconducting current is quantized - it is equal to an integer number of flux quanta $\Phi_0$).

Let's see how the magnetic flux inside a superconducting bulb with a weak coupling and the magnitude of the current in the bulb change when the external magnetic field changes. Let initially the external field and the current in the circuit be equal to zero (Fig. 5,a). Then the flux inside the loop is also zero. If we increase the external field, a superconducting current will appear in the circuit, the magnetic flux of which exactly compensates for the external flux. This will continue until the current in the circuit reaches the critical value $I_k$ (Fig. 5, b). Let us assume for definiteness that at this moment the external field creates a flux equal to half a quantum: $\Phi_0/2^*$).

Its magnitude remains the same, but the direction will change to the opposite. Indeed, if before the entry of the quantum of flux $\Phi_0$ the current $I_k$ completely screened the external flux $\Phi_0/2$, then after entering it should enhance the external flux $\Phi_0/2$ to the value $\Phi_0$. Therefore, at the moment of entry of the flux quantum, the direction is also in the opposite direction.

With a further increase in the external field, the current in the bulb begins to decrease, superconductivity in the bulb will be restored and the flux inside the ring will remain equal to $\Phi_0$. The current in the loop goes to zero when the external flux also becomes equal to $\Phi_0$ (Fig. 5,e), and then it begins to flow in the opposite direction (screening again!). Finally, when the external flux value is $3\Phi_0/2$, the current will again become equal to $I_k$, superconductivity will be destroyed, the next flux quantum will enter, etc.
