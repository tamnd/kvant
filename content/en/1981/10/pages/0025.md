The hemispheres were folded together so that they formed a half-sphere, and their edges touched as closely as possible. Cutouts were made in each of them to create holes for the glass rod to come out.

This way I got an inner ball surrounded by a hollow ball. There was no connection between him through which electricity could pass from one to the other.

Then, using a wire passing through one of the hemispheres and touching the inner ball, I created a connection between them. A piece of silk thread was attached to the end of the wire, with which I could pull the wire out if desired.

Following this, I lestriated the hemispheres using a wire connected to the positive lining of the Leyden jar, then separated it and instantly pulled out the wire connecting the inner and outer balls. The wire, removed using a silk thread, could not discharge either the ball or the hemisphere. I then separated the hemispheres, being careful not to let them touch the inner ball, and applied a couple of small cork balls suspended on thin linen threads to the ball to see if the inner ball had gelled.

To be more confident in the result of the experiment, I used other equipment. In principle, it was more complicated than necessary, this one-step was so important to me that I wanted to check it very carefully.

Thanks to the action of various springs, one movement of the hand led to the following sequential actions: the wire with which the hemispheres were formed was removed, the wire connecting the hemispheres and the inner ball was pulled out, the hemispheres were separated and the Tt stick was brought to the inner ball. In addition, it was provided that the electricity located on the hemispheres and the wire through which they were charged was removed immediately after the dilution of the hemispheres; otherwise, the action of this electricity could cause the cork balls to disperse, even if the inner ball were not nalestrated.
