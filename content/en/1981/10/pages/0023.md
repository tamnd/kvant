Some of them became known only after the publication of Cavendish's works on electricity by the outstanding English physicist Maxwell. The history of this publication is interesting. In 1861, William Cavendish, Duke of Devonshire, who himself had once graduated from this university and showed aptitude for mathematics, was elected Chancellor of the University of Cambridge. In 1870, he expressed a desire for a physical laboratory to be built at the university, and a fund to finance construction was created. In 1871, the Cavendish Department was established, to which, on the recommendation of such English physicists as Stoks, Rayleigh, W. Thomson, Maxwell was invited. He accepted this offer and actively began building the laboratorium. Three years later, construction was completed. At the opening ceremony of the Cavendish Laboratory, which played an outstanding role in the formation and development of modern physics, the Duke presented Maxwell with the Cavendish manuscripts, which were kept in the family archives. Maxwell agreed to look at them. Already his first acquaintance with Cavendish's heritage amazed him. He wrote to one of his friends: “In his manuscripts, he [Cavendish - S.F.] will reveal familiarity with the laws of parallel and series connection of conductors... He conducted very general research in the field of conductivity of salt solutions in pipes, which can be used for wiring made of different metals. The impression is created that he is worthy of even greater honors, since he surpassed Om long before permanent points were discovered. His capacitance measurements will have us sweating in the Cavendish laboratories before we reach the point where he left off...”

During the last years of his life, Maxwell devoted exceptional attention to preparing for the publication of Cavendish's manuscripts describing electrical research. Cavendish's works were published in 1879, a few months after Maxwell's death.

What new has the scientific world learned about Cavendish’s work? A simpler listing of the results speaks of a polyatom: he was the first to clearly define the concept of capacity and use the capacity of a ball of given dimensions as a reference; studied the dependence of the conductivity of aqueous salt solutions on its concentration and solution temperature; 14 years before Coulomb’s work, he established the dependence of the force of electric repulsion and attraction on distance; long before Ohm, he predicted the laws of the constructed current.

And all these results remained unpublished!

For experiments on studying the thermal properties of substances, he created a very accurate thermometer. Externally, this Cavendish device is almost no different from modern evaluation (precision) thermometers.
