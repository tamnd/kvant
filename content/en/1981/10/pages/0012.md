Quantum magnetometer

The Josephson effect, combined with the phenomenon of magnetic flux quantization, became the basis for the creation of a whole family of ultrasensitive alternating devices. They are called “superconducting quantum interference devices” or “SQUIDs” (from the combination of the first letters of the corresponding English words Superconducting Quantum Interference Devices *). One of them - a quantum magnetometer (a device for measuring weak magnetic fields) - will be discussed in this chapter.

The simplest quantum magnetometer consists of a superconducting bulb, the target of which includes a Josephson element (Fig. 5). As you already know, in order for current to occur in a normal tunnel contact, at least a small voltage must be applied to it. But in a superconducting tunnel contact this is not necessary. If a superconducting current is created in the bulb, then it can also flow through the Josephson element: superconducting pairs will tunnel through a thin layer of dielectric. So, when a Josephson element is included in a superconducting circuit, the superconductivity is not completely destroyed, but a place appears in the circuit in which the superconductivity is weakened (as they say, a weak coupling will arise). This is precisely the basis for the use of such a circuit for accurate measurement of magnetic fields. Let's try to understand how this is done.

If the circuit were completely superconducting (did not contain weak coupling), then the magnetic flux through its area would be strictly constant. Indeed, according to the law of electromagnetic induction, any change in the external magnetic field leads to the appearance of an inductive emf $\mathcal{E}_i = -\Delta \Phi_{\text{внеш}} / \Delta t$, and therefore to a change in the current in the circuit.
