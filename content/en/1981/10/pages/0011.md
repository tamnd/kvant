As already mentioned, electrons from the upper film have excess energy $eU$ compared to electrons from the lower film. They acquire this energy by being accelerated by the electric field in the dielectric layer through which they tunnel. Once in the lower film, they must shed excess energy and enter an equilibrium state. If the film were in a normal state, then this would be easy to do - just a few collisions with the crystal lattice of the metal are enough and the excess energy of the electrons will eventually go into an equilibrium state.

So all that remains is for electrons to emit this energy in the form of a quantum of electromagnetic radiation with energies $h\omega$. Therefore, part of the radiation $\omega$ is associated with the applied voltage $U$ simply by the relation

$$h\omega = 2eU.$$

You noticed that the electron charge is directed to the right - we must remember that it is not individual electrons that tunnel, but superconducting pairs.

Here's an amazing effect presented by Josephson: a constant voltage applied to a superconducting tunnel junction (also called a Josephson element) leads to the generation of electromagnetic radiation. This effect was first discovered experimentally at the Kharkov Institute of Low Temperature Physics and Technology by scientists I.M. Dmitrienko, V.M. Svistinov and I.K. Yanson in 1965.

The first thing that comes to mind when talking about the practical use of Josephson energy is the creation of a generator of electromagnetic radiation. But in reality, everything is not so simple: the radiation is quite difficult to remove from the narrow gap between the superconducting films where it is generated (which is why the experimental detection of the Josephson energy was not an easy task), and the radiation power is very low. Therefore, Josephson elements are now used primarily as detectors of electromagnetic radiation, but are sensitive to light in some frequency ranges.

This application is based on the phenomenon of resonance between external electromagnetic oscillations (in the registration wave) and natural oscillations that arise in the Josephson element when a constant voltage is applied to it.
