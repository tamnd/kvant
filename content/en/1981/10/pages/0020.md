While carrying out one of his electrical experiments, Cavendish, passing an electric spark through a mixture of air and oxygen in the presence of potassium, noticed that after the formation of nitric acid reacting with potassium and the removal of excess oxygen, an insignificant residue is obtained (1/120 of the initial volume of the gas), which is not affected by any means. This discovery was found in everyday life; it was remembered only at the end of the 19th century, after the discovery of invert gases.

Cavendish's research on topical chemistry coincided with the work of many other scientists of that time: Lavouzier, Watt, Priestley. However, Cavendish's research method was original: it is characterized by a clearly expressed desire for precise quantitative experimentation. Cavendish, for example, not only proved the existence of hydrogen, but also carried out calculations to find that this gas is 11 times lighter than air*). Using a specially acquired device, the eudiometer, the scientists studied the volumetric correspondences between air, hydrogen and water formed during the explosion of the mixture.

There is an opinion that the scientist spent his entire life conducting experiments without publishing their results at all. This is a somewhat exaggerated idea. Indeed, Cavendish, for reasons that are not very clear, rarely published his scientific results. Significantly, publications were divided much later than the wholesales, and this led to acute priority disputes. Nevertheless, one can count more than a dozen of his works on physics and chemistry published in the journal Philosophical Transactions, published by the Royal Society of London.
