At average density, when for each molecule there is a volume varying from $l^3$, but greater than $a^2l$, the orientational order will, of course, be incomplete, but still noticeable. And this order is connected with the fact that molecules cannot penetrate each other due to strong repulsion.

The density of rod-shaped molecules can be changed without appreciable change in the total volume of the liquid when such molecules are dissolved in some bulk solvent, such as water. By increasing the water content in accordance with the general solution, we obtain a conditional non-oriented liquid. With a very low solvent content, a nematic or cholesteric liquid is formed, depending on the details of the structure of the molecules. The rasters of polymer molecules discussed in the previous article are just such nematic liquid crystals.

The work of animal cells is largely determined by liquid crystals - solutions that are formed from special molecules. These molecules are more complex. Their interaction with each other and with solvent molecules is characterized by reactions of repulsion and links of electrostatic attraction. Such a molecule is schematically depicted at figure 4, $a$. It consists of a small head, which is an electrostatic diopole, and a long, uncharged tail. Let us recall that water molecules are also electrostatic diopoles. The oppositely charged ends of the diopoles attract each other, and therefore the molecular heads attract water. At the same time, the tails of chemical molecules are designed in such a way that they repel water, like fat or wax molecules.

For example, when determining the concentration of such molecules in water, liquid crystals can be obtained in which the molecules not only have the same orientation, but also form a rigid crystal lattice. Only this lattice is only partly similar to the usual lattice of a solid body, periodic in three mutually perpendicular directions. There can be only two or even one such directions in special liquid crystals.

figure 4, $b$ shows a stack of layers formed at a not very low concentration of molecules in water. The tails of molecules seem to “hide” from water behind shells of diopole heads. Water is traceable between double layers of molecules. A stack of such layers forms a crystal lattice that is periodic only in one direction—along the $Z$ axis. In this direction, the rigidity of the lattice is the same as in a solid, while in transverse directions the layer can slide freely, that is, along the layers of the system it behaves like a liquid. This structure is medium soap, which is why such liquid crystals are called smectic.
