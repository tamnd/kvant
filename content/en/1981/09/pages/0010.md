(By the way, in Figures 1, a and 2, b, a left-handed spiral is depicted - moving along the axis of the spiral, we see that the molecules rotate their long axes counterclockwise.)

Of course, such discrepancies about the structure of cholesteric fluid are rather rough and approximate, but they give a clear idea of ​​the structural features of such substances. In these discrepancies one can find further and pointer than the angle α and the helix pitch h are already equal. Of course, these quantities are the parameters of a substance consisting of specific molecules. But their approximation can be assessed if we proceed from the reasonable assumption that the angle α is smaller, the lower the energy of interaction of the shoots with the main sections of the molecules and the greater the interaction of the flat sections with each other. Since, roughly speaking, approximately one atom enters the appendage, and approximately 100 atoms enter the flat section, the ratio of these interaction energies is about 1/100. Accordingly, the angle α is approximately equal to a hundredth of a radian, that is, half a degree. In turn, the helix pitch h with the transverse molecular size a≈10Å is several thousand angstroms, that is, comparable to the wavelength of light in the visible part of the spectrum. We see that this is extremely valuable.

Selective reflection of cholesteric light

The most important optical effect observed in cholesteric liquid is the selective reflection of light by the cholesteric layer. Cholesteric planes, like ordinary crystalline planes, can reflect waves incident on them. Waves reflected by different planes can, through interference, both weaken and hear each other. If the light falls perpendicular to the planes, then the scining condition is as follows.

Lenya is this: strictly half a wave of incident light should be placed between adjacent equivalent planes. From figure 1, b it is clear why this happens. We want the vibration in the waves reflected from the first and second planes to occur in the same phases, that is, the values ​​of the electric field $\vec{E}$ at each point and at any time are the same. In this case, the amplitudes $\vec{E}_0$ of both waves add up - the light is amplified. This will happen if wave 2 (see Fig. 1, b), which entered the crystal, on the way from the first to the second plane and back lags behind wave 1 exactly by wavelength λ. But in cholesteric such planes are at a distance h/2 from each other (at this distance the $\vec{n}$ axis rotates by 180°). Therefore, the amplification conditions in this case are h/2=λ/2, or h=λ.

The property of cholesteric to reflect light with a selected wavelength ($\lambda=h$) provides the corresponding color of the substance, which depends on the pitch of the cholesteric helix. And the pitch of the spiral is very dependent on temperature. Typically, a substance at a high temperature (in an isotropic state) is colorless, then, at the moment of transition to cholesteric (at a lower temperature), it turns blue (remember what was observed by F. Reinitzer) and at the same temperature, it successively acquires all the colors of the spectrum, from blue to red. This means that as the cholesteric liquid cools, the pitch of the spiral increases, and when heated, it decreases. This behavior of the cholesteric helix is ​​not difficult to explain.
