The story of one misconception

We all read with satisfaction books about outstanding personalities - political activities and positive scientists and thoughtful writers and artists. Proof of this is the wide popularity that the biographical series “The Lives of Remarkable People” has gained, numbering more than one hundred volumes. It is no less interesting to get acquainted with the history of the origin and development of scientific concepts, to learn about the evolution of views and ideas. There are also many books related to this, including books in the recently emerged series “The Life of Wonderful Ideas.”

However, few people have had the opportunity to hold in their hands a book telling about history...
initial misconceptions. Meanwhile, on the path to the development of science and the improvement of human knowledge, there were quite a few such misconceptions. Take alchemy, astrology, and the theory of the all-pervading ether. These were indeed delusions, chimeras, roads leading nowhere. But can we say that they turned out to be completely unprofitable?
Not at all. Medieval alchemists, in an effort to discover the “philosopher’s stone,” laid the foundations of true science - chemistry; astrology, which tried to predict the fate of people and people by the location of the planets, shared rich observed material for astronomy; and from the concept of the ether modern electrodynamics was born.

Before us is a book by the English engineer and popularizer of science Arthur Ord-Xbioma *), dedicated to another mistaken idea - an attempt to create perpetuum mobile, or perpetual motion.

This is a new species idea, the origins of which go back to ancient times, affecting thousands of people. No, no, and even now committees for inventions, patent offices, academies of sciences, and sometimes the editors of popular science magazines are receiving letters outlining the idea of ​​a new version of perpetutum mobile.

“Perpetual motion,” the author rightly writes, “is a serious problem. This is all the sheer stupidity that some inventors present to the public.”
The history of the idea of ​​perpetual motion would be extremely important for two reasons. Firstly, in the process of birth and mainly refutation of certain specific constructions, many provisions of mechanics and thermodynamics arose and were refined, and the fundamental laws of physics were tested. Among those who with their research refuted the evolving principles of perpetual motion were Galileo and Huygens, Faraday and Carnot, Bolshan and Helmholtz. Secondly, many proposed design solutions for perpetual motion scales are ingenious and non-trivial. It will be useful for readers of the book Ord-Xbioma to try to find flaws in the described “perpetual motions” themselves, to prove their impracticability, which, by the way, is not always so easy to do.

Let's turn to the contents of the book.

The first chapter, called “Perpetual Motion and Physics,” sets out the laws of nature that have become an insurmountable obstacle to the creation of mobile perpetuum. First of all, this is the law of conservation of energy. In order for a perpetual motion machine to work, it must provide itself with energy, produce it in greater quantities than it receives from it. But this contradicts the law of conservation of energy. Another general proposition that immediately negates any project of perpetual motion is the second one at the beginning of thermodynamics. Research by scientists and technical solutions by engineers are aimed at preserving these losses, but no one would even think of trying to eliminate them completely, or this would mean an attempt to create perpetual motion. Now this has become a very obvious truth.

Apparently, the first perpetual motion machine was described in the Sanskrit manuscript on astronomy "Siddhanta Siromani" (Vvek AD). There are two rows of holes on the outer rim of the wheel, arranged in an argyle pattern at an equal distance from each other. The holes were filled with mercury and tightly closed. It was argued that such a wheel, mounted on an axle and set in motion, would rotate indefinitely.
