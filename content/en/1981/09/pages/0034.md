A. Kalinin

This amazing ligature of rings

«Kvant» has already talked about the Borromean rings (A. Savin “Olympic Rings” - «Kvant», 1980, No. 7). These are three rings intertwined so that they do not come apart, although any two of them are not engaged. They have been known for a long time - once they so captured the imagination of the founders of the ancient families of Normandy, Burgundy and Lombardini that they placed these rings in their coats of arms. According to the ancient Italian family of patricians from the city of Milan, the Borromeo family, whose warriors wore stripes of three intertwined rings on their clothes, these rings began to be called Borromeo rings. And on the tombstone of the Great Michelangelo (see the second page of the cover) there are three wreaths: laurel, olive and oak, intertwining with each other like Borromean rings, symbolizing the three interrelated types of art in which Michelangelo Buonarroti became famous, sculpture, painting and architecture. The same interweaving of venks is shown on one of the medals minted in honor of this Great Artist of the Renaissance (Fig. 1).

But we are not interested in the natural or symbolic side of the matter, but in the mathematical one. Why won't Borromean rings come apart? Is it possible to hook a larger number of rings in a similar way? Do hooked rings have useful applications? Help us answer these questions

Wire models

Let's make Borromean rings from flexible wire (or rope). Having chosen some two rings (say, red and blue), we move them apart, without bending, from each other (Fig. 2). In this case, the third (yellow) ring will bend, forming something like a double bracket. This staple is inserted into the blue ring, and the red ring serves as a lock, preventing us from removing the staple from the blue ring. But if you break any ring, everything will fall apart!
