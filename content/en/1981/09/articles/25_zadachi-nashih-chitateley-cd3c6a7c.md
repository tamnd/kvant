In the 18th-19th centuries, mechanical engines were replaced by perpetual engines, in which it was proposed to use steam, natural magnets, as well as electromagnetic phenomena. They are discussed in the fourth chapter of the book. Perhaps the most widespread are various versions of such a system: an electric motor drives a generator, which in turn provides this motor with energy. There is no need to prove that friction, heating of wires and closing contacts nullify this idea.

And one more phenomenon - capitularity - trying to use inventive perpetual motion machines. This is discussed in chapter five. Surprisingly, the theoretical prerequisites for such a device were formed by the famous physicist Robert Boyle, who believed that perpetual motion in the very concepts of this term is carried out on the scale of our entire Earth in the form of a continuous steep rise of water rising up and flowing down again. (He did not take into account such a “little thing” that the energy supplier for the circulation of water is the Sun.) Boyle’s high authority stimulated the birth of many perpetuum mobile projects with wettable sponges, that is, using capillary forces. However, Boyle was far from the only scientist - a real scientist! — who believed in the possibility of realizing perpetual motion. One can name at least the outstanding mathematician, mechanic and philosopher Johann Bernoulli - one of those who laid the scientific foundations of modern hydrodynamics and hydraulics. All the more incomprehensible is the fact that it was Bernoulli who put forward the idea of ​​​​creating a hydrodynamic perpetuum mobile.

In the following chapters, the reader will find almost detective stories of moneymakers and adventurers who sinned their hands on the piston of creating perpetual motion machines, and in the same chapter will get acquainted with various versions of “perpetual” clocks, for example, with the clock of the replacement English mechanic and inventor James Cox, which was powered by ... a difference in atmospheric pressure.

The book concludes with chapters dealing with some significant issues. Here we talk, in particular, about applications for perpetual motion machines received by the patent offices of England,

France, the USA and other countries, and gradually stop accepting such applications for consideration. Thus, the Paris Academy of Sciences refused to consider any projects of perpetual motion machines back in 1775, and the US Patent Office a century later. Nevertheless, the inventors of perpetual motion machines did not disappear and, as the author claims, never disappeared. He even said one of the chapters on the freestyle meaningfully: “The eternity of the problems of perpetual motion.”

Ord-Hume says: “The fact is that today those who are essentially occupied with the problem of reducing perpetual motion very often do not realize this, believing that the subject of their research is some other issue, the successful solution of which will cause a sensation in the scientific world.” It is very important to be present to solve a particular problem, to clearly formulate a constant task from the very beginning and, of course, the dignity of the rare inventors of perpetual motion.

Arthur Ord-Hume's book is written in a lively, engaging manner and has numerous illustrations. She, without a doubt, will not leave the reader equal to zero.
