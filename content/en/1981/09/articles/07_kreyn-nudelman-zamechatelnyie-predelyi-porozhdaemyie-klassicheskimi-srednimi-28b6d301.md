Wonderful backgrounds generated by classic averages

For two positive numbers $a$ and $b$, as is known, the number $\frac{a+b}{2}$ is called the arithmetic mean, and the number $\sqrt{ab}$ is called the geometric mean. Less common than these averages is the harmonic average $\frac{2ab}{a+b}$ (it is equal, for example, to the average speed of a machine gunner who covered the first half of the journey at a speed of $a$, the second at a speed of $b$). Each of these averages is located between the numbers $a$ and $b$ (make sure of this).

The validity of her equalities is easily verified

$$\frac{2ab}{a+b} < \sqrt{ab} < \frac{a+b}{2}$$

for any positive $a \neq b^*$.

We will deal with the next question here. Let the given two positive numbers $a$ and $b$, $a < b$. Having calculated some pair of averages of these numbers, we obtain the numbers $a_1$ and $b_1$. Then for the numbers $a_1$ and $b_1$ we calculate the same averages - we get the numbers $a_2$ and $b_2$. We will repeat the same procedure with them and so on. As a result, we obtain the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$.

For example, taking the geometric mean and the arithmetic mean and starting from the numbers 1 and 3, we get

$$a_1 = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732050808, \quad b_1 = 2;$$
$$a_2 \approx 1.861209718, \quad b_2 \approx 1.866025404;$$
$$a_3 \approx 1.863616006, \quad b_3 \approx 1.863617561;$$
$$a_4 \approx 1.863616784, \quad b_4 \approx 1.863616784;$$
etc.

We see that in our example the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ converge very quickly. Will it always be like this? It turns out that such sequences always have a common limit - this is relatively easy to prove. But how to find the value of this limit?

Arithmetic-harmonic mean

We will start with the case where the chosen pair of means is the harmonic mean and the arithmetic mean; thus, the terms of the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are defined by the formulas

$$a_{n+1} = \frac{2a_nb_n}{a_n+b_n}, \quad b_{n+1} = \frac{a_n+b_n}{2}$$

($n=0, 1, 2, \dots; a_0=a, b_0=b$).

The problem of finding their common limit was presented at the Second Team Olympid by the Omsk school («Kvant», 1979, No. 12).

From inequalities (1) it follows

$$a < a_n < a_{n+1} < b_{n+1} < b_n$$

that is, the sequence $(a_n)$ returns “towards” the killing sequence $(b_n)$.

Thus, both sequences are monotonic and bounded; therefore, by Weierstrass's theorem (Algebra and Principles of Analysis 9, paragraph 32) they have a limit $\alpha = \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n, \beta = \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n$. Passing to the limit in one of equalities (2), we obtain

$$\beta = \lim_{n \to \infty} b_{n+1} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n+b_n}{2} =$$
$$= \frac{1}{2} \left( \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n + \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n \right) = \frac{1}{2} (\alpha + \beta),$$

whence $\alpha = \beta$: the sequences $(a_n)$, $(b_n)$ have a common limit. This limit is called the arithmetic-harmonic average of the numbers $a$ and $b$. Let's find him. From (2)

$$a_{n+1} \cdot b_{n+1} = a_n \cdot b_n = \ldots = a_1 b_1 = ab,$$
because
$$a^2 = \left( \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \right)^2 = \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \times \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \times b_n = ab,$$

Thus, the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ converge quickly enough to $\sqrt{ab}$. Therefore, they can be useful for approximate calculation of square roots. To calculate $\sqrt{c}$ you need to start the sequences $(a_n)$, $(b_n)$ with numbers $a$ and $b$ such that $c = ab$ (for example, $a = 1, b = c$), and the process will converge the faster, the smaller the difference between these factors (for example, to calculate $\sqrt{56}$ it is better not to be $a = 1$, $b = 56$, and $a = 7$, $b = 8$). It is easy to verify that the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are obtained by the formulas
$$b_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2} \left( b_n + \frac{c}{b_n} \right), a_{n+1} = \frac{c}{b_n}.$$
To illustrate the calculation of $\sqrt{12}$, put $b = 4$. We get $b_1 = \frac{1}{2} \left( 4 + \frac{12}{4} \right) = 3,5$, $b_2 \approx 3,464285715$, $b_3 \approx 3,464101620$, $b_4 \approx 3,464101615$ and then all signs are stabilized:
$$\sqrt{12} \approx 3,464101615.$$

Arithmetic-geometric average Fourteen-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss*) discovered from numerical examples that when calculating the sequence $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ using geometric and arithmetic averages:
$$a_{n+1} = \sqrt{a_n b_n}, b_{n+1} = \frac{a_n + b_n}{2}$$

($n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots; a_0 = a, b_0 = b$) these sequences converge very quickly with the nearest ones.

*) The reader will find the biography of the “King of Mathematicians” and interesting information about his scientific activities in the article by S. Gindikn “Carl Friedrich Gauss” («Kvant», 1977, No. 8).

Exercise 2. Prove the existence and coincidence of the limits of the sequence (3).

This general limit is called the arithmetic-geometric average of the numbers $a$ and $b$ and is denoted by $\mu(a, b)$. Finding an explicit expression for $\mu(a, b)$ via $a$ and $b$ is not easy. it was first obtained by Gauss as a result of incredibly sharp and masterly dissections and transformations using the properties of the so-called elliptic intergoals. Let us present this expression without proof*):

$$\mu(a, b) = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{a^2 \sin^2 x + b^2 \cos^2 x}}.$$

Geometric-Gharionch average

If we construct the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ using gramonic and geometric means:
$$a_{n+1} = \frac{2a_n b_n}{a_n + b_n}, b_{n+1} = \sqrt{a_n b_n}$$

($n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots; a_0 = a, b_0 = b$),

then in this case it is easy to prove that they converge to a common limit. Let's call it the geometric-Garyonic number of $a$ and $b$ and denote it by $\nu(a, b)$. However, there is nothing significant here in comparison with sequences (3), since from (4) we see that
$$\frac{1}{a_{n+1}} = \frac{1}{a_n} + \frac{1}{b_n}, \frac{1}{b_{n+1}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{a_n} \cdot \frac{1}{b_n}}.$$
Because
$$\frac{1}{\nu(a, b)} = \mu\left(\frac{1}{b}, \frac{1}{a}\right),$$
or, by virtue of Gaus's formula.

*) If this expression is used “in the opposite direction”, we get a quick way to approximate the calculation of the integral in the denominator: it is equal to $\frac{\pi}{2\mu(a, b)}$, and the average $\mu(a, b)$ is calculated approximately using the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$. In our computer age, these calculations can be carried out with tremendous speed and accuracy.
