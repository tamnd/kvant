This year marks the 140th anniversary of the birth and 70th anniversary of the death of the famous puzzle master, inventor of the famous "game of 15", one of the pioneers of the mathematician, the classic of this genre, Sam Loyd. In 1980, the publishing house Mir published Loyd's Mathematical Mozik, a collection of his best mathematical problems and puzzles.

The article published below, evident from reading this unconventional collection, is not a solution in the generally accepted sense of the word. Its author, a professional mathematician, prefers to retell Loyd in waves and examine specific puzzles in detail, proposing new ones along the way, instead of a general discussion of the content, advantages and disadvantages of the book. Despite the numerous absences from the letter of Loyd's problems (and perhaps thanks to them), the article, it seems to us, better than the promised solution conveys the festive spirit of wave invention, wit and fun, characteristic of Loyd's book.

Loyd's special fiction is bright, full of wit, texts that are not too related to the essence of the problems, but create a high mood in the reader and inspire him to shoot the scales of intricate and tricky questions. “What is learned with satisfaction and interest,” says Loyd in one of the puzzles through the mouth of the jester Beppo, “is never forgotten... A teacher who forces you to memorize the rules is only good for receiving!”

Sometimes Loyd changes this principle by providing recipes for solving his puzzles without any obligation. Here, for example, is problem No. 145 about Tom, the son of labor. In English-speaking countries, everyone remembers a little rhyme from childhood: “Tom, Tom is the son of labor, he stole a pig and gave it a go...” The end of this story is sad: the pig was eaten, and Tom was beaten. Lloyd tells us its beginning: how Tom's chase for pigs came about (Fig. 4).

...Tom, the son of labor, decided to steal the pig. When he ran after the pigs, he was 250 brights south of her. Both the pig and Tom ran at the same time at constant speeds (Tom is 4/3 times faster than the pig). The pig runs towards the east. Instead of running across the path*, Tom the builder is so mad that at every moment he moves directly towards the pig. How far will the pig run before it can be caught?

Further, the conditions say: “The simple rule that allows you to solve problems of this type is based on elementary arithmetic, but it may be new to many puzzle lovers.”

I don’t know how I am a mathematician, but such a statement is alarming for a mathematical professional: let’s denote by $R_T$ and $R_C$ the distances that Tom and the pig will run; Tom beats on a rather complex curve (according to the so-called “linini chase”), and in general, whistling $R_C$ (and therefore $u_R = \frac{4}{3} R_C$) using elementary arithmetic causes distrust.

Let's enter the alphabetic data together: empty $r$ - the initial distance between Tom and the pigs, $v_T$ and $v_C$ - their speeds and $\omega = |v_C|/|v_T|$ (in the problem $r = 250$ yards, $\omega = 3/4$).

Let's consider two auxiliary problems:

Let Tom and the pig run in one straight line, once - Tom follows the pigs, another time - towards each other. Then (check), let's grab the pig, Tom will run the corresponding distance $R_1 = r/(1-\omega)$ and $R_2 = r(1+\omega)$.

In the solution to problem No. 145, Loyd, without explanation or evidence, reports the rules: $R_T = (R_1 + R_2)/2$.

As soon as they look at this incredibly simple formula, the majority of artificial mathematicians (who have never studied the chase problem) will exclaim: “This cannot be!” - and will be surprised how the editor of the book, Martin Gardner, and the translator, Yu. N. Sudarev (in general, deserving of the highest praise) did not notice this obvious mistake!

The most amazing thing is that the rule is... correct! Oddly enough, $R_T$ is real equal to $r/(1-\omega^2) = (R_1 + R_2)/2$, which gives us an incredibly simple formula for $R_C$: $R_C = r(\omega)/(1-\omega^2) = (R_1 - R_2)/2$.

And yet, is there any harm in saying that the formulas for $R_C$ and $R_T$ are based on elementary arithmetic: their derivation*) requires familiarity with the beginnings of mathematical analysis.
