Again about liquid crystals

In the August issue of the magazine, the article “Liquid Crystals” talked about one type of liquid crystals - nematic liquids, what the molecules of such a liquid are, how they interact with each other, and the most important areas of application of these liquid crystals. Now we want to introduce you to two other types of liquid crystals that play an extremely important role in science, technology and life. We will talk about cholesteric and smectic liquid crystals.

Cholesteric fluid

The structure of cholesteric liquid (cholesteric) is in many ways similar to nematic liquid, but has one significant difference. We can say that cholesteric has the nematic state of the latter, that is, it consists of a stack of nematic layers (Fig. 1, a). But if the layers parallel to each other are rotated through a certain angle, and for two adjacent layers this angle amounts to a small value $\alpha \approx 0,5^\circ$. The distance between adjacent layers is approximately equal to the transverse size of the molecules $a$. If we move along the $Z$ axis, perpendicular to the plane of the layers (see Fig. 1, a), then after the number of layers $N = \pi/a$ the orientation of the molecules will become the same as in the very first layer (directions $\vec{n}$ and $\vec{n}$ are physically equivalent). The distance $h = a \cdot 2\pi/a$, through which the orientation of molecules in space is repeated, is represented by twice the period of a peculiar lattice (Fig. 1, b). The value $h$ is usually called the pitch of the spiral, which is formed in space by the ends of molecules lying in successive layers.

The described periodic lattice - it is called a cholesteric helix - is surprising in that the clear periodicity in it concerns only the orientation of the molecules. At the same time, molecules in each nematic layer can move freely and change places; in a word, the cholesteric liquid flows freely along such planes, but the spiral is almost not disturbed. molecules can jump from layer to layer, turning at an angle α, but this is not so easy for them. All this determines the special properties of cholesteric liquid, similar to the properties of a solid crystal. The structural features of cholesteric liquid are most pronounced when the temperature of the substance changes and under various external influences. The cholesteric spiral has bright optical properties; it senses the slightest damage to the table of the free-flowing lattice. All this aroused enormous interest in the study and application of cholesteric liquid crystals. However, before we talk about the use of cholesteric liquid crystals, let’s figure out what causes this cholesteric structure.

We know today that the explanation lies in the particular structure of the molecules that make up these substances. cholesteric molecules are almost the same as in the nematic liquid, but have a small extension at their end (Fig. 2, a). This appendage is formed usually by a few atoms that protrude from the main plane containing the vast majority of the atoms of the molecules. The symmetry of the molecules is broken due to the process and resembles the symmetry of the hand, which, as is known, can only be right or only left.

How does this shape of molecules affect the orientational order of the liquid? Similar molecules can be arranged parallel to each other in a certain plane, for example, in the plane in which the molecules themselves lie. It is these planes that form the individual cholesteric elephants (Fig. 2, b). How can these elephants be “attached” to each other? It is obvious that the molecules of layer 2 can be parallel to the molecules of layer 1 (we are talking about the main, flat sections of the molecules), if the layer is located from each other at a distance approximately equal to the height of the processes. In this case, the processes do not prevent the molecules from remaining parallel. If the distance between the layers is less than the height of the processes, then the vectors $\vec{n}_1$ and $\vec{n}_2$ cannot be strictly parallel - less processes. Therefore, between the vectors $\vec{n}_1$ and $\vec{n}_2$ there is a small angle α.

Selective reflection of cholesteric light

The most important optical effect observed in cholesteric liquid is the selective reflection of light by the cholesteric layer. Cholesteric planes, like ordinary crystalline planes, can reflect waves incident on them. Waves reflected by different planes can, through interference, both weaken and hear each other. If the light falls perpendicular to the planes, then the scining condition is as follows.

Lenya is this: strictly half a wave of incident light should be placed between adjacent equivalent planes. From figure 1, b it is clear why this happens. We want the vibration in the waves reflected from the first and second planes to occur in the same phases, that is, the values ​​of the electric field $\vec{E}$ at each point and at any time are the same. In this case, the amplitudes $\vec{E}_0$ of both waves add up - the light is amplified. This will happen if wave 2 (see Fig. 1, b), which entered the crystal, on the way from the first to the second plane and back lags behind wave 1 exactly by wavelength λ. But in cholesteric such planes are at a distance h/2 from each other (at this distance the $\vec{n}$ axis rotates by 180°). Therefore, the amplification conditions in this case are h/2=λ/2, or h=λ.

The property of cholesteric to reflect light with a selected wavelength ($\lambda=h$) provides the corresponding color of the substance, which depends on the pitch of the cholesteric helix. And the pitch of the spiral is very dependent on temperature. Typically, a substance at a high temperature (in an isotropic state) is colorless, then, at the moment of transition to cholesteric (at a lower temperature), it turns blue (remember what was observed by F. Reinitzer) and at the same temperature, it successively acquires all the colors of the spectrum, from blue to red. This means that as the cholesteric liquid cools, the pitch of the spiral increases, and when heated, it decreases. This behavior of the cholesteric helix is ​​not difficult to explain.

Let us recall that, due to the peculiarities of the interaction of molecules with processes, the $\vec{n}_1$ and $\vec{n}_2$ axes cannot form too small an angle with each other (just as, say, in a crystal lattice, neighboring atoms cannot converge to a too small distance, less than the diameter of the atom). Liquid crystals - dispersions

Liquid crystalline states can also be obtained by dissolving suitable substances in a solvent, for example water, which does not itself form a liquid crystal. This produces a variety of liquid crystals. If the molecules of the substance being dissolved are in the form of rods, a nematic liquid is obtained; if the rod-shaped molecules have extensions - cholesteric fluid. More complex states can also be obtained.

The work of animal cells is largely determined by liquid crystals - solutions that are formed from special molecules. These molecules are more complex. Their interaction with each other and with solvent molecules is characterized by reactions of repulsion and links of electrostatic attraction. Such a molecule is schematically depicted at figure 4, $a$. It consists of a small head, which is an electrostatic diopole, and a long, uncharged tail. Let us recall that water molecules are also electrostatic diopoles. The oppositely charged ends of the diopoles attract each other, and therefore the molecular heads attract water. At the same time, the tails of chemical molecules are designed in such a way that they repel water, like fat or wax molecules.
