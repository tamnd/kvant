Dirac's problem

As students of Moscow State University, we almost always attended meetings of the P. N. Lebedev Physical Society, where new works in physics were discussed. These were memorable years of the rapid development of quantum mechanics; one after another, the fundamental works of Dirac, Heisenberg, de Broglie and others appeared, soon becoming classics. At the meetings, Igor Evgenievich Tamm, later an academician and State and Nobel Prize laureate, recently gave a heated speech in Moscow.

Named at these meetings, Moscow physicists introduced a unit of solution speed “1 Tump”, equal to a fifth of normal units. However, soon an even larger unit appeared: “1 Glagolev-Arkadyev”, equal to the heel of the “Tampa”.

Neutron → antineutron?

The sixth issue of «Kvant» talked about how physicists are trying to detect the decay of a proton, that is, its transformation into light particles (for example, into a positive mo-meson and neutrinos). And although such a decay (if it really exists) is an extremely rare phenomenon, there is a real hope of detecting it if at least one of the 10$^{31}$ protons decays within a year.

But if it turns out that the proton actually turns into light particles, then the neutron will almost certainly undergo a similar transformation. However, accumulating tons of neutrons and detecting neutron decay is, at least for now, a hopeless task.

Several years ago, V.A. Kuzmin, an employee of the Institute of Nuclear Research of the USSR Academy of Sciences, noticed that he was essentially implementing another process - the transformation of a neutron into an antineutron. A proton cannot by itself transform into an antiproton: for this to happen, its charge sign would have to change; the charge in nature remains. The neutron has no charge, just as the antineutron does not, so the transformation of $n \rightarrow n$ into the principle can occur. A flying neutron can periodically turn into an antineutron and be processed without violating the fundamental laws of nature.

Calculations have shown that if we work with neutrons flying at a speed of 100 m/s (these are the so-called cold neutrons): ideal gas molecules would have such an average speed at a temperature of ~0.8 K), then at a distance of 5 m from the reactor antineutrons could be detected if the neutron→anti-neutron transformation occurs at least once every 10$^7$ s, that is, an example of once every 4 months.

This is one of the most interesting and unexpected experiments that physicists are currently preparing.
