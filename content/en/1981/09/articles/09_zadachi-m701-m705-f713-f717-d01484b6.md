M662. The piggy bank contains four rubles in copper coins. Prove that these coins can be used to pay three rubles without change.

It is enough to prove that you can cry without changing rubles.

The piggy bank can contain four types of coins: one-kopeck, two-kopeck, three-kopeck and nickel coins. Therefore, coins of some type are no less than rubles.

If these are nickels, two-kopecks or one-kopeck coins, then it is clear that you can pay rubles without change.

Let us therefore consider the case when there are more three-kopeck coins than in rubles, and there are fewer coins of some other type than in rubles.

If there is at least one one-kopeck coin in the piggy bank, then adding it to 33 three-kopeck coins, we get rubles.

If there are two two-kopeck coins or two nickels, then by adding three-kopeck coins to 32 (respectively, 30), we get rubles.

Thus, there remains one case when there are no one-kopeck coins, two two-kopeck coins (398 is not divisible by 3); if there are no two-kopeck coins, then there are at least two nickels (395 is not divisible by 3). So, in this case we have one nickel and one two-kopeck coin, and we can collect rubles by adding one nickel and one two-kopeck coin to 31 three-kopeck coins.

It is interesting to figure out what amounts can generally be paid without change with a four-wheel set of copper coins in a piggy bank.

For example, if there are only two-kopeck coins in the piggy bank, then no odd amount can be paid; if only nickels, then no amount divisible by 5; if, finally, there are 133 three-kopeck coins and one one-kopeck coin in the piggy bank, then no amount can be paid that, when divided by 3, leaves a remainder of 2.

This means that if a certain amount can be paid for any four-wheeled set of copper coins in a piggy bank, then it is divisible by 10 and, when divided by 3, does not leave a remainder of 2. Can all such amounts be paid?

A. Kushirenko

1°. There are $2^N$ different light display patterns, because each of the $N$ light bulbs can be in two states - on or off. Let us press the key from the initial pattern $B_0$ you can get $m$ different patterns $B_0, B_1, \dots, B_{m-1}$. Then from any initial pattern $X$ one can obtain the same number of patterns - these are those patterns that differ from $X$ by the set $B_0, B_1, \dots, B_{m-1}$. Thus, if all $2^N$ patterns are divided into several keys and ss: assign to one class those patterns that can be obtained from each other by pressing a key, then in each class there will be $m$ patterns. Therefore $2^N$ is divisible by $m$; therefore, $m$ is a power of two.

2°. Let the key be numbered in such a way that none of the $s$ patterns $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_s$ can be obtained by a combination of the previous ones (with lower numbers), and each of the remaining $k-s$ patterns $A_{k+1}, \dots, A_k$ can be obtained by a combination of some operations $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_s$. Then the total number of patterns $m$ that can be obtained from the initial state $B_0$ is $2^s$. In fact, all combinations of the operations $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_s$, corresponding to $2^s$ of different subsets of the set $\{1, 2, \dots, s\}$, give different patterns: if any two of them coincided:

$$A_{i_1}A_{i_2}\dots A_{i_m}=A_{j_1}A_{j_2}\dots A_{j_l},$$

then the pattern $A$ with the most of the numbers included in this equality could be obtained by a combination of the previous ones.

3°. Let's first look not at all $N$ light bulbs, but at the first $L$ of them. Let these light bulbs from the initial state $B_0$ be used to obtain $m_L$ different patterns. Then let's look at the $(L+1)$th light bulb. If the state of the $(L+1)$ bulb is completely determined by the set of states of the first $L$ bulbs, then $m_L+1=m_L$. If there are at least two light bulbs, but the differences are at the $(L+1)$th, then you can get a pattern in which the first $L$ bulbs are not lit, but the $(L+1)$th is lit. Then from each pattern of our $L$ bulbs you can get two different patterns from the first $L+1$ bulbs, that is $m_L+1=2m_L$. Since one (first) light bulb can be in two states, it is clear that our interest $m=m_N$ will be the power of two.

Many readers have noticed that it is convenient to represent the operations $A_i$ and table states (“pattern”) as a set of $N$ numbers $+1$ and $-1$ (in this case, the operation of pressing the keys corresponds to the multiplication of sets) or as a set of $N$ numbers $0$ and $1$ (then the sets are added modulo 2, then there is $1+1=0$).

But each of the remaining $x_{ij}$ of these $m+n-1$ numbers is already determined uniquely, since under any admissible operations the equality $x_{ij}x_{11}=x_{i1}x_{1j}$ will be preserved.

A similar reasoning (using the point $x_{ij}x_{1j}x_{i1k}x_{i1k}x_{i1j}=x_{111}x_{11k}x_{11j}x_{i11}$) shows that a table having the shape of a straight parallelepped, a state of $m \times n \times p$ light bulbs, in which it is allowed to switch any row of light bulbs parallel to the edge of the parallelepped, the number of patterns in one class is $2^{mnp-(m-1)(n-1)(p-1)}$. that is, the number of pattern classes is $2^{(m-1)(n-1)(p-1)}$ (see solution $1^\circ$ to problem a)).

F673. The load hangs on an elastic thread. If a force is applied to the load, slowly increasing from zero, the thread will break when the force reaches the value $F_1$. At what minimum force value will the thread break if the applied force is less than a certain value and then remains insignificant?

An elastic thread can be considered as a circle. Let the rigidity of this clamp be equal to $k$. According to the conditions of the problem, with a slow increase in force, the thread will appear when the total force $F_\pi$ applied to it is equal to

$$F_\pi = mg + F_1,$$

where $m$ is the mass of the suspended load. Let's see what happens in the case when the applied force instantly reaches a certain value $F$ and does not change subsequently.

At the initial moment (when the force is zero), the thread is stretched under the action of the gravitational force of the load $mg$ by an amount $x_0$, determined by the condition $kx_0 = mg$, that is, the coordinate of the thread in the equilibrium position is equal to $x_0 = mg/k$ (the position of the end of the thread in the absence of a load is taken as the origin). When a force $F$ is applied, the coordinate of the end of the thread in the equilibrium position will be equal to $x' = mg/k + F/k$. However, the system will reach a state of equilibrium only after the air oscillations have died down. The initial amplitude of these oscillations is determined by the initial deviation of the system from the equilibrium position, that is, $A = x' - x_0 = F/k$. Since the damping of oscillations is small, after the period of the positive period the total tension of the clamps will be equal to

$$x_\pi = \frac{mg + F}{k} + A = \frac{mg}{k} + 2 \frac{F}{k}.$$

This means that the total force is applied to the thread

$$F'_\pi = mg + 2F.$$

The thread will break if this force is greater than the force $F_\pi$ (see (*)), which determines the tensile strength of the thread. Thus, the minimum force $F_{\min}$ at which the thread will break is found from condition $F'_\pi = F_\pi$, that is

$$mg + 2F_{\min} = mg + F_1;$$

from here

$$F_{\min} = F_1/2.$$

G. Baronov

F676. A square wire frame with perimeter 4a and mass m imparts a certain initial velocity in the horizontal direction. The frame moves in a vertical plane, all the time being in a magnetic field perpedicular to the plane of the frame (see figure). The field induction changes according to the law $B(z) = B(0) + kz$, where $k$ = const. The resistance of the frame is equal to R. After some time, the speed of the frame becomes constant and equal to v. Find the initial speed imparted to the frame. Acceleration of free nadiya g.

In the absence of a magnetic field, the frame would move in the Earth's gravitational field with a constant horizontal speed $\vec{v}_0$ along the X axis and uniformly accelerated by free padding $\vec{g}$ along the Z axis. Obviously, the motion of the frame would not change if it fell in a uniform magnetic field. In our case, the field is not uniform (along the Z axis): $B(z) = B_0 + kz$, that is, the field induction grows linearly with increasing z; therefore, when the frame falls, the magnetic induction flux F, an arbitrary contour of the frame, will change and an induced emf will appear in the frame contour. Since the frame is a closed conducting circuit, an induction current will flow through it. In this case, according to Ampere's law, forces from the magnetic field will act on the sides of the frame. Let's find the directions and magnitudes of these forces.

Let at some moment of time the center of mass of the frame be at a point with coordinates $x_t$, $z_t$ and the projections of the velocity of the center of mass on the X and Z axes are equal to $v_x$ and $v_z$ (see figure). The flux of magnetic induction Ф, an arbitrary frame at this point in time, is equal to

$$\Phi = \frac{(B_0 + k(z_t - a/2)) + (B_0 + k(z_t + a/2))}{2} a^2 = (B_0 + kz_t) a^2.$$

Here $B_0 + k(z_t - a/2)$ and $B_0 + k(z_t + a/2)$ are the values ​​of the magnetic field induction corresponding to the upper and lower sides of the frame; since the dependence $B(z)$ is linear, to calculate Ф we obtain the average (along the height z) value of the induction.

The induced emf in the frame at a given time is equal to

$$|\mathcal{E}| = \frac{|\Delta\Phi|}{\Delta t} = ka^2 \frac{|\Delta z|}{\Delta t} = ka^2 |v_z|,$$

induced current is equal to

$$I = \frac{|\mathcal{E}|}{R} = \frac{ka^2}{R} |v_z|.$$

According to Lenz's rules, the resulting current will flow clockwise. According to Ampere's law, a force will act from the magnetic field on the upper side of the frame

$$|\vec{F}_1| = (B_0 + k(z_t - a/2)) Ia = (B_0 + k(z_t - a/2)) \frac{ka^3}{R} |v_z|,$$

on the lower side - strength

$$|\vec{F}_2| = (B_0 + k(z_t + a/2)) Ia = (B_0 + k(z_t + a/2)) \frac{ka^3}{R} |v_z|.$$

The forces $\vec{F}_3$ and $\vec{F}_4$ acting on the sides of the frame, very/no, will be equal in magnitude and opposite in sign:

$$|\vec{F}_3| = |\vec{F}_4| = \frac{(B_0 + k(z_t - a/2)) + (B_0 + k(z_t + a/2))}{2} Ia =$$

$$= (B_0 + kz_t) \frac{ka^3}{R} |v_z|,$$

$$\vec{F}_3 + \vec{F}_4 = 0.$$

Therefore, $v_x = const$, that is, the frame will move along the axis $X$ with a constant speed equal to the initial speed $v_0$.

Thus, the nature of the movement of the frame in the direction of the $Z$ axis is determined by the forces $\vec{F}_1$, $\vec{F}_2$ and the gravitational force $m\vec{g}$. At a steady speed $v$ of the frame, the projection of the velocity onto the axis $Z$ is constant, that is, the acceleration $\vec{a}_z$ along the axis $Z$ is zero:

$$m|\vec{a}_z| = m|\vec{g}| + |\vec{F}_1| - |\vec{F}_2| = mg - \frac{k^2 a^4}{R}|v_z| = 0.$$

From here we find the projection $v_{yct. z}$ onto the axis $Z$ of the steady speed of the frame:

$$v_{yct. z} = mgR/k^2 a^4.$$

The steady speed of the frame is equal to $v = \sqrt{v_0^2 + v_{yct. z}^2}$, where $v_0$ is the projection of the speed $v$ onto the axis $X$, equal, as we have shown, to the initial speed imparted to the frame. Thus,

$$v_0 = \sqrt{v^2 - v_{yct. z}^2} = \sqrt{v^2 - \left(\frac{mgR}{k^2 a^4}\right)^2}.$$

The speed $v_{yct. z}$ can also be found from energy messages. When the motion of the frame is steady, the change in time $\Delta t$ of the potential energy of the frame in the Earth’s gravitational field is equal to the thermal energy released in the frame during this time:

$$mgv_{yct. z}\Delta t = l^2_{yct. z}R\Delta t = \left(\frac{ka^2}{R}\right)^2 v^2_{yct. z}R\Delta t.$$

From here

F677. Using a system of concentric mirrors (Fig. 1), an image of the Sun was obtained on the screen. The radius of the mirrors is $R_1 = 12$ cm, $R_2 = 30$ cm. What should be the focal length of a thin line in order for it to produce an image of the Sun of the same size?

The problem can be solved in two ways.

1. In the focal plane of the first mirror (radus $R_1$), an image of the Sun is obtained, which is the subject of the second mirror (radus $R_2$). If the angular size of the Sun is $a$, then the size of this image is $l = aF_1 = aR_1/2$; the image is located at a distance $d_2 = R_2 - R_1/2$ from the second mirror. The magnification of the second mirror is $\Gamma_2 = F_2/(d_2 - F_2) = R_2/(R_2 - R_1)$. The image size obtained in the mirror system is equal to

$$L_3 = l\Gamma_2 = \frac{aR_1}{2} \frac{R_2}{R_2 - R_1} = a \frac{R_2 R_1}{2(R_2 - R_1)}.$$

A thin line creates an image of the Sun in its focal plane size

$$L_\pi = aF_\pi.$$

To obtain an image size $L_\pi = L_3$, you need to take a lens with a focal length

$$F_\pi = \frac{R_2 R_1}{2(R_2 - R_1)} = 10\text{ см}.$$
