At the end of 1609, Galileo examined the Moon through his work and discovered that “the surface of the Moon is not smooth and uneven and not perfectly spherical, as a legion of philosophers believed in relation to its goals, but, on the contrary, uneven, rough, riddled with depressions and elevations, similar to the surface of the Earth.” In addition, Galileo draws attention to the ashen light on the part of the Moon not illuminated by the Sun. He considers this light to be “the reflection of the Earth.”
Later it turned out that at the same time the Englishman Harriot and his student Lower were observing celestial bodies using a telescope (their observations were not known to contemporaries). Lower wrote in a letter to his teacher that Luna filled the jam pie his cook had baked for him the previous week. Leonardo da Vinci and Mestlin, Kepler's teacher, already spoke about the ashen light on the Moon.

Then, before Galileo’s eyes, the Milky Way disintegrated into individual stars: “all the disputes that tormented philosophers for centuries fell silent by themselves, gratefully visualized and very excited... The Milky Way is nothing more than a cluster of countless stars, as if located in quaches...”

Finally, on January 7, 1610, Galileo directed the work towards Jupter. Near Jupter he discovered three stars. He had no doubt that he was seeing ordinary “fixed” stars, but something attracted his attention. The next night, Galileo, “led by an unknown destiny,” again examines Jupter. He had every reason not to regret it! He again saw the familiar stars, but... their position relative to Jupter had changed: yesterday they were located on different sides of Jupter, and today they were all on the same side. For now, we can continue to consider the stars motionless, and the change in their relative position can be explained by the movement of Jupter. On January 9, “the sky was covered with clouds on all sides.” On January 10 and 11, Galileo found only two of the three stars. January 13...four appeared!

A new solution is brewing: these stars are moving relative to Jupter, these are its satellites - the moons, and their disappearance is explained by their eclipse. By the end of the month, “the transition from the error of the riddle to the feeling of admiration,” Galileo is absolutely sure of this. He writes to the Florentine minister Vinta: “the greatest of all miracles seems to be that I discovered four new planets and their own movements observed by him and the differences in their movements relative to each other and relative to the movements of other stars. These new planets are moving around a large star in the same way that Venus and Mercury and perhaps other known planets move around the Sun."

Until March 2, Galileo observes the satellites of Jupter, taking advantage of every cloudless night, and already on March 12, his sign “The Starry Messenger” comes out, bringing great and very amazing sights and offering for everyone’s consideration, especially philosophers and astronomers, what Galileo Galilei, the Florentine patrician, State Mathematician of the Padua Hypnasia, observing with a telescope, recently invented, on the surface of the Moon, among the countless fixed stars, in the Milky Way, the nebulous stars, and above all on the four planets, revolving around the stars of Jupter at unequal distances with unequal periods and with amazing speed.”
