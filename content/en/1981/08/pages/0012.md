So the light passes through a layer of nematic liquid and reaches the second polarizer. And here a situation already familiar to us arises. In cases a and b (Fig. 5), light cannot pass through the optical system, but in case c it passes continuously. Now let’s imagine an intermediate case, when the axes $\vec{n}$ on the walls are crossed with each other, but in the thickness of the layer, due to some influence, they turn almost perpendicular to the glass. In this situation, almost no light passes through the second polarizer. There is one more step left before the mass application of such a system: one must learn to control the optical axis of a nematic liquid so that in the absence of influence this axis is oriented as at figure 5, and when the influence is turned on, it tilts at a noticeable angle, as in Figures 5, 6 and 2. After the influence is turned off, the molecules occupy and are located in their previous positions due to the conditions on the glass surfaces and the interaction with each other.

And it turned out that it is very easy to do this in a nematic liquid crystal using an electric field, enclosing a layer between polished glasses on which high-quality electrodes are applied. By connecting a weak battery to these electrodes and short-circuiting the target, we will make our optical system light-transmitting, and by opening the target, we will make it pronounceable, which is what Fredericks first accomplished. It remains to discuss an interesting physical question: why the electric field turns the molecules the way we need, and how strong it should be.

The answer to the first part of the question is easy to give with the help of figure 7. Let a molecule, in which a dipole is easily formed along the length axis, be in an electric field and have a certain angle between the vectors $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{n}$. Then, in the formed dipole, the charges $+Q$ and $-Q$ are acted upon by the forces $\vec{F}_+ = +Q\vec{E}$ and $\vec{F}_- = -Q\vec{E}$; Thus, a pair of forces arises, creating a spinning moment. This moment of force rotates the molecule so that its length axis is oriented along the vector $\vec{E}$.

The important thing to note here is that you actually need to rotate a very large number of molecules at the same time, but fortunately there is no need to rotate each molecule individually. Since the molecules interacting with each other are oriented in the same way, then, roughly speaking, it is enough to just remove one so that the others, like dominoes, turn each other around the first. Therefore, to implement the described effect, a certain finite value of the potential difference across the electrodes is required—the threshold voltage. This threshold value is determined from the condition of equality of the moments of two forces: the force acting from the electric field, and the restoring force of interaction between molecules, which tends to orient the molecules in the same way as the molecules stuck to the glass are oriented. And it turns out that regardless of the thickness of the layer, the threshold voltage can be a fraction of a volt, and the thickness of the layer used in practice is a fraction of a millimeter! This is many times less than what is required to obtain the same optical effects in solid crystals, which has led to enormous practical interest in liquid crystals when creating dials of all kinds.
