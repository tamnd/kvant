⟦folio 25⟧

Johannes Kepler

Jupiter, Mars and Venus; Mercury, the very last of the Sun’s surroundings, is so immersed in its rays that Kepler still could not notice anything similar in it.” Kepler looks for numerical patterns everywhere! Then he thinks that we can talk about planets revolving around “unmoving stars”, and not the Sun. Bruno remembers the countless worlds of the Jordans and is already thinking “about the possibility, after this, of making discoveries of countless more new planets there.”

Meanwhile, Emperor Rudolf II (Kepler was the imperial astronomer) receives the Starry Messenger. Kepler unconditionally trusts Galileo's message: “Perhaps I will seem too bold if I so easily believe your statements, without being supported by any of my own experience. But why should I not believe the most learned mathematician, whose rightness is evidenced by the very strength of his judgment, who is far from the essence and, in order to gain general recognition, will not say that he saw what he actually did not see, without hesitating, out of love for the truth, to contradict widespread changes? Kepler points Galileo to a number of predecessors (Mestlin spoke of the ashen light of the Moon, Porto predicted the possibility of creating a Kritenian group). Kepler finds that the Sun is brighter than the fixed stars, and he wants to believe in the exclusivity of our world: “our world does not belong to a simple herd of other infinite ones.” There is no limit to Keplar’s ​​imagination: “the assumption that not only on the Moon, but also on Jupiter there are inhabitants will not be unlike the truth... Give ships or adapt sails to the celestial air, and there will be people who are not afraid of such vastness.”

Keplar himself, of course, is concerned about the patterns in the distribution of the number of satellites of the planets: “I would rather have a critical task ready, with which I would overtake you in the discovery of two (as, it seems to me, the proportion required) satellites of Mars and six or eight Saturns, to which, perhaps, one or two will be added around Venus and Mercury.” (Kepler did not know whether to stick with the arithmetic or geometric progression!)

Majini tries to win Keplar over to his side. Kepler is at first implacable: “We are both Copernicians, making our own happy.” But Majini does not calm down. His goal is “to expel and destroy these four new minions of Jupiter.” A series of pamphlets against Galileo was opened in May 1610 by Martin Gorky, an astronomer from Magini's circle. In his “Shortest Strangestip against the Star Springer,” the satellites of Jupiter were declared an optical illusion. Kepler is not constant in his attitude towards Gorsky. In a letter to Galileo, this work is called impudent; he is “surprised at the boldness of this young man.” To Gorsky himself, Kepler writes: “I am not surprised and will not blame you; the opinions of those who philosophize must be free.”
