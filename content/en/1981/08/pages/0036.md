⟦folio 34⟧

Circle
nine points
and Euler's straight line

The simplest of polygons - the triangle - is produced by attractive geometricians. After Euclid, beautiful theorems were proved about him by such wonderful scientists of antiquity as Apollonius, Heron, Menelaus and Ptolemy. Closer to our time, the triangle was fascinated by Euler, Poncelet, Simson, Desargues, Klein, Hadamard.

Nowadays, the triangle has gone a little out of fashion, but has not lost its former beauty. You can verify this by reading about the circle and line associated with the triangle, named after Leonhard Euler. And the problems considered later, we hope, will show that there is still something to think about around the triangle.

Euler circle
Let's start with the following remarkable theorem, published by Euler in 1765:

The bases of the altitudes, the bases of the medians and the points located in the middle of the segments from the orthcenter*) to the vertices of the triangle lie on the same circle.

This circle is called the circle of nine points or the Euler circle (Fig. 1).

Before proceeding with the proof of this theorem, let us pay attention to several simple but useful facts related to the geometry of a triangle (we recommend proving them yourself).

Let $O$ be the center of the circle circumscribed about triangle $ABC$, $M$ be the point of intersection of its medians (for other notations, see figure 1).

(1) The points $A_3, B_3, C_3$ are the centers of the circumcircles corresponding to the triangles $AB_1C_1, A_1BC_1, A_1B_1C$. (Indication: AN, VN, CH are the diameters of these circles - see Fig. 2).

(2) Triangles $AB_1C_1, A_1BC_1, A_1B_1C$ are similar to each other and similar to the original triangle $ABC$. (Hint: Matching vertices are indicated by the same letters; $ABC = A\widehat{HC} = A\widehat{B_1C_1}$).

(3) Let $A, B$ and $C$ be the values ​​of the corresponding angles of triangle $ABC$. If these angles are acute, then the angles of the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ are equal to the corresponding angles $180^\circ - 2A, 180^\circ - 2B, 180^\circ - 2C$; if, for example, $A > 90^\circ$, then they are equal to $2A - 180^\circ, 2B, 2C$ (Fig. 2).

(4) The distance from the point $O$ to each side of the input triangle is less than the distance from the anti-route vertex to the orthocenter $|A_2O| = \frac{1}{2}|AH|, |B_2O| = \frac{1}{2}|BH|, |C_2O| = \frac{1}{2}|CH|$.

(Hint: Distance of a triangle whose sides pass through points $A, B, C$ parallel to $BC, AC, AB$ respectively.)
