⟦folio 22⟧

The Signoria of Venice could have forked out resources for some invention that had undoubted practical application.

...In 1608, spotting scopes appeared in Holland that made it possible to smooth out individual objects; they were sometimes called “new glasses.” Leonardo da Vinci also spoke about glasses that allow you to see the Moon larger; Roger Bacon talked about glasses making a person the size of a mountain. Master optics Lippershey and Andriansen lived up to the honor of the invention.

By the middle of the year, pipes appeared in Paris. At the same time, some stranger tried to sell a telescope to Venetian resources, without giving details about its origin. Paolo Sarpi, a friend of Galileo, gave a negative review of the possibility of using the telescope "in war, on land and at sea." The first pipes were still very imperfect.

Galileo heard about the trumpets while he was in Venice. “Having learned about this, I returned to Padua, where I was then living, and began to think about the problem. On the very first night after my return, I solved it, and the next day I made an instrument, which I reported to Venice to the same friends with whom I had discussed this subject the previous day. I immediately set about making another, more advanced instrument, which I arrived in Venice six days later.” In the Starry Messenger, Galileo described the situation in even more solemn terms: “...without sparing either labor or expense, I achieved that I made an instrument so perfect that when viewed through it, objects seemed almost a thousand times larger and more than thirty times closer than those seen naturally. The above would be completely unnecessary considering how convenient such instruments are both on land and at sea.”

In fact, the characteristics of the pipes were more modest. Galileo's first trumpet gave a threefold magnification, and the trumpet brought to Venice gave an eightfold magnification. Galileo decided, with the help of his perfect trumpet, to win the favor of the members of the Signoria (perhaps it was Sarpi’s idea). On August 21, the respected people of Venice looked at the separated quarters of the city from the bell tower of St. Mark's Cathedral, and on August 24, Galileo solemnly donated his trumpet to Venice. Galileo did not skimp on advertising his gift. In Leonardo Donago's last panel of Venice, he says that he derived the idea of ​​the device "from the most secret messages about perspective."

Then they said a lot that Galileo took someone else’s invention for himself (this is discussed in Brecht’s play “The Life of Galileo”). Galileo himself, at least in his publications, always admitted that he built his pipe after hearing about the Dutch invention (but without having detailed information and without seeing the “Flemish perspective”). Later he emphasized the originality of his path. “Now we know for sure that the Dutch inventor of the telephone was a simple craftsman who made ordinary glasses. Listening through the glasses of different types, he looked immediately through the glasses, one convex, the other concave, located at different distances from the eye... and thus opened the instrument. I, driven by the highest extraction, found the tool through reasoning.”

We can say that Galileo demonstrated the superiority of theory over practice. For many years, no one could create trumpets comparable to Galileo's trumpets (because of this, in part, Galileo's astronomical observations were not exposed).

Galileo's trumpet fulfilled its purpose: he was assigned a lifetime annual wish of a thousand florins, unprecedented for a mathematician. Galileo must make 12 trumpets for the signoria, and no more trumpets should be provided to anyone.
