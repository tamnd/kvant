The world through the eyes of an engineer

Recently, the publishing house “Mir” (in the series “In the World of Science and Technology”) published a book by the famous English engineer, specialist in the theory of readability, professor at the University of Reading, James Edward Gordon. This book is called “Constructions, or Why Things Don’t Break.” It is devoted to problems of design and the physical foundations of theory and readability.

The topic touched upon in the book is complex both for specialists who are well aware of all the difficulties and problems that have arisen when working in this area, and for laymen in particular who are ignorant. True, two hundred years ago (not to mention more ancient times) the specialists will only get away with the fact that, in the same case, being in the dark about exactly how to build, they still dared to build at their own peril and risk. Quite often, the price paid for such villages was piles of rubble. Fortunately, the results of the work of engineers and builders have not always been crushed. Unique architectural monuments clearly convince us of this. However, building by trial and error, without knowing for sure whether they succeeded in completing the construction, was quite expensive. Brilliant minds have gathered dust to transform the process of construction and design from an art into a strict and exact science. How difficult this task was can be judged by this fact. At the very beginning of the 19th century, Jung introduced the concept of the modulus of elasticity (now bearing his name) - the fundamental concept of the theory of elasticity. This is how he explained it: “The modulus of elasticity of any substance is a column of this substance capable of exerting pressure on its base, which is as related to the weight causing a certain degree of compression as a wedge of a substance is to a decrease in this length.” It is not surprising that before Young's module could wrap its head around the minds of engineers, an adding degree took place. Then things moved at a faster pace (mainly due to a successful alliance with engineering practice), but until the very end of the 19th century there were many successfully working practicing engineers who used only their own intuition and looked down on “newfangled theories.” To be fair, it should be noted that the theoretical engineers of that time, having carefully carried out all the calculations, were mistaken no less often than their practical colleagues. True, the theorists had one advantage that ultimately ensured their victory: once they made a mistake, they did not repeat it again.

Throughout the book, Professor Gordon, as a declared detective, deals with Japanese ideas, destroying everything that comes his way: be it houses, bridges, ships or dams. As the case progresses, it turns out that it is necessary to fight not with one criminal, but with a whole gang: the culprits of the disasters were also the concentration of charges, causing holes to appear and leading to cracking of materials; and deformation—tension, tension, torsion; and personal pressures that insidiously exceed acceptable boundaries. According to the laws of the genre, representatives can eventually be identified and, to some extent, neutralized by creating conditions (that is, such structures) in which their destructive abilities cannot pass through. But as soon as an engineer or designer loses sight of at least one of the heroes of the story, he will not notice to remind himself of himself with a pile of debris and ruins that have arisen on the site of imprudently boldly created structures.

Comparing a server book to a detective story in this case is quite appropriate. The book is read with great interest, which can primarily be explained by its literary merits. The author does not simply present to the reader facts that are worthy of attention in themselves. He leads an interesting narrative, providing the facts with such reliable details that the reader is closely observed with complicity in the events. So, for example, talking about the ingenious and complex frame of Parsi ships, J. Gordon writes: “Each section of the mast is made from a separate log and holds the tracking in the desired position by a complex and ingenious system of shrouds and stays. The system is designed in such a way that, if necessary, all the upper parts of the mast and yards can be disassembled and lowered onto the deck. Since the largest beams weigh several tons, it requires not only skill, but also a lot of presence of mind to lift and lower such bulky objects on a rocking ship. The large ship had a crew of approximately 800 men, most of whom would put both steeplejacks and trainees to shame.
