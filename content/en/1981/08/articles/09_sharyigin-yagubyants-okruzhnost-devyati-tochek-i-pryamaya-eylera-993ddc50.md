The simplest of polygons - the triangle - is produced by attractive geometricians. After Euclid, beautiful theorems were proved about him by such wonderful scientists of antiquity as Apollonius, Heron, Menelaus and Ptolemy. You can verify this by reading about the circle and line associated with the triangle, named after Leonhard Euler. And the problems considered later, we hope, will show that there is still something to think about around the triangle.

Euler circle
Let's start with the following remarkable theorem, published by Euler in 1765:

The bases of the altitudes, the bases of the medians and the points located in the middle of the segments from the orthcenter*) to the vertices of the triangle lie on the same circle.

This circle is called the circle of nine points or the Euler circle (Fig. 1).

Before proceeding with the proof of this theorem, let us pay attention to several simple but useful facts related to the geometry of a triangle (we recommend proving them yourself).

Let $O$ be the center of the circle circumscribed about triangle $ABC$, $M$ be the point of intersection of its medians (for other notations, see figure 1).

(1) The points $A_3, B_3, C_3$ are the centers of the circumcircles corresponding to the triangles $AB_1C_1, A_1BC_1, A_1B_1C$. (Indication: AN, VN, CH are the diameters of these circles - see Fig. 2).

(2) Triangles $AB_1C_1, A_1BC_1, A_1B_1C$ are similar to each other and similar to the original triangle $ABC$. (Hint: Matching vertices are indicated by the same letters; $ABC = A\widehat{HC} = A\widehat{B_1C_1}$).

(3) Let $A, B$ and $C$ be the values ​​of the corresponding angles of triangle $ABC$. If these angles are acute, then the angles of the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ are equal to the corresponding angles $180^\circ - 2A, 180^\circ - 2B, 180^\circ - 2C$; if, for example, $A > 90^\circ$, then they are equal to $2A - 180^\circ, 2B, 2C$ (Fig. 2).

(4) The distance from the point $O$ to each side of the input triangle is less than the distance from the anti-route vertex to the orthocenter $|A_2O| = \frac{1}{2}|AH|, |B_2O| = \frac{1}{2}|BH|, |C_2O| = \frac{1}{2}|CH|$.

(Hint: Distance of a triangle whose sides pass through points $A, B, C$ parallel to $BC, AC, AB$ respectively.)

Euler's line

Note that from statement (4) it immediately follows that the points O, M and H lie on the same straight line, since the straight line OH divides the median AA₂ in the same ratio as the point M. This straight line is called the Euler straight line of the triangle ABC. It is easy to see that |OM| = $\frac{1}{2}$ |MH|.

Let us now prove that points A₁, B₁, C₁, A₂, B₂, C₂, A₃, B₃, C₃ lie on the same circle. Let us confine ourselves to the case of acute triangle ABC. (For an obtuse triangle, do the reasoning yourself.)

First, note that the points A₁, A₂, B₂, C₂ lie on the same circle (Fig. 3), since B₂A₁ and C₂A₁ are the medians of right triangles AA₁C and AA₁B drawn to the hypotenuse, and therefore B₂A₁C₂ = B₂A₁A + AA₁C₂ = A₁A B₂ + A₁A C₂ = A = B₂A₂C₂. It is proved in an absolutely analogous way that points B₁, A₂, B₂, C₂ lie on the same circle (point B₁ is a point of the “same kind” as A₁!) and that points C₁, A₂, B₂, C₂ lie on the same circle. Thus, six points A₁, B₁, C₁, A₂, B₂, C₂ lie on the same circle. On the other hand, if we consider, for example, the quadrilateral A₃B₃A₂C₃ (Fig. 4) and express some of its two opposite angles in terms of angles A, B and C (which is not difficult to do), we will be convinced that their sum is equal to 180°; it follows that the quadrilateral A₃B₃A₂C₃ is cyclic. Thus, six points A₃, B₃, C₃, A₂, B₂, C₂ also lie on the same circle. This means that all nine points A₁, B₁, C₁, A₂, B₂, C₂, A₃, B₃, C₃ lie on the same circle (points A₂, B₂, C₂ are included in the general circle). Euler's theorem has been proven.

Tough task

Many interesting problems have been written on the topic of the nine-point circle and the Euler line. Here is one of them, published in the book “Selected Problems from the American Mathematical Monthly” (M., “Mir”, 1977):

Given triangle ABC; AA1, BB1, CC1 - its heights. Prove that the Euler lines of triangles AB1C1, A1BC1, A1B1C intersect at a point P of the circle of nine points of triangle ABC for which one of the segments PA1, PB1, PC1 is equal to the sum of the other two segments (Fig. 5).

Let, as before, H be the orthocenter of triangle ABC, A3, B3, C3 be the midpoints of segments AH, BH, CH (remember, they are the centers of circles circumscribed about similar triangles AB1C1, A1BC1, A1B1C).

Let us first prove an auxiliary statement. If straight lines are drawn through points A3, B3, C3, identically located with respect to triangles AB1C1, A1BC1, A1B1C, then such straight lines intersect on the circle of nine points of triangle ABC.

Let us denote by P the point of intersection of two of these straight lines - to the right, straight lines drawn through A3 and C3 (Fig. 6). Since lines A3P and C3P are equally located relative to triangles AB1C1 and A1B1C, A1A3P=A1C3P. This means that a circle passing through points P1, A1, C3 will pass through points A3. But then this circle is the circle of the nine points of triangle ABC! Thus, any two of the drawn lines will intersect on the circle of nine points of triangle ABC, it is easy to conclude that all three lines will intersect at one point.
