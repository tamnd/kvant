By the middle of the year, pipes appeared in Paris. At the same time, some stranger tried to sell a telescope to Venetian resources, without giving details about its origin. Paolo Sarpi, a friend of Galileo, gave a negative review of the possibility of using the telescope "in war, on land and at sea." The first pipes were still very imperfect.

Galileo heard about the trumpets while he was in Venice. “Having learned about this, I returned to Padua, where I was then living, and began to think about the problem. On the very first night after my return, I solved it, and the next day I made an instrument, which I reported to Venice to the same friends with whom I had discussed this subject the previous day. I immediately set about making another, more advanced instrument, which I arrived in Venice six days later.” In the Starry Messenger, Galileo described the situation in even more solemn terms: “...without sparing either labor or expense, I achieved that I made an instrument so perfect that when viewed through it, objects seemed almost a thousand times larger and more than thirty times closer than those seen naturally. The above would be completely unnecessary considering how convenient such instruments are both on land and at sea.”

In fact, the characteristics of the pipes were more modest. Galileo's first trumpet gave a threefold magnification, and the trumpet brought to Venice gave an eightfold magnification. Galileo decided, with the help of his perfect trumpet, to win the favor of the members of the Signoria (perhaps it was Sarpi’s idea). On August 21, the respected people of Venice looked at the separated quarters of the city from the bell tower of St. Mark's Cathedral, and on August 24, Galileo solemnly donated his trumpet to Venice. Galileo did not skimp on advertising his gift. In Leonardo Donago's last panel of Venice, he says that he derived the idea of ​​the device "from the most secret messages about perspective."

Then they said a lot that Galileo took someone else’s invention for himself (this is discussed in Brecht’s play “The Life of Galileo”). Galileo himself, at least in his publications, always admitted that he built his pipe after hearing about the Dutch invention (but without having detailed information and without seeing the “Flemish perspective”). Later he emphasized the originality of his path. “Now we know for sure that the Dutch inventor of the telephone was a simple craftsman who made ordinary glasses. Listening through the glasses of different types, he looked immediately through the glasses, one convex, the other concave, located at different distances from the eye... and thus opened the instrument. I, driven by the highest extraction, found the tool through reasoning.”

We can say that Galileo demonstrated the superiority of theory over practice. For many years, no one could create trumpets comparable to Galileo's trumpets (because of this, in part, Galileo's astronomical observations were not exposed).

Galileo's trumpet fulfilled its purpose: he was assigned a lifetime annual wish of a thousand florins, unprecedented for a mathematician. Galileo must make 12 trumpets for the signoria, and no more trumpets should be provided to anyone.

At the end of 1609, Galileo examined the Moon through his work and discovered that “the surface of the Moon is not smooth and uneven and not perfectly spherical, as a legion of philosophers believed in relation to its goals, but, on the contrary, uneven, rough, riddled with depressions and elevations, similar to the surface of the Earth.” In addition, Galileo draws attention to the ashen light on the part of the Moon not illuminated by the Sun. He considers this light to be “the reflection of the Earth.”
Later it turned out that at the same time the Englishman Harriot and his student Lower were observing celestial bodies using a telescope (their observations were not known to contemporaries). Lower wrote in a letter to his teacher that Luna filled the jam pie his cook had baked for him the previous week. Leonardo da Vinci and Mestlin, Kepler's teacher, already spoke about the ashen light on the Moon.

Then, before Galileo’s eyes, the Milky Way disintegrated into individual stars: “all the disputes that tormented philosophers for centuries fell silent by themselves, gratefully visualized and very excited... The Milky Way is nothing more than a cluster of countless stars, as if located in quaches...”

Finally, on January 7, 1610, Galileo directed the work towards Jupter. Near Jupter he discovered three stars. He had no doubt that he was seeing ordinary “fixed” stars, but something attracted his attention. The next night, Galileo, “led by an unknown destiny,” again examines Jupter. He had every reason not to regret it! He again saw the familiar stars, but... their position relative to Jupter had changed: yesterday they were located on different sides of Jupter, and today they were all on the same side. For now, we can continue to consider the stars motionless, and the change in their relative position can be explained by the movement of Jupter. On January 9, “the sky was covered with clouds on all sides.” On January 10 and 11, Galileo found only two of the three stars. January 13...four appeared!

A new solution is brewing: these stars are moving relative to Jupter, these are its satellites - the moons, and their disappearance is explained by their eclipse. By the end of the month, “the transition from the error of the riddle to the feeling of admiration,” Galileo is absolutely sure of this. He writes to the Florentine minister Vinta: “the greatest of all miracles seems to be that I discovered four new planets and their own movements observed by him and the differences in their movements relative to each other and relative to the movements of other stars. These new planets are moving around a large star in the same way that Venus and Mercury and perhaps other known planets move around the Sun."

Until March 2, Galileo observes the satellites of Jupter, taking advantage of every cloudless night, and already on March 12, his sign “The Starry Messenger” comes out, bringing great and very amazing sights and offering for everyone’s consideration, especially philosophers and astronomers, what Galileo Galilei, the Florentine patrician, State Mathematician of the Padua Hypnasia, observing with a telescope, recently invented, on the surface of the Moon, among the countless fixed stars, in the Milky Way, the nebulous stars, and above all on the four planets, revolving around the stars of Jupter at unequal distances with unequal periods and with amazing speed.”

A year of great discoveries. The year 1610, which began with the discovery of the satellites of Jupiter, was extremely happy for Galil the astronomical scientist: he made almost all of his remarkable astronomical observations in this year. On July 25, Galileo again observed “Jupiter in the morning in the East with his retinue.” After this, he discovered “another extraordinary miracle.” He reports his discovery to Florence, keeps it secret until publication: “The star of Saturn is not only one, but consists of three, which seem to touch each other, but do not move or change among themselves..., and the middle one is about three times larger than the two side ones; and they are arranged in this form.”

To Kepler, Galileo sends the phrase, protected as an angram: “The highest planet of the triple observable.” Galileo later wrote: “I found a whole courtyard at Jupiter and two henchmen at the old man [Saturn - S.G.], they support him and never bounce off his sides.”

Galileo did not reveal his secret for five months. Kepler and Rudolf II were impatient to find out the solution, and the most incredible assumptions were made. “Satisfy our passions as quickly as possible,

Two years later, Saturn suddenly stopped being triple. Galileo related this to the movement of Saturn around the Sun and predicted that it would soon be visible again as three stars. The event was predicted, but Galileo did not unravel the secrets of Saturn. The mystery was revealed when, in 1655, Huygens, viewing Saturn with a telephoto lens with 92x magnification, discovered that Saturn was surrounded by a ring, which, at varying magnifications, appeared to be lateral to stars. The ring becomes invisible when it is edge-on towards terrestrial observation. This is a rare phenomenon and Galileo was lucky enough to observe it. Such was the evolution of the critical ones in the impression from Saturn as the telephopes became stronger: from an olive tree to a ball surrounded by a ring. Huygens also discovered Saturn's largest moon, Titan.

Soon after a letter was sent to Kepler with a solution to the anagram, news appeared on other planets. Galileo had been closely observing Venus for a long time. Both the supporters of Ptolemy and the supporters of Copernicus had a lot of trouble with Venus and Mercury. The first could not agree on where their “spheres” were located - inside or outside the “sphere” of the Sun. It was clear to the Copernicans that if these planets were dark bodies, then, since they were located between the Sun and the Earth, incomplete disks of the planets (a phenomenon similar to the phases of the Moon) should be observed at times. This problem will not arise if it is assumed that the planets shine with their own light (apparently, Kepler thought so) or that they are transparent (this possibility was discussed on the server).

Castelli recalls this problem in a letter to Galil dated December 5, 1610. “Since (as I believe) Copernicus’ position is correct that Venus revolves around the Sun,” writes Castelli, “it is clear that it is necessary for us to sometimes observe her horned, sometimes not... if, however, the small size of the horns and the emission of rays does not prevent us from observing these differences in our construction.” But did Galileo need this reminder? Already on December 10, he sent to Kepler in Prague, through the Tokan ambassador Giuliano Medici, an encrypted message about the discovery of the phase of Venus with the accompanying letter: “I am sending you an encrypted message about another of my untrained observations, which leads to the resolution of the most important disputes built in and which contains the most important argument in favor of the Pythagorean and Copernican systems.” Kepler, as always, is eager to find out the answer.
