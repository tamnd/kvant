Algebra without numbers

In the 20th century, the role of algebra in modern mathematics increased so much that now they talk about the “algebraization” of mathematics. This process is almost not reflected in the school course, but it would be very useful if schoolchildren interested in mathematics could get acquainted with it. An excellent guide for this can be the book by L. A. Kaluzhin and V. I. Sushansky “Transformation and Permutations” (M., “Nauka”, 1979).

The purpose of the book is to introduce students to the beginnings of group theory, which has wide applications in mathematics and the natural sciences, such as nuclear physics, relativity, and crystallography.

Group-theoretic concepts and results are presented in the book within the framework of the theory of the group of permutations of finite sets. This allows the authors to show various and interesting applications of group theory and sufficiently justify the introduction of the concept of group.

You will probably agree that, say, a square is a red trapezoid, but the harm to the sum obliges,

why do you think so? It turns out that group theory allows you to indicate a certain natural “measure of beauty” of various geometric figures, and it is this measure, you believe, that you are subconsciously guided by when comparing figures.
