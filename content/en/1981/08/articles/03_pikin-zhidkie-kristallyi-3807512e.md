Liquid crystals

The amazing world of liquid crystals opened up to the eyes of scientists relatively long ago, but over the past 15-20 years there has been a huge leap in understanding the nature of the liquid crystalline state, the physical properties of these substances, and their role in modern science and technology. And now there is no longer any doubt that further scientific and technological progress cannot do without these materials, varied in their properties, highly economical, and relatively simple to manufacture and use.

And the very first information about such substances was reported in 1888 by the original botanist F. Reinitzer, who synthesized untrained crystals. When they were heated, a liquid was obtained, which, depending on the temperature, was either cloudy, or pronounced, or acquired a blue color. The German physicist O. Lehmann began a systematic study of such substances and found that a special state had been discovered, inherent in many organic compounds.

Nematic liquid

The liquid is very different from gases and solid crystals. The atoms or molecules that make up a liquid cannot move very far from each other. This means that in a liquid, the forces of attraction between atoms or molecules are very important. The same can be said about a solid crystal, but in a crystal these forces are only great that the atoms are forced to occupy strictly defined places in it, forming a three-dimensional crystal lattice. In such a lattice there are always selected directions, called crystal axes. Along these directions, the atoms are arranged in a strictly periodic order. In a general liquid there are no designated directions, it does not have its own shape, because the molecules of the liquid are not so much connected with each other and can mix in space - jump from place to place.

Thus, in a flowing liquid the molecules are only on average located at a certain characteristic distance from each other. The answer to the question of how molecules interact with each other and what is the average distance $a$ between them is given by quantum mechanics. It turns out that at large distances between molecules their interaction is determined by attractive forces, and at very short distances by repulsive forces. Consequently, molecules cannot stick together over a very short distance due to very large repulsive forces - in this case they say that molecules cannot penetrate each other. At a distance $a$, approximately equal to the size of the molecules, the weak interaction between molecules becomes zero.

This is how a general fluid is constructed, consisting of relatively simple molecules or atoms. However, a remarkable discovery awaits us if molecules have a pronounced anisotropic shape, that is, if some characteristic axes can be clearly identified in the molecules. How does polarized light pass through an oriented layer of nematic liquid and through the depicted optical systems in general? If the polarization of light is parallel to the $\vec{n}$ axis (the $\vec{E}$ vector oscillates along the $\vec{n}$ direction), then the light passes through the liquid crystal without changing its polarization (Fig. 5, a). The same happens if the polarization of light is perpendicular to the optical axis (Fig. 5, b). In the case of a twisted orientation $\vec{n}$, the polarization of light also rotates behind the $\vec{n}$ axis (Fig. 5, c).

You can understand why this happens.

To do this, let us recall the basic properties of light and the conditions for its propagation in crystals. Firstly, light as an electromagnetic wave can be emitted when electric charges oscillate, for example, during a rapid periodic change in the distance between unlike charges in an atom (Fig. 3, a) or a molecule. In this case, near the charges with the same period, the electric field changes, which creates a periodically changing magnetic field $\vec{B}$, and the latter generates, already at a greater distance from the charges, an alternating electric field $\vec{E}$, etc. (this is explained by the well-known phenomenon of Faraday electromagnetic induction). This is how a periodically changing electromagnetic field arises in the space around oscillating charges. In such an electromagnetic field, as is known, the vectors $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ are perpendicular to each other and the direction of wave propagation (Fig. 6). The waves travel in all directions from the source. If the light source is a diopole, then the light is initially emitted in a direction perpendicular to the axis of the diopole. There is no radiation along the diopole axis, since in this direction the magnetic fields generated by the oscillations of the electric field on opposite sides of the axis exactly cancel each other out.

If an electromagnetic wave propagates in a medium consisting of atoms and molecules, then vibrations of the vector $\vec{E}$ cause electrical vibrations in the atoms and molecules. As a result, diopoles will arise, in which the electrical distance between different charges changes. But such diopole oscillations, in turn, lead to the emission of a light wave. This means that both the first wave and secondary waves emitted by molecules and atoms under the influence of the field of the first wave actually propagate in the medium. These waves interfere with each other, that is, they strengthen or weaken each other, depending on whether they arrive at a given point in space in phase or antiphase at a given moment in time. In phase - this means that the amplitudes of the waves simply add up; in antiphase - amplitudes are subtracted.

What happens in the liquid crystal layer when light passes through it? In a liquid crystal, it is useful for light waves to cause the separation of charges in molecules and the appearance of diopole vibrations. Let us assume that in a crystal molecule the electrons easily mix along the length of the molecule axis, that is, along the $\vec{n}$ direction. Then, in the case depicted in Fig. 5, the incident wave and secondary waves propagate throughout the thickness of the layer, and the vectors $\vec{E}$ in the waves coincide in direction. In the case depicted in Fig. 5, b, diopoles do not form and secondary waves are not emitted; This means that the incident wave passes through and is not weakened. Finally, in case 5, the polarization of light changes its direction in accordance with the rotation of the optical axis $\vec{n}$ along the thickness of the layer. Rotation of the vector $\vec{E}$ in time with the axis $\vec{n}$ ensures the emission of secondary waves that are not attenuated at any depth of the layer. So the light passes through a layer of nematic liquid and reaches the second polarizer. And here a situation already familiar to us arises. In cases a and b (Fig. 5), light cannot pass through the optical system, but in case c it passes continuously. Now let’s imagine an intermediate case, when the axes $\vec{n}$ on the walls are crossed with each other, but in the thickness of the layer, due to some influence, they turn almost perpendicular to the glass. In this situation, almost no light passes through the second polarizer. There is one more step left before the mass application of such a system: one must learn to control the optical axis of a nematic liquid so that in the absence of influence this axis is oriented as at figure 5, and when the influence is turned on, it tilts at a noticeable angle, as in Figures 5, 6 and 2. After the influence is turned off, the molecules occupy and are located in their previous positions due to the conditions on the glass surfaces and the interaction with each other.

And it turned out that it is very easy to do this in a nematic liquid crystal using an electric field, enclosing a layer between polished glasses on which high-quality electrodes are applied. By connecting a weak battery to these electrodes and short-circuiting the target, we will make our optical system light-transmitting, and by opening the target, we will make it pronounceable, which is what Fredericks first accomplished. It remains to discuss an interesting physical question: why the electric field turns the molecules the way we need, and how strong it should be.

The answer to the first part of the question is easy to give with the help of figure 7. Let a molecule, in which a dipole is easily formed along the length axis, be in an electric field and have a certain angle between the vectors $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{n}$. Then, in the formed dipole, the charges $+Q$ and $-Q$ are acted upon by the forces $\vec{F}_+ = +Q\vec{E}$ and $\vec{F}_- = -Q\vec{E}$; Thus, a pair of forces arises, creating a spinning moment. This moment of force rotates the molecule so that its length axis is oriented along the vector $\vec{E}$.

The important thing to note here is that you actually need to rotate a very large number of molecules at the same time, but fortunately there is no need to rotate each molecule individually. Since the molecules interacting with each other are oriented in the same way, then, roughly speaking, it is enough to just remove one so that the others, like dominoes, turn each other around the first. Therefore, to implement the described effect, a certain finite value of the potential difference across the electrodes is required—the threshold voltage. This threshold value is determined from the condition of equality of the moments of two forces: the force acting from the electric field, and the restoring force of interaction between molecules, which tends to orient the molecules in the same way as the molecules stuck to the glass are oriented. And it turns out that regardless of the thickness of the layer, the threshold voltage can be a fraction of a volt, and the thickness of the layer used in practice is a fraction of a millimeter! This is many times less than what is required to obtain the same optical effects in solid crystals, which has led to enormous practical interest in liquid crystals when creating dials of all kinds.
