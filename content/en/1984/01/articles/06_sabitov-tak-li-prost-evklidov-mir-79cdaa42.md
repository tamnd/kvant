Is the world so simple?

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences I. Kh. SABITOV

The geometry studied at school is called Euclidian in honor of the ancient Greek scientist Euclid, who managed to systematize this science based on the acciatic method. Among the axioms of geometry proposed by Euclid, the fifth one, the parallel axiom, is the most famous. This akisiom (or the “fifth postulate,” as Euclid called it in his “Principles”) essentially states that through a point not lying on a given line, one can find the only straight line parallel to the given one. True, in the Elements, Euclid formulated this statement in a different (but equivalent) form: “If a straight line intersects two straight lines and forms interior one-sided angles in the sum of less than two straight lines, then with an unlimited continuation of these two straight lines they will intersect on the side where the angles are less than two straight lines.”

So how can we experimentally test the parallel axiom?

Is the sum of the angles of a triangle the touchstone of the geometry of the world?

It turns out that this is, in principle, a possibility. The fact is that Euclid’s axiom about parallel lines is equivalent to the statement that the sum of the angles of an arbitrary triangle is equal to 180° *), and in Lobachevsky’s geometry it turns out that this sum is less than 180°. Therefore, Gauss, using geodetic measurements, decided to calculate the sum of the angles in a triangle formed by three high peaks (Brocken, Hohenhagen and Inselberg) in the Gary mountain range, approximately 100 km apart. Lobachevsky chose cosmic scales for his calculations - he measured the sum of the angles of a triangle, the vertices of which were the Earth, the Sun and the brightest star of the northern hemisphere, Sirius. But in both experiments, the resulting deviations from 180° were within the permissible measurement error, so they did not give any conclusion about the geometry of the real world.

However, let us imagine that someone succeeds with impeccable precision in setting that the sum of the angles of a certain triangle is equal to 180°. Does this mean that our geometry is Euclidean?

The answer is yes, if we consider Legendre's theorem true. This theorem, of course, is proven using other axioms of Euclid. But do the remaining axioms hold in the real world? Then we will come to the following question: suppose that everywhere in real space all the axioms of Euclidean geometry are fulfilled “in the small,” that is, within the limits of accessibility for our changing devices (no matter where the observed one is); is it then true that real space is Euclidean "as a whole" (that is, all Euclidean three-dimensional space is properly a model of all physical space)? This important (essentially cosmogonic) question gives a purely mathematical formulation, which will interest us here.

Statement of the problem

Let in the neighborhood of each point in space (say, in a certain ball with a center at this point) all positions of Euclidean geometry are fulfilled. (Of course, formed so that they make sense within the sphere; for example, the axiom of parallels should be replaced by the statement “The sum of the angles of a triangle is 180°,” etc.) What then can be said about the geometry of all space?

A space with the Euclidean property in the neighborhood of each point is called locally Euclidean.

A mathematically more precise definition of local Euclidean space follows from the existence of at least one triangle with a sum of angles of 180° (see the proof in the book Encyclopedia of Elementary Mathematics, M., “Nauka”, 1966, vol. V, p. 399). If a one-to-one correspondence can be established between their points that preserves distances (this means that the distance |ab| is equal to |a'b'| for any points a, b ∈ A, if a', b' is the corresponding point from A'). So, a space is called locally Euclidean if the distance between any two of its points is defined in it and if each of its points has a neighborhood that is variable to some ball of ordinary Euclidean space.

Before answering these questions, let's look at one somewhat unusual example. Let there be two parallel roads in a certain area, along which road stations are quite often located; From each station there is one telephone line - to a symmetrical station on another road. In interior areas there is no telephone service, and messages are transmitted, say, by runners. Let us take as the distance between two points the time required for the transmission of any message. Then it is clear that in order to quickly transmit a message from point A to point B, sometimes it is better for the messenger to run not directly from A to B, but in the opposite direction, to a nearby telephone exchange.

The same kind of unusual distance occurs on our strip with glued edges. Indeed, consider points A, B, C in strip P. For points A and B (the coordinates $y_A$ and $y_B$ differ by less than half the width of the strip - π) the distance is conditional, that is, the length of the segment $AB$. In the language of the “telephone” example, it is more profitable to send a messenger from C to point K, transmit the message instantly by telephone to K' and deliver it to A with a second messenger along the segment $K'A$.

Flattor

Let a rectangle $T$ with vertices $A, B, C, D$ (Fig. 7) be given on the plane $Oxy$. Let us identify the side $AB$ with the side $CD$ so that $A$ is identified with $D$, and $B$ with $C$; further we will identify the side $BC$ with the side $AD$ so that $B$ coincides with $A$, and $C$ with $D$ (with this identification it turns out that all the vertices of the rectangle $T$ merge into one point). Let us define the “distance” between two points of a rectangle as the length of the shortest path between these points, taking into account the above according to the identification.

For example, let's calculate the “distance” between points $M\left(-\frac{3}{4}a; \frac{1}{4}b\right)$ and $N\left(\frac{3}{4}a; b\right)$ (see Fig. 7). First of all, we replace that the segment $MN'$, where $N'=\left(\frac{3}{4}a; -b\right)$, is shorter than the segment $MN$; therefore, the shortest path from $M$ to $N$ must be sought among the paths connecting $M$ with $N'$ (since $N'$ and $N$ are the same point according to the identification condition). The right identity of the side $AD$ is at the same time the right identity of the side $BC$; Therefore, by “attaching” the side $a \leq x < 2a$, $-b \leq y \leq b$ to the side $BC$ (see Fig. 7), we see that every other path from $M$ to $N'$, including the segment $MN'$, is longer than the segment $M'N'$. This means that the shortest path from $M$ to $N$ is the union of the segments $MM_1$ and $M'_1N'$ and the “distance” between $M$ and $N$ is $\sqrt{(a/2)^2+(3b/4)^2}$.

Conclusion

So, the principle of equal demands on all axioms has certainly justified itself: we discovered that the Euclidean geometry of the plane is not based on a single axiom about parallels, but that other axioms significantly determine its appearance. Moreover, it turned out that even Euclideanity in the neighborhood of each point and the satisfiability of the fifth space in space do not lead to Euclideanity as a whole (example: a cylinder and a flat torus). This means that even if in all separately studied parts of space the geometry turns out to be Euclidean, then in general the structure of such a world is not necessarily as simple as a two-dimensional plane or three-dimensional space.

In higher geometry courses it is proven that besides the five examples listed - the plane, the cylinder, the flat torus, the infinite Möbius strip and the flat Klein bottle - there are no other complete local “worlds”. (Completeness, roughly speaking, means that one can unlimitedly extend a “line” with each point, even if only along itself.)

As for the local Euclidean spaces of three dimensions, they differ significantly from them, there are 18 types. Of these, we will mention only one example: this is a layer of space between two parallel planes, which are considered identified by points symmetrical with respect to the middle plane in the layer.
