On the key problems of physics and astrology

Academician V. L. GINZBURG

If we liken physics to a task, then it is a huge multi-story building with a powerful foundation, basements and efficient turrets. Nearby there are underpasses leading to factories, clinics, and training grounds. Many thousands of people, numerous laboratories, hundreds of different installations and instruments, large libraries, hundreds of magazines... The building is being delivered and expanded all the time. Thousands of problems and tasks, much is interconnected, difficult to separate from its neighbors and stand out from the general. On the other hand, differentiation and development are taking place; people have been working side by side for years, but remain strangers. The building begins to resemble a Babylonian base.

At the same time, physics is characterized (perhaps it is more correct to say - still characteristic) by a deep internal unity of methods. The destruction of the building only leads to the fact that it becomes increasingly difficult to identify this unity, trace and use it. More than once I have come across this picture: well, a capable student or already a scientific worker, the author of original articles, has a very narrow outlook. Let's say he is familiar with the deep and difficult problems of quantum field theory, but he does not have a sufficient understanding of neutron stars, black holes, ferroelectrics, superconductors, liquid gelli-tiri (³He) and many others, even at a qualitative level. Meanwhile, to be successful in your work, it is very important to see a broad horizon and, in any case, not limit yourself to “your topic.” In general, the well-known formulation “everything about one thing and something about everything” is empty and not in the literal sense, but still reflects the goal to which one must strive.

Of course, for a schoolchild such a goal is still quite far in the future, but I am completely convinced that starting it is possible and necessary already at school: not limiting the school curriculum and textbooks, getting acquainted with a wide range of things, with the latest scientific achievements and discoveries. Of course, we are talking here about understanding at a qualitative level, in a certain respect - about the surface of familiarization. But this is not at all small and can greatly facilitate the further path. Yes, finally, for those interested in science, it is simply very interesting to know how physics, astronomy, and biology “live and breathe” today, no less exciting than for fans of the corresponding literature to read about travel and adventures.

Perhaps today we need a slightly different approach to popularization. I just want to talk about one of the possible ways here.

Among the huge number of problems, tasks and issues that physicists and astrophysicists study, some are highlighted due to their great potential importance for technology, the existence of some difficult obstacles to overcome when trying to solve them, their special complexity, depth, etc. Therefore, it makes a certain sense to compile a list of “especially important and interesting” or “key” problems in physics and astrophysics. Of course, such a list cannot but be somewhat conditional, and in general, some clarifications and reservations are necessary here, which will be discussed below.

One way or another, more than ten years ago I compiled a similar list and wrote an article for the review journal “Success of Physical Sciences” “What problems in physics and astrophysics now seem especially important and interesting?” (UFN, vol. 103, p. 87, 1971).

At the same time, I considered compiling a list and comments on it as a neo-story pedagogical technique, a way to broaden the horizons of young physicists. But I still don’t know the opinion of young people. This completely unexpected article brought upon me a hail of reproaches from fellow physicists of the middle and older generation. Some of them believe that it is generally impossible to single out “especially interesting and important problems”; others are offended if the list of such problems does not include those that they themselves occupy, etc., etc. I do not agree with such criticism; in response to it, I turned the mentioned article into a small book, already translated into many languages ​​*). The latter at least indicates that the discussion of “key issues” is of interest. I recently published an article with the same title as in 1971, but with the subtitle “ten years later” (UFN, vol. 134, p. 469, 1981).

*) The book is called “On Physics and Astrophysics”. The latest (third) edition was published by the Nauka publishing house in 1980.

The division of all these problems into macrophysics, microphysics and astronomy is quite arbitrary. Selecting 23 topics rather than, say, 20 or 30 is also arbitrary. The almost complete absence of questions related to biology is not due to underestimation of the importance of the connections between physics and biology (on the contrary, I treat biophysics with special respect and interest), but to the desire to somehow limit the topic and, moreover, to my completely insufficient competence in biology. Finally, the main thing: there cannot and should not be a concern to occupy ourselves only with “key” problems. The latter are somehow highlighted and attract special attention due to the features noted above. However, no matter how rationally one defines the concept of “key problem,” will it be harmful even for two physicists who compile exactly the same lists of such problems?

I just wanted to demonstrate that understanding the basics is not difficult at all. The same applies to most of the problems mentioned in our list. For example, it is easy to prescribe what a neutron star is. Such a star is like a giant atomic nucleus, consisting mainly of neutrons, but with an admixture of the same number of protons and electrons. The density of matter in common atomic nuclei is of the order of $Q_\rho = 3 \cdot 10^{14} \text{ g/cm}^3 = 3 \cdot 10^{17} \text{ kg/m}^3$.
The average density of matter in neutron stars, roughly speaking, is of the same order (the density itself at a given point of the star depends on the mass of the star and the distance to its center).
It is immediately easy for Pozny to estimate that a star with mass $M_\odot = 2 \cdot 10^{30} \text{ кг}$, having turned into a neutron star, will have a radius

$$r_n \sim \left( \frac{3M_\odot}{4\pi Q_\rho} \right)^{1/3} \sim 10^4 \text{ m} = 10 \text{ km}$$

(radius factors - the visible surface of the Sun - $r_\odot = 7 \cdot 10^8 \text{ m}$).

In 1967-68, the so-called pulsars were discovered - cosmic sources of periodically repeating radiogreening pulses. It turned out that pulsars are rapidly rotating and highly magnetized neutron stars (for example, the pulsar PSR 0531, located in the sign of the Crab Nebula - the remnant of a supernova explosion in 1054, makes 30 revolutions per second; the magnetic field on the surface of the pulsar is thousands of billions of times stronger than the Earth's magnetic field).

Why and how do normal stars turn into neutron stars?
What is the structure of these stars at different depths? Why do they turn quickly? These and many similar questions can also be answered qualitatively. For example, the rapid rotation of neutron stars is associated with the law of conservation of angular momentum - when the body is scanned, it begins to rotate quickly (everyone knows how this circumstance is used in ballet and in figure skating on ice).

I would like the student reader (and especially such an almost adult person, such as a 15-17-year-old student in the 9th or 10th grade) to also draw conclusions. Firstly, one should not think that even the latest achievements in physics, astrophysics and, say, biology are inaccessible to a schoolchild’s understanding. For example, in most cases, a qualitative idea of ​​the day of the case and the nature of the discoveries can be obtained without a lot of labor. Secondly, for a person who loves science, learning about new phenomena and effects, getting acquainted with scientific news, feeling the pulse of initial thought is great satisfaction and joy. I think that these feelings are quite comparable and comparable to those that people experience when they “encounter” works of art. Thirdly, you need to take an active position. Unfortunately, both in school and in institutes, they are not aware and do not always care about making students aware of scientific news and the achievements of modern science. This acquisition will not occur when playing the guitar, or even in the process of playing a game like chess. (I beg the reader not to take this remark as advice not to play musical instruments or chess. I just wanted to emphasize that this should not interfere with those engaged in science.) You need to look for answers yourself, read popular science books and magazines. Perhaps the given list of key problems of physics and astrophysics, with all its conventionality and limitations, will also help to understand what could be interesting to learn about.
