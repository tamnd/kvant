Artificial radioactivity

(to the 50th anniversary of the opening)

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. BOROVOY

At the end of the century, the famous Swedish chemical engineer, inventor of dynamite and one of the richest industrialists, Alfred Nobel, left an overstatement according to which interest from his capital of 31 million crowns goes to pay prizes for outstanding work in physics, chemistry, physiology or medicine, literature and for active work to strengthen peace.

The first prize in physics in 1901 was received by the German scientist Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen for the discovery of radiation, which he called “X-rays”, and we called it RENTGENOVSKIUM.

Very soon the Nobel Prize in Natural Sciences became a generally recognized international award. It will be awarded on December 10. Later, the Nobel laureates give a traditional lecture in the hall of Stockholm University.

The committee that selects the winners considers it imperative that the discovery stands the test of time. For one reason or another, for many scientists it dragged on for decades.

However, James Chadwick, Irene and Frederic Jollus-Curie, who spoke to Swedish and foreign scientists on December 12, 1935, could not afford a long wait.*) The scale of their discovery was immediately appreciated, since they caused a real revolution in physics.

Chadwick performed first that evening. The famous scientist, who worked for many years with his teacher Ernest Rutherford, talked about the discovery of the neutron. It was made in the spring of 1932.

Then the Jollus-Curie spouses rise to the podium one by one. In January 1934, French physicists reported the discovery of a new phenomenon - artificial radioactivity.

“For the first time it became possible for an external cause to cause radioactivity in certain atoms of nuclei within a certain period of time and in the absence of the cause that caused it.”

Now they were talking about this discovery, which, although short-lived, had a rather dramatic background, connected in the most obscure way with the discovery of the neutron.

Neutron

The Nobel lecture continues:

“In recent years, various kinds of artificial transformations have been discovered: one is caused by α-rays... others by neutrons, that is, neutral particles with mass 1, which Professor Chadwick spoke about.”

...Physicists have been studying natural radioactivity for quite a long time - since 1896. Radium, thorium, polonium, ... - about 30 substances in total - emitted α-particles (gel nuclei), β-particles (electrons) and γ-rays (electromagnetic radiation). In 1919, Rutherford expanded the nucleus with fast alpha particles and discovered that the nuclei included protons - hydrogen nuclei.

In 1930, German physicists W. Bothe and G. Becker, bombarding targets made of light elements with alpha particles, discovered radiation of extraordinary penetrating power. They called it “berylium rays” (since the best experiment was with beryllium) and decided that these were γ-rays with enormous energy. For about a year their work did not attract attention, until Jollu-Curie became interested. They carried out beautiful experiments - they placed substances containing hydrogen (for example, paraffin) in the path of beryllium rays - and discovered that mysterious particles knocked out hydrogen nuclei - protons - from the target. Researchers measured the speed of these protons and did not value the energy of beryllium rays, believing that these were γ quanta. It turned out to be huge.

On January 11, 1932, the work was reported to the French Academy of Sciences. Jollus-Curie continued their experiments, still believing that they were dealing with γ-rays. It is possible that it would take them very little time, a few more weeks, to discard this hypothesis and find the right solution. But they were ahead of them.

Chadwick, who had been unsuccessfully searching for the neutron for 12 years (the existence of which was predicted by his teacher Rutherford), immediately understood what particles the French physicists were dealing with. His laboratory equipment was ready, and it took only a month to prove that beryllium rays were a stream of heavy neutral particles - neutrons. The nuclear reaction of the interaction of α-particles with beryllium should have been written as follows:

$$9\mathrm{Be} + 4\mathrm{^2} \alpha \rightarrow 12\mathrm{C} + 1\mathrm{n}.$$

One can imagine the disappointment of the French physicists, who lost the victory so close to physics.

Positron

From the Nobel lecture:

“We observed that aluminum and boron emit not only protons and neutrons but also positive electrons when irradiated.”

We watched...

The time has come to briefly talk about the instruments, or rather, about one, the most beloved Prefederico Jollus-Curie instrument - the Wilson chamber, with the help of which these observations were carried out. In this chamber, droplets of liquid forming along the trajectory of a charged particle make its traces visible and make it possible to form it. Most of the equipment in the Jollus-Curie laboratory was made by the experimenters themselves, or, in any case, designed by them. And the cloud chamber was large and the oars were advanced, so that the particle trace could grow almost 80 times compared to the conventional version of the device. In addition, the camera was placed in a magnetic field, and the traces of positive particles in it were deflected in one direction, and negative ones in the other. By curvature

...The Jolnot-Curies continued their work, irradiating boron, fluorine, and sodium with alpha particles, and soon proved that neutrons (sometimes protons) also appear in these reactions. Here they encountered difficulties, which became a continuation of the discovery. The difficulties were that when a neutron is emitted, for example, aluminum, as a result of the reaction

Phosphorus-30 nuclei should form, which has never been seen in nature, while silicon-30 nuclei - $$\frac{30}{14}\text{Si}$$ - are capable of existing.

Probably, silicon still appears during irradiation - they decided to use it, and began to invent a mechanism in which a phosphorus-30 nucleus with a charge of +15 could lose a unit of charge without changing its mass.

At first it was assumed that it captures one of the atomic electrons during the reaction and turns into a silicon nucleus:

$$\frac{30}{15}\text{P} + e \rightarrow \frac{30}{14}\text{Si}.$$

If only I knew everything in advance! After all, the $$\frac{30}{15}\text{P} \rightarrow \frac{30}{14}\text{Si}$$ transition could also occur with the emission of a positive electron—a positron. Moreover, in photographs in a cloud chamber, French physicists have long replaced traces emerging from the target, which by all indications are electronic, but only turning in the magnetic field in the direction corresponding to the charge $e^+$. They explained them by electrons flying back to the target.

“Niels Bohr became interested in this chimkammi, but we made the mistake of not paying enough attention to these traces.”

Several more months passed before Anderson discovered the existence of the positron in cosmic rays. Everything seemed to fall into place, and the Jolnot-Curies could now write down the reaction of the interaction of aluminum nuclei with alpha particles in the form

$$\frac{27}{13}\text{Al} + \frac{4}{2}\alpha \rightarrow \frac{30}{15}\text{Si} + \frac{1}{0}n + e^+.$$

It was the second time they were so close to discovering a new particle, and the second time they were too late!

Artificial radioactivity

Now comes what might be called the opening formula:

“There are two processes that occur differently in time. First, an α-particle is captured and a neutron is instantly emitted with the formed... phosphorus-30... Then this unstable, that is, a new radioactive element... decays according to an exponential law with a half-life of three minutes and forms silicon.”

But before this explanation there were still places of hard work. In the fall of 1933, at the Solva Congress, in the presence of leading physicists, the Jolnot-Curie spouses spoke about their experiments in which, under the influence of α-rays, neutrons and positrons were emitted from the nuclei of aluminum, phosphorus, and sodium. “Most of the physicists present at the congress did not believe in the correctness of our experiments. After the jam, we were very upset, but at that moment Niels Bohr responded to the side... and said that he attaches more importance to the results obtained. Following this, Pauli supported us with his approval” (F. J-K).

Returning from the congress to Paris, Jolnot-Curie began work again. And finally the day came, or rather the January evening of 1934, when this happened.
