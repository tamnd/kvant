Pricipl Farm

The basis of geometric optics, which operates with the concept of “light ray”, is made up of three laws - the laws of rectilinear propagation, reflection and refraction of light. In ancient times, when these laws were formulated, the question of the nature of light had not yet arisen, and nothing about the physical realization of light was hidden behind the concept of “ray”. This is how it was done in the textbook “Physics 10” (§§ 37 and 65). However, at the end of the 19th - beginning of the 20th centuries. It became clear that light has not only wave properties, but also corpuscular properties. From the point of view of corpuscular (quantum) nature, light is a stream of elements of light particles - photons. In a homogeneous medium, the beam can be considered the trajectories of photons.

But it is interesting that long before this an amazing principle was formulated, from which all the basic laws of propagation follow.

Fig. 1.

light. This principle, found by the French mathematician Pierre Fermat (1601-1665) around 1660, says: of all possible paths between two points, light travels along the one along which the travel time is called the shortest.

From Fermat’s principle (as it is generally called) it follows that in a homogeneous medium (in such a medium the speed of light is the same everywhere) light should propagate straight: a straight line is the shortest distance between two points, therefore, the propagation time is the shortest.

Let us now show that the law of light reflection is also a direct consequence of Fermat’s principle.

Law of light reflection. Let MM be a plane mirror. There is a light source at point A, and we are interested in the path along which the light, reflected from the mirror, comes from point A to point B (Fig. 1). figure 1 shows some of the possible paths - AA'B, ACB, AB'B. There are countless such “routes” for light that can be avoided. They vary in length, so they take different times to complete. It depends on which point of the mirror the ray hits and, after being reflected, goes to B.

From simple geometric messages it is easy to find out exactly where the beam should fall so that the time it takes to travel along the “route” point A - mirror - point B is minimal. At figure 2 one of the possible paths is presented - ACB. Let us lower a perpendicular from point B to the mirror MM and extend it on the other side of the mirror to point B', located at a distance |OB'| = |OB|. Let's draw a line CB'. The resulting triangles COB and COB' are equal to each other, since they are rectangular, they have a common side OC

and $|OB| = |OB'|$. Therefore, $|CB| = |CB'|$, from which it follows that the length of the path of the ray $ACB$ is equal to the sum of the length from $A$ to the point $C$ of incidence of the ray on the mirror and from this point to the point $B$. It is clear that this sum will be minimal if the point $C$ lies on the straight line connecting the points $A$ and $B'$ (Fig. 3). Then the sum of the lengths $|AC|$ and $|CB|$, that is, the length of the entire path of light, will be the smallest. The travel time of light along this path will also be the smallest.

From figure 3 it is clear that $\widehat{BCO} = B'\widehat{CO}$ (triangle $BCB'$ is equilibrium, therefore $CO$ is the bisector of the angle at the vertex), and $\widehat{B'\widehat{CO}} = A\widehat{CM}$ (as vertical). This means that the angles of inclination of the incident and reflected rays to the mirror are equal to each other. This is the law of light reflection. It is customary, however, to calculate angles not from the plane of the mirror, but from the normal to it at the point of incidence. But it is clear that if angles $i$ and $i'$ are equal, then angles $a$ and $\gamma$ are also equal. The law of reflection is usually written in the form

$$\alpha = \gamma.$$

This law, as we see, is a consequence of the fact that light, as it were, “chooses” the path that travels in the least amount of time. It is easy to see that Fermat’s principle also implies the statement that the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal to the mirror at the point of incidence lie in the same plane. If this were not so, then the path would be longer and would require a lot of time.

Let us note another important feature related to the reflection of light from the mirror. If at point $A$ (see Fig. 3) there is a light source, and at point $B$ there is an eye, then the eye will perceive the light as if the light source is not at $A$, but at $A'$, and there is no mirror at all. If the mirror is removed and the source moves from $A$ to $A'$, then the eye will not notice such a replacement.

The law of light refraction. From Fermat’s principle one can also obtain the law of refraction of light (more precisely, light rays). Here we are talking about the transition of light from one medium (medium $I$ on figure 4) to another (medium $II$) across the interface between them. The difference between the media is that the speed of light propagation in them is different.

We will consider the case where the medium $I$ is a vacuum in which the speed of light is $c$, and the second medium is some product (for example, glass, water, etc.) in which the speed of light $v$ is less than $c > v$.

Between the points $A$ in the medium $I$ and $B$ in the medium $II$ we can also imagine an infinite number of paths, but, according to Fermat’s principle, light “chooses” the one that takes the least time to travel. It is clear, for example, that path $AA'B$ is not such a path, because here light travels a short (shortest) distance in a medium with high speed and a long distance in a medium with low speed. Perhaps it is more advantageous than path $AB'B?$ Here light in a low-speed medium travels the minimum part of the path, and the most frequent one travels to a high-speed medium. But is this particular path the most profitable in terms of saving time? It may be more profitable to slightly remove the paths in the environment $II$ in order to shorten the paths in the environment $I?$. In short, you need to find at what point in the world

(the beam) needs to cross the interface between the two media so that the travel time from $A$ to $B$ is minimal. It is clear that this point lies somewhere between $A'$ and $B'$ (including possibly the $B'$ point itself).

Let us denote the distance between $A'$ and $B'$ by $d$. If we need a point $C$ of intersection of the interface boundaries at a distance $x$ from $A'$, then from $B'$ it will be at a distance $d-x$ (see Fig. 4). The path $AC$ traversed by light in the medium $I$ is equal to $\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}$, and the travel time of this path

$$t_1 = \frac{\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}}{c}.$$

The path $CB$ traversed by light in the medium $II$ is equal to $\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}$, and the time required to travel this path is

$$t_2 = \frac{\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}}{v}.$$

The total time $t$ is given by

$$t = t_1 + t_2 = \frac{\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}}{c} + \frac{\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}}{v}.$$

Time $t$ depends only on $x$ - the coordinates of the point where the bow falls, since the values ​​$y_1, y_2, c, v$ and $d$ are constant, that is, the same for all values ​​of $x$. So we need to find at what value $x$ the time $t$ will be the smallest. This problem cannot be solved using general algebra. To solve it, we need to take advantage of the fact that in this case the value of $x$, at which $t$ is minimal, the derivative function on the right side of equation (1) is equal to zero.*) This leads us to the following conditions for $x$:

$$\begin{align*}
\frac{x}{c\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}} &= \frac{d-x}{v\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}}. \\
\frac{x}{\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}} &= \sin A'AC = \sin \alpha; \\
\frac{d-x}{\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}} &= \sin CBB' = \sin \beta,
\end{align*}$$

where $\alpha$ is the angle between the incident ray and normal to the interface at the point of incidence (incidence angle) and $\beta$ is the angle between this normal and pre-field ray (pre-incidence angle). Condition (2) therefore takes the form:

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{c} = \frac{\sin \beta}{v},$$

or $$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = \frac{c}{v}.$$

This is the law of refraction for our case: the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is equal to the ratio of the speeds of light propagation in vacuum and in the medium that borders it. The ratio $c/v$ is a constant value of character for a given environment. This is called the refractive index of a substance and is denoted by the letter $n$, so

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = n.$$

In the general case, when light passes from an arbitrary medium in which the speed of light is $v_1$, into a medium with the speed of light in it $v_2$, the law of refraction has the form

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = n_{21},$$

where $n_{21}$ is the relative refractive index of media 2 and 1.

Fermat's principle is valid, of course, not only for those simplest examples of reflection and refraction of light that we have considered here. Using this principle, you can understand and accurately calculate the course of rays in the face, in the face, and in any complex system of prisms, lenses, and mirrors.
