Manana Vansovskaya, a tenth grade student at the Nabakevo secondary school in the Samtred district of the Georgian USSR, proposes a method for squaring three-digit numbers ending at 25:

“To obtain the square of a three-digit number that adds up to 25, write 625 at the end, then multiply the number of hundreds by 5, write the last digit of the resulting number ahead of 625, and remember the first digit. Then we square the number of hundreds of a given number and add the figure that we just memorized, and write the resulting result in front of the numbers we wrote.”

For example, when squaring 325, we find 3 \cdot 5 = 15, write 5 as a pre-number of 625 and remember 1:

$$(325)^2 = 10000(3^2 + 1) + 1000 \cdot 5 + 625 = 105625.$$

Manana also gives a similar example for squaring four-digit numbers that end at 125. And we invite our readers to justify the method outlined by Manana for squaring a three-digit number that ends at 25, and also independently come up with her “similar example” for four-digit numbers.

A curious way of using fingers to fill in the values ​​and signs of the sine and cosine of the main angles was proposed by seventh-grader Misha Pervakov from school No. 2 in Yegoryevsk, Moscow region:

“Let’s look at the palm of the left hand and number the fingers like this: ring finger - 0, ring finger - 1, middle finger - 2, index finger - 3, thumb - 4. With fingers widely spaced, they roughly correspond to the “main” angles of the first square: 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90° (Fig. 1). The sines of these angles will be equal to half the square root of the number assigned to the finger.”

For example,

$$\sin 45^\circ = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{homер среднего пальца} = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{3}.$$

The cosine value is found in the same way, only the fingers need to be numbered in reverse order: the larger one is 0, ..., the minus one is 4.
