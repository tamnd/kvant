figure 4 shows the receiving film, and below it the corresponding parts of the red and blue lines are shown (we project the blue line on a vertical plane, it does not change significantly from this). In our filmgram we see that two tangents first move towards each other and pass through each other. At this moment (frame No. 2), a self-intersection point is obtained on the red line, but not on the blue line (the corresponding tangent touches the torus at two points! - however, this only passes if the tangent reaches the canopy). Then, at some moment (at the moment of merging two pieces of the section into one - see frames No. 9 and 10 of the first filmogram), two new tangents appeared in the center of each, which quickly diverged. Then the speed with which they diverge decreases, but they still catch up with two old tangents (frame No. 5 of the second filmogram). And then all the tangents disappear.

We will also look at the last point about the visitation. To do this, on the third film we will use even greater slowdown, even greater magnification and - another film trick - we will make time move backwards. On pieces of the section that fall into the field of view, there is an inflection point, and the tangent to the section at the inflection point turns counterclockwise over time. As long as it remains in the direction of our rays, considering part of the curve it does not have tangents parallel to the rays at all. Then such a tangent appears, and at the moment of its appearance it is a tangent at the point of inflection. Immediately after its appearance, it separates, and the points of contact of the two tangents diverge very quickly, and the tangents themselves diverge very slowly (see filmogram on figure 5). Therefore, the two branches of the red line adjacent to the base of the tangent at the inflection point are very close to each other - we have a cusp point; the corresponding branches of the blue line diverge in different directions, that is, the blue line is the value of the considered point of the general eye of the curve.

You can notice that at the points corresponding to the return point of the red line, the tangent to the blue line has the direction of our rays. This alone is enough to verify the existence of return points near the red line. Indeed, the red line results from projecting the blue line along the rays. When projecting a spatial curve onto a plane in the direction of the tangent to this curve, at some point on the projection a cusp point is obtained. To see this, take a curved wire and look at it with one eye so that the tangent in some projections passes through the center of your screen. You will see the return point.

In conclusion, we note that our “red and blue line” construction is applicable not only to the surface of a torus, but to any surface arbitrarily parallel to straight lines. In this case, the red line may have an intersection and return point, while the blue line, as a rule, does not have them.

Note that an artist depicting a three-dimensional object using lines actually depicts part of our red line corresponding to the surface of the object - a part consisting of the bases of those tangents that do not intersect the object between the point of contact and the screen. This part of the red line forms a “visible outline” of the object, such as on the vessel at figure 6 (to the right of the visible outline, draw invisible parts of the red line with a dotted line - this is not customary to do in drawings, but is customary to do in drawings).

Otherwise, the return points will not appear on the visible contour: at least one of the two branches of the red line entering the return point will be invisible.
