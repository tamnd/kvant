Let us denote the distance between $A'$ and $B'$ by $d$. If we need a point $C$ of intersection of the interface boundaries at a distance $x$ from $A'$, then from $B'$ it will be at a distance $d-x$ (see Fig. 4). The path $AC$ traversed by light in the medium $I$ is equal to $\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}$, and the travel time of this path

$$t_1 = \frac{\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}}{c}.$$

The path $CB$ traversed by light in the medium $II$ is equal to $\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}$, and the time required to travel this path is

$$t_2 = \frac{\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}}{v}.$$

The total time $t$ is given by

$$t = t_1 + t_2 = \frac{\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}}{c} + \frac{\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}}{v}.$$

Time $t$ depends only on $x$ - the coordinates of the point where the bow falls, since the values ​​$y_1, y_2, c, v$ and $d$ are constant, that is, the same for all values ​​of $x$. So we need to find at what value $x$ the time $t$ will be the smallest. This problem cannot be solved using general algebra. To solve it, we need to take advantage of the fact that in this case the value of $x$, at which $t$ is minimal, the derivative function on the right side of equation (1) is equal to zero.*) This leads us to the following conditions for $x$:

$$\begin{align*}
\frac{x}{c\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}} &= \frac{d-x}{v\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}}. \\
\frac{x}{\sqrt{y_1^2+x^2}} &= \sin A'AC = \sin \alpha; \\
\frac{d-x}{\sqrt{y_2^2+(d-x)^2}} &= \sin CBB' = \sin \beta,
\end{align*}$$

where $\alpha$ is the angle between the incident ray and normal to the interface at the point of incidence (incidence angle) and $\beta$ is the angle between this normal and pre-field ray (pre-incidence angle). Condition (2) therefore takes the form:

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{c} = \frac{\sin \beta}{v},$$

or $$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = \frac{c}{v}.$$

This is the law of refraction for our case: the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is equal to the ratio of the speeds of light propagation in vacuum and in the medium that borders it. The ratio $c/v$ is a constant value of character for a given environment. This is called the refractive index of a substance and is denoted by the letter $n$, so

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = n.$$

In the general case, when light passes from an arbitrary medium in which the speed of light is $v_1$, into a medium with the speed of light in it $v_2$, the law of refraction has the form

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = n_{21},$$

where $n_{21}$ is the relative refractive index of media 2 and 1.

Fermat's principle is valid, of course, not only for those simplest examples of reflection and refraction of light that we have considered here. Using this principle, you can understand and accurately calculate the course of rays in the face, in the face, and in any complex system of prisms, lenses, and mirrors.
