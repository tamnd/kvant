⟦folio 30⟧

Visible outlines

The top figure shows a photograph of a torus (the surface of a donut or lifebuoy) made of thin glass. We see in this photograph a dark line of an intricate shape: it has two points of self-intersection and four points of return - these are the names of the points at which the line abruptly changes its direction by 180°, forming something like a sharp beak. What does this line represent, why are there points of self-intersection and cusp points on it, and what does the line corresponding to it look like on the surface itself? To answer these questions, let’s translate them into familiar geometric language.

A ray of light that does not intersect the surface at all or intersects it at a large angle does not lose or almost does not lose its intensity. At the same time, a ray close to the tangent to the surface *) passes through a significant thickness of glass (Fig. 1) and the corresponding place in the photograph or on the screen will be darker. Thus, our problems have the following geometric reformulation.

A torus lying in space is produced by a bundle of parallel lines (rays of light). Let us leave in this bundle only those lines that touch the torus, and mark on each of these lines the point of contact and the point of intersection with a pre-fixed plane - the “screen”. The marked points make up lines on the torus and on the screen. We want to understand the structure of these lines.

It turns out that if the angle of inclination of the straight lines to the plane of the axial circle of the torus is not very large, then the lines will look like shown in figure 2; in part, the lines on the torus will not have any features - neither points of self-intersection, nor points of cusp). To verify this, let us draw vertical (parallel to the torus rotation axis) planes through our rays. These planes will intersect the torus along lines, which we will further cut with sections. The change in the cross-section during smooth movement of the vertical plane is depicted in the first “cinegram” (Fig. 3). In each of our vertical planes the rays form a family of parallel lines. We select those that touch the sections and mark off the points of tangency with it with a pencil, and the points of intersection of the tangents with the straight line - the trace of our screen - with a red pencil (see filmogram). The vertical planes that make up our filmogram correspond to parallel lines on the screen. Let's draw them and mark the found colorful dots on them (see Fig. 3). The resulting picture allows you to trace the progress of almost the entire line on the screen, with only two markings in figure places remaining. In the remaining two places (it is enough to distance one of them, since they are symmetrical), we will use the “slow motion filming” method, and will limit ourselves to only the central part of the frame.

*) A tangent to a surface is, by definition, a straight line tangent to some line lying on this surface.
