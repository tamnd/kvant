In this article, we do not have the opportunity to write down local vsklide spaces of three dimensions, but will limit ourselves to considering local vsclide spaces of only two dimensions. We will call them local sclide planes. Our problem can be formulated as follows: how are the local sclidian planes structured as a whole?

Cylinder and its development

Of course, the simplest example of a local vsclide plane is the vsclide plane itself. We will not dwell on it - everything is clear here - but we will start with the next simplest example - an infinite cylindrical surface (or, in short, a cylinder). On figure 3 it is represented as the set of all points on horizontal lines (generators) passing through all possible points of a single circle $C_0$ (director) located in the vertical plane $a$.

But we don't have any "geometry" on the cylinder yet. We need to determine the distance between points, find out what to put under the “straight line” on the cylinder, etc. To do this, we cut our cylinder $U$ along one of the generators and turn it onto the coordinate plane $Oxy$ in the form of an infinite plane $P$, the points $(x; y)$ of which satisfy the inequality $0 \leq y \leq 2\pi$ (Fig. 4). We will assume that a flat strip $P$ with “glued edges” (that is, a strip in which all pairs of points $(b; 0)$,

(b; 2π) are considered identical) and defines the “geometry” on the cylinder. As mathematicians say, we identify pairs of points of the form $(b; 0)$ and $(b; 2\pi)$ for all $b$ and consider the geometry on the strip $P$, taking this relation into account.

Let us check the local slip properties of such a geometry on a cylinder. To do this, take an arbitrary point $A(x; y) \in \Pi$. If the point $A = A_1$ does not lie on the edges of the strip (see Fig. 4), then everything is obvious: take a circle of radius $r$, less than the distance from $A$ to the nearest edge; this circle lies entirely in the strip $P$ and, of course, appears as a general Vsklidovo circle. If the point $A = A_2$ lies on the edge of the strip (see Fig. 4), then its coordinates have the form $(b; 0) \equiv (b; 2\pi)$ and the circular neighborhood is, for example, the union of two (glued!) half-groups of unit radius with centers at points $(b; 0)$ and $(b; 2\pi)$, and since these half-groups are glued along the diameter $MN \equiv M'N'$, after gluing, you get a common single rounded circle.

On the cylinder $U$, as on a surface in space, the distance between points is naturally given as the length of the shortest line along $U$ and connecting these points. Based on this, some geometry can also be developed on the cylinder. A thoughtful reader will project: will this geometry coincide with the geometry on the strip $P$ with the relationship between the edges? It turns out that it will. However, the substantiation of this fact (relates to the so-called differential geometry) is beyond the scope of our article.
