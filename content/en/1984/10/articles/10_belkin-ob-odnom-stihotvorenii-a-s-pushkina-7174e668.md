About one poem by A. S. Pushkin

Among the posetic treasures left by our great national poet A.S. Pushkin, there is a poem that directly relates to physics, or more precisely, to mechanics. This poem is called "Movement". It is small in volume - it has only 8 lines, but is very rich in content.

In the first four lines we read:

There is no movement, said the bearded sage.
The other remained silent and began to walk around with object.
He could not have objected more strongly;
Everyone praised the answer to plan.

Here the post talks about the legendary dispute between two ancient Greek philosophers. “The Bearded Sage” is Zeno of Elea; his opponent in the dispute is Diogenes of Sinope. The first argued that movement is impossible; the second began to silently “walk before him,” as if clearly showing the absurdity of such a statement.

But further A.S. Pushkin writes:

But, gentlemen, this is a funny case
Another example comes to mind:
After all, the sun moves every day, but Galileo is right.

With these words, Pozt shows that Diogenes’ proof is not at all as flawless as it seemed to those who praised “the answer to plan.” Indeed, the sun does every day what Diogenes did when marching before Zeno: it “walks before us.” In reality, as “stubborn Galileo” always asserted after Copernicus, the Sun will end, but the Earth moves (rotates around its axis). But Diogenes could not give any other evidence, so he “kept silent.”

Now the incorrectness of Zeno's statement can be proven without resorting to Diogenes' method. Let's try to do this. First of all, let’s try to imagine how Zeno came to the very absurd conclusion that “there is no movement.”

Zeno apparently reasoned as follows. The body, following a certain trajectory, at any moment of time can be here in another state of rest. In each such state of rest, the displacement of the body is naturally equal to zero. Adding up this continuous set of zeros, Zeno, of course, ended up with zero: “There is no movement”!

So, Zeno’s mistake lies precisely in the fact that he considered the speed of the body at each point of the trajectory to be equal to zero. In fact, at every moment of time the moving body has a speed - the so-called instantaneous speed v (“Physics 8”, § 11). And since it has speed, then in any suitably small period of time Δt the body makes a small movement Δs=vΔt. The fact is that in Newton’s time the word “density” could not be familiarized until it is familiarized now. After all, the concept of mass was introduced into physics by Newton. Before Newton there was no concept of mass, and there was no way to understand density as the mass of a unit volume. What do I associate the word “density” with in those days?

Newton believed that all bodies consist of completely identical particles, but in different substances they move differently (we learned about this from another of Newton’s books - “Optics”). The difference in the movements of particles determines the difference in all the properties of substances and bodies. The word “density” then had a literal meaning - it shows how tightly the particles adhere to each other, that is, how tightly they are “packed.” In the same “Optics” Newton mentions, for example, that water is 19 times lighter, and therefore 19 times rarer than gold, that in water there are many larger voids between particles than in gold. With this understanding, density simply means the number of particles per unit volume, and multiplied by the volume of the body it gives the total number of parts in the body. It is this quantity that Newton calls the quantity of materials (substance). Since the particles in all substances and bodies are the same and the masses of all parts in any substance are the same, the mass of a body is determined only by the number of parts in it, that is, the amount of substance in the body. If the masses of two bodies are the same, then the amount of matter in them is also the same.

In our magnitude, “amount of matter” has essentially the same meaning as in Newton’s. This is the number of parts contained in the body; those particles of which the body is composed. For a bar of iron, this is the number of iron atoms; for water in a vessel, this is the number of water molecules. The natural only measurement of the amount of matter would be 1 particle. But then the number expressing this magnitude would be unimaginably large. We agreed to take as a single quantity of a substance the quantity that contains only particles (atoms, molecules), as many atoms are contained in 0.012 kg of carbon isotope with atomic mass 12, that is, 6.02·10²³ particles (Avogadro’s number Nₐ). This unilua molle is called.

Although a mole of any substance contains the same number of particles, the mass of a mole of different substances is different. The mass M of a mole (molar mass) is equal to 10⁻³·M_r kg, where M_r is the relative molecular mass (“Physics 9”, § 2). A mole of iron, for example, has a mass of 0.056 kg, a mole of aluminum has a mass of 0.027 kg, etc. Thus, 5 kg of iron is approximately 90 moles of iron, and 5 kg of aluminum is approximately 185 moles. The masses are the same, but the amount of substance is different.

The housewife who bought a polygram of cheese in a store is, of course, not interested in the mass of this piece, but in the amount of cheese in it. However, there is still no urgent need to replace kilograms with moles, because the mass and amount of a substance (for a given substance) are proportional to each other, so that 0.5 kg of cheese is half as much as 1 kg. And measuring the number of kilograms is much easier than the number of moles.

In physics, however, mass and quantity of matter are not always interchangeable. Let's give one example.

Both experience and theory show that the product of the pressure p of an ideal gas and its volume V is proportional to the temperature T of the gas and its mass m:

pV~mT.

The proportionality coefficient in this correspondence is different for different gases. But if pV is proportional to m, then it is proportional to the number of parts in the substance:

pV~NT.

In this correspondence, the cofactor of proportionality turns out to be the same for all gases:

pV=kNT, (1)

where k=1.38·10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann’s constant. The amount of substance v (in moles) is related to the number of part N by a simple relationship: v=N/Nₐ. Therefore we can write

pV~vT.

The proportionality coefficient will now be different, but also the same for all gases:

pV=RvT. (2)

Here R=8.31 ​​J/(mol K) is the universal gas constant. This equation, like equation (1), is an ideal gas constant equation.
