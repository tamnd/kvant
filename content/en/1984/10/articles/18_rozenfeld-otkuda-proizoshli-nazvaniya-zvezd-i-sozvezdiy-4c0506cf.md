Where do the names of stars and constellations come from?

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
B. A. ROSENFELD

If you look at the starry sky, then, with some imagination, blame various figures for the scattering of more or less bright stars. These figures can be composed in various ways. The stars α and β of the constellation Gemini have their own names - Castor and Polux; they call it by the names of two mythical twins - the sons of Zeus and Leda, and α Hound Dogs - the Heart of Charles, named by E. Very few stars have Greek and Latin names, most of these names are of Arabic origin. This is explained by the fact that in the Middle Ages the center of advanced science was in the Near and Middle East, where the language of science was Arabic (as before in Hellenistic countries - Greek, and later in Europe - Latin). An important contribution to the science of that time was made by scientists from Central Asia and Azerbaijan: al-Khorezmi, al-Biruni, al-Fergani, Ibn Sina (Avicenna), Omar Khayyam, Nasir ad-Din at-Tusi, Ulugbek and scientists from his school in Samarkand; many important discoveries were also made by scientists from Iran, Iraq, Syria, Egypt, North-West Africa and Muslim Spain. The works of these scientists came to Western Europe through Spain and Italy, and then through Constantinople, and were converted into Latin. Europeans became acquainted with many works of ancient science first through their Arabic translations and only then with their Greek originals.

Most Arabic names arose in the following way. In the replaced work of the Alexandrian astronomer Claudius Polemaeus (2nd century AD), usually called by us “Almagest”, having a catalog of 1022 stars, the positions of which were changed by astronomers of that time. Europeans became acquainted with the “Almagest” through its Arabic translation, which explains its name: one of the Greek names of this work, “Megiste syntax” - “The Greatest System” - was transferred by the Arabs to the Almagest, which is where our name came from. Polemaius characterized each star with a small description indicating the place of this star in the constellation. It is from these descriptions in Arabic translation that our names come from. Some names, however, go back not to Polemaey, but to the Old Arbian names of stars. A table of the names of some stars is given on p. 39.

Note that the name Antharas is explained by the fact that this star, like Mars, is red in color and is, as it were, a substitute for Mars (our names of the planets are the names of the Roman gods, corresponding to the Greek gods Herms, Aphrodite, Ares, Zeus and Chronos, whose names were named by the Greeks).

The word “regulate” comes from the name of the star Regulus, since this star was used to regulate useful work in Ancient Egypt.

The names Mira and Proxima were given by scientists relatively recently: the name Mira was dado to this star for its amazing properties (this star is a long-period variable star), the name Proxima was given by scientists after it was discovered that this star is located close to the mass of stars in the Solar System.
