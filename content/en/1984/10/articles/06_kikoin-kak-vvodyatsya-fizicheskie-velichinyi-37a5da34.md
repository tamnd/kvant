How physical quantities are entered
Academician I. K. Kikoin

Physics differs from other natural sciences in that the object laws established in the study of physical natural phenomena are expressed quantitatively (mathematically). To do this, physicists introduce quantities that characterize the phenomenon or process being studied. Mathematical correspondences between quantities are established experimentally in the form of corresponding equations or formulas, which are called the physical laws of nature.

How are physical quantities introduced? For each physical quantity, the method of measurement must be indicated. More precisely, it is impossible to introduce physical quantities without indicating at least the fundamental method of measurement. Let us explain this with some examples. Let's start with the quantities used in mechanics.

1. Speed

The concept of speed has been introduced since time immemorial. And you can imagine how this was done. Perhaps some person who had a device for measuring time - this could be, for example, a sundial - observed the movement of caravans in the desert. He determined the distance the caravan travels in a certain period of time. perhaps he did this by counting the number of steps the camel took. A camel's stride is a measure of length. Corresponding, expressing a period of time and the distance traveled, he established a curious correspondence between them. It turns out that the ratio of the paths traveled by the caravan for any time intervals is equal to the ratio of these time intervals. In modern language, this means the following: if we denote the path traveled by a caravan during a period of time $t_1$ by $s_1$, and the path traveled by a caravan during a period of time $t_2$ by $s_2$, then $\frac{s_1}{s_2} = \frac{t_1}{t_2}$. From a number of experiments carried out under a variety of conditions, we can conclude that a body infinitely distant from all other bodies cannot move by acceleration, if we consider the movement relative to some well-defined reporting systems, which are called inertial reporting systems. Such a body is either at rest or moves uniformly and is motionless relative to the inertial reporting system. If a body moves with acceleration, then it is always possible to indicate another body or other bodies, the influence of which causes the accelerated movement of this body. In such cases, they say that bodies interact with each other. Let's consider the simplest case - when two bodies interact. Experience shows that in this case both bodies receive accelerations whose directions are mutually opposite. As for the accelerated model of both bodies, they can be different. But the ratio of the accelerated model to both bodies remains constant, regardless of the conditions under which the interaction occurs. Thus, it does not depend on the relative position of these bodies in space, does not depend on time, does not depend on the speed of both bodies, does not depend on the environment, etc. This relationship depends only on the properties of the interacting bodies themselves.

In physics, only those properties of a body are studied that can be expressed by numbers, and to study certain properties in physics, physical quantities are introduced that characterize these properties. The greater the acceleration of a body, the greater, obviously, the change in its speed over a given period of time. And vice versa, if the acceleration of a body is small, this means that over the same period of time its speed changes little. Thus, in mechanics, three types of forces are usually studied: the power of universal gravitation, the power of elasticity and the power of friction.

If any of these forces applied to a body of mass $m$ causes its acceleration equal to $\vec{a}$, then the value of this force is always equal to $m\vec{a}$. In other words, if a body of mass $m$ needs to be given an acceleration equal to $\vec{a}$, then a force $\vec{F}$ equal to $\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$ needs to be applied to this body (it is clear that the direction of the applied force $\vec{F}$ must coincide with the direction of the acceleration $\vec{a}$ that needs to be imparted to the body). In this case, the force $\vec{F}$ can be a gravitational force (the force of universal gravitation), an elastic force, a friction force, or, finally, a geometric sum of these forces. This is the meaning of Newton's second law.

Let's try to find out what the latter is the basis for the formation of Newton's second law. Let's imagine two materials points $A$ and $B$ interacting with each other, that is, imparting acceleration to each other. Experience shows that the directions of these accelerations are mutually opposite.

Obviously, both interacting point materials are “equal” in the sense that their designations can be mutually changed. In other words, none of the interacting points has any advantage over the other. It follows that there must be physical quantities that are the same for both interacting point materials. It is not difficult to find such a value. From correspondence (2) it follows:

$$\frac{m_A}{m_B} = \frac{a_B}{a_A},$$

where $m_A$ and $m_B$ are the masses of the interacting materials of the points, $a_A$ and $a_B$ are the models of their accelerations. Hence,

$$m_A a_A = m_B a_B.$$ (5)

Considering that acceleration is a vector quantity, we rewrite correspondence (5) in the form

$$m_A \vec{a}_A = -m_B \vec{a}_B.$$

Denoting $m_A \vec{a}_A = \vec{F}_A$ and $m_B \vec{a}_B = \vec{F}_B$, we get

$$\vec{F}_A = -\vec{F}_B.$$

The vector quantities $\vec{F}_A$ and $\vec{F}_B$ Newton are called forces.
