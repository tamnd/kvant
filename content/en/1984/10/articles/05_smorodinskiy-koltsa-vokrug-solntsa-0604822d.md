Rings of the Sun

When Galileo first pointed a telescope at the sky in 1610, he, in particular, discovered a somewhat strange appearance of the planet Sun. It seemed to consist of three stars, as if touching each other, and the middle star looked about three times larger than the side stars (the “highest* planet of triple observation”). A little over fifty years later, Heugens, with the help of a more powerful telescope, explained the uneducated appearance of the Sun by the fact that it, the only one in the sky, wears a beautiful decoration - rings (at a lower magnification, the rings appeared as side stars). The first theoretical study of the rings of the Sun belongs to Maxwell. In 1859, he completed a paper on the stability of rings, in which he proved that the rings are not continuous, but consist of many individual particles. This was Maxwell's first work to receive a prize from the Royal Society of London.

Only recently did Saturn lose its monopoly on rings. In 1977, the rings of Uranus were discovered (discovered by the eclipse of the brightness of stars that appeared behind the rings for an earthly observer), and in 1979, the rings of Jupiter (invisible from Earth.*) were photographed from space. Now the rings of the planets of the Solar System have ceased to be a collectible rarity, and their existence has become almost common. True, we must admit that the laws of motion of small particles and large fragments that make up the rings are fraught with many mysteries.

*) During the time of Galileo, Saturn was considered the most distant planet from the Sun (Uranus, Neptune and Pluo were discovered later).
