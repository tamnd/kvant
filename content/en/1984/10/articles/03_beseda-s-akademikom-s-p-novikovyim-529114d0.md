Conversation
with an academician
S. P. Novikov

Of the talented generation of mathematicians who entered science in the late 50s and early 60s, the scientific successes of Sergei Petrovich Novikov are perhaps among the most impressive. Starting with one of the most difficult and abstract areas of mathematics - modern algebraic topology, S. P. Novikov completed his candidate's thesis at the age of 25, his doctorate at 27, was elected a corresponding member of the Academy of Sciences at 28 and an academician at 43. In 1967, Sergei Petrovich received the Lenin Prize, and in 1970, at the World Mathematics Competition, S.P. Novikov became the first Soviet winner of the Fields Prize, awarded every four years to the world's leading mathematicians (under the age of 40). Now Sergei Petrovich combines work at the Institute of Theoretical Physics. L.D. Landu (his work is related to mathematical physics, in particular, to the theory of solitotes) and at the Mathematical Institute. V. A. Steklova with the head of the department of higher geometry and topology at Moscow University.

Sergei Petrovich, a member of the editorial board of the magazine «Kvant», answered questions from our correspondent A.V. Sosinsky at his home. The conversation took place in a small office, entirely filled with books (not only on mathematics!), lively and relaxed. We will reproduce here (virtually without editorial changes) a tape recording of this conversation, only adding in the form of a somewhat nuanced way to Sergei Petrovich’s answers.

— Sergei Petrovich, when did you always become interested in mathematics? What will influence this choice more: school, competitions, family?

— As you know, I come from a mathematical family*). I would, of course, put the influence of family first. It cannot be said that I seriously became interested in mathematics at school; but anyway I'm a lot

*) Father Sergei Petrovich, academician P. S. Novikov (1901-1975), - an outstanding specialist in function and set theory, algebra and mathematical logic; mother, L.V. Kelysh (1904-1975), Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, a well-known specialist in set theory and geometric topology.

— Have you participated in the Olympics?
— I took part in the Olympiads in the lower grades - in grades 5, 6, they were then eliminated in Moscow by the Institute of Conditional University. I also took part in the Olympiad organized at Moscow University, but having received second prize in the 7th grade (in 1952), I lost interest in it. Although later I went to clubs and competitions, I was not active and did not show brilliant results.

— Sergei Petrovich, were you a diligent student?
— I studied decently: without C grades. But he was not an excellent student.
— How did you choose your future scientific specialty within mathematics itself at the university?
— In our time, the international*) had a very reasonable system for providing coursework topics already in the second year. Then, by the time they were assigned to departments (at the beginning of the third year), the students were scientifically prepared. We all served in special courses and participated in the work of specialists, even “semi-creative” ones, already in our first year. The mathematical education that existed at that time was well prepared for the choice of a scientific specialty. In this case, an important role, for example, for me, was played by the desire to occupy myself with some completely different range of problems, not those that were mainly known for generations. I wanted to occupy myself not with set theory and the theory of functions of a real variable, not with general algebra and mathematical logic, but with some completely different areas. This is how I chose algebraic topology for myself. A significant role was played by the fact that the brilliant professor, soon laureate Lenin*

*) Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics of Moscow State University. M.V. Lomonosov, where S.P. Novikov was a student in 1955-1960.

— We often hear that in science, including mathematics, narrow specialization prevails today. At the same time, such mathematicians of the 20th century as Poincaré, Hilbert®), Bourbaki are spoken of as “universals”. What is the current situation in mathematics in this regard?

— There is some strange mistake in your question. A mathematician named Bourbaki**) does not exist in any way, and the talented scientists who made it up, and even the most prominent among them, were not half-universal. Poincare and Hilbert. Personally, I especially like the figure of Poincaré, who is closest to the natural sciences.

It seems to me that the narrow specialization of many mathematicians is associated with an overly abstract and over-conventional language. I would say that this narrowness is also due to the weakening of the connection between theoretical mathematics and applications, which took place at a certain stage. We have only provided a clear criterion of what is absolutely necessary to know. Everyone knows only what he wants, or what his supervisor ordered him to read. Until the natural-beginning character of mathematical education is restored, this universalism cannot be revived.

— Mathematicians are not given Nobel Prizes. Is this fair?

“I don’t believe in the famous jokes that mathematicians tell about this. Upon completion of the Nobel Prize, prizes are also not given to physics-theoretical or biological scientists. The formulation of its completion is completely clear. Prizes established by A. Nobel, the inventor of dynamite, are awarded for works such as inventions that are directly useful to mankind. Biology receives a Nobel Prize for research that is essentially medical, there is actually no Nobel Prize for biology. When theoretical physicists receive a Nobel Prize, there is some confusion that has been added to them over the decades. The testament is that prizes are awarded for inventions, but not for scientific discoveries. If the Nobel Prize was not given to biology and theoretical physics, why should it go to mathematics? I would recommend that mathematicians not discover that the engineer A. Nobel did not remember mathematics in his will. It doesn't make her any worse.

— Do you have service hobbies other than science? Hobby, argument, access?

— For us, scientists, sport exists, as we believe, to maintain health and harm. I can tell you something new about this.

And if we talk about intellectual hobbies, for some time I have been using historical books, but primarily not books written by historians, but original books written by active people themselves, ancient and modern (unfortunately, only in Russian or English). Because I loved to understand how they lived and acted, how they differed from us, how they were similar to us.

For me, reading the history of historical books is simply my favorite reading; I would not, however, advise any mathematician to pursue hobbies of this kind with the activities of real professionals.

— What can you advise those of our readers who are always interested in physics and mathematics?

— A person who wants to become a scientist faces two tasks. The first of them is to learn creative work and make at least small scientific discoveries in a certain specialization, at a competent scientific level. The second is not to tire you in the narrow area with which you (perhaps accidentally) started. These two problems must be solved harmoniously. However, one of them requires the education and training of creative skills, the other requires the expenditure of enormous labor to acquire an education, although this work may seem at first glance useless, based on synonymous interests. The combination of these two components of the creative process is what a scientist needs for the literary period.
