⟦folio 8⟧

Perhaps, in time immemorial, the speed is changed by the body over time intervals $t_1$ and $t_2$, respectively. This is, for example, the motion of bodies freely falling in a vacuum, or the motion of visual bodies along an inclined plane without friction.

Such movements are studied by the Italian physicist Galileo. He wrote down equation (1) in the form $\frac{2s_1}{t_1^2} = \frac{2s_2}{t_2^*}$ and called these relations and the relation $\frac{2s}{t^2}$ in general the acceleration of the body (material point). The concept of acceleration is obviously connected with the fact that the very speed of movement of a body can change over time. If the speed is proportional to time, that is, $v = v_0 + at$, then $a = \frac{v - v_0}{t}$, where $(v - v_0)$ is the change in speed over a period of time $t$.

Thus, a new physical quantity was introduced - acceleration. The well-known formula $s = \frac{at^2}{2}$ (it is assumed that the initial velocity of the body is zero) also does not express any law of nature, but is a consequence of the accepted definition of acceleration.

Later, a clarification was introduced: to correctly describe various types of motion, it is necessary to take into account that the displacement $s$, speed $v$ and acceleration $a$ are vector quantities.

2. Acceleration

There are also movements in which the ratio of the paths traveled over any time intervals is equal to the ratio of the squares of these time intervals. In modern language this is written as the following equality:

$$\frac{s_1}{s_2} = \frac{t_1^2}{t_2^2},$$

where $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the distances covered by the moving body during time intervals $t_1$ and $t_2$, respectively. This is, for example, the movement of bodies freely falling into a vacuum, or the movement of bodies along an inclined plane without friction.
