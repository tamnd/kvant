$$F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

(Newton's law of universal gravitation). In particular, in the conditions of the Earth, the glory of universal gravity manifests itself in the form of a gravitational force acting on any body and directed towards the center of the Earth.

Electrostatic force is given by the formula

$$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

(Coulomb's law). In electric train mechanics, the forces characterizing the interaction between the charged particles that make up the body manifest themselves in the form of elasticity and frictional forces. The elastic force occurs when a body is deformed, that is, when the relative position of a part of the body changes, for example, when stretching or sagging a spring. Friction force arises, as is known, when bodies in contact move relative to each other.

Thus, in mechanics, three types of forces are usually studied: the power of universal gravitation, the power of elasticity and the power of friction.

If any of these forces applied to a body of mass $m$ causes its acceleration equal to $\vec{a}$, then the value of this force is always equal to $m\vec{a}$. In other words, if a body of mass $m$ needs to be given an acceleration equal to $\vec{a}$, then a force $\vec{F}$ equal to $\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$ needs to be applied to this body (it is clear that the direction of the applied force $\vec{F}$ must coincide with the direction of the acceleration $\vec{a}$ that needs to be imparted to the body). In this case, the force $\vec{F}$ can be a gravitational force (the force of universal gravitation), an elastic force, a friction force, or, finally, a geometric sum of these forces. This is the meaning of Newton's second law.

Let's try to find out what the latter is the basis for the formation of Newton's second law. Let's imagine two materials points $A$ and $B$ interacting with each other, that is, imparting acceleration to each other. Experience shows that the directions of these accelerations are mutually opposite.

Obviously, both interacting point materials are “equal” in the sense that their designations can be mutually changed. In other words, none of the interacting points has any advantage over the other. It follows that there must be physical quantities that are the same for both interacting point materials. It is not difficult to find such a value. From correspondence (2) it follows:

$$\frac{m_A}{m_B} = \frac{a_B}{a_A},$$

where $m_A$ and $m_B$ are the masses of the interacting materials of the points, $a_A$ and $a_B$ are the models of their accelerations. Hence,

$$m_A a_A = m_B a_B.$$ (5)

So we found values ​​that are the same for both interacting calves. This value characterizes the mutual influence of the materials of the points on each other, causing their acceleration. The existence of such a quantity is the basis for the formation of Newton’s second law.

Considering that acceleration is a vector quantity, we rewrite correspondence (5) in the form

$$m_A \vec{a}_A = -m_B \vec{a}_B.$$

Denoting $m_A \vec{a}_A = \vec{F}_A$ and $m_B \vec{a}_B = \vec{F}_B$, we get

$$\vec{F}_A = -\vec{F}_B.$$

The vector quantities $\vec{F}_A$ and $\vec{F}_B$ Newton are called forces. As you can see, with which the material point $A$ acts on the material point $B$, is equal in modulo, but opposite in direction to the force with which the material point $B$ acts on the material point $A$.
