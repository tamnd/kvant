6) The inequality $S_n \leqslant 3n$ can be proven by induction on $n$.

For $n=3$, consider two cases: 1) all numbers are equal, 2) among the numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3$ there are different ones. In the first case, all terms in (*) are equal to 2 and $S_3=6<3\cdot 3$. In the second case, all the most of the numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3$ (empty will be $a_1$) are strictly greater than the other two ($a_1>(a_2+a_3)/2$). Then the number $(a_2+a_3)/a_1$ is less than 2, and since this number is a natural number, $a_1=a_2+a_3$. Hence,

$$S_3 = \frac{a_2+a_3}{a_1} + \frac{a_3+a_1}{a_2} + \frac{a_1+a_2}{a_3} = 3 + \frac{2a_3}{a_2} + \frac{2a_2}{a_3},$$

and the numbers $k=2a_3/a_2=(a_1+a_3)/a_2-1$ and $l=2a_2/a_3$ are natural numbers. Considering that $kl=4$, we get the following options: $k=1, l=4$ - then $a_2=2a_3$ and $S_3=8<9$, or $k=2, l=2$ - then $a_3=a_2, S_3=7<9$ or $k=4, l=1$ and again $S_3=8<9$.

Let us now assume that our inequality is true for $n-1$ numbers and prove that $S_n<3n$. If all numbers $a_i$ are equal, then $S_n=2n<3n$. Otherwise, the most of the numbers $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ is possible (if there are several most of the numbers, choose the one that is strictly greater than at least one of its neighbors). Let this be the number $a_n$, then $a_n>(a_{n-1}+a_1)/2$. As above, what is missing is $a_n=a_{n-1}+a_1$, i.e.

$$\frac{a_{n-2}+a_n}{a_{n-1}} = 1 + \frac{a_{n-2}+a_1}{a_{n-1}}, \frac{a_n+a_2}{a_1} = 1 + \frac{a_{n-1}+a_2}{a_1},$$

where $(a_{n-2}+a_1)/a_{n-1}$ and $(a_{n-1}+a_2)/a_1$ are natural numbers. Thus, the set $a_1, \ldots, a_{n-1}$ satisfies the conditions of the problem and the sum $S_{n-1}<3(n-1)$ is considered for it. Therefore, $S_n=S_{n-1}+3<3n$.

From the above it is clear that when adding the number $a_{n+1}=a_1+a_n$ to the set $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ (between $a_1$ and $a_n$), the sum $S_n$ increases by 3. From here, by induction, it is easily deduced that the sum $S_n$ can take any integer value $S$ from the interval $2n \leqslant S < 3n$.
