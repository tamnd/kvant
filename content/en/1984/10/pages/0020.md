The principle is relative
relative

Kakidat of physical and mathematical sciences
A. A. DOZOROV

On a natural train, looking out the window at the train that had just started, a five-year-old boy asked:
- Mom, who went, us or that train?
“Train,” my mother answered.
- Why does it seem to me that we went?

Mom once answered, not paying attention to the fact that her son was formulating one of the fundamental questions of physics.

One of the first to formulate a similar question and answer it was the great Italian scientist Galileo Galilei. This happened in 1636, that is, approximately 350 years ago. Here is Galileo's reasoning regarding the so-called principle of the relative, as set out in his book “Dialogue on the two most important systems of the world - Polemaic and Copernican”:

“Seclude yourself with one of your friends in a spacious room under the deck of some ship, stock up on flour, butterflies and other similar small flying insects; Let you also have a large vessel there with water and small fish swimming in it; Next, hang a bucket at the top, from which water will fall drop by drop into another vessel with a narrow neck placed below. While the ship is standing still, watch diligently how small flying animals move at the same speed in all directions of the room; the fish, as you will see, will swim indifferently in all directions; all the falling drops will fall into the substituted vessel, and when you throw an object, you will not have to throw it with greater force in one direction than in the other if the distances are the same; and if you jump with both feet at once, you will jump the same distance in any direction. Observe all this carefully, although you will not have any doubt that if you show the stop repeatedly, everything should happen exactly like this. Now make the ship move at any speed, and then (if only the movement is uniform and without rocking in one direction or the other) in all the named phenomena you will not find the slightest change and from none of them you will be able to determine whether the ship is moving or standing still... And the reason for the consistency of all these phenomena is that the movement of the ship is common to all objects on it, as well as to the air.”

Isaac Newton in his book “Mathematical Sciences of Natural Physiophy”, published in 1687, formulated the principle of the relative: “The relative motions relative to each other of bodies contained in any space are the same, whether this space is at rest or moves uniformly and rectilinearly without rotation.”

In its modern formulation, Galileo's relative principle usually reads as follows: the laws of mechanics are the same in all inertial systems of absence.

This principle was not subject to any revision for a very long time. Only in 1905, in his landmark works on the special theory of the relative, Albert Einstein generalized the mechanical principle of the relative, applying it to all physical phenomena: “The laws according to which the states of physical systems change do not depend on which of the two coordinate systems, moving uniformly and rectilinearly relative to each other, these changes of state are related to.”

Thus, no experiments carried out in any inertial system make it possible to say whether this system is moving rectilinearly and uniformly or is at rest.
