Since we are interested in the linear expansion of the rod, we can assume that

$$\frac{1}{1+\alpha t_i} = \frac{1-\alpha t_i}{1-\alpha^2 t_i^2} \approx 1-\alpha t_i.$$

Then

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{1+\alpha t_i} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} (1-\alpha t_i) = n-\alpha \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( t_1 + \frac{t_2-t_1}{n} i \right) =$$

$$= n-\alpha \left( nt_1 + \frac{t_2-t_1}{n} \cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \right) \approx n \left( 1-\alpha \frac{t_1+t_2}{2} \right)$$

(we took into account that $n \gg 1$). Thus,

$$l_0 = \frac{l}{n} n \left( 1-\alpha \frac{t_1+t_2}{2} \right),$$

where

$$l = \frac{l_0}{1-\alpha \frac{t_1+t_2}{2}} \approx l_0 \left( 1+\alpha \frac{t_1+t_2}{2} \right).$$

L. G. Markovich

F886. Along two parallel metal guides, inclined at an angle $\alpha$ to the horizon and located at a distance $b$ from each other, the mass bridge $m$ can slide without metal friction. The guides are closed to an independent capacitor emkocti $C$, and the entire structure is in a magnetic field, the induction $B$ of which is directed vertically (see figure). At the initial moment, the jumper is held at a distance $l$ from the base of the “torque”. How long will it take the jumper to reach the base of the torque after it is released? What speed will it have at the base? The resistance of the conductors is pretent.

When the jumper moves, the magnetic flux changes, producing a circuit “closed” by the jumper. As a result, an induced emf will arise in the circuit.

During a short period of time $\Delta t$, when the instantaneous speed $v$ of the jumper can be considered unchanged, the instantaneous value of the induced emf is equal to

$$\delta_i = \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t} = vbB \cos \alpha.$$

So, the current through the jumper at this time is equal to $i = \Delta q/\Delta t$, where $\Delta q$ is the charge accumulated on the capacitor during the time $\Delta t$, that is

$$\Delta q = C \cdot \Delta \delta_i = CbB(\cos \alpha) \cdot \Delta v$$

(since there is no resistance of the lead wires, the instantaneous value of the voltage across the capacitor is $\delta_i$). So,

$$i = CbB \cos \alpha \cdot \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = CbBa \cos \alpha,$$

where $\alpha$ is the acceleration with which the jumper moves.

The jumper is acted upon by gravity and the Ampere force. Let us write down the equation of motion of the jumper:

$$ma = mg \sin \alpha - ibB \cos \alpha = mg \sin \alpha - Cb^2B^2a \cos^2 \alpha.$$

From here we find $\alpha$:

$$a = \frac{mg \sin \alpha}{m + Cb^2B^2 \cos^2 \alpha}.$$

The time it takes for the jumper to descend to the base of the slide is determined from the condition $l = \frac{at^2}{2}$:

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{mg \sin \alpha}} \left( m + Cb^2B^2 \cos^2 \alpha \right).$$

Jumper speed at base -

$$v_i = at = \sqrt{\frac{2lmg \sin \alpha}{m + Cb^2B^2 \cos^2 \alpha}}.$$

A. I. Buzdin
