"Law of Odd Numbers"
for free fall of the body

The motion of bodies freely falling to the Earth is, as is known, motion with constant acceleration $g = 9,81 \text{ m/c}^2$. If the body ends relative to the Earth at the initial moment, the law of free fall is mathematically expressed by the equation

$$h = \frac{gt^2}{2},$$

(1)

where $h$ is the movement of the body (vertically), $t$ is the time missing from the beginning of the fall.

It turns out that formula (1) leads to one curious consequence, which is usually not mentioned in physics textbooks, but which Galileo, who discovered the law of free fall, knew about: It in itself can be considered a law, and it can be used when solving specific problems.

What kind of consequence are we talking about?

Formula (1), obviously, allows one to calculate the displacement of a body for any time, including a time of $t = n\Delta t$ seconds (here $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, \Delta t = 1$ s). Moreover, using the same formula, you can determine the displacement not only for all $n$ seconds, but also for each of the $n$ seconds - for the period, the second, etc.

In fact, if in $n$ seconds the movement $h_n$ is free, and in $(n-1)$ seconds - $h_{n-1}$, then in $n$ seconds (here $n$) the displacement $h(n)$ is naturally equal to the difference of $h_n$ and $h_{n-1}$ (here $n$ — number of seconds):

$$h(n) = h_n - h_{n-1} = \frac{g(n\Delta t)^2}{2} - \frac{g((n-1)\Delta t)^2}{2} = \frac{g}{2}((n\Delta t)^2 - ((n-1)\Delta t)^2) = \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2}(2n-1).$$

From formula (2) it is clear that the displacement $h(1)$ during the first seconds ($n=1$) is equal to

$$h(1) = \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2}.$$

Similarly, we can find that in the next second the body moves:

$$h(2) = 3 \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2}, \quad h(3) = \dot{5} \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2},$$

$$h(4) = 7 \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2}, \quad h(5) = 9 \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2},$$

$$h(6) = 11 \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2}, \dots, \quad h(n) = (2n-1) \frac{g(\Delta t)^2}{2}.$$

Thus, movements in successive equal intervals of time of 1 second are related to each other as 1:3:5:7:9:11:...:(2n-1), that is, as odd numbers (note that the value $(2n-1)$ for any value $n$ is an odd number).

It is interesting that Galileo formulated the law of free fall of a body in precisely this form. In the words of Galileo himself: “The spaces traversed by a falling body in equal intervals of time are related to each other by successive odd numbers.”

One should not think that the “law of odd numbers” applies exclusively to the case of a freely falling body. It is, of course, valid for any equilibrium motion without initial velocity. After all, this pattern is a direct consequence of the fact that the displacement of a body during such movement is proportional to the square of time. One should also not think that this “law” is true only when the time intervals for which the movements are calculated are equal to one second. It is, of course, true for any equal periods of time, as noted by Galileo.

In conclusion, we invite readers to find out for themselves what the “law of odd numbers” looks like in the case of equilibrium motion.
