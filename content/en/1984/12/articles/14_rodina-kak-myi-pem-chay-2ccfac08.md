How we drink tea
(or about how to solve qualitative problems in physics)
Doctor of Pedagogical Sciences
H. A. MOTHERLAND

In one of the episodes of the television program “What? Where? When?" The “experts” were asked a question (we present it in a slightly clarified form):

“Dear experts! There is a glass of tea in front of you. Take a sip and answer the question: why does tea rise up, while water usually flows down?”

The answer that the experts settled on after a minute of service was this: “we suck in the water.” It was found correct, and the experts received a point and a reward - a book.

What would you answer if you were asked such a question not in a television program, but in a physics lesson? After all, this question is a real qualitative physical problem (this is the conventional name for problems in which there is no numerical data and the solution of which does not necessarily have to be expressed in the form of a formula or number). But before we think about the answer, let’s agree on what we will consider the answer. To solve a problem in physics qualitatively means to give an answer to its question, justified by the law and rules of physical science, expressed in pleasant terms, presented logically, so that each new stage of reasoning is a consequence of the previous one. You will probably agree that from this point of view, the answer given by the expert to the “tea problem” cannot be considered satisfactory.

So how to solve the problem? Let's listen to how two sixth-graders do it - Sasha and Alyosha.

Sasha. It's all about atmospheric pressure!

(I must say that Sasha preferred to answer quick questions.)

Alyosha. Wait, we need to start from the very beginning - with the condition: a motionless body - tea in a glass - began to move. We know that the phenomenon of inertia exists - if no other bodies act on the body, no force acts, then the body is at rest or moves uniformly and in a straight line. The body cannot change this state on its own, and the tea cannot begin to move without the action of a force.

Sasha. But all bodies are affected by the force of gravity, and it also affects tea. It doesn't pour, it doesn't fall, because the glass has a bottom.

Alyosha. This explanation - the glass has a bottom - can be given by a small child. From a physics point of view, this is not an explanation. Anyone who has studied physics will say: under the influence of weight, the tea will sag and an upward elastic force will arise. It acts on the tea and balances the force of gravity acting on it.

We explained why the tea initially does not move relative to the glass. But our problem is not about the movement of the whole body - water in a glass - as a whole, but about the movement of parts of water

Sasha. This is worse for communicating vessels. When the pressure of the columns of liquid in the vessels is not the same, then one column goes down and the other rises. In our problem, one column of tea is the one located near the mouth, and the second is all the rest of the tea. Until we drink tea, the pressure of these columns of water is the same, but as soon as...

Alyosha. It is necessary to explain why those two so many teas that you spoke about produce the same pressure on each other.

Sasha. We will prove this later, and now we will continue solving the problem. So, we are given: the tea in the glass was motionless, and when the person touched the glass with his lips, the tea began to rise.

Alyosha. Firstly, in physics there are more precise terms, and it must be said this way: the tea was at rest relative to the glass; and secondly, it is necessary to take into account all the features of the phenomenon. You didn’t pay attention to the fact that if the glass is not full, you have to tilt it until your lips touch the tea. Then the person takes a breath - and only then does the tea begin to rise.

I propose this, in my opinion, a clear formulation of the task.

Given: there is tea in a glass; it will accumulate relative to the glass. The person leans towards the glass or tilts it so that the tea and lips touch. Then he inhales, and the tea rises at this point and enters the person’s mouth. The rest of the tea is omitted. Problem question: why does that part of the tea that touches a person’s lip rise up?

Sasha. But all this was not in the conditions of the task!

Alyosha. Was. You just need to imagine the phenomenon that is mentioned in the conditions. And after this, you need to “introduce” those data that may not be mentioned, but which can be obtained from the observed or natural problem. In my opinion, the correct and completely clear conditions of the problem are already the beginning of the solution.

Sasha. Then let's continue with the solution. A person takes a breath, that is, draws in air,....

Alyosha. Wait. What does it mean to draw in air? A Bodox person does this: by tightening his muscles, he expands his chest, the volume of his lungs increases, and the air in them expands. As a result, the density of the air in the lungs and in the position communicating with it decreases, and its pressure becomes less than the pressure of the outside air. The air will be mixed from a place of higher pressure, that is, outside, to an area of ​​lower pressure, that is, into the lungs.

Sasha. And since a person, while inhaling, touches the surface of the tea with his lips, and touches it tightly, this is what will happen: atmospheric pressure acts on the entire surface of the tea, and on the surface adjacent to the person’s lips, the air pressure in the position of the mouth acts, and it is less than atmospheric pressure. Everything is clear! I told you that the reason for everything is atmospheric pressure! Again you say: wait?

Alyosha. I'll tell you. Why does atmospheric pressure, which we know acts on tea from top to bottom, cause tea to rise up?

Sasha. Because liquids and gases transmit pressure in all directions without change. This is Pascal's law.

Alyosha. In my opinion, we can already formulate the final solution to the problem.
