Classical theory

In modern physics, classical are called theories created by the classics of science of past centuries, before the emergence of quantum physics and the theory of the relative part. Most often, the word “classical” is used by physicists as the opposite of the word “quantum”.

The classical theory is different in that it does not involve quantum. Such theories are very simple; to modern physicists they seem somewhat naive. They often bring great benefit, but, as a rule, they do not fully resolve the issue. In classical theory, there is always something missing for a modern physicist.

In many books, including textbooks (and even very well), it is written that the electrical resistance of a metal comes from the collision of electrons carrying current with atoms*) of the crystal lattice. Is this understanding of resistance correct?

It is best not to say either “yes” or “no”, but to say that it expresses the classical theory of electrical resistance. Everything that we just said about classical theories in general applies to it. But in this case, the classical theory turns out to be very weak: it cannot explain many of the most basic features of electrical resistance.

In classical theory, one thing is certainly true: resistance comes from the fact that electrons transfer part of their energy and momentum (momentum) to the crystal lattice. But exactly how this transfer will occur is not a simple and very interesting question.

Temperature dependence

The resistance of pure metals increases greatly with temperature. For many of them it is approximately proportional to the absolute temperature. At low temperatures this simple relationship is broken. This is clearly visible in figure 1, which shows how the resistivity of copper, the most common conductor of electric current, changes with temperature. As can be seen from figure, in the region of high temperatures the dependence of good appears to be straight linear. But if this straight line is continued towards low temperatures (shown with a dotted line in figure), it does not go to absolute zero, but to a slightly higher temperature - for copper about 60 K. In fact, at low temperatures the dependence becomes more complex: the resistance changes in proportion to the fifth power of temperature. As absolute zero approaches, the resistance of pure metals becomes very small. There is a group of metals in which resistance completely disappears at a temperature several degrees above absolute zero. We will not touch upon this phenomenon, called superconductivity, now. Quite a lot of interesting things can be said about simpler things.

If we explain the electrical resistance by collisions of electrons with atoms, then the temperature dependence of the resistance of pure metals remains completely incomprehensible. After all, the situation is as if the electrons collided only with atoms that had undergone thermal motion, but with free flights of mismo stationary ones. They say so (as if it is easier for an electron to get into a moving atom), but this is a mistake. This is the same as thinking that when shooting without aiming, it is easier to hit a swinging magnet than a stationary one. So, the classical theory is not able to explain the dependence of resistance on temperature.

Residual resistance

When the resistance of pure metals is changed at very low temperatures, a remarkable thing is discovered. Continuing the curve figure 1 to absolute zero, we get the resistance that should remain if it were possible to reach absolute zero in the experiment. It is called residual resistance. It turns out that it varies greatly from sample to sample. The remaining resistance is extremely sensitive to low impurities, mechanical and thermal treatment. By carefully cleaning the metal, shaping it so as to achieve a perfect crystalline structure, the residual resistance can be reduced, and there is no limit to this decrease. So, experience leads to an amazing conclusion: an ideal crystal at a temperature of absolute zero should have no electrical resistance. In other words: the electrical resistance of pure metals comes only from disturbances in the crystalline structure, which are caused by thermal movement, impurities and defects (irregularities) of the crystalline solution.

Miracles without miracles

Even more amazing facts have long been known. Mechanical and thermal treatment significantly affect the electronic resistance of metals. For ease of use in technology, we will use the single resistivity Ohm·mm²/m=10⁻⁴ Ohm·cm=10⁻⁶ Ohm·m. In these units, the resistivity of total commercial copper at 20 °C is expressed as 0.0172. After cold drawing, the resistance of copper wiring increases to 0.0177. Further winding the wire around the coil is enough to increase its resistance. If you subject the wiring to annealing, that is,

prolonged heating, and then cool it again to 20 °C, the resistance value returns to normal. Obviously, resistance is felt by himself to slight disturbances in the crystal structure (such properties are called structure sensitive). Another remarkable thing is the sensitivity of the resistance to insignificant impurities. Thorough cleaning reduces the copper resistance at 20°C to 0.0169 ohm mm²/m. This circumstance is of great importance for technology. After all, if you reduce the resistance of the wires, then the useless losses of electrical grains due to their heating are also reduced. Therefore, copper intended for electronic purposes is subject to special purification through electronism. This peculiar metallurgical process is somewhat filled with “pouring from empty to empty.” At the anode, copper is dissolved, and copper is deposited at the cathode, the purity of which varies by “three nines” after the decimal point: 99.999%, that is, impurities in electronic copper account for only a thousandth of a percent.

Let's now see how the amount of impurities affects the resistance of copper. It is enough to add 1% manganese to copper so that its resistivity increases to 0.048 Ohm mm²/m, that is, almost three times! Meanwhile, the resistance of pure manganese is 0.05 ohm mm²/m. Thus, 1% manganese in copper is enough for its resistance to become that of 100% manganese! This is not solely a matter of chance. About the same

From the point of view of classical theory, the exorbitant effect of small impurities is a pure miracle.

In alloys containing large proportions of impurities, the resistance is very high. Metallurgists have developed remarkable sieves of high-resistance alloys: nickel, magganin, constantan, nichrome and others. The resistance of these alloys is several times greater than that of each of the component parts. Thus, constantan, consisting of 60% copper and 40% nickel, has a resistivity of 0.44 Ohm mm²/m, while for pure copper it is 0.017, and for nickel 0.072 of the same units *).

The “king” of such alloys can be called the well-known nichrome, the resistivity of which is about 1 Ohm mm²/m. It is not for nothing that it has found such wide application in heating devices.

It is striking that the temperature dependence of the resistance of alloys is completely different than that of pure metals. The resistance of most alloys also increases with temperature, but much less. Especially for scientific instruments, where it is desirable to have a constant resistance, the already mentioned constantan was developed, the name of which means “constant”. In the temperature range from 0 ° to 400 ° C, its resistance changes only from 0.441 to 0.448 Ohm mm²/m.

From the point of view of classical theory, the constancy of the resistance of alloys is as little understood as the proportional resistance to temperature for pure metals. The resistance of an alloy should, within the meaning of this theory, be added up from the resistance of its constituent parts according to simple mixing rules (such as heat specificity). If we carefully consider everything that is known about electrical resistance, it will become clear that even this simple phenomenon forces us to turn to quantum for help for its concept.

Electron waves and atomic waveguides

Everything falls into place if we remember that, according to the laws of quantum mechanics, an electron has simultaneous properties of a particle and a wave. The width of atomic corridors is close to the wavelength of the electron, and therefore, when moving along such corridors, the wave properties of the electron are fully manifested.

Waveguides are widely used in S-wave radio engineering. These are metal tubes of round or rectangular cross-section. Radio waves propagate along the waveguide, following all its bends. Light is also electromagnetic waves, and it can be hidden in a light guide - a tube with mirror walls *).

The effect of a light guide with mirror walls is easy to understand. Light waves are constantly reflected from the walls and thus remain inside the light guide. Radio waves are also reflected well from smooth metal surfaces. If the surface is rough, then the wave seems to break against the jagged edges and, as a result, is scattered and absorbed. Its energy turns into heat.

In quantum theory, the flow of electric current through a metal is described as the propagation of electron waves along atomic corridors that act as waveguides. If atoms are located on a plane in perfect order, at equal distances from each other, then such a plane completely reflects electron waves - like an ideal mirror. Scattering and absorption of electron waves occurs only when the strict order in the arrangement of atoms is violated. This amazing conclusion was first obtained in a rather complex mathematical way from the equation of quantum mechanics. But it could also be done on the basis of the properties of electrical resistance established experimentally, if they were treated more carefully.
