Vavilov-Cherekkov radiation

This year marks five hundred years since the first reports about the discovery of one of the most beautiful physical phenomena appeared in the journal “Reports of the USSR Academy of Sciences”.

At that time, in 1934, a young aspiring scientist Pavel Alekseevich Cherekkov, under the leadership of Academician Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov, studied the lominescence (cold glow) of raster equilibrated salts under the influence of gamma radiation. To study measurements, it was necessary to see how the solvents themselves glow. At that time, it was believed that pure liquids did not give any glow at all, but Cherekkov’s experiments revealed a weak visible glow from solvents. This glow had nothing to do with lominescence, but such conclusions were not immediately reached.

In Vavilov’s laboratory, a special measurement technique was developed that allows one to determine whether the glow of different substances is laminescence or not. Cherekkov conducted corresponding experiments with the glow of pure liquids he discovered. Based on their results, Vavilov concluded that the radiation found was not lominescence.

What is the nature of this glow? The first reports about this were also made by S.I. Vavilov. Gamma radiation, that is, high-energy electromagnetic radiation, passing through matter selects electrons from atoms, which then move through the matter in a direction close to the direction of propagation of the gamma ray beam. It is these electrons that give the glow. Vavilov also made some proposals about how exactly high electrons emit, but they were not confirmed later. However, the idea that the source of the observed glow was electrons flying through the medium turned out to be completely correct and determined the entire course of further research.

Vavilov constantly discussed the range of problems associated with Cherekkov’s experiments with many physicists - the properties of others (S. I. Vavilov was the director of the P. N. Lebedev Physical Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences) and students. In particular, he managed to interest Igor Evgenievich Tamm and Ilya Mikhailovich Frank in these questions. Repeated joint discussions helped to understand the natural physical phenomenon. An important role for understanding was played by the fact that the glow turned out to be directional - the light was emitted forward, at an acute angle to the direction of movement of the knocked out electrons.

Three years after the first reports of the discovery of a new type of glow were published, Tamm and Frank developed a complete theory of the phenomenon. They showed that the glow is emitted by electrons that move through spatial matter at a constant (!) speed, exceeding (!) the speed of light in this matter.

According to the theory of relative technology, the speed of light in a vacuum is the maximum speed for all materials of bodies. An electron cannot outrun light in a vacuum. But it can outrun light by moving in a transparent medium, where the speed of light is much less than in vacuum. It was then that the radiation discovered by Vavilov and Cherekkov would appear.

The theory of Tamm and Frank is explained not only by the property of the directionality of radiation, but also by all its other properties - spectral composition, intensity, polarization. After the appearance of the Cherekkov theory, nearby measurements were carried out to test it. Their results, like the results of previous radiations, turned out to be in good agreement with the theory of Tamm and Frank.

Here it should be said that the radiation carried out by Cherekkov was necessary work, because the glow was extremely weak, the human eye could not detect it, and instruments better than the eye did not yet exist at that time. Cherekkov coped with all the difficulties with great skill and obtained promising results.

In the first years after the discovery and explanation of the new glow, I did not think about its practical application - this glow was too weak. But ten years later, very sensitive devices such as photomultiplier tubes were developed, and then the idea arose of using Vavilov-Cherekkov radiation to detect rapid contamination of particles.

Quite soon, on this principle, corresponding devices were created, called Cherekkov counters. Such a counter consists of a transparent substance in which a flying charged part creates Vavilov-Cherekkov radiation. This eclipse radiation enters the photomultiplier tube and is converted into an easily recorded voltage pulse. Now Cherekkov counters have become part of the arsenal of necessary devices in all laboratories (both ground-based and space-based - located on satellites) where research is being conducted on the study of fast particles.

The discovery of Vavilov-Cherekkov radiation was recognized in the absence of world science. In 1946, Vavilov, Tamm, Frank and Cherekkov were awarded the USSR State Prize, and in 1958 Cherekkov, Tamm and Frank (S.I. Vavilov died in 1951) were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery and explanation of Vavilov-Cherekkov radiation, embroidering a sign of international scientific recognition.

And even later, in 1974, almost simultaneously in England and the USSR, they remembered that the passage of a light through a substance at a speed exceeding the speed of a candle in this substance was considered in the end by the Lenian physicist and mathematician Oliver Heaviside. He studied this phenomenon quite fully (although not as fully as Tamm and Frank), but at that time the very idea of ​​​​the possibility of motion with columnar speeds seemed fantastic, and Heaviside’s work was firmly and long forgotten.
