A theorem that allows solving basic problems of electrostatics

It is known that the electrostatic field is often depicted using lines of force. Let's try to establish a connection between the number of field lines $N$ and the charge $q$, which created the electrostatic field. To do this, we introduce the concept of electrostatic field flow.

The flow of an electrostatic field through a certain surface will be called the product $ES$, where $S$ is the surface area, and $E$ is the model of the direction vector of the electrostatic field perpendicular to this surface.*) (The concept of “flow” is introduced here by analogy with the flow of liquid flowing through a cross-section of a pipe with an area of $S$ per unit time, which, as is known, is equal to $vS$ ("Physics 8", § 65).)

Let's start with the simplest case - one precise charge. The picture of the field lines of force created by the positive point behind the charge $q$ is depicted in figure 1. The distance of a sphere of radius $r$, the center of which is the charge $q$ itself, and we determine the flow of the electrostatic field through the surface of this sphere. The field lines emerging from the charge are perpendicular to the surface of the sphere, and at each point of the sphere the field strength mode is equal to

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi r^2\varepsilon_0},$$

(1)

where $\varepsilon_0 = 8,85 \cdot 10^{-12} \text{ KJ}^2 / (\text{H} \cdot \text{m}^2)$ is the electrostatic constant. But $4\pi r^2$ is the surface area of ​​the sphere. Denoting it by $S$, we get:

$$ES = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}.$$

(2)

From this it is clear that the flow through the surface of the sphere of the electrostatic field created at the point of the charge does not depend on the radius of the sphere, but depends only on the charge $q$ itself. Therefore, if a series of concentric spheres is drawn, then the flow of the electrostatic field through these spheres will be the same. Obviously,

*) If the electrostatic field is not perpendicular to a given surface, when calculating the flux of the electrostatic field, only the projection of the direction vector of the normal to the surface must be taken into account.

1. What is the electrostatic field strength inside the conductor?

It is known that a conductor is a body in which there are free charges. These charges can indeed move freely throughout the entire volume of the conductor. The only transformation for their movement is

the surface of the conductor, which they themselves cannot leave.

Consider an insulated conductor that is imparted with an electric charge. An electrostatic field is, of course, created around such a conductor. Let us prove that there is no electrostatic field inside a charged conductor, that is, the direction of the field is zero.

As is known, in an independent conductor, the negative charge of all electrons is exactly balanced by the positive charge of all protons, and their total charge is zero. But if the conductor is charged, then the balance of charges is upset. A conductor creates an excess of free electrons if it is negatively charged, or an excess of protons (a shortage of electrons) if it is positively charged. In the first case, repelling each other, the excess electrons will move away from each other to the maximum possible distances, as a result of which they will be located on the surface of the conductor (which they cannot leave). Inside the conductor, the balance of charges will be restored, and there the total charge will again become equal to zero.

In the second case, on the contrary, some of the electrons from the surface of the conductor, due to the forces of attraction towards positive charges, will rush inside the conductor and the excess positive charges will be balanced. The total charge inside the conductor will again become zero, and the excess positive charge will concentrate on its surface.

It turns out that a charge of any sign imparted to a conductor is located on its surface. Inside the conductor, that is, inside the closed surface, which in this case serves as the surface of the conductor itself, the charge is zero ($q=0$). But then from Gauss’s theorem it follows that

$$E = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0 S} = 0,$$

that is, there is no field inside the conductor.

2. How are the forces directed at the surface of a charged conductor?

Any free electron located on the surface of a charged conductor is subject to forces from the remaining surface charges (in the volume of the conductor, the sum of positive and negative charges is zero). Having the ability to move freely along the surface, the electrons themselves will arrange themselves so that the resulting force acting on each of them along the surface becomes zero. This means that the projection of the field direction onto the direction of the tangent to the surface of the conductor at any point is zero. And this is only possible under the condition that the field lines are directed perpendicular to the surface of the charged conductor (Fig. 2).

3. What is the direction of the field created by the charged plane?

figure 3 shows a section of the charged surface of the plane with the plane S, on which the charge q arrives.

We know that the field lines created by this plane are always perpendicular to it. What is the model for the direction of the electric field?

Let us encircle the selected section of the plane of a closed surface through which the lines of force pass at right angles to it. For the plane of such a surface, for example, a straight parallelepid with bases parallel to the plane is used. The field lines of force are perpendicular only to these bases, the other four faces of the parallelepid are parallel to the flat faces. The area of ​​the common bases is 2S.

From Gauss's theorem it follows that

$$E \cdot 2S = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0},$$

whence $E = \frac{q}{2\varepsilon_0 S}$.

This formula is given in §45 “Physics 9” without derivation. From the formula it is clear that the direction of the field at any point does not depend on the distance to the charged plane. Such a field is called homogeneous.
