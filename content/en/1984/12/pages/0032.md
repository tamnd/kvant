⟦folio 30⟧

It's the same everywhere. When a person brought his lips close to some place on the surface of the tea and inhaled, that is, expanded his lungs, in this place the air pressure on the surface of the tea became less than the atmospheric pressure acting on the rest of the surface. The pressure difference caused the tea to mix in an area of ​​lower pressure - so much of the tea under the person's lips rose, and the rest of the tea fell.

It seems to me that we need an exemplary solution to the problem.

Alyosha. Let's ask the author of the article how we can evaluate our solution.

Author. I am a physics teacher, and sixth-graders Sasha and Alyosha would give a grade of “five” for this name in solving the problem. The solution felt very good. I really liked that as the discussion progressed, Alyosha kept having “related” questions. After all, the answers to them were the stages of solving the entire problem.

But is this decision exemplary? Depending on what the practical solutions to the problem were and what the knowledge of the person solving it was, the solution can be assessed differently. But it can be improved, please, for free. For example, it would be possible to indicate why and under what conditions the density of air changes when its volume changes, and, moreover, give an explanation based on knowledge about molecules; the role of Pascal’s law in the observed phenomenon can be explained in more detail, etc.

I advise the reader to think through these additional explanations and those that Sasha and Alyosha talked about when solving the problem.

In conclusion, I offer you an independent solution to such a high-quality task.

Everyone knows how to use a pipette. Why does the liquid rise in the pipette? Will the pipette “work” if instead of a rubber canister, you put a small polystyrene bag on the glass tube?
