An analogue of the theorem “the altitudes of a triangle intersect at one point” is the statement of the following problem.

Problem 3. Prove that three planes passing through the edges of a triangular angle of redistribution to the opposite grams (“heights”) have a common straight line.

(This means that the indicated planes are uniquely defined, that is, at least two plane angles, to determine ASB and ASC, are not straight.) We reduce the proof to the application of a similar planimetric theorem.

Let us draw through point A, taken on the edge SA, a plane perepredkulyalny to this edge (Fig. 5). Let B and C be the points of intersection of this plane with the lines SB and SC, respectively, and AA1, BB1, CC1 be the lines of intersection of the planes from the problem with the plane ABC.

The plane SAA1 is perepredicular to the planes SBC (by condition) and ABC (it passes through the perepredicular SA to (ABC)), therefore it is perepredcular to their line of intersection - straight line BC. It follows that (AA1) ⊥ (BC), that is, that AA1 is the altitude of triangle ABC.

The plane ASC is perepedicular to the planes ABC and SBB1, therefore it is pereticular to the line BB1, therefore, (BB1) ⊥ (AC), similarly (CC1) ⊥ (AB), which means [BB1] and [CC1] are also altitudes of triangle ABC.

The altitudes AA1, BB1 and CC1 of triangle ABC intersect at one point - point H, therefore the three planes about which

$$\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin A} = \frac{\sin \beta}{\sin B} = \frac{\sin \gamma}{\sin C}$$

(theorem of sines); (1)

$$\cos \alpha = \cos \beta \cos \gamma + \sin \beta \sin \gamma \cos \hat{A}$$

(first cosine theorem); (2)

$$\cos \hat{A} = -\cos B \cos \hat{C} + \sin B \sin C \cos \alpha$$

(second cosine theorem). (3)

Proof of the theorem of sines. Take a point P on the edge SA, |SP|=1; let Q, R and H be its projections onto the lines SB, SC and the plane SBC (Fig. 6). In a right triangle PQH, the angle at vertex Q is equal to $\hat{B}$ (if $\hat{B} \leq \pi/2$ - Fig. 6 a) or $\pi - \hat{B}$ (if $\hat{B} > \pi/2$ - Fig. 66); in any case |PH|=|PQ|sin $\hat{B} = |SP|sin \gamma \cdot sin \hat{B} = \sin \gamma \cdot sin \hat{B}$.

From the right triangles PRH and SPR, it follows similarly that |PH|=sin $\beta \cdot sin \hat{C}$. Equating these two expressions, we find that sin $\beta/sin \hat{B} = sin \gamma/sin \hat{C}$. The second equality of the theorem is proved in exactly the same way.

Proof of the first cosine theorem. Let the vectors $\vec{SA}, \vec{SB}, \vec{SC}$ have length 1, P and Q are the projections of points B and C onto the straight line SA (Fig. 7). Then $\vec{SA} \cdot \vec{SB} = \cos \gamma, \vec{SA} \cdot \vec{SC} = \cos \beta, \vec{SB} \cdot \vec{SC} = \cos \alpha$, the angle between the vectors PB and QC is equal to $\hat{A}, \vec{SP} = \cos \alpha \vec{SA}, \vec{SQ} = \cos \beta \vec{SA}$ and (regardless of whether the angles β and γ are acute, right or obtuse) |QC|=sin $\beta$, |PB|=sin $\gamma$. Further, QC=SC-SQ, PB=SB-SP and due to the scalar product property

$$\vec{QC} \cdot \vec{PB} = \vec{SC} \cdot \vec{SB} - \vec{SC} \cdot \vec{SP} - \vec{SQ} \cdot \vec{SB} + \vec{SQ} \cdot \vec{SP},$$

where

$$\sin \beta \sin \gamma \cos \hat{A} = \cos \alpha - \cos \beta \cos \gamma - \cos \beta \cos \gamma + \cos \beta \cos \gamma,$$

that is

$$\cos \alpha = \cos \beta \cos \gamma + \sin \beta \sin \gamma \cos \hat{A}.$$

The theorem has been proven.

If angle A is a right angle, then cos $\alpha = \cos \beta \cos \gamma$. This statement is sometimes called the Pythagorean theorem for triangular angles.
