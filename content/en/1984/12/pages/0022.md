Since the ball conducting the force lines of the field is always directed perpendicular to its surface, that is, along the radii (Fig. 4). What is radius?

Everyone has probably seen this amazing multi-colored “sky arch” stretching across half the horizon. After a refreshing passing downpour, it pleases the eye with its multicolored colors against the backdrop of leaden clouds carried away by the wind.

How does this differentiating maturity arise and what does it represent? First of all, we will describe the radius itself, as it appears halfway across the horizon.

It is typical that a radiga can only be seen in a flock with its back to the sun, if there is a cloud in front of the eyes, and the height of the sun above the horizon does not exceed approximately 42°. The radigue is a part of the circle of the base of the cone, the apex of which is the eye. The axis of the cone is straight, coming from the sun through the eye and directed (Fig. 1). The center of the circle is below the horizon line, so the radius is not a semicircle. It approaches this if the sun is close to sunset. The angle at the apex of the cone is approximately 84°.

When moving, it is observed along with it, so there is no point in trying to get to the base of the radig and look at it “from the side.” A radiga is not something that is located in a specific place. A radigue is something that can be seen in a certain direction (at an angle of about 42° to said axis).

The outer (upper) part of the radig is red, the bottom is purple. Between them are “all the colors of radiga.”

Basic phenomena in radiga

The cloud, against which the radigue is visible, consists of water drops. It is in them that the optical phenomena due to which it arises occur. Basically these are:

1) Refraction of light at the air-water boundary. According to the law of refraction, sin α/sin β=n, where α is the angle of capture of light, β is the angle of refraction and n is the refractive index of the second medium (water) relative to the first (air).

2) Reflection at the water-air boundary. It occurs according to the general law of reflection - the angle of capture is equal to the angle of reflection.

3) Dispersion of light, that is, the dependence of the refractive index on the wavelength of light. Visible sunlight contains different wavelengths - from the longest, about 750 nm, causing the sensation of red, to the shortest, about 400 nm, violet. Water has a refractive index for red light n=1.331, for violet light - n=1.344. The n values ​​for other colors lie between these numbers.

How does radiga arise?

Using the above formulation of n, you can understand how a radigue is formed.

Consider a drop of water on which sunlight falls (Fig. 2). Let a beam of light (of one wavelength for now) fall on a drop near point A, be refracted in it and reach point B. Here it is reflected, at point C it is refracted again and distorted.
