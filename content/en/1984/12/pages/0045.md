⟦folio 43⟧

My meetings with Debye
(on the 100th anniversary of the birth of Peter Debye)

The outstanding physicist and chemist Peter Joseph Wilhelm Debye (1884-1966) is known to a wide circle of physicists as the author of the theory of heat capacity of solids.

At the beginning of our century, the development of such a theory seemed to be a difficult problem. The fact is that in the last century an interesting pattern concerning heat capacity was discovered. Numerous measurements have shown that the product of specific heat capacity and molar mass of a substance is constant for all solids, practically independent of temperature and equal to approximately 25.2 J/(mol K). This is Dulong and Petit's law.

At the beginning of this century, it was experimentally established that at low temperatures the heat capacity of solids decreases with decreasing temperature. And if you plot a curve for the dependence of heat capacity on temperature, then it tends to zero at absolute zero temperatures. There was no satisfactory explanation for this.

Many scientists have tried to express the found temperature dependence of heat capacity using a mathematical formula. But apart from more or less approximate empirical formulas, the formula maker was unable to obtain anything. The first real theory of heat capacity of solids was given by Einstein. It used Planck's idea that the energy of atoms changes discretely, a multiple of hv, where v is the oscillation frequency of the atoms. Einstein first suggested that the frequencies are the same for all atoms (which is actually not the case), and by summing up he built theories of heat capacity in a comparative group of approximations.

Taking advantage of Planck's same basic idea, Debye created theories in 1912 that took into account that different atoms oscillated at different frequencies. This theory makes it possible to calculate the law of Dulong and Petit at relatively high temperatures and to derive a fairly accurate dependence of the heat capacity on temperature at low temperatures. In accordance with the measurement data, it followed from the theory that the heat capacity is proportional to the third degree of absolute temperature. This relationship is now called Debye's law of heat capacity.

Subsequently, it was calculated which temperatures should be considered high and which should be considered low. It turns out that for each substance in the solid state there is a characteristic temperature - the Debye temperature, above which the classical theory of heat capacity is applicable, and below which the introduction of quantum theory is required.

From Debye's theory, in particular, which ones should be considered high and which ones should be considered low. It turns out that at low temperatures the thermal conductivity of solid dielectrics should increase with decreasing temperature. And this is true. For example, for diamond the Debye temperature is about 2000 °C, room temperatures are low for it, and the thermal conductivity is very high - it is comparable to the thermal conductivity of good conductors (copper).

Debye's name is associated not only with the theory of heat capacity and thermal conductivity of solids; it is a sign of scientists involved in magnetism, electrochemical dissociation, etc. For major scientific achievements, Debye was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1936.

I managed to meet Debye in 1928 at the All-Union Conference of Physicists, which was attended by many famous foreign scientists. I was a student then, and for us Debye was a class act, since seeing his name often was an event for me and other young physicists.

Debye was 44 years old at the time. When his report was announced, a man of athletic build came to the podium, and we prepared the conditions for thunderheads. Imagine everyone’s surprise when he began to speak in a very high pitched voice, almost like a treble.

We knew Debye well as a theoretician and knew little about his experimental work. It was said at the congress that when some issue was discussed on the sidelines and theorists turned to Debye, Debye said that he was an experimenter and that theory was not his specialty. And if experimenters approached, Debye said that he was a theoretician. In reality, of course, he was both.

Debye was a cheerful and witty man. We were surprised when he jokingly started to fight with fellow physicist Robert Pohl, a man also of athletic build, and defeated him.

After graduation, the foreign delegates went home via Batuti. Debye went with us to Leningrad and said that he was more interested in eating with students than with scientists. We went in the general version. On the way, Debye entertained us with numerous stories from the lives of physicists and from his own too. By the way, along the way he taught us German (Debye was born in the Netherlands, but worked in Germany), and we taught him Russian. He was, apparently, a better teacher than us, since we understood him, but he practically did not understand us.

I met Debye for the second time in 1930, when, after graduation, they were sent to Germany and visited Leippig, where Debye then headed the department of physics at the university. Remembering our acquaintance, he greeted me cheerfully, showed me the laboratory, invited me to a lecture and seminars. I became convinced that much of Debye's experimental work was left with great skill and absence.

I. K. Kikopi
