⟦folio 28⟧

How we drink tea
(or about how to solve qualitative problems in physics)
Doctor of Pedagogical Sciences
H. A. MOTHERLAND

In one of the episodes of the television program “What? Where? When?" The “experts” were asked a question (we present it in a slightly clarified form):

“Dear experts! There is a glass of tea in front of you. Take a sip and answer the question: why does tea rise up, while water usually flows down?”

The answer that the experts settled on after a minute of service was this: “we suck in the water.” It was found correct, and the experts received a point and a reward - a book.

What would you answer if you were asked such a question not in a television program, but in a physics lesson? After all, this question is a real qualitative physical problem (this is the conventional name for problems in which there is no numerical data and the solution of which does not necessarily have to be expressed in the form of a formula or number). But before we think about the answer, let’s agree on what we will consider the answer. To solve a problem in physics qualitatively means to give an answer to its question, justified by the law and rules of physical science, expressed in pleasant terms, presented logically, so that each new stage of reasoning is a consequence of the previous one. You will probably agree that from this point of view, the answer given by the expert to the “tea problem” cannot be considered satisfactory.

So how to solve the problem? Let's listen to how two sixth-graders do it - Sasha and Alyosha.

Sasha. It's all about atmospheric pressure!

(I must say that Sasha preferred to answer quick questions.)

Alyosha. Wait, we need to start from the very beginning - with the condition: a motionless body - tea in a glass - began to move. We know that the phenomenon of inertia exists - if no other bodies act on the body, no force acts, then the body is at rest or moves uniformly and in a straight line. The body cannot change this state on its own, and the tea cannot begin to move without the action of a force.

Sasha. But all bodies are affected by the force of gravity, and it also affects tea. It doesn't pour, it doesn't fall, because the glass has a bottom.

Alyosha. This explanation - the glass has a bottom - can be given by a small child. From a physics point of view, this is not an explanation. Anyone who has studied physics will say: under the influence of weight, the tea will sag and an upward elastic force will arise. It acts on the tea and balances the force of gravity acting on it.

We explained why the tea initially does not move relative to the glass. But our problem is not about the movement of the whole body - water in a glass - as a whole, but about the movement of parts of water
