water. Electron waves are scattered by disturbances in the lattice. Part of the electron energy is absorbed and transferred to the body. This is the quantum mechanism of electrical resistance. Correctly atomic waveguides are disrupted by tell oscillations, on which electron waves are scattered (figure 4). This gives rise to the tell part of the electrical resistance, which depends on temperature. By cooling the metal, it can be made as small as possible. At the same time, residual, or structural, resistance is retained, associated with permanent defects (damage) of the crystal structure (figure 5 and 6). Defects exist in every crystal and depend on its “biography” - on the conditions in which it originated, grew and existed. The introduction of foreign atoms causes serious damage to the crystalline lattice (figure 7). In this case, one impurity atom can be from the place of hundreds of atoms of the “host” crystal. This is why impurities increase resistance. It has been noticed that the effect of an impurity is stronger, the larger its atoms in size and another factor of difference from the atoms of the “host”, the more they violate the correct crystal lattice.

If you cool the metal, then the tell (that is, origination from the tell movement) part of the resistance drops. When it becomes much smaller than the temperature-independent part of the structure, then with further cooling the resistance almost does not change. In alloys with a disordered structure, the resistance part of the structure is so large that even at high temperatures it prevails over the tell structure. This explains the weak dependence of the electrical resistance of alloys on temperature.
