Electricity is a property of a conductor

(to solution problem F891)

It is known that the charge imparted to a conducting body is located on its surface. In this case, the charge distribution over the surface is such that the direction of the electric field inside the conductor at any point is zero. The conductor is an equipotential region (any surface lying entirely inside the conductor is equipotential). Linn's directionality of the electric field redistributes the conductor.

The electric field created by a conductor with charge $q$ at any point in space is determined by the superposition of fields created by individual charged sections of the conductor. Each such section has a charge $\Delta q_i = \sigma_i \cdot \Delta s_i$, where $\Delta s_i$ is the area of ​​the section, $\sigma_i$ is the surface charge density in this section of the conductor. What happens if a conductor imparts a charge $n$ times greater (or less) than $q$? The charge density at any point in the conductivity of the conductor will change $n$ times. This means that the elementary work done by the electric field on a unit positive charge will change $n$ times in any area. In the column, the work of the electric forces acting on a single positive charge will change when moving it from the surface of the conductor to infinity - that is, the potential state remains unchanged. Size

$$C = \frac{q}{\varphi}$$

called the electrical capacity (or simply capacitance) of a remote conductor. The more charge that can be imparted to a conductor at a given potential, the greater the conductor's capacitance. We can say that electrical capacity characterizes the ability of a conductor to accumulate charge at a given potential. For a given conductor (conductor with capacitance $C$), there is a single value of charge $q$, which must be imparted to the conductor in order for its potential to be equal to $q$.

Electronity is uniquely determined by the formula of the conductor, its dimensions and the electrical properties of the environment.

If the dimensions of the conductor are reduced by $n$ times, then the conductor capacitance will decrease by $n$ times. Let's show this.

Let the remote conductor $N$ with capacitance $C$ have a charge $q$; The potential of the conductor is obviously equal to $\varphi = q/C$. Let's give the conductor an additional small charge $\Delta q$,

$$\Delta A = -\varphi \cdot \Delta q = -\frac{q}{C} \cdot \Delta q = -\Delta \left( \frac{q^2}{2C} \right).$$

This work goes to change the potential energy of the conductor. Therefore, with the addition of charge $\Delta q$, the potential energy of the conductor changes by

$$\Delta U = -\Delta A = \Delta \left( \frac{q^2}{2C} \right).$$

But this means that the potential energy of a conductor with a capacitance $C$, which imparts a charge $q$, is equal to

$$U = \frac{q^2}{2C}.$$

We can say that this energy is due to the repulsive forces acting between similarly charged individual sections of the conductor and represents the potential energy of the interaction of identical exact charges distributed over the surface of the conductor. Two elementary conductivity sections of the conductor $N$ (Fig. 1, a) contribute to the total potential energy

$$\Delta U_{12} = k \frac{\Delta q_1 \cdot \Delta q_2}{R_{12}},$$

where $\Delta q_1$, $\Delta q_2$ are the charges of these sections, $R_{12}$ is the distance between them.

Now let's turn to the solitary conductor $N'$, the linear dimensions of which are $n$ times smaller than $u$ of the conductor $N$. What can be said about the electrical capacity $C'$ of this conductor?

Let conductor $N'$ have the same charge $q$ as conductor $N$. Let us consider two elementary conductivity sections of conductor $N'$ (Fig. 1, b), similar to sections 1 and 2 of conductor $N$. The contribution made by the interaction of the charges of these sections to the total potential energy is equal to

$$\Delta U_{12}' = k \frac{\Delta q_1' \cdot \Delta q_2'}{R_{12}'}.$$

From the similarity of the linear dimensions of conductors $N'$ and $N$ it follows that the areas of sections $I'$ and $I'$ and 2 are connected by the relations $\Delta s_1' = \Delta s_1/n^2$, $\Delta s_2' = \Delta s_2/n^2$. From the uniqueness theorem we can obtain between the charge densities in these sections: $\sigma_1' = \sigma_1 n^2$, $\sigma_2' = \sigma_2 n^2$. Hence $\Delta q_1' = \sigma_1' \cdot \Delta s_1' = \Delta q_1$ and $\Delta q_2' = \sigma_2' \cdot \Delta s_2' = \Delta q_2$. And since $R_{12}' = R_{12}/n$, $\frac{\Delta U_{12}}{R_{12}} = \frac{1}{n}$, that is $\Delta U_{12}' = n \cdot \Delta U_{12}$. Consequently, the total potential energy of the conductor $N'$ is greater than the potential energy of the conductor.
