Edison effect

More than a thousand different technical devices were patented during his life by Thomas Alva Edison (1847-1931), a quick inventor and a cunning businessman, a tireless worker and a gambling man, who retained the mischievous habits of Markt’s Tom Sawyer until a very old age. Edison made a huge contribution to the development of telephone technology and technical lighting; he invented the first sound recording apparatus - the phonograph, a new type of battery, fuse, electromagnetic brake, etc.

The famous inventor and physical discovery have one, but very important, one to his credit. This is the discovery of the phenomenon of thermionic emission, later called the “Edison effect.” He first observed this effect in 1883, but published a message in print only the following year, 1884.

Like many other scientific discoveries, thermionic emission has been discovered to the best of our knowledge. At the end of 1879, Edison was mainly working on the creation of an electronic superimposition tube and was now working hard on the problem of extending its service life. This lamp was essentially not much different from the modern one - only a thread made of charred bamboo fiber was used together with the tungsten spiral. The first such lamps could burn continuously for about 40 hours—a “durability” that initially delighted Edison and his assistants. However, it soon became clear that “two-day lamps” were not suitable for mass use.

While searching for the reason for the rapid burnout of the carbon filament, Edison noticed that the inner surface of the lamp bulb was covered with a dark coating of carbon particles, apparently flying out of the hot filament. Hoping to somehow suppress this undesirable phenomenon, Edison placed an additional electrode in the lamp near the filament, the electronic potential of which could be changed. At the same time, he was surprised to discover that when the potential of this electrode is higher than the potential of the thread (positive with respect to the potential of the thread), there is a vacuum gap between it and the thread! — electron current flows. Despite all his technical discoveries, Edison could not extract any practical usefulness from his discovery, and it had to be blocked.

The Edison effect could only be explained after the discovery of the electrode (1897). The explanation was given by the English physicist J. A. Fleming (1849-1945), who worked for Edison in 1882-85, and who himself told him about the discovered effect. Fleming realized that the electron current in the lamp cylinder is created by electrons emitted by a hot filament and pressed positively charged by an additional electrode.

Continuing to reflect on the phenomenon of thermionic emission, in 1904 Fleming patented the first technical radio tube in history. It was a vacuum didok, entirely based on one-way conduction, associated with the Edison effect, and allowing alternating current to be absorbed. Fleming conceived his lamp as a detector for radio waves.

In 1906, the American engineer L. de Forest (1873-1961) invented a three-electrode lamp - a triode, historically the first electronic amplifier device. The discovery of an effective way to listen to weak electronic signals marked the birth of a new field of technology and science - electronics. Before the advent of transistors (50s of the 20th century), radio tubes reigned supreme, the operation of which was based on the Edison effect, an effect that the famous inventor did not attach much importance to at one time.

Now thermionic emission is used in hundreds of millions of light sources, primarily in fluorescent lamps.

B. E. Yavelov
