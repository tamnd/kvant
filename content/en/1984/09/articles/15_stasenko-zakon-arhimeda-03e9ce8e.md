Take a cube made of wood or plastic, which may have remained from the ancient times of kindergarten. Hold it in the air somewhere between the floor and the ceiling and release it. The cube will fall to the floor. Why? It is clear: he is attracted by the Earth with a force called gravity.

Now, roll up your sleeves, place this cube in a bathtub filled with water, somewhere between the bottom and the surface of the water, and release. The cube will float up. Why? Doesn't the force of gravity of the Earth act on him through the water? It can’t be: after all, the duck or dumbbell sinks to the bottom!

This means that there is still some kind of upward force acting - a buoyant force. In the case of a cube, it “overpowers” ​​the force of gravity; in the case of a dumbbell, it is not enough to force the dumbbell to move upward.

How can we find out what this buoyant force is equal to? Let's turn to figure 1 and see what effect water has on a cube dropped into it. Water pressure forces act on all faces of the cube. But, as you know, the pressure in a liquid is different at different depths - the greater the depth of immersion, the greater it is. This means that the pressure forces acting on the upper and lower faces of the cube are different. It is not difficult to guess that the force acting on the lower boundary is greater. Let's see how much more.

The upper face is acted upon by a downward pressure force equal to

$$F \downarrow = \varrho_* g y^2.$$

The force acting from the water on the lower boundary is directed upward and is equal to

$$F = F \uparrow - F \downarrow = \varrho_* g (y + a) a^2 - \varrho_* g y^2 = \varrho_* g a^3.$$

This is the pushing force. But $\varrho_* g a^3$ is the weight of water that would be poured into our cube more if it were hollow, that is, this is the weight of water in the volume of the cube. Now it’s clear: if the pushing force is greater than the force of gravity, which “pulls” the body down, the body will float up; if this force is less

The validity of our calculations can also be verified experimentally. Take a piece of ice, for example, from the refrigerator, place it at the bottom of the glass and pour water into the glass to the brim. The ice will float on the surface, which means that the force of gravity acting on the ice is balanced by the buoyant force. The cube, like a ship, has a surface and an underwater part. After some time, the ice will melt and its surface part will disappear. What will happen to the water level in the glass? It turns out he won't change! This means that the ice, having become water, filled exactly the volume that was previously occupied by its underwater part. We shaded this water (former ice) at figure 2. The force of gravity acting on it is exactly equal to the force of gravity acting on the ice cube. It turns out that for water in a glass, keeping an ice cube afloat is the same as keeping shaded water. From the side of the water in the glass, a force directed upward acts on the shaded water, numerically equal to the weight of this water. This is the buoyant force that balances the force of gravity.

So, we came to the conclusion that a body immersed in a liquid is acted upon by a buoyant force directed upward and is numerically equal to the weight of the liquid in the volume of the immersed part of the body. This is Archimedes' law of imitations.

1) The forces acting on the side faces of the cube cancel each other - they are equal in pairs and directed in opposite directions. Therefore, we did not take them into account in our calculations.

Archimedes himself formulated this idea in other words: “A solid body that has equal weight in equal volume with a liquid is immersed in it so much that no part of its surface suffers above its surface, and does not sink below.” We would now translate this as follows: if the density of a solid body is equal to the density of a liquid, then the body is completely immersed in this liquid and does not sink. And Archimedes also said this: “Bodies that are relatively heavier than a liquid sink down to the very bottom and become one column lighter in the liquid, the weight of a volume of liquid equal to the volume of the body.”

Archimedes' Law, people, even without knowing it, used products when they crossed the river on a log, on a fur or a flat boat. But when in the last century they began to move from wooden sailing ships to iron steamships, people far from physics and technology could use it because iron, unlike wood, sinks in water.

Yes, an iron dumbbell sinks in water, but if you roll it into a thin sheet and bend it into a “boat” or in the shape of a hemisphere, it will float (after all, empty cans float in ore and the river, thrown by unfriendly tourists). Both the can and the boat will displace such a volume of water that the buoyant force, numerically equal to the weight of this volume of water, balances the force of gravity acting on the ship. And the weight of the water displaced by the dumbbell is small, the buoyancy force is small - and the force of gravity lowers the dumbbell to the bottom.

So, we have found out under what conditions bodies float in liquid, and under what conditions they sink. Now let's continue the little experiment. Take an apple, cut it in half and put the half in a pan of water - the half floats. And place the other half on the bottom of an empty pan, medium down, and carefully fill it with water, so that water does not penetrate between the apple and the bottom of the pan (to do this, you can grease the edges of the pan with oil in advance). This half will lie on the bottom as if glued. (The fact that everything will happen exactly this way is evidenced by the photograph on the cover of this issue of the magazine.) What explains the columnar behavior of apple halves? The fact is that in the second case - the half is at the bottom - there is no buoyancy force, since there is no water under the apple. Until now, we have been talking about liquid all the time. What about air?
