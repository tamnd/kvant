A solid state is a state in which the potential energy $W_p$ of the interaction of molecules is overwhelming compared to the kinetic energy $W_k$:

$$W_p \gg W_k.$$

How can this condition be changed? For example, heating a solid. It is clear that when heated, the kinetic energy of a body part increases, but their potential energy practically does not change. In this way, it is possible to ensure that the kinetic energy becomes approximately equal to the potential energy. Then a transition from the solid to the liquid state will occur, which is characterized by the approximate equality of $W_p$ and $W_k$:

$$W_p \approx W_k.$$

In this state, the potential energy is still high enough so that the molecules cannot fly apart from each other, and the kinetic energy has already reached high enough for the molecules to acquire a certain mobility, that is, the ability to move within the volume. The liquid retains its volume, but becomes flowing and therefore does not retain its shape.

We will continue to supply energy, heating the liquid now. The kinetic energy of the molecules will grow and at some point exceed the potential energy of their current interaction, so that it (the potential energy) can be exceeded:

$$W_p \ll W_k.$$

Six will go into a state that corresponds to an ideal gas (“Physics 9”, § 6).

What if we continue heating, now with gas? Will a further increase in the kinetic energy of molecules lead to another aggregate transition? It turns out yes. But only because molecules (atoms) are not just very small balls. They are complex systems of charged parts, even smaller than atoms. When the gas reaches a high temperature, the collisions of its parts lead to the fact that the molecules disintegrate into atoms, and the atoms into their constituent charged particles.

The result is a special kind of substance, consisting of an equal number of positively and negatively charged particles (in general, electron neutral). This is the fourth state of matter - plasma.

Unlike other states of aggregation, plasma consists of electron interactions between two parts. This is why the properties of plasma differ significantly from the properties of matter in other states.

Oscillatory movement is a periodic movement, that is, one in which the coordinate of the body is repeated at certain intervals of time (the minimum interval is called the oscillation period). A typical example of oscillations is the movement of a body attached to a circle (“Physics 10”, §§ 2 and 4). This movement of the layer is caused by elasticity $F_x = -kx$, changing from point to point, so the acceleration of the body also changes. When moving, a oscillating body also passes through a position in which the force acting on it becomes zero. This is the equilibrium position; it serves as the center of oscillation. The maximum deviation of the body from the equilibrium position is called the vibration amplitude.

Since the elastic force is a variable force, then solve the equation of Newton’s second law

$$-kx = ma_x$$

and it is impossible to find how the coordinate $x$ changes with time using general algebra. Therefore, we will temporarily replace physics with geometry and consider a completely different movement, but “like two peas in a pod” it is similar to the movement of a body held together by a string.

Let some body (material point) move with a constant angular velocity $\omega$ along a circle of radius $A$ (Fig. 1). Circular motion is also periodic motion, since at certain intervals of time $T = 2\pi/\omega$ (period of revolution) the body ends up in the same place on the circle. Let us consider, however, the movement not of the point itself, but of its

projections onto the diameter XX of the circle. It is immediately clear that when a point moves along a circle, its projection moves along the diameter XX and this movement is the actual position of the movement of the body attached to the spring. Moving along the diameter, the projection moves away from the center of the circle to a distance no greater than the radius A. We can therefore say that the projection oscillates with amplitude A. The center of the circle O plays here the same role as the equilibrium position in the experiment with a spring. The period of rotation of a point along a circle is at the same time the period of oscillation of its projection. But if for a point moving in a circle, $\omega$ is the angular velocity, then for the projection $\omega$ it is the so-called cyclic frequency, that is, the number of oscillations in 2π seconds:

$$\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi v.$$

Here $v$ is the oscillation frequency, that is, the number of oscillations per second.

Let us assume that at some instant of time, which we take as the initial one ($t=0$), a point moving along the circle is in position $M_0$ (Fig. 2). Let us draw the vector $\overrightarrow{OM_0}$ from the center of the circle as from the origin of coordinates. It is called the radius vector (the modulus of this vector is A). The radius vector determines the position of a point on the circle, and the projection of its end onto the diameter XX determines the position of the projection $M'_0$ of the point, that is, the coordinates $x_0$ of this projection, subtracted from the center of the circle O. The movement of a point along the circle means at the same time the rotation of the radius vector around the center O with the same angular velocity $\omega$. From figure 2 it is clear that the initial coordinate $x_0$ of the projection is equal to A cos $\varphi_0$, where $\varphi_0$ is the angle measured from the X axis counterclockwise to the vector $\overrightarrow{OM_0}$.

Let after a time interval $t$ a point moving along a circle ends up in M, and its projection ends up in $M'$. The rotation angle of the radius vector increased by $\Delta\varphi=\omega t$ and became equal to $\Delta\varphi+\varphi_0$. The $x$ coordinate of the point's projection is now equal to

$$x=A \cos(\Delta\varphi+\varphi_0)=A \cos(\omega t+\varphi_0).$$

Formula

$$x=A \cos(\omega t+\varphi_0)$$

and shows how the coordinate $x$ of the projection moving along the circle of a point, that is, the coordinate of the point oscillating, depends on time. Oscillations in which the coordinate of the oscillating body (point) depends on time according to the law (*) are called harmonic. The value $\varphi=\omega t+\varphi_0$ is called the oscillation phase, and $\varphi_0$ is the initial phase.

As we have already said, the movement of the projection of the end of the radius vector onto the diameter of the circle throughout the whole position is based on the movement of the body attached to the spring. The converse is also true: the motion of a body attached to a spring is similar to the motion of the projection of a point moving along a circle onto the diameter of this circle. Therefore, each oscillating body can be associated with a rotating radius vector, the projection of the end of which is the oscillating body (point). This is shown in figure 3 for the case of a body attached to a horizontal spring.

Suppose we need to add these two oscillations:

$$x_1 = A_1 \cos(\omega t + \varphi_0),$$
$$x_2 = A_2 \cos(\omega t + \varphi_0).$$

As can be seen from the written formulas, oscillations can differ in amplitudes ($A_1$ and $A_2$) and initial phases ($\varphi_01$ and $\varphi_02$). To find out what happens as a result of the addition, let us depict the radius vectors $\vec{A}_1$ and $\vec{A}_2$ corresponding to the added oscillations of the rotator as if they entered at the initial moment of time and show the circles along which their ends move (Fig. 4; for simplicity, incomplete circles are shown at figure). Obviously, the resulting breaking oscillation will occur with the same frequency $\omega$, but with a different amplitude and a different initial phase:

$$x = A \cos(\omega t + \varphi_0).$$

We get the values ​​$A$ and $\varphi_0$ if we add the vectors $\vec{A}_1$ and $\vec{A}_2$ according to the parallelogram rules. The diagonal of the parallelogram $A = |\vec{A}|$ is the amplitude of the total oscillation, and the angle $\varphi_0$, which the vector $\vec{A}$ forms with the axis $X$, is the initial phase of this oscillation.

According to the well-known cosine theorem, we can write

$$A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 - 2A_1A_2 \cos OA_1A,$$

where $OA_1A = 180^\circ - A_2OA_1 = 180^\circ - (\varphi_02 - \varphi_01)$. Since $\cos(180^\circ - (\varphi_02 - \varphi_01)) = -\cos(\varphi_02 - \varphi_01)$, then

$$A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2 \cos(\varphi_02 - \varphi_01).$$

The result of the addition of two oscillations depends, therefore, on the difference in the phases of the oscillations-components. Since the oscillations occur with the same frequency, the phase difference $\varphi_2 - \varphi_1$ of the oscillations at any time is equal to the difference $\varphi_02 - \varphi_01$ of the initial phases.

Two extreme cases are of particular interest:

1) Phase difference $\varphi_2 - \varphi_1 = 0$ (or $2\pi$, $4\pi$, etc.). Oscillations whose phases are the same at any time are called in-phase. In this case, $\cos(\varphi_02 - \varphi_01) = \cos(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1) = 0$, and $A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2$, or $A = A_1 + A_2$ - the amplitude of the resulting oscillation is equal to the sum of the amplitudes of the added oscillations. In other words, oscillations result from addition.

2) Phase difference $\varphi_2 - \varphi_1 = \pi$ (or $3\pi$, $5\pi$, etc.). Such oscillations are said to have opposite phases (antiphase oscillations). In this case, $\cos(\varphi_02 - \varphi_01) = \cos(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1) = -1$, and $A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 - 2A_1A_2$, or $A = A_1 - A_2$ - the resulting oscillation turns out to be weakened: the amplitude is equal to the difference in the amplitudes of the component oscillations. If $A_1 = A_2$, then the amplitude of the resulting oscillation is zero, that is, there are no oscillations at all: one oscillation “extinguished” another.

In this state, one of the most important properties of vibrations is that when added, they can be compressed or weakened. This property plays an important role in wave processes (“Physics 10”, § 36).
