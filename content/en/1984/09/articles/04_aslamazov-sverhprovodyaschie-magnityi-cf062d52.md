Superconducting magnets

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. G. ASLAMAZOV

Strong magnetic fields can be produced by passing a strong current through a coil. The more current flows through the coil, the greater the magnetic field it creates. But if the coil has electrical resistance, then a tello is released in it. You have to spend enormous energy to maintain the current, and gray problems arise related to the tapping of the body (initially the coil may melt). Thus, in 1937, a magnetic field with an induction of 10 Tesla was first obtained. But it was possible to maintain this field only at night, when other consumers of the power plant supplying current to the winding were turned off. The released body was removed with running water, and at the same time, every second 5 liters of water were brought to a boil.

This is precisely what limits the possibility of obtaining strong magnetic fields in conventional solenodes.

The idea of ​​using superconductivity to create strong magnetic fields arose immediately after its discovery. It would seem that all that is required is to wind a coil of superconducting wire, close its ends and send a sufficiently strong current through such a circuit. Since the electrical resistance of the coil is zero, the body does not release. And although cooling the solenoid to liquid gel temperatures at which superconductivity occurs creates certain difficulties, the advantages would outweigh the disadvantages if... the magnetic field itself did not destroy superconductivity.

Discovery of superconductivity

figure 1 shows a diagram of the Kamerling-Onnes experiment, which was done in 1911 in Leiden. Dutch scientists made a coil of lead, connected it to an EMF source and, placing the coil in liquid gel, cooled it to the boiling point of the gel (4.2 K). At the same time, the electronic resistance of lead disappeared - it went into a superconducting state. Then they changed the position of the key and short-circuited the coil - an undamped superconducting current began to circulate through it.

This current creates a magnetic field, the induction of which is proportional to the strength of the current. It would seem that the stronger the current in the coil, the greater the magnetic field that can be obtained in this way. The result, however, turned out to be disappointing: when a field of several fractions of a Tesla was inducted, the solenoid returned to its normal state (it developed resistance). They tried to make coils from other superconductors, but even in them, the destruction of superconductivity occurred at relatively low magnetic fields. What's the matter?

Meissner effect

The solution to this “inconvenient” behavior of superconductors was found in 1933 in Berlin in the laboratory of W. Meissner. It seemed that the superconductor had the ability to completely displace the magnetic field; in it only the magnetic field induction is zero.

Let's imagine that a metal cylinder (a piece of wire) was cooled to a low temperature and transferred to

superconducting state. Then a magnetic field with induction $\vec{B}_{\text{внешш}}$ was turned on. According to the law of electromagnetic induction, steep points (figure 2) will appear on the surface of the cylinder, which create a magnetic field in the cylinder with induction $\vec{B}_{\text{ток}}$, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the induction of the external field. These points are superconducting and will not fade. Therefore, in the thickness of the superconductor, the total induction is zero: $\vec{B} = \vec{B}_{\text{внешш}} + \vec{B}_{\text{ток}} = 0$. Magnetic field lines in a superconductor do not peak.

Well, what if we change the sequence of operations - first place the metal in an external magnetic field, and then cool it to a superconducting state. It would seem that the magnetic field induction does not change and there is no reason for the appearance of shielding surface points. This is exactly what Meissner thought when he checked Laus’s calculations; this refers to the first method of testing an experiment.*) I thought about it, but still decided to check it. The result of the modified experiment was surprising. It turned out that in this case, too, the magnetic field completely flows out of the superconductor and does not penetrate into it.

This phenomenon was called the Meissner effect.

It is now clear why a magnetic field destroys superconductivity. After all, a certain amount of energy is spent on the appearance of surface points. In this sense, the superconducting state is not
no better than the normal state, when the magnetic field penetrates the metal and there are no shielding surface points. The greater the external field induction, the stronger the current must flow across the surface to provide shielding. At a certain value of magnetic field induction, superconductivity is necessarily destroyed, and the metal goes into the normal state. The field at which superconductivity is destroyed is called the critical field of the superconductor. It is important to remember that an external magnetic field is not necessary to destroy superconductivity. The current flowing through the superconductor itself creates a magnetic field. When, at a certain current value, the induction of this field reaches a value corresponding to the critical field, superconductivity is destroyed.

The magnitude of the critical field increases with decreasing temperature, but even at absolute zero, the critical field in pure superconducting metals is not large (see graph on figure 3). In the best cases, it is only ten parts of a teska. So, it would seem, there is nothing to think about creating strong magnetic fields using superconductors.

But further research into superconductivity showed that there is a way out. It was discovered that there is a whole group of superconductors that retain superconductivity even in very strong magnetic fields, albeit in a somewhat weakened form.

magnetic field is not so simple. Just like with pure superconductors, at certain induction values ​​the magnetic field begins to penetrate inside the superconductor. But in alloys, the magnetic field usually does not immediately fill the entire volume of the superconductor. In its thickness, at first only individual structures of magnetic field induction lines (figure 4) are formed. Each such structure contains a strictly defined portion of the magnetic flux $\Phi_0 = 2 \cdot 10^{-15}$ Wb. The quantity $\Phi_0$ is called the magnetic flux quantum.

The greater the external magnetic field, the more such structures, and consequently, magnetic flux quanta, penetrate into the superconductor. Therefore, the magnetic flux in a superconductor does not change continuously, but in jumps, discretely. Generally, the discreteness of physical quantities manifests itself in the microscopic dimension (for example, the energy of electrons in an atom is quantized). Here we are faced with an amazing phenomenon - the laws of quantum mechanics “work” on a macroscopic scale.

Each structure of magnetic field induction lines in a superconductor is surrounded by continuous continuous currents (see figure 4), which fill the outlet in a liquid or gas. That is why such line structures surrounded by superconducting currents are called Abrikosov outputs. Inside each outlet, superconductivity is dimensional and destroyed. But in the space between the exits it is preserved! Only at very strong fields, when the output becomes so large that they begin to block, does the complete destruction of superconductivity occur.

Sometimes you need a wire that can withstand not only a strong magnetic field, but also a strong electric current. And this, it turns out, is not the same thing.

What is pinning?

It is known that a force acts on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field. Where is the counterforce applied, which arose according to Newton’s third law? If, for example, a magnetic field is created by another conductor with current, then it is acted upon by a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction (the forces of interaction between conductors with current are determined by Ampere’s law).

In our case, the situation is more complicated.

When the superconductor is in a displaced state and current flows through it, then in those areas where there is a magnetic field (vortex cores), interaction forces between the current and the field will arise. As a result, the current distribution changes, but the areas in which the magnetic field is concentrated do not remain stationary, but begin to mix. Abrikosov vortices move under the influence of current!

The force acting on a current in a magnetic field is perpendicular to the inductance of the magnetic field and the direction of the conductor. The force exerted by the current on the Abrikosov vortex is also perpendicular to the inductance of the magnetic field and the direction of the current. If, for example, in a superconductor in a displaced state, shown in figure 5, a current flowing from left to right is created, then the Abrikosov vortices under the influence of the current begin to move up or down (depending on the direction of the magnetic field inductors). But the movement of an apricot vortex through a superconductor is the movement of a normal, non-superconducting core. With such a movement, a kind of friction will arise, which leads to the release of the body. This means that when current flows through a superconductor, which is in a biased state, resistance still appears and such superconductors cannot be used to create solenodes.

What is the solution? It is necessary to allow the vortices to move, to secure them in place.

It turns out that this can be done. You just need to “spoil” the superconductor and create some defects in it. Defects usually occur spontaneously as a result of mechanical or thermal processing of materials. figure 6, for example, shows an electron microsopper photograph of a film of nubium nitride (the critical temperature of which is 15 K), obtained by depositing the metal on a glass plate. The granular (grainy) structure of the materials is clearly visible. It is quite difficult for the vortex to cross the grain boundary. That is why until the value of the current is determined (it is called the critical current), the vortices remain motionless. Electrical resistance in this case is zero.

This phenomenon is called pinning - from the English word pinning, which translated into Russian means pinning.

Thanks to pinning, it is possible to obtain superconducting materials with a high value of both the critical field and the critical current. Moreover, if the value of the critical field is determined by the properties of the materials themselves, then the value of the critical current (more precisely, its density, that is, the current strength per unit area of ​​the section) largely depends on the method of preparation and processing methods of the materials. Technology has now been developed that allows superconducting materials to have high values ​​of all critical parameters.
