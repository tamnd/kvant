Geometric motifs are present in the paintings of great painters. Geometric schemes with greater or lesser order are viewed in the composition itself, which is a lot of trouble. They can be called pyramidal, circular, diagonal, spiral, etc., depending on the geometric figure that is the basis of the composition. In this case, the artist often acts intuitively, and artificially, exploring the composition, reveals its basis, and brings the picture to a simplified geometric scheme. Our note is an example of such research regarding the artistic heritage of the great Raphael Santi, whose 500th anniversary was celebrated last year.
To proceed further we will need the legacy of the great Raphael Santi, whose 500th birthday was celebrated last year.

Two geometric designs

Even the ancients introduced the golden ratio into consideration - such a division (“section”) of a given segment of length $a$ into two unequal parts $x$ and $y$, $a=x+y$, $x>y$, in which the changing part relates to the larger one in the same way as the larger part relates to the whole, that is, $x:y=x:a$. From this definition it follows

$$\frac{a-x}{x} = \frac{x}{a}$$

or $x^2+ax-a^2=0$. Solving this quadratic equation and discarding the negative root, we find

$$\frac{x}{a} = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2} \approx 0,62.$$

This proportion is often used in ancient Greek architecture, for example in the construction of the famous Parthenon. Architects understood that in visual perception a rectangle, the ratio of its sides

On the contrary, the suppression of dynamics and volition is manifested most strongly in another simple geometric figure - a spiral. A spiral is a flat line formed by a moving point, which moves away according to a certain law from the beginning of a ray that rotates uniformly around its origin. If the point moves away uniformly from the origin ($r=k\varphi$, where $r$ is the distance from the origin, and $\varphi$ is the angle of rotation of the ray), then an Archimedes spiral is obtained. If the point is removed according to the exponential law ($r=ke^{m\varphi}$, figure 1), then a logarithmic spiral is obtained. A special case of a logarithmic spiral is the golden spiral of interest to us: it is obtained if the beginning of the ray and the parameters $k$ and $m$ of the exponential law are chosen so that the spiral passes through three of the four vertices of each of the “golden rectangles” successively constructed on figure 1.

Problem. Find the parameters $k$ and $m$ for the golden spiral.

"Ship Rose"

In this significant painting by I. I. Shishkin (figure 2), the motifs of the golden ratio are clearly visible. It divides the right side of the picture horizontally according to the golden ratio. To the left of the main pine there are many pines - if you wish, you can successfully continue dividing the picture according to the golden ratio further. "Massacre of the Babies"

The multi-figure composition (see figure on p. 15), executed in 1509-1510 by Raphael, when the famous painter created his frescoes in the Vatican, is precisely distinguished by the dynamism and drama of the plot. Raphael never brought his plan to completion, however, his sketch was engraved by the famous Italian graphic artist Marcantono Raimondi, who, based on this sketch, created the engraving “Massacre of the Innocents” (figure 3).

In Raphael's preparatory sketch, we drew red lines from the semantic center of the composition - the point where the warrior's fingers curled around the child's ankle - along the figures of the child, the woman holding him close, the warrior with the suspended ball, and then along the figures of the same group on the right side of the sketch. If you naturally connect these pieces with a curved dotted line, then with very great accuracy you get... a golden spiral! This can be checked by changing the ratio of the lengths of the segments cut by a spiral on straight lines, passing through the beginning of the curve.

We do not know whether Raphael actually drew the golden spiral when creating the composition “Massacre of the Innocents” or only “felt” it. However, it can be said with certainty that the engraver Raimondi saw this spiral. This is evidenced by the new elements of the composition he added, emphasizing the reversal of the spiral in those places where it is only indicated by a dotted line. These elements can be seen in Raimondi's final gravure: the arch of the place, strangling from the woman's head, is on the left side of the composition and the reclining body of the child is in its center.
