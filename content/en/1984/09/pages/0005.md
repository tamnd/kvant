Superconducting magnets

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. G. ASLAMAZOV

Strong magnetic fields can be produced by passing a strong current through a coil. The more current flows through the coil, the greater the magnetic field it creates. But if the coil has electrical resistance, then a tello is released in it. You have to spend enormous energy to maintain the current, and gray problems arise related to the tapping of the body (initially the coil may melt). Thus, in 1937, a magnetic field with an induction of 10 Tesla was first obtained. But it was possible to maintain this field only at night, when other consumers of the power plant supplying current to the winding were turned off. The released body was removed with running water, and at the same time, every second 5 liters of water were brought to a boil.

This is precisely what limits the possibility of obtaining strong magnetic fields in conventional solenodes.

The idea of ​​using superconductivity to create strong magnetic fields arose immediately after its discovery. It would seem that all that is required is to wind a coil of superconducting wire, close its ends and send a sufficiently strong current through such a circuit. Since the electrical resistance of the coil is zero, the body does not release. And although cooling the solenoid to liquid gel temperatures at which superconductivity occurs creates certain difficulties, the advantages would outweigh the disadvantages if... the magnetic field itself did not destroy superconductivity.

Discovery of superconductivity

figure 1 shows a diagram of the Kamerling-Onnes experiment, which was done in 1911 in Leiden. Dutch scientists made a coil of lead, connected it to an EMF source and, placing the coil in liquid gel, cooled it to the boiling point of the gel (4.2 K). At the same time, the electronic resistance of lead disappeared - it went into a superconducting state. Then they changed the position of the key and short-circuited the coil - an undamped superconducting current began to circulate through it.

This current creates a magnetic field, the induction of which is proportional to the strength of the current. It would seem that the stronger the current in the coil, the greater the magnetic field that can be obtained in this way. The result, however, turned out to be disappointing: when a field of several fractions of a Tesla was inducted, the solenoid returned to its normal state (it developed resistance). They tried to make coils from other superconductors, but even in them, the destruction of superconductivity occurred at relatively low magnetic fields. What's the matter?

Meissner effect

The solution to this “inconvenient” behavior of superconductors was found in 1933 in Berlin in the laboratory of W. Meissner. It seemed that the superconductor had the ability to completely displace the magnetic field; in it only the magnetic field induction is zero.
