gathered in two university classrooms - the Bolshoi Physics (schoolchildren) and the Bolshoi Chemistry (Rabotakovites). Everyone was given sheets of paper with a task tray on each. Whether they were the same for everyone or whether there were some options, I don’t know. Each one was the same. Each person, having solved the problem, wrote his name on a piece of paper and left, placing the solved problem on the table at the bottom of the classroom. A total of about 600 people took part in this tour. About 100 of them were invited to the third round.

For the time remaining until the third round (approximately a month), we were given new collections of interesting works, published in a literary manner. They were much more difficult than the previous ones, and we, forgetting everything in the world, were busy solving them. In the same place we were invited to the university to listen to lectures on mathematics. Ya. S. Uflyand remembers a lecture by G. M. Fikhtengolts, I remember on algebra by V. A. Kretschmar and two lectures on geometry by B. N. Delaunay. On one of them, using an epidan (or film projector?), B. N. Delaunay demonstrated three-dimensional projections of a four-dimensional cube on the screen. K.V. Tagantsev recalls that Delaunay came to this lecture with a backpack from which he had embroidered three-dimensional projections of some four-dimensional figures (maybe a cube?). In his lectures, delivered with a brilliance we have never seen before, Delaunay seemed to open the door to the world of the mysteries and beauties of mathematics.

The third round of the Olympiad took place in the main building of the university, in several classrooms at the same time. Participants in this tour entered their names and corresponding classroom numbers on lists that were posted on the doors of the main entrance to the university. Each auditorium had two projectors. In the one I ended up in, there was Delaunay himself and the projector V.A. Kretschmar. In case I couldn’t cope and needed to run away, I stayed a couple closer to the door.

Each of us received a sheet of paper with his last name at the top, and two problems - one geometric, the other algebraic (this was the case for me; others, I thought, might have had problems in trigonometry). As we dealt with our task, I projected my own projector with us. Let me tell you that since no Olympics exist yet, I and others didn’t think about it at all. It’s just very interesting to read this test of your abilities.

The winners of the Olympiad became the same person and received bonuses. A miraculously preserved expression from the newspaper “Evening Leningrad” of that time reports that the winners were: Ananov (school 23 of the Central district), Bogomolov and Wallander (school 2 of the Narva district), Georg and Kondrashev (worker of the University), Kizewalter (worker of the Hydrotechnical Institute), Kasatkin (worker of the Electrical Engineering Institute), Mintzberg (school 15 of the Smolensk region), Olovyanshinkov (factory “Red Chemist”), Sanov (school 7 of the Volodarsky district) and Tagantsev (Hydrotechnical Institute). Of those awarded, I remembered only Lieberman, Uflyand and Smirnov.

A few days later, the winners and prize recipients were invited to the hall of the University Academic Council, where the first was presented with a gift - a briefcase with a metal strip on which was engraved: “Winner of the first mathematical Olympiad”, as well as several books - textbooks on various sections of higher mathematics, also with corresponding books. The winners were selected, and their portraits were hung in the Institute of Mathematics of Leningrad University.

Most of the winners and prize recipients entered the Faculty of Mathematics and Mechanics of the university, others - the Faculty of Physics, Chemistry, and Geology. The “pure mathematics” group had very strong students. The “spirit of the Olympiad” reigned in it for a long time - in the first place, between lectures and classes, we crowded around the doxies and worked on solving difficult problems. In such an environment, everyone grew quickly. Some of us received our scientific results as students.

Regardless of what scientific or administrative ranks the winners and prime ministers of the first Olympiad achieved in their careers, I know well that they all worked and are still working in their chosen field with constant enthusiasm.

I wanted to remember those whose lives were cut short by the war. Sergei Olovyanshinkov came to the Olympiad and to the university out of volition, in addition to school and work. Already as a student, his true mathematical talent became apparent. There is “Olovyanshinkov’s theorem”, which is included in a number of functional results of geometry. After graduating from university in 1941, he immediately went to the front. I witnessed how, after being wounded in the poisoning battalion, Sergei showed the result of his new work to A.D. Aleksadrov*! The work remained unfinished. Soon after the quarrel, they returned to the active army, Sergei Olovyanshinkov died on the banks of the Neva.

Another participant in the Olympiad, Joseph Lieberman (he was among those awarded) completed his second year of graduate school in June 1941. In the summer of 1941, in the uniform of a naval officer, he defended his thesis, and in the fall he died, wandering into the city of Tallinn.

Olympiad winner Sasha Smirnov, who graduated from the Faculty of Mathematics and Mechanics with a degree in control theory, also died in the war.

It seemed interesting to me that four of those awarded at the Olympiad the year before had together graduated from the same technical school (by modern standards, vocational school) at the Precision Engineering Plant (now the Printing Machines Plant). These are: Aleksadrova, Kiesewalter, Kondrashev and Uflyand.
