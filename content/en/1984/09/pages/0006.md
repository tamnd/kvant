According to the law of electromagnetic induction, steep points (figure 2) will appear on the surface of the cylinder, which create a magnetic field in the cylinder with induction $\vec{B}_{\text{ток}}$, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the induction of the external field. These points are superconducting and will not fade. Therefore, in the thickness of the superconductor, the total induction is zero: $\vec{B} = \vec{B}_{\text{внешш}} + \vec{B}_{\text{ток}} = 0$. Magnetic field lines in a superconductor do not peak.

Well, what if we change the sequence of operations - first place the metal in an external magnetic field, and then cool it to a superconducting state. It would seem that the magnetic field induction does not change and there is no reason for the appearance of shielding surface points. This is exactly what Meissner thought when he checked Laus’s calculations; this refers to the first method of testing an experiment.*) I thought about it, but still decided to check it. The result of the modified experiment was surprising. It turned out that in this case, too, the magnetic field completely flows out of the superconductor and does not penetrate into it.

This phenomenon was called the Meissner effect.

It is now clear why a magnetic field destroys superconductivity. After all, a certain amount of energy is spent on the appearance of surface points. In this sense, the superconducting state is not
no better than the normal state, when the magnetic field penetrates the metal and there are no shielding surface points. The greater the external field induction, the stronger the current must flow across the surface to provide shielding. At a certain value of magnetic field induction, superconductivity is necessarily destroyed, and the metal goes into the normal state. The field at which superconductivity is destroyed is called the critical field of the superconductor. It is important to remember that an external magnetic field is not necessary to destroy superconductivity. The current flowing through the superconductor itself creates a magnetic field. When, at a certain current value, the induction of this field reaches a value corresponding to the critical field, superconductivity is destroyed.

The magnitude of the critical field increases with decreasing temperature, but even at absolute zero, the critical field in pure superconducting metals is not large (see graph on figure 3). In the best cases, it is only ten parts of a teska. So, it would seem, there is nothing to think about creating strong magnetic fields using superconductors.

But further research into superconductivity showed that there is a way out. It was discovered that there is a whole group of superconductors that retain superconductivity even in very strong magnetic fields, albeit in a somewhat weakened form.
