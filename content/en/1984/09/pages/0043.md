(Instead of the sum of pairwise distances, as the “degree of sparsity” of the distribution, you can also take the sum of their squares, or the sum of the squares of the room numbers of all pianists.)

Let us now prove that the distribution between rooms cannot become too “sparse”, that is, there are two numbers $m$ and $M$, $m < M$ such that pianists will always remain in rooms with numbers from $m$ to $M$.

To do this, note that if on some day at least one pianist lives in three adjacent rooms (with numbers $k-1$, $k$ and $k+1$), then in the future at least one of them will always be occupied. (In fact, one can leave these rooms only by moving from the $(k+1)$th to the $(k+2)$th or from the $(k-1)$th to the $(k-2)$th room; but both of these are possible only if a pianist lived in the $k$th room, moving, respectively, to the $(k-1)$th or $(k+1)$th room.) Consequently, a pianist from the $k$th room cannot get into a room with a number greater than $k+3N$, where $N$ is the number of pianists (after such a move, in each of the $N+1$ tracks of rooms with numbers $(k, k+1, k+2)$, ..., $(k+3N, k+3N+1, k+3N+2)$ must remain pianist 们); for the same reason, it is impossible to move from the $k$th room to the $(k-3N)$th room. So, pianists will always stay in rooms numbered $a-3N$ to $b+3N$, where $a$ and $b$ ($a \leq b$) are the numbers of the outermost rooms originally occupied by pianists.

It follows from this that the sum $s(t)$ of pairwise distances between pianists cannot increase indefinitely, and by virtue of (*) it follows that migrations will stop after a finite number of days.

F878. The two main gutters, forming equal angles $a$ with the horizon, are located in the same vertical plane (Fig. 1). From point $A$ of one zone and from point $B$ of another zone, simultaneously slide two bodies together without initial velocities. The time of movement to the horizontal plane of the first body (from point $A$) is $t_1$, the second is $t_2$. How long after the body began to move was the distance between them the smallest?

When bodies move, the difference in heights at which they will be at the same moment in time will always be the same and equal (see Fig. 1) $\Delta h = h_1 - h_2$. Consequently, the distance between the bodies will be minimal when they are on the same vertical, that is, the horizontal distance between the bodies will be zero. Since the initial velocities of both bodies are equal to zero and the angles of inclination of their trajectories to the horizon are the same, it is clear that the bodies appear on the same vertical at the moment when, having traveled the same paths, the bodies shift along each vertical by $\Delta h/2$. So the time $t_{\min}$, through which the distance between the bodies will be minimal, is the time during which the body from the initial point will shift in height by $\Delta h/2$, that is

$$\frac{g' t_{\min}^2}{2} = \frac{\Delta h}{2} \Rightarrow t_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{\Delta h}{g'}}$$
