Trajectory, path, movement

Three words in the title of this note are associated with the movement of bodies (material points). The first two shrikas are used in everyday life, the third is used mainly in the science of the movement of bodies - mechanics.

A trajectory is a continuous line described by a moving point. It can be straight lines. A body (point) moving along such a trajectory is said to be in linear motion. The trajectory can be a curved line of any shape. A body moving along such a trajectory coincides with curvilinear motion. For example, the trajectory of a freely falling body in relation to the Earth (if we neglect its rotation) is a vertical straight line, and the trajectory of an artificial Earth satellite is a curve (circle or ellipse).

If the trajectory is known in advance, then the task of mechanics may be, say, determining the point on the trajectory where the moving body is located at one time or another. The trajectory may be unknown, then the task is to find the type of trajectory. The laws of motion allow us to solve both problems.

The path traveled by a body (point) is the length of its trajectory (measured in meters, kilometers, etc.). It can be determined, for example, by the kilometer counter in the automation.

To solve problems in mechanics, knowing the distance traveled is usually not enough, and here’s why. The phenomenon of body movement is that the coordinates of the body change over time. The path with a change in communication coordinates is not always the same. It may even be that the distance traveled is not zero, and the change in coordinates is zero (the body has passed along a closed path). That is why the path, as a rule, is not included in the mechanics equation.

To describe the movement of a body (point) in mechanics (more precisely, in kinematics), another physical quantity is used - displacement. Let us recall that displacement is a directed line segment (vector) connecting some initial position of the point’s movement with some subsequent position (“Physics 8”, §3).

The importance of this particular quantity for mechanics follows from the fact that the projections of the displacement vector on the coordinate axes are equal to the change in the corresponding coordinates (“Physics 8”, §5). This means that if the displacement vector is known, the coordinates of the body can also be found. True, for this you also need to know the initial coordinates.

The question arises: aren’t the other modules of the displacement vector and the distance traveled (both are scalars) equal? It turns out that in the general case - no, they are not equal.
