⟦folio 21⟧

Almost all rays go to one point - the size of the object’s scattering circle is usually on the order of 20 microns. However, if the screen is displaced by amounts $a$ from point $B$, a circle of size $a \times g$ is formed on it, where $a$ is the angle corresponding to the extreme rays passing through the lens. The dependence of the size of this circle on the angle, and therefore on the diameter of the lens, is used by photographs to obtain photographs with a large depth of field.

Focusing the camera involves moving the lens relative to the film (which in this case plays the role of a screen on figure 3). The lens is placed in such a position that the image of the photographed object is located exactly in the plane of the film. In this case, obviously, images of objects located further from the photographed object appear in front of the film, and images of objects located in front of it appear behind the film. Individual small elements of these objects create illuminated circles on the film, the diameter of which is proportional to the magnitude of the object's diff. Since the quality of a photographic image is also determined by the grain of the film, images of objects will not be degraded compared to the case of precise focusing if the size of the scattering circle does not exceed the radius of the film grain. And this can be achieved by appropriately selecting the diameter of the object. This is why, when photographing scenes containing objects that are far apart from each other (along the optical axis), or, as they say, having great depth, it is necessary to reduce the diffraction. Let us confirm this with an experiment.

Everyday routine 2. Take a camera, preferably with a mirror viewfinder (for example, “Zenith”): Draw several horizontal and vertical lines on a white sheet, mount the sheet on the wall and illuminate it with a lamp.

From a distance of about 1 m, focus the camera with the lens lens fully open, move it away from the first position by 20-30 cm so that the image becomes blurry, and stop the lens. The depth of field will increase and the image will become clear again.

Open the diffuser again to its maximum value. Attach a cardboard screen to the lens in the form of a slot about 3 cm and 2-3 mm wide. If you orient the slit horizontally, the image of the horizontal lines becomes sharp, while the vertical lines remain blurred; if the click is vertical, vertical lines will be clearly visible. The photograph reproduced on figure 4 illustrates this fact (on the left is an undistorted object, and on the right is its view through a horizontal lens).
