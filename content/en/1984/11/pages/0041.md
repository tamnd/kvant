M877. From a sheet of dumpling paper measuring 29 × 29 squares, 99 squares measuring 2 × 2 each were cut out. Prove that you can cut another square from it.

On figure there are 100 2×2 squares shaded in grey. By cutting out any 2x2 square from the original 29x29 square, we “touch” exactly one square. Therefore, after 99 such operations, one of the gray squares will remain untouched.

S. V. Fomin

M878. Prove that if the sum of the plane angles at the top of the paramda is greater than 180°, then each side edge of the paramda is less than the half-perimeter of its base.

Let $SA_1 \dots A_n$ be a given paramid. Let's cut its lateral surface along edge $SA_1$ and turn it onto a plane (see figure, on which $SA_1$ and $SA_1'$ are two copies of edge $SA_1$ formed as a result of cutting, so $|SA_1| = |SA_1'|$). From the conditions of the problem it follows that the point $S$ lies inside the polygon $A_1 \dots A_nA_1'$. Continuing the segment $A_1'$ $S$ beyond the point $S$ to the intersection with the broken line $A_1 \dots A_nA_1'$, we get the point $B$, which splits the broken line into two parts: the broken line $A_1 \dots B$ of length $a$ and the broken line $A_1' \dots B$ of length $b$. From inequalities $|SA_1| < a + |SB|$, $|A_1'S| + |SB| = |A_1'B| < b$ it follows that $2|SA_1| = |SA_1| + |SA_1'| < a + |SB| + |A_1'S| = a + b = |A_1A_2| + ... + |A_{n-1}A_n| + |A_nA_1'|$.

It remains to note that on the right side there is a stoppermeter of the base of the paramid.

Yu. I. Ionin

M880*. In the sequence 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, ... each term, starting from the seventh, is equal to the last digit of the sum of the previous ones. Prove that this sequence does not contain six numbers in a row: 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1.

This problem can be schematically represented as follows: given a transformation of a certain set (a set of sixes of numbers; the six $(x_1, \dots, x_6)$ is associated with $(x_2, \dots, x_6, x_7)$, where $x_7$ is the last digit of the sum $x_1 + \dots + x_6$); it is required to prove that by repeating this transformation, from one given “point” of the set (six $(1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0)$) it is impossible to obtain another (six $(0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1)$). Very often, such solution problems can be solved by finding an invariant, that is, a value that does not change under a given transformation: it is enough to establish that the value of the invariant for two given “points” is different *).

In our case, the last digit of the number $s(x_1, \dots, x_6) = 2x_1 + 4x_2 + 6x_3 + 8x_4 + 10x_5 + 12x_6$ can serve as such a value. In fact, a simple calculation shows that the difference $s(x_2, \dots, x_7) - s(x_1, \dots, x_6)$ (hereinafter referred to as $x_1 + \dots + x_6$) is equal to $10x_7 + 2(x_7 - (x_1 + \dots + x_6))$, that is, completely zero.

*) We have talked about invariants more than once: see, for example, the article by A. Khodulev “Dispersal of Physeks” («Kvant», 1982, No. 7, p. 28); There are also links to other articles on this topic. A number of problems on invariants can be found in «Kvant» No. 10 for 1983 on p. 30.
