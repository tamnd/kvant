⟦folio 12⟧

Leibnitz accepts the invitation of Duke Johann Philipp and moves to Mainz. He wants to take advantage of the situation and, albeit within the framework of a fairly modest state, creates his own perfect law. Gradually his plans become more expansive and at the same time... less realistic. He plans to restructure the entire legal science and begins three large monographs. He has quite a few interesting ideas, but soon it will be the turn of a completely different idea.

The famous diplomat Boineburg, who lived in Mainz at that time, captivated Leibnitz with grandiose plans to change European politics. Their plans are cramped in provincial Mainz. They take up the proposal of the Elector of Brandenburg to find a reason for electing a German prince to the Polish representation. Leibnitz composes a brilliant nonsense, which, however, did not help win the case: correct practical diplomacy turned out to be more effective than political pamphlet. The next project concerned the organization of an alliance of German states against France. It contained many clever moves, but it was not possible to implement it. Finally, the third grand plan: to involve France in a war with Turkey in order to free its influence in Europe. To realize this plan, in 1672 Leibnitz went to Paris. The only result was that Leibnitz essentially lost the support of the Elector, who was not very interested in the Soviets, who was trying to restructure European politics over his head.

Left out of work, Leibnitz switches to mathematics. Initially, mathematics was intended to play a supporting role in Leibniz's plans. In 1666, he published a “Dissertation on the Art of Combination” in Leipila, in which he states that he is not interested in the discovery of new arithmetic truths, mathematics should help him develop a “logic of discovery.” And in Mainz it is located for “mathematical access”. In 1670, he worked on the design of an arithmetic machine and became interested in Pascal's machine. Leibniz came to Paris in 1672 with his mathematical results. In the autumn of 1672 they had discussions with Huygens, who was then working in Paris. We were talking about summing the number series $a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n + \dots$. By selecting a sequence $b_1, b_2, b_3, \dots$ such that $a_n = b_n - b_{n+1}$. Then $a_1 + \dots + a_n = b_1 - b_{n+1}$. Leibnitz looks at a number of examples where his rule works; The example proposed by Huygens fits the rule well

$$\frac{1}{1 \cdot 2} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3} + \dots + \frac{1}{n(n+1)} + \dots \left( b_n = \frac{1}{n} \right).$$

Neither of them knew, however, that this technique was not new, and it was a very private matter. Leibniz, however, had a high opinion of his achievements at the time. Later he assessed the situation more soberly:

“When I arrived in Paris in 1672, I was a self-taught mathematician, but my experience was not great, I did not have the patience to achieve the goal of proof... I wanted to swim on my own, without a teacher... In this high mathematical ignorance, I paid attention only to history and law, and saw their study as my goal. However, mathematics was a more enjoyable pastime for me.”

"Smychiy learning from mortals"

In 1673, Leibnitz visited London as part of the Mainz diplomatic mission. Contacts with English mathematicians had a sobering effect on him. He learned that his main results were not new, and modern mathematics was far ahead. Leibniz had only one way to enter modern mathematics - to start all over again. 27 years old is not the most suitable age to start teaching young people, but this does not bother Leibniz. He had every reason to later call himself “a humble student of mortals” (letter from J. Bernoulli, 1703).

In the autumn of 1673, Leibniz’s “years of mathematical apprenticeship” began, skillfully guided by Huygens. Huygens recognized a genuine gift in the self-confident “overgrown man.”

“...Huygens, who, I suppose, considered me more capable than I really was, gave me a copy of the just published “Pendulum.” For me, this was actually or a reason for deeper mathematical studies.”

So, it all started with the great book “Pendulum Clock”. This was followed by a study of the works of St. Vincent, Descartes, Sluz, Wallis and, above all, Pascal.

Leibniz would do well to read more classical texts, but he bargains and makes his way “to geometry ponsteen from the back door.” Results appear that are surprising to Huygens, for example, a series of calculations of a number:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{9} - \dots$$
