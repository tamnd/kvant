The differential dy is determined from the condition $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{l}$, where y is the ordinate, l is the length of the subcaste, dx is an arbitrarily chosen segment. Its “alienation” from the original function and arbitrariness are emphasized (further on at figure it is brought out). It would seem that it could be set equal to 1 and then we would get an arbitrary one, but Leibniz does not establish this. He emphasizes that one can arbitrarily change the choice of an independent variable and its differential, in one phase formulating his undoubtedly hard-won statement about the “invariance of the differential,” that is, in essence, the rules for differentiating a complex function. Newton's independent variable was fixed (time). In further examples, Leibniz demonstrates how useful it was to let the choice of x and dx be arbitrary.
