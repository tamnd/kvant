$$\frac{C_x}{C_x + C_0} = \sqrt[n]{\frac{q_n}{q_0}} = \sqrt[n]{\frac{I_n}{I_0}}, \quad C_x = \frac{C_0}{1 / \sqrt[n]{\frac{I_n}{I_0}} - 1}.$$

where $I_n$ is the drop of the milliammeter needle after $n$ switching of the key $K_2$.

Another interesting solution was found by I. Linduen (Leningrad). He assembled a circuit according to figure 5. The resistance ratio of the resistors was previously determined: $R_2/R_1 = 2$, he charged a capacitor with a capacity of $C_x$ using $n$ connections of a charged capacitor with a capacity of $C_0$. In this case, the number of switching switches $K$ was selected so that when the milliammeter was connected, no current flowed through it. This means that the voltage across the unknown capacitor has reached $U_n = U_0 R_1 / (R_1 + R_2) = U_0 / 3$. It is easy to show that $U_n = U_0 (\alpha^n - 1) / \alpha^n$, where $\alpha = 1 + C_0 / C_x$, hence

$$C_x = \frac{C_0}{\sqrt{3/2} - 1}.$$

The method proposed by Lindwen gives good accuracy only if the resistances of the resistors are determined with a small error. Determining the ratio $R_2/R_1$ using the ratio of currents varied by a milliammeter gives an error of the order of 10%, which leads to an error $\sim 100\%$ when calculating the unknown capacitance $C_x$. It was possible to significantly increase the accuracy of the changes by swapping the resistors and repeating the experiment again.

After $n$ switches of $K_2$ switch in the circuit shown in figure 6, the direction on the unknown capacitor decreases from the value $U_0$ to $U_n$:

$$U_n = \beta^n U_0,$$

From here

$$\beta^n = \frac{U_n}{U_0} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}.$$

Having swapped the resistors, after $n$ switchings, as a result of which the readings of the milliammeter, as in the previous experiment, will be equal to zero, we obtain

$$U_m = \beta^m U_0,$$

where

$$\beta^m = \frac{U_m}{U_0} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}.$$

It's obvious that

$$\beta^n + \beta^m = 1, \beta < 1.$$

Let's solve this equation graphically. To do this, we will plot the dependence $\beta^n(\beta)$ and $1 - \beta^m(\beta)$ (Fig. 7). The intersection point of the graphs gives the value for $\beta$, and therefore for $C_x$:

$$C_x = C_0 \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta}.$$
