8.7. Let us denote by k the weight of the heaviest weight, and by S the number of weights of weight 1. Note that S≥k. Indeed, we have a weight of weight k, S weights of weight 1, and another n−S weights, the weight of each of which is not less than 2. Since the weight of all weights by condition is equal to 2n, the inequality 2n≥k+S+2(n−S) is true, whence S≥k.

After the first weight was placed on the whole, the difference between the weights of the weights on the cups became equal to k. It is clear that in the future the weight of the heavier cup will differ from the weight of the lighter cup by no more than k. In fact, with each subsequent step we place the weight on a lighter cup. If after this it remains lighter, then the difference in weights on the cups has decreased. If after this the lighter cup becomes heavy, but the new difference between the weights does not exceed the weight of the weight placed, and it does not exceed k.

Suppose that we put weights of weight greater than 1 on the scales, and we are left with S weights of weight 1. Since S is greater than or equal to the difference in the weights on the cups, then by putting a certain number of weights of weight 1 on the scales, we will bring them to a state of equilibrium. At this point, the total weight of the weights on the scale will be an even number, so the number of remaining weights of weight 1 will also be an even number. In the future, we must place them alternately on one cup or another. Consequently, due to the even number of remaining weights of weight 1, the scales will eventually be in equilibrium.

Obviously, in the notation of an absolutely prime number, not a single even digit can occur, and if the number of digits in the notation is greater than or equal to, then the digit 5 ​​cannot occur either (if the digits 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 5 were present, then by rearranging it to the last place, we would get the remaining number). Consequently, in the notation of an absolutely prime number only the numbers 1, 3, 7 and 9 can appear. Let us assume that all these digits are present in the notation of some prime number. In this case, by rearranging them, from this number we can obtain the following seventh absolute prime number:

$$M_1=\overline{a_1a_2...a_m1379}=M+1379,$$

where M=a₁a₂...aₘ000,

$$M_2=M+3179, M_3=M+9137, M_4=M+7913, M_5=M+1397, M_6=M+3197, M_7=M+7139.$$

This, however, is impossible, since the numbers 1379, 3179, 9137, 7913, 1397, 3197 and 7139 give different remainders when divided by 7 (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, respectively), and therefore the averages of the numbers M₁, M₂, ..., M₇ are necessarily divisible by 7.
