By the 60s, a large number of different problems had been solved, they learned how to construct tangents to wide classes of curves, and many areas were calculated. Kepler, Cavalieri, Descartes, Fermat, Pascal, Huygens and many others took part in this great work. There is a need to systematize these disparate results and transform individual artificial techniques into general methods. It was necessary to transform the theory of infinitesimals into calculus - a set of simple formal, but widely valid, sieves. It was necessary to transform theory from art into craft. In this form, not only could it be taken out from a narrow circle of initiates, but it would also allow major mathematicians to go part of the way without effort and concentrate their efforts on deeper questions.

This work, building on the achievements of their great predecessors, was carried out by Newton (1642-1727) and Leibniz. “God said: let there be Newton! and the light came,” said A. Popa in the popular couplet. Newton created his calculus in 1665-1667 during his “plague holiday” in Woolsthorpe, when, after graduating from Cambridge University, he found himself cut off from the outside world on his farm due to the plague. Newton's detailed work, almost completed by 1670-1671, did not find a publisher and remained unknown for a long time. Only scattered information about him was distributed among mathematicians. At the same time, observing an unusual decline in mathematics. As N. Bourbaki writes, by the 70s, “only Newton in Cambridge and J. Gregory, who was surprised in Aberdeen, remained active, and Leibniz soon joined with all the enthusiasm of the newly initiated.”

Leibniz and his path in mathematics

Throughout his life, Leibniz was focused on global problems, on comprehensive theories. His path in mathematics was not standard - he preferred the century method, when more

valued concrete results. Leibniz had many plans in her life. Some are striking in their severity. New notes supplanted the old ones, but before we got carried away, the author lacked implementation. He did not finish almost any of the books he planned, and left most of them at the very beginning (only a few books on philosophy achieved the best part). But how difficult it is to maintain realism when the plans of the distant overtake the century!

Already from the age of 13-14, Leibniz dreamed of redefining logic, of creating an alphabet of human thoughts, in which all thought processes could be recorded. The main idea of ​​his life gradually matures: the creation of a “universal characteristic”, a “universal language”. “Universal mathematics is, so to speak, the logic of the imagination,” it must occupy everything “that in the field of imagination is supported by a precise definition.” Language must be protected from recording incorrect thoughts: “chimeras that even the one who creates them does not understand cannot be written in his signs.” He talks about a machine that will prove theorems, wants to turn thinking into calculus, argue it so that it is possible to replace reasoning with calculations and resolve disputes using mathematical calculations. Leibniz was present once upon a time to realize his ambitious plan, which was far ahead of its time, but remained behind, having completed only the first steps. Only in the 20th century did much of what Leibniz conceived turn out to be a reality within the framework of mathematical logic and modern computer technology. It became clear that his plans were not commonplace, but spontaneous.

Leibniz is interested in the varied applications of mathematics and believes in its limitless possibilities. He is preparing to become a lawyer and at the age of 18 he is trying to build a legal theory with axioms and theorems, thinking about the use of probabilistic messages in daily production. At the age of 20, he refused the chair at the University of Nuremberg; he was not attracted by a capable academic career. Leibniz's plans are more natural:

“For a long time in my soul I thought something else” and further “I considered it unworthy for a young man to sit as if glued to the place; my spirit was burning with the desire to acquire great scientific service and look into the world.”
