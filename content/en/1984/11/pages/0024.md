For lines parallel to the slit, the depth of field turns out to be large (the magnitude of the diffram for them is determined by the width of the slit), and for lines perpendicular to the slit, it is small (the magnitude of the diffram for them is determined by the length of the slit).

As we have already said, if a source sends light in all directions, the size of the circle of dispersion on the screen is determined by the diameter of the lens diffram. If the source emits a narrow beam, changes in the diffram do not play a role and the diameter of the circle is entirely determined by the beam itself.

Let us carry out an experiment, the purpose of which is to study the dependence of image sharpness on the divergence of the illuminating beam. We will need camera objects, two flashlights and two colored pieces of glass, for example red and blue. Handle the object carefully to avoid damaging the lens surfaces.

A regular sheet of black paper and use a razor to cut a thin cross in it. Place this sheet in front of the object, and attach another sheet of paper - the screen - behind the object. Light the cross with a flashlight. By moving both sheets and the lens relative to each other along the optical axis, add a position where the image of the cross on the screen will be clearest. Make sure that the image will remain clear enough when moving the screen in some area along the optical axis.

Now direct the light from two flashlights to the cross, aligning the red glass with one of them and the blue glass with the other (Fig. 5). Red rays incident at an angle β form a red image of a cross, and blue rays incident at an angle β form a blue image. These images are superimposed on each other only in one screen position (Fig. 6, a). Screen displacement causes the image to shift transversely relative to the optical axis - a colored outline of the image appears on the screen (Fig. 6, b). However, both red and blue images remain clear. Remove the colored glass - the image of the cross becomes wireless and less clear.

The explanation for the experience is as follows. Both red and blue images are formed by narrow beams. Therefore, the diameter of the scattering circle on the screen created by the light of each point is small. Due to the tilt of the beam to the axis, the center of the circle, and therefore each single-light image, is shifted to the side, but remains quite sharp. If you remove the colored glass, then the white light from the two flashlights seems to form a common beam with an opening angle of 2β, which significantly exceeds the opening angles of the red and blue beams. Therefore, if you sharply increase the radius of the circle on the screen, the image becomes less clear.

We have told only about a few experiments with the object, which were carried out interestingly and usefully. Of course, their number can be increased at your discretion.
