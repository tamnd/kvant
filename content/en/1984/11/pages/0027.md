The acceleration of a body moving under the action of the elastic force of a spring is measured. Then bodies with different, but also known, masses are attached to the same and similarly deformed spring (so that the force remains the same) and their accelerations are measured again.

Since the force applied to different bodies is the same, then the changes should result in a certain value associated with acceleration that is the same for all bodies. It must be equated to the force applied to all bodies (“Physics 8”, § 25). The described experiments show that the product $ma$ turns out to be the same value for all bodies. Means,

$$F = ma.$$

Both methods give, as we see, the same result. They are equivalent to each other, because it makes no difference whether different forces act on the same body or the same force acts on different bodies. In the Physics 8 textbook, Newton's second law is "discovered" using the second method, perhaps because it is easier to implement. The fact is that the first method assumes that a certain stage of force has been selected - some kind of somehow deformed spring. The second method assumes that a certain mass stage is selected. In physics and technology they followed exactly this path - an essentially international stage of mass in the form of a platinum-day cylinder. The standard of mass is easier to store and reproduce than the standard of strength.

Thus, the fundamental law of dynamics - Newton's second law - is a purely experimental law, however, like any other law of nature. At the same time, the formula $F = ma$ of the second law can also serve as a definition of force as a physical quantity: force is a physical quantity equal to the product of the mass of the body to which it is applied and its acceleration.

This is not the only case when the same formula expresses a certain law of nature and at the same time serves as a definition of a certain physical quantity.

Gas turns into liquid
(From the history of physics)

For a long time, the only gas known to science was atmospheric air, which was also considered something like an element, an integral part of everything essential. And only in the second half of the 18th century, through the efforts of many chemical scientists, it was established that there are other gases, that the air itself is a mixture of gases that are different in nature and properties. However, the word “gas” was not in use then. What we now call gas was then called “air.” Hydrogen is flammable air, oxygen is fiery air, nitrogen is suffocating air, aminak is alkaline air, etc.

But at the end of the 18th century, Khimki, and above all the French chemist Antoine Lavouzier, managed to understand their new gas “economy”. Lavouzier gave names to all 20 gases known at that time. He proposed the word “gas” as a general name for this entire class of substances. Lavouzier was the first to introduce the concept of aggregate states of matter.

How to turn gas into liquid? On the possibility of transforming gas into liquid, Lavouzier expressed the following idea: “... If we could place the Earth in some very cold region, for example, in the atmosphere of Jupiter or Saturn, then... the air, or at least some of its components, would cease to be invisible and turn into liquid. A transformation of this kind would open up the possibility of obtaining new liquids, about which we still have no idea.”

This was proof of an outstanding chemist, proof of other things. But there was another possibility.
