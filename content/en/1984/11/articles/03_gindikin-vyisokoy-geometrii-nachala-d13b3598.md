Leibniz and his path in mathematics

Throughout his life, Leibniz was focused on global problems, on comprehensive theories. His path in mathematics was not standard - he preferred the century method, when more

valued concrete results. Leibniz had many plans in her life. Some are striking in their severity. New notes supplanted the old ones, but before we got carried away, the author lacked implementation. He did not finish almost any of the books he planned, and left most of them at the very beginning (only a few books on philosophy achieved the best part). But how difficult it is to maintain realism when the plans of the distant overtake the century!

Already from the age of 13-14, Leibniz dreamed of redefining logic, of creating an alphabet of human thoughts, in which all thought processes could be recorded. The main idea of ​​his life gradually matures: the creation of a “universal characteristic”, a “universal language”. “Universal mathematics is, so to speak, the logic of the imagination,” it must occupy everything “that in the field of imagination is supported by a precise definition.” Language must be protected from recording incorrect thoughts: “chimeras that even the one who creates them does not understand cannot be written in his signs.” He talks about a machine that will prove theorems, wants to turn thinking into calculus, argue it so that it is possible to replace reasoning with calculations and resolve disputes using mathematical calculations. Leibniz was present once upon a time to realize his ambitious plan, which was far ahead of its time, but remained behind, having completed only the first steps. Only in the 20th century did much of what Leibniz conceived turn out to be a reality within the framework of mathematical logic and modern computer technology. It became clear that his plans were not commonplace, but spontaneous.

Leibniz is interested in the varied applications of mathematics and believes in its limitless possibilities. He is preparing to become a lawyer and at the age of 18 he is trying to build a legal theory with axioms and theorems, thinking about the use of probabilistic messages in daily production. At the age of 20, he refused the chair at the University of Nuremberg; he was not attracted by a capable academic career. Leibniz's plans are more natural:

“For a long time in my soul I thought something else” and further “I considered it unworthy for a young man to sit as if glued to the place; my spirit was burning with the desire to acquire great scientific service and look into the world.”

In the autumn of 1672 they had discussions with Huygens, who was then working in Paris. We were talking about summing the number series $a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n + \dots$. By selecting a sequence $b_1, b_2, b_3, \dots$ such that $a_n = b_n - b_{n+1}$. Then $a_1 + \dots + a_n = b_1 - b_{n+1}$. Leibnitz looks at a number of examples where his rule works; The example proposed by Huygens fits the rule well

$$\frac{1}{1 \cdot 2} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3} + \dots + \frac{1}{n(n+1)} + \dots \left( b_n = \frac{1}{n} \right).$$

Neither of them knew, however, that this technique was not new, and it was a very private matter. Leibniz, however, had a high opinion of his achievements at the time. Later he assessed the situation more soberly:

“When I arrived in Paris in 1672, I was a self-taught mathematician, but my experience was not great, I did not have the patience to achieve the goal of proof... However, mathematics was a more enjoyable pastime for me.”

"Smychiy learning from mortals"

In 1673, Leibnitz visited London as part of the Mainz diplomatic mission. Contacts with English mathematicians had a sobering effect on him. He learned that his main results were not new, and modern mathematics was far ahead. Leibniz had only one way to enter modern mathematics - to start all over again. 27 years old is not the most suitable age to start teaching young people, but this does not bother Leibniz. He had every reason to later call himself “a humble student of mortals” (letter from J. Bernoulli, 1703).

In the autumn of 1673, Leibniz’s “years of mathematical apprenticeship” began, skillfully guided by Huygens. Huygens recognized a genuine gift in the self-confident “overgrown man.”

“...Huygens, who, I suppose, considered me more capable than I really was, gave me a copy of the just published “Pendulum.” For me, this was actually or a reason for deeper mathematical studies.”

So, it all started with the great book “Pendulum Clock”. This was followed by a study of the works of St. Vincent, Descartes, Sluz, Wallis and, above all, Pascal.

Leibniz would do well to read more classical texts, but he bargains and makes his way “to geometry ponsteen from the back door.” Results appear that are surprising to Huygens, for example, a series of calculations of a number:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{9} - \dots$$

Leibniz, like no one before him, understands the importance of successful symbolism, and not only in mathematics. The infinitesimal calculus gave him an excellent reason for developing a symbol that not only simplifies the use of calculus, but is essentially necessary for mastering it. In 1678, Leibniz wrote to his colleague Tschirnhaus:

“Care should be taken to ensure that the signs are easy to open. This is achieved to the greatest extent when the signs briefly express and, as it were, reflect the deep nature of a thing, and at the same time the work of thinking is amazingly reduced.”

Leibniz was looking for an opportunity to introduce convenient symbols everywhere. Stop mentioning that the method of solving systems of linear equations using a determinant goes back to him, in connection with which he wrote to L'Hopitalo in France (1693):

“Part of the secret of analysis lies in the art of using the signs used well, and from this small sample you see, sir, that Viete and Descartes have not yet learned all its secrets.”

It should be emphasized that there was no developed symbolism in Newton's calculus. Newton himself wrote that he “did not give his method in the form of symbols, nor adhere to any particular form of them.” Probably, the symbolism was the decisive reason why we use the analogue in Leibniz’s version today.

Already in 1674, Leibniz was confident that “the whole doctrine of sums and squares can be reduced to analysis - a thing that has not yet been focused on.” By the end of 1675, the calculus was constructed as a first approximation. Leibniz will have reason to be convinced of his effectiveness in solving Debon's problem, which Descartes studied, but could not complete:

“Last year I set myself a question that can be answered as one of the most difficult in all geometry, since distance methods so far give almost nothing here. Today I have found its solution and I will give its analysis” (November 11, 1675).

We are talking about finding a curve with a constructed sub-section - a segment between the projection of point A onto the x-axis and the point of intersection of the tangent at point A with the x-axis (Fig. 1). He states that “thanks to this calculation, everything appears to the eyes and in the mind with delightful brevity and clarity.”

Newton and Leibnitz

Leibnitz, of course, heard that Newton owned some powerful methods and decided to discuss his new method with him. Through the mediation of Oldenburg (secretary of the Royal Society), an exchange of letters took place in 1676. Leibnitz lists the problems that he has to solve, asks to tell about Newton's methods, and generally tells about his method. Even earlier, Leibnitz wrote to Oldenburg that the creation of a method is “a single thing” that he attaches importance to. Leibniz's results did not surprise Newton. He immediately noticed that Debon's problem would coincide with the quadrature of the hyperbola (logarithms), and regarding the series for π he noted that it would take 1000 years to count 20 decimal places of π.
