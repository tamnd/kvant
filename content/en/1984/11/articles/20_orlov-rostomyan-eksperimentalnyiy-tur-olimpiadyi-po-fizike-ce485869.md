Teachers and researchers from the Yerevan State University, the Yerevan Physical Institute and the Institute of Physical Research of the Academy of Sciences of the Armenian USSR took part in the preparation of the final type of experimental tasks of the XVIII All-Union Olympiad for schoolchildren in physics. We present the conditions of all experimental tasks and options for their solution proposed by the Olympada participants.

8th grade

Task 1. Determine the ratio of the masses of the vessels.

Equipment: two separate vessels made of the same material (glass), a bucket of water, adhesive tape for marking water levels, a bulb for pouring water.

At the first stage of solving problems, it was necessary to express the ratio of the masses of vessels through the ratio of certain volumes, and at the second stage, it was necessary to find a method for determining this ratio without using a ruler.

We pour such an amount of water into one of the vessels (let’s call it the feather one) that when we lower this vessel into a bucket of water, it sinks to the brim, but does not sink (Fig. 1). In accordance with the floating conditions of bodies, we have

$$m_1 g + q_0 V_0 g = q_0 (V_0 + V_1 + V_c) g,$$

where $m_1$ is the mass of the first vessel, $q_0$ is the density of water, $V_0$ is the volume of water in the vessel, $V_1$ is the volume of the vessel not filled with water, $V_c$ is the volume of glass from which the vessel is made. It follows from this:

$$m_1 = q_0 (V_1 + V_c) = q_0 (V_1 + m_1 / q_c) = \frac{V_1}{1/q_c - 1/q_c},$$

where $q_c$ is the density of the glass. Similarly, for the mass of the second vessel we obtain

$$m_2 = \frac{V_2}{1/q_c - 1/q_c}.$$

Therefore, the mass ratio

$$\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{V_1}{V_2}.$$

The volume ratio $V_1/V_2$ can be determined in different ways. For example, let’s first mark the liquid levels in the vessels with adhesive tape in experiments figure 1, fill the vessel completely with water, and pour water out of the second vessel. Using a bulb, fracture from the first vessel to the second volume $V_1$ and mark the level

water in it. We repeat this transfusion, each time removing a new level, until the second vessel contains the maximum integer number of volumes $V_1$. Let's carry out the same procedure, pouring volumes $V_2$ from the second vessel into the first. Thus, the first vessel will be graded at $V_2$, and the second - at $V_1$. Now, taking a certain volume $n_1V_2$ of liquid from the first vessel, pouring it into an empty second vessel and determining its value on the scale of the second vessel: $n_1V_2=n_2V_1$. Then the required mass ratio

$$\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{n_1}{n_2}.$$

O. Volkov (Gorky), who received the highest mark for solving this problem, defined the ratio $V_2/V_1$ as follows: using a pear, he should pour water into the smaller (first) vessel to the brim (from level $V_0$), and then pour this finished volume of water $V_1$ into the second vessel as many times as necessary until it was completely filled (from level $V_0$). The number of such transfusions is equal to the required ratio $V_2/V_1$.

The absolute winner of the 8th grade Olympiad, M. Sverdlov (Minsk), solved the problem in another way. Lowering first a large vessel into a bucket of water, and then two vessels at once (one inside the other) and holding them in a vertical position, using adhesive tape he removed the volumes of the immersed part of the large vessel (Fig. 2). From the equilibrium equation $m_2=\varrho_0V_2$ and $m_1+m_2=\varrho_0(V_1+V_2)$ we obtain $m_2/m_1=V_2/V_1$. Then, by pouring water into the larger vessel to the upper mark and pouring the volume of water $V_1$ into the empty smaller vessel, the water level in it was adjusted. Next, water from volume $V_2$ was poured in portions of $V_1$ using a smaller vessel with a mark, and these portions were counted.

In all cases, the desired ratio of vessel masses was approximately equal to the third.

The maximum number of points for this task, in addition to the participants named above, was received by A. Kurmic (Riga) and N. Adezaryan (Yerevan).

Task 2. Determine the resistances of three resistors.

Equipment: battery, two resistors with a given resistance $R_1=1$ kOhm and $R_2=1,3$ kOhm, three resistors with unknown resistances $R_3$, $R_4$ and $R_5$, milliampere with unknown measurement limits, connecting wires.

The solution to the problem was essentially reduced to calibrating the milliammeter scale in ohms, connecting it to the circuit shown in figure 3. Using two reference resistances $R_1$ and $R_2$, it was possible to obtain two points on the milliammeter scale. Since the points turned out to be close to each other, the section of the scale adjacent to these points could be considered approximately linear. Using this, it is easy to find the resistance $R_3$ of an unknown resistor by connecting it in series with the first resistor and determining their total resistance $R_1+R_3$:

$$R_1+R_3=1200\text{ Ом}, \Rightarrow R_3=200\text{ Ом}.$$

Resistance $R_4$ is determined in the same way, only this resistor had to be connected in parallel with a resistor with resistance $R_2$ and resistance $R_2R_4/(R_2+R_4)$ was determined:

$$\frac{R_2R_4}{R_2+R_4}=1200\text{ Ом}, \Rightarrow R_4\approx 20\text{ Ом}.$$

Resistance $R_5$ is determined directly using the linearity of the scale around the selected area:

$$R_5=1,5\text{ КОм}.$$

Most of those who solved the problems were successful. Having measured the current strength $I_1$ and $I_2$, corresponding to resistors $R_1$ and $R_2$ (in terms of units), and taking into account that the resistance of the battery and ammeter is much less than the resistance of the eton resistors, I calculate the voltage of the battery (in terms of units), and then, using Ohm’s law, the resistances $R_1+R_3$, $R_2R_4/(R_2+R_4)$ and $R_5$, from which the resistances $R_3$ and $R_4$ were calculated.

The best results in completing this task were shown by N. Adezaryan (Yerevan), N. Matulyonis (Vilnius), G. Nikolashvili (Tbilis), A. Sannikov (Leningrad), M. Sverdlov (Minsk).

9th grade

Task 1. Determine the unknown capacitance of the capacitor.

Equipment: capacitor of unknown capacity $C_x$, capacitor with given capacity $C_0$ ($C_x>C_0=2\text{ мкФ}$), battery, two resistors whose resistance exceeds $10^3\text{ Ом}$, two switches, milliampere and connecting wires.

Most of the participants tried to estimate the unknown capacitance directly by comparing the ballistic throws of the milliampere arrow when connecting capacitors $C_0$ and $C_x$ to the device, charged to the battery voltage. However, due to the fact that the capacitance of the capacitor $C_0$ is small, the deviation of the milliampere needle was so small that the measurement error reached 500%. This decision was not highly regarded.

The circuit shown in figure 4 was drawn up. First, the capacitor $C_x$ was charged to the battery voltage, and the ballistic throw of the arrow $I_0$, corresponding to the charge $q_0$, was measured. Then use switch $K_2$ to charge part

was filmed on a capacitor with capacity $C_0$. It is easy to show that after $n$ switchings a charge $q_n = (C_x / (C_x + C_0))^n q_0$ remains on the unknown capacitor. Where it follows:

$$\frac{C_x}{C_x + C_0} = \sqrt[n]{\frac{q_n}{q_0}} = \sqrt[n]{\frac{I_n}{I_0}}, \quad C_x = \frac{C_0}{1 / \sqrt[n]{\frac{I_n}{I_0}} - 1}.$$

where $I_n$ is the drop of the milliammeter needle after $n$ switching of the key $K_2$.

Another interesting solution was found by I. Linduen (Leningrad). He assembled a circuit according to figure 5. The resistance ratio of the resistors was previously determined: $R_2/R_1 = 2$, he charged a capacitor with a capacity of $C_x$ using $n$ connections of a charged capacitor with a capacity of $C_0$. In this case, the number of switching switches $K$ was selected so that when the milliammeter was connected, no current flowed through it. This means that the voltage across the unknown capacitor has reached $U_n = U_0 R_1 / (R_1 + R_2) = U_0 / 3$. It is easy to show that $U_n = U_0 (\alpha^n - 1) / \alpha^n$, where $\alpha = 1 + C_0 / C_x$, hence

$$C_x = \frac{C_0}{\sqrt{3/2} - 1}.$$

The method proposed by Lindwen gives good accuracy only if the resistances of the resistors are determined with a small error. Determining the ratio $R_2/R_1$ using the ratio of currents varied by a milliammeter gives an error of the order of 10%, which leads to an error $\sim 100\%$ when calculating the unknown capacitance $C_x$. It was possible to significantly increase the accuracy of the changes by swapping the resistors and repeating the experiment again.

After $n$ switches of $K_2$ switch in the circuit shown in figure 6, the direction on the unknown capacitor decreases from the value $U_0$ to $U_n$:

$$U_n = \beta^n U_0,$$

From here

$$\beta^n = \frac{U_n}{U_0} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}.$$

Having swapped the resistors, after $n$ switchings, as a result of which the readings of the milliammeter, as in the previous experiment, will be equal to zero, we obtain

$$U_m = \beta^m U_0,$$

where

$$\beta^m = \frac{U_m}{U_0} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}.$$

It's obvious that

$$\beta^n + \beta^m = 1, \beta < 1.$$

Let's solve this equation graphically. To do this, we will plot the dependence $\beta^n(\beta)$ and $1 - \beta^m(\beta)$ (Fig. 7). The intersection point of the graphs gives the value for $\beta$, and therefore for $C_x$:

$$C_x = C_0 \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta}.$$

And finally, the best solution was proposed by V. Petrov (Moscow). First, the unknown capacitor is charged to the battery voltage $U_0$, and then discharged in series

Task 2. Investigate the experimental oscillations of an eruz suspended on two threads (a pendulum with a bifilar suspension), at a constant value $\gamma$ (Fig. 10): a) find a parameter that uniquely determines the period of oscillation of this eruz by comparison with the oscillations of a mathematical pendulum (eruz on the length of a thread); b) determine the dependence of the period of eruz fluctuations on the found parameter.

Equipment: magnet for bifilar pendants, mathematical pendulum, ruler and graph paper for plotting.

By changing in turn the various parameters ($l_1, l_2, d$) of the bifilar pendulum and adding each time the synchronous oscillation of the bifilar and mathematical pendulums, we can replace that the length $l$ of the mathematical pendulum coincides with the segment $L$ of the vertical drawn from the load to the suspension (see Fig. 10). A series of such optics shows that the parameter $L$ uniquely determines the oscillation period of the bifilar suspension.

Then it was necessary to experimentally find the dependence of the period $T$ of oscillations of the bifilar pendulum on the parameter $L$. Since there was neither a clock nor a stopwatch, a mathematical pendulum had to be used as the standard of time. Without changing the length of the mathematical pendulum ($T_0=$CONST), the period of oscillations of the bifilar pendulum was changed at different values of $L$ in units of $T_0$, for which, for each value $L$ the number of oscillations $N_0$ of the mathematical pendulum for a certain period of time and the number $\Delta N$ of attempts to match the states of oscillations of the general mintniks during this time:

$$\frac{T}{T_0} = \frac{v_0}{v} = \frac{v_0 t}{v t} = \frac{N_0}{N} = \frac{N_0}{N_0 + \Delta N}.$$

By plotting the dependence of $T/T_0$ on $L$ and $(T/T_0)^2$ on $L$, we could be convinced that $T \sim \sqrt{L}$.

10th grade

Task 1. Determine the ratio of the masses of the eruses and the stiffness of the springs.

Equipment: two different springs, two different springs, a tripod.

Since in this work it is not intended to use numerical clocks and rulers, it was necessary to use the natural frequency of one weight suspended on one spring as a reference for determining the natural frequency of the second weight suspended on the second spring:

$$\frac{v_{11}}{v_{22}} = \frac{N_{11}}{N_{22}} = \sqrt{\frac{k_1}{k_2} \frac{m_2}{m_1}},$$

where $k_1$ and $k_2$ are the stiffness of the springs, $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of the springs, $N_{11}$ and $N_{22}$ are the number of oscillations of the first and second oscillatory systems at the same time $t$ (the first number in the index shows the number of the spring, the second - the number of the spring).

To find the original ratios $k_1/k_2$ and $m_1/m_2$ one more equation is needed. It can be obtained by swapping the weights and repeating again:

$$\frac{v_{12}}{v_{21}} = \frac{N_{12}}{N_{21}} = \sqrt{\frac{k_1}{k_2} \frac{m_1}{m_2}}.$$

From the resulting expressions it follows:

$$\frac{k_1}{k_2} = \frac{N_{11} N_{12}}{N_{22} N_{21}}, \frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{N_{12} N_{22}}{N_{21} N_{11}}.$$

Calculate the number of oscillations of one system $N_{11}(N_{12})$ and the number $\Delta N_1(\Delta N_2)$ of coincidences of the phases of oscillations of common systems, we obtain the calculated formulas:

$$\frac{k_1}{k_2} = \frac{N_{11} N_{12}}{(N_{11} + \Delta N_1)(N_{12} + \Delta N_2)},$$

$$\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{N_{12}(N_{11} + \Delta N_1)(N_{12} + \Delta N_2)}{(N_{12} + \Delta N_2)N_{11}},$$

where $\Delta N_1$ and $\Delta N_2$ are equal to ±1, ±2, ±3, ... The choice of the “+” or “-” sign is determined by taking into account the operation or phase lag of the second system from the first by ±2π, ±4π, ±6π, ...

The best solutions were presented by A. Alekseev (Leningrad), A. Deshkovsky (Baranovich), V. Karalnik (Alma-Ata), I. Kurnikov (Gatchina).

Task 2. Determine the experimental position of the main foci of the optical system of two lenses $L_1$ and $L_2$, located at a distance of $L = 10$ cm from each other. Check for this system the applicability of Newton's formula $x_1 x_2 = F^2$, where $x_1$ is the distance from the object do front focus, $x_2$ is the distance from the back focus do image.

Equipment: converging lens $L_1$, diverging lens $L_2$, auxiliary converging lens $L$, battery, light bulb, screen, ruler, magnifying glass for viewing images, graph paper.

The position of the main foci of an optical system can be found by directing a parallel beam of light along the main optical axis of the system. To do this, the light bulbs are installed at the main focus of the auxiliary lens.
