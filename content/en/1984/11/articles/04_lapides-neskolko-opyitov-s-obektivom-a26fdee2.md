Several experiments with object

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. LAPIDES

Probably, each of you has had to deal with a biocle or a projection device, a photo or film camera, a microscope or a telefalcon. The main element of all these optical devices is the lens, which allows one to obtain actual images of various objects.

Modern lenses are very complex in their design, but all of them, as a rule, are a collection of specially similar lenses. So the lens can be considered longer than today's objects.

At the end of the 17th century, the Inland naturalist A. Leeuwenhoek, having polished a piece of glass, made a lens that gave a magnification of 300 times. With its help, Leeuwenhoek was able for the first time to explore the world of ciliates and bacteria, observe the movement of blood in capillaries and much more that could not be seen with the naked eye.

The laws of geometric optics will ensure the ability of a lens to create an image - light rays emitted by any point of an object are refracted in the lens so that they are again collected at a point. However, this does not always happen. Any single lens has a number of disadvantages.

Firstly, rays emitted by a luminous point and falling at small angles to the optical axis of the lens are collected at one point (B), and rays falling at large angles to the axis are collected at another (B′) (Fig. 1). Therefore, if we install a screen in the plane passing through image B, we will see a bright spot around point B. It is often called the circle of dispersion.

Secondly, due to the phenomenon of dispersion, the lens refracts light of different lengths (different colors) differently. This means that rays of different colors are collected after refraction at different points and together with one bright point a spot is observed on the screen, and a colored one at that.

These and other lens errors lead to images of extended objects being distorted, not entirely clear, and sometimes colored.

The nature of the distortion may vary depending on various reasons. But, in any case, the greater the distortion, the greater the angle formed with the optical axis by the rays coming from the object. Let's check this experimentally.

Experiment 1. Take glasses, place them at a distance of 20-30 cm from the eyes and look at some object, for example, a page of text. Mixing the text and glasses relative to the face, add a position in which you see the text enlarged. Note that the letter images near the edges are distorted; in the center the distortions are small (Fig. 2). (Think why people who wear glasses don't notice such distortions?)

Is lens distortion large or small? As always in physics, this question in itself is meaningless. It is necessary to have some “standard” with which to compare the magnitude of distortions. In this case, such a “standard” may be the size of the smallest characteristic area of ​​the image. If it is much larger than the circle of dispersion, then the distortions of the lens should be considered insignificant - they are simply observed and will not be noticed. If the image is such that the size of its small elements is comparable to the diameter of the circle of dispersion, then they will seem blurry and unclear.

Is it possible to correct the situation and reduce image distortion?

It turns out that this can be done by introducing an additional lens along the path of the rays, the distortions of which are opposite in sign to the distortion of the given lens. So, for the lens shown in figure 1, the role of a “corrective” lens will be played by a scattering lens, which resists the central rays more strongly and the peripheral rays less strongly (this is easy to verify by making the corresponding drawing). In modern optical instruments, the number of lenses used for this purpose can reach 8-10 or even more. The resulting lens system is called an object lens. It has the basic property of a single lens - the ability to create an image, but provides a significant reduction in distortion.

Almost all rays go to one point - the size of the object’s scattering circle is usually on the order of 20 microns. However, if the screen is displaced by amounts $a$ from point $B$, a circle of size $a \times g$ is formed on it, where $a$ is the angle corresponding to the extreme rays passing through the lens. The dependence of the size of this circle on the angle, and therefore on the diameter of the lens, is used by photographs to obtain photographs with a large depth of field.

Focusing the camera involves moving the lens relative to the film (which in this case plays the role of a screen on figure 3). The lens is placed in such a position that the image of the photographed object is located exactly in the plane of the film. In this case, obviously, images of objects located further from the photographed object appear in front of the film, and images of objects located in front of it appear behind the film. Individual small elements of these objects create illuminated circles on the film, the diameter of which is proportional to the magnitude of the object's diff. Since the quality of a photographic image is also determined by the grain of the film, images of objects will not be degraded compared to the case of precise focusing if the size of the scattering circle does not exceed the radius of the film grain. And this can be achieved by appropriately selecting the diameter of the object. This is why, when photographing scenes containing objects that are far apart from each other (along the optical axis), or, as they say, having great depth, it is necessary to reduce the diffraction. Let us confirm this with an experiment.

Everyday routine 2. Take a camera, preferably with a mirror viewfinder (for example, “Zenith”): Draw several horizontal and vertical lines on a white sheet, mount the sheet on the wall and illuminate it with a lamp.

From a distance of about 1 m, focus the camera with the lens lens fully open, move it away from the first position by 20-30 cm so that the image becomes blurry, and stop the lens. The depth of field will increase and the image will become clear again.

Open the diffuser again to its maximum value. Attach a cardboard screen to the lens in the form of a slot about 3 cm and 2-3 mm wide. If you orient the slit horizontally, the image of the horizontal lines becomes sharp, while the vertical lines remain blurred; if the click is vertical, vertical lines will be clearly visible. The photograph reproduced on figure 4 illustrates this fact (on the left is an undistorted object, and on the right is its view through a horizontal lens).

This experience can be explained as follows. The introduction of slits changes the shape of the scattering spot - instead of a round one, it becomes a straight lamp, and the length of the side of the straight lamp is oriented

wine parallel to the slit. For lines parallel to the slit, the depth of field turns out to be large (the magnitude of the diffram for them is determined by the width of the slit), and for lines perpendicular to the slit, it is small (the magnitude of the diffram for them is determined by the length of the slit).

As we have already said, if a source sends light in all directions, the size of the circle of dispersion on the screen is determined by the diameter of the lens diffram. If the source emits a narrow beam, changes in the diffram do not play a role and the diameter of the circle is entirely determined by the beam itself.

Let us carry out an experiment, the purpose of which is to study the dependence of image sharpness on the divergence of the illuminating beam. We will need camera objects, two flashlights and two colored pieces of glass, for example red and blue. Handle the object carefully to avoid damaging the lens surfaces.

A regular sheet of black paper and use a razor to cut a thin cross in it. Place this sheet in front of the object, and attach another sheet of paper - the screen - behind the object. Light the cross with a flashlight. By moving both sheets and the lens relative to each other along the optical axis, add a position where the image of the cross on the screen will be clearest. Make sure that the image will remain clear enough when moving the screen in some area along the optical axis.

Now direct the light from two flashlights to the cross, aligning the red glass with one of them and the blue glass with the other (Fig. 5). Red rays incident at an angle β form a red image of a cross, and blue rays incident at an angle β form a blue image. These images are superimposed on each other only in one screen position (Fig. 6, a). Screen displacement causes the image to shift transversely relative to the optical axis - a colored outline of the image appears on the screen (Fig. 6, b). However, both red and blue images remain clear. Remove the colored glass - the image of the cross becomes wireless and less clear.

The explanation for the experience is as follows. Both red and blue images are formed by narrow beams. Therefore, the diameter of the scattering circle on the screen created by the light of each point is small. Due to the tilt of the beam to the axis, the center of the circle, and therefore each single-light image, is shifted to the side, but remains quite sharp. If you remove the colored glass, then the white light from the two flashlights seems to form a common beam with an opening angle of 2β, which significantly exceeds the opening angles of the red and blue beams. Therefore, if you sharply increase the radius of the circle on the screen, the image becomes less clear.

We have told only about a few experiments with the object, which were carried out interestingly and usefully. Of course, their number can be increased at your discretion.
