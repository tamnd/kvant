In conclusion, we invite you to demonstrate an experiment simulating the behavior of a valve in a water tap.

Using a rubber hose, connect a mouthpiece with the dimensions shown in figure 2 to the tap. Open the water, take a three-kopeck coin in your hand and place it on the end of the mouthpiece (with its coat of arms facing the mouthpiece). After some training, you can ensure that the coin is held steadily over a fairly wide range of water flow rates and at any position of the mouthpiece. There are no vibrations observed, and the whole structure resembles a sprinkler for watering vegetable gardens.

Interestingly, a disc the same size as a coin, but with a smooth surface, does not hold on the mouthpiece. But if you press it against the mouthpiece with your finger, vibrations appear, and with increasing pressure, the frequency of vibrations increases. In this way, you can touch the faucet valve model with your hands and make sure that vibrations are really possible.

For the last experiment, it is not necessary to maintain the specified dimensions of the mouthpiece and “flap”. It is only important that their surfaces are flat and smooth. Try to come up with other experiments on your own that simulate the processes that occur in a humming (“noisy”) water supply system.
