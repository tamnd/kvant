⟦folio 16⟧

Lion's Heart (1157-1199). In a number of Italian cities he started harems, which did not suit the “most Christian” emperor. He attracted Arabs and Jews to teach mainly at the University of Naples, along with Christian scholars. Moreover, rumor attributed to him the authorship of the atheistic pamphlet “On the Three Deceivers,” which meant Monsey, Christ and Mohammed.

The knightly tours, so beloved by him, at which the guards mutilated each other for the amusement of the public, were not recognized by Frederick II at all. Instead, he cultivated less bloody modern mathematical cities (exactly a cross between mathematical optimization and KBH), in which opponents exchange not sword blows, but tasks.

It was at such tournaments that the talent of Leonardo Fibonacci shone. This was facilitated by the good education given to his son by the merchant Bonacci, who took him with him to the East and assigned Arab teachers to him. Subsequently, Fibonacci enjoyed the constant patronage of Frederick II. This patronage facilitated Leonardo's further trade trips to northern Africa, Egypt, Syria, and Byzantium, during which he became deeply acquainted with Arab and Greek science. (The works of the classics of ancient science were then known mainly in Arabic translations.) The patronage of Frederick II also stimulated the publication of scientific treatises by Fibonacci: the general “Book of Abacus” (1202, revised and expanded version, 1228), “Practice of Geometry” (1220), “Book of Squares” (1225). Using these books, which surpassed Arabic and medieval European works in their level, mathematics was taught until the time of Descartes (XVII century) and Euler (XVIII century).

The essence of all Leonardo's books is their origin from mathematical tours. The main role in them is played by numerous solutions and commented problems, sometimes those proposed at the tours by Leonardo himself or his main rival, a native of the Silian merchants Johann of Palermo, the court philosopher of Frederick II. Let us also note that the abacus is an ancient counting doxa, something like Russian abacus. However, “The Book of Abacus” is devoted not to the practice of counting, but to theoretical arithmetic in the broad sense of the term. In particular, through this book, Europeans became acquainted with Arabic (more precisely, Indian) numerals and with the modern system of recording numbers. Likewise, “Practice of Geometry” is devoted not to applied (surveying) geometry, but to theoretical geometry.

To characterize Leonardo’s achievement, it is enough to mention two of the problems he solved: to find three numbers x, y and z such that their sum, increased by both x² and x²+y² and x²+y²+z², would be a perfect square (Leonardo’s solution: x=16/5, y=48/5 and z=144/5) and, especially, the problem of solving the cubic equation x³+2x²+10x=20. Leonardo proves that the root of this equation cannot be expressed in square radicals (the first “proof of impossibility” in European mathematics after the ancient theorems about the irrationality of √2 and other numbers), and then calculates the root of the equation with an error not exceeding 0.00000000003 (fantastic accuracy).
