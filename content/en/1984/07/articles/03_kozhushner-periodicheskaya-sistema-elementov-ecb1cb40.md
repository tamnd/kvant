Periodic Table of Elements

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
M. A. KOZHUSHNER

January 27 marked the 150th anniversary of the birth of the great Russian scientist Dmitry Ivanovich Mendeleev, and in February - 115 years of his greatest creation - the periodic table of elements. The story of how Mendeleev went to one of the largest scientific discoveries of the 19th century, how the table was gradually improved, how the elucidation of the complex structure of atoms and the discovery of quantum laws governing the movement of atomic particles finally led to an understanding of the physical basis of the periodic system of elements is extremely interesting and inspiring. Now, when the structure of atoms is already well known to science, in retrospect this story fills a fascinating detective story, when a large army of scientists in different years and in different countries gradually accumulated facts, put forward various hypotheses, most often not true in general, but containing some approximation to the truth - a correct picture of the structure of atoms.

The scientific history of the periodic table of elements can be divided into four stages:

Stage 1 - accumulation of facts about the properties of elements, first attempts to systematize them, creation by D. I. Mendeleev of the first version of the table;

Stage 2 - clarification of the table, filling in empty cells, final confirmation of its truth;

Stage 3 - development of knowledge about the structure of atoms, discovery of quantum mechanics, qualitative explanation of the periodic law;

Stage 4 - creation and development of quantitative methods for calculating the structure of atoms, creation of new elements, expansion of the periodic table.

While still a student at the St. Petersburg Pedagogical Institute, D. I. Mendeleev, who chose chemistry as his main specialty under the influence of his teacher, the great Russian chemist A. A. Voskresensky, thought about the patterns of changes in chemical properties from element to element. At that time, by the middle of the 19th century, a huge amount of factual information about the chemical properties of elements had been accumulated, groups of chemically similar elements were found (silk metals - Li, Na, K, Rb, Zs, alkaline earth metals, halogens), and within these groups a certain pattern was noticed linking the chemical activity of elements with their atomic masses. However, they promised, there was no picture. Attempts were made to somehow classify elements according to the qualitative characteristics of their formation or chemical compounds. But they turned out to be untenable due to too great a variety of such compounds and their properties.

Thus, at the end of the twenties of the 20th century, the periodic table of elements received a final physical explanation. D.I. Mendeleev’s dream came true that “the reason for periodic regularity will be discovered and understood.”

By the thirties, the periodic table contained 88 elements and there were 4 independent cells - with numbers 43, 61, 85 and 87. There are practically no corresponding elements in nature - their atomic nuclei are unstable. Billions of years ago, during the formation of the Earth, they should have existed, but over time, their earthly reserves disappeared as a result of radioactive decay. Of these four elements - technical ($N_{2}43$), promethium ($N_{2}61$) and actate ($N_{2}85$) - were obtained artificially, and the fourth - French ($N_{2}87$) - was discovered during the natural α-decay of actium nuclei. With the discovery of these elements, the periodic table is filled up to uranium ($N_{2}92$).
