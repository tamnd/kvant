“Take a lighter bullet, and your shot will be accurate...” “Uncas, boy, you are wasting gunpowder and pouring too much of it. The gun recoils and the bullet flies poorly... Your bullet hit two inches higher...” These words belong to the hero of Cooper’s novel, the hunter Nathanizl Bumpo. Judging by the novels, he was truly a remarkable hunter. But how accurate was his advice from a physics point of view? Does a lighter bullet and not too much powder really improve the accuracy of the shot? Let's try to understand this issue.

First, it is useful to remember that the shot is always accompanied by separation. If the butt of the gun rests on the shooter’s bullet, then when fired, the gun “pushes” it. An attentive observer will notice that at the same time as the bullet is pushed into the bullet, the arrow is held upward when fired - the gun rotates around the point at which it is fired.

It hits the bullet. This is also explained separately.

To understand how this all happens, turn to figure 1. Section OB is the barrel of the gun, section OA is the butt, at point A the gun rests on the shooter’s bullet. The pressure of the powder gases acts on the bullet moving in the barrel. The gun in the horizontal direction is affected by the same abscylotic magnitude, directed in the opposite direction. The projection fcos α of this force determines the push into the bullet, and the projection fsin α creates a moment that causes the gun to rotate around point A. We will assume that the shooter’s shoulder is motionless (to a first approximation this is true, since the time of the shot

It is clear that the pressure force of the powder gases depends on the amount of gunpowder expelled during a shot. The larger the powder charge, the greater the pressure, the greater the force $f$ (see Figure 1) and the greater the speed the bullet gains while moving in the barrel. On the other hand, the greater the force $f$, the greater the rotational moment caused by its projection $f$ sin $\alpha$, and the greater the deviation of the barrel from the horizontal when fired. We will try to estimate this deviation and discuss how it depends on the mass and speed of the bullet.

We will consider a simplified “model” problem, and our calculations cannot be considered as an exact solution to the problem of the mechanics of a gun when fired. We can judge the degree of correctness of our qualitative reasoning by the reasonableness of the quantitative estimates to which they lead.

To denote approximate equalities we use the symbol $\sim$; this symbol is often used when talking about comparisons by order of magnitude$^*$).

Figures 2 and 3 show the positions of the gun at the initial moment (horizontal barrel) and at the moment the bullet leaves. Point $A$, as mentioned above, can be considered stationary. The center of gravity of the gun is at point $O$. We denote the length of the barrel by the letter $L$, and the distance $AO$ by $l$. The angle between $L$ and $l$ is $\alpha$, the angle of deflection of the barrel at the moment when the bullet leaves the gun barrel is $\varphi$. The average pressure force of the powder gases acting on the bullet is equal to $f$, the bullet moves in the barrel of the gun accelerated, but it is convenient for us to use the average speed of the bullet for assessments; let's denote it $\bar{v}_n$. Shot time $t$ is the time the bullet moves along the barrel; Then

$$\bar{v}_n\tau \sim L.$$  (1)

The rotation of the gun barrel, generally speaking, is also uneven, but you can calculate the average angular velocity $\bar{\omega}$;

$$\bar{\omega} \sim \frac{f \sin \alpha - Mg \cos \alpha}{M},$$  (4)

$$\bar{v}_p \sim \bar{a}_p \tau$$  (5)

(here $M$ is the mass of the gun). You can immediately take advantage of the fact that the typical values ​​of $\alpha$ are not large ($\alpha \leq 2 \cdot 10^{-1}$ rad), and count $\sin \alpha \approx \alpha$, $\cos \alpha \approx 1$. After this, from the formulas (2)–(5) written above it is easy to obtain

$$\varphi \sim \frac{L}{l} \left( a \frac{m}{M} - \frac{g L}{\bar{v}_n^2} \right).$$  (6)

Let's now look at the ascending formula (6). When deducing it, we did not teach that before the shot, somewhere near point $O$, the gun is still held by the hunter’s hand. But we are interested in the upward deflection of the barrel, and if the hunter’s hand does not reach, then in the model situation we are considering we can assume that the gun “teared off” from the supporting hand at the very moment when the trigger was pressed. Therefore, in formulas (4) and (6) only the forces $\vec{f}$ and $M\vec{g}$ are taken into account. The action of force $\vec{f}$ along direction $OA$ (projection $f$ cos $\alpha$) is “extinguished” by the shooter’s shoulder (the latter action on the gun with the same magnitude, but oppositely directed force).

Our next choice is based on the fact that the gun "jumps" upward; this means that the first term in brackets in formula (6) is greater than the second, that is

$$\alpha \frac{m}{M} > \frac{gL}{\bar{v}_n^2}$$

and therefore

$$\bar{v}_n > \left( \frac{MgL}{\alpha m} \right)^{1/2}.$$

Let's now try to make a numerical estimate. Let the mass of the gun be $M \sim 5$ kg, the length of the barrel $L \sim 1$ m, the mass of the bullet $m \sim 10$ g, $\alpha \sim 2 \cdot 10^{-1}$ rad. It is easy to see that inequality (7) leads to the value $\bar{v}_n > 100$ m/s. Real speeds for military weapons are $\sim 600$—800 m/s. Thus, we are convinced that the second term in formula (6) is less than the first, and in order of magnitude

$$\varphi \sim \alpha \frac{m}{M} \frac{L}{l} \sim 0,2 \frac{m}{M} \frac{L}{l}.$$

From (8) it follows that the smaller $\alpha$, the more accurate the shooting. So, is it better if the gun looks like an uncurved stick? Of course not. We must not forget that formula (8) is valid only when the first term in (6) exceeds the second. There is also a purely practical reason - a gun with a very small angle $\alpha$ is very difficult to shoot, it is simply inconvenient to aim with it.

If we take $(m/M) \sim 10^{-3}$ and take into account that $L/l \sim 1$, then for the angle $\varphi$ we get the estimate $\varphi \sim 10^{-3}$ rad. What does this mean? With this value of $\varphi$ at a distance of about 100 m, the bullet will deviate upward from the aiming point by about 10 cm (naturally, if there are no other reasons for misses and if it is distracted for now from the influence of gravity on the flying bullet).

So, as our evaluations have shown, a light steering wheel actually improves shooting accuracy. The deflection of the bullet from the target discussed above does not depend on the mass of the powder charge.

We have discussed so far only the question of how to calculate the deviation of the gun from the aiming line (in our case, from the horizontal direction) while the steering wheel is moving in the barrel bore. It is this deviation that is essential for shooting accuracy. But even after the steering wheel has left the barrel, the gun itself continues to move upward, the speed of the gun’s center of gravity is not zero. Try to estimate for yourself by what additional angle the gun deflects after the rudder leaves the barrel. (We will present a solution to this problem in the next issue of the journal.)

Let us now turn again to the question of bullet speed and the size of the powder charge. We have already said that the more gunpowder a hunter pours, the greater the speed of the bullet. What does this have to do with shooting accuracy? We must also take into account that the rudder deflects downwards during its flight, this is due to the action of gravity. This deviation depends, of course, on the speed of the bullet and the range of the target. If the rudder flies out of the gun horizontally, then its deflection is determined by the formula $gt^2/2$, where $t$ is the flight time. Counting $t \sim (X/\bar{v}_n)$, where $X$ is the target range, estimate the deviation $gt^2/2 \sim gX^2/2\bar{v}_n^2$ at $\bar{v}_n \sim 8 \cdot 10^2$ m/s and $X \sim 100$ m. You will see that the bullet also deflects downwards by several centimeters ($\sim 10$ cm). In principle, the deflection, determined by the angle $\varphi$, and the deflection under the influence of gravity can even compensate each other to one degree or another. It is now understood that the bend of the gun ($\varphi$) is structurally justified.

And now the reader may again ponder the question of whether the Pathfinder knew physics.
