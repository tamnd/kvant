Why does the water supply system “sing”?

E. I. PALCHIKOV

Agree that the “penine” of water pipes, especially at this hour, does not bring much pleasure. Trying to get rid of unpleasant sounds or at least understand the reason for their occurrence, you open the water tap. Often this leads to nothing. But sometimes it happens like this: it is enough to open the faucet, which was previously closed, and the buzzing either changes to a different note or disappears altogether. So it's all due to the tap? It turns out yes. More precisely, a faulty faucet.

For an experimental physicist, a “freezing” water supply can serve as a good object for research, and getting rid of “singing” can be a good LAB laboratory work with a useful outlet for others.

As is known, the source of sound can be any body that vibrates with a sound frequency.*) Let us consider where and how oscillations occur in the water supply-faucet system.

figure 1 shows one of the possible designs of a water tap. In it, a barrier consisting of a metal washer and a rubber gasket is pressed with a screw against the side, limiting the round hole through which water flows to the tap. What happens if the faucet is opened slightly? From figure it is clear that the narrowest place through which water passes is the gap between the edges of the answer and the bottom side of the barrier. Since the volume of water passing per unit time through any cross-section of the water pipeline remains constant, in the narrowest place the speed of water movement will be the highest. On the upper side of the barrier, the water speed is significantly less than on the lower side. When the speed of the liquid moving through the water pipe changes, the pressure also changes. In accordance with Bernulia's law, where the speed is higher, the pressure is lower and vice versa.*)

At certain rates of water flow and “favorable” dimensions of the barrier, response and gap, it may turn out that the sum of the pressure forces acting on the force barrier will be much less than the pressure forces from above, and the barrier will be pressed against the response. The damper oscillations that we have considered can occur over a fairly wide range of frequencies. However, it is known from practice that each water supply “sings,” as a rule, on one, its own note. At most, two or three. Why?

It turns out that the main role here is played by water pipes filled with water. Under the influence of the damper, oscillations also arise in them, and the water responds most strongly to those frequencies at which it itself can oscillate. This is a specific manifestation of the well-known phenomenon of resonance, so it is no coincidence that the corresponding section of the water pipeline is called resonance. Thus, from all possible frequencies of oscillation of the damper in the tap, only those are “selected” at which such water can oscillate in the sections of the pipeline adjacent to the tap.

In this case, the vibrations of the water table help the damper vibrations and control them. The damper takes part of the energy from the water flowing from the tap and separates this energy from the amount of water oscillating in the pipes. In other words, vibrations of the damper cause the water to oscillate, which in turn causes the damper to oscillate. In order for the oscillations not to weaken, the valve must oscillate in time with the oscillations of the water table adjacent to it.

And one more thing. The entire process resulting from the pipeline is, of course, much more complicated than what was described above. Nevertheless, the concept of the physical essence of the processes that occur in the “drinking” water supply can provide real practical assistance - finding faulty taps.

For example, if when you open your previously closed tap, the “penine” changes or stops, then this means that the section of the pipe with your tap enters the resonator. A change in “penin” usually occurs due to changes in the size and shape of the resonator (or losses in it). The cessation (disruption) of oscillations may be a consequence of a change in pressure in the pipeline, an increase in losses in the resonant, or a decrease in the energy supplied from the tap. And if, after opening your tap, the “penine” does not change, does this mean that your section of the pipe is not resonating? Think about this question for yourself.

In any case, the best way to get rid of the “penin” is to find and repair the tap (or taps) in which the gasket has become damaged or has popped out. It is enough to change the rubber gasket, and, if necessary, reduce the ability to move the damper - and the vibrations will disappear. How can this be done practically?

To do this, you first need to turn off the water supply with an emergency tap (which is usually located in the bathroom or in the kitchen under the sink), then use a key or pliers to open the body of the tap being repaired (in no case an emergency one!). In order not to disturb the bottom nickel plating, you need to open the case cover carefully, placing several layers of paper or fabric under the pliers. After this, a new gasket is cut out of rubber of a suitable thickness (usually from 2 to 4 mm) and placed in place of the faulty one (or missing one at all).
