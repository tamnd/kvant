Let's look at some of these properties.

Water is one of those rare substances that, under normal conditions of our lives, is found in the form of a solid, a liquid, and a gas. How many of you have seen, for example, solid gasoline (its melting point is lower - 60°C)? We can see liquid iron only during an excursion to a metallurgical plant, and liquid air - in special laboratories. But we know water well in all three states. In winter, standing on the bank of the river, we see ice - the solid state of water, we see water in the hole, we exhale water vapor along with the water (it is invisible).

It is known that all bodies expand when heated and contract when cooled. This applies to water. This explains, for example, the heating of water by convection. But if we observe the expansion and decline of water as its temperature changes more carefully, we will notice a feature of water that is of great importance to humans.

Imagine that two identical flasks are filled with liquid. One flask contains water, the other contains any other liquid, say gasoline, and both liquids have the same temperature, for example, 15°C. We will gradually cool these liquids to 0°C. At first, the water will not show any “miracles”; both liquids will skim off. But after the temperature of the liquid becomes equal to 4°C, the gasoline will continue to skim off, and the water will begin to expand!

Let's consider the reverse process: we will heat these liquids from 0°C. Gasoline (and other liquids) will only expand, and water will first close together, and only after 4°C it begins to expand, that is, it will behave like other liquids.

You can continue the experiment proving this amazing property of water. Pour cold water into a jar or glass to about half the height (if possible, take colder water, but not colder than 4°C) and lower a thermometer into the water that can change the temperature from 0°C and above (for example, a school laboratory thermometer or a room thermometer is suitable). The thermometer's reservoir should be kept close to cold if you secure the thermometer so that it can only be moved up and down). Now carefully place small pieces of ice with a layer of 2-4 cm on the surface of the water and observe the thermometer readings. The thermometer will drop, but when it gets close to 4°C (it can differ by 1-1.5°, this depends on the quality of the thermometer, that is, its “class”, on the presence of particles in the water and other reasons), you will see that the thermometer column will remain. Make sure it stays still and then carefully lift the thermometer until its reservoir is in the top layer of water where the ice is. You will see that the temperature there is close to 0°C. You may have to add ice during the experiment; do this carefully so that it does not sink down and mix with the lower layers of water.

From our observations it follows that water is most dense at 4 °C; when heated above this temperature and when cooled below, the density of water decreases. This property of fresh water (sea water does not have it) is called the tellan expansion anomaly (anomaly - irregularity, deviation from the general pattern).

figure 1 shows a graph of the volume of water versus temperature (for 1 g of water). Let’s use it and draw a graph of the density of water versus temperature.

Thanks to the anomaly of the tellan expansion of water, fresh water reservoirs do not freeze until the day in winter. As cold weather sets in, the air above a lake, river or pond gradually cools. The upper layer of water colliding with it also cools. As it is left, it becomes denser and sinks to the bottom - it drowns in less dense warm water. Gradually, the bottom layer of water becomes colder, and finally its temperature reaches 4 °C. The waters overlying it continue to cool from 4 °C to 0 °C, but now their density becomes less, and they do not sink any higher. Therefore, a relatively warm layer of water remains at the bottom of the reservoir even in winter, in which fish, crustaceans, and algae overwinter.

However, the question arises: why is the temperature of the water in the reservoir not equalized? Why do reservoirs still not cool to the bottom due to thermal conductivity during a long winter? This is explained by the fact that water has very low, or, as they say, poor thermal conductivity.

A layer of water 1 m thick and 1 m² in area with a surface temperature difference of 1 °C transfers 0.58 J of energy from more to less heated surfaces in one second. This value - 0.58 J/(m³ °C s) - is called the thermal conductivity coefficient of water. For comparison, we indicate the values ​​of the thermal conductivity coefficients of copper - 395 J/(m³·°C·s) (660 times more than water), and mineral conductivity - 204 J/(m³·°C·s).

Conducts heat and ice poorly. And if there is also freshly melted snow on top of the ice, and it consists of small ice crystals with air between them, it is a very poor conductor of heat, having a thermal conductivity coefficient

Let's discuss another wonderful property of water. Look at the table of specific heat capacities of the body (textbook for grades 6-7). Water has the highest specific heat capacity! To heat 1 kg of it by 1 °C requires 4200 J of energy. For kerosene, under the same conditions, 2100 J are required, and for others - 140 J. This means that even when cooling by one degree, a kilogram of water will give up twice as much energy to surrounding bodies as kerosene, and 30 times more than mercury. Therefore, when increasing the Greek water, it is not only easiest, but also most profitable to fill it with ordinary water.

Calculate how much heat will be released when a grekka with a volume of 2 liters cools from 100 °C to 20 °C in two cases: when the grekka is filled with water and when it is filled with vegetable oil (the density of water is 1000 kg/m³, the density of oil is 900 kg/m³, their specific heat capacities are, respectively, 4200 J/(kg °C) and 2000 J/(kg °C).

Now let's look at this phenomenon on a different scale. In summer, in warm weather, the amount of heat absorbed from the surrounding air by the waters of the oceans, seas, large lakes, which is significantly mitigated by the heat in those places of land that lie near large bodies of water. Winter comes, and the water, cooling, releases this enormous amount of heat into the air. Therefore, winter in such places of the Earth is not as severe as in others lying on the same thorns.

Let's calculate, for example, how much heat the Black Sea gives up when the season changes from summer to winter. Observations show that the average temperature of the surface waters of the Black Sea in summer is about 25 °C, and in winter about 7 °C. Its surface area is approximately 420,000 km², and its average depth is about 1200 m.

Volume of water in the sea
$$V = (420 000 \cdot 10^6) \text{ m}^2 \cdot 1200 \text{ m} \approx 5 \cdot 10^{14} \text{ m}^3.$$

Let's calculate the mass of water, knowing that the density of sea water is 1030 kg/m³: $$m = 1030 \text{ kg/м}^3 \cdot 5 \cdot 10^{14} \text{ m}^3 \approx 5,1 \cdot 10^{17} \text{ кг}.$$

The specific heat capacity of sea water is 4000 J/(kg °C). Therefore, the total amount of heat that the sea gives up as it cools is equal to:
$$Q = 4000 \text{ Дж/(кг·°C)} \cdot (25-7) \cdot 10^3 = 3,7 \cdot 10^{22} \text{ Дж}.$$

Calculate for yourself how much coal would need to be burned to obtain this amount of heat.

Water is used as a coolant for internal sorting engines and reactors because it absorbs a lot of heat when heated.

Water dissolves many substances well, and therefore can be made both salty and sweet. The boiling point of water is such that most of the products consumed by humans can be cooked in it... We also suggest that you look at the table of specific heats of vaporization of substances, make sure that of all the substances listed in it, water has the most specific heat, and think about what benefits (or maybe inconveniences?) this brings to a person.
