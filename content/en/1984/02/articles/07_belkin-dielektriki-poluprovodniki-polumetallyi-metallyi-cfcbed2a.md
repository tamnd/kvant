Let us choose the interval $\Delta t$ so small that during this time the force $F_x$, and therefore the acceleration $a_x$, can be considered constant. We know the force $F_{0x}$ because we know how the force depends on the coordinate; We also know $a_{0x}$, since $a_{0x} = F_{0x}/m$. Then consider the movement of the body in the next equally small period of time $\Delta t$. The initial coordinate will now be the value $x_1$ we found and the initial speed will be the value $v_{1x}$. During this second interval, another force will act on the body - $F_{1x}$. $$v_{2x} = v_{1x} + a_{1x} \Delta t,$$

and the coordinate is

$$x_2 = x_1 + v_{1x} \Delta t + \frac{a_{1x} \Delta t^2}{2}.$$

These values ​​of speed and coordinates will be the initial values ​​for the movement of the body during the next, third, interval, and we will find the values ​​of speed and coordinates by the end of this interval in the same way. So, “moving” step by step, we will be able to determine the coordinate $x$ of the body at any time. Similarly, we can find the coordinates $y$ and $z$.

In the procedure we have considered, it is not difficult to notice the systematic error that we make. It consists in the fact that force and acceleration are considered constant during each small period of time $\Delta t$, and at the end of the interval they change abruptly. Meanwhile, in reality, both force and acceleration always change continuously. You can mitigate this error by decreasing the value of $\Delta t$. The error will almost completely disappear if the time intervals are made infinitesimal. But then the number of “steps” will become infinitely large, and this will make the process impossible.

Especially for such cases, a special mathematical apparatus was developed, called differential and integral calculus (Newton invented it precisely for this purpose). With its help, the main problem of mechanics is solved. But the principles of the solution are exactly the ones we have considered here. It is in this way that modern computers calculate complex mechanical movements (for example, satellites and rockets).

In conclusion, we note the following. When we say that the main task of mechanics is to find the coordinates of a body’s motion using known forces and initial conditions, this does not mean that this is all mechanics does. It also happens that the movement of a body, that is, its position at any moment in time, is known from observations. Then the laws of motion allow us to find the force acting on the body. This problem, the so-called inverse problem of mechanics, is as important as the primary problem we considered, but, as is easy to understand, it is much simpler.

Modern physics distinguishes two more intermediate states - semiconductors (Physics 9, § 78) and semimetals. Semiconductors. A semiconductor differs from a dielectric only in that the width Δ of the band gap separating the valence band from the conduction band is much smaller (ten times). At T=0, the valence band in a semiconductor, as in a dielectric, is completely filled, and current cannot flow through the sample. But due to the fact that the energy Δ is not large, even with a slight increase in temperature, some electrons can move into the conduction band (Fig. 3). Then an electric current will arise in the substance, and through two “channels” at once.

Firstly, in the conduction band, electrons, saving energy in the electric field, move to higher energy levels. Secondly, it includes in the electric current... empty levels left in the valence band by electrons that have flowed into the conduction band. Indeed, the Pauli principle allows any electron to occupy a vacant level in the valence band. But, having occupied this level, it leaves its own level free, etc. If you follow not the movement of electrons along the level in the valence band, but the movement of the empty levels themselves, then it turns out that these levels, which have the initial name *holes*, also become a current carrier. The number of holes, very indo, is equal to the number of electrons entering the conduction band (the so-called *conduction electrons*), but holes have a positive charge because a hole is a missing electron.

Thus, in a semiconductor, the electric current is the current of electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band. This conductivity of a semiconductor is called *intrinsic*.

Electrons and holes, when moving through a crystal, interact with atoms of the crystal lattice, losing their energy. Associated with this loss is the electrical resistance of the substance. As the temperature increases, energy loss increases, so the resistance of the semiconductor should also increase with increasing temperature.
