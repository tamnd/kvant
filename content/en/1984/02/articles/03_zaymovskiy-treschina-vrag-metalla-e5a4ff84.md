Crack is the enemy of metal

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. A. ZAIMOVSKY

The problem of the development of metals concerns each of us, since trains, ships and airplanes, mostly rails, as well as many other products and parts, on the reliable operation of which our safety depends, are made from metal. The destruction of a small part of the steering control can automatically have very unpleasant consequences, and if a rail is destroyed or a place collapses, if the hull of a ship or aircraft breaks, then the consequences can be tragic.

The most “insidious” type of destruction of metals and other structural materials is the so-called brittle fracture. Its peculiarity is that it “sneaks up” unnoticed, without warning of its approach until the most critical moment. When this moment comes, most often nothing can be done. The process of destruction is developing at tremendous speed and cannot be left behind. It was brittle destruction that caused the death of many planes and ships, the cause of disasters associated with the destruction of places, gas pipelines, etc. The number of such accidental destructions of large structures amounts to thousands, and the damage from them is enormous.

And the direct “executor of the sentence”, the instrument of destruction, is the fragile trehina.

Grifficis Crack

The role of cracks in the processes of brittle destruction was first understood by the English scientist and engineer A. Griffitsis in the early 20s of our century.

In those years, Grifficis worked as an engineer at an aviation research center and was researching the strength of glass. Glass was not used in the aircraft industry as a structural material and Grifficis needed it as a model on which to obtain brittle fracture. Grifficis, however, took a different approach in his analysis. He drew attention not to overloaded, but on the contrary - to underloaded sections of materials in close cracks. The picture of the distribution of force lines on figure 1 shows that not only the hole itself does not participate in the work of materials, but some volumes above and below the hole also “shirk” from work. In other words, when a crack appears in a deformed body, one section of the individual unloads and transfers its responsibilities to other sections located to the left and right of the crack. Conventionally, we can consider this situation as if there had been a complete unloading of a certain volume (effective), the dimensions of which are comparable to the length of the crack (at figure 1, the boundary of the effective volume is shown by a red line).

The elastic energy that is released when the effective volume is unloaded is spent on the formation of new surfaces - “shores” of the crack (after all, the surface has increased energy, and the destruction of the sample will increase its total surface). As calculations show (they are given at the end of the article in the Appendix), the surface energy increases linearly with the growth of the crack, and the elastic energy released as the effective volume increases is proportional to the square of the crack size.

figure 2 shows graphical changes in the energy of the deformed plate depending on the length of the crack. As can be seen from figure, there is a certain critical crack size ($c_{\text{кр}}$), at which the energy of the deformed body is maximum. When the sample is deformed (at a given stress), cracks whose length is less than $2c_{\text{кр}}$ will not grow, since this would lead to an increase in the energy of the plate. If there is a crack of length $2c > 2c_{\text{кр}}$ in the sample, then its growth will be beneficial - the energy of the sample will be understood.

Grifficis obtained a correspondence linking the critical crack size ($c_{\text{кр}}$) and stress σ in the sample (see Appendix):

$$c_{\text{кр}} = \frac{2E\gamma_s}{\pi\sigma^2},$$

where $\gamma_s$ is the surface energy per unit area (for solids this energy is significantly greater than for liquids).

So, if the stress that arose in the sample under a given deformation is known, it is possible to indicate at what critical crack size the sample material will fail slightly. Rewriting the Griffis correspondence in the form

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma_s}{\pi c}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{E\gamma_s}{c}} = c_{\text{кр}},$$

we will find out the other side of the issue. If there is a crack of length $2c$ in the sample, then we can find from this formula the critical stress value $\sigma_{\text{кр}}$, corresponding to the pre-

By checking the formula he had obtained for glass, Grifficis obtained good agreement between the calculated data and the experimental data. However, for metals, as it was found out later, the Grifficis formula gave a very large error. The reason for this discrepancy was established by the non-Ger physicist E. Orovan. It turned out that when processing a crack in the metal, in a thin layer adjacent to the edges of the crack, there are necessarily some residual deformations, albeit small ones (this is why the shards of the destroyed metal cannot be combined as accurately as it can be done with the shards of a glass cup).

However, this does not contradict the idea of ​​energy balance, which Grifficis developed. In calculations, it is necessary to take into account that part of the elastic energy released during unloading of the effective volume is spent on plastic deformation of the layer of materials adjacent to the crack edges. This leads to the fact that in the Grifficis formula the term $\gamma_p$ is added to the value $\gamma_s$ - the energy of plastic deformation per unit area of ​​the free surface of the crack:

$$\sigma_{kp} \approx \sqrt{\frac{E(\gamma_s + \gamma_p)}{c}}.$$

This formula (called the Grifficis-Orowan formula) perfectly describes the real situation with brittle fracture of metals.

This is what it looks like at high magnification (several thousand times) of the steel fracture surface. “Hills”, “valleys” and other features of the surface topography are clearly visible. These are traces of plastic deformation accompanying the growth of a crack in the metal. Analysis of such photographs, carried out using special methods, makes it possible to reconstruct the details of the behavior of a crack during the destruction of metal.

So, the main conclusions of the Grifficis theory are as follows. First, you can specify the size of the cracks from which the material can still operate reliably at a given voltage. Secondly, after reaching a critical ratio between the voltage and the length of the crack, the process of further growth of the crack becomes uncontrollable, like an avalanche. The top of the crack accelerates very quickly to enormous speeds*).

The maximum speed of a crack is approximately half the speed of sound in a given material. For experimental steel, the speed of movement of cracks was recorded to be about 2.5 km/s, or ~10,000 km/h! It is interesting that a crack can develop such a huge speed in one thousandth of a second, that is, the acceleration at the acceleration stage is of the order of $10^6$ m/s$^2$. This is approximately how the destruction of a gas pipeline will occur, the results of which you see in the photograph on p. 11 - the crack has traveled several miles in a matter of seconds.

Of course, if the operating stresses in the structure are low, then the critical length of the crack will be very large. Thus, after the destruction of one of the places, it was discovered that before the onset of the disaster there was a crack 3 m long in it. It went away slowly, over the course of perhaps many years, as evidenced by the oxidized surface of its banks. This is why it is easy to distinguish a “subcritical” crack from a “svekgeo” fracture. But more often you have to deal with cracks of much shorter length, and special methods are used to detect them.

*) Of course, the concept of speed and acceleration of a crack is relative, since a crack is not a material body. We are talking about the movement of its top.

Fatigue and survivability of metals

How do cracks arise and how do they grow until the critical moment arrives? One of the most common fusion technologies in technology is the so-called installation of metals. This term defines the processes of gradual accumulation of damage and growth of cracks during periodically changing deformation of materials. It is precisely such deformations that occur, for example, on a rotating curved shaft. At that point of the shaft, which is currently located below (point A at figure 3, a), the material grows. But when this point is at the top, it will fall into the skatium zone. Rotation leads to repeated repetition of this situation; the deformation, and therefore the direction in the vicinity of point A, will change over time as shown in figure 3, b.

There are a lot of similar details around us. This is the crankshaft moving, and the axles of railway cars, and Max cars, and much, much more. Fatigue inevitably arises in the “work” of any place: the place is always deformed as a result of the action of gravity, and when a train (or car) passes over it, the deformation increases, then decreases to the original level, etc.

The appearance of the installation is free of free processes of plastic deformation, which do not cover the entire volume of materials, but are concentrated in the “weakest” points of the part - where there are sharp corners, holes, etc., around which concentrated stress is created. The emerging crack itself becomes an effective stress concentrator. The gradual growth of a crack increases the stress concentration, and its growth rate increases. As a result, sooner or later the length of the crack reaches a critical value determined by the Griffist-Orowan correspondence. This is followed by rapid final development.

Studying the laws of crack formation and movement is necessary - knowledge of these laws allows us to develop ways to protect metals from destruction. And it is no coincidence that accidents and disasters associated with insidious brittle destruction are becoming increasingly rare.

Application

Let us consider a crack formed in a plate of thickness $d$. Let us, like Griffist, assume that if the crack has an elliptical shape - such as in figure 1 - then the defect volume is limited (in the platinum plane) by an ellipse, resulting in $c$ and $2c$, and is equal to $2\pi^2 d$. The energy stored in the plate through the total action of its deformation is equal to the work done by the elastic force $F$ when moving all parts of the body to a distance $\Delta l$ ($\Delta l$ is the absolute deformation of the body):

$$A = \frac{1}{2} F \cdot \Delta l$$

( $\frac{1}{2} F$ is the average value of the elastic force, which during deformation increases linearly from 0 to $F$ ). Energy occurring per unit volume is

$$a = \frac{A}{Sl} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{F}{S} \cdot \frac{\Delta l}{l}$$

But $F/S$ is the stress $\sigma$ in the plate, and $\Delta l/l$ is the relative strain $\varepsilon$. If the deformation is elastic, then Hooke’s law is valid and

$$\sigma = \varepsilon E$$

where $E$ is the elasticity model of the plate materials.

So, the energy of elastic deformation per unit volume of the plate is

$$a = \frac{1}{2} \varepsilon \sigma = \frac{\sigma^2}{2E}$$

and, therefore, when unloading the effective volume $2\pi^2 d$, energy is released

$$W_1 = \frac{\pi \sigma^2 c^2 d}{E}$$

When processing cracks of length $2c$, two new surfaces were revealed, each with an area of ​​$2cd$ (we neglect the curvature of the crack faces). The energy that was expended on processing these surfaces is equal to

$$W_2 = 4cd\gamma_s$$

( $\gamma_s$ - surface energy, occurrence per unit area).

Thus, the appearance of a crack changed the energy of the deformed plate by amounts

$$W = W_2 - W_1 = 4cd\gamma_s - \frac{\pi \sigma^2 c^2 d}{E}$$

The graph of dependence $W(c)$ shown on figure 2 has a maximum at the point with abscissa $c = c_{\text{кр}}$. At this point the processing $dW/dc$ becomes zero:

$$\frac{dW}{dc} = 4d\gamma_s - \frac{2\pi \sigma^2 cd}{E} = 0$$

and from this condition we find $c_{\text{кр}}$:

$$c_{\text{кр}} = \frac{2E\gamma_s}{\pi \sigma^2}$$
