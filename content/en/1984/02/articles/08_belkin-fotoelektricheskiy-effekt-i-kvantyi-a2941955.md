Photoelectric effect and quanta

The photoelectric effect was discovered by the German experimental physicist W. Gallwack in 1888.*) He discovered that when a negatively charged conductor is illuminated with ultraviolet light, the charge of the conductor quickly decreases.

The photoelectric effect was studied in detail in 1888-1890 by the Russian scientist A. G. Stoletov and somewhat later by the German scientist F. Lenard. Thus, in 1899, Lenard proved that when a metal is illuminated with ultraviolet (and sometimes visible) light, negatively charged particles - electrons - escape from the metal.**)

The fact that electrons are ejected from metal under the influence of light itself was not surprising. By this time it was already known that light is an electromagnetic wave. This means that light falling on the surface of a metal brings with it an alternating electric field. In that

In a coma field, an electron in a metal must oscillate, and when it reaches a large amplitude, it can fly out of the metal, where it is held by the force of attraction to a positively charged particle (remember that the amplitude determines the oscillation energy). You can expect that it will take some time for the electron to “swing” (calculations show that it may even take minutes). The number of electrons emitted per unit time must be determined, which can even take minutes. So, the light of a powerful projector should naturally pull out more electrons than the light of a flashlight. The energy of the emitted electrons should also depend on the intensity of the supplying light.

Thus, based on the wave theory of light, we can expect that:

1. Any light, that is, any wavelength, must pull electrons out of the metal, since all light brings with it an alternating electric field.

2. It takes time to eject an electron from a metal. Therefore, photoelectrons (electrons emitted by light) should appear some time after the light is turned on.

3. The number of electrons ejected and their energy must be proportional to the intensity of the light (that is, the square of the amplitude of vibrations in the light wave).

However, studies carried out by A. G. Stoletov, V. Gallvak and F. Lenard showed that the laws of the photoeffect are not at all as expected. It turned out that:

1. Not all light ejects electrons from a metal. For each metal there is a certain smallest frequency $v_{\min}$ (longest wavelength $\lambda_{\max}$), such that light less than the frequency (longer wavelength) does not emit electrons at all. If the vibration frequency in a light wave is less than this limit

But if the frequency is greater than $v_{\min}$, then even a small flashlight will cause electrons to fly out. This lowest frequency (longest wavelength) is called the red edge of the photoelectric effect.

2. The emission of electrons from the metal begins almost simultaneously with the beginning of its illumination. The instantaneous action is what made the photo effect so important in various automatic devices.

3. Electrons aligned with light have different values ​​of kinetic energy - from the smallest to some maximum value $mv^2_{\max}/2$. This maximum photoelectron energy depends linearly on the frequency of the light, not on its intensity. The light intensity is proportional only to the number of aligned electrons (in this state, Stoletav’s law for the photoelectric effect).

Thus, the main role is played by the frequency of light, which determines its color, but has nothing to do with the energy of the wave. The laws of the photoelectric effect therefore turn out to be incompatible with the wave theory of light.

A complete theoretical explanation of the photoelectric effect was found in 1905 by A. Einstein. He showed that in this case light behaves not like a wave, but like a stream of particles, quanta of light, the energy of which is expressed by the equality $E=hv$, introduced five years earlier by M. Planck to explain a completely different process.

The photo effect itself consists in the fact that light particles, colliding with metal electrons, transfer their energy and momentum to them (the quanta themselves disappear in this case). If the energy of the quanta of the incident light is greater than the work that the electron must do against the forces of attraction to positively charged particles of the substance, the electron can fly out of the metal. If the energy of the quanta is less than this work (the work function of an electron from the metal), one electron will not fly out of the metal.

The meaning of the cutoff frequency, that is, the red border of the photoelectric effect, becomes clear: to tear an electron out of a metal, the energy of the quanta must be no less than $hv_{\min}$. This energy is equal to the work function $A$ of an electron from a given metal. In the case when the energy $hv$ of incident quanta is greater than $A$, the maximum kinetic energy of electrons is equal to the difference $hv-A$:

$$\frac{mv^2_{\max}}{2} = hv-A.$$

This is Einstein's formula for the photo effect. Obviously it is written in the form

$$hv = A + \frac{mv^2_{\max}}{2}.$$

What is interesting about light? Does it play any role? Yes, he is playing. If light causes a photoelectric effect, then the number of electrons emitted per unit time is proportional to the light. But the interest of light is now associated not with the amplitude of oscillations in a light wave, but with the number of quanta emitted by the source per unit time. A lamp that is twice as “strong” emits twice as many rings of quanta; the light of such a lamp and electrons from the metal are doubled into a ring. But the energy of the electrons emitted does not depend on the light intensity of the lamp, but on the color of light it emits.
