⟦folio 38⟧

Perspective

Society that parallel lines extending into the distance appear to us to be converging and that this phenomenon is called perspective. Since ancient times, artists have used perspective to create the illusion of a spatial image on a flat canvas. An illustration can be a painting by Gonzago, whose photograph you see in front of you.

A much more accessible flat image of spatial objects is photography. We can consider that photographing is a mapping that corresponds to each point of space (more precisely, its part) to a certain point on a sheet of paper as follows. A point is fixed—the “focus” F—and a plane—the “screen” E (a plane parallel to the back wall of the apparatus). To avoid the image realignment that occurs in a real camera, we place this plane between the focus and the object being photographed. After this, we photograph a part of space at point A in correspondence with point A' of the intersection of the screen with straight line FA (Fig. 1).

Let's see how straight lines, planes and angles are depicted on the screen.

1°. A straight line segment that does not pass through Φ is depicted as a straight line segment (a segment of the lines of intersection of the plane E with a plane that passes through a given segment and point Φ; Fig. 2).

2°. A piece of a straight line l extending to infinity, not parallel to E and not passing through Ф, is depicted as a finite interval ending at the point of intersection of the plane E with a line parallel to l and passable through Ф. This point is called the image of an infinite distant point of the straight line l (see Fig. 2).

3°. Images of infinitely distant points of parallel lines (not parallel to E) coincide (“parallels converge at infinity!”).
