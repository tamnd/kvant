Let's look at some of these properties.

Water is one of those rare substances that, under normal conditions of our lives, is found in the form of a solid, a liquid, and a gas. How many of you have seen, for example, solid gasoline (its melting point is lower - 60°C)? We can see liquid iron only during an excursion to a metallurgical plant, and liquid air - in special laboratories. But we know water well in all three states. In winter, standing on the bank of the river, we see ice - the solid state of water, we see water in the hole, we exhale water vapor along with the water (it is invisible).

It is known that all bodies expand when heated and contract when cooled. This applies to water. This explains, for example, the heating of water by convection. But if we observe the expansion and decline of water as its temperature changes more carefully, we will notice a feature of water that is of great importance to humans.

Imagine that two identical flasks are filled with liquid. One flask contains water, the other contains any other liquid, say gasoline, and both liquids have the same temperature, for example, 15°C. We will gradually cool these liquids to 0°C. At first, the water will not show any “miracles”; both liquids will skim off. But after the temperature of the liquid becomes equal to 4°C, the gasoline will continue to skim off, and the water will begin to expand!

Let's consider the reverse process: we will heat these liquids from 0°C. Gasoline (and other liquids) will only expand, and water will first close together, and only after 4°C it begins to expand, that is, it will behave like other liquids.

You can continue the experiment proving this amazing property of water. Pour cold water into a jar or glass to about half the height (if possible, take colder water, but not colder than 4°C) and lower a thermometer into the water that can change the temperature from 0°C and above (for example, a school laboratory thermometer or a room thermometer is suitable). The thermometer's reservoir should be kept close to cold if you secure the thermometer so that it can only be moved up and down). Now carefully place small pieces of ice with a layer of 2-4 cm on the surface of the water and observe the thermometer readings. The thermometer will drop, but when it gets close to 4°C (it can differ by 1-1.5°, this depends on the quality of the thermometer, that is, its “class”, on the presence of particles in the water and other reasons), you will see that the thermometer column will remain. Make sure it stays still and then carefully lift the thermometer until its reservoir is in the top layer of water where the ice is. You will see that the temperature there is close to 0°C. You may have to add ice during the experiment; do this carefully so that it does not sink down and mix with the lower layers of water.
