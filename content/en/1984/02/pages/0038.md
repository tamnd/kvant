From our observations it follows that water is most dense at 4 °C; when heated above this temperature and when cooled below, the density of water decreases. This property of fresh water (sea water does not have it) is called the tellan expansion anomaly (anomaly - irregularity, deviation from the general pattern).

figure 1 shows a graph of the volume of water versus temperature (for 1 g of water). Let’s use it and draw a graph of the density of water versus temperature.

Thanks to the anomaly of the tellan expansion of water, fresh water reservoirs do not freeze until the day in winter. As cold weather sets in, the air above a lake, river or pond gradually cools. The upper layer of water colliding with it also cools. As it is left, it becomes denser and sinks to the bottom - it drowns in less dense warm water. Gradually, the bottom layer of water becomes colder, and finally its temperature reaches 4 °C. The waters overlying it continue to cool from 4 °C to 0 °C, but now their density becomes less, and they do not sink any higher. Therefore, a relatively warm layer of water remains at the bottom of the reservoir even in winter, in which fish, crustaceans, and algae overwinter.

However, the question arises: why is the temperature of the water in the reservoir not equalized? Why do reservoirs still not cool to the bottom due to thermal conductivity during a long winter? This is explained by the fact that water has very low, or, as they say, poor thermal conductivity.

A layer of water 1 m thick and 1 m² in area with a surface temperature difference of 1 °C transfers 0.58 J of energy from more to less heated surfaces in one second. This value - 0.58 J/(m³ °C s) - is called the thermal conductivity coefficient of water.
