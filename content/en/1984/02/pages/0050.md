In this “meeting table,” each column corresponds to a chess card from country $A$, and a row corresponds to a chess card from $B$ (here $n=6$). Units are placed at the intersection of rows and columns corresponding to previously encountered chess players, and zeros are placed in other places. The cells corresponding to the parameters of the chess players are marked in yellow, and one of the maximum systems of independent zeros is marked in red.

In our case, the rows and columns in the matrix (“meeting table” - see Fig.) are the same (and equal to $n$); the condition of the problem means that in any set of independent numbers there is a unit, that is, the maximum number of independent zeros is less than $n$. By Köng's theorem, it is possible to specify fewer than $n$ lines containing all zeros. Then at the intersections of the marked lines - $a$ columns and $b$ rows, where $a+b>n$ - there are ones, and this, in essence, is what is stated in the problem.

Another equivalent statement, often not used in optimization problems, is Hall’s theorem on different representations (or “property theorem”): let each of the $n$ girls know some of the $n$ girls, and for any $k$ people ($k=1, 2, \dots, n$) there are at least $k$ girls who know at least one of them; Then they can be divided into pairs so that in each pair there will be a familiar girl and an ionisha. This theorem in different formulations and with several proofs can be found in the articles of M. I. Bashmakov («Kvant», 1970, No. 4, p. 14) and V. N. Vaguten («Kvant», 1974, No. 11, p. 23).
