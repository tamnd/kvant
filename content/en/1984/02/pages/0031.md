Unfortunately, there were no ideal crystals, and zero temperature was impossible to achieve. In reality, electrons lose energy by interacting with the vibrating lattice atoms, so the resistance of a real metal increases with temperature (unlike the resistance of a semiconductor). But the most important thing is that at any temperature the electronicity of a metal is significantly higher than the electronicity of a semiconductor because the metal contains many more electrons capable of conducting electronic current.

Photoelectric effect and quanta

The photoelectric effect was discovered by the German experimental physicist W. Gallwack in 1888.*) He discovered that when a negatively charged conductor is illuminated with ultraviolet light, the charge of the conductor quickly decreases.

The photoelectric effect was studied in detail in 1888-1890 by the Russian scientist A. G. Stoletov and somewhat later by the German scientist F. Lenard. Thus, in 1899, Lenard proved that when a metal is illuminated with ultraviolet (and sometimes visible) light, negatively charged particles - electrons - escape from the metal.**)

In a coma field, an electron in a metal must oscillate, and when it reaches a large amplitude, it can fly out of the metal, where it is held by the force of attraction to a positively charged particle (remember that the amplitude determines the oscillation energy). You can expect that it will take some time for the electron to “swing” (calculations show that it may even take minutes). The number of electrons emitted per unit time must be determined, which can even take minutes. So, the light of a powerful projector should naturally pull out more electrons than the light of a flashlight. The energy of the emitted electrons should also depend on the intensity of the supplying light.

Thus, based on the wave theory of light, we can expect that:

1. Any light, that is, any wavelength, must pull electrons out of the metal, since all light brings with it an alternating electric field.

2. It takes time to eject an electron from a metal. Therefore, photoelectrons (electrons emitted by light) should appear some time after the light is turned on.

3. The number of electrons ejected and their energy must be proportional to the intensity of the light (that is, the square of the amplitude of vibrations in the light wave).
