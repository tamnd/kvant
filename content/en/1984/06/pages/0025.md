The geometric structures that were the focus of our story play an important role in solid state physics. The $D_r(O)$ regions are known in crystal physics under the name Brillouzon zones, named after the French scientist Leon Brillouzon, who in the early 30s studied in detail the quantum laws of electron motion in a crystal. In a few words it can be understood this way.

The properties of the electrical conductivity of a crystal are mainly dependent on the crystal mainly depending on the presence of “energy gaps” - intervals in which the resulting electron energy values ​​do not fall. For a free electron (not interacting with the crystal), the graph of energy versus momentum is a parabola (kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, Fig. 7, $a$). If the electron interacts with the pons of the crystal lattice, then discontinuities appear in the graph of the dependence of energy on momentum at certain values ​​of momentum. For a "one-dimensional" crystal, the graph will look something like the one shown in figure 7, $6$. For a “two-dimensional” and a real “three-dimensional” crystal, the impulse $\vec{p}$ is a vector. Having agreed to plot these vectors $\vec{p}$ from one point $O$ - the origin of coordinates, we obtain a (two-dimensional or three-dimensional) space, the points of which correspond to the values ​​of the impulses - the so-called impulse space. A given lattice of $A$atoms in general space is naturally associated with some “dual” of the $A$ lattice $P$ in momentum space (physicists usually call it an inverse lattice). With a suitable choice of scale in momentum space, it is given as follows: the vector $\vec{p}$ belongs to $P$ if the scalar product $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{p}$ is an integer for all vectors $\vec{a}$ connecting two lattice points $A$.
