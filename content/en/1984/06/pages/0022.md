⟦folio 20⟧

By fiddling with the pictures, two observations can be made that suggest the origins of two different pieces of evidence. We will teach them from them.

Observation 1. Consider the simplest square lattice. On figure 3 the area $D_6(O)$ is highlighted in red; in addition, the plane is divided into equal squares by thick black lines so that each node $Q$ serves as the center of one of the squares; this square - let's call it $D(Q)$ - is obtained by transferring the central square $D(O)=D_1(O)$ to the vector $\overrightarrow{OQ}$. If we cut the entire plane along bold lines into squares $D(Q)$ and transfer all the squares so that they coincide with the central $D(O)$, then the red pieces $D_6(O)$ exactly fill the square in one layer, without overlapping each other (the same will be true for pieces of the area $D_r(O)$ for each $r=2, 3, ...$; we suggest the reader check this for so many $r$ so that there is no doubt about this). From here, of course, it will immediately follow that $D_6(O)$ (and $D_r(O)$ for each $r$) has the same area as $D_1(O)$.

Observation 2 will be illustrated, for the sake of variety, by the example of a lattice of vertices of regular hexagons filling a plane. figure 2 shows the $D_r(O)$ areas for $r=1, 2, ..., 6$.

Similarly, we can construct regions $D_r(Q)$, taking any other node $Q$ of the lattice as the central one. At figure on p. 19 several areas $D_4Q$ are highlighted - each has the same color as its center $Q$; all areas $D_4Q$ for different $Q$ fill the plane in one layer, without overlapping each other. (The same is true for the area $D_r(Q)$ for every $r=1, 2, ...$) So the area $D_4Q$ (and $D_r(Q)$ for every $r$) is the “average” flattened by one node. We will clarify what this is below, but for now we formulate a lemma that explains both observations.

Key lemma. Region $D_r(O)$ consists of those points $M$ of the plane for which node $O$ is the rth distance from point $M$.

Let us first consider the case $r=1$. Note that the perpendicular drawn to the segment $PO$ in its middle divides the plane into two half-planes so that the points $M$ in one of them (containing $O$) are closer to $O$ than to $P$, in the other - vice versa. Regions $D(O)=D_1O$ are the intersection of such (containing $O$) half-planes for all possible nodes $P$ other than $O$. Therefore, $D(O)$ consists of a point $M$, for which the node $O$ is close to all other nodes $P^*$).

*) A similar development on the “Dirichlet domain” was used in the solution problem M765 (see Kvant, 1983, No. 2, p. 41 and 1984, No. 3, p. 47).
