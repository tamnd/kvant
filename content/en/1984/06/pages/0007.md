No geometry can be more true than another.
Anir Poincaré

By the second century BC, the axiomatic construction of geometry, outlined by Euclid in the thirteen-volume work “Elements,” was completed. The practical experience of measuring angles, lengths and areas accumulated over generations was transformed into a harmonious mathematical theory with axioms and constructs, theorems, definitions, and proofs. The main actions of the face of geometry were revealed: point, plane, straight line.

But something mysterious was felt in these abstract concepts. The practical activity of people, especially at that time, could not tell what was happening to straight lines very far away, “at infinity.” Perhaps for this reason, Euclids formulated the significant axiom about parallels (Postulate V) carefully: if a straight line intersects two straight lines and forms internal one-sided angles in the sum less than two straight lines, then if these two straight lines are not extended, they will intersect on the side where the angles are less than two straight lines.

Apparently, the ancients described operating with infinity, considering the plane as if glued together from identical finite pieces that were present to consciousness. This is no coincidence. According to the prevailing ideas of that time, the Earth was considered a large flat disk. So big that it was impossible to change it. And it was not necessary, there was no need for this - the spatial boundaries of the known world were already too narrow.

In the era of further passion and geographical discoveries, these boundaries expanded significantly; but it took another century before changes in the understanding of the surrounding world affected the development of geometry.
