Tacitus is behind him on the sled rope. The rope is always parallel to the tangent to the slide at the point where the small one is located. The top of the slide is located at a height H and at a distance l from its base (Fig. 1). How much work will the child do when lifting the sled up the hill? The mass of the sled is m, the friction coefficient of the sled on the snow is μ.

and work against the friction force $A_2$. To find $A_2$, consider small movements $\Delta s$ of the sled along the slide. Let the tangent to the hill at this place make an angle α with the horizon (Fig. 2). Since the rise is slow and the tension force T in the rope is directed along the tangent (along $\Delta s$), we can assume that the sled is in equilibrium all the time, and

$$f_{\tau p} = \mu N = \mu mg \cos \alpha.$$

The work $\Delta A_2$ performed against the friction force when moving to $\Delta s$ is equal to

$$\Delta A_2 = \mu mg \cos \alpha \cdot \Delta s.$$

As can be seen from figure 2, $\Delta s \cdot \cos \alpha = \Delta x$ is the movement of the sled in the horizontal direction. So it is clear that the resulting work against the friction force when lifting the sled to a height H is equal to

$$A_2 = \mu m g l,$$

since $l$ is the total horizontal movement of the sled during lifting.

Thus, the total work done by the small one is equal to

$$A = A_1 + A_2 = mg H + \mu m g l = mg (H + \mu l)$$

and does not depend on the profile of the slide.

◆ With a dense packing of molecules (which is completely true for a liquid), each molecule has 12 neighbors. (Show this yourself. Hint: look at the solid angles at which its neighbors are visible from the center of the molecule.) Of these 12 molecules, 6 surround it with a kolyst, and three more molecules are located on each side of the plane of the lye. When we create a flat free surface, we tear off 3 molecules out of 12 from the molecule, that is, we break a quarter of all bonds.

So, by creating a free surface of area S, we do work

$$A = \sigma S = \frac{1}{4} r m = \frac{1}{4} r V \varrho = \frac{1}{4} r S d \varrho,$$

where $d$ is the diameter of the molecules (“height” of one layer). From here

$$d = \frac{4 \sigma}{r \varrho}.$$

For ruti $\sigma = 0,47$ J/$m^2$, $\varrho = 13,6 \cdot 10^3$ kg/$m^3$, $r = 2,82 \cdot 10^5$ J/$\mathrm{kg}$, and $d \approx 5 \cdot 10^{-10}$ m.

Let's estimate the diameter of a molecule in the standard way - by the volume occupied by 1 molecule: volume per 1 molecule

$$v = \frac{\mu}{\varrho N_A} = \frac{1}{6} \pi d^3,$$

where

$$d = \left( \frac{6 \mu}{\pi \varrho N_A} \right)^{1/3}.$$

For ruti $\mu = 0,2$ kg/$\mathrm{mol}$, and $d \approx 3,6 \cdot 10^{-10}$ m, that is, our estimate for energy binds is completely reasonable.
