Circular movement

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
Y. A. SAMARSKY

The movement of a body in a circle is often found in nature and technology. This is a useful satellite of the Earth and the movement of automatic machines on curves in the road, the rotation of a ball on a string and the movement of charged particles in a magnetic field, the rotation of parts of various mechanisms and the movement of an aircraft when the pilot performs a “dead loop”.

If the dimensions of the body are much smaller than the radius of the circle, the body can be considered a material point. Let us recall the main features of the motion of a material point along a circle.

As is known, the speed $\vec{v}$ of a body is always directed tangentially to the circle and can vary both in model and in direction. In this case, the acceleration vector of the body does not coincide with the direction of the velocity, but makes an angle with it. To analyze motion, it is convenient, instead of acceleration $\vec{a}$, to consider two of its projections - on the direction of velocity (tangential to the circle) and on the direction perpendicular to the speed (along the radius of the circle). The first projection is called the tangential acceleration $a_k$, and the second is called the centripetal acceleration $a_u$ (Fig. 1). Tangential acceleration characterizes a change in the velocity pattern, and centripetal acceleration characterizes a change in the direction of velocity.

The modulus of centripetal acceleration at each point of the trajectory is equal to the square of the modulus of velocity divided by the radius of the circle: $a_u = v^2/R$. The tangential acceleration modulus depends on the nature of the body's circular motion. In particular, with uniform motion $a_k = 0$ and the total acceleration coincides with the centripetal one, changing only the direction of speed. In this case, it is convenient to isolate the centripetal acceleration through the angular velocity $\omega$ or the period $T$ of the body’s motion along a circle of radius $R: a_u = \omega^2 R = 4\pi^2 R/T^2$.

For a body moving in a circle, as well as for any movement, Newton’s second law is valid: the vector sum of all forces acting on the body is equal to the product of the body’s mass and its acceleration. When moving in a circle, the body remains in one plane all the time, therefore Newton’s second law is conveniently represented by a system of two scalar equations written for the projections of all vectors onto the direction along the radius of the circle and perpendicular to it.

The physical nature of the forces that ensure the movement of a material point along a given circle can be of various weights. Let's illustrate this with a few specific problems. Almost all of them were offered at the entrance exams to the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology in different years.
