Conversation with Academician A. A. Logunov

— Anatoly Alekseevich, you head the largest in the country. Could you please describe Moscow State University - how would you see it today, how would you like to see it tomorrow?

— Moscow State University named after M.V. Lomonosov is the largest center of science, education and culture. About 27 thousand students study at the university. The team of teachers, researchers and other employees numbers over 25 thousand people, of which over a thousand are professors and doctors of science. Every year about 5 thousand young specialists graduate from Moscow University. More than 4 thousand people study at the university’s graduate school. During the existence of the graduate school at Moscow State University, over 20 thousand specialists of the highest scientific qualifications have been trained. A quarter of the scientists working in the system of the USSR Academy of Sciences are graduates of Moscow State University. Currently, over 120 academicians and corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences and allied resource bodies work at Moscow University.

Our university represents practically all the main areas of modern science. Within its walls a number of scientific schools emerged that became world famous. Speaking about the further development of university science, we understand that not all of its areas can be developed on our own experimental basis. Therefore, we do not set ourselves the goal of having, say, the largest accelerators or reactors at the university. But we are comprehensively developing cooperation with major scientific centers, and this allows our scientists to use the country’s enormous scientific and technical potential in personnel training and, at the same time, creative areas in solving the most important problems.

However, it cannot yet be said that we will use scientific potential with maximum efficiency. It is still insufficiently based on the laboratories of modern computer technology. Great strides in this area are forthcoming. Over the past few years, we have doubled our computing power. Computers are widely introduced into the educational process; students of almost all natural sciences are trained in high-speed computer technology. But we are not going to stop there, because this is just the beginning.

— I think that young people now gravitate toward the humanities; Jacobs showed a cooling towards physics and mathematics. Does this affect the competition for science departments?

I must say that competition for science faculties on average is not decreasing. However, the level of preparation of applicants entering, say, the Faculty of Physics or Mechanics and Mathematics, in my opinion, is not high enough. Many schoolchildren prepare independently, solve problems, and do additional training immediately before entering a service university. But the main base of knowledge and skills should be provided by school. And as the results of entrance exams show, applicants are often perplexed by the simplest trigonometric equations, the situation is poor with technical algebraic calculations, the ability to make estimates, and in general the culture of calculations has become lower. This forces us to somewhat reduce the level of difficulty of the exam options. Now the tasks offered to applicants are simpler than, say, ten years ago. Maybe schoolchildren’s general understanding of some issues is better now, but it is technology that often suffers, and very significantly.

I have already said that competition for science faculties on average is not decreasing. But the fact that it is not growing cannot make us happy. I think that at some point we, scientists, weakened our attention to secondary school, and this affected the preparation of schoolchildren. I have already spoken on this issue at the Supreme Soviet of the USSR. The fact that many leading scientists in the country are now showing great interest in the problems of teaching mathematics in secondary schools, I hope, will soon change the current situation. Undoubtedly, reform, which the country is committed to implementing, will play a central role in improving secondary education.

Let's now talk about those who, having successfully passed the entrance exams, spent their required years at Moscow State University. What awaits them after graduating from university?

Anyone who studies well and successfully graduates from Moscow State University need not worry. It will find application everywhere - either in the system of the Academy of Sciences, or at our university, or in scientific research institutes, or in leading industries. The main thing is to study well. Unfortunately, there are still students who simply take up space. This, of course, should not be the case; We have a lot to think about on this matter. Studying should never be a burden, and if it is a burden, it means that the student needs to do something else, find something he likes and at the same time is necessary for society.

It is known that university education is functional and broad. But a graduate of, say, the Faculty of Physics must immediately after graduating from university in an average, advanced science, participate in solving specific scientific problems. Therefore, already in the learning process, it is necessary to lead the student into problems that have arisen quite recently. What attention do you, as the head of the department of high-energy physics and at the same time lecturer of the most important course in theoretical physics - electronic science, remove these questions?

Teaching how to use general methods and giving functional meaning is the main task of university education. After all, it is impossible to teach everyone; it is important to give a deep understanding of physical phenomena, develop a creative approach to the laws and equations being studied, and better understand the limits of their applicability. After graduating from university, this approach allows graduates to quickly adapt to a narrow field of knowledge, be able to creatively enrich it with scientific achievements in other fields and make a significant contribution to the development of science.
