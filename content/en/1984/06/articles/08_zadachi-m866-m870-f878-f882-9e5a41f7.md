**M852. Let $x, y, z$ be the lengths of the sides of the triangle. Prove that the quantities**
$$\left| \frac{x-y}{x+y} + \frac{y-z}{y+z} + \frac{z-x}{z+x} \right|$$
less $a)$ 1; $b)$ $^*$ 1/8.

◆

Let us first prove that the sum of the three fractions under consideration is equal in absolute value to their product. Replacing the numerator of the last fraction $z-x$ with $(z-y) + (y-x)$, we get:

$$\frac{x-y}{x+y} + \frac{y-z}{y+z} + \frac{z-x}{z+x} =$$
$$= (x-y) \left( \frac{1}{x+y} - \frac{1}{z+x} \right) + (y-z) \left( \frac{1}{y+z} - \frac{1}{z+x} \right) =$$
$$= \frac{(x-y)(y-z)}{z+x} \left( -\frac{1}{x+y} + \frac{1}{y+z} \right) =$$
$$= \frac{(x-y)(y-z)(x-z)}{(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)}.$$

Now we will evaluate this work.

a) Since $x, y, z$ are positive numbers, the inequalities $|x-y| < x+y, |y-z| < y+z, |z-x| < z+x$ are valid, therefore

$$\left| \frac{x-y}{x+y} \cdot \frac{y-z}{y+z} \cdot \frac{z-x}{z+x} \right| < 1.$$

(For this point, the condition that $x, y, z$ are the lengths of the sides of the triangle is unimportant.)

b) The lengths of the sides of the triangle satisfy the following inequalities: $|x-y| < z, |y-z| < x, |z-x| < y$, therefore

$$\left| \frac{x-y}{x+y} \cdot \frac{y-z}{y+z} \cdot \frac{z-x}{z+x} \right| < \frac{zxy}{(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)} =$$
$$= \frac{\sqrt{xy}}{x+y} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{yz}}{y+z} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{zx}}{z+x} \leqslant \frac{1}{8}$$

(in the last transition we used the inequality between the geometric mean and the arithmetic mean: $\sqrt{ab} \leqslant (a+b)/2$ for $a > 0, b > 0$).

A more detailed study of mathematical analysis tools allows us to establish an accurate estimate:

$$\left| \frac{x-y}{x+y} + \frac{y-z}{y+z} + \frac{z-x}{z+x} \right| < \frac{16\sqrt{2}-10\sqrt{5}}{3} \approx 0,089;$$

equality in this estimate is achieved for a degenerate triangle with sides $z=1, y=(\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{5} + \sqrt{2})/2, x=z+y$. Try to get this accurate estimate yourself.

A. V. Ermilov

A beam of rays passing through the lens is collected at its focus (Fig. 2). Rays, indices of focus, fall on the mirrors at obtuse angles (since $d/F<2$). Therefore, after the first reflection from one of the mirrors, the rays fall on the second mirror. After reflection from two mirrors, all rays (all light flux) fall on a screen with a lens.

For a lens, the source of these rays is the minimum image $S$ of the lens focus, created by the mirror faces of the angle at a distance $2a+F$ from the lens. Only rays lying inside the solid angle $\Omega$ pass out through the lenses (at figure 2 the rays limiting the outgoing beam are shown).

The fraction $k$ of the luminous flux reflected from the mirrors and re-passing through the lens is equal to the relative luminous flux propagating the internal solid angle $\Omega$ to the luminous flux reflected from the mirrors. But the luminous flux propagating the internal solid angle $\Omega$ is equal to the luminous flux entering the lens of the internal beam of diameter $\delta$ (Fig. 2), and the luminous flux reflected from the mirrors is equal to the luminous flux entering the lens, that is, the luminous flux of the internal beam.

◆ With a dense packing of molecules (which is completely true for a liquid), each molecule has 12 neighbors. (Show this yourself. Hint: look at the solid angles at which its neighbors are visible from the center of the molecule.) Of these 12 molecules, 6 surround it with a kolyst, and three more molecules are located on each side of the plane of the lye. When we create a flat free surface, we tear off 3 molecules out of 12 from the molecule, that is, we break a quarter of all bonds.

So, by creating a free surface of area S, we do work

$$A = \sigma S = \frac{1}{4} r m = \frac{1}{4} r V \varrho = \frac{1}{4} r S d \varrho,$$

where $d$ is the diameter of the molecules (“height” of one layer). From here

$$d = \frac{4 \sigma}{r \varrho}.$$

For ruti $\sigma = 0,47$ J/$m^2$, $\varrho = 13,6 \cdot 10^3$ kg/$m^3$, $r = 2,82 \cdot 10^5$ J/$\mathrm{kg}$, and $d \approx 5 \cdot 10^{-10}$ m.

Let's estimate the diameter of a molecule in the standard way - by the volume occupied by 1 molecule: volume per 1 molecule

$$v = \frac{\mu}{\varrho N_A} = \frac{1}{6} \pi d^3,$$

where

$$d = \left( \frac{6 \mu}{\pi \varrho N_A} \right)^{1/3}.$$

For ruti $\mu = 0,2$ kg/$\mathrm{mol}$, and $d \approx 3,6 \cdot 10^{-10}$ m, that is, our estimate for energy binds is completely reasonable.

F866. The electropolitic bath is connected via an ideal diode to an alternating voltage network (Fig. 1). Find the reading of the thermal ampere (A) if it is known that during the time t=1 hour m=1.078 g of copper is released at the katode. Electrochemical equivalent of copper k=0.33 · 10⁻⁶ kE/CL.

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

According to Faraday's First Law, m=kq, opening

$$q = \frac{m}{k},$$

(1)

where q is the charge passing through the electrolyte during time t.

figure 2 shows the change in current in a circuit with an ideal diode. As can be seen from figure, the total charge passed through the circuit during time t is equal to

$$q = q_{T/2} \cdot \frac{t}{T},$$

where $q_{T/2}$ is the charge passing through the circuit during half the period of current oscillations in the network, equal to

$$q_{T/2} = \int_{0}^{T/2} I_m \sin \omega t dt = -\frac{I_m}{\omega} \cos \omega t \Bigg|_{0}^{T/2} = \frac{I_m}{\pi} T.$$

Thus,

$$q = \frac{I_m}{\pi} T \cdot \frac{t}{T} = \frac{I_m}{\pi} t.$$

(2)

From (1) and (2) the current value in the network is found:

$$I_m = \frac{\pi m}{kt}.$$

(3)

The amount of body releasing at the amperage during time t is equal to Q=Q_{T/2} \cdot \frac{t}{T}, where Q_{T/2} is the heat releasing during half the period of current change, equal to

$$Q_{T/2} = \frac{I_m^2}{2} R \frac{T}{2}.$$

Hence,

$$Q = \frac{I_m^2}{4} R t.$$

At established thermal equilibrium, the average power released at the amperage is equal to

$$\bar{p} = \frac{I_m^2}{4} R.$$

The thermal ampere is calibrated according to the effective current value. This means that the reading of the amplifier I_A is determined by the correspondence

$$\bar{p} = I_A^2 R = \frac{I_m^2}{4} R \Rightarrow I_A = \frac{I_m}{2}.$$

Substituting the found expression for I_m (see (3)), we find:

$$I_A = \frac{\pi m}{2kt} = \frac{3,14 \cdot 1,078 \text{ r}}{2 \cdot 0,33 \cdot 10^{-3} (\text{r/KJ}) \cdot 3600 \text{ c}} \approx 1,43 \text{ A}.$$
