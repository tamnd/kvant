The photosphere (in Russian - sphere of light) is the deepest layer of the solar atmosphere accessible to our observation. The name - "sphere of light" - very well illustrates the meaning that it has for us, but from it we receive most of the light and body. The thickness of the photosphere is very small - about 100 km, and this is due to the extreme sharpness of the Sun, which appears on its hard surface. The level of the photosphere is also interesting because it is here that sunspots are observed, the number and size of which serve as the most striking characteristics of solar activity.

The region above the photosphere is characterized by a sharply decreasing density. This area is called chromosphaeus (in Russian - “colored sphere”). During a total solar eclipse, when the intense light of the photosphere blocks the passage between the Sun and the Earth by the Moon, the chromosphere is visible as a spiky reddish color surrounding the disk of the Sun. This color is due to the fact that the chromosphere largely consists of hydrogen, and this gas has a bright visible spectral line in the red region of the spectrum.

The next region of the solar atmosphere, the corona, is visible during a total solar eclipse as a magnificent crown of white color, contrasting with the reddish light of the chromosphere. The solar corona consists of rarefied plasma with a temperature of the order of 10⁶ K. Research has shown that the hot coronal gas (plasma) constantly emitted from the Sun reaches the Earth. But its density near the Earth is so small that it cannot have any significant significance.

The continuous outflow of coronal gas is called the solar wind. The solar wind consists mainly of isons H⁺ and ⁴He⁺⁺, but other components have been discovered, including ³He⁺⁺, ⁴He⁺, O⁺⁵, O⁺⁶, O⁺⁷ A generally accepted theory explaining the physical processes occurring in the solar wind has not yet been created, although there are several hypotheses that give different values for density - from 6.75 to 13 particles/cm³ (at 0 °C and atmospheric gas pressure density ≈ 3·10¹⁹ particles/cm³), temperature - from 9·10⁴ to 2.8·10⁵ K and solar wind speed - from 165 to 350 km/s.

The Sun rotates about its axis in the same direction as the Earth, but unlike the Earth, it does not rotate as a rigid body. The polar regions of the Sun rotate more slowly than the equatorial regions, a property called differential rotation. The rotation frequency at various points of the Sun is approximately determined by the empirical formula

ω = 1/T₀ (1−1/8 sin²φ−1/6 sin⁴φ) revolutions per Earth day, where φ is the gelyugraphic spiking, T₀ is the duration of the solar day at the equator; T₀=26 Earth days. The rotation period varies from 26 days at the equator to 37 days at the poles, where φ=90°=π/2.

It is learned from spectral observers that there is a magnetic field around the Sun. In the polar regions of the Sun, the directed field is about 10⁻⁴ T, and the directional vectors are directed in opposite directions. However, unlike the Earth's magnetic field, the Sun's field is not symmetrical. Its source is the complex movements of plasma, which is a substance with high electrical conductivity.

Of little interest are areas of the solar surface in which the direction of the magnetic field reaches high values ​​- 0.2-0.4 Tesla. Such a strong magnetic field acts on the driving charges of the plasma, that is, onns and electrons, with significant force and thereby prevents their mixing. After all, driving charges are an electric current on which a magnetic field acts with a force proportional to the current and direction of the field. Because of this, areas with a strong magnetic field turn out to be colder and, when observed, dark. These dark “islands” are called spots. The area of ​​sunspots is conditionally changed in millionths of the area of ​​the solar hemisphere (MSA). Sunspots sometimes reach sizes of up to several thousand ms, and the largest sunspot recorded (observed on April 7, 1947) had an area of ​​more than 6000 ms, that is, more than 15 billion square kilometers! Large spots can be observed even with small binoculars. (Just don’t look at the Sun, it won’t sew up your eyes first!) It’s best to focus the image on a white screen placed behind the eyepiece. If you are lucky and point the device at the Sun while there is a large group of spots on its observed surface, then you will also be able to see brighter areas around the spots - flocculi. If you are very lucky, you will be able to see how suddenly a bright glow appears near a group of spots, covering a significant area. Know that you saw a very large solar flare.

Solar flares originate in the lower layers of the solar corona, when enormous energy is released into a relatively small area in a short time - up to $10^{25}$ J (about the same as the explosion of $2 \cdot 10^{15}$ tons of standard explosive). The temperature of the substance in the flare region can reach $10^8$ K. The flare is accompanied by powerful radiation. The greatest intensity of this radiation occurs in the RENTEN and ultraviolet regions of its spectrum, as well as in the visible red region of the spectrum. And only the most powerful flashes are visible in white light. This is a very rare event.

To date, there is still no satisfactory theory to explain

the origin of solar flares. But many scientists consider the most likely cause of the flare to be the so-called annihilation of a strong magnetic field in the solar corona, in which the energy of the magnetic field is converted into the kinetic energy of charged particles that make up the coronal gas.

Many years of observations have shown that the pattern of sunspots changes over time. There are days when there are no spots at all, so they appear, grow, their number increases, reaches a maximum, and then decreases to a minimum. The sunspot formation of the Sun is periodic. On average, this period is 11 years, but in general prana formation is a very complex phenomenon, and in reality the duration of the cycle varies from 7 to 17 years. To characterize the activity of the Sun, it uses the so-called Wolf number $W$, which is determined by the expression

$$W = k(10f + g),$$

where $g$ is the number of individual spots at a given time, $f$ is the number of groups of spots, $k$ is the proportionality coefficient, depending on the specific device used for observation. figure 1 shows a graph of changes in the Wolf number over time (spots have been observed regularly since 1700).

As is known, radio communication on Earth between points located at a great distance from each other is possible only thanks to the beginning of highly electrically conductive layers in the Earth’s atmosphere, called the inosphere. These layers are located at altitudes from ~70 to 400 km. Their electrical conductivity is caused by ions and electrons resulting from the ionization of air by ultraviolet radiation from the Sun.

The possibility of long-distance radio communication is determined by the ability of the inosphere to reflect radio waves, which can thus go far beyond the visible horizon. Radio waves are reflected from the inosphere because it is electrically conductive. Just like metals reflect visible light due to their high electrical conductivity. The electron density is high in places (about $10^{22}$ electrons/cm$^3$). Radio waves of different frequencies are reflected at different heights. The fact is that the wave is reflected from the inosphere if the frequency $v$ of the wave is less than a certain critical value. The value of this critical frequency depends on the electron density in the inosphere and is determined by the formula

$$v = \frac{e^2}{m\varepsilon_0} n,$$

where $e, m$ is the charge and mass of the electron, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \text{Ф/m}$ is the electrical constant, $n$ is the electron density. Waves with a higher frequency pass through the inospheric layer and are not reflected from it. It is clear that the larger the wave (the lower the frequency), the lower the density of the charged parts the reflection will pierce.

The electron density in the atmosphere varies at different altitudes (figure 2). However, the value of the electron density at a given altitude is constantly lower due to many principles. Since the upper atmosphere is influenced by solar radiation, it is clear that the most ionization occurs at noon, when the Sun is highest in the sky. The least ionization is observed at night, not long before dawn. Probably, those who like to tinker with radio receivers have long noticed that a radio station that should only be heard on short waves can be heard very well in the mid-wave range. If you notice this effect, know that you are faced with one of the effects of solar terrestrial physics!

Of course, in addition to significant electron densities, there are other reasons for changes in electron density. Quite a long time ago it was noticed that geomagnetic opportunities and bright auroras were more frequent and stronger during the maximum of the solar cycle. Most of the increases can be associated with the passage of spots across the solar disk or with powerful flares. But at the same time, exactly the same spots pass across the disk without causing geomagnetic possibilities; There are strong outbreaks that have no consequences on Earth. Therefore, one has to conclude that sunspots and flares themselves are not directly related to geomagnetic capabilities. But there is one constant effect that indicates the presence of streams of charged particles moving from the Sun. This is a trend of magnetic storms repeating with a period of about 27 days. This number is close to the rotation period of the Sun. It follows that the solar “agent” that causes them spreads in the form of a limited path following the solar rotation. Interestingly, the 27-day repeatability is more pronounced during the minimum solar activity, when the “spotlight” is small. These thin-walled regions of the Sun, causing geomagnetic possibilities, are called M-regions, and for a long time they could not be deposited with any specific formations on the Sun. The situation has become somewhat clearer recently, when so-called coronal holes were discovered. This name was given to regions of the solar corona with exceptionally low density and temperature. These areas are characterized by low levels of radiation in the extreme ultraviolet, RENTEN microwave ranges.

It is assumed that the presence is related to the structure of the solar magnetic field - in the vicinity of the hole the field is directed in such a way that it does not have a noticeable effect on the movement of charged particles of the coronal gas, and it is from this region that the most powerful streams of charged particles rush into outer space.
