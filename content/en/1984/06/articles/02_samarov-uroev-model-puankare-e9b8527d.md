No geometry can be more true than another.
Anir Poincaré

By the second century BC, the axiomatic construction of geometry, outlined by Euclid in the thirteen-volume work “Elements,” was completed. The practical experience of measuring angles, lengths and areas accumulated over generations was transformed into a harmonious mathematical theory with axioms and constructs, theorems, definitions, and proofs. The main actions of the face of geometry were revealed: point, plane, straight line.

But something mysterious was felt in these abstract concepts. The practical activity of people, especially at that time, could not tell what was happening to straight lines very far away, “at infinity.” Perhaps for this reason, Euclids formulated the significant axiom about parallels (Postulate V) carefully: if a straight line intersects two straight lines and forms internal one-sided angles in the sum less than two straight lines, then if these two straight lines are not extended, they will intersect on the side where the angles are less than two straight lines.

Apparently, the ancients described operating with infinity, considering the plane as if glued together from identical finite pieces that were present to consciousness. This is no coincidence. According to the prevailing ideas of that time, the Earth was considered a large flat disk. So big that it was impossible to change it. And it was not necessary, there was no need for this - the spatial boundaries of the known world were already too narrow.

In the era of further passion and geographical discoveries, these boundaries expanded significantly; but it took another century before changes in the understanding of the surrounding world affected the development of geometry. One way or another, in the 18th century, in the book of the English teacher Playfair,

In the twenties of the 19th century, Lobachevsky geometry was born*). It preserves all the axioms and postulates of Euclid’s geometry, except for one - the fifth postulate is replaced by the following statement: through a point not lying on a given line, there pass at least two straight lines that do not intersect the given one and lie in the same plane with it.

Almost all of Lobachevsky's contemporaries were sure that his geometry was erroneous. They not only denied its applicability to the outside world, but considered it to be flawed from within - internally contradictory, that is, they believed that with the further development of the theory, contradictory statements would inevitably be obtained from the idea of ​​parallels adopted by Lobachevsky.

We will not touch here on the complex issue of the connection between Lobachevsky’s geometry and the outside world - it is revealed in all its depth only in the relative theory, created at the beginning of our century**). Our goal is different - to talk about how the mathematician was daring with Lobachevsky’s geometry and accused him of inconsistency. It was for this purpose that at the end of the century the French mathematician A. Poincaré built a model of Lobachevsky geometry (1882). But in order to tell what the Poincaré model is and why it establishes the consistency of Lobachevsky's geometry, we must first deal with the properties of... the general circle.

Inversion and its properties

Inversion ($O, r$) (or the symmetry of a relative circle with center $O$ and radius $r$) is a transformation of the plane in which each point $M$ is associated with a point $M'$ on the ray $OM$ such that
$$|OM| \cdot |OM'| = r^2.$$
The point $O$ is called the center of inversion (it is the only point where the transformation is not defined), the number $r$ is the inversion radius, and the circle ($O, r$) is the inversion circle.

Inversion is a real kind of symmetry of a relative circle; it “turns” the inside of the circle into its outside (and vice versa), uniquely distorts the shape and size of the figures (see illustrations to the article “Inversion” in «Kvant» No. 5, p. 26). The main properties of the inversion ($O, R$) are shown in figure 2, where it can be seen that
1) points lying inside the inversion circle go to points lying outside it, and vice versa;
2) the points of the inversion circle, and only they, remain in place;
3) straight lines passing through the centrium of inversion turn into themselves;

3') straight lines that do not pass through the centrium of inversion become circles;

4) circles passing through the centrium of inversion turn into straight lines;

4') circles that do not pass through the centrium of inversion become circles;

5) the values ​​between the lines are preserved;

6) inversion, like other types of symmetry, is self-imaging; in other words, applying inversion twice returns all points (except $O$) to their place.

These properties are not difficult to prove; the reader can find their proofs in the article “Inversion” in the previous issue «Kvant», however, their analysis is not necessary to understand further. Let us present (already with a proof) another important property of inversion.

7) Any circle that follows the circle of inversion turns into itself during this inversion.

Axiom checking

We will not check all the axioms of Lobachevsky geometry for the Poincaré model - this is a boring and tedious task, but we will limit ourselves to proving only a few characteristics of the main provisions of Lobachevsky geometry. Exactly the next ones.

I. Any displacement transforms the “flatness” λ into itself, and “straight lines” turn into “straight lines”.
II. Through any two “points” of the plane λ there passes one and only one “straight line”.
III. “Plane displacement” λ preserves the lines between “straight lines”.
IV. Any two are “straight” congruous, that is, there is a “movement of the plane” that transfers one to the other.
V. Through every point that does not belong to a given “line” there pass at least two “lines” that do not have common points with it. (This is Lobachevsky’s “parallel axiom”; Fig. 5).

Let us prove Statement I-V.
I. The rotation of the circle λ around its center and symmetry with respect to its diameters will not conduct λ into itself. As for the inversion with respect to any circle τ orthogonal to γ, it takes γ into itself (property 7)), moreover, the two regions into which τ splits λ change places, that is, λ goes into itself.
II. This follows from the corollary of Problem 5.
III. This is true for all basic movements (see, in part, property 5)), and therefore for their compositions.
IV. This follows from problem 4.

V. Let a given straight line m intersect γ at points A and B. Let us first consider the special case when this point is nothing more than the center O of the circle λ. Then the diameters (OA) and (OB) are straight lines that do not intersect τ. Let us remember that the points of the circle γ, in part the points A and B, are not “points” of the “Lobachevsky plane”. The general case is reduced to considering a special case using Problem 3.

So, the main provisions (I-V) of Lobachevsky geometry are proven in the Poincaré model.
