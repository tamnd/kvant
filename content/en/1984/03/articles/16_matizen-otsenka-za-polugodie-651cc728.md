⟦folio 50⟧

Grade
for receiving

I summed up my results in tenth grade. Right in class. I calculated the average score using a microcalculator and rounded it to the nearest whole number. And if there were two of them at once, then in favor of the latter in obtaining a grade, thus creating the appearance that the result does not depend on the arbitrariness of the teacher, but on mathematical laws. After that, I read out the final grade.

The class, accustomed to this procedure, did not show much interest in my calculations, and only G., sitting right in front of me on the first desk, from time to time raised his head and craned his neck, trying to see what was being done in the magazine. Involuntarily, I looked ahead at his column - although there were only fours there, there were more fours, so the result was clear. We met G.'s eyes, I sharply shook my head down, showing him not to be distracted. And then I noticed on his draft calculations that in no way corresponded to the task that he had to solve. I was about to reprimand him, but he defined me.

- I get four, right?
“Obviously,” I said, clearly showing that I was dissatisfied with his impatience.
“That’s if we take the arithmetic average,” he suddenly said. - Why don’t you take the mean square?
The class tore their heads away from the couples and became wary.
— Isn’t it very geometric? - I asked, recovering from surprise: nothing like this had happened in many years of my work at school - until G. this idea never occurred to me.
“I don’t want to,” he said. “You’re grading in algebra, not geometry!”
The class has changed.
There was nothing to object to this argument, and I took the car, hoping that the increase in point was not accidental. But the tablet mercilessly highlighted values ​​greater than four and a half.

“Yours took it,” I said, sighing, and immediately realized what would happen next.
- What about us?!!! — the class immediately cried out behind my thoughts, but I had already told them how to get out of the situation... I had to take a risk.
“Okay,” I said, looking intently at G., “just blame yourself if the new number is less than the old one.”
G. opened his mouth... but didn’t say a word. The class was moving around.
- Well, who is brave? — I asked with an intonation showing that it was better for them not to risk it.
Several people began to count feverishly.
“First of all, tell me what the mean square is...” the girl from the last desk said plaintively.
“Well,” I said, not without relief, “it turns out that G. clearly earned his five points: he not only knew what it was, but also knew that it was always no less than the arithmetic average, not to mention the fact that he managed to calculate everything in advance.”
The class gasped with disappointment.
“But the path will prove that there is always no less,” said D. vindictively, who rarely missed an opportunity to give a ride to a successful comrade.
- Let him prove it, if he does!!! - the class screamed triumphantly.
-Have you heard what the people demand? - I asked.
G. cocked it heavily, but there was nothing to do. He went to the doctor, took a chalk, scratched his head with it, wrote an inequality... and after some time he proved it:

$$\frac{x_1^2+x_2^2+\dots+x_n^2}{n} \geq \frac{x_1+x_2+\dots+x_n}{n}.$$

For homework, I asked him to find out with what minimum number of marks for receiving such a story could be served.

V. E. Matizen
