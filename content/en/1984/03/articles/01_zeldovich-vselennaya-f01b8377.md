If we take a sufficiently large volume in the Universe and divide the total amount of matter in it by the volume, we get the average density. The average density of matter in the Universe is between $10^{-30}$ and $10^{-31}$ g/cm$^3$. A large volume had to be taken in order to avoid accidentally ending up in an area where the substances are much larger or smaller than the average.

Atomic nuclei, as we know, consist of protons and neutrons (in turn, protons and neutrons consist of quarks, but this does not exist for us now). On average, the largest free protons in the Universe are hydrogen nuclei. They make up approximately 70% of the total number of nucleons (protons and neutrons). About 15% of protons are bound in other nuclei, mainly in helium nuclei. And finally, ~15% are neutrons, also bound in nuclei. All helium in the Universe is approximately 25-28%. Only about 2% of the nucleons remain for the nuclei of heavy elements.

The density of electrons (the number of pieces in 1 cm$^3$) is equal to the density of protons, so their electric charges balance each other. On average, the Universe is electroneutral. Protons and neutrons have approximately the same mass $1,67 \cdot 10^{-24}$ g, the electron mass is much smaller, it is equal to $9,1 \times 10^{-28}$ g. It is now clear that the average (for large volumes!) number of neutrons and protons in 1 cm$^3$ can be estimated by dividing $(10^{-30} \div 10^{-31})$ g/cm$^3$ by $1,67 \cdot 10^{-24}$ g. It turns out that in 1 cm$^3$ there are on average $6 \cdot (10^{-7} \div 10^{-8})$ nucleons in the Universe. In other words, one nucleon (proton or neutron) comes on average several cubic meters!

Let us emphasize once again that within those large volumes that we talked about, the substance is distributed extremely unevenly. For example, at the center of our Sun the density is about 100 g/cm$^3$, $(10^{32} \div 10^{33})$ times greater than the average. There are also horizontal dense stars, the density in the central region of neutron stars is ~10$^{14}$ g/cm$^3$. And at the same time, between galaxies the density of matter is less than average.

The number of relict photons is counted using radio telecommunications. Technically, this is quite a difficult task, since there are many photons of local, terrestrial and solar origin on Earth. There is reason to believe that in addition to photons, the Universe is also permeated by other particles, in particular, neutrinos and antineutrinos of different types. If the rest mass of neutrinos is zero, then their contribution to the total density is approximately equal to the contribution to the density from radiation. However, even a very small rest mass of one type of neutrino (25,000 times less than the rest mass of an electron) would radically change our understanding of the average density of matter in the Universe.

Modern physics is already quite willing to attribute a small rest mass to the neutrino, although it does not define the number. 4. The universe does not remain in the same state all the time - it evolves. This can be judged by measuring the speed of movement of galaxies of galaxies. Observations show that even galaxies are moving away from us with a speed $u$ proportional to the distance $r$ to them. Formula $\vec{u} = Hr$ expresses the significant Hubble law. The proportionality coefficient $H$ is called the Hubble constant and is approximately equal to 50 (km/s)/Mpc. Let us note here that the name “constant” in this case only indicates the independence of $H$ from the magnitude and direction of the vector $\vec{r}$. At the same time, $H$ depends on time.

In order to establish this law, it was necessary to learn how to determine the speed of movement of galaxies and the distances at which they are located.

The principle for determining speed is as follows. The emission of galaxies contains a spectrum

The laws of physics are the same. The wavelength of the determined line at the moment of emission is $\lambda_{изл}$ the same on Earth and in any of the most distant galaxies. If the source moves away, then according to the Doppler coefficient, the observed one at rest will receive radiation mixed towards the red end of the spectrum, in other words, the wavelength for the observed $\lambda_{набл}$ will increase. The relative magnitude of the displacement in cosmology is denoted by the letter $z$ and is given by the formula

$$z = \frac{\lambda_{набл} - \lambda_{изл}}{\lambda_{изл}}.$$

For the case when the speed $u$ is much less than the speed of light $c$, $z \approx u/c$. In the case when the speed $u$ approaches $c$, the formula connecting $u$ and $z$ becomes more complex, but we do not need it.

It is therefore understood that by changing the spectral lines in the spectra of celestial bodies and galaxies, it is possible to calculate their speed of removal (this is the name for the speed component directed along the “ray of sight”))⁺).

The Doppler effect makes it possible not only to change the speed of a galaxy moving away from us, but it also “works” on automatic roads. Traffic inspectors are armed with a special device—a “pistol”—that measures the speed of approaching or receding vehicles. 5. Big bang. The process of galaxies moving apart will consist of an increase in distances between them over time. One may ask the question: when did this movement begin?

Determining the moment when all the galaxies were “in the same place” (“at a point”) is quite simple if we assume that the speed $u$ of each galaxy moving away from us remains constant over time. To do this, it is enough to divide the middle distance $r$ by the speed $u$. We get:

$$t = \frac{r}{u} = \frac{r}{Hr} = \frac{1}{H} = 20 \text{ млд лет.}$$

The assumption of constancy of $u$ does not contradict Hubble's law: although the distance $r$ increases with time, $H$, as mentioned above, also depends on time.

More accurate calculations show that the rate at which each galaxy is moving away also decreases over time. The reason for this is that galaxies have to assume the gravitational forces acting on them from other galaxies. This leads to the fact that in reality the time product since the beginning of the expansion (the age of the Universe) turns out to be closer to $t_0 = \frac{2}{3} \frac{1}{H} \approx 13$ billion years. (When calculating age, we substitute the average value of the Hubble constant $H$ into the formulas, which is equal to the value of this constant.)

So, it is firmly established that the Big Bang occurred about 13 billion years ago. Moreover, a sequence of events has been traced, starting with the expansion of very dense matter. This substance was also very hot, so it is common to talk about the theory of a “hot” Universe. Expansion is accompanied not only by a decrease in density, but also by a decrease in temperature. Based on the laws of physics known to us, we can trace what processes occur in the Universe beginning from the moment when the temperature of the substance is about $10^{13}$ degrees, density $10^{33}$ g/cm$^3$, pressure $10^{54}$ din/cm$^2 = 10^{53}$ H/m$^2 \approx 10^{48}$ atm. Under these conditions, matter is represented as a “sup” of all kinds of particles and antiparticles. “Heavy” particles and antiparticles mainly annihilate, while quanta of electromagnetic radiation (photons) and neutrinos “survive”. The amount of substance in the “supp” is not much greater than the amount of the antimovement. Therefore, a small number (compared to photons and neutrinos) of protons, neutrons and electrons survive. The surviving neutrons and some protons combine to form gel nuclei. Much later (after 300,000 years), the protons and nuclei of the gel combine with electrons and turn into neutral atoms. The calculated amount of gel forming agrees well with that observed. This strengthens our confidence in the correctness of the hot universe theory.

Meanwhile, the general expansion continues. Photons remain evenly distributed in space to this day. They form the cosmic background radio emission already mentioned above - cosmic microwave background radiation.

Atoms, when disrupted by general expansion, form local “condensations” - stars, quasars, galaxies, clusters of galaxies. This is the history of the evolution of the Universe in a very brief outline. Confidence that this picture of evolution as a whole is correct does not preclude the need for further difficult work, in the course of which errors will arise and be discovered.

It is necessary, for example, to study in detail the last stage associated with the formation of individual celestial bodies and galaxies and their location in space. One of the central questions of cosmology is the question of what is the current structure of the Universe and how this structure was formed. This also includes the question of the origin of clusters and superclusters of galaxies. The answers to these questions depend on the initial small density inhomogeneity, which was then strengthened by gravity.

An increase in density inhomogeneity manifests itself in the case of a gaseous substance. In three-dimensional space, the “gas cloud” could collapse in each of three mutually perpendicular directions. However, simultaneous rolling along two or three axes is an uncharacteristic phenomenon. As a rule, each “cloud” rolls down in one specific direction. This process leads to the formation of thin layers, they are called “pancakes”. The density of matter inside these layers increases as they roll down. If there are new portions of gas on the already formed “pancakes,” they heat up and dissolve in the direction of their plane. At later stages, the “pancakes” begin to intersect. As a result, a complex cellular structure is formed that fills the honeycomb. In this structure, layers of rolled gas are surrounded by clouds where there is practically no gas. The sizes of individual cells reach hundreds of megalarks, and their “walls” are formed by strongly flattened superclusters. It is in “pancakes” that individual stars, galaxies and their clusters originate.

The formation of a structure is described by an equation that determines the position of a part of a substance at an arbitrary moment of time t. If the position of the part in the inhomogeneous system of absence is specified by the vector $\vec{X}$, then the equation has the form:

$$\vec{X} = t^{2/3}\vec{x} + t^{4/3}\vec{v}(\vec{x}).$$

Here $\vec{x}$ is the vector that determines the initial position of the part (at $t=0$). The first term on the right side of this equation describes the overall distribution of the Universe. The function $\vec{v}(\vec{x})$ specifies the initial emergence for the particle to which the vector $\vec{x}$ corresponds, and the second term generally characterizes the growth of the particle that has arisen over time.
