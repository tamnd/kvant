Ambient light colors

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. BOROVOY

The blue of the sky, the white clouds that constantly change their shape, the colors of sunrise and sunset have always evoked a feeling of admiration in people. They fed the imagination of poses and artists, and naturally attracted the attention of scientists.

“Aerial blue is born from the solid density of the illuminated air, located between the upper surface and the earth,” Leonardo da Vinci wrote. About a hundred years ago, in 1869, the famous English physicist and popularizer of science J. Tyndall first conducted experiments in which he observed the blue tint of scattered light. However, it took a little more effort and work of scientists such as Rayleigh, Smoluchovsky, Mandelstam, Einstein to finally explain the colors of the daytime and evening sky with diffused light.

We will begin our acquaintance with this phenomenon with simple model experiments, then we will try to qualitatively explain the results obtained and from them “build a bridge” to the color of the sky.

I. For experiments, you need a straight-walled vessel with strong walls, for example an aquarium. Its length should be at least 30 cm. In addition to the vessel, you will need a light source that produces a weakly diverging beam of rays. You can use a slide projector, placing in place of the slide a piece of black paper with a round deviation of 2-3 mm in diameter in the center, or you can use a fairly strong electric flashlight for this purpose.

The rituals must be carried out in a darkened room.

Pour water into the vessel and direct a light beam through it along the long side of the vessel (as shown at figure). Now look at it from the side. The cleaner the water, the fewer bubbles, dust particles and other foreign inclusions in it, the less noticeable the beam. It only slightly illuminates the vessel at the boundaries of air - glass and glass - water. However, one has only to disrupt the homogeneity

(Here we can recall a well-known fact: a ray of sunlight piercing through a gap in the curtain is visible to us from the side by the dust particles glowing in it.)

From this experiment we can conclude that light, propagating in a homogeneous liquid, is not scattered (or scattered very weakly).

Now we will add milk and a good size liquid to the water drop by drop. The particles of fat containing eros in milk do not dissolve in water, but are suspended there. After adding the first drops, the diffused light will become much more visible. Please note - it has a bluish tint. (Sometimes the same effect is observed if in a light fog you look from the side at the headlights of a passing car.)

By gradually increasing the concentration of milk, we will see that the bluish light of the scattered light has mixed at the end of the path into a yellowish or even reddish one. The light passing through the vessel will also be colored with the same light.

When there is a large amount of milk, the scattered light turns white.

Several more conclusions can be drawn: violating the homogeneity of a liquid by adding very small parts to it leads to an increase in scattering, while blue light is scattered more strongly, and red light is scattered weaker, so that white light passing through such a liquid is “enriched” in red; if there are many parts and they form entire clusters, the scattered light turns out to be white.

II. A qualitative explanation of wholesale can be understood based on the following facts:

a) Any electric charge moving at an accelerated rate (for example, oscillating) emits electromagnetic waves. The more intense the radiation (of the oscillation particle), the greater the acceleration.

b) An electromagnetic wave at each point in the medium where it reaches creates an alternating electromagnetic field.

b) Waves from charges oscillating with one part add up in such a way that at one point they can amplify and at others cancel each other.

d) Light is electromagnetic waves. Visible white light is a set of electromagnetic waves in which violet and blue colors have a shorter wavelength ($\lambda = (4 - 5) \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ m}$), and longer wavelengths for yellow and red ($\lambda = (6 - 7) \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ m}$).

When light passes through a homogeneous medium, its alternating field excites vibrations of electrons in atoms, and they begin to wash away the electromagnetic waves of the same particle. But, as calculations show, their radiation repels each other in all directions except the direction of light propagation. If the medium were ideally homogeneous, we would not see scattered light at all.

It's another matter when the environment is poor. The presence of imperfections creates all kinds of conditions for the addition of secondary waves and light scattering in various directions will occur.

If the imperfections are small in size (much smaller than the wavelength of the incident light), light scattering is described by the law discovered in 1871 by the English physicist Rayleigh: the intensity of the scattered light is directly proportional to the fourth power of the particle (inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wave). Consequently, blue light is scattered much more strongly than gaseous surrounded light, and the color of the light passing through the liquid turns out to be reddish.
