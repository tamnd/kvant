Electric and magnetic fields

Around any electric charge $q$ at rest in some system, there is an essentially electrostatic field. If this charge is accurate, then the direction of the field at any point at a distance $r$ from it is determined by the formula:

$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2}.$$

The interaction force between two stationary exact charges $q$ and $q'$, located at a distance $r$ from each other, is equal to

$$F_{\text{эл}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{qq'}{r^2}.$$

If the charges are the same ($q=q'$), then

$$F_{\text{эл}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q^2}{r^2}.$$

This is the well known Coulomb's law. We emphasize that this law was established experimentally. The quantity $\epsilon_0$ included in formulas (1), (2) is the electric constant equal to $8,85 \cdot 10^{-12} \text{KJ}^2 / (\text{H} \cdot \text{m}^2)$.

Let us now discuss the situation with a charge moving at a constant speed $\vec{v}$. We measure the speed $\vec{v}$ relative to a pre-selected reporting system, which we under fixed conditions and denote by the letter $K$.

A moving charge is an electric current, and around the current there is a significant magnetic field. This means that the movement of the charge leads to the appearance of a magnetic field in the reporting system $K$ we are considering.

Please note that if we were to move in the reporting system $K'$, moving relative to the system $K$ with a speed equal to the speed of the charge $\vec{v}$, there would be no magnetic field in this system (the magnetic needle (compass) placed there would remain at rest).

Let us return, however, again to the system $K$. From experience we know that a magnetic field acts on an electric current, and therefore on a moving charge. It is clear that there must be a close connection between electrical and magnetic phenomena.

Let's look at a simple example. Let the charge $q$ move with speed $\vec{v}$ (in the system $K$). Then in the space around it there are both electric and magnetic fields. Let now at some point where the electric and magnetic fields are created by the charge $q$, there is another charge $q'$, which moves with a speed of $\vec{v}$. Obviously, it will be affected by electric and magnetic forces. A similar statement can be made about the charge $q$.

If the charges move at a speed that is not too high (what “not too high” is, find out below), then their electric fields at each moment of time are practically no different from the case of charges at rest, and the forces of electrical interaction between charges can be calculated according to Coulomb’s law (2).

We will do this for two charges whose velocities at some moment turn out to be parallel to each other and perpendicular to the line connecting these charges (Fig. 1).

First of all, we need to understand how the magnetic field induction $\vec{B}$ created by a moving charge is related to the magnitude of this charge $q$ and its speed $\vec{v}$. Let us draw from the point where the charge $q$ is located to the point at which we want to calculate the induction $\vec{B}$, the radius vector $\vec{r}$. A generalization of the experimental results shows that the magnitude of induction is determined by the formula

$$B = kq \frac{v}{r^2} \sin \varphi. \tag{3}$$

Here $r = |\vec{r}|$, $\varphi$ is the angle between the vectors $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{r}$, and $k$ is a constant determined empirically and equal to $10^{-7} \text{H} \cdot \text{c}^2 / \text{KJ}^2$. For those we are interested in serving $\varphi = 90^\circ$ and

$$B = kv \frac{q}{r^2}. \tag{3'}$$

The direction of the vector $\vec{B}$ is determined by the rules of the gimlet (right vinat): if you rotate the vector $\vec{v}$ towards the vector $\vec{r}$ in the plane in which they both lie, and so that the rotation occurs in the direction of the minimum angle between $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{r}$, then the vector $\vec{B}$ is directed perpendicular to the specified plane and “looks” where the screwed gimlet would “look” (Fig. 2).

The minimum magnetic and electric forces for multi-phase interstices turns out to be small. For example, the usually directed speed of electrons in a metal during the passage of current is ten fractions of a millimeter per second. In such cases, the magnetic interaction of electrons is approximately $10^{24}$ times less than their electrical interaction. Is it worth considering the magnetic interaction of moving charges at all? It turns out it’s worth it and here’s why. In any conductor there are equal numbers of charging particles of both signs. Therefore, conductors carrying electrical current are practically neutral. Electrical forces in conductors are concentrated with great precision, and in their absence, even very small magnetic forces exert significant forces. In electric motors, the electrons moving in their windings interact precisely with magnetic forces. It is due to magnetic forces that the engine is driven.

Let us now remember that in our calculations we made the assumption that “speeds are not too high.” Terver, we can say that we meant the small speed of particles compared to the speed of light. We needed this so that there would be no need to use more complex (relativistic) formulas. For example, when the speed of charged particles is close to the speed of light, it is no longer possible to write the formula for the direction of the electric field in the form (1).

Just like other physical quantities, for example, the speed and acceleration of particles, the forces acting on them, the direction of the electric field and the magnetic field induction depend on the reporting system in which we consider (measure) them. We have already said above, for example, that if a magnetic field exists in the system $K$, then it does not follow from this that it will be preserved in the system $K'$. The system $K'$, which we considered, was moving at a speed equal to the speed $\vec{v}$ of the charge interfering with us. And there was no magnetic field in the $K'$ system. In the general case, both the vector $\vec{E}$ and the vector $\vec{B}$ are relative, that is, it depends on the reporting system in which the observer is located with the instruments that measure these quantities.

What exists for us is that at speeds small compared to the speed of light, the electric field changes little. The quantity characteristic of this smallness is precisely the relation $(v/c)^2$.

Note, however, that formula (8) also turns out to be valid at speeds close to the speed of light. Therefore, for relativistic particles moving at a speed only slightly less than the speed of light, the magnitudes of the magnetic and electrical forces may not differ very much.

Summarizing our calculations, we will once again prove that for multi-phase interstices the magnetic forces turn out to be significant.

Let us dwell very briefly on other distinct magnetic and electric (electrostatic) fields. However, one of these differences is that the action lines of electric forces (the force lines of the electrostatic field) have a beginning and an end. They start on positive charges and end on negative ones. The work done when a charge moves between two fixed points in an electric field does not depend on the shape and length of the trajectory, and the work done when the charge moves along a closed path is zero. Thanks to this property of the electric field, each point is characterized not only by direction, but also by potential.

Lines of magnetic induction, unlike electric field lines, have no beginning and end. They are closed and cover the current. Each point of the magnetic field is characterized by the magnitude of the magnetic induction, but not by the potential.

The force acting on a charge in an electric field is always directed tangentially to the lines of force. If we consider sections of current-carrying conductors or moving charged particles in a magnetic field, then the forces acting on them are directed not tangentially to the induction lines, but perpendicular to them (Lorentz force, Ampere’s law). The force in a magnetic field may not cause translational motion at all, but only rotation (a frame with current in a uniform magnetic field; movement of a charge in a circle in a plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field).
