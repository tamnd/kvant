From the prehistory of radio
(to the 125th anniversary of the birth of A. S. Popov)

Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences
S. M. RYTOV

How the invention arose, why it arose precisely at the end of the century, how the structure built by the inventors grew into a columnar and magnificent building - all this becomes truly understandable only if we consider that the radio pioneers could build on a wide and solid foundation, the construction of which was completed several years before the invention itself.

Academician L. I. Mandelstam

Radio is one of the main elements of modern civilization, determining its appearance. Not only within the Earth, but also on an international scale, in cosmic and even cosmological problems, radio waves are now both a means of promise and a means of knowledge.

If we compare the modern significance of radio with its humble beginnings, it may seem that the entire path of development of radio - both ideological and practical - ends with our century. But this is a misconception. As the outstanding Soviet radiophysicist and radio engineer Academician L.I. Mandelstam wrote, “the invention of radio, as well as its development, became possible only thanks to the revolution in ideas about electronic phenomena that is associated with the names of Faraday, Maxwell and Hertz. The history of the development of these ideas is, in essence, the prehistory of radio.”

In 1831, Faraday discovered the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction - the appearance of an electric field with any change in the magnetic field. In 1833 he discovered the laws of electrolysis; in 1837 - detects the influence of the environment on the capacitance of the capacitor; Since 1845, a number of further brilliant discoveries have followed, including the discovery of rotation of the plane of polarization of light in a magnetic field, the discovery of diamagnetism and parametgnetism. In all his titanic experimental work, Faraday relied on the idea of ​​strong lines, on the idea of ​​the role of the medium in electromagnetic phenomena, on the concept of short-range action from point to point, as opposed to Newtonian ideas of instantaneous long-range action. Despite the vagueness of his ideas about the nature of electromagnetism, despite the lack of understanding and description of its concepts, Faraday followed his own path, and this path led him from one discovery to another. But among the natural data side were such brilliant physicists as Ampère, Weber, Kirchhoff, and W. Thomson. The only one who was deeply imbued with the ideas of Faraday and who set himself the task of clarifying and clarifying these ideas,

was James Clerk Maxwell. In the preface to the “Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism” he later wrote, he writes: “Mainly in the hope of making it [Faraday - S.R.] the basis of mathematical theory, I undertook the writing of this treatise.”

But, pursuing this goal, Maxwell himself made a greatest discovery, new not only in content, but also in method: the discovery came not from experiment, but from a mathematical generalization of the theory. He predicted the opposite phenomenon to that discovered by Faraday - the appearance of a magnetic field with any change in the electric field. Thus, a new macro-synthesis of electrodynamics was created, which is mathematically expressed by Maxwell’s equation, in physics it covers a gigantic circle of electromagnetic phenomena, and historically means the victory of Faraday short-range interaction, the birth of the concept of field in physics.

An extremely important conclusion arising from Maxwell's theory was the following: there must be electromagnetic transverse waves, the speed of propagation of which is finite and determined by the product of the electric and magnetic constants

$$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \mu_0}}$$

$\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ were changed earlier and the values ​​of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \mu_0}}$ turned out to be equal to 193,088 miles / s, that is, very close to the value of the speed of light 193,118 miles / s, obtained in the Fizue optical experiment. It should be noted that Maxwell himself modestly believed that the electromagnetic theory of light was proposed by Faraday. He writes directly about this in 1864, referring to the purely speculative speculations of Faraday in his 1846 article “Thoughts on Ray Oscillations.” Modesty, of course, adorns a person, but cannot destroy the difference between promises in words and the direct consequence of the theory, a consequence that supports new phenomena. However, of these predictions, perhaps the most important was electromagnetic waves. Neither Faraday, nor even Maxwell himself had in mind that direct connection between optics and the electromagnetic, which so clearly emerged as a result of a theory created initially only for electromagnetic phenomena. No wonder Heinrich Hertz said about Maxwell’s theory, about his equations in his report “On the correspondence between light and electricity” (Heidelberg, 1889):

“It is impossible to study this wonderful theory without sometimes making the mistake that the mathematical formula of the prize is an independent life and its own mind, that they are smarter than us, smarter even than the one who discovered them, that they give even more than was originally put into them.”

Hertz did a lot to master the mathematical form of the theory, but his main achievement was the experiments he conceived and carried out, “classical for all eternity,” as the famous Russian physicist O. D. Khvolson said about them.

On the occasion of the centenary of Maxwell's birth (that is, in 1931), J. J. Thomson, himself an accomplished experimentalist, said of these experiments: “Hertz's research is one of the most remarkable triumphs in the entire history of physics of experimental skill, ingenuity and caution in conclusions.” And further: “The discovery of electric waves was not only of scientific interest, although it was the only one that contributed to it. Like Faraday's discovery of electromagnetic induction, it had profound

influence on civilization. It must provide a working method that allows us to bring the inhabitants of the globe closer to each other within hearing distance, which contains social, educational and political opportunities, the significance of which we are only beginning to understand.”

It was these waves that gave birth to joy. After all, joy is nothing more than the use of electromagnetic waves for communication, which arose among many immediately after Hertz’s experiments. It is therefore interesting what Hertz himself thought about this matter.

In 1889, engineer Huber from Munich approached Hertz with the question whether Hertz’s discovery of waves could be used for wireless telephones. In 1897 (that is, 3 years after Hertz’s death), Huber forwarded the response letter received from Hertz to a German electrical engineering journal, where it was sent without comment. Here is the translation of this letter.

"Dear Sir! I answer with satisfaction your kind letter of December 1st. Magnetic lines of force propagate like rays, just like electric lines of force, only when their vibrations are fast enough; in this case, both types of lines of force are inseparable from each other, and the rays or waves discussed in my studies can with equal justice be called both magnetic and electric. But the oscillations in a transformer or telephone are not much slower. Let's assume that we have 1000 oscillations per second, which already seems like a fairly high number of oscillations; this would correspond to a wave 300 km long in the ether; the focal lengths of the mirrors used must have dimensions of the same order. If you were able to build wave mirrors the size of a continent, then you could perfectly perform the experiments you have in mind. But with common mirrors, practically nothing can be done, and you will not be able to detect the slightest action. So, at least I think.

With complete respect, your devoted G. Hertz.”

We will not comment on this letter either. Further, it is difficult for a general person to look ten years ahead and guess how to solve a technical problem, in this case, radiotelephony.

Proposals to use electromagnetic waves for communication were, of course, just wishes. It was necessary to show how to do this.

This is exactly what Aleksandr Stepanovich Popov did with his great invention.

What exactly played the main role in Popov’s invention? After all, it would seem that all the elements of his instruments were already known.

It was known that a spark discharge, be it from a Leyden jar, an electrophore machine, an induction coil or a lightning discharge, represents damped electrical oscillations. Thus, it was known how to create high-frequency electrical oscillations.

After Hertz's experiments, it became known that such oscillations generate electromagnetic waves in the surrounding space. The question of increasing the discharge power, of course, was not perceived as something fundamental, but was presented as purely technical.

Thus, the central task was to create a sensitive receiver. A small spark in the receiving vibrator that Hertz used in his experiments, of course, could not serve as a practical method of receiving.

Five years before Popov's invention, a device called a coherer appeared. This is a tube with two electrodes, between which there are metal filings. Under the influence of a high-quality field, the resistance of sawdust changes from hundreds of thousands to tens and even units of ohms. This occurs as a result of “sintering” of sawdust (due to microsoptic sparks at the points of their contact) upon the arrival of an electromagnetic wave. Thus, the coherer is an excellent solution for a high-quality signal closed by an entire local battery. The only trouble is that with each comparison this is a decision: it is necessary to meet sawdust so that the coherer again becomes high-resistance and can give a jump (sharp drop) in resistance at the next signal. Of course, such a device is not a receiver ready to receive a signal in a timely manner.

We have now come close to what A. S. Popov created new.

“Having achieved satisfactory constancy of sensitivity when using a tube with platinum leaves and iron powder *), I set myself another task: to achieve such a combination that the connection between the sawdust, caused by electric vibration, is immediately destroyed automatically” [emphasis added by me - S.R.], - Popov wrote in his article “A device for detecting electric vibrations” in the January issue of the “Journal of the Russian Physico-Chemical Society” in 1896 And he coped with this task quickly. We can say that in his receiver Popov electromechanically implemented not only amplification, but also what we now call feedback.

Popov called his receiver a “lightning detector,” but in the experiments described in the article, the receiver recorded not only lightning strikes, but also discharges of an electrophore machine, and discharges in a large Hertzian vibrator, and even the rapid movements of a charged ebonite stick.
