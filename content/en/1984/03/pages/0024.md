III. In everyday life, we often come across such a case when in a homogeneous medium (liquid or gas) there are very small (compared to the wavelength of visible light) built particles. These are smoke, fog or dust in the air, small particles of solid matter in water, various emulsions, etc. All of them can cause light scattering.

So, is the blue color of the spring sky or the colors of the sunset associated with dust and smoke in the air? That's what we thought at first. But this was contradicted, for example, by the fact that high in the mountains, where there is no dust or smoke, the color of the sky can be brighter than in a dusty city.

The explanation is different. Due to the chaotic movement of air molecules, it is possible for significant density deficiencies to appear in it. These imperfections cause light to scatter and produce a blue or cyan color in the daytime sky. And the sun's rays, which at dissipation or sunset reach a much greater thickness of air than during the day, acquire red or yellow shades.

Of course, this does not exclude the influence of dust, smoke, and fog on the color of the sky. In this regard, we can cite an interesting episode *), described by K. Paustovsky in the book “Golden Rose”:
