5. It is known that the circumference of a circle of radius $R$ is equal to $2\pi R$. But we will “prove” that the circumference of a circle is twice its diameter.

Let's construct a semicircle with diameter $AB$ (Fig. 7). Let us divide the segment $AB$ in half by the point $C$ and construct semicircles on the segments $AC$ and $CB$, placing them on opposite sides of the straight line $AB$. Each of the constructed semicircles has a diameter that is half the diameter of the original semicircle. Therefore, their lengths are half the length of the original semicircle. Therefore, the length of the wavy line placed on figure number 1 is equal to the length of the given semicircle. Let us now divide each of the segments $AC$ and $CB$ in half and construct a wavy line 2. Its length is also equal to the length of the original semicircle. Let's continue this process, obtaining a wavy line of the same length at each step. The distance from the points of this line to the straight line $AB$ does not exceed the radius of the semicircles that make up this line, that is, it tends to zero. Consequently, the sequence of wavy lines tends to the segment $AB$. Since their lengths are the length of a given semicircle, the length of the segment $AB$ will also be the same length. Therefore, the circumference of a circle is equal to twice its diameter.

6. Let us prove that the area of ​​a sphere of radius $R$ is equal to $\pi^2 R^2$.

Consider a hemisphere with stripe $O$ and divide its equator into $n$ equal parts. The area of ​​the hemisphere is $n$ times the area of ​​each of the small spherical triangles shown in figure 8. Consider such a triangle. Its base is $2\pi R/n$, and its height tends to $\pi R/2$ at $n \to \infty$. Consequently, its area tends to $\pi^2 R^2/2n$. Therefore, the area of ​​the hemisphere is equal to

$$n \cdot \frac{\pi^2 R^2}{2n} = \frac{\pi^2 R^2}{2},$$

and the area of ​​the sphere is $\pi^2 R^2$.
