This is the history of the evolution of the Universe in a very brief outline. Confidence that this picture of evolution as a whole is correct does not preclude the need for further difficult work, in the course of which errors will arise and be discovered.

It is necessary, for example, to study in detail the last stage associated with the formation of individual celestial bodies and galaxies and their location in space. One of the central questions of cosmology is the question of what is the current structure of the Universe and how this structure was formed. This also includes the question of the origin of clusters and superclusters of galaxies. The answers to these questions depend on the initial small density inhomogeneity, which was then strengthened by gravity.

An increase in density inhomogeneity manifests itself in the case of a gaseous substance. In three-dimensional space, the “gas cloud” could collapse in each of three mutually perpendicular directions. However, simultaneous rolling along two or three axes is an uncharacteristic phenomenon. As a rule, each “cloud” rolls down in one specific direction. This process leads to the formation of thin layers, they are called “pancakes”. The density of matter inside these layers increases as they roll down. If there are new portions of gas on the already formed “pancakes,” they heat up and dissolve in the direction of their plane. At later stages, the “pancakes” begin to intersect. As a result, a complex cellular structure is formed that fills the honeycomb. In this structure, layers of rolled gas are surrounded by clouds where there is practically no gas. The sizes of individual cells reach hundreds of megalarks, and their “walls” are formed by strongly flattened superclusters. It is in “pancakes” that individual stars, galaxies and their clusters originate.

The formation of a structure is described by an equation that determines the position of a part of a substance at an arbitrary moment of time t. If the position of the part in the inhomogeneous system of absence is specified by the vector $\vec{X}$, then the equation has the form:

$$\vec{X} = t^{2/3}\vec{x} + t^{4/3}\vec{v}(\vec{x}).$$

Here $\vec{x}$ is the vector that determines the initial position of the part (at $t=0$). The first term on the right side of this equation describes the overall distribution of the Universe. The function $\vec{v}(\vec{x})$ specifies the initial emergence for the particle to which the vector $\vec{x}$ corresponds, and the second term generally characterizes the growth of the particle that has arisen over time.
