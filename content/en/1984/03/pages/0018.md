But, pursuing this goal, Maxwell himself made a greatest discovery, new not only in content, but also in method: the discovery came not from experiment, but from a mathematical generalization of the theory. He predicted the opposite phenomenon to that discovered by Faraday - the appearance of a magnetic field with any change in the electric field. Thus, a new macro-synthesis of electrodynamics was created, which is mathematically expressed by Maxwell’s equation, in physics it covers a gigantic circle of electromagnetic phenomena, and historically means the victory of Faraday short-range interaction, the birth of the concept of field in physics.

An extremely important conclusion arising from Maxwell's theory was the following: there must be electromagnetic transverse waves, the speed of propagation of which is finite and determined by the product of the electric and magnetic constants

$$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \mu_0}}$$

$\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ were changed earlier and the values ​​of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \mu_0}}$ turned out to be equal to 193,088 miles / s, that is, very close to the value of the speed of light 193,118 miles / s, obtained in the Fizue optical experiment. It should be noted that Maxwell himself modestly believed that the electromagnetic theory of light was proposed by Faraday. He writes directly about this in 1864, referring to the purely speculative speculations of Faraday in his 1846 article “Thoughts on Ray Oscillations.” Modesty, of course, adorns a person, but cannot destroy the difference between promises in words and the direct consequence of the theory, a consequence that supports new phenomena. However, of these predictions, perhaps the most important was electromagnetic waves. Neither Faraday, nor even Maxwell himself had in mind that direct connection between optics and the electromagnetic, which so clearly emerged as a result of a theory created initially only for electromagnetic phenomena. No wonder Heinrich Hertz said about Maxwell’s theory, about his equations in his report “On the correspondence between light and electricity” (Heidelberg, 1889):

“It is impossible to study this wonderful theory without sometimes making the mistake that the mathematical formula of the prize is an independent life and its own mind, that they are smarter than us, smarter even than the one who discovered them, that they give even more than was originally put into them.”

Hertz did a lot to master the mathematical form of the theory, but his main achievement was the experiments he conceived and carried out, “classical for all eternity,” as the famous Russian physicist O. D. Khvolson said about them.

On the occasion of the centenary of Maxwell's birth (that is, in 1931), J. J.
