galaxy clusters consist of hundreds of billions of stars. Stars consist of hot gas formed by atomic nuclei and electrons. A small portion of the gas is found in the space between the stars. If we take a sufficiently large volume in the Universe and divide the total amount of matter in it by the volume, we get the average density. The average density of matter in the Universe is between $10^{-30}$ and $10^{-31}$ g/cm$^3$. A large volume had to be taken in order to avoid accidentally ending up in an area where the substances are much larger or smaller than the average.

Atomic nuclei, as we know, consist of protons and neutrons (in turn, protons and neutrons consist of quarks, but this does not exist for us now). On average, the largest free protons in the Universe are hydrogen nuclei. They make up approximately 70% of the total number of nucleons (protons and neutrons). About 15% of protons are bound in other nuclei, mainly in helium nuclei. And finally, ~15% are neutrons, also bound in nuclei. All helium in the Universe is approximately 25-28%. Only about 2% of the nucleons remain for the nuclei of heavy elements.

The density of electrons (the number of pieces in 1 cm$^3$) is equal to the density of protons, so their electric charges balance each other. On average, the Universe is electroneutral. Protons and neutrons have approximately the same mass $1,67 \cdot 10^{-24}$ g, the electron mass is much smaller, it is equal to $9,1 \times 10^{-28}$ g. It is now clear that the average (for large volumes!) number of neutrons and protons in 1 cm$^3$ can be estimated by dividing $(10^{-30} \div 10^{-31})$ g/cm$^3$ by $1,67 \cdot 10^{-24}$ g. It turns out that in 1 cm$^3$ there are on average $6 \cdot (10^{-7} \div 10^{-8})$ nucleons in the Universe. In other words, one nucleon (proton or neutron) comes on average several cubic meters!

Let us emphasize once again that within those large volumes that we talked about, the substance is distributed extremely unevenly. For example, at the center of our Sun the density is about 100 g/cm$^3$, $(10^{32} \div 10^{33})$ times greater than the average. There are also horizontal dense stars, the density in the central region of neutron stars is ~10$^{14}$ g/cm$^3$. And at the same time, between galaxies the density of matter is less than average.

Therefore, we can not only talk about the flux and density of radiation energy, but also calculate the number of photons present in a unit volume at a given moment.

It turns out that this number is great - 500 photons per 1 cm$^3$. The average photon energy is $1,6 \cdot 10^{-15}$ ergg = $1,6 \cdot 10^{-22}$ J. The total energy of all photons per unit volume is about $8 \cdot 10^{-13}$ ergg/cm$^3$ = $8 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m$^3$. To compare with the average density of matter in the Universe, we express the energy of photons in terms of mass. Let's use Einstein's formula $E = mc^2$ for this. Then the corresponding “photon mass density” is $8 \cdot 10^{-13}$ ergg/cm$^3$ : $(3 \cdot 10^{10} \text{ cm/c})^2 \approx 8 \cdot 10^{-34} \text{ г/cm}^3$.

So, in order of magnitude, the “photon mass density” is $10^{-33} \text{ г/cm}^3$).
