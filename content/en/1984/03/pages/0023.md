By gradually increasing the concentration of milk, we will see that the bluish light of the scattered light has mixed at the end of the path into a yellowish or even reddish one. The light passing through the vessel will also be colored with the same light.

When there is a large amount of milk, the scattered light turns white.

Several more conclusions can be drawn: violating the homogeneity of a liquid by adding very small parts to it leads to an increase in scattering, while blue light is scattered more strongly, and red light is scattered weaker, so that white light passing through such a liquid is “enriched” in red; if there are many parts and they form entire clusters, the scattered light turns out to be white.

II. A qualitative explanation of wholesale can be understood based on the following facts:

a) Any electric charge moving at an accelerated rate (for example, oscillating) emits electromagnetic waves. The more intense the radiation (of the oscillation particle), the greater the acceleration.

b) An electromagnetic wave at each point in the medium where it reaches creates an alternating electromagnetic field.

b) Waves from charges oscillating with one part add up in such a way that at one point they can amplify and at others cancel each other.

d) Light is electromagnetic waves. Visible white light is a set of electromagnetic waves in which violet and blue colors have a shorter wavelength ($\lambda = (4 - 5) \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ m}$), and longer wavelengths for yellow and red ($\lambda = (6 - 7) \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ m}$).

When light passes through a homogeneous medium, its alternating field excites vibrations of electrons in atoms, and they begin to wash away the electromagnetic waves of the same particle. But, as calculations show, their radiation repels each other in all directions except the direction of light propagation. If the medium were ideally homogeneous, we would not see scattered light at all.

It's another matter when the environment is poor. The presence of imperfections creates all kinds of conditions for the addition of secondary waves and light scattering in various directions will occur.

If the imperfections are small in size (much smaller than the wavelength of the incident light), light scattering is described by the law discovered in 1871 by the English physicist Rayleigh: the intensity of the scattered light is directly proportional to the fourth power of the particle (inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wave). Consequently, blue light is scattered much more strongly than gaseous surrounded light, and the color of the light passing through the liquid turns out to be reddish.
