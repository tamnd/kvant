Objection 2. But the bisector may coincide with the middle perpendicular.

Answer. I have two immediate responses to this objection. Firstly, in this case the bisector also coincides with the height and the triangle again turns out to be equilateral. Secondly, if the bisector coincides with the middle perpendicular, then the point $N$ can be chosen arbitrarily on the bisector and carry out all the arguments of the proof.

Objection 3. What if the bisector intersects the middle perpendicular at the base $AC$, that is, $N = M$?

Answer. In this case, the bisector coincides with the median $BM$. From here it easily follows that the triangle $ABC$ again turns out to be equilateral.

Objection 4. You have not considered the case when the bisector intersects the mean perpendicular inside the triangle $ABC$.

Answer. This case is selected at figure 2. Reasoning similarly to what we did when considering figure 1, we conclude that $|AN| = |NC|$, $|NP| = |NQ|$ and therefore $\Delta APN = \Delta CQN$. Therefore $\angle NAP = \angle NCQ$. In addition, $\Delta ANC$ is equipotential, so $\angle NAM = \angle NCM$. Adding equal angles, we get that $\angle BAC = \angle BCA$. Therefore, the triangle $ABC$ is equilateral.

However, they can also imagine another counterargument: the case chosen at figure 2 is impossible, since the bisector of a triangle always lies between its medina and the altitude.
