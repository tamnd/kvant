⟦folio 9⟧

According to my rough calculations, you, my readers, are 4-5 times younger than me. In the language of cosmology, you are “at another stage of evolution.” So let me give you some advice.

1) Learn how to set goals. Learn this always and always. Almost any phenomenon in life, in nature, in everyday life can be a source of “tasks”, a source of reflection.

A drop of water falling, waiting for a bus, a stick falling, the chalking of two fences, the color of an oil film on the water...
Kozma Prutkov also said: “When throwing pebbles into the water, look at the circles they form; otherwise such throwing will be empty fun.”
Exams and competitions are aimed at solving problems that others set for you. Discoveries - big or small - are usually the setting of problems.

2) Physics is inseparable from mathematics. Only in the language of mathematics can the laws of physics be formulated. My view on how to teach higher mathematics is outlined in the preface to my book “HIGHER MATHEMATICS FOR BEGINNERS.” Recently a book by I.M. Yaglom and mine was published with a similar title, thicker, more aimed at the teacher, rather than at the student. These books give many specific examples of problems available to schoolchildren *).

3) Educate yourself. Look for problems and solve them - alone, or even better together. If your partner is stronger than you, he will teach you a lot. If your partner is weaker, then leading him along, teaching him, you will ask better and deeper questions.

4) Alternate between acquiring knowledge and applying knowledge to solving issues that interest you. Moreover, try to give the teaching the character of a discovery, a scientific work. You can take the eyes of a textbook and read in detail, step by step, page by page, formula by formula, understand and understand the material. But you can do it differently: quickly glance through your eyes, understand - at least very approximately - what we are talking about, what tasks

*) Zeldovich Ya. B. Higher mathematics for beginners and its applications to physics, 5th ed. M.: Nauka, 1970; Zeldovich Ya. B., Yaglom I. M. Higher mathematics for beginning physicists and technicians. M.: Nauka, 1982.

are posed and solved in this chapter. Then turn off the textbook and try to come up with a solution yourself or reconstruct the flow of the proof. After suffering for several days, take the textbook and look carefully to see whether the approach you found yourself was correct, or, in another case, how the textbook overcomes the difficulties that you encountered but were unable to cope with.

The time spent on the Japanese route is not lost. If you come up with something yourself that is already known to science, you will know it better than if you learned the same thing passively. Years will pass, and you will discover new things just as you are now discovering what is already known. But even if you did not find the answer, you still better understood the difficulties of the question and better appreciated what is written in the textbook.

A future physicist (or a scientist in general) should not limit himself during his school years to classroom studies, standard software and stable textbooks.

I write about all this, remembering my school years, which were not simple, but inspiring.

Learn to create! And to all those who embark on this difficult path, I sincerely wish success.
