Astronomers, studying and classifying objects relatively close to us, have found among them those whose luminosity and size are at least approximately known. This made it possible to find distances to similar objects, finding them further.

By comparing distances with mixtures of spectral lines, astronomers established Hubble's law and found the constant $H$.

On the other hand, when the Hubble constant $H$ is already known, the change in distances can be reduced to a certain rate $u$ (or mixing $z$).

Of course, we have no reason to think that we occupy any special position in the Universe. It is natural, therefore, that an observer in any other galaxy will also see a picture of galaxies running away from him. The relative speed of any two galaxies is proportional to the distance between them. This is precisely the statement that follows from Hubble's law (check it out!).

5. Big bang. The process of galaxies moving apart will consist of an increase in distances between them over time. One may ask the question: when did this movement begin?

Determining the moment when all the galaxies were “in the same place” (“at a point”) is quite simple if we assume that the speed $u$ of each galaxy moving away from us remains constant over time. To do this, it is enough to divide the middle distance $r$ by the speed $u$. We get:

$$t = \frac{r}{u} = \frac{r}{Hr} = \frac{1}{H} = 20 \text{ млд лет.}$$

The assumption of constancy of $u$ does not contradict Hubble's law: although the distance $r$ increases with time, $H$, as mentioned above, also depends on time.

More accurate calculations show that the rate at which each galaxy is moving away also decreases over time. The reason for this is that galaxies have to assume the gravitational forces acting on them from other galaxies. This leads to the fact that in reality the time product since the beginning of the expansion (the age of the Universe) turns out to be closer to $t_0 = \frac{2}{3} \frac{1}{H} \approx 13$ billion years. (When calculating age, we substitute the average value of the Hubble constant $H$ into the formulas, which is equal to the value of this constant.)

So, it is firmly established that the Big Bang occurred about 13 billion years ago. Moreover, a sequence of events has been traced, starting with the expansion of very dense matter. This substance was also very hot, so it is common to talk about the theory of a “hot” Universe. Expansion is accompanied not only by a decrease in density, but also by a decrease in temperature. Based on the laws of physics known to us, we can trace what processes occur in the Universe beginning from the moment when the temperature of the substance is about $10^{13}$ degrees, density $10^{33}$ g/cm$^3$, pressure $10^{54}$ din/cm$^2 = 10^{53}$ H/m$^2 \approx 10^{48}$ atm. Under these conditions, matter is represented as a “sup” of all kinds of particles and antiparticles. “Heavy” particles and antiparticles mainly annihilate, while quanta of electromagnetic radiation (photons) and neutrinos “survive”. The amount of substance in the “supp” is not much greater than the amount of the antimovement. Therefore, a small number (compared to photons and neutrinos) of protons, neutrons and electrons survive. The surviving neutrons and some protons combine to form gel nuclei. Much later (after 300,000 years), the protons and nuclei of the gel combine with electrons and turn into neutral atoms. The calculated amount of gel forming agrees well with that observed. This strengthens our confidence in the correctness of the hot universe theory.

Meanwhile, the general expansion continues. Photons remain evenly distributed in space to this day. They form the cosmic background radio emission already mentioned above - cosmic microwave background radiation.

Atoms, when disrupted by general expansion, form local “condensations” - stars, quasars, galaxies, clusters of galaxies. Heavy elements are born later - in the processes of nuclear combustion in stars.
