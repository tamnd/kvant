We will limit ourselves further to serving not very fast moving charges. In this case, you can compare magnetic and electric forces quite simply. We will do this for two charges whose velocities at some moment turn out to be parallel to each other and perpendicular to the line connecting these charges (Fig. 1).

First of all, we need to understand how the magnetic field induction $\vec{B}$ created by a moving charge is related to the magnitude of this charge $q$ and its speed $\vec{v}$. Let us draw from the point where the charge $q$ is located to the point at which we want to calculate the induction $\vec{B}$, the radius vector $\vec{r}$. A generalization of the experimental results shows that the magnitude of induction is determined by the formula

$$B = kq \frac{v}{r^2} \sin \varphi. \tag{3}$$

Here $r = |\vec{r}|$, $\varphi$ is the angle between the vectors $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{r}$, and $k$ is a constant determined empirically and equal to $10^{-7} \text{H} \cdot \text{c}^2 / \text{KJ}^2$. For those we are interested in serving $\varphi = 90^\circ$ and

$$B = kv \frac{q}{r^2}. \tag{3'}$$

The direction of the vector $\vec{B}$ is determined by the rules of the gimlet (right vinat): if you rotate the vector $\vec{v}$ towards the vector $\vec{r}$ in the plane in which they both lie, and so that the rotation occurs in the direction of the minimum angle between $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{r}$, then the vector $\vec{B}$ is directed perpendicular to the specified plane and “looks” where the screwed gimlet would “look” (Fig. 2).

Formulas (3) and (3') include as a factor the same ratio $q/r^2$, which is also contained in the expression for the direction of the electric field created by the charge $q$ at the point where the charge $q'$ is located. This remark allows us to establish a connection between the quantities $B$ and $E$. Taking into account (1), we obtain

And

$$\frac{F_{\text{магн}}}{F_{\text{эл}}} = \left( \frac{v}{c} \right)^2. \tag{8}$$
