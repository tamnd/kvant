Philosophical ideas of V.I. Lenin and the development of modern physics

(to the 75th anniversary of V. I. Lenin’s book “Materialism and Empirocriticism”)

Academician I. K. KIKOIN

In his book “Materialism and Empirocriticism” V.I. Lenin gave an exhaustive analysis of the philosophical problems of contemporary physics. The great significance of this work of Lenin lies in the fact that it developed a method for analyzing the philosophical problems of science, which is still used by processive scientists.

Vladimir Ilyich Lenin was not only a great political and statesman, but also a great scientist.

The special role of physics in the development of both technology and philosophy is the reason for Lenin’s introduction to the issues of new physics (perhaps Lenin’s interest in physics was to some extent influenced by the fact that his father Ilya Nikolaevich Ulyanov was a physics teacher).

The Leninist method of scientific work is especially close to the hearts of physicists. In his theoretical work, Lenin always relied on experience, on practice as a criterion of truth. Lenin, in the following words of Engels, the idea of ​​a “criterion of practice” appears: “The moment when, informed by the perceived property of any thing, we use it for ourselves, we at that very moment subject the truth or falsity of our sensory perceptions to an unmistakable test. (My italics - I.K.)〈...〉 ... The success of our actions provides proof of the correspondence (Übereinstimmung) of our perceptions with the objective (gegenständlich) nature of the things we perceive "*).

But the history of science knows many examples indicating that scientists’ ideas that were clearly incorrect from a modern point of view, nevertheless, led to “the success of our actions.”

For example, take the old theory of magnetism, in which a magnetized piece of steel was considered to be a magnetic dipole, consisting of two magnetic poles, or “magnetic charges” (analogous to an electric dipole). Using this idea, physicists create a magnetostat, on which all practice and technology of using magnets is based. This practice quickly advanced the theory of "magnetic charges". Meanwhile, it is now well known that no magnetic charges actually exist. And when we talk about magnetic poles now, we always stipulate that this is a fictitious concept.

It turns out that the criterion of practice cannot serve as a reliable basis for clarifying the truth of our ideas about a particular subject? This, of course, is not a separate question.

One of the largest theoretical physicists of our time, Richard Feynman, trying to figure out what the philosophical interpretation of a physical law is, illustrates the significance of this issue with the following example.

“Let those who insist that all that matters is the agreement between theory and experiment imagine a conversation between a Mayan astronomer and his student. The Mayans were able to imagine with amazing accuracy, for example, the time of an eclipse, the position of the Moon, Venus and other planets in the sky. All this was done with the help of arithmetic... They did not have the slightest idea about the rotation of celestial bodies.

Imagine that a young man comes to our astronomer and says: “This is what came to my mind. Maybe all this is believable, maybe these are balls of stone... and their movement can be calculated completely differently.” Further, to learn that the young man should not yet calculate the movements of the planets, the Mayan astronomer will answer him that we can already calculate eclipses quite accurately, so there is no need to bother with your ideas.

As we see, Feynman concludes, it is not an easy task to decide whether or not it is worth thinking about what lies behind our theories.

This is indeed no easy task. After all, if we accept unconditionally the statement that the criterion of truth is practice, then such a statement, as can be seen from the examples given, can lead to stagnation in science.

This difficult task was brilliantly solved by V.I. Lenin.

Arguing that “the point of view of life, practice must be the first and main point of view of the theory of knowledge,” Lenin adds: “Of course, we must not forget that the criterion of practice can never, in the very essence of the matter, completely confirm or refute any human idea. This criterion is also insistently “indefinite” in order not to allow a person’s knowledge to turn into “absolute”, and at the same time insistently defined in order to wage a relentless struggle against all varieties of idealism and agnosticism).**.**

“...From here,” Lenin continues, “follows the recognition that the only path to this truth is the path of science, standing on a materialist point of view.”*** (Italics mine. - I.K.)

The overwhelming majority of physicists are consciously or spontaneously guided by precisely this Leninist concept of the criterion of practice.

Experience served Lenin as a reliable support when he formulated the foundations of his philosophical views. The following example can illustrate this.

Lenin considered it necessary to test the history of science (that is, test experimentally, as a physicist would say) one of the main provisions of dialectics - the so-called “unity of opposites.” Lenin begins his note “On the Question of Dialectics”: “The disintegration of the unified and the knowledge of its contradictory parts... is the essence (one of the “essences”, one of the main, if not the main, features or feature) of dialectics.〈...〉 The correctness of this aspect of the content of dialectics must be verified by the history of science."**.)

A revolution comparable to that brought about by the theory of relativity was also accomplished in physics by quantum mechanics. Richard Feynman, already mentioned earlier, testifies, for example, that “... there was a time when newspapers wrote that the theory of relativity of concepts was only being taught by man.” Disagreeing with this opinion and believing that many scientists, having read Einstein’s article, one way or another understood the theory of relativity, Feynman continues: “It doesn’t seem to me, I can safely say that he never understands quantum mechanics.”

Modern physics is quantum physics. The success of quantum mechanics can be called exceptional.

It allows you to reveal the secret of the structure.atoma. Using quantum mechanics, you can calculate atom with any degree of accuracy, that is, calculate the detailed electronic structure of atom. These calculations are in amazing accuracy with experiment.

All modern quantum electronics with its various technical applications (lasers, masers, etc.) are a product of quantum mechanics. Quantum mechanics has not yet become a subject of study at school, but its applications are already being studied in technical schools.

And despite all this, the greatest authority in this field of physics claims that no one understands it!

Let's try to figure out the reasons for the misunderstanding of quantum mechanics.

The fact is that even at its very conception, quantum mechanics contained contradictions. Let us turn, for example, to the simplest manifestation of the quantum nature of light—the photoelectric effect.

In the theory of the photoelectric effect, developed by Einstein, the light of particle v is considered as a flow of “particles” - photons, the energy of which is E = hv (h is Planck’s constant). When photons reach the metal surface, some of them are absorbed by electrons. An electron that absorbs a photon acquires energy hv. Possessing such energy, an electron can leave the metal and fly out. At the same time, he loses part of the acquired energy, spending it on the “work function” A. Therefore, the maximum kinetic energy with which an electron will fly out of the metal is equal to

$$\frac{1}{2}mv^2=hv - A.$$

This substitute Einstein's formula for the photoelectric effect is confirmed by numerous experiments and underlies the endless applications of this phenomenon.

So, if you think about the meaning of Einstein’s formula (it was also obtained by him in 1905), then the contradiction immediately becomes clear.

The quantity E=hv included in this formula is the energy of a “light particle” (photon), and the quantity v is the frequency of light consisting of particles. But frequency is a quantity that characterizes

wave. The concept of the frequency of light appeared after it was established in the 19th century that light is a process of propagation of vibrations. This process is called a wave. But a wave, by the very meaning of this concept, occupies a large area of ​​space (strictly speaking, all space). A particle is something that is localized in space and occupies a small volume. Therefore, the basic expression $E=hv$, which connects the background energy, that is, particles, with a frequent light wave, seems absurd from the point of view of “common sense”.

Photoelectric phenomena have irrefutably proven that light is a stream of particles.

On the other hand, there is overwhelming experimental evidence that light is a wave process. Indeed, the very characteristic property of waves is the following: if you superimpose two waves on top of each other so that the crest of one coincides with the trough of the other, then the waves will stop altogether. Conversely, when the crests of one wave coincide with the crests of another, the excitement intensifies. This well-known phenomenon is called wave interference. It can be argued that if the phenomenon of interference is observed in the experiment, then we are dealing with a wave.

The wave theory of light was established at the beginning when it was shown that certain points of a screen illuminated simultaneously by two identical light sources turn out to be dark, whereas when each of the sources acts separately, the screen is illuminated evenly.

Practice, therefore, has led to the need to accept the paradoxical fact that both the wave and quantum theories of light are correct, and the formula $E=hv$ establishes the connection between these contradictory theories.

This challenge to “common sense” reached its peak in 1924, when the dual concept of “wave-particulate” was extended through theoretical reasoning to electrons and atoms. In other words, the Atom and the electron, which since their discovery have all the properties of particles, must also behave like waves. And very soon experiments confirmed these theoretical conclusions.

The foundations of quantum mechanics were laid in 1925-1927. The German physicist Werner Heisenberg made a significant contribution to its development. He formulated the so-called correspondence of uncertainties, around which a community of philosophical discussions heated up. This correspondence led many famous physicists to the camp of philosophical idealists.

At first glance, the correspondence of uncertainties looks completely harmless:

$$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \frac{h}{2\pi}.$$

Here $\Delta x$ is the uncertainty (inaccuracy) of the particle coordinates, $\Delta p$ is the uncertainty of the particle momentum and $h$ is Planck’s constant. Translated into colloquial language, the formula introduces the fact that it is impossible to accurately know the location and speed of a particle at the same time. In other words, if we try to determine the exact value of the velocity of a particle located at a given moment at the built-in point of its trajectory, then we were unable to do this. And vice versa, if we force a particle to move at a certain speed, then we will not be able to accurately indicate at which point in the trajectory it is at a given moment in time. From here it is easy to make the “simplest” conclusion of a clearly ideal nature that human knowledge is accepted, since we are not given the opportunity to answer such a simple question. Moreover, from here we can draw a conclusion, ideal in its meaning, that events in the world are unpredictable, that is, the principle of integrity is violated.

Indeed, we have arrived at the conclusion that classical mechanics allows us to predict the future movement of a body if its initial position and speed, as well as the forces acting on it, are known. All classical mechanics is based on this. Thus, the successes of astronautics are based on the fact that, by knowing the launch site of the package (initial coordinates), by telling the package a known initial speed, we can, according to the laws of classical mechanics, predict in advance where the package will be at any time.

The situation is different with a particle that obeys quantum mechanics. Since we, in accordance with the correspondence of uncertainties, cannot accurately indicate its coordinates and speed at a given moment in time, then, obviously, we cannot predict what they will become in the future. It is not difficult to guess that all this is a consequence of the fact that particles (electrons, atoms, neutrons, etc.) have wave properties.

In fact, let us consider the following roughly schematized experiment, which, nevertheless, is very close to the actual experiments carried out. Let's imagine that electrons emitted by some source, for example a hot wire, fly through two branches in the screen. Behind the screen we can move the electron counter parallel to it. The counter registers each electron that enters it. This means that the number of electrons can be realized indefinitely.

It is natural to assume that each electron entering the counter has gone through one of two reactions. Therefore, if we count the number of electrons that entered the counter through the second branch closed, then do the same with the electrons that entered the counter through the second branch while the first one was closed, we can expect that the number of electrons that entered the counter through both branches will be equal to the sum shown by the counter. We are sure that this would be the case if we fired from a machine gun through an armored shield with two arms, behind which in some place there was a box of pitch, where the leaked bullets got stuck. There is no doubt that the number of bullets that fall into a box with pitch when opening both slots in the screen is equal to the sum of the numbers of bullets that fall into the same box through each of the slots separately (of course, over the same period of time, say, an hour).

But when we “shoot” electrons, this doesn’t work! Moreover, it may turn out that a counter installed in the proper place, which registers the same number of electrons as they pass through each branch separately (when one of the branches is closed), is not registered, and does not register a single electron when both branches are open.

The question naturally arises: how can this be?

It is not difficult to see here the same phenomenon that is observed when the screen is illuminated by two identical light sources, that is, the phenomenon of interference.

Let us now try to answer the question why quantum mechanics is incomprehensible. This will help us find the roots of the idealistic conclusions that some philosophers and physicists have come to when analyzing the current situation in physics.

The main difficulty in understanding distances above physical phenomena lies in the fact that when analyzing them we try to use the same concepts to which we are accustomed in higher life. The centuries-old experience of mankind has led to the fact that a person considers to himself understandable what he can imagine in the form of a geometric or mechanical image. This experience, the practice of studying the world around us, led to the creation of a number of concepts with the help of which the real world is reflected in the human brain. But until the 20th century, mankind was concerned only with macro-specialized topics moving at relatively low speeds. Macro-special bodies are those that can be seen, determined their shape and size (create a geometric image) and study their movement (create a mechanical image). Corresponding concepts were also adapted to this experience.

Newtonian mechanics, developed in relation to the movement of macroscopic bodies, established that the mechanical state of the body is uniquely determined by the coordinates and implus (or speed, if the mass of the body is considered unchanged).

But now we have moved to the world of objects obeying quantum law, alien to Newtonian mechanics. Human experience has not yet had time to develop images of objects and concepts corresponding to this world.

If experience shows that these objects have both the properties of particles and the properties of waves, then in reality they are not waves or particles, but must be something else, “one”

It is precisely because we cannot imagine quantum objects in the form of geometric and mechanical images that we consider them incomprehensible.

But if this is so, then what grounds do we have to assume that the state of quantum objects should be represented by the same quantities, that is, coordinates and momentum, that determine the state of celestial bodies? In short, the question “what are the coordinates and momentum at a given moment in time?” in relation to quantum objects - an illegal question. After all, not every question is legitimate. For example, it is impossible to answer the question “what color is the Pulkovo meridan?” But it does not follow from this that the possibility of knowledge is limited!

To the same extent, apparently, the question “what are the coordinates and momentum of the electron?” is illegitimate.

The words of Lenin are best applied to the current situation in quantum mechanics: “The movement of bodies is transformed in nature into the movement of something that is not a body with a constant mass, into the movement of something that is an unknown charge of unknown electricity in an unknown ether - this dialectic of materials of transformations carried out in laboratoriums and factories serves in the eyes of idealism... as a confirmation not of the materials of natural dialectics, but as an argument against materials...”).

From what was said above, it is clear that the “incomprehensibility” of quantum mechanics does not provide any basis for idealistic conclusions about the limited possibility of knowing nature.

The idealistic conclusion about the violation of principles, that is, about the unpredictability of events, is also unfounded.

According to quantum mechanics, the state is completely determined by a special quantity, the so-called wave function. There is an equation for it, by solving which the physicist can fully imagine what the result of the experiment will be. And there was no case when the equation of quantum mechanics failed experimenters!

In his time, Lenin showed that the physicists of his time were unable to “directly and immediately rise from metaphysical materials to dialectical materials.” But he foresaw that “modern physics is and will take this step.”
