We need to prove that with such a strategy the first cerrok. Let us assume that, on the contrary, after he has circled some side a, an “almost-cycle” first appears, and before the cycle he will not reach one side b; let x₁, ..., xₙ be the remaining sides of this cycle. These sides had already been circled before the last move (a) of the second cerok, therefore the sides x'₁, ..., x'ₙ, symmetrical to it relative to the diagonal, as well as sides a', symmetrical to a, were circled before this move. And since x₁, ..., xₙ and a form an “almost-cycle”, x'₁, ..., x'ₙ and a' also form an “almost-cycle” (which will not reach side b' before the cycle). M850*. Through the point of intersection of the bisector A of triangle ABC and the segment connecting the bases of the other two bisectors, a straight line is drawn parallel to side BC. Prove that the length of the smaller base of the resulting trapezoid is equal to the resulting lengths of its sides.

Fig. 1.

Let us introduce the following notation: $a, b, c$ are the lengths of the sides of a given triangle, $ABC, A_1, B_1, C_1$ are the bases of its bisectors, $K$ is the point of intersection of the bisector $AA_1$ with the segment $B_1C_1$, $MN$ is the segment drawn through it parallel to side BC (see Fig. 1). As triangles AMN and ABC are prepared, the following are true:

$$|MN|:|BC|=|AK|:|AA_1|,$$
$$|BM|:|AB|=|CN|:|AC|=|A_1K|:|AA_1|.$$

Expressing from here $|BM|, |CN|$ and $|MN|$, we get

$$\frac{|BM|+|CN|}{|MN|}=\frac{(|AB|+|AC|)\cdot|A_1K|/|AA_1|}{|BC| \cdot |AK|/|AA_1|}=\frac{b+c}{a} \cdot \frac{|A_1K|}{|AK|}.$$

Now it remains to prove that $|A_1K|:|AK|=2a/(b+c)$.

The shortest proof is provided by the method of centers of mass, which I discuss in detail in the article by M. B. Balk and V. G. Boltyansky («Kvant», 1984, No. 4, p. 18). Let us place mass 2$a$ at point $A$, and masses $b$ and $c$ at points $B$ and $C$. (Fig. 2). The center of two masses - mass $b$ at point $B$ and mass $a$ at point $A$ - is located at point $C_1$ (since, by the well-known property of the bisector of a triangle $^*$) $|AC_1|:|C_1B|=b:a$); similarly, the center of mass $c$ at point $C$ and the remaining mass $a$ at point $A$ is at $B_1$. Therefore, the common center of mass lies on the segment $B_1C_1$. On the other hand, the center of mass of $b$ and $c$ is the point $A_1(|BA_1|:|A_1C|=c:b)$, therefore the common center of mass lies on the segment $AA_1$, and therefore at the point $K$, and divides it in the ratio $|A_1K|:|KA|=2c:(a+b)$, which was what needed to be proven.

Let us give one more, vector, proof. Let us express the vector $\vec{A}_1$ in terms of the vectors $\vec{b}=\vec{A}C$ and $c=\vec{A}B$ and their lengths, using the bisector property:

$$\vec{A}_1=\vec{AB}+\vec{BA}=\vec{AB}+\frac{|BA_1|}{|BC|} \cdot \vec{BC}=\vec{c}+\frac{c}{b+c} \cdot (\vec{b}-\vec{c})=\frac{b\vec{c}+c\vec{b}}{b+c}.$$

The vector $\vec{A}K$ is expressed similarly in terms of $\vec{b}_1=\vec{A}B_1$ and $\vec{c}_1=\vec{A}C_1$ and the lengths of the vectors $b_1$ and $c_1$:

$$\vec{A}K=\frac{b_1\vec{c}_1+c_1\vec{b}_1}{b_1+c_1}.$$

But $\vec{b}_1=\frac{cb}{a+c}, \vec{c}_1=\frac{bc}{a+b}$, and $\vec{A}K=k\vec{A}_1$, where $k=|AK|:|AA_1|$.

⟦folio 49⟧

From here it is easy to obtain that

$$\overrightarrow{AK} = \frac{b\vec{c} + c\vec{b}}{2a + b + c} = k \frac{b\vec{c} + c\vec{b}}{b + c},$$

that is, $k = (b + c) / (2a + b + c)$. Therefore, $|A_1K|:|KA| = (1/k) - 1 = 2a / (b + c)$.

From the solution of the problem it follows that each of the segments $MK$ and $KN$ is half as long as the side of the trapezoid adjacent to it.

After the soccer player hit, the ball flew in the direction of the goal, located at a distance of $L = 32$ m, at a speed of $v = 25$ m/s at an angle of $\alpha$ to the horizon ($\cos \alpha = 0,8$). ♦ Consider the motion of a ball in the reporting system $K$, moving with speed $\vec{u}$. Let us associate with $K$ a rectangular coordinate system $XYZ$ with the origin at the impact point $A$ such that the plane $XY$ is horizontal and the axis $X$ is parallel to the plane of the goal $P$ (see figure).

The initial speed of the ball in this system

$$\vec{v}_1 = \vec{v} - \vec{u}.$$

In the system $K$ the air is motionless, and the trajectory of the ball $ABC$ will be in the vertical plane in which the vector $\vec{v}_1$ lies, and the trajectory will not be a parabola, since in addition to the gravitational force, the ball is also affected by the resistance force from the air, directed against the instantaneous speed of the ball. Let us immediately note that the nature of the dependence of the resistance force on the speed is not given in the problem and is not required for the solution.

During the flight time $t$ to the goal plane, the ball will move in the system $K$ along the axis $Y$ to a distance $L$, and along the axis $X$ to a distance $s_1$, and (see figure; point $C$ lies on the plane $P$)

$$\frac{s_1}{L} = \frac{u}{v \cos \alpha},$$

where

$$s_1 = \frac{Lu}{v \cos \alpha}.$$

(1)

During the same time $t$ the system $K$ will shift along the axis $X$ in the opposite direction by a distance

$$s_2 = ut.$$

(2)

The actual horizontal displacement of the ball (in

visual reporting system) occurs in the negative direction of the $X$ axis and is equal to
$$s = s_2 - s_1.$$

F859. A ball of radius $R$ and mass $m$ moves along a horizontal surface under the action of a constant tension force $F$ of a light, inextricable thread. The point of attachment of the thread is at a height $h$ above the surface (see Fig.1), the thread is horizontal all the time, the ball moves forward. Is this possible at every maximum friction coefficient?
What is the acceleration of the ball?

◆ When the ball moves, the thread can be parallel to the horizontal surface only if there is friction between the ball and the surface. Otherwise (in the absence of friction), a non-zero moment of force $F$ (relative to the point $O$) would rotate the ball, that is, the ball moves without translation.

Let us write down the condition for the translational motion of the ball (the sum of the moments of the forces of the relative point $O$ acting on the ball is equal to zero):
$$F_{\text{тр}} R = F(R - h).$$
From here
$$F_{\text{тр}} = F \left(1 - \frac{h}{R}\right).$$
On the other side,
$$F_{\text{тр}} = \mu mg.$$
From the last two equations we find $\mu$:
$$\mu = \frac{F}{mg} \left(1 - \frac{h}{R}\right).$$
We find the acceleration of the ball from Newton's law:
$$ma = F - F_{\text{тр}} = F \frac{h}{R},$$
where
$$a = \frac{F}{m} \frac{h}{R}.$$
L. G. Markovich

F860. In a finite non-conducting hole with an angle of 2α at the apex at a depth $h$ there are two small equally charged bodies connected by a thread (Fig.1); the mass of each body is $m$. After surviving, the body threads begin to slide up the surface of the hole and fly out of the hole at a height of $H$ above its base with a speed of $v$. The coefficient of friction on the surface of the hole is $\mu = a \lg a (a < 1)$. Determine the charges of the body.

◆ At the initial moment of time (at the moment the thread burns out), the entire energy of the system is the electrical energy of the body:
$$W_1 = E_1 = k \frac{Q^2}{2(H - h) \lg a}$$
(potential energy at depth $h$ is taken equal to zero). At the moment the body leaves the hole, the energy of the system consists of electrical energy
$$E_2 = k \frac{Q^2}{2H \lg a},$$ kinetic energy of body $K = mv^2$ and their potential energy $\Pi = 2mgh$:
$$W_2 = E_2 + K + \Pi.$$
According to the law of conservation of energy, $W_1 = W_2 + A,$

OR

$$E_1 = E_2 + K + \Pi + A \Rightarrow E_1 - E_2 = K + \Pi + A,$$

where $A$ is the energy spent on overcoming friction forces. Let's find this energy.

Let us consider the movement of a body in a small area $\Delta s$ of the dimple surface such that the electrostatic repulsion force $f_s$ in this area can be considered constant. The friction force $f_{\tau p}$ acting on the body in this area is constant and equal to (Fig. 2)

$$f_{\tau p} = \mu N = \mu (f_s \cos \alpha + mg \sin \alpha).$$

The work done by the friction force in section $\Delta s$ is numerically equal to

$$\Delta a = f_{\tau p} \cdot \Delta s = \mu f_s \cos \alpha \cdot \Delta s + \mu mg \sin \alpha \cdot \Delta s =$$

$$= \mu ctg \alpha f_s (\sin \alpha \cdot \Delta s) + \mu tg \alpha mg (\cos \alpha \cdot \Delta s) =$$

$$= \mu ctg \alpha (f_s \cdot \Delta x) + \mu tg \alpha (mg \cdot \Delta h)$$

(see Fig. 3). But $f_s \cdot \Delta x$ is the change in the electrical energy of the body, and $mg \cdot \Delta h$ is the change in its potential energy. Thus, the total work done by the friction force during body motion is equal in absolute value

$$A = \mu ctg \alpha \cdot (E_1 - E_2) + \mu tg \alpha \cdot \Pi.$$

So, according to the law of conservation of energy,

$$E_1 - E_2 = K + \Pi + \mu ctg \alpha \cdot (E_1 - E_2) + \mu tg \alpha \cdot \Pi.$$

F861. In an oscillatory RLC circuit, the resistance is low, so the oscillations are weakly damped in it. To obtain an insignificant oscillating system, there must be no less energy lost due to Joule losses in the resistance.

By Farad's law of induction $\mathcal{E}_{\text{инд}} = -\frac{\Delta \phi}{\Delta t}$ and $I_{\text{инд}} = -\frac{1}{R} \frac{\Delta \phi}{\Delta t}$. When the coil is rapidly stretched, $\Delta t \rightarrow 0$, and since the current cannot reach infinitely large values, then in this case $\Delta \phi \rightarrow 0$, that is, in this process of conservation of magnetic flux $\Phi$.

The work done during stretching only goes to increase the energy of the magnetic field:

$$\Delta A = \Delta W_+ = \frac{L' I'^2}{2} - \frac{L I^2}{2} = \frac{L'^2 I'^2}{2L'} - \frac{L^2 I^2}{2L} =$$

$$= \frac{\phi^2}{2L} \left( \frac{L}{L'} - 1 \right) = \frac{\phi^2}{2L} \left( \frac{l + \Delta l}{l} - 1 \right) = \frac{\phi^2}{2L} \frac{\Delta l}{l} = \frac{L I^2_a}{2} \frac{\Delta l}{l},$$

⟦folio 52⟧

where the primes are placed at the value related to the state of the system after stretching the coil, $I_a$ is the amplitude value of the current (at the moment of stretching, the current is maximum).

The coil is circuited when the charge on the capacitor is maximum and, therefore, the current in the circuit is zero. Therefore, during shatininikai no work is done.

With low damping, the loss of energy due to the release of heat at the resistance is small compared to the total energy of the system, so we can assume that over the period the amplitude of the oscillations changes little, the current is almost sinusoidal. Then we can obtain the following estimate for energy losses for half a period:

$$\Delta W_- = I_a^2 R \frac{T}{2} = \frac{1}{2} I_a^2 R \pi \sqrt{LC},$$

where $I_a$ is the effective current value.

The oscillations will be undamped if $\Delta W_+ \geqslant \Delta W_-$, that is

$$\frac{LI_a^2}{2} \frac{\Delta l}{l} \geqslant \frac{1}{2} I_a^2 R \pi \sqrt{LC},$$

where

$$\frac{\Delta l}{l} \geqslant \pi R \sqrt{\frac{C}{L}}.$$

Note. Put $v_2=v_3=1$ and show the recurrence relation

$$v_n = v_2v_{n-1} + v_3 \cdot v_{n-2} + ... + v_{n-1}v_n, n \geqslant 3.$$

Use this to prove the relation $(\alpha(z))^2 - \alpha(z) = z$, where $\alpha(z) = v_2z + v_3z^2 + v_4z^3 + ...$, find $\alpha(z)$ from there and differentiate.

Answer. $v_n = \frac{(2n-4)!}{(n-2)!(n-1)!}$. These numbers are called Catalan numbers.
