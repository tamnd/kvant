Inversion
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. M. UROEV

In this article we talked about one remarkable geometric transformation of the plane. This transformation - inversion - allows you to solve problems that are very difficult to even approach at first glance.

§ 1. What is inversion

Suppose a circle C of radius R with center O is given on the plane α. The inverse of the plane α with respect to this circle is called a mapping in which each point A of the plane α, with the exception of point O, is associated with a point A' lying on the ray (OA) such that

$$|OA'| = \frac{R^2}{|OA|}.$$  (1)

From this definition it is clear that during inversion, points A and A' change places, mapping into each other. Center O of circle C, called the center of inversion, has no image, and no point during inversion falls into it (Fig. 1). The interior of a circle bounded by circle C is transferred by inversion to the exterior region, since from the inequality |OA| < R it follows that |OA'| > R, and vice versa, the outer area is mapped to the inner one.

The points of circle C remain in place during inversion, and from the fact that the inside of the circle goes into the outside, it follows that there are no other fixed points.

Sometimes inversion is called symmetry about a circle (by analogy with symmetry about a circle. Like axial symmetry, inversion about a circle $C$ has an interesting property: its repeated execution returns the points to their original position. Note also that if the point $A$ is chosen closer and closer to $O$, then $|OA'|$ will increase without limit. Inversion as would “turn” the inside of the circle onto its outside and, accordingly, the outside of the circle “turns inside” onto its inside.

This is observed with the idea of ​​adding a point $\infty$ that does not belong to the “infinite distance” plane and considering that during inversion the center $O$ goes to $\infty$, and $\infty$ to the point $O$.

§ 2. Properties of inversion

Let us see how the lines and circles of the plane $a$ are transformed during inversion. It follows directly from the definition that a straight line passing through the center of inversion goes into itself. In this case, the center $O$ and $\infty$ change places. The primaya, as it were, “turns along the plane”: the part of it that is outside the circle, together with the “infinity,” turns into the diameter of the circle, and the diameter turns into two rays, and the point $O$ turns into $\infty$, as if “connecting” these rays.

What happens to the line that does not pass through the center $O$?

Task 1. If, during inversion with respect to a certain circle $C$, the points $A$ and $B$ of the circle $T$ transform into each other, then the circle $T$ transforms into itself (see Fig. 5).

Solution. The center of inversion $O$ must lie on the straight line $AB$, which transforms into itself, and $OA \cdot OB = R^2$, where $R$ is the radius of the circle $C$. Let us draw a tangent $OD$ to the circle $T$. By the proven theorem $|OA| \cdot |OB| = |OD|^2$. So, $|OD| = R$. Let us now draw an arbitrary secant $OK$ to the circle $T$. By the theorem about the square of the tangent $|OK| \cdot |OL| = |OD|^2 = R^2$, and, therefore, the points $K$ and $L$ transform into each other, and therefore the circle $T$ transforms into itself.

Task 4 (Polemeus' theorem). Prove that in any cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ the product of the lengths of the diagonals is equal to the sum of the products of the lengths of opposite sides (Fig. 7):

$$|AC| \cdot |BD| = |AB| \cdot |CD| + |BC| \cdot |AD|.$$

Proof. Let's perform an inversion centered at vertex $A$. Then the circumscribed circle $T$ will go to the line $T'$, the vertices $B, C, D$ - respectively, to the points $B', C', D'$ on the line $T'$. Let us denote $R$ as the radius of the circumscribed circle. Turning to Problem 2, we get

$$|C'B'| = \frac{R^2 \cdot |BC|}{|AB| \cdot |AC|},$$

$$|B'D'| = \frac{|BD| \cdot R^2}{|AD| \cdot |AB|},$$

$$|C'D'| = \frac{R^2 \cdot |CD|}{|AC| \cdot |AD|}$$

From this it is clear that the statement of Polemaeus’ theorem expresses the following fact: $|B'D'| = |B'C'| + |C'D'|$. Indeed, substituting the found values ​​of $|C'B'|, |B'D'|, |C'D'|$ into this equality and bringing them to a common denominator, we arrive at the desired correspondence.

§ 4. Construction problems

Inversion can be used with great success when solving construction problems.

The solution to all problems is based on the method of constructing a point $A'$ symmetrical to a given point $A$ relative to the circle $C$ (see Fig. 14). This method is as follows (for the point $A$ lying outside the circle $C$). Draw a tangent $AM$ and from point $M$ lower the perpendicular to $OA$. The base of the perpendicular is the required point $A'$ (see problem 3 from § 3).

§ 5. Inversion and stereographic projection

Consider the sphere $Q$ tangent to the plane $a$ at point $O$. Let point $N$ be diametrically opposite to point $S$. The stereographic projection of the sphere $Q$ onto the plane $a$ is called a mapping, which to each point $B$ of the sphere, with the exception of the point $N$, associates the point $A$ of the plane $a$, obtained as a result of the intersection of the line $NB$ with the plane $a$ (Fig.16). Note that any point $A$ of the $a$ plane is the image of some point $B$ of the sphere in stereography

physical projection. Point $B$ is the intersection of line $NA$ with sphere $Q$.

Let us show how the set of points on the sphere is arranged, corresponding to some well-known lines in the plane $a$. In this case, it will be convenient for us to use “geographical” terminology, calling the sphere $Q$ a globe, and the points $S$ and $N$ the south and north pole.

Parallels, that is, circles drawn on the sphere “parallel” to the plane $a$, are mapped onto concentric circles with center $S$. The “South Pole” $S$, which belongs to both the sphere and the plane, turns into itself. The “North Pole” has no image.

Let us construct a set of points of a sphere mapped onto a straight line $l$ lying in the plane $a$. Since a single plane $B$ can be drawn through the pole $N$ and the line $l$, then all the rays emanating from the point $N$ and carrying out the stereographic projection of the original set onto the line $l$ lie in the plane $B$ (Fig. 17). Consequently, the line $l$ is associated with the set of points of the sphere belonging to the intersection of the sphere $Q$ with the plane $B$, i.e., the circle with the discarded point $N$. In particular, the "meridans" passing through points $S$ and $N$ are mapped onto rays emanating from point $S$. Note that if the point $A$ on the plane $a$ moves away indefinitely from the point $S$ (for example, along a straight line), then the corresponding point $B$ of the sphere approaches the pole $N$. This message makes it possible to redefine the concept of an infinite distant point of the plane $a$ as a mental image of the pole $N$ under stereographic projection.
