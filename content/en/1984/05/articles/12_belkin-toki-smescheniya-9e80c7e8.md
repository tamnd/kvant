The intersection of members refers to the section $S_1$ in one direction, and to the section $S_2$ in the other:

$$p_1 + \frac{q v_1^2}{2} = p_2 + \frac{q v_2^2}{2}.$$

This equality expresses the relationship between pressure and fluid velocity. It's called Bernoulli's law. According to this law, where the speed of fluid flow is greater, the pressure is less and vice versa. Note that the quantity $\frac{q v^2}{2}$ is the kinetic energy of a unit volume of liquid. Thus, at any place in the work, the sum of pressure and kinetic energy per unit volume of liquid is the same.

Bias currents

In the section “School in «Kvant»” in the note “Electrical vibrations. Oscillatory circuit" (see «Kvant», 1983, No. 11) it has already been said that the cause of the appearance of a magnetic field can be not only moving electric charges (toks), but also an alternating electric field. This second source of Maxwell's magnetic field is called displacement. The existence of displacement currents is one of the basic laws of electrodynamics. Therefore, we will consider this issue in more detail. Let us show with a specific example when displacement currents arise and what their magnitudes are.

![Diagram of magnetic field around a cylinder](image)

Imagine that the space between two concentric conducting spheres, charged with charges of the same model, but opposite in sign (spherical capacitor), is filled with a conducting medium (see figure). Then, in this medium, currents flow along the radii, and the capacitor is discharged. How is the magnetic field that arises during the discharge of a capacitor “arranged”?

In this problem there is no preferred direction and it will not be possible to disrupt the system of magnetic lines that satisfies the symmetry conditions. What does this mean? Only that a magnetic field will not arise at all during the discharge of a spherical capacitor. How is it that currents flow, but there is no magnetic field? This means that there is another “source”, a created magnetic field that compensates for the field created by the currents.

Let us calculate the current strength per unit cross-sectional area, that is, the current density, at a distance $r$ from the center of the sphere. The total current $i$ is equal to the rate of change of the capacitor charge:

$$i = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}.$$

This current is uniformly distributed over the area of ​​a sphere of radius $r$; therefore, the current density

$$j = \frac{i}{S} = \frac{1}{4\pi r^2} \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}.$$

How does the electric field change when a capacitor is discharged? Between the plates of a spherical capacitor the field is the same as the field of the exact charge $Q$ placed at the center of the sphere, therefore at a distance $r$ from the center of the transverse field is determined by the formula

$$E = k \frac{Q}{r^2} = \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r^2}.$$

Then the rate of change in directionality is equal to

$$\frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r^2} \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}.$$

Let's compare formulas (1) and (2). As can be seen, the current density and the rate of change of the electric field are proportional to each other. If we assume that an alternating electric field, just like an ordinary current, creates a magnetic field, then we can explain the absence of a magnetic field in the capacitor by mutual compensation of magnetic fields.
