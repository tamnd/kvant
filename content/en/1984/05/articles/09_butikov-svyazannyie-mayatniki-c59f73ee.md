The patterns of oscillations of complex systems, such as the binding of matniki, can be studied experimentally using the simplest means. All you need to reproduce the experiments described here are two identical small groups (for example, massive weights (for example, massive weights), two tripods, which can be successfully replaced with the backs of chairs, strong threads, a stopwatch or a watch with a second hand.

We attach weights to threads of equal length 50-60 cm, and tie the threads to a transverse thread about 1 m long, placing them symmetrically at a distance of 40-50 cm from each other. We secure the ends of the transverse thread in tripods or tie them to the backs of chairs. As a result, we get a system of two mats, approximately the same as shown in the photograph. This is tying the matniki. It is desirable to achieve the possibility of a more perfect symmetrical system. You also need to know how to change the distance between the tripods, the regularity, thereby increasing the transverse thread.

Despite its simplicity, this device makes it possible to verify many interesting observations. Let us limit ourselves to studying only the free (or natural) oscillations of the system in the case when the mats swing perpendicular to the plane of their equilibrium.

While holding one of the mats in the equilibrium position, we tilt the other one at a small angle in the transverse direction, then release them simultaneously. We will closely monitor their future behavior. We will see that the amplitude of oscillations of the first initial deflected mat is gradually decreasing, but at the same time the second mat is swinging more and more strongly. At some point, the matniks will oscillate with the same amplitudes. But after some time, the first matnik is completely abandoned, “freezes” in the equilibrium position, and the second swings so much that its amplitude will be the same as at the initial moment of the first matnik. The vibrations seem to completely “transition” from one mat to another.

We continue to watch further. Now all processes occur in the reverse order: the amplitude of the second mat decreases, and the first swings until it reaches its previous amplitude. Then everything repeats again.

Why are vibrations transmitted from one pendulum to another? Let us observe the cross thread to which they are called. It is easy to notice that when the pendulums oscillate, it does not remain motionless: by deviating to the side, one of the pendulums pulls it along with it and thereby sets in motion the suspension point of the second pendulum. Thus, the crossed thread connects the pendulums with each other. That’s why the mints are called “connected.”
Intuition suggests that pendulums are connected the more tightly, the more the thread sags. Let's check this experimentally. Let's reduce the tension of the thread, slightly licking the points of its attachment, and repeat the experiment described above. We will see that the full cycle of vibration transmission from one pendulum to another and back is shortened. For example, as the tension of the thread increases, the connection between the pendulums weakens, and the exchange of vibrations occurs more slowly. But it is interesting that even when the weakened connection is noticed, the mints completely exchange energy. (Of course, if we take into account the gradual attenuation of the oscillations of the pendulums due to friction, then with a very weak connection of energy exchange, the decay time of the oscillations may become longer. The oscillations of the excited pendulum will stop before it has time to transfer a noticeable part of its energy to another pendulum. Under such conditions, the connections of the pendulums become insignificant, and they can be considered independent.)

Let's think about why, after the amplitudes, and therefore the energies of the pendants, become the same, the process of energy exchange does not stop, but continues until the excited pendant transfers all its energy to another. It's all about phase correspondence: when the amplitudes are equalized, the oscillations of the swinging pendulum lag behind in phase by the fourth period. The speed of its suspension point is maximum, and the force acting on it from the side of the transverse thread is directed in the same direction where the suspension point is moving. This means that the most favorable conditions have developed here for the continuation of energy transfer within the direction limit.

We saw that when considering the method of excitation (the initial deflection of one of the pendulums), the oscillations that arose are not harmonic (although with a weak connection they can be considered “almost harmonic” with a slowly changing amplitude). Is it possible to excite vibrations in such a way that the movement of each of the pendulums is purely harmonious? It is easy to say that for vibrations perpendicular to the equilibrium plane, there are two types of motion.

First, you can tilt both pendulums at the same small angle in the same direction and release them at the same time without pushing. Then all systems will be qualitatively as a single whole. Try to excite such oscillations and change their frequency $\omega_1$. Since the pendulums oscillate in the same phases, the displacement depends on time for each of them and is expressed by the same harmonic function:

$$x_1(t) = x_2(t) = x_m \cos \omega_1 t,$$

(1)

where the amplitude $x_m$ is equal to the initial deviation of the pendulums from the equilibrium position.

Secondly, you can answer both pendulums at the same angles in opposite directions and release them at the same time. In this case, the pendulums will also perform harmonic oscillations with the same amplitude and the same frequency $\omega_2$ (this frequency is slightly greater than $\omega_1$), but in opposite phases. Therefore, the time dependence of the mixture has the form

$$x_1(t) = x_m \cos \omega_2 t,$$

(2)

How does the model relate to the previously studied beneys? Let us recall the initial conditions under which each of them appears: for the first matrix it is necessary to deflect in one direction, for the second - in the opposite direction. If we want to excite both models at the same time, then we will get the initial conditions corresponding to the excitation of beneia: one matrix is ​​rejected, the second is in an equilibrium position. Thus, we come to the conclusion that beneya is obtained as a result of the superposition of normal matrices with frequencies $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ and equal amplitudes.

We obtain the mixing of each matrix at any moment of time $t$ by adding the corresponding expressions from formulas (1) and (2) and using the known trigonometric precisions:

$$x_1(t) = x_m(\cos \omega_1 t + \cos \omega_2 t) = 2x_m \cos \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \cos \frac{\omega_2 + \omega_1}{2} t,$$

$$x_2(t) = x_m(\cos \omega_1 t - \cos \omega_2 t) = 2x_m \sin \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \sin \frac{\omega_2 + \omega_1}{2} t.$$

If the difference between the frequencies of the normal matrix $\Delta \omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1$ is small compared to the average frequency $\omega = (\omega_2 + \omega_1)/2$, then in the right-hand sides of formulas (3) the first trigonometric function changes slowly compared to the second. This means that $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ describe “almost harmonic” oscillations with a frequency $\omega$ and slowly pulsating amplitudes. The corresponding graphs are shown in figure. The representation of a complex oscillation in the form of a sum of harmonic oscillations is spoken of as decomposition into a spectrum. The spectrum of any transverse vibration of matrices in systems we study, including beneys, consists of only two components with frequencies $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, since these exhaust the entire set of transverse normal vibrations. The entire variety of possible free oscillations that arise under different initial conditions is due to different correspondences of the amplitudes and phases of these spectral components. Try to set different initial conditions than before (for example, unequal initial deviations) and watch for the occurrence of oscillations.

We can do many more interesting experiments with our connected matrices, if we do not limit ourselves only to transverse movements. In this system there are three more models corresponding to the oscillations of matrices in the plane formed by the threads of equilibrium. Try to excite these models and follow them.
