Neutron star, black hole or...?

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. A. BRONSHTEN

It all started quite positively. In 1977, American astronomers B. Stephenson and N. Sandulak, having completed many years of work, published a new catalog of Milky Way stars with bright hydrogen lines in the spectrum.

The very next year, one of the stars in the new catalogue, designated SS 433, attracted everyone's attention. This is a faint star (14th magnitude) in the constellation Aquila; it can only be observed with large telescopes. However, if bright lines are observed in the spectrum of a star, then the star is exhibiting non-learning activity. They decided to “test” her. It turned out that in the vicinity of this star there is a source of radiogreen, reminiscent of the remnant of a supernova explosion. An X-ray source was also detected at almost the same point in the sky. But the most surprising thing was still connected with the spectrum of the star itself.

Beginning in July 1978, astronomers from the University of California (USA) began to systematically photograph the spectra of the new object. They turned out to be kind of strange. Instead of each linear hydrogen, gel and other gases, three lines were observed: one where it should be, and two more on the sides. It was soon discovered that the lateral lines would mix: they seemed to converge, approaching each other. In September of the same year, the extreme lines converged, but did not coincide with the middle line! Then they began to disperse again. Observations carried out over a period of one and a half years showed that the lines converge and diverge with a period of 165 days.

How can we explain the splitting of each spectral line into three?

In principle, there are three possible reasons: displacement of lines in a gravitational field, splitting of lines by a strong magnetic field and an effect associated with the movement of the radiation source (Doppler effect). The first two mechanisms were answered because there was no corresponding evidence for them. The Doppler effect remains - a displacement of the lines when the source moves relatively is observed. But this means that the object under study must contain at least three sources of radiation: one stationary and two moving in opposite directions. According to calculations, the speeds of the latter reach 80,000 km/s - more than the speed of light!

What is this system of sources? Perhaps this is a central star with two satellites orbiting it with a period of 165 days? But such a period and the indicated giant speeds of satellite motion correspond to an orbital radius that is 1000 times greater than the average distance from the Earth to the Sun, and a mass of the system of the order of 10^10 solar masses. Such stars couldn't be simpler!

This object cannot be a quasar (a distant quasi-stellar source of radio emission): the object is located within our Galaxy.

Development of a possible model of the star SS 433 began. One of the first was proposed by the English astrophones E. Fabian and M. Riess. According to their hypothesis, a certain central body, as a result of an as yet unknown process, ejects two gas streams in two opposite directions at speeds of the order of 80,000 km/s. At the same time, all systems experience precession - the well-known movement of the system’s axis along a cone, like a child’s wolf or our own planet. The period of this precession is equal, according to updated data, to 164 days. But what is in the center?

According to Fabian and Riess, the central body could be, for example, a black hole. Soviet astrophilist I. S. Shklovsky expressed another hypothesis. He proposed that in the center there is a neutron star with a strong magnetic field, and not far from it there is a normal star. The matter of a normal star, under the influence of attraction, flows towards the neutron star, forming a gas disk around it. The magnetic field rapidly rotating the neutron star, like a giant fountain, throws out two gas scabs: one in the direction of the Earth, the other in the opposite direction.

Developing this model, the Soviet scientist I. D. Novkov and his collaborators proposed that a neutron star emits streams of fast particles, which, interacting with the matter of the disk, accelerate this matter to ultra-high speeds.

Still, we cannot now give a definitive answer to the question of what is in the center of object SS 433. Research continues!
