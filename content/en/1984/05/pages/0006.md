For example, take the old theory of magnetism, in which a magnetized piece of steel was considered to be a magnetic dipole, consisting of two magnetic poles, or “magnetic charges” (analogous to an electric dipole). Using this idea, physicists create a magnetostat, on which all practice and technology of using magnets is based. This practice quickly advanced the theory of "magnetic charges". Meanwhile, it is now well known that no magnetic charges actually exist. And when we talk about magnetic poles now, we always stipulate that this is a fictitious concept.

It turns out that the criterion of practice cannot serve as a reliable basis for clarifying the truth of our ideas about a particular subject? This, of course, is not a separate question.

One of the largest theoretical physicists of our time, Richard Feynman, trying to figure out what the philosophical interpretation of a physical law is, illustrates the significance of this issue with the following example.

“Let those who insist that all that matters is the agreement between theory and experiment imagine a conversation between a Mayan astronomer and his student. The Mayans were able to imagine with amazing accuracy, for example, the time of an eclipse, the position of the Moon, Venus and other planets in the sky. All this was done with the help of arithmetic... They did not have the slightest idea about the rotation of celestial bodies.

Imagine that a young man comes to our astronomer and says: “This is what came to my mind. Maybe all this is believable, maybe these are balls of stone... and their movement can be calculated completely differently.” Further, to learn that the young man should not yet calculate the movements of the planets, the Mayan astronomer will answer him that we can already calculate eclipses quite accurately, so there is no need to bother with your ideas.

As we see, Feynman concludes, it is not an easy task to decide whether or not it is worth thinking about what lies behind our theories.

This is indeed no easy task. After all, if we accept unconditionally the statement that the criterion of truth is practice, then such a statement, as can be seen from the examples given, can lead to stagnation in science.

This difficult task was brilliantly solved by V.I. Lenin.

Arguing that “the point of view of life, practice must be the first and main point of view of the theory of knowledge,” Lenin adds: “Of course, we must not forget that the criterion of practice can never, in the very essence of the matter, completely confirm or refute any human idea. This criterion is also insistently “indefinite” in order not to allow a person’s knowledge to turn into “absolute”, and at the same time insistently defined in order to wage a relentless struggle against all varieties of idealism and agnosticism).**.**

“...From here,” Lenin continues, “follows the recognition that the only path to this truth is the path of science, standing on a materialist point of view.”*** (Italics mine. - I.K.)

The overwhelming majority of physicists are consciously or spontaneously guided by precisely this Leninist concept of the criterion of practice.

Experience served Lenin as a reliable support when he formulated the foundations of his philosophical views. The following example can illustrate this.

Lenin considered it necessary to test the history of science (that is, test experimentally, as a physicist would say) one of the main provisions of dialectics - the so-called “unity of opposites.” Lenin begins his note “On the Question of Dialectics”: “The disintegration of the unified and the knowledge of its contradictory parts... is the essence (one of the “essences”, one of the main, if not the main, features or feature) of dialectics.〈...〉 The correctness of this aspect of the content of dialectics must be verified by the history of science."**.)

*) Agnosticism is a philosophical doctrine that denies the possibility of knowing the objective world, limiting the role of science only to the knowledge of phenomena. (My note. - I.K.)
