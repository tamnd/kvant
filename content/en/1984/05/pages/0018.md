What conducts current best?

Based on their ability to conduct electric current, all solids are divided into three large groups: metals, semiconductors and dielectrics (or insulators). Here we can add superconductors - a number of metals and their compounds that conduct current at very low temperatures without loss of energy; their electrical resistance is zero.

The best electrical conductors are metals. The resistivity of metals is of the order of $10^{-8} - 10^{-6}$ Ohm m. Dielectrics have a resistivity greater than $10^{3}$ Ohm m. Semiconductors occupy an intermediate position.

figure 1 shows the resistivity values ​​of some metals, semiconductors and dielectrics. You see, the resistivity of the core is $10^{24}$ (!) times less than that of Teflon. No physical characteristic changes as much from substance to substance as resistivity.

Dielectrics, semiconductors, and metals are widely used in technology as insulators. Semiconductors form the basis of modern electronics. Semiconductor devices can be found in many devices, from household radio equipment to modern computers, the creation of which would be unthinkable without semiconductor elements. Connecting wires are made from metals, as well as various electrical power plants (current generators, electric motors, transformers, etc.).

Recently, in technology starting from invading and superconductors. The main advantage of superconductors is their ability to conduct current without losing energy. This property of superconductors turns out to be very valuable not only in the design of future power transmission lines from powerful hydroelectric power stations to consumers, but also in those areas where energy costs are relatively low. In particular, when constructing super-powerful (in terms of the number of operations performed, and not in terms of power consumption) computers with a computing speed on a table or a thousand million operations per second.*)

The disadvantage of essentially superconductors is the need to cool them to very low temperatures (hereinafter, the “high-temperature” Nb$_3$Ge alloy goes into the superconducting state only at 21.3 K). Therefore, it is clear that the task of creating a current conductor that would become a superconductor at ordinary temperatures or at least at the temperature of liquid nitrogen equal to 77 K is very tempting (liquid nitrogen is much cheaper than liquid gel; it is now used even when transporting perishable products). Many original proposals have already been made in this direction. Interesting and promising ideas were expressed in 1964.

*) See the article by L. G. Aslamazov “Computers on superconductors” in «Kvant» No. 11 for 1983.
