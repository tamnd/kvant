$$E_1 = E_2 + K + \Pi + A \Rightarrow E_1 - E_2 = K + \Pi + A,$$

where $A$ is the energy spent on overcoming friction forces. Let's find this energy.

Let us consider the movement of a body in a small area $\Delta s$ of the dimple surface such that the electrostatic repulsion force $f_s$ in this area can be considered constant. The friction force $f_{\tau p}$ acting on the body in this area is constant and equal to (Fig. 2)

$$f_{\tau p} = \mu N = \mu (f_s \cos \alpha + mg \sin \alpha).$$

The work done by the friction force in section $\Delta s$ is numerically equal to

$$\Delta a = f_{\tau p} \cdot \Delta s = \mu f_s \cos \alpha \cdot \Delta s + \mu mg \sin \alpha \cdot \Delta s =$$

$$= \mu ctg \alpha f_s (\sin \alpha \cdot \Delta s) + \mu tg \alpha mg (\cos \alpha \cdot \Delta s) =$$

$$= \mu ctg \alpha (f_s \cdot \Delta x) + \mu tg \alpha (mg \cdot \Delta h)$$

(see Fig. 3). But $f_s \cdot \Delta x$ is the change in the electrical energy of the body, and $mg \cdot \Delta h$ is the change in its potential energy. Thus, the total work done by the friction force during body motion is equal in absolute value

$$A = \mu ctg \alpha \cdot (E_1 - E_2) + \mu tg \alpha \cdot \Pi.$$

So, according to the law of conservation of energy,

$$E_1 - E_2 = K + \Pi + \mu ctg \alpha \cdot (E_1 - E_2) + \mu tg \alpha \cdot \Pi.$$

Substituting the expressions for $E_1, E_2, K, \Pi$ and $\mu = a tg \alpha$, we get

$$\frac{kQ^2}{2(H - h)tg \alpha} - \frac{kQ^2}{2Htg \alpha} =$$

$$= mv^2 + 2mgh + a \left( \frac{kQ^2}{2(H - h)tg \alpha} - \frac{kQ^2}{2Htg \alpha} \right) + 2a tg^2 \alpha \cdot mgh.$$

From here comes $Q$:

$$Q = \sqrt{\frac{2mH(H - h)tg \alpha \cdot (v^2 + 2gh(1 + a tg^2 \alpha))}{kh(1 - a)}}.$$
