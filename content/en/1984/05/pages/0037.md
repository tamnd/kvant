Annihilation and birth of couples

The textbook “Physics 10” says that when a positron meets an electron, these particles disappear (annihilate), generating high-energy photons in their place. The reverse process is also possible—the formation of an electron-positron pair.

Let's look at these processes in more detail. Let us show, for example, that during annihilation one photon cannot be produced.

Let us imagine that in some reporting system an electron is motionless, and a positron attacks it with a speed of $v_0$. Then we will move to a reporting system moving at a constant speed $v_0/2$ (you can switch to any other inertial reporting system according to Galileo’s relative principle, which is discussed in detail in the textbook “Physics 8”). In this system, the speed of the positron is $v_0/2$, but the electron is no longer stationary - its speed is $v_0/2$. Thus, in the selected reporting system, both particles move with equal but oppositely directed velocities. Since the electron and positron have the same mass $m$, their total momentum $m\vec{v}_0/2 - m\vec{v}_0/2$ is zero.

Now it is clear why only one photon cannot be born as a result of annihilation. After all, a photon is a quantum of light, and its speed in all inertial reporting systems is the same and equal to $c$. Therefore, in the reporting system we have chosen, the photon would also have a non-zero momentum. This means that the basic law of nature would be violated - the law of conservation of momentum, which governs any processes occurring in a closed system. But everything falls into place if we assume that during annihilation two photons are born flying in opposite directions (see figure). Then their total momentum is also zero, and the law of conservation of momentum is not violated.

Using the law of conservation of energy, we can find the frequency and wavelength of the corresponding radiation. We will assume that the speed of the particle is compared to the speed of light. Then the main supply of their energy is the rest energy, determined by the Eischstein formula $E = mc^2$. Equating the rest energy of a particle to the energy $hv$ of quantum radiation, we obtain

$$2mc^2 = 2hv,$$

where

$$v = \frac{mc^2}{h} \approx 1,3 \cdot 10^{20} \text{ Гц},$$

$$\lambda = \frac{c}{v} = \frac{h}{mc} \approx 2,4 \cdot 10^{-12} \text{ м},$$

which corresponds to gram radiation.

Similarly, it can be shown that a γ-quantum flying in vacuum cannot by itself give birth to an electron-positron pair. To do this, you need a third (for example, the core), which can be given an “extra” impulse. The process of pair creation is also possible when two photons collide.
