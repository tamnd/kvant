A simple way to enhance the magnetic field is to scavenge the plasma layer (that is, to decrease the value of $S$). This idea is used to provide the simplest explanation for the giant magnetic fields of pulsars. A pulsar is a compact neutron star with a radius $r$ of the order of 10 km. If we assume that a neutron star was formed by the descent of an ordinary star with a radius of $r_0$ about 1 million km, then, during the process of its descent, its radius decreased by a factor of $10^5$. Magnetic flux conservation is introduced.

$$\pi r_0^2 B_0 = \pi r^2 B,$$

where $B_0$ is the field of the original star, $B$ is the field of the pulsar. Consequently, during the downward slope the field is enhanced by $(r_0/r)^2 = 10^{10}$ times. Therefore, the modest magnetic field of an ordinary star with a magnetic induction of, say, 0.01 T turns into a field with a magnetic induction of $10^8$ T.

On the other hand, plasma movements lead to bending and entanglement of magnetic lines. And for small-scale fields, as we have seen, Joule losses become significant.

It turns out that there may be such motions of the plasma that the effect of a successful large-scale field predominates or at least is not inferior to the weakening. In this case, the moving plasma works as a generator.

It is natural to call such a machine “dynamics” (the generator was once called a dynamo machine). The action of a dynamo is usually demonstrated by the example of a current-carrying frame rotating in the field of a magnet.

Hydromagnetic dynamo

The dynamics in the conducting medium that we are talking about should, unlike a technical generator, operate without wires, windings, and most importantly, without an external magnetic field, which must be present in a conventional generator *). Since the source of energy in this dynamo is only hydro-
dynamic motion of plasma, it was called “hydromagnetic”. To trigger a hydromagnetic dianon, only a weak initial magnetic field is zeroed. Generally weak fields exist due to various effects of interaction between charged parts of the plasma.

The idea of ​​explaining the magnetic fields of the Earth and the Sun using hydromagnetic movements was put forward by the famous English physicist J. Larmore in 1919. But is it possible for a self-excited hydromagnetic dianono to “work” without external sources of EMF? A positive answer to this question is best used by an example first demonstrated in 1971 by Academician Ya. B. Zeldovich to participants in a symposium in Krakow.

Let's take a ring tourniquet consisting of individual rubber rings (figure 3, a). Let the rubber band imitate magnetic lines that have the same direction (it is convenient to place it with arrows). Let's fold the strand into a figure eight (figure 3, b), place one ring of the figure 8 on top of the other and stretch the doubled strand to the previous size (figure 3, c). Pay attention to the direction of the arrows; they are the same. Thus, the number of magnetic lines passing through any cross-section of the bundle has doubled. By repeating the process of folding and stretching, you can each time double the number of magnetic lines in the bundle until the elasticity of the rubber accommodates this. Note that if you started from some point in the original tourniquet, you could return to the same point by going around one circle. After doubling the return to the same point, two rounds of the circle are required. Thus, the doubled bundle is not entirely equivalent to the original one. To get rid of this, you can each time cut the strand at the shiba point of the figure eight and stitch the freed circles, then folding them into one double strand, equivalent to the original one.
