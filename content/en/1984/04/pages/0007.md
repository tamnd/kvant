In gaseous stars (such as our Sun), the magnetic field is generated in an outer, partially ionized shell about 1/10th the radius of the star.

So, cosmic magnetic fields are created by currents flowing in an electrically conductive medium. In a similar way, fields are created in the electromagnet coil (figure 1, 6). But to maintain current in the electromagnet, an external energy source is needed. Meanwhile, in space conditions, “battery” EMF, acting due to chemical or thermal effects, is usually weak or completely absent. The main source of magnetic fields in space conditions is the movement of the conducting medium itself.

Cosmic magnetic fields are not constant. For example, the large-scale field of the Sun almost periodically changes its polarity every 11 years. Associated with these changes is the cyclicity of solar activity, which manifests itself in the form of dark spots, the solar corona, flares and other phenomena. In recent years, cyclicity has been discovered in many stars. The Earth's magnetic field close to us also turns out to be unstable. After large intervals of time - approximately in the course of years - it changes its direction by 180°: the north magnetic pole becomes south and vice versa.

Why does the magnetic field change in a conducting medium?

The electric field created by a charge disappears if this charge is neutralized by a charge of the opposite sign. In a plasma consisting of an equal number of moving positive and negative charges, any excess charge of the same sign that accidentally appears in any area is quickly neutralized. It is clear that the higher the electrical conductivity of the plasma, the shorter the electric field neutralization time. The magnetic field in plasma is created by moving charges, that is, electric currents. Obviously, as the current changes, the field will change.

Let us first discuss how currents change not in plasma, but in ordinary metal wires that are found in every room.

Consider a turn of radius $r$ of wire with cross section $\pi l^2$ with resistivity $Q$ (Ohm m). Let the coil first be connected to a battery and a current $I$ (A) flow through it, which creates a magnetic field $B$ (T) in the space around the coil. The maximum field is achieved at the center of the coil. It is perpendicular to its plane and equal in magnitude to $B_{\max} = I/2r$. The magnetic energy of this system can be estimated as

$$W \approx \mu_0 \frac{B^2_{\max}}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \quad (\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \Gamma_{\mathrm{H}} \cdot \mathrm{m}).$$
