Such turbulence is called spiral. Despite its chaos, there is on average some additional twist. Helicity is that organized property that is distinguished by turbulence in rotating cosmic bodies of low density (in the cores of planets, in stars and galaxies).

Obviously spiral turbulence in cosmic bodies acts in conjunction with negative rotation. As we saw (figure 4), under-rotation turns a field directed along the diameter into a field directed almost along the azimuth, or more precisely, along a spiral. Spiral turbulence raises many loops near the azimuthal field and turns them in different directions (figure 7). But since the screws of the same sign predominate, then after adding the loops there remains a non-common radial field of the determined direction. If the new radial one then coincides in direction with the original one, then the field is strengthened. When the direction of the resulting field is opposite to the original one, then a “reversal” of the field is possible. This makes it possible to understand the phenomenon of repeated reversals of field direction that are observed in the Sun and other stars. Note that in the case of the figure eight, we received a dynamo with a continuous increase in the magnetic field without reversals.

It should not be overlooked that the hydromagnetic dynamo operates under conditions of strong turbulent entanglement and destruction of magnetic lines. Thus, the growth or maintenance of the field occurs against the background of the continuous birth of new and dying of old magnetic fields.

Hydromagnetic dynamo is widely used to describe the magnetic fields of stars, planets and galaxies. In part, the hydromagnetic dynamo appears to be the main “engine” of repeating solar activity.
