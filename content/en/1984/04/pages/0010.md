Non-uniform rotation

The simplest movement, characteristic of all planets, stars and galaxies, is rotation. However, if all parts of the body rotate with the same angular velocity, that is, the rotation is uniform, then the magnetic lines will rotate with the body, remaining practically unchanged. For bending, stretching and revolutions of the linear relations of the motion of neighboring parts of the plasma, that is, the rotation must be non-uniform. It is clear that solid bodies rotate uniformly, otherwise they collapse. A liquid or gaseous body is capable of performing non-uniform rotation. Observations of the Sun have shown that the equatorial parts of our star complete a revolution several days faster than the circumpolar regions. Indirect evidence indicates that the inner, not directly observable, regions of the Sun rotate even faster. Inhomogeneous rotation of the gaseous disk of the Galaxy: at the same time, its internal parts make more revolutions than the external ones.

To visualize non-uniform rotation, imagine that in front of you are several concentric circles of carcels (figure 4, a). Those that are close to the center circle, say, fast carcels located further from the center.

Let's place several people on the carselia, so that they are all located along the same diameter. Let's give them a rubber rope attached to the ends of a diameter that imitates a magnetic line, and turn on the rotation. It is easy to verify that in a few turns the rubber rope (magnetic line) will twist into a spiral (figure 4, b). Initially, the line was located along the diameter, but now it has become close to the circle. Thus, the inhomogeneous rotation turned the radial magnetic field into a field directed almost along the azimuth.
