Heat capacity of an ideal gas

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. M. KOZEL.
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. SHERONOV

Determination of heat capacity in connection with the first law of thermodynamics - the law of conservation and transformation of energy in thermal processes. According to this law, the amount of heat $Q$ transferred to a body (or system of bodies) goes to change $\Delta U$ its internal energy and to perform work $A'$ on external bodies:

$$Q = \Delta U + A'.$$

For a homogeneous body, it is convenient to talk about the heat capacity of one mole of a substance. Molar specific heat $C$ is the amount of heat received by one mole of a substance when it is heated by one degree. If during the heating process an amount of substance $v = m/M$ (here $m$ is the mass, $M$ is the molar mass of the substance) an amount of heat $Q$ was transferred, and its temperature changed from $T_1$ to $T_2$, then

$$C = \frac{Q}{v(T_2-T_1)} = \frac{Q}{(m/M)(T_2-T_1)}$$

In the International System of Units (SI), the molar specific heat is expressed in J/(K mol).

Along with the molar heat capacity $C$, it is possible to determine the specific heat $c$, that is, the heat per unit mass of a substance. Obviously $c = C/M$.

Knowing the heat of a substance in a given thermodynamic process, it is easy to find the amount of heat received by the body:

$$Q = mc(T_2-T_1) = \frac{m}{M} C(T_2-T_1) = vC(T_2-T_1).$$

It is very important to understand that the work $A'$ performed on external bodies depends on the conditions under which the heat transfer process occurs. Therefore, heat is determined not only by the property of the substance, but also by the type of thermodynamic process.

For example, during isothermal expansion of a gas ($T = \text{const}$), a body is supplied to it, but its temperature remains unchanged. From the definition it follows that the heat of gas in an isothermal process is infinitely large. In this case, all the quantitative heat transferred to the system is used to perform work: $Q = A'$. If the same gas participates in a diabatic process, then its temperature changes, and the amount of heat received by the gas is zero. Consequently, in diabatic processes the heat specificity is zero, and the work $A'$ of the gas on external bodies is performed due to the internal energy: $A' = -\Delta U$.

So, the heat of a gas depends on the thermodynamic process. Processes in which the heat temperature remains unchanged are usually called polytropic. Examples of polytropic processes, for example with isothermal and diabatic, are also isochoric ($V = \text{const}$) and isobaric ($p = \text{const}$) processes.
