⟦folio 51⟧

Matchstick and extreme
R. S. DANELIA

This problem came to my mind when I was looking at my friend’s collection of match boxes. He collects not only labels, as many people do, but entire casings. You can see crusts of a wide variety of shapes and sizes. While going through the exhibits of the cork, I thought: what size should the most “profitable” cork be? After all, this is a purely mathematical question; it can be given the following strict formulation:

The average of all matched shells of a given volume V of declared design (that is, consisting of a retractable shell open at the top and an outer part open on the sides; see Fig. 1) find the shape (that is, the ratio of the ribs x:y:z) that ensures the lowest cost of materials during manufacturing (the thickness of the materials is not taken into account).

To solve this problem we need the following Lemma. If the product of three positive variables a, b, c is constructed, then their sum takes on the smallest value when a = b = c.

Proof. Due to the inequality between the arithmetic mean and the geometric mean

$$\frac{a+b+c}{3} \geq \sqrt[3]{abc},$$

(1)

the correspondence abc = k = const gives a+b+c ≥ 3√k, and equality is achieved only when a = b = c.

Although proofs of inequality (1) are given in many textbooks, it would not be amiss to introduce the reader to another proof of it - different from the traditional ones.

If we introduce the notation

$$x_1 = \frac{a}{\sqrt[3]{abc}}, \quad x_2 = \frac{b}{\sqrt[3]{abc}},$$

$$x_3 = \frac{c}{\sqrt[3]{abc}},$$

then we require the inequality to be written as follows:

$$x_1 + x_2 + x_3 \geq 3,$$

(2)

Moreover, equality is achieved only when x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = 1. So, if the product x_1x_2x_3 is equal to 1, then the sum x_1 + x_2 + x_3 is not less than 3 and is equal to 3 only if x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = 1.

In fact, consider two possible cases:

1. All three x are equal to each other, that is, x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = 1. It is clear that in this case x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 3.

2. Not all x are equal. Since their product is equal to one, if one is greater than 1, then the other is less than 1. For definition, let’s put: x_1 > 1, x_2 < 1. We have:

$$(x_1 - 1)(x_2 - 1) < 0 \iff x_1x_2 + 1 < x_1 + x_2 \iff x_1 + x_2 + x_3 > x_1x_2 + x_3 + 1.$$

(3)

But x_1x_2x_3 = 1, and therefore

$$x_1x_2 + x_3 + 1 = \frac{1}{x_3} + x_3 + 1 = \frac{1}{x_3} + x_3 - 2 + 3 = \frac{(x_3 - 1)^2}{x_3} + 3 \geq 3.$$

This, according to (3), gives x_1 + x_2 + x_3 > 3, which is what needed to be proven.

Now we can move on to solving our problem.

Let the dimensions of the shell be x, y, z (Fig. 1). The box consists of two parts, with the surface area of ​​the first part S_1 = xy + 2xz + 2yz, and the second part S_2 = 2xy + 2xz. The total area - their sum - will be S = 2yz + 3xy + 4xz.

It is clear that the material required to make the shell is proportional to S, that is, we must select x, y, z so that S is the smallest. To do this, let us represent S in the following form:

$$S = xyz \left( \frac{2}{x} + \frac{3}{z} + \frac{4}{y} \right)$$

or, given the equality xyz = V,

$$S = V \left( \frac{2}{x} + \frac{3}{z} + \frac{4}{y} \right).$$

The product of terms inside the bracket is constant (it is equal to 24/V), therefore, by virtue of the lemma, the smallest value is achieved when the terms are equal, whence x:y:z = 2:3:4.

Thus, the consumption of materials will be the least when the length, width and height of the shell are in the ratio 4:2:3. Note that more often than not, factories do not comply with this ratio (Fig. 2), but there are also shells prepared specifically taking into account this condition (Fig. 3).
