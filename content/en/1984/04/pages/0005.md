Both stations have side-looking radar installations, which make it possible to study the topography of the planet’s surface in previously inaccessible polar regions. Thus, in the near future, our science will have a detailed map of the surface of Venus in all its regions. From what we already know about this planet, it has massive mountain ranges, and the height of the access mountains is sometimes 13 kilometers, which is much higher than Everest.

The stations “Venera-15” and “Venera-16” are also equipped with equipment that allows recording the thermal radiation of various layers of the Venusian atmosphere located above the cloud layer, at altitudes from 65 to 95 kilometers above the surface of the planet. It is at these altitudes that the so-called “greenhouse effect” occurs - the absorption by the atmosphere of a significant part of the heat leaving the surface of Venus into outer space. This is what leads to additional heating of the planet. There is an assumption that a similar effect may occur in the Earth’s atmosphere under the influence of fuel combustion products. This could lead to undesirable changes in our climate. The study of this phenomenon is of great interest to meteorologists and climatologists.

An experiment to study the temperature of different layers of the atmosphere of Venus was prepared by Soviet scientists together with scientists from the GDR.

In 1983, the Soviet space observatories Astron and Prognoz-9 were launched, intended to explore the distant depths of the Universe. Astron was founded by the largest optical telescope located outside the earth's atmosphere. The telescope explores in detail stars of various types, as well as distant galaxies and quasars. Stars have been discovered in whose atmosphere there is hundreds of times more uranium, lead and tungsten than in the atmosphere of the Sun. Several galaxies with unknown high levels of ultraviolet radiation have been recorded.

The X-ray telescope installed on Astron also operates successfully. It uses supernovae to study supernovae, as well as flaring X-ray stars - bursters*. This telescope allows you to register the effect of “turning on” the source of X-ray rays in the constellation Hercules.
