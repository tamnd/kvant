ka, keep the goal closed. At first glance, the current will immediately stop due to the resistance of the conductor $R$. However, in reality the current will not disappear instantly. The rate of its decay determines the rapid Joule loss $N = I^2 R$ (J/s). The current is supported by the magnetic energy stored in the coil. (Note that magnetic energy is much greater than the kinetic energy of current carriers - electrons.) This energy disappears over time

$$\tau \approx \frac{W(\Delta x)}{N(\Delta x/c)} \sim 10^{-6} \sigma l^2(c),$$

where $\sigma = 1/\varrho$ is the conductivity of the coil material.

Let us estimate the field decay time $t$ for a copper wire with a transverse section radius $l = 1$ cm. The conductivity of copper is about $6 \cdot 10^7 (\Omega \cdot \Omega)^{-1}$. We get that it's less than a hundredth of a second.

Let us now turn to cosmic plasma. Its electrical conductivity is usually the same as that of bad metals, but the size of the area occupied by currents is gigantic. In this case, the characteristic time of field change becomes very large. For example, in the upper shell of the Sun the electrical conductivity of the plasma is about $10^6 (\Omega \cdot \Omega)^{-1}$ (at room temperature, mercury has approximately the same $\sigma$, lower and higher). The radius of the Sun is ~700 thousand km, that is, $7 \cdot 10^8$ m. Consequently, the time of disappearance of the field created by currents flowing in a shell the size of 1/10 of the radius is equal in order of magnitude to $10^{15}$ s, or about 100 million years! Of course, the estimate is very approximate. But compare with fractions of a second for a copper wire. It turns out that the habitual electrical resistance practically does not change the once created large-scale magnetic fields. How then can we explain that, say, the solar magnetic field changes over a shorter period of time - 11 years?

The reason is that the environment is moving. The movement of a medium in which there are free positive and negative charges is equivalent to the movement of a conductor. The textbook “Physics 9” shows that the movement of a conductor in a magnetic field is accompanied by the appearance of electric movements of force, and if the ends of such a conductor are closed, a current will arise in the circuit. This secondary current itself creates a magnetic field, which, when added to the original field, changes it. It is remarkable that as a result of such a change in a perfectly conducting plasma, the resistance of which is zero, the total magnetic flux $\Phi = BS$ (S is the area of ​​the circuit) should remain unchanged. This means that the magnetic field created by a moving conductor seems to move with it. Such a field is usually called a magnetic field frozen into the plasma. One can clearly imagine a magnetic field in a moving conducting medium, where on the surface of a light thread thrown into a flow: the thread is not only transferred, but also stretches if the particles moving in the flow move away from each other.

When frozen, the movement of the plasma as a whole carries magnetic lines. The movement of some parts of the plasma relative to other deformations of the magnetic lines: they stretch and bend. The magnetic line, which initially passes through two close parts of the plasma, will continue to pass through these same particles, that is, follow their movements (figure 2). The number of magnetic lines that form any area, covering a closed loop of moving liquid particles, remains unchanged (at $\varrho = 0$). Magnetic induction $B$ of the field can occur during deformations, when areas with the same field direction come together, and weakens when areas with the opposite direction of the field come closer.
