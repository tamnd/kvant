Heat capacity of an ideal gas

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. M. KOZEL.
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. SHERONOV

Determination of heat capacity in connection with the first law of thermodynamics - the law of conservation and transformation of energy in thermal processes. According to this law, the amount of heat $Q$ transferred to a body (or system of bodies) goes to change $\Delta U$ its internal energy and to perform work $A'$ on external bodies:

$$Q = \Delta U + A'.$$

For a homogeneous body, it is convenient to talk about the heat capacity of one mole of a substance. Molar specific heat $C$ is the amount of heat received by one mole of a substance when it is heated by one degree. If during the heating process an amount of substance $v = m/M$ (here $m$ is the mass, $M$ is the molar mass of the substance) an amount of heat $Q$ was transferred, and its temperature changed from $T_1$ to $T_2$, then

$$C = \frac{Q}{v(T_2-T_1)} = \frac{Q}{(m/M)(T_2-T_1)}$$

In the International System of Units (SI), the molar specific heat is expressed in J/(K mol).

Along with the molar heat capacity $C$, it is possible to determine the specific heat $c$, that is, the heat per unit mass of a substance. Obviously $c = C/M$.

Knowing the heat of a substance in a given thermodynamic process, it is easy to find the amount of heat received by the body:

$$Q = mc(T_2-T_1) = \frac{m}{M} C(T_2-T_1) = vC(T_2-T_1).$$

It is very important to understand that the work $A'$ performed on external bodies depends on the conditions under which the heat transfer process occurs. Therefore, heat is determined not only by the property of the substance, but also by the type of thermodynamic process.

For example, during isothermal expansion of a gas ($T = \text{const}$), a body is supplied to it, but its temperature remains unchanged. From the definition it follows that the heat of gas in an isothermal process is infinitely large. In this case, all the quantitative heat transferred to the system is used to perform work: $Q = A'$. If the same gas participates in a diabatic process, then its temperature changes, and the amount of heat received by the gas is zero. Consequently, in diabatic processes the heat specificity is zero, and the work $A'$ of the gas on external bodies is performed due to the internal energy: $A' = -\Delta U$.

So, the heat of a gas depends on the thermodynamic process. Processes in which the heat temperature remains unchanged are usually called polytropic. Examples of polytropic processes, for example with isothermal and diabatic, are also isochoric ($V = \text{const}$) and isobaric ($p = \text{const}$) processes. The corresponding molar heat capacities are denoted by $C_V$ and $C_p$. Distance these processes in more detail (Fig. 1).

In the first case ($V = \text{const}$), the piston is fixed (see Fig. 1, a), and the gas does not do any work when heated. All the body received by the gas goes to increase its internal energy. In the second case ($p = \text{const}$), the piston is free (see Fig. 1, b), the gas expands when heated and does work. The body produced by the gas is only partially used to increase the internal energy; the remaining parts are spent on

completion of distribution work. It is clear that to increase the gas temperature by one degree in the second service, a large amount of heat is transferred to the gas, therefore, $C_p > C_V$.

Let us now find the number of connections between $C_p$ and $C_V$. From molecular-kinetic theory it is known that the internal energy of an ideal gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature. The Physics 9 textbook shows that for a monatomic gas $U = 3/2 RvT$ (where $R$ is the universal gas constant). For an isochoric process $A' = 0$, $Q = \Delta U = C_V v (T_2 - T_1)$. It follows that for a monatomic gas the molar specific heat at constant volume is $C_V = 3/2 R$.

The work done by a gas under an isobaric distribution is calculated by the formula
$$A' = p (V_2 - V_1) = p \Delta V.$$
If in the thermodynamic process under consideration the pressure changes, this formula can also be applied, but only for very small sections of the process, in which the change in volume $\Delta V$ is small and therefore the pressure $p$ can be approximately considered constant. We can write the warm law of thermodynamics as
$$Q = C_V v (T_2 - T_1) + p (V_2 - V_1) = C_V v \Delta T + p \Delta V.$$
From here, using the definition of molar heat capacity, we obtain the formula for the heat capacity of an ideal gas in an arbitrary process:
$$C = \frac{Q}{v (T_2 - T_1)} = C_V + \frac{p}{v} \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T}.$$
Changes in volume $\Delta V$ and temperature $\Delta T$ in this formula are related by the equation of state of an ideal gas $pV = vRT$ and the equation of the thermal process in which the gas participates. In particular, for the isobaric process
$$p (V_2 - V_1) = vR (T_2 - T_1),$$ or $\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T} = \frac{vR}{p}$.
Consequently, the heat $C_p$ of one mole of an ideal gas in the process at $p = const$ is
$$C_p = C_V + R.$$
For a monatomic gas $C_p = 5/2 R$.

Note that solid and liquid heat capacities $C_p$ and $C_V$ differ little from each other. This is due to the fact that when solid or liquid bodies are heated, their volume changes very little, so that under normal conditions (that is, at atmospheric pressure or close to it), the practical work done during distribution is zero. In this case, the specific heat is equal to the change in the internal energy of the body when heated by one degree.

Problem 1. Some quantities of an ideal gas are heated at a constant pressure $p = 1$ atm from a temperature $T_1 = 300$ K to a temperature $T_2 = 400$ K. What work was done by the gas in this process if its initial volume was $V_1 = 3$ l?

Let $v$ be a given amount of substance, $C_V$ and $C_p$ the molar heat capacities of the gas at constant volume and pressure, respectively.

The amount of heat supplied to the gas
$$Q = v C_p (T_2 - T_1),$$
and the increase in internal energy of the gas
$$\Delta U = v C_V (T_2 - T_1).$$
The work done by the gas $A'$, according to the first law of thermodynamics, is
$$A' = Q - \Delta U = v (C_p - C_V) (T_2 - T_1) = v R (T_2 - T_1).$$
From the equation of state of an ideal gas we find
$$vR = pV_1/T_1,$$
then finally for the gas work we get
$$A' = pV_1 (T_2/T_1 - 1) = 100 \text{ Дж.}$$

Of course, the given solution is not the only possible one. In this problem, you can bypass the concept of heat and directly use the formula for calculating the work of a gas during an isobaric process.

Problem 2. A mole of an ideal gas from a state with a temperature $T_1 = 100$ K is distributed isobarically, and then goes isochorically to a state with an initial temperature (Fig. 2). How many times does the volume of the gas change if, in order to transfer the gas from the initial state to the final state, an amount of heat $Q = 831 \text{ Дж?}$ had to be supplied to it?

Heat is supplied to the gas in the isobaric section and removed in the isochoric sections:
$$Q = v C_p (T_2 - T_1) + v C_V (T_1 - T_2) = v R (T_2 - T_1).$$

For the isobaric section from the equation of state, $T_2 - T_1 = T_1(T_2/T_1 - 1) = T_1(V_2/V_1 - 1)$ is found.
From these relations we obtain
$$\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 1 + \frac{Q}{vRT_1} = 2.$$
Problem 3. From the initial state with a known temperature $T_1$ the milk is ideally baked into a state with the same temperature in two ways (Fig. 3). In one of them, the zaza is first heated isobarically to a temperature of $T_2 = 2T_1$, and then isobarically cooled. In the other, the zaza is first isobarically cooled and then isobarically heated. Find the difference in the amount of heat supplied to the spacer in both cases.

The solution to this problem is similar to the previous one:
$$Q_1 = vC_p(T_2 - T_1) + vC_v(T_1 - T_2),$$
$$Q_2 = vC_v(T_3 - T_1) + vC_p(T_1 - T_3),$$
And
$$Q_1 - Q_2 = vR(T_2 + T_3 - 2T_1).$$
Taking the equation of state $pV = vRT$ for the isobaric sections of processes 1 and 2, we find the relationship between the temperatures $T_1$, $T_2$ and $T_3$:
$$p_1 = \frac{vRT_1}{V_1} = \frac{vRT_2}{V_2}, \quad p_2 = \frac{vRT_3}{V_1} = \frac{vRT_1}{V_2},$$
where
$$T_1^2 = T_2T_3.$$
By condition $T_2 = 2T_1$, therefore $T_3 = T_1/2$. Finally we get
$$Q_1 - Q_2 = vRT_3 = vRT_1/2.$$
Problem 4. The mole is ideally distributed according to the law $pV^2 = const$. Determine the molar temperature of the zaz in this process.
As mentioned earlier, the molar temperature
$$C = C_V + \frac{p}{v} \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T}.$$

From the state equation $pV = vRT$ and the process equation $pV^2 = const$ we obtain
$$VT = const.$$
This equality is valid for any $V$ and $T$, therefore
$$(V + \Delta V)(T + \Delta T) = VT.$$
Opening the brackets and neglecting the small term $\Delta V\Delta T$, we find the ratio of the change in volume $\Delta V$ to the change in temperature $\Delta T$:
$$V\Delta T + T\Delta V = 0, \text{ или } \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T} = -\frac{V}{T}.$$
Then the final molar heat capacity turns out to be
$$C = C_V - \frac{pV}{vT} = C_V - R.$$
It is easy to verify that during expansion according to the law $pV^2 = const$ cools (this directly follows from condition $VT = const$). Due to its internal energy, work is done on expansion ($A' > 0$) and a certain amount of heat is given to the surrounding bodies ($Q < 0$).
Problem 5. A mole is ideally placed in a cylinder with a movable non-isomal piston, fastened to the bottom with a spring, the tension of which rises to Hooke’s law (Fig. 4). Determine the heat capacity of the zaz, considering the external pressure equal to zero and neglecting the pressure pressure.
Let $p$ be the gas pressure, $V = Sx$ the volume occupied by the gas, where $S$ is the cross-section of the cylinder, $x$ is the length of the stretched spring. Let us denote the spring stiffness by $k$ and write down the piston condition:
$$pS = kx, \text{ или } pS^2 = kV.$$
From the last formula it follows that changes in pressure $\Delta p$ and volume $\Delta V$ are related to the relations
$$\Delta p = \frac{k}{S^2}\Delta V = \frac{p}{V}\Delta V.$$
From the equation of state $pV = vRT$, valid for any $p, V, T$, we find
$$(p + \Delta p)(V + \Delta V) = vR(T + \Delta T).$$
Opening the brackets in the last equality and neglecting the small term of the form $\Delta p\Delta V$, we obtain
$$p\Delta V + V\Delta p = vR\Delta T,$$
or, given the connections $\Delta p$ and $\Delta V,$
$$\frac{p}{v} \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T} = \frac{R}{2}.$$
