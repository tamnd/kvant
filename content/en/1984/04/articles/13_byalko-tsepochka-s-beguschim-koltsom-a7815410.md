Chain with running ring

Candidate
physical and mathematical sciences
A. V. BYALKO

Your friend's birthday is coming soon. It's not easy to come up with an original and inexpensive gift. This is how, by spending only fifty kopecks and half an hour of time, you can make something that will undoubtedly bring pleasure to an inquisitive person and will, in our opinion, be a wonderful gift.

In the photo on the left is a chain. The double interweaving of shiny rings creates a red spiral. But this is not a purely decorative toy, this chain has its own secret, its own special property. Attention, focus!

You hold the chain by the top ring, grab the adjacent one with your other hand, and let go of the top ring, as if you were even throwing it down. And suddenly the ring... begins to run down. Slipping along other rings, it weaves along the entire chain and, having reached the bottom position, stops. And in your hands you have exactly the same chain as it was before.

Your friend, becoming interested, takes the chain, and after several unsuccessful attempts - not each of the upper rings has the ability to slip down - and he succeeds in the same trick: seconds - and the ring, having sparkled, turns out to be stuck up. However, how this happened remains a mystery - the eye does not have time to follow the movement of the ring.

After a fair amount of fiddling with the chain, your friend agrees that the ring running down is an illusion. All rings are sold into each other, and therefore each cannot move relative to its neighbors by more than a diameter. Each run of the ring along the chain is actually such (by diameter) successive movements of individual rings. Above the ring that is currently in motion, and below it, the chain is motionless - so it seems that a separate ring is moving along the chain, as if a distant wave is moving. How does this happen?

Let's consider the external connection of rings. It turns out that our chain is two common identical chains intertwined sideways - photo 1. We will call these two vertical sequences of rings branches. The arrangement of the rings in photo 1 is unstable - that is why the chains have to be fixed both from above and from below. It becomes stable when one of the branches is on one ring higher than the other.

Obviously, the chain has two such stable positions: the top can be either one or the other branch of the chain. Each ring run occurs when you move the chains from one position to another. In this case, single branches must “swap places” - the upper branch becomes and vice versa. This, however, does not occur simultaneously along the entire chain. If you grab the top ring of the branch that shows in the lower position, and let go of the ring of the other branch, then the lower part of the chain still “does not know” that a “palace coup” has occurred above. After all, the position of each ring in the chain is determined by its neighbors, and a ring can take the correct position only after all the rings located above it are already in a new state.

Look at the right chain on the splash screen. It records the moment when the correct position is occupied by the ring located in the middle of the chain (the arrow points to it).
