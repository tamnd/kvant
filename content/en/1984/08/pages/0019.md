⟦folio 17⟧

Let's find $P(30:0)$ — the probability of the author winning the first two goals in a row. Obviously
$$P(30:0) = P(15:0) \cdot p = p^2 = 0,36.$$
But is this really so? Why did we overdo the probabilities? In fact, here we are using the “theorem for multiplying the probabilities of successive independent events” (you can read about it in the book by A. N. Kolmogorov and others, quoted above). Let's try to sacrifice this calculation in the language of the middle part of the winnings. If the author consistently wins the reader an average of 60 balls out of every 100 balls (that is, an average of 3/5 balls), then out of 60 balls (played immediately after winning the first ball), the author will also win on average 3/5, that is, $60 \times (3/5) = 36$ times out of 100 (on average), the author will win the first two balls in a row.

Likewise,
$$P(0:30) = P(0:15) \cdot q = q^2 = 0,16$$
— the reader has little chance of winning more than two goals in a row!

Now let's count $P(15:15)$. We have (think why)
$$P(15:15) = P(15:0) \cdot q + P(0:15) \cdot p = 2pq = 0,48$$
- the score “fifteen” after the first two goals will occur almost as often as the other two scores (30:0 and 0:30) combined.
