People have been inventing and using algorithms for thousands of years, but it was only in the early thirties that the precise mathematical concepts of an algorithm emerged. However, with the first steps, the new theory of algorithms brought mathematics a series of major discoveries that largely determine its modern appearance.

Already the first definition of an algorithm, known as *primitive recursive functions*, allowed the Austrian mathematician K. Gödel to prove his significant theory of true but unprovable statements in 1931. This is a very deep result, which, on the one hand, established the limiting formal theories, and on the other hand, substantiated the limitlessness of the process of human cognition. Another definition of an algorithm, reminiscent of the well-known rule of substitution and replacement of variables when calculating using formulas, allowed the American mathematician A. Church in 1936 to discover the existence of algorithmic unsolvable problems, that is, a series of problems that can be formulated precisely mathematically, for which there cannot be a single solution algorithm. An example of an intractable problem is this: find a general way for any algorithm to detect whether it gives a result for any of its arguments or whether there is such data for which it will work indefinitely.

Naturally, as soon as such restrictive results appeared, they began to think that perhaps these restrictions might not be so restrictive if they were somehow visible or strengthened by the definition of the algorithm. And here mathematicians encountered another amazing phenomenon: the most different definitions of an algorithm, invented from completely dissimilar premises, turned out to be completely equivalent to each other.

According to the general opinion of the participants, the Urgen Symposum became a unique meeting. According to one of its organizers, American computer scientist D. Knuth, “...it was an unforgettable experience. Instead of a “general” conference, where participants read prepared reports in advance, we held mutual discussions on fundamental problems of computer science. The desert landscape surrounding the Khorezm oasis and the breath of history distracted us from the everyday missions that beset us at home and helped us focus on the most profound and long-term philosophical reflections.”

On the evening of September 22, 1979, the symposum concluded its work. The participants left. The square was empty, the cotton workers also went home. Near the hotel, the full moon illuminated the stone placed by the participants of the symposum at the base of the monument to Muhammad al-Khorezmi, a native of Khorezm, who left the man with the Indian positional system of numbering, arithmetic, algebra and his good name.
