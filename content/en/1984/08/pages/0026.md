⟦folio 24⟧

The channel through which light propagates almost without loss is called a waveguide. Once inside such a waveguide as if in a trap, light, sound or radio waves can no longer escape from it until they reach its end. This is useful for transmitting various signals from one device to another. Methods for manufacturing waveguides vary depending on the purpose of their application. For microwaves, for example, on which locators operate, metal pipes serve as waveguides. For light, waveguides are made in the form of thin flexible glass fibers.

Relatively recently, it was discovered that there are natural sound waveguides in the ocean. They form alternating salt waters of different temperatures or salinities. The faint sound of a small bell ringing off the coast of America crosses the Atlantic unhindered along such a waveguide and, under favorable conditions, can be heard off the coast of France.

For radio waves, the waveguide is the entire space between the earth's surface and the ionosphere. The assumption indicator for radio waves kills in the atmosphere with altitude. Radio rays, moving away from the Earth, experience refraction, like light rays: they become more and more bent until they are “reflected” from the ionosphere and returned to Earth. What our receivers “catch” is the radio image of the transmitting station.
