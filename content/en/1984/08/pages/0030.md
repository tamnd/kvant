The word “al-mukabala” means the subsequent operation of bringing similar terms, so that in the end in the equation for each degree there remains only one positive term, located on one side or another. So, for example, thanks to the first operation of the equation, $3x^2 - 5x + 6 = x^2 + 7x + 2$ turns into $3x^2 + 6 = x^2 + 12x + 2$, and thanks to the second, into $2x^2 + 4 = 12x$. Thus, the entire science of equations (“algebra”), and then the use of the set of symbols required for it, and, finally, the theory of abstract operations received a name that at first referred to only one, now out of use, operation “al-jabr”; at present we - unlike the Greeks, the Arabs and their non-presidential successors in Europe - do not demand that there be only positive terms on both sides of the equation.

In his “algebra” al-Khorezmi considers linear and quadratic equations, but without any algebraic formalism. Everything here is expressed using words; Thus, a variable in an equation is called a root, and its square is simply a square. Six types of equation are considered:

"squares equal roots"
"squares equal numbers"
"the roots are equal to the number"
"squares and roots are equal to numbers"
"squares and numbers are equal to roots"
“roots and numbers are equal to squares”*).

The first three types are broken down into the following example equations:

$x^2 + 10x = 39; \ x^2 + 21 = 10x; \ 3x + 4 = x^2$ (these equations then appear frequently in subsequent algebraic texts). The equation $x^2 + 10x = 39$ is described as follows: “a square and its ten roots are thirty-nine dirichems**), ... this means that if you add to a certain square what is equal to ten roots, you get thirty-nine.
