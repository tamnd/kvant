It follows that the greater the refractive index of the medium, the smaller the angle of incidence of the beam on the interface, that is, the greater the angle that the beam makes with the interface itself (angle $\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta_i$). As the refractive index increases, the beam deviates more and more from the interface (in our example, from the horizontal) and is more and more “pressed” toward the normal to the interface (in our example, to the vertical).

We considered the case when along the path of propagation of a light beam the refractive index increased, that is, the beam passed from an optically less dense medium to an optically more dense one. What will happen during the “reverse” path of the beam - when moving from an optically denser medium to a less dense one? In this case (figure 1.6) the beam will deviate more and more from the normal to the interface. At some of the boundaries, the angle of incidence may be such that the corresponding angle of refraction should be equal to $\frac{\pi}{2}$. As can be seen from the match $n_i \sin \theta_i = n_k \sin \theta_k$, this will happen "when $\sin \theta_i = \frac{n_k}{n_i}$". In this case, the refracted ray appears at the angle of the medium when $\sin \theta_i > \frac{n_k}{n_i}$, then the ray will be reflected from the boundaries, return to the medium with an index of $n_i$ and will subsequently propagate in the “reverse” direction - move from an optically less dense medium to a more dense one.

If there is no sharp interface between the media, and the properties of the medium (its refractive index) change smoothly in any direction, then the light beam propagating in such a medium will not be a broken line, but a smooth curve.
