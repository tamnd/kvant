From old experiences

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. VARLAMOV

Under this title, we publish articles about the sunset and at the same time significant reverent experiments in physics, which were carried out by curious winterists in the last century.*)

Today we offer our readers a story about experiments demonstrating the mutual influence of water scab and... sound.

How many of you are not familiar with the microphone? You see it on television, in theater and cinema, use it while recording on tape, its structure is even described in a school physics textbook. But they probably know little about how to make a water microphone. Yes, yes, don't be surprised. It turns out that using water scab can successfully amplify various sounds. This non-teaching device was made by the American Alexander Graham Bell, who is known to the world as one of the inventors of the telephone. But before we start making a water microphone, let’s introduce the user to some of the properties of its “amplifier” - the water scab.

If you drill a small round hole in the bottom of a vessel with water, you will notice that the scab of water flowing through it consists of two parts, different in their properties. The upper part of the scab is mottled and so motionless that it appears glassy. As it moves away from the source, it becomes thinner, and at the most contracted point the lower part begins, variable in shape and opaque. At first glance, this part of the scab, like the upper one, seems continuous, monolithic. However, sometimes you can quickly run it through your finger without getting it wet.

French physicist Felix Savard conducted a similar study of the properties

The English physicist John Tyndall, known to you from previous publications, repeated Savart’s experiments in his laboratory. He managed to create a stream (a vein, in Tindal's words), the protruding part of which reached a length of about 90 feet (≈27.4 m). When exposed to a sound of organiza- tions corresponding to rasstia and moderate strength, this pronounced vein immediately turned cloudy, disintegrating into a huge number of water drops.

In one of the articles, Tyndall describes his observation of a water jet falling into a pool of water: “When the outgoing jet crosses the liquid surface above the break point (the point of transition from the normal part of the jet to the muddy part - A.V.), and the pressure is not too strong, it enters the liquid silently; but when this surface crosses the stream below the break point, a murmur is immediately heard and many bubbles appear. In the first case, not only is there no strong splashing of the liquid, but around the base of the vein it collects in a heap, in a protrusion, where the movement is opposite to the stream.” Try to repeat these simple experiments with a stream from a water tap and, say, a basin of water. Pay attention to the pressure under which the stream flows out of the tap.

The described properties of the water jet are now used to create a water microphone. Prepare a copper string with a diameter of 2 cm and a length of 20 cm, a piece of rubber from the bursting of silicate and several glass strings. On the side surface of the copper string, stepping back 3 cm from the upper end, continue the answer and solder into it a pipe slightly smaller in diameter and about 3 cm long. Put a funnel (horn) glued together from cardboard onto the pipe.
