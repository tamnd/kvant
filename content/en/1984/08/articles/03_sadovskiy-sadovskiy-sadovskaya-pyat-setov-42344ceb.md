Rules of the game

The place for playing tennis is a level area (court) of defined dimensions (Fig. 1) with lines marked on it and divided in half by a net.*)

The ball is put into play by one of the two **) keepers - this blow is called *serve*. Serve diagonally: the flock in the first service position goes to the opponent’s first service box, the flock in the second – in the second. Subchacha is performed alternately from each position. A ball put into play by a racket hit must travel over the net and hit the court within the opponent's respective service court (see Fig. 1). If the first serve to a given field was incorrect, the player must repeat to the same field. After the second incorrect serve, the point is considered negative for the server.

The served ball must be returned by the receiver's racquet after the first but before the second landing of the ball. After receiving the serve (during a point), the ball is allowed to be returned not only between the first and second landings, but also “volleyed” (before landing). The point is played by each opponent taking turns deflecting the ball aimed at him and not allowing him to land on his side more than once. The ball is in the player; either opponent will be unable to hit the ball before the second touchdown or will send it off the net or out of bounds; he then countered this ball.

A tennis *meeting* is divided into sets (games), sets into games (games), games are formed by playing individual balls (points). Within one game, the game is played until one of the sides wins at least four goals (points), provided that this side has gained at least two goals.

Scoring in a game has a feature that has been preserved from the times when the game was developed for “interest”. In France, the price of the game is a coin of 60 sous: it was placed in four of 15 sous. These latter, apparently, amounted to the price of four blows: 15, 30, 45, 60. True, in the 20th century, judges became more laconic, shouting “forty” together with “forty-five.”

So, when either side wins the first point in the game, it often gets 15:0, when the same side wins the second point, another 15 is added and the score becomes 30:0 on its run. If the third point is scored, the score becomes 40:0, and the fourth point (the “game”, as the referees say) brings the end of the game to that side.

If one of the sides, after scoring the first point, scores a second point for the opponent (the score is set to 15:15), etc. Therefore, the score (and it is always kept, starts with the server’s points) in the game can only be as follows: 15:0, 30:0, 40:0, 0:15, 0:30, 0:40, 15:15, 30:15, 40:15, 15:30, 30:30, 40:30, 30:40, “exactly”, “bigger”, “smaller”; Having lost immediately after the score is 40:0, 40:15, 40:30, the server wins the game, and the opponent wins after the score is 0:40, 15:40, 30:40.

Let's try to build a mathematical model of the tennis game and, with its help, understand the question posed.

Main proposals of the model

There is undoubtedly an element of randomness in Tennis, and our model must take this into account. The winning outcome of the ball cannot be predicted unambiguously in advance, but it is possible to objectively assess the “chances of winning” of each opponent. This estimate of the odds—the number—is called probability, and that's why models like our mathematics are called probability (or statistical) models.

But don't worry, reader! The concept of our simple model does not require deep knowledge of probability theory. The small amount of understanding from this science that will be needed, we will understand as we go along. And the reader who wants to delve deeply into this theory, we recommend turning to issue 23 of the library «Kvant» (A. N. Kolmogorov, I. G. Zhurbenko, A. V. Prosorov. Introduction to probability theory, M.: Nauka, 1982).

So, in our model, each individual ball draw is a random trial with two possible outcomes—the author wins (A) and the reader wins (R). Each outcome is assigned its probability—the numbers P(A) and P(H) also, which
$$0 < P(A) < 1, \quad 0 < P(Ч) < 1,$$
$$P(A) + P(Ч) = 1.$$

What does, say, the number P(A) mean? It expresses the percentage of balls (per unit) that the author wins on average from the reader. If the author wins on average 60 balls out of 100 from the reader, then the probability of his winning, P(A), is equal to 0.6. (We believe that the author, an experienced first-rate reader, plays a slightly better reader. In our case, of course, P(H) = 0.4 and the inequalities and equality written above are satisfied.)

We will further propose that the probability P(A) = 0.6 (and therefore P(H) = 0.4) is the same for all plays of the ball throughout the match. Based on this preposition, we want to find out what the chances (probability) of the author winning first the game, then the set and finally the whole match.

Discussion of the academic validity of the model

Of course, any mathematical model is only a diagram that idealizes a real process or phenomena. By simplifying reality, we ignore certain aspects of it. Thus, considering the probability P(A) unchanged during the match, we ignore, in part, the physical preparation of the opponents (at the end of the match, the author will probably have established more than his younger opponent and will make more mistakes) and psychological factors (having lost a window, especially two, the author usually plays attention and evil - his chances of winning the next window increase). Our model also has a more serious drawback, which any experienced tennis player will replace - it does not take into account the advantage of the server, which is especially erroneous on a fast court (grass, wood or plastic).

In fact, all the names of the factors (and some others) can be included in the probability of the tennis game model. We do not do this here - this would significantly complicate the calculation scheme and technique, while our simplified model already allows us to draw interesting conclusions, sometimes unexpected, but confirmed by practice.
