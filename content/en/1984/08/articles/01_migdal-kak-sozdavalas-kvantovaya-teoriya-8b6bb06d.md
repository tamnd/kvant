How quantum theory was created

Academician A. B. MIGDAL

How does a theory arise? First, as a result of the analysis of accumulated experimental and theoretical knowledge, a deep and clear physical idea appears, say, “the movement of celestial bodies and the fall of a stone on Earth are caused by the same cause,” or “time flows differently in a stationary reporting system and in a uniformly moving one.” From the first yen Newothen deduced his theory of gravitation, from the second - Einstein - the theory of relative gravity. With the most important physical theory of our time - quantum theory - everything was completely the opposite: its main results arose before their meaning became clear!

Using the example of the creation of quantum theory, one can see in action the multinatural theoretical features of physics. It was a movement in the dark, at random, through vague guesses, which were often not confirmed and led astray, but this luck was unveiled; The scientists turned out to be visionaries - their assertions, built on shaky foundations, were most precisely confirmed later.

Let's try to follow and feel this complex and unusual move indeed.

It all started with a disaster

The paradox under the gloomy name “Rayleigh-Jeans catastrophe” reached physicists as a legacy from the last century. It arose when the laws of statistical physics were applied to equilibrium body radiation, which is installed inside a box with heated walls. This radiation is standing electromagnetic waves.

Statistical physics has established a wonderful law of “equidistribution of energy”: in warm equilibrium for each “degree of freedom”*)

have the same energy. Thus, for each possible oscillation there is an energy equal to $kT$ (k is Boltzmann’s constant, $T$ is the absolute temperature). If there are many possible oscillations in a system, then such a system will absorb a lot of energy. The total energy of the oscillatory system is equal to $NkT$, where $N$ is the number of possible oscillations.

Let's see how many standing electromagnetic waves can be formed in the box. In order for a standing wave to arise, an integer number of semi-waves (figure 1) must fit from wall to wall. The shorter the wavelength, the more opportunities to fulfill this condition. Therefore, the number of possible standing waves increases with decreasing wavelength - and, therefore, with increasing frequency. If each oscillation accounts for the same energy, and the number of oscillations increases with increasing frequency, then the intensity of the wavelength changes - and, therefore, with increasing frequency. Meanwhile, precise experiments have established that the intensity of the wavelength changes - and, therefore, with an increase in frequency. But the main thing is that the total number of possible standing waves in the box turns out to be infinite, and they must absorb all the energy of the walls, no matter how much heat we supply to them. This would truly be a disaster - all objects around would have to cool, their heat would be transferred into a “bottomless barrel of radiation.”

In 1900, Max Planck found the only way to explain the described paradox. He suggested that particles emitting waves with a frequency $v$ can change their energy not in a discontinuous manner, but only in jumps - in discrete portions $hv$. The proportionality coefficient $h$ entered into science under the name “Planck’s constant”.

The heated walls of the box can be imagined as a set of particles that emit electromagnetic waves of various frequencies. According to Planck's hypothesis, these emitters cannot emit electromagnetic waves with energies then greater than the average thermal energy $kT$ arriving per particle. High-frequency emitters are, as it were, “frozen” in a state with minimal energy. For the emergence of such emitters, they must transfer energy $hv$, much greater than thermal energy. But according to the laws of statistical physics, this event is unlikely, so only a small fraction of such emitters are generated, and the intensity of the light emitted by them is small. This is how Wien's empirical law found its explanation.

Planck obtained a formula that described the results of experiments on the distribution of radiation intensity of all frequencies at different wall temperatures; you just had to choose the right CONSTANT $h$. The numerical value of this quantity turned out to be equal to $h=6,62 \times 10^{-34}$ J s. It is explained why the abruptness in the change in the energy of the emitters was not replaced in increased life - the portions of the energy of the present are small, that its change seems to be continuous.

What is light?

Well-known experiments on interference and diffraction prove that light is a wave. Newton, explaining the finite speed of propagation of light, suggested that a luminous body emits particles - corpuscles - that transmit light. But he was unable to explain the phenomena of interference and diffraction in this way, and the corpuscular theory of light was killed for a long time.

In 1905, Albert Einstein, explaining the phenomenon of the photoelectric effect - the expression of electrons from gas molecules when irradiated with ultraviolet light -

The theory of the photoelectric effect partially confirmed Newton's corpuscular theory. So what is light - a particle or a wave? No one could explain this then, and the contradiction remained something of a thorn.

Quantization rules

In 1913, Niels Bohr established rules so mysterious even to their creation that he called them “postulates”—unprovable assumptions.

Bohr took another step along the unknown road that Planck and Einstein walked - he extended discreteness to the atom, arguing that not all electron orbits are permissible, but only some. According to the planetary model of the atom that existed in classical physics, an electron, revolving around the nucleus, moves with centrifugal acceleration and, therefore, should emit electromagnetic waves - after all, according to the law of classical electrodynamics, only a charge moving in a straight line at a constant speed does not emit. The emitting electron, continuously losing energy, would fall onto the nucleus.

According to the rule introduced by Bohr, an electron can emit quanta

Bohr expressed the frequencies of light emitted by an atom in terms of the charge of the nucleus, the charge and mass of the electron, and Planck's constant.

One more road

The event that was destined to explain the meaning of Bohr's actions occurred in 1923. But at first it only feels that it has touched a larger place - the problem of waves-particles.

The French physicist Louis de Broglie proposed that the electron is not actually a particle, but a wave, or rather, both a particle and a wave... Particles must describe a wave process characterized by a wavelength $\lambda$ related to the momentum of the particle $p$ in the same way that the wavelength of a photon is related to its momentum.

Four years later, the American physicists Davisson and Germer and, independently of them, the English Thomson*) discovered the diffraction of electrons on crystals (see figure 3). So the electron is actually a wave!

This confirmed not only de Broglie’s amazing proposal, but also his exact formula for the electron wavelength. History repeated itself in reverse order: light was first studied as a wave, then as a set of particles, and the electron - vice versa.

Simple example

Let us assume that the particle moves between impenetrable walls located at a distance $l$ from each other. At each of the walls, the wave function must vanish, that is, it turns into a wave function on the outside, which is equal to zero, since the particle does not go beyond the walls. Therefore, in order to obtain a stationary state with number $n$, an integer number of half-waves must fit within the length $l$: $2l/\lambda=n$. So, in the first state, when a wavelength shower is placed between the walls, the wave function is equal to zero on the walls and has a maximum in the middle. A standing wave can be represented as two waves running towards each other. Therefore, the momentum of a standing wave is zero. The absolute value of the impulse is the same as that of the corresponding traveling wave, and is related to its length by the de Broglie correspondence: $p=h/\lambda=nh/2l$. Let's find the energy $E=p^2/2m$.*)

From here it is easy to obtain the formula $E=\pi^2\hbar^2n^2/2ml^2$. So in this simple case we found the energies.

So, at the first stage of the development of quantum mechanics, the discreteness of the emerging values ​​of a number of physical quantities was established; one of the main scales of nature has been found - Planck’s constant, which distinguishes the region of the phenomenon that describes classical physical phenomena and phenomena of quantum nature**); it is clear that particles act as carriers of both wave and corpuscular properties; an equation was found for the wave function describing the state of the quantum system.

But the main question remained unresolved: what is the wave function, the main tool of the theory?

In 1905, Einstein overturned the traditional ideas about space and time, after which the formulas obtained earlier by Lorentz spontaneously arose and were understood. An equally important step had to be taken in quantum mechanics. Physics is not just formulas, but a deep understanding of physical phenomena, enriched with mathematical language.

Coordinate or speed?

In 1927, one of the creators of quantum mechanics, Werner Heisenberg, reflected on the concepts of position and velocity of a particle. He did thought experiments and realized that it was impossible to precisely change the coordinate and speed at the same time - the more accurately you change the coordinate of an electron, the less certain its speed becomes...

To change the coordinate, you need to look through the microscope at an electron illuminated by short-wave light. In this case, the electron coordinate will be changed with an uncertainty of the order of the wavelength of the light used*). But, interacting with the wave, the electron will receive a recoil. Its momentum changes by values ​​on the order of the momentum of one photon. And this impulse, as we already know, is equal to $h/\lambda$. The smaller $\lambda$, the better the coordinate will be changed, but the more uncertain the implus will become.

So the position uncertainty is $\Delta q \sim \lambda$ and the momentum uncertainty is $\Delta p > h/\lambda$. Multiplying the expression for $\Delta p$ by $\Delta q$, we get

$$\Delta q \cdot \Delta p > h.$$

This is the Heisenberg uncertainty correspondence.

After doing many similar thought experiments, Heisenberg came to the conclusion that for microelectrics there is a fundamental limitation that nature imposes on the concept of coordinates and momentum. Classical physics did not know this limitation; it does not introduce changes into the description of large bodies due to the smallness of $h$.

Especially quantum mechanics is that the properties of microsoptic objects cannot be studied, distracting from the method of application. Depending on it, the electron manifests itself either as a wave or as a particle. Of course, there are properties that do not depend on the method of application: mass, charge, particle spin**)...

The fundamental uncertainty of some quantities is a consequence of the application of classical concepts to the description of uncertain objects. But this is inevitable - after all, changing instruments must give certain readings, and in this sense they are necessarily classical. Doesn't this mean that our description of the objective properties of micro-objects is vague and ambiguous? No, the language of classical physics, which is spoken by the means of communication, in which we form thoughts, allows us to fully explore the quantum properties of micro-objects.

Quantum mechanics finally helped to pull out the splinter and apply the idea of ​​the electron as a particle and a wave. Fairy-tale mythical creatures - centaurs, horse-people - in one situation they question human wisdom and benevolence, in another they show violent animal rights and intemperance; one of them raises heroes, the others harm them. The particle-wave electron is either a person, or a horse, or a horse, depending on the situation, on the questions asked by the experiment.

An electron hitting it will “illuminate” the emulsion grain, and its coordinate is determined accurate to the grain size. As a result of diffraction, a beam of electrons will illuminate a circle with a radius $R$, significantly larger than the response radius. If you reduce the intensity of the electron beam so that, say, one electron per minute hits the screen, the diffraction pattern will not change, you just need to wait longer for it to appear. This means that one electron must be assigned the probability of getting into one or another place in the photoplastic...

Analysis of such experiments allows Max Born in 1926 to assume that the square of the wave function determines the probability of a particular value of the coordinate or momentum of the electron, depending on the type of experiment performed.

What helped you come to these conclusions? Let us remember that the theory of wave phenomena of light - interference and diffraction - was developed long before Maxwell's equation*), before the electromagnetic nature of light was understood. It was only assumed that the light source emits waves of an unknown nature, and the intensity of the light is proportional to the square of the value that fluctuates. In the modern concept, electric and magnetic fields vibrate in a light wave in time and space, and the light intensity is proportional to their square. But almost all wave manifestations of light are bound regardless of its nature. It was also natural for waves associated with microparticles to assume that there is a certain wave process, and the intensity - in our case, the probability - is proportional to the square of the wave function.

At first it was assumed that the wave property of the particle corresponds to a real physical field, similar to the electromagnetic field in a light wave. But then one electron would give a diffraction pattern in one act, but it darkens only one grain... This means that the wave function of a particle is not a physical field and not a physical wave. This is a “wave of information”. It is a record of the potential outcomes of a particular follow-up.

The wave function is the maximum complete admissible description of the state of a particle. It replaces the classical state, which is specified by coordinates and velocities.

The amazing properties of the wave function seemed physically unacceptable to Einstein. Einstein's dispute with Bohr continued for many years and contributed to the clarification of quantum mechanics. If we accept that the wave function is a wave of information, and not a physical field, everything falls into place. After all, if the wave function is a description of probabilities, it means that an experiment done in one place can abruptly change the results of measurements in another place. This abrupt change in confidence is caused by clarification, new information. We ask the question: “What is the probability of getting a bad mark in physics?” Adding to this a clarification determined by conditions, for example: “without looking at the textbook at home,” we get an abrupt change in belief, it will jump to one. The opposite conditions: “having fully understood and felt the lesson,” faith again jumps to zero.

Let's imagine that two particles with a total momentum of zero have separated, and we need to find the momentum of one of them in Moscow, the other at the North Pole. The probability will change abruptly if we change the momentum of the Moscow particle and look for another momentum, provided that Moscow is known to us. The probability depends on what question we pose, whether we select any outcomes of the northern experiments or only those in which the Moscow impulse has a given value. There is nothing strange or unusual about this.

Quantum mechanics does not give an unambiguous answer to some questions, but only the certainty of one or another result.

Is causality violated?

Amazing conditions of celestial mechanics in the 17th-18th centuries; internal deep faith in the uniqueness of prediction. Proud of the ability to build the properties of science, the French astronomer, mathematician, physicist Pierre Laplace said: “Give me the coordinates and velocities of all particles, and I will predict the future of the Universe!”

But we learned that it is impossible to accurately specify the coordinates and velocities of particles at the same time! According to quantum mechanics, one can only indicate the probability of one or another value of coordinates and velocities.

Science has already dealt with the accuracy of predictions; classical statistical physics can answer the question: what is the probability of finding a particle of heated gas with such and such energy? But this confidence is born of the complexity of the system, the imprecision of the definition of the initial state, and behind it lies the uniqueness of the mechanical laws.

Application of quantum mechanics to the electromagnetic field and other physical fields

The application of quantum mechanics to a system oscillating around an equilibrium position—oscillary—had surprising consequences. If a classical oscillator - a pendulum or a weight on the outside - is at rest in an equilibrium position, a quantum one - makes “zero” oscillations. The kinetic and potential energy of these oscillations is of the order of $h\nu$. The average value of the oscillator coordinate is zero, but the average value of the squared coordinate is nonzero*)! The energy of the quantum oscillator changes in portions $h\nu$ and for the state with number $n$ is equal to $E_n = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)h\nu$.

If we consider the sound oscillations of a solid body as a set of quantum oscillations, it turns out that at zero temperatures the atoms of the solid body are not motionless - they perform zero oscillations. This is exactly confirmed by experiments on the scattering of light by solids. But electromagnetic waves in emptiness can also be considered a set of oscillators, and, therefore, in a vacuum, where there are no particles or quanta, zero oscillations of the electromagnetic field should be found.

Let's try to figure out what a quantum of light is - a photon.

To do this, let's do an experiment. Let's imagine that between parallel metal screens a standing electromagnetic wave has formed perpendicular to them. If the distance between the screens is $l$, a standing wave with a maximum length $\lambda$ such that $l = \lambda/2$ can decrease between them. The frequency of the wave $v = c/\lambda$ is related to the possible values ​​of the wave energy by the correspondence $E_n = (n+1/2)h\nu$. In the most $n=0$ state there are no quanta between the screens. In the state with $n=1$, one quantum with wavelength $\lambda = 2l$ appears. At $n=2, 3, \dots$ there are two, three, and so on quanta with this wavelength.

For the next lines of waves (overtones) with lengths $\lambda=l, l/2, \dots$ you can repeat the same thing. For the $m$th wave $\lambda_m = 2l/m$. In this state there can also be how many corners of quanta, they are determined by the degree of excitation of a given oscillator, and the $m$ icon determines the wavelength, that is, the type of oscillator. The number $n_m$—the excitation number of the oscillator—shows the number of quanta of this type.

Quanta are usually characterized not by wavelength, but by a quantity called the “wave vector.” The magnitude of the wave vector $k$ is simply related to the wavelength: $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Each $k$ has its own oscillator and its own quanta, the number of which is equal to the excitation number of the oscillator.

And periodic oscillations will occur in the traveling wave. The wave energy for each wave vector can also be written as the energy of an oscillator that changes the orders of $h\nu$. But a traveling wave has momentum, which means the quantum will have momentum. A particle with zero mass has momentum equal to its energy divided by the speed of light: $p = h\nu/c$.
