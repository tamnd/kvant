Now you, like a good obedient schoolboy, draw all the forces (Fig. 1,a), write the equations: “sum of forces horizontally”, “sum of forces vertically” and “sum of moments about the center of gravity” are equal to zero. And - horror of horrors - you have three equations and four unknowns. Problem writers have a way to deal with this situation - to “strangle” one unknown, in this case to declare that there is no friction at the upper point of contact: friction coefficient P 0 (Fig. 1, b). Naturally, this turns off one of the forces, but where have you seen a wall without friction? By the way, it is obvious that if you declare P 0 at the lowest point, it can be life-threatening. This problem is one of many “statically indeterminate problems.” This is the name for statics problems in which the number of unknowns is greater than the number of equations. At school, they usually consider flat statics problems, simply because pictures for them are easy to draw on a flat surface: on paper, a board or a screen. In such problems there are three equations - two about the sum of forces along the coordinate axes and one about the sum of moments. At your leisure, think about how many equations there will be for a three-dimensional statics problem. It is clear that in real life, no matter what is done with bodies, no matter how they are positioned, all forces eventually acquire some meaning. The “unsolvability” of these problems arises because we have neglected something, and to solve it we need to take this “something” into account. But keep in mind that if we take this “something” into account and the problem receives its solution, this will mean that we have solved this problem only in this approximation. In order to improve the accuracy of the solution, you may need to take something else into account. Let's look at a simple example, and then return to our staircase. Let the beam hang on two cables and you need to find their tension (Fig. 2). The problem is trivial: there are no horizontal forces, so there are two equations and two unknowns. We attach the third cable (Fig. 3) and... the guard comes. Let's try to take into account the deformations - but whose? The deformation of the cables in the school obeys Hooke's law, but the deformation of the beam in the school cannot be calculated. Therefore, we will limit ourselves to cables; their deformation is shown in figure 4 conditionally in the form of springs. We will add six known quantities: initial lengths and coefficients in Hooke’s law, three unknowns: final lengths and three equations: the dependence of rope lengths on tensions, i.e. equations of Hooke's law, but not in the form F kx, but in the form $L L_{0} N k$, where $L_{0}$ is the initial length, N is the tension force, k is the stiffness coefficient. However, just as we lacked one equation, we still lack it now. Do you see another equation at figure? Equations of this type are called deformation compatibility equations. Indeed, the deformations of structural elements are somehow connected; the dimensions of structural elements cannot be arbitrary. For example, if the points of attachment of the cables on the beam lay on the same straight line, then they will lie on the same straight line, and if the deformations are small, then the cables are parallel and the final lengths are related by a trivial equation. This will be the seventh equation that we were missing. The problem can be solved even with large deformations, the solution will just be longer. We can also consider a wide range of initial lengths and stiffness coefficients, and in this case we can write a program that will suspend the beam on cables directly on the screen and show us the behavior when varying the initial values.
