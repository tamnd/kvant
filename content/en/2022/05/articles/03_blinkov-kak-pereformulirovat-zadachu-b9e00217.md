THE ARTICLE WILL CONSIDER the construction associated with the orthocenter of the triangle. Using classical facts, you can notice the equivalence of two seemingly initially dissimilar problems, reformulate an already known Olympiad problem, or try to come up with your own. This is what we will talk about. So, let's remember the following fact. Fact 1. The altitudes of a triangle are the bisectors of its orthotriangle. Recall that an orthotriangle is a triangle with vertices at the bases of the altitudes of the given triangle. The orthocenter of an acute triangle is the center of the incircle of the orthotriangle, and the vertices of the triangle are the centers of its excircles.

Exercise 1. Prove fact 1. Direction (Fig. 1). You can use, for example, the fact that the points $A,^{A} 1, B и^{B} 1$ (and similar quadruples of points) lie on the same circle. It follows from this that angle B 1 A 1 C is equal to angle BAC (and similar angle equality).

How can you use these facts? Suppose that in some problem we are talking about the heights of a triangle. Then the problem can be reformulated by replacing the triangle with an orthotriangle, and the heights with a bisector.

And vice versa, if the problem considers, for example, the bisectors of internal or external angles and their intersection points (centers of inscribed or excircles), then you can replace, for example, the centers of excircles with the vertices of the triangle, its bisectors with the altitudes of the resulting triangle and, accordingly, the incenter of the original one with the orthocenter of the resulting one. What might be attractive about such a reformulation? First, it leads to other ways of proving known statements. Let's look at classic examples. Example 1. Lines containing the altitudes of a triangle intersect at one point. Proof (see Fig. 1). For any triangle, consider the triangle formed by the bases of its altitudes (orthotriangle). Then the heights will be the bisectors of the orthotriangle, which, as is known, intersect at one point. The question may arise: what about an obtuse triangle? In fact, there are no problems: lines containing two heights will become bisectors of the external angles of the orthotriangle, and the intersection will be the center of the excircle of the orthotriangle (the vertex of the original triangle).
