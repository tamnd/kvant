The first conference, organized by Gamow and Tuve in Washington, took place in 1935. Before the outbreak of World War II, five conferences were held, attended by Bohr, Fermi, Bethe (atomic physics), Chandrasekhar (star physics), Delbrück; European physicists became acquainted with American science and established contacts with American colleagues. This turned out to be important both for them and for US science, which has been leading since then. Gamow, Teller and Thewe invited physicists and astronomers to a conference in 1938, the topic was the physics of stars. Back in 1926, Arthur Eddington (astrophysics) suggested that the source of energy for stars is a nuclear reaction, the synthesis of helium from hydrogen. What is important for him is the tunneling of particles through a potential barrier, which also occurs during alpha decay. Having learned about this effect from Gamow in 1928, Houtermans and Atkinson began calculating nuclear transformations in stars, 10 years later Gamow and Teller continued them and created the theory of “red giants” (like Betelgeuse), and Hans Bethe “finished off” the problem after a conference and conversations with Gamow.

These transformations are now called the carbon-nitrogen cycle or Bethe cycle. As for smaller stars, the size of the Sun or even less massive, lithium and beryllium participate in the reactions, this is the proton-proton cycle. Gamow said that he could have come up with this cycle himself, “if he were smarter.” In fact, he began the corresponding calculations together with his student Charles Critchfield (atomic physics) earlier than Bethe, but he turned out to be more stubborn and methodical. Back in 1911, Einar Hertzsprung (astronomy) and Henry Russell (astrophysics) noticed that there was a relationship between the visible color of stars and the energy they emitted. The arrangement of all stars on a graph with color-luminosity axes (both depending on the temperature of the star) has since been called the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram. Stars, during their evolution, move from the lower right corner of the diagram to the upper left, changing both luminosity and color as they develop. Such stars are said to be on the main sequence. There are also additional branches, and, according to the memoirs of the astronomer and astrophysicist Walter Baade, the relationship of one of the branches with the main sequence was solved by Gamow, who reported this in a letter and did not publish it.
