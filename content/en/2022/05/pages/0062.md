2. 769999. First solution. The sum of the last five digits of a number is divisible by 6 (the quotient is equal to the first digit). The sum of the last four digits of the number is also divided by 6 (the quotient is equal to the second digit). Therefore, the second digit, as the difference of two numbers divisible by 6, is also divisible by 6. This means that the second digit is 0 or 6. If it is equal to 0, then the sum of the last four digits is also equal to 0, which means the first digit is also equal to 0, but a six-digit number cannot start from zero. So this option fails and the second digit is 6. Then the sum of the last four digits is $равняется^{6} ^{6} 36$, which means they are all 9. Finally, the sum of the last five digits is 42, and therefore the first digit is $равняется^{42} :^{6} 7$. Thus, the required number is 769999. Second solution. The sum of the last 4 digits is 6 times greater than the second digit, which means the sum of the last 5 digits is equal to the 7 second digits and equal to the 6 first digits. Therefore, 7 second digits are equal to 6 first ones, which means that the first digit is 7 and the second is 6; then the sum of the last four is $равна^{36,}$ and this is achievable only if all the last 4 digits are nines. Comment. You can solve the problem by going through all possible options (from 0 to 9) for the value of the second digit of the number. For example, if this digit is 9, then the sum of the digits to the right of it is 6 times greater, $т.е.^{9} ^{6} 54$, but then the sum of all digits to the right of the first is $равна^{9} 54^{63}$. The plot of the problem refers to a well-known thought experiment, most vividly described by the German mathematician and philosopher of the turn of the 17th–18th centuries, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz: “A hungry donkey, finding itself at the same distance from two completely identical armfuls of hay, will die of hunger, never having chosen which one to eat.” This experiment was not invented by Leibniz; it is also found among other philosophers who discussed freedom of choice - the 17th-century Dutch philosopher Benedict Spinoza and even the ancient Greek scientist Aristotle in his treatise “On Heaven,” where he is ironically cited in a dispute with the sophists as an example of an absurd conclusion. And the donkey itself is usually called Buridan's in honor of the 14th century French philosopher Jean Buridan. The expression “Buridan's donkey” entered the language as a designation for an indecisive, doubtful, hesitant person.
