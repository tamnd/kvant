The method of recording genetic information using the four-letter alphabet of nucleotides is universal, the same for all life on Earth - animals, plants, bacteria and viruses. Every word in the genetic text is the name of an amino acid, every sentence defines a protein. But how are letters made into words? Gamow asked himself this question in 1954. Obviously, the number of words must be at least 20. If we assume that each word consists of two letters, then there will be $4^{2} 16$ such distinct pairs. This is not enough. Gamow made the assumption that every word should have three letters. There would be $4^{3} 64$ such three-letter words in a four-letter alphabet. This is no less, but, on the contrary, more than the number of amino acids. Perhaps we should consider that words do not necessarily consist of each of three letters? Or perhaps there are synonyms among the 64 three-letter words? Gamow settled on the second possibility as simpler: let there be 64 words, but several of them mean the same amino acid. An experiment must find out the correspondence between 64 words of the language of life and 20 amino acids. New research by Crick, the work of biochemists Marshall Nirenberg, Severo Ochoa, Har Koran and others soon showed that Gamow's idea of ​​​​a universal code with three-letter words was correct. It was a triumph of genetics and Gamow's personal success. He celebrated the victory, and he knew how to rejoice at luck, as they say, very well. In a 1968 interview, when asked about the work that gave him the most pleasure, Gamow replied: “Perhaps biology; it was something completely new, and it was so fun to unravel the codes.” Gamow continues to work cheerfully and with pleasure. Everyone who writes about Gamow writes about his jokes. Odessa residents, with their characteristic unbending humor, believe that the reason for Gamow’s jokes and pranks was his Odessa origin. Rarely is a publication about Gamow complete without mentioning two of his jokes. In one of his scientific articles, he referred to Landau’s publication in the non-existent journal “Chervony Gudok”, which some meticulous readers began to look for. Another joke, DEJ, is described above. There are rumors that he tried to persuade another of his employees to change his last name to make it “alpha-betagamma-delta,” but he turned out to be not Odessa enough. The third joke is that while studying the role of neutrinos in the cooling process of stars, Gamow called this phenomenon the URCA process. The results were obtained jointly with Mario Schoenberg (star physics), whom Gamow met at the Casino de Urca in Rio de Janeiro. In case the “Physical Review” became interested in the strange name, Gamow prepared an explanation - this is an abbreviation for unrecordable cooling agent, an undetectable cooling agent. Western physicists believed that this was a hint of pumping money out of the pockets of gambling house visitors - similar to pumping energy out of a star with a stream of neutrinos.
