GAMOW:

Georgy Gamov

GEORGE

ANTONOVICH,

Biographies should begin with a story about their ancestors. On his father's side, Gamow came from an ancient (there are documents from the 17th century) noble family; on his mother's side, his ancestors belonged to the southern Russian clergy. One grandfather was a colonel, commander of the Chisinau garrison, the other was an archpriest of the Odessa Cathedral. It is significant that in both lines there were mathematicians among his ancestors, and it is certainly important that his father was a teacher of Russian language and literature, and his mother was a teacher of history and geography. Knowledge and its transmission were valued in the family - the mother taught the child French, the teacher taught German, and there was a large library in the house. In later questionnaires, Gamow writes: “I read and translate with a dictionary - ancient Greek, I read and can explain myself - French, I speak fluently - German, English, Danish.” By the way, about ancient Greek - this is probably one of his jokes. But he writes seriously: “At the age of seven, I read Jules Verne and dreamed of traveling to the moon.” A large library, of course, contributes to success in science, but, as life shows, it does not in itself determine it.

But here is a direct and not accidental push: in 1910 (George is six years old) he receives a telescope through which he observes Halley’s Comet (he did not live to see its next appearance in 1986, but said that he wanted to). At the age of 13, he is given a microscope, and he analyzes the so-called “body of Christ”, received in church and not eaten on the spot, but brought home. He described the experiment as follows: “For comparison, I prepared in advance a small bread crumb soaked in red wine. Looking through the microscope, I could not see the difference between the two samples. The microstructure of the two pieces of bread was exactly the same and did not at all resemble the microstructure of thin pieces of my skin, which I had previously cut off from my finger with a sharp knife. The color of the sample was still reddish, but my microscope was not strong enough to see individual red blood cells. So it was only semi-proof, but I think it was the experiment that made me a scientist." At school, Gamow studies well, especially in mathematics, physics, languages, and in the last grades he is interested (is this a coincidence?) in the theory of relativity. Next - the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Odessa University, studies mathematics and works part-time as a computer operator at the observatory. But he studied at the university for only a year. Having heard about the rapid development of physics in Petrograd, he plans to go there, and to make this possible, his father sells the remains of the family silver. This is the most important moment, this is a fork in the road. In Petrograd, Gamow studied at Petrograd University and graduated in December 1924, passing almost all exams with the highest grade. Enters graduate school, studies the anomalous dispersion of light in potassium vapor, comes up with a method for quality control of optical glass; but theoretical physics attracts him more.
