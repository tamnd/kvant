There he wrote eight articles and his first scientific monograph, “The Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Radioactivity,” which was published in English and German. Note that simultaneously with his first original publications, Gamow began publishing review articles on physics, which, generally speaking, is not typical for young scientists. In the spring of 1931, Gamow returned to Russia. In 1931, Gamow - he was 28 years old - was elected a corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences, 42 out of 43 votes were cast for him, and he is one of the youngest corresponding members in history. Physics is developing rapidly. However, the proposal of the young people to create a central academic Institute of Theoretical Physics in Leningrad, headed by Gamow and Landau, was not accepted - the older generation was not going to give up the commanding heights. It was probably offensive to them. Gamov works at the Radium Institute, the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology, and the Leningrad University. In 1932, he and Lev Mysovsky (nuclear physics) developed a project for the first cyclotron in Europe, and in 1937 the machine started working. While still abroad, Gamow received an invitation from Guglielmo Marconi (one of the creators of radio) to take part in the first International Congress on the Atomic Nucleus (Rome, 1931) and present an article on the structure of the nucleus. However, he was not allowed to travel; the report was read by Max Delbrück (biophysics). Congress delegates sent Gamow a card regretting his absence, signed by, among others, Marie Curie (radioactivity), Wolfgang Pauli (particle physics), Enrico Fermi (theoretical and experimental physics), Lise Meitner (nuclear physics) and other celebrities. summer school in Michigan. Gamow receives another invitation from abroad, this time to the International Solvay Congress on Nuclear Physics, which was to be held in Brussels in October 1933. And he goes to the congress, and together with his wife, a graduate of the physics department of Moscow State University Lyubov Vokhmintseva. This was a fork in the road: if Gamow had not been allowed to take his wife with him, he would not have gone. What to do next - return to the USSR or not? Paul Langevin (theoretical physicist) vouched for him; Gamow cannot let him down, but Marie Curie learns about the problem: she talks to Langevin, and he agrees not to consider Gamow bound by his petition. This is a pattern, and this is a fork in the road. After the Solvay Congress, the scientist lectured at the Radium Institute in Paris, at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge under Rutherford, and in Copenhagen under Niels Bohr. In 1934, Gamow sailed to the USA, where he spent his life over the next thirty-odd years. In a letter to Peter Kapitsa (results in various fields of physics, organizer of science), Gamow wrote: “Now I want to follow in your footsteps and, if possible, move into the so-called “Kapitza-Zustand” [“Kapitza state”], i.e. live abroad with a Soviet passport. I wrote to Moscow, asking in firm expressions [strong expressions] to extend the business trip for a year." Gamow’s goal was the opportunity, like Kapitsa, to work abroad, visit scientific centers and events, and at the same time come to the USSR at any time. However, this desire did not find understanding among the authorities; in October 1934 he was dismissed from the Radium Institute and the Institute of Physics and Mathematics, and in 1938 he was expelled from the Academy. So, 1934, Gamow and his wife are in the USA. Two years later, Gamow received American citizenship. Vokhmintseva worked sporadically during her trips abroad, mainly as a laboratory assistant, and also in the editorial office of a magazine - she wrote abstracts of Soviet articles on optics and spectroscopy.
