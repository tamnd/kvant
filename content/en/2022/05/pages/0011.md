Europe has exceptional news: Otto Hahn (nuclear physics) and Fritz Strassmann (nuclear physics) have discovered a new type of nuclear reactions. By irradiating uranium nuclei with neutrons, they discovered that when a neutron is captured, the nucleus does not transform into a heavier isotope of the same element, but splits into two large fragments. The key word “fission” was contained in an article by Otto Frisch (nuclear physics) and Lise Meitner, who built the first theory of the phenomenon. A new chapter of science and a new chapter of history had begun. American physicists showed some hesitation at first; they were unfamiliar with the use of science for military purposes, but the Europeans better understood what Nazism was and became more active. Leo Szilard (nuclear physics), Eugene Wigner (particles, nucleus), Fermi and Teller made the first attempts to obtain government funding for research into the military use of nuclear physics and proposed to limit open publication on this topic in the United States. Why did Gamow stay away from the atomic problem? It seems that both Gamow’s friends and his superiors considered him not serious enough, too frivolous for such matters. He had a reputation as a cheerful, talkative, sociable person, and a lover of friendly feasts. Albert Einstein (theoretical physicist), John von Neumann (mathematician) and George Gamow were recruited into defense research by the US Navy at the outbreak of the war. The topics of their work were related to the physics and technology of explosives - conventional, not nuclear. Einstein could not regularly come to Washington on these matters, and therefore his superiors decided that someone should go to Princeton for consultations with him. Gamow was appointed to this role, so he traveled to Princeton once every two weeks. In 1948, Gamow became involved in work on the hydrogen bomb project. When he appeared at Los Alamos, his longtime protégé Teller was one of the main characters in the project, and from 1949–1952

Gamow joked that his main contribution to the American hydrogen bomb was that he brought Teller to America. But the organization of Washington conferences should be considered no less important. It’s not just Teller alone; such a big task is always the work of many people. Gamow never forgot cosmology, the science of his youth. He took it seriously in 1946 and devoted more than ten years to it. He wanted to “cross cosmological science with nuclear physics.” Once he succeeded in something similar - he crossed nuclear physics and astronomy, and advanced the question of nuclear energy sources for stars. Now Gamow suggested that the primary substance of the world was not only very dense, but also very hot. For some reason, he himself believed that the idea of ​​the hot beginning of the world belonged not to him, but to Friedman, but in the cosmological works of the latter there is not a word about the temperature of the early Universe.
