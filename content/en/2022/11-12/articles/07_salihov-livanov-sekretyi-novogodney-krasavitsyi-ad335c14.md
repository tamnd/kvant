LET'S CONDUCT A LITTLE EXPERIMENT. Take a glass of boiling water and start pouring sugar into it, stirring with a spoon. What will we see? We won’t see sugar for sure; it has dissolved in water. But if you taste the water, it will be sweet. So sugar is still present in water - in the form of tiny particles, molecules. If you continue to add sugar, which those with a sweet tooth love to do, then at some point it will stop dissolving. No matter how much we stir, we will not be able to dissolve the sugar. And one more important observation - in cold water the amount of dissolved sugar will be less. In one liter of water at room temperature (20 degrees Celsius) you can dissolve two kilograms of sugar, and in hot water (80 degrees) - already three and a half. Why is this happening? The higher the temperature, the greater the volume of liquid, i.e. the average distance between water molecules is greater. This means that more sugar molecules fit between them. It is interesting that the solution can be encountered... in the cloud. After all, solutions can be liquid or gaseous (in this case they are called mixtures), and even solid. The following happens to the cloud. When water evaporates under the influence of sunlight, water vapor molecules become airborne. The higher the air temperature, the more water vapor the air can contain. As with sugar in tea, different amounts of water vapor can also be dissolved in the air at different temperatures. A sugar crystal under a glass microscope can reveal some sugar. Where did he come from? It turns out that during cooling, the “excess” sugar fell out of solution, and we succeeded in doing what academic institutes are diligently doing - growing crystals. True, these crystals do not look very impressive. But if you try, you can still achieve some success in growing crystals. The same thing happens with water in the air. Cooled air cannot hold as much water vapor as hot air. And the water molecules in such inhospitable air have no choice but to unite into small droplets. This happens both when the cloud cools, and when hot air with steam leaves the spout of the kettle, and early in the morning when dew falls. Drops of dew settling on the cold grass or on the railing of the stairs are nothing more than the result of inhospitality. In this case, they say that the air is oversaturated with water vapor. (By the way, the same is said about a sugar solution and other solutions.) For example, at 10 degrees Celsius, one cubic meter of air can contain 9.5 grams of water, and at 20 degrees – almost 17 grams. By the way, completely clean air can be oversaturated with water vapor quite significantly. However, the atmosphere always contains a large number of different dust particles, salt crystals and other substances that serve as condensation centers for water droplets.

If the air temperature is below zero degrees, the freezing point of water, then the water molecules in the cloud will immediately form tiny ice crystals. The size of these crystals is no more than one tenth of a millimeter. True, for this you again need an assistant - a small speck of dust, which will play the role of a seed. It is on a small speck of dust that the first ice crystal forms. Yes, yes, snowflakes start as dust. First, water evaporates from the surface of any body of water. Then water vapor rises into the air, condenses on dust particles already in the cloud and moves down the cloud in air currents that behave rather strangely. And the end result is magical stars that captivate with their beauty. Then the crystal begins its journey across the cloud and turns into a snowflake. If the temperature in the cloud or any part of it is above zero degrees, then our crystal will eventually melt and will subsequently travel in a different capacity - like a drop of water. A snowflake will be able to get out of the cloud only in winter, when the ambient temperature drops below zero. Only then will a snowflake be able to fall to the ground, delighting us with pure white creaky snow. What happens to a snowflake as it moves from the top of the cloud to the ground? The journey begins when, in a cold cloud unable to hold much water vapor, microscopic drops of water are attracted to the smallest specks of dust, freeze, and then this nondescript ice crystal grows into a beautiful star.

Only in books about Santa Claus, all snowflakes are beautiful six-pointed stars. In fact, snowflakes are different. Sometimes these are simple plates, in some cases they are prisms and even needles. The shape of a snowflake is determined by two main conditions: temperature and the degree of saturation of the air with water vapor. Under different conditions, snowflakes of different shapes are obtained. Beautiful snowflakes love high humidity and do not like severe frost. They say that no two snowflakes are alike. But there is something that still unites them. Taking a closer look at different snowflakes, you will notice that their shape is always based on a hexagon. And again, the best illustration is… sugar. If you have enough patience, you can grow quite large crystals of sugar or salt. All salt crystals have the shape of small bricks: rectangular parallelepipeds. If it is possible to grow a large salt crystal, then its shape will be a rectangular parallelepiped, and under certain growth conditions - a cube. Having broken this large cube, for example with a hammer, it is easy to make sure that all the fragments will also have the shape of parallelepipeds. Humanity has long been interested in unusual stones that have the correct shape and cut. The ancient Greeks, by the way, considered rock crystal (quartz) crystals to be water frozen forever. The number of crystals known to man has constantly increased. Various magical properties began to be attributed to crystals. Rich people wanted to own rare and unusual crystals. Interestingly, the study of snowflakes back in the 17th century led scientists to the brilliant idea that crystals consist of tiny and identical particles with a certain shape. So it would not be a great exaggeration to say that snowflakes laid the foundation for the development of a large branch of physics - crystallography. The basis of an ice crystal is a regular hexagon formed by water molecules. It turns out that in a water molecule the angle between the oxygen-hydrogen bond is 104 degrees, but in the solid state the oxygen atoms form a regular hexagon. More precisely, two regular triangles, slightly offset from each other along an axis passing through their centers. efficient! We looked at the symmetry of snowflakes, the symmetry of the arrangement of atoms in an ice crystal, due to internal factors. But there are also a lot of external factors. Looking at snowflakes under a magnifying glass or under a microscope, you can see that the angles between the rays are always equal to 60 degrees, but the rays of the snowflakes are developed unequally: some are longer, others are shorter. You can often see several snowflakes merging into one, forming snow flakes. In both cases, the real shape of most snowflakes differs from the ideal symmetrical regular six-pointed star. This happens because external factors always bring changes to the ideal picture. What are these insidious external reasons that destroy the fascinating symmetry? All deviations of snowflakes from the norm are associated with the process of their growth. For example, if a snowflake fell freely, rotating evenly around its axis, then its shape will be close to correct. But if for some reason the uniform rotation did not work out and the snowflake, say, flew most of the way on one side, then such a fall will be reflected in its asymmetrical shape. Other adventures can occur with a falling snowflake that prevent the development of a regular hexagonal star. For example, the smallest drop of water can freeze to it - and the snowflake will no longer be symmetrical. And it also happens that in good, windless weather, up to two hundred snowflakes stick together, forming beautiful snow flakes. It turns out that not only the atomic laws, according to which water molecules are arranged in a crystal lattice into regular hexagons, play a role in the formation of the real shape of a snowflake. The symmetry of the external environment in which the snowflake was born, grew and went from cloud to earth also determines its appearance. You just need to learn to “read” the fate of a snowflake by its shape.
