Thus, different conditions for crystal growth both in the cloud and during the journey of a snowflake from the cloud to the ground (when a cold snowflake, flying through warmer layers of atmospheric air saturated with water vapor, continues to grow) form completely different shapes. But one thing will always be common in all snowflakes - they will have the shape of a six-pointed star or hexagon. In other words, a shape that is determined by the internal structure of the crystal. The amazing transformation that occurs with snowflakes, willy-nilly, makes us think about the role played by the laws of symmetry - namely, they delicately sharpen the rays of snowflakes - in nature. After all, every snowflake begins its journey with processes that seem to have no symmetry or order at all: evaporation from a puddle, a speck of dust as a seed, and so on. But nature seems to be telling us about the primacy of symmetry, that the laws by which atoms line up in the crystals of snowflakes defeat all chance. Unfortunately, not everyone knows about the rules of symmetry and how a snowflake is properly structured. Therefore, in New Year's books and magazines, in the New Year's decorations of our cities, there are a lot of snowflakes that have four, five or even eight rays, but such snowflakes do not exist in nature. But you and I can now easily distinguish real snowflakes from fake ones.

Merging snowflakes into snow flakes

⟦column⟧

efficient! We looked at the symmetry of snowflakes, the symmetry of the arrangement of atoms in an ice crystal, due to internal factors. But there are also a lot of external factors. Looking at snowflakes under a magnifying glass or under a microscope, you can see that the angles between the rays are always equal to 60 degrees, but the rays of the snowflakes are developed unequally: some are longer, others are shorter. You can often see several snowflakes merging into one, forming snow flakes. In both cases, the real shape of most snowflakes differs from the ideal symmetrical regular six-pointed star. This happens because external factors always bring changes to the ideal picture. What are these insidious external reasons that destroy the fascinating symmetry? All deviations of snowflakes from the norm are associated with the process of their growth. For example, if a snowflake fell freely, rotating evenly around its axis, then its shape will be close to correct. But if for some reason the uniform rotation did not work out and the snowflake, say, flew most of the way on one side, then such a fall will be reflected in its asymmetrical shape. Other adventures can occur with a falling snowflake that prevent the development of a regular hexagonal star. For example, the smallest drop of water can freeze to it - and the snowflake will no longer be symmetrical. And it also happens that in good, windless weather, up to two hundred snowflakes stick together, forming beautiful snow flakes. It turns out that not only the atomic laws, according to which water molecules are arranged in a crystal lattice into regular hexagons, play a role in the formation of the real shape of a snowflake. The symmetry of the external environment in which the snowflake was born, grew and went from cloud to earth also determines its appearance. You just need to learn to “read” the fate of a snowflake by its shape.
