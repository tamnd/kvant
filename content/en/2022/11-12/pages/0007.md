In classical physics, knowledge of the state of an object at a given moment in time determines the exact state of the object at subsequent moments. But in new (quantum) mechanics, it is possible to predict and calculate only the probabilities of a state, for example, based on the solution of the Schrödinger equation. And the quantities measured in experiments can only be average values ​​found according to the rules of mathematical statistics, and fluctuations - deviations from these averages. Heisenberg formulated a rule when it is necessary to use a quantum approach to the mechanics of the microworld. This principle is called the “uncertainty relation.” In this regard, for the first time in physics, the need arose to deal with the philosophical question of what reality is. In classical physics of the macrocosm, the symbols of reality - mass, speed, energy, characteristics of the electric and magnetic fields - always allow for experimental verification. The accuracy of measurements depends only on the perfection of the device. But in the microworld, the usual characteristics of a state, for example, position and velocity, turned out to be fundamentally impossible to measure accurately, even with the most advanced instruments, if these measurements were made simultaneously. The criterion for the transition to such a principle was Planck’s constant, intuitively introduced earlier by Max Planck, Elbert Einstein and Niels Bohr to solve specific problems (the theory of thermal radiation, the corpuscular behavior of electromagnetic radiation, the behavior of the atomic electron). It turned out that Planck's constant is a world constant that must always be taken into account when solving problems in the microworld. Bohr went even further. Analyzing the uncertainty principle, he came to the conclusion that the very fact of measuring a quantity changes the state of a micro-object in such a way that its further behavior becomes different from what it would have been without this measurement. Despite the theoretical difficulties, the application of new mechanics turned out to be very useful. Increasingly accurate measurements of the spectra of chemical elements and compounds have been confirmed and predicted. At the same time, the concept of electron spin was born in quantum theory, confirmed by the refinement of quantum approaches made by Paul Dirac. Wolfgang Pauli introduced the principle of approaching a collective - an “ensemble” of particles with the same characteristic spin (1 2), and showed how, using quantum mechanics, one can understand the structure of the periodic system of elements, i.e. structure of electronic shells of atoms. This did not require the solution of fundamental questions about probabilistic causality and physical reality and became the first major achievement of quantum mechanics. A quantum explanation of the chemical bond in a molecule soon followed—quantum chemistry was born. And only on its basis it was possible to understand the structure of the “molecules of life” DNA and RNA. Solid state quantum physics emerged and was used to create semiconductor materials. Perhaps it is this application of quantum mechanics that is most significant for people today. After all, without quantum mechanics, all the electronic devices that surround us would not have been invented and created - from pocket gadgets, which have so greatly changed the possibilities and features of communication and cognition, to supercomputers. The “mysterious” phenomena of ferromagnetism and superconductivity were explained. And finally, quantum mechanics turned out to be absolutely necessary in nuclear physics. It is impossible to doubt the truth of quantum mechanics; it is as necessary for humanity as classical mechanics (Newtonian) and classical electrodynamics (Maxwellian) once became necessary.
