Five years after Bell’s article, an article by four experimental physicists from three American universities was published. Based on the first letters of the last names, this article is called CHSH. The first in both the letter of the surname and the meaning in this article was one of the current trio of laureates - John Klauser. The article was entitled "Experiments Proposed to Test Local Theories with Hidden Parameters." In this article, it was proposed to work with photons instead of electron pairs, the spin direction of which was called the direction of photon polarization (the orientation of the plane in which the electric intensity vector oscillates in the wave model of a quantum). In a simplified wave model, a photon rotates around the axis of its propagation, having two possible states - clockwise or counterclockwise (so-called circular polarization). In quantum mechanics, polarization of a photon means that the photon has a spin, which can have two values: h or h. You can control the direction of photon polarization or fix this direction using polaroid crystals. This is much simpler and cheaper than controlling electron spins using a magnetic field. The difficulties of these experiments lie in something else: in particular, it is difficult to obtain individual photons or beams of photons with the same wavelength and the same polarization direction. The first success was achieved by D. Klauser (together with his graduate student S. Friedman). In these experiments, photons were produced in a device containing a pair of excited calcium ions and released in pairs. In the path of the pairs there were polaroids, the rotation of which could be controlled and the direction of polarization of the photons could be recorded. Ex-

The experiment lasted several days, and during this time a huge amount of statistical data on photon polarization angles was collected. Automatic processing of these data made it possible to calculate the function, which for this experiment was expressed by Bell’s second inequality 1 d F d 0. The value of the correlation function turned out to be greater than zero. This indicated a violation of Bell's inequality, i.e. about the unnecessaryness of searching for hidden parameters and the validity of standard quantum mechanics. But it was necessary to continue experiments in order to more reliably determine the correlation function. The next step was taken by A. Aspe and his colleagues in 1982. At this time, laser technology had already become widely developed, and Aspe, being a laser physicist, used laser beams to produce pairs of photons. This made it possible to obtain pairs of photons much more efficiently and quickly. The Bell function was reliably determined to be greater than zero: F 0.101 r 0.020. Moreover, the value of this function, calculated using standard quantum mechanics, was 0.112, i.e. right in the middle of the confidence interval. The results of these experiments reliably implied the fact that the photons of each pair were in an entangled state. The completeness of quantum mechanics was confidently confirmed once again. In Aspe's experiments, it was possible to change the orientation of photon spins every 10 nanoseconds by rotating polaroids. The distance between the polaroids, which recorded the direction of spins in a pair of photons, was 12 meters. Light travels this distance in 40 nanoseconds, and the change in state of photons occurred 4 times more often. Therefore, the correlation between one and the other photons of a pair occurred not because of information transfer, but because each pair of photons going in opposite directions initially represented something in common, an entangled two-photon object for which the distance between its parts simply does not exist. And the speed of light has nothing to do with it.
