Comment. Participants were required to find only one example. We'll show you how to find them all.

Let the number A be written at least 222 times. Someone wrote A at least three times (otherwise the number of A in the answers will be no more than 100 2 222). Let's call this Council member the Master. The Magister's numbers add up to either 102 or 98. It is easy to see that cases where one of these numbers is 100 or 101 do not apply. This means that we can consider both numbers written by the Master to be two-digit (if one of them is single-digit, we will write zero in place of the tens). So, one of the Master's numbers is AA. If the second begins with the number A, then their sum is either no more than 44 49 98, or no less than 55 50! 102. So the Master wrote AA and BA. Since the sum of these numbers ends in 2 or 8, then the number A can be equal to 1, 6, 4 or 9. The following 4 cases are possible: 1) if AA 11, then BA 102 – 11 91; 2) if AA 66, then BA 102 – 66 36; 3) if AA 44, then BA 98 – 44 54; 4) if AA 99, then BA 98 - 99 0 is not suitable. With AA 11, BA 91, there are actually gnomes and elves (in some order) either 11 and 89, or 9 and 91. In the first case, the number 1 is named no more than three times by each of the 11 people and a maximum of once by each of the 89, i.e. less than 200 times in total. In the second case, 1 can be named three times by each of the 91 people and not once by each of the 9, i.e. total maximum 91 3,273 times. This case fits and gives two options: 9 elves and 91 dwarves, 9 dwarves and 91 elves. Similar reasoning shows that in case 2 the council could have 66 elves, 34 dwarves (or vice versa - 66 dwarves, 34 elves), and case 3 is not suitable.
