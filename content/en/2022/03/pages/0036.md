straight lines when viewed through a calcite crystal may bifurcate to varying degrees or even not bifurcate at all. Why is this happening? Let's put several points on a sheet of paper and examine them through calcite (Fig. 4). We will see two images of each point corresponding to the e-rays. For all points, the lines connecting their images are parallel and vertically oriented. This figure allows one to explain the observed pattern of line bifurcation. For example, the three left dots on the paper were located on a vertical line. We see that after passing through the crystal, the images of the e-rays of these points are also on the same vertical line. It follows that the original vertical line, made up of many points, will not bifurcate. Now we will rotate the crystal over figure with points (Fig. 5 and 6). We see that for each point the image corresponding to one ray remains stationary, while the second one moves around it. In this case, the line connecting them remains parallel to the diagonal of the obtuse angle of the upper face.

Let us recall that in our experiment the initial rays fall on the crystal perpendicular to its rhombic face {1011}. Therefore, firstly, the o-ray (for which the refractive index in calcite is $n_{o}$ 1.658) retains its original direction and does not change it when the crystal rotates. The corresponding point image remains motionless. Secondly, the e-ray (the refractive index in calcite $n_{e}$ varies in the range 1.486–1.658) experiences refraction in the crystal. The amount of refraction depends on the crystallographic direction in which the original beam is incident. Rotation of the crystal causes the e-ray to rotate around the o-ray. Where in calcite does the optical axis pass (line O O c on figure 1), i.e. the direction along which light, when propagating, does not experience double refraction? This direction is [0001], coinciding with the 3rd order symmetry axis in the calcite lattice (when rotated around this axis by an angle of 2 S 3, the body turns into itself). In our experiment, light rays fall on the crystal not in the direction of the optical axis, but at an angle of 57 q 30 c to it. That's why we see a picture of birefringence (birefringence).
