Are you really familiar with this?

PHYSICS + CHEMISTRY

What, another collaboration between physics and adjacent natural sciences, another interdisciplinary alliance? Certainly! All sorts of combinations arise if you need to combine efforts, if increasingly complex problems require a synthesis of methods, ideas, and approaches from various sciences. It is not surprising, by the way, that in Moscow there are two academic institutes - chemical physics and physical chemistry. And in school courses we will find points of intersection between physics and chemistry - when it comes to molecular kinetic theory, the structure of crystals, electrolysis or the interaction of radiation with matter. Well, let's walk hand in hand with physics and chemistry around the vicinity of these points.

It's interesting that...

... the smallest particles - individual molecules - of inorganic substances such as table salt or soda do not exist. When dissolved, their crystals fall apart, forming ions, and we will not find any “independent” molecules in the solution. ... the author of the gas law known from the course of physics, the English scientist Robert Boyle, back in the 17th century, invented the first chemical device - an indicator of the acidity of the medium (litmus paper), which became the initial step from ancient alchemy to modern science. ... after conducting many experiments with 45 substances at various degrees of dilution, the Swedish physical chemist Svante Arrhenius created the theory of electrolytic dissociation, which, however, was met with hostility by eminent scientists, including Mendeleev. However, the theory turned out to be extremely fruitful for the development of science, and Arrhenius was one of the first to be awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1903. ...the very first Nobel Prize laureate in chemistry was the Dutch scientist Jacob Van't Hoff. Like Arrhenius, he came to understand the dependence of the rate of chemical reactions on temperature, which, by the way, is especially important for many processes of organic chemistry, including cooking. ... quantum mechanics received its final and clear formulation in 1926, in the famous equation derived by the Austrian theoretical physicist Erwin Schrödinger. This equation placed all chemistry on a solid theoretical foundation: the meaning of the atomic number in the periodic table and the value of valency became clear, and the nature of chemical bonds was clarified. ... discovered by physicists in the forties of the last century, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) turned out to be one of the most important analytical methods in chemistry, indispensable in the study of complex organic molecules. ...in 1981, physicists G. Binnig and G. Rohrer from the Swiss branch of IBM created a scanning tunneling microscope, which led to the ability to assemble molecular structures from individual atoms. This is how a direction called “nanotechnology” arose. ... among the achievements of electrochemistry, lithium-ion batteries stand out, increasingly expanding the scope of their application. So, for unmanned aerial vehicles they turned out to be good because they do not leave a thermal trace and are superior to other types of similar sources of electricity in terms of specific energy.
