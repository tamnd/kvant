AN OLD COUNTING DEVICE – the so-called “Napeer sticks” – is described in many sources. The device, first described in the book “Rabdology” (“Rabdologi seu Numerationis per Virgulas libri duo”) by the Scottish baron and mathematics enthusiast John Napier (by the way, was the first to propose the concept of a logarithm), published in 1617, made it possible to quickly obtain the product of a multi-digit number by a single-digit number. The last digit of the product – 5 – is equal to the number under the slash on the rightmost stick. The penultimate is the sum of the number above the line on the rightmost stick - 4 - and the number below the line on the penultimate stick - 5, i.e. 9. The remaining numbers were determined similarly (from right to left): 0 + 0 = 0, 3 + 5 = 8, 2 (first digit of the result). The entire product is equal to 28095. Of course, there were cases when, when determining the next digit, the unit was transferred in the mind to the digit adjacent to the left, where it should also be taken into account (check this, for example, for the case of multiplying the number 8365 by 8). In the 80s of the 19th century, the French engineer Henri Genay, at the suggestion of the mathematician Edouard Luc, automated these calculations. The device, called the “Zhenay-Luc Bars,” is shown in figure 4. It consisted of a series of narrow wooden bars, the long edges of which were depicted with numbers and dark triangles. How did this device work? Before answering this question, let’s explain why many of the numbers are located on it in this way and what these dark triangles are. Let's consider multiplying a 10-digit number by a single-digit number, assuming that all the digits of the multiplicand are different, for example 2607194385. Since the result can be 11-digit, we’ll make a table with 11 separate tablets - these will be our “bars”.

At the top of the plates we indicate the corresponding digits of the multiplicand (Fig.5). We will henceforth call the leftmost plate the “base” one. Let's divide it into two columns. In the first column of the base table we will provide 8 cells with possible multiplier digits (2–9), and in the second column for each digit we will write down all possible values ​​of the first digit of the product by it. What could they be, these first numbers? When multiplied by a single-digit $число^{n}$, the first digit of the product can be equal to $1, 2, …,^{n}$ – 1. Let’s write this in the table (blue numbers), also taking into account the number 0 (the desired product can be 10-digit). In each of the tablet-bars, except for the base left one, we also write down the possible values ​​of the number of units in the product of the number in its upper part by the corresponding number in the base table. The last digit of the product is equal to 0, and the two is transferred to the adjacent table. It should use the number 4 (2 + 2): due to the transfer of two, a shift occurs two positions down. The number 4 will be the second digit of the result from the right. In this case, the triple will also be transferred to the adjacent most significant digit. It must use the number 5 (2 + 3). This will be the third digit from the right of the desired product. In the next digit, taking into account the transfer of one gives the next digit 7 (6 + 1). Continuing to move from right to left, taking into account possible transfers, we get numbers; at figure 6 they are circled in red circles. So, the result is 10428777540. Impressive, isn't it? But that's not all. Is it possible to take into account the transfer automatically, rather than carry out calculations and draw arrows every time, as we did? It turns out that it is possible! This was realized by Henri Zhenai. The dark arrows and triangles on the bars do exactly this transfer. Look at figure 4. For example, the triangle arrow on the block with the number 6, located opposite the number 7 on the base plate, takes into account the transfer of 4 to the place on the left. On the bar with the number 3, located opposite the number 6 in the base table, there are two triangle arrows, since both carry 1 and carry 2 can occur in the digit on the left. As an example of the application of all of the above, consider calculating the product of 7569 and 4 using Zhenaya bars (Fig.7). Moving along the direction of the triangle arrows, we quickly get 30276 as a result.
