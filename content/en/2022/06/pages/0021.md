In the 80s of the 19th century, the French engineer Henri Genay, at the suggestion of the mathematician Edouard Luc, automated these calculations. The device, called the “Zhenay-Luc Bars,” is shown in figure 4. It consisted of a series of narrow wooden bars, the long edges of which were depicted with numbers and dark triangles. How did this device work? Before answering this question, let’s explain why many of the numbers are located on it in this way and what these dark triangles are. Let's consider multiplying a 10-digit number by a single-digit number, assuming that all the digits of the multiplicand are different, for example 2607194385. Since the result can be 11-digit, we’ll make a table with 11 separate tablets - these will be our “bars”.

⟦column⟧

At the top of the plates we indicate the corresponding digits of the multiplicand (Fig.5). We will henceforth call the leftmost plate the “base” one. Let's divide it into two columns. In the first column of the base table we will provide 8 cells with possible multiplier digits (2–9), and in the second column for each digit we will write down all possible values ​​of the first digit of the product by it. What could they be, these first numbers? When multiplied by a single-digit $число^{n}$, the first digit of the product can be equal to $1, 2, …,^{n}$ – 1. Let’s write this in the table (blue numbers), also taking into account the number 0 (the desired product can be 10-digit). In each of the tablet-bars, except for the base left one, we also write down the possible values ​​of the number of units in the product of the number in its upper part by the corresponding number in the base table.
