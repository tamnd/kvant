Vertical ice floes

Snow itself is a very good heat insulator. And even when it gets sharply warmer around, it keeps the cold inside. Moreover, even having reached equilibrium with the surrounding air, snow cannot melt very quickly, since for this it needs to not only be heated, but also melted (the amounts of heat of melting and heating are summed up). On the shore, when the snow melts, water is formed, which goes into the ground. Snow does not allow the underlying layers to warm up. Therefore, with colder temperatures and subsequent warming, the surface of the river becomes icy, and the snowdrifts on the banks remain unshakable in their structure. However, with general warming, they gradually melt and become smaller, sinking into the ground or melting onto the ice of the river. The deeper the river is near the banks, the smaller the snowdrifts along its edges. They can also crack and break into layers - this depends on the soil. The ice structure itself is formed depending on the soil. There may be lenses and other formations inside the ice. Sandy soil is very dense and practically does not allow water particles to pass through, while clay soil does. Therefore, when water freezes, clay soils harden, and when the ice begins to melt, water quickly flows through their pores into the soil.

If the river did not have a sandy bed, it would quickly become shallow. In addition, when ice freezes, it expands and the soil swells. Also, in severe frosts, freezing ice can crack as it expands. Thus, it turns out that it is dangerous to walk on ice not only during a thaw, but also when frost strikes sharply.

Cracks in the snow layer on the shore

Why do some rivers become shallow in winter and show their sandy banks covered with snow? Two processes take place here: firstly, the evaporation of water and the subsequent removal of evaporated masses, which, unlike other seasons, do not return to the reservoir and fall out as precipitation evenly throughout the earth, and secondly, the loss of water into the ground and its freezing. The intensity of the second process depends on the type of soil and the depth of the river. In spring, the snow melts and water from the banks flows into the river, where it returns to its previous volume. Rivers often have steep banks that are gradually washed away by the current, and the river itself usually flows in the lowest place, flowing down from the surrounding banks. In summer, precipitation flows into the river from the surrounding banks, but in winter it does not, so evaporation does not return. In spring, evaporation and melted snow do not go to the clouds, not only because of the cold nights, but also because the vegetation absorbs moisture. But sometimes on a particularly sunny day, moisture can fall in separate droplets without collecting into clouds and rain. In conclusion, we note that meltwater flows into a river or underground, forming a structure of ice floes. As a result, the water level in the reservoir rises. That is why with the arrival of spring the so-called high water comes.
