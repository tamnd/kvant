How rivers become free of ice

T. MOROZOVA

The process of melting ice on rivers and lakes seems trivial at first glance, although this is not entirely true. Often in the spring, warming is followed by cooling, and this happens several times until nature exhausts its reserves of cold and finally releases spring into the expanses of land and water. During winter frosts, ice builds up on rivers. Thus, the ice thickness in the Dnieper is 0.5 meters, in the rivers of the Central European Plain - about 1 meter, and on Siberian rivers - up to 1.5 meters. The more frosty days a year, the thicker the ice. Frozen rivers open up new paths for movement from place to place and provide unique space for walking and traveling. But when the ice begins to melt, it becomes dangerous to walk on it.

The heterogeneity in ice during its chaotic freezing and unfreezing with small temperature fluctuations creates a kind of frame. However, if there are voids in it, then this also poses a danger. After melting, the coastal strip freezes faster and on cold spring nights it is safer to walk along it than along the middle of the river. When moving on ice, it cracks where there are irregularities and curvature of the relief. In such places, you should not cross a frozen body of water during the period of ice melting and its subsequent freezing. If you walk on ice on skis, the pressure on it will be much less than when walking. If you run, the shocks will create great pressure and a greater danger of breaking the ice.
