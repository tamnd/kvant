Thus, we can consider that we have completely solved problem 2 - and the answer and solution turned out to be quite straightforward. Let us now try to explore the question of intransitivity in a different direction. We saw that in the chain from Example 2, the winning probabilities of p A f B, p B f C and p C f A are all equal to 5 9, which means a rather small average payoff - each set beats the next one 5 times out of 9, but loses 4 times. We would like to learn how to win as much as possible - so how much can all the probabilities of winning in a chain exceed 1 2? Could, for example, in a non-transitive chain each set be at least twice as strong as the next one, i.e. win twice as often as you lose? The answer to this question is yes. And in fact, in Efron's set of cubes (example 3) each cube is exactly twice

stronger than the next one, since the probability of winning in this chain is always 2 3. It should be noted, however, that this is only possible because the Efron chain consists of four dice. For three cubes this would be impossible. Problem 3. Given three six-sided cubes A, B and C. Prove that at least one of the three numbers $p A^{f} B, p B f C$ and p C f A does not exceed 7 12. Or maybe there is such an intransitive chain where each set is three times stronger than the next one? Or even five times stronger? But the answers to these two questions turn out to be negative, regardless of the length of the chain and the size of the sets of numbers.
