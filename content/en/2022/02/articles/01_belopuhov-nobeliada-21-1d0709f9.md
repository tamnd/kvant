IPO PHYSIOLOGY AND MEDICINE, and in chemistry, and in economics, this year’s awards are well in line with Nobel’s will - “the annual award of those individuals who, during the previous year, have been able to bring the greatest benefit to humanity.” True, for a long time now we have been talking not about the previous year, but about the previous time, sometimes quite significant. The award in physiology and medicine is impressive. A solution has been found to the reaction of human nervous tissue not only to such a phenomenon as pain, but also to much more intimate feelings - heat, cold and touch. Perhaps the solution to such a touch as tenderness and caress is not far off. It is not for nothing that an article about this award in the journal “Science and Life” is called “The Solution to Warm Hugs” by the author. I would like to call this year’s award medical and poetic. I would like to call the work awarded the prize in chemistry unusual, “inverse”. The usual path for the catalytic synthesis of asymmetric molecules is given by nature at the cellular level. And now, for a number of reactions, a path has been found for the catalytic synthesis of such molecules without the participation of a biological cellular mechanism - as biochemists say, “in vitro”, i.e. in glass, in a test tube, and not “in vivo”, i.e. in a living organism. This opens up (and has already opened up) broad opportunities for the synthesis of drugs with targeted action, especially for hereditary diseases. The prize in physics stands somewhat apart. But back in 1896, the famous Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius expressed the idea that the main cause of warming is an increase in the concentration of carbon dioxide in the earth's atmosphere. Indeed, on Venus, for example, a high concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is the main reason for the high average temperature on this planet (above 400 q C). Syukuro Manabe

Syukuro Manabe, at the age of 26, received a doctorate in meteorology from a Japanese university, after which he went to the United States in 1957, where he began studying atmospheric physics and became one of the first in the world to look for the relationship between the growing concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere and the rise in global temperature. He not only recorded this relationship, but, most importantly, built physical models to explain it. Manabe's models took into account vertical convection of air masses and the thermal contribution of the global water cycle in nature - heat transfer during moisture condensation. At first, he was not afraid to make a one-dimensional approximation - the behavior of a vertical column of carbon dioxide 40 km high. His further work was significantly complicated by the transition to a three-dimensional model and taking into account the movement of other atmospheric gases. It turned out that the concentrations of nitrogen and oxygen have virtually no effect on atmospheric temperature, while doubling the concentration of carbon dioxide (from 0.02% to 0.04%) increases the temperature by 2 K. And this is a lot!

Back in 1977, the Belgian chemical physicist Ilya Prigogine became a Nobel laureate in chemistry “for his work on the thermodynamics of irreversible processes and their use in chemistry and biology.” Prigogine postulated the existence of nonequilibrium thermodynamic systems, which, under certain conditions, absorbing energy and matter from the surrounding space, can make qualitative leaps with the transition of the system from one state to another, and these leaps are unpredictable. Self-organization occurs in the system. The scientific direction that Prigogine began to engage in, and after him his many followers, was called synergetics. Prigogine himself studied the question of how such an important function of the state of a system as entropy, a quantitative measure of chaos, behaves in time and space. A typical example of the emergence of order from chaos is phase transitions. Many researchers began to analyze phase transitions from the perspective of synergetics. However, it turned out

that the mathematical methods of Prigogine and his followers are not enough to predict the behavior of complex systems. Having taken up this problem, Giorgio Parisi achieved success precisely in the mathematical substantiation of the pattern of appearance of those gigantic fluctuations at the micro level that lead to qualitatively new phenomena at the macro level. Parisi and his collaborators studied such phenomena as combustion fronts (flame fronts), wetting, crystallization, boundaries of turbulent zones in liquid crystals, in the most modern physics - deep inelastic scattering in quantum chromodynamics (the appearance of unexpected complex quark structures) and such an unexpected problem for a physicist as the distribution of transport flows. To this list we must add the search for emissions on the Leasing curve - fluctuations in climate change. The variety of these hobbies of Parisi evokes admiration and interest. But the thing is that all these various tasks were united by one common feature. Their degree of complexity is very high. Each of them contains a huge number of different influences, and even the most modern supercomputers cannot cope with solving such problems head-on. Parisi's mathematical methods allow mathematics itself to highlight those influences that lead to gigantic fluctuations in the behavior of the system. Ultimately, his technique comes down to finding a certain number of energetically favorable (lower energy) states of the system, and the mathematics used is similar in different problems. Back in the 1980s, Parisi himself theoretically discovered a number of patterns of the possible behavior of some materials that seemed completely disordered, and created a mathematical apparatus. The most complete solution was obtained by Parisi to consider the possible states of so-called “spin glasses”.
