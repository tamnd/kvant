But back in 1896, the famous Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius expressed the idea that the main cause of warming is an increase in the concentration of carbon dioxide in the earth's atmosphere. Indeed, on Venus, for example, a high concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is the main reason for the high average temperature on this planet (above 400 q C). But in the 20th century, a significant part of scientists, as well as economists, technologists, and politicians, argued that global warming is an anthropogenic factor associated with a sharp increase in the consumption of carbon fuels for vehicles and thermal power plants that occurred at the turn of the 60s of the last century. This topic was very beneficial for discussion and creating a certain image among politicians and obtaining grants for research in scientific laboratories. But gradually the excitement around this problem began to subside; all possible grants were received and spent. And serious scientific research began. Their results did not receive wide coverage in the scientific and pseudo-scientific literature at that time; they did not go beyond highly specialized scientific journals. The researchers understood that it would take time to be confident in obtaining reliable results. And finally, these works received high praise - the Nobel Prize in Physics. The work was not only narrowly focused - to understand the problem of global warming. The researchers had a clear understanding of the complexity of the issue, so it was solved as a special case of studying a physically complex system. Generally speaking, this is not the first Nobel award for work studying climate change and its consequences. In 2007, the Nobel Peace Prize was awarded to the United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and former US Vice President Al Gore "for their efforts to promote knowledge of human impacts on climate change and to define the basic principles that

which must be developed to counteract such changes.” This work by a group of UN experts continues to this day. The Nobel Peace Prize was awarded for their fourth UN report. And recently, on August 9, 2021, the sixth report took place. The 2018 Nobel Prize in Economics was awarded to Paul Romer and William Nordhaus (USA) “for integrating climate change and technological innovation into long-term macroeconomic analysis.” But these prizes were not rewards for research-based scientific work. They only recorded work on summarizing the available data. Norwegian physicist Vilhelm Bjerknes's work “Weather Forecasting as a Physical Problem” was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Physics twelve times (from 1924 to 1945). But the Nobel Committee for Physics did not consider this work worthy of a high award. And finally, this year the Nobel Committee in Physics decided to reward directly those scientists who laid the foundations for our modern understanding of the processes of planetary climate change. This decision was long awaited and restores justice. Half of the prize (5 million Swedish kronor, which corresponds to approximately 500,000 euros or 40 million rubles) was awarded to climatologists Syukuro Manabe (Japan) and Claus Hasselmann (Germany) “for modeling the Earth’s climate, mathematical description of variable systems and accurate prediction of global warming.” As is clear from this motivation, the prize was given for a mathematically accurate prediction of global warming, in contrast to numerous speculative works and discussions on this topic. The second half of the prize was received by the Italian scientist Giordgio Parisi (Italy) “for the discovery of interactions in chaos and fluctuations in physical systems from atomic to planetary scales.”
