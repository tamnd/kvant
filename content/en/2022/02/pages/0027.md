At this time, his friend Charles David Keeling, a great fan of mountain travel, at a high-altitude weather observatory on the Hawaiian islands on the top of Mauna Loa, carried out long-term measurements of the concentration of $CO_{2}$ in an area far from civilization, where all anthropogenic influences on the atmosphere were supposed to be averaged. It was Keeling who obtained reliable data on the change in carbon dioxide concentration from 3.15 ppm (1 ppm 0.1%, denoted 1) in 1958 to 3.8 in 2005, when Keeling died of a heart attack. But work at this observatory continues now, and for 2020 they have already given a carbon dioxide concentration of 4.15 . And awarding the prize to his friend and scientific collaborator Hasselman perpetuates the scientific achievements of both friends. In the Keeling-Hasselman tandem, the first was a “getter” of facts, and the second was their careful analyzer. He applied a number of mathematical methods for analyzing information, which received the figurative names “random walks” and “fingerprints.” Hasselman's main focus was finding the standard deviations from the average values ​​caused by seasonal changes in carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, connecting the highest and lowest deviations with specific weather conditions in these years, i.e. efforts to link weather and climate and make weather predictions more reliable. At the same time, he reliably showed that if there had not been an increase in the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, which coincided in time with a sharp increase in the number of thermal power plants and other anthropogenic factors, then the situation with the temperature of the atmosphere would not have been so critical at all, it would practically not have increased any more.
