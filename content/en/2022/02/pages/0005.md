The optical property of a parabola was discovered in Ancient Greece by one of the greatest mathematicians of antiquity, Apollonius of Perga (262 BC - 190 BC). Nowadays it is used everywhere. The parabolic mirror in a flashlight creates a narrow, directional beam of light. To do this, the light bulb is placed in focus. The operating principle of a parabolic antenna or parabolic reflector in a telescope is also based on the property of a parabola to transform a beam of parallel rays (and therefore rays coming from a distant source) into a beam converging at one point.

From a parabola you can make a surface of rotation with arbitrarily low resistance. To do this, take the arc of the “inverted” parabola $y x^{2}$ from its vertex to the point located at the focus level (Fig.16). Now let’s compress this arc relative to the point F many times (i.e., make a homothety relative to F) and reflect it relative to the point F. We have obtained a figure from two arcs of parabolas - a large and a small one. They are homothetic with respect to Fig. 16 of their common focus F. A particle flying upward hits a large arc, then, according to the optical property of a parabola, is reflected and flies through the focus and, according to the same optical property, reflected from a small arc, flies vertically upward. So, after two reflections from the parabolic arcs, the particle will fly in the same direction and at the same speed as before.
