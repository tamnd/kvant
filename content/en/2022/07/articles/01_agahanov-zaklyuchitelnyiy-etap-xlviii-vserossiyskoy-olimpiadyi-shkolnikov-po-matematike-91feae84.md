The cillograph shows that at an input AC frequency of 20 kHz with an amplitude of $I_{0} 10$ mA, the average value of current $I_{2cp}$ flowing through resistor $R_{21}$ is not zero and is about 0.9 mA (Fig. 3). In turn, this means that the average speed of the “vibration boat” should be $v_{cp} a Z_{в} I_{2cp} I_{0}$, and in this particular case, with an amplitude of 5 cm and a frequency of 1 Hz, it will be 28 mm s. In other words, during one oscillation of an unbalanced load, the boat moves more than half the amplitude of the oscillations. This, of course, happened with a relatively large average value of the drag coefficient of the medium $O_{сp} | 1,7$ kg s. With such a drag coefficient, the boat, moving with an initial speed of 1 m s,

stops after traveling a distance of only 5.8 cm. How will the average speed change for other values ​​of the drag coefficient? What will change if the vibrations of an unbalanced body are not harmonic? And in general, is such vibrational movement “through the wall” possible if there is a medium, the vibrations are not harmonic, and the drag coefficients when the boat moves back and forth are the same? There are many questions. One of the ways to answer these questions and not only these is not to rush into the manufacture and testing of “vibration walkers”, “vibration boats”, “vibration planes” and other incomprehensible devices, but turn to dynamic analogies for help.
