And when in 1960 the International Committee of Weights and Measures adopted the International System of Units (SI) for preferred use, the Kelvin degree became one of the seven basic units of this system. And it began to be called not “degree kelvin” (q K), but simply “kelvin” (K). Let us dwell briefly on questions about the approval of systems of units and their “registered” names. Today, almost 30 units of measurement of physical quantities have “names”. In the SI system, the 7 basic units are kelvin and ampere, and the other 5 basic units are meter, kilogram, second, mole and candela. Their names were established spontaneously and originated from Latin roots in French pronunciation. The desire to call units of measurement with some everyday word arose at the dawn of human history. These were, for example, the units of length cubit, span, foot, yard, field. It is difficult to establish the origin of such measures as gram, pound, pood, second, minute, hour. Naturally, these names arose and were established in a relatively small area of ​​residence of one people or even a tribe and then spread to states that united different peoples. And very often in the state there were simultaneously several units of measurement of the same quantity. Of course, this made it difficult for people living in different areas to communicate. This was how it was at first with monetary units. But in this case, state authorities established (although not immediately) uniform units and their names. For example, in the vast Roman Empire there was one main gold unit - the aureus - and three silver coins of decreasing denomination. Government authorities paid little attention to other units of measurement. Julius Caesar made such attempts for units of weight and length, but was unable to complete them. A masterpiece of confusion, perhaps, can be considered the existence in France until the 19th century of over a thousand (!) different units of length. For example, the length of a piece of cloth was measured in units representing the width of a loom in a given area, and the area of ​​an arable plot was measured in units of days required for a peasant to plow that land. Only one old name has survived to this day - lie. In Russia there was still relative order compared to such anarchy. The concerns of the reformer tsars from Ivan III to Catherine II left only 8 units - palm, span, elbow, foot, step, arshin, fly fathom and oblique fathom. One of the concerns of the French Academy of Sciences was the work on unifying units of measurement, and primarily units of length. The Academy of Sciences, as part of the Astronomical Institute, included the Bureau of Longitudes, which dealt with this issue. Many different options have been proposed, for example, the length of a certain standard pendulum and a number of astronomical options. But success was achieved only in 1795, when the Bureau of Longitude, called the Chamber of Weights and Measures, was headed by several scientists, including the outstanding French scientist, mathematician, astronomer and physicist Pierre-Simon Laplace. Laplace finally chose the astronomical, or more precisely “universal” unit as the unit of length - one forty millionth of the earth’s meridian passing through Paris. And this unit was called the meter (from the ancient Greek PHWURQ - measure, meter). The Chamber of Weights and Measures attracted scientists to measure the (angular) section of the earth's meridian, to establish a material standard for the meter and, accordingly, to express all previous units of length, area and volume in terms of the meter. Units of weight (mass) and time were also established. The ISS system of units appeared - meter, kilogram, second.
