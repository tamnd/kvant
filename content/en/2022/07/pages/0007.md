Both Gauss himself and Maxwell immediately drew attention to the fact that in the Gaussian system, in the formulas where electric charge and magnetic quantities were present, a dimensional coefficient equal to 3 $10^{10}$ cm s appeared. This is exactly the value of the refined value of the speed of light in vacuum, which was reliably measured in the middle of the 19th century. And it was Maxwell who connected electromagnetism with wave motion, predicting the existence of electromagnetic waves. However, practice and the emerging new science of “electrical engineering” urgently required the separation of electrical units into a special system. In addition, the electric charge was poorly conserved in the Thomson drop galvanometer. It turned out to be impossible to protect electrified water from the loss or acquisition of other charged particles, and above all electrons. Thomson came to the conclusion that what needs to be standardized is not the electrostatic quantity - charge, but the electrodynamic quantity - current strength. For many conducting objects, the force acting on this object from magnets, pieces of iron or its oxides turned out to be comparable to the force of gravity when a current flowed through the object, then obtained from galvanic elements. In 1848, Thomson developed a design and created a device with his own hands, which was named “magnetic scales”. In this device, it was possible to ensure that a current-carrying wire remained stationary in a magnetic field under the influence of opposing forces - gravity and the Ampere magnetic force. The improved design of Thomson's magnetic balances is still the standard for the ampere unit of current in the international modern SI system of units. The situation of smallness of many units in the GHS system compared to practical values ​​has developed not only in electromagnetism. In thermal engineering, the amounts of energy and power with which human life and activity are associated also turned out to be equal to hundreds of millions and billions of ergs or power units of the GHS system. All these inconveniences in mechanics, heat engineering and electrical engineering began to lead to the appearance in different countries of different units both in name and in relation to the units of the Gaussian system. Teaching physics and new scientific and technical disciplines has become more complicated. The need for reforms was on the agenda. In Russia, with its arshin and pound, the transition to a unified system of units was led by the great D.I. Mendeleev. Since 1893, he was director of the Russian Main Chamber of Weights and Measures. It is no coincidence that the village near Moscow, where the All-Russian Research Institute of Physical, Technical and Radio Engineering Measurements is now located, is called Mendeleevo. In England, where this transition was led by W. Thomson, the situation was especially difficult, and in fact the transition has not yet ended. The British, and after them the whole world, measure TV screens not in centimeters, but in inches, and the power of cars is not in kilowatts, but in horsepower. Chemists were the first to come to their senses and, for their needs, introduced a special energy unit - the calorie (equal to 41.87 $10^{6}$ erg). This unit represents the energy required to raise the temperature of one liter of water by 1 q C. It is convenient for expressing the amount of heat released in chemical reactions.
