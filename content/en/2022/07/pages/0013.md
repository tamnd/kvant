KELVIN AND UNIT SYSTEMS

Glasgow's new waterway, the River Clyde. Refreshed after a swim, the daughter of one of Kelvin’s friends suggested: “Why not choose $слово^{Кельвин?}$ for the title, it’s ancient and so beautiful!” Everyone really liked it. All that was left was to buy the land where it was planned to build a house. There was no question of funds to purchase land. Thomson was at this time sufficiently secure in income from his directorship in the telegraph company and from his patents. True, he spent a lot of money on purchasing equipment for the university physics laboratory. But there was a lot left. In any case, there was enough to buy land and even build a castle. And the problem with the title was solved. So the inconspicuous river Kelvin became the prototype of the unit of thermodynamic temperature. In the last decade of the century, Lord Kelvin not only reaped the fruits of his many years of scientific creativity, but also led an active life. For example, he was the chairman of the international Niagara Commission, which ultimately approved the project of the Niagara hydroelectric station, presented by a group of engineers with the participation of the Serbian-American electrical engineer Nikola Tesla. In Scotland, Kelvin oversaw the construction of a hydroelectric power station to supply electricity to one of the world's first aluminum smelters. In the House of Lords, he does not miss meetings and speaks on issues of the development of higher natural science education.

In total, during his life, Kelvin wrote 20 books, more than 600 scientific articles and received 70 patents for various instruments - high-precision ammeters and voltmeters, a navigation compass and a ship's speed meter, the already mentioned droplet electric charge generator, telegraphy instruments and demonstration installations for lectures. Despite difficult personal events (the death of his father when Thomson was not yet 40 years old, the death of his wife with whom he lived for 18 years), he remained friendly towards his colleagues and students. His sense of humor never left him. It was a great blessing for Kelvin to have a friend who admired him, but at the same time criticized and supported him. It was the famous physicist and mathematician George Stokes, professor at Cambridge University. He was 5 years older and lived exactly as long as Kelvin. Their friendship lasted 60 years and nothing overshadowed it. They often met in Cambridge and corresponded. Hundreds (!) of Kelvin's letters to Stokes have survived. Kelvin lived a happy life. The most important thing in her was work, life in science. While still studying at Cambridge, he drew up a schedule; it is preserved in the Kelvin Museum: “Get up at 5 am and light the fire, read scientific literature until 8.15, attend classes until 1 pm, solve problems and write articles until 4 pm, visit church after lunch and until 7 pm, walk until 8.30 pm (if possible in any weather), sleep at 9 pm.” He tried to follow this routine all his life. And only illness in the last two years of his life brought him down. Kelvin died on December 17, 1907 at his Scottish home. Unfortunately, this house has not survived; after Kelvin, no one lived in it - he did not have any heirs. But grateful physicists restored this house in Berlin, where the Kelvin House Museum is now located
