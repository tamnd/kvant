KELVIN AND UNIT SYSTEMS

ical temperature is significant only for a very large number of particles in the system. It makes no sense to talk about the temperature of atomic electrons or nuclear particles. Kelvin was the first to introduce, back in 1848, the concept of absolute zero thermodynamic temperature, which he, extrapolating from gas laws, defined as the reciprocal of the thermal coefficient of expansion of a gas per degree Celsius: 1 0.00366 | 273.2. Thus, on the Celsius scale, absolute zero is equal to 273.2 q C and, conversely, 0 q C corresponds to 273.2 K. Today, the value of absolute zero has been refined to 273.15 q C, and 1 K is defined as the temperature that leads to a change in the energy per degree of freedom of the particle by 1.3806 $10^{23}$ J (the value of the constant Boltzmann). Kelvin standardization involves measuring the exact triple point temperature of water of a given isotopic composition (approximately 0.008 q C). Kelvin's introduction of the concept of thermodynamic temperature played a huge role in the development of thermodynamics. The lowest temperature on Earth is 184 K (–89 q C). It was recorded in 1983 at the Russian Antarctic station “Vostok” near the South Pole. Among the southern polar explorers, even the proverb “The East is a cold matter” was born, paraphrasing Sukhov’s “The East is a delicate matter” (from “The White Sun of the Desert”). A slightly higher temperature was recorded in the Siberian “pole of cold” near Verkhoyansk. The lowest temperature of the planets and satellites of the Solar System is 36.6 K on the surface of Triton, a satellite of Neptune, and 43 K on the surface of Pluto. Interestingly, in the deep craters of the Moon, where the Sun never looks, the temperature is apparently even lower. The estimate for the lowest gas temperature in the Galaxy is approximately 1 K (in the dark cloud of the Boomerang Nebula, 5000 light-years from Earth). In the fall of 2021, a record low temperature of 3.8 $10^{11}$ K was reached. And this is not the limit! In thermodynamics, Kelvin did a lot besides his epoch-making discovery. This is both the Joule–Thomson effect for real gases and the thermoelectric Thomson effect. He also studied molecular physics, experimentally measuring the pressure under the curved surface of liquids due to surface tension. Considering the instability of the movement of a large number of connected drops of liquid, he, together with Helmholtz, established the possibility of the appearance of so-called “turbulent clouds”, grandiose cloudy and foamy sea eddies actually observed in nature.
