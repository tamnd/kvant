MATHEMATICAL

You may recognize the famous Thue-Morse sequence.

Thue–Morse sequence

The Thue–Morse sequence is contained in OEIS number A010060. The Encyclopedia uses a more common version of the sequence, in which the numbers 0 and 1 appear instead of A and B. The Thue–Morse sequence is obtained by sequentially assigning to an existing fragment a second one of the same kind, but in which all elements are replaced by their opposites (0 to 1, 1 to 0). Let's start from 0:

0

01

0110

01101001

0110100110010110...

This sequence is self-similar: it coincides with its own subsequence, consisting of every second member (as well as with the second subsequence 1001..., but with the elements replaced by opposite ones):

0110100110010110 …

0 1 1 0 1 0 0 1 …

This observation suggests another way to get the sequence: start with a string of zeros and repeatedly substitute 0 o 01, 1 o 10. Another way to get the same sequence is to count the number of ones in the binary notation of n and take the remainder when dividing that number by 2. This gives you the nth term of the Thue-Morse sequence (think about why). This approach also suggests how to construct an analogue of the Thue–Morse sequence, consisting of the numbers 0, 1, 2. You need to add the digits in the ternary notation of the number n and take the remainder of division by 3 (you get 012120201... - sequence A053838 in OEIS); similarly for writing in a number system with any other base. You can describe these generalized Thue–Morse sequences in substitution language (think about how).

WORLD

⟦column⟧

One of the properties of the Thue–Morse sequence is that no fragment of it is repeated more than two times in a row, while non-consecutive repetitions are possible. This property led to the rediscovery of the sequence from an unexpected angle. The future world chess champion and mathematician Max Euwe pointed out that if the 0 and 1 in the Thue-Morse sequence correspond to some different sequence of moves that return the position on the board to the original one, then this sequence will allow the game to continue indefinitely. Let us recall that at the beginning of the 20th century there was a rule in chess according to which a draw was declared when the same sequence of moves was repeated three times in a row. For Euwe's design to stop working, modern rules require the game to be completed by repeating any position three times. Another application of the Thue–Morse sequence, on the one hand, beautiful, and on the other hand, interesting, arises if you try to draw a broken line consisting of links of the same length, according to the following rules. When a 0 occurs in the Thue–Morse sequence, draw a link and turn 60q to the left; when 1 is encountered, we remain in place and change the direction of drawing the next link to 180q. Thus, after the first link there is a left turn at 60q, after the second - a right turn at 120q (60q to the left and another 180q), etc. Draw at least a dozen links. What happens? Note that the Thue-Morse sequence also appears in sports programming. The interested reader can find numerous other applications of the Thue–Morse sequence by visiting oeis.org. We will return to the arrows. In article [3] it was proven that the fair order of shots is indeed described by the Thue–Morse sequence. To do this, we first show that if we group the terms of the sequence into pairs, then each pair will have two different values ​​(AB BA BA AB...), and then we prove the self-similarity property by induction.
