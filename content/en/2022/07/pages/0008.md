And when electrical engineers began to understand the units of electromagnetism, the joule proposed by Karl Wilhelm Siemens was chosen as the unit of measurement of energy, both mechanical, electrical and thermal, and the watt was chosen as the unit of power. But oddly enough, the calorie has not gone into oblivion until now. It lasts the longest in the food industry. Modern food packaging has a dual energy label - in kilojoules and in kilocalories, with the former called the energy value and the latter the calorie content or nutritional value. This, of course, is connected with medicine, where all the advice on combating overeating is given in the form of tables, in which kilocalories are still often present on the Internet. This is how they are recorded in the memory of most people who unconditionally believe in medicine. Even now, many consider the easy-to-remember 2000 kilocalories per day to be a good norm for the energy value of food for those who lead a sedentary lifestyle. The calorie also has a slight advantage over the joule - the specific heat capacity of water is almost exactly equal to 1 cal g deg. Phedrahs had to calculate and measure pressure in different ways. How did this transition to a single international system of units take place? It all started in 1875, when, on the initiative of the French Academy of Sciences, representatives of 20 states gathered in Paris, or rather in the Paris suburb of Sèvres, where the French Chamber of Weights and Measures was located. And on May 20, 1875, the International Convention on Weights and Measures was signed, which announced the creation of the International Bureau of Weights and Measurements, which soon became known as the International Committee on Weights and Measures. The convention was signed by 18 parties, who had powers from their governments. On behalf of Russia, the convention was signed by embassy adviser Grigory Okunev “on behalf of Emperor Alexander II.” According to the convention, the 18 members of the committee must meet annually to report on the organization of metrological studies in the participating countries; for reports on the activities of regional Chambers of Weights and Measures; to make recommendations to general conferences on weights and measures. At these conferences, once every 4 years, provisions on recommended changes in unit systems should be discussed and approved, and the head of the International Committee should be elected for 4 years. Since then, over almost 150 years, 26 General Conferences have taken place. Some 4th anniversaries were missed, including due to world wars. Among the elected committee leaders were metrologists from different countries. For 20 years, from 1915 to 1936, the leadership role was played by the Swiss scientist Charles Guillaume, who in 1920 won the Nobel Prize for creating alloys with a very low coefficient of thermal expansion, necessary for length standards. Since the beginning of 2013, the organization has been headed by the French scientist Martin Milrie.
