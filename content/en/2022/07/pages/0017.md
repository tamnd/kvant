KELVIN AND UNIT SYSTEMS

The “Age of Steam” began to change and turn into the “Age of Electricity”. Kelvin considered the appearance of radio, the discovery of the electron and x-rays to be natural consequences of electrodynamics. And he did not imagine that these discoveries could seriously affect the development of civilization. In love with physics and captivated by its successes, Kelvin was convinced that thermodynamics and electrodynamics were the pinnacle achievements of human understanding of nature and that science should be concerned with translating these achievements into technology for the benefit of improving living conditions. But still, something bothered him. Thus, on April 27, 1900, at a lecture at the Royal Institution, he repeated what he had already published in an article entitled “19th-Century Clouds Over Dynamical Theory.” Here are his words: “The beauty and clarity of the dynamic theory, according to which heat and light are forms of motion (energy), is currently overshadowed by two clouds. The first of them is the question of how the Earth can move through an elastic medium, what is essentially the luminiferous ether? The second is the Maxwell-Boltzmann doctrine of energy distribution, which is not consistent with reliable measurements of the thermal radiation of bodies." And the pessimistic conclusion: “The simplest way is simply to ignore the existence of these clouds.” Referring to Kelvin's articles and speeches, a number of physicists at European universities declared the end of physics as a developing science. “Everything important has already been done, all that remains is to make more and more accurate measurements,” this is what one of the professors at the University of Munich, with whom Planck consulted about choosing a topic for his dissertation, told the young Max Planck. Fortunately for science, Planck did not heed the advice of the venerable professor and took up theoretical thermodynamics. The result of this choice is known. But Heinrich Hertz, who was the first to experience electromagnetic radiation, shortly after this discovery, gave a lecture to students, when asked what the application of this radiation could be,

This would mean that man has learned to split the atom.” Kelvin's words about two “clouds” darkening the orderly edifice of physical science were based on the brilliant knowledge of physics that he developed. And his genius was manifested in the fact that it was from these “clouds” that modern physics was born, which is not only not completed, but the end of its completion is not visible. The “luminiferous ether,” whose vibrations were considered to be electromagnetic waves, turned out to be unnecessary after Michelson’s experiments and the creation of the theory of relativity and was soon forgotten. All that remains is a figurative mention of this word as a synonym for radio communications. The difficulty with the theory of thermal radiation (the second “cloud”) even received the frightening name “ultraviolet catastrophe.” But already at the very end of 1900, Planck’s brilliant work showed that there was no catastrophe. Quantum ideas about radiation were born. Kelvin did not live to see the triumph of quantum theory and the creation of quantum mechanics. Who knows, he would not have repented of his words and thoughts, which he so actively preached at the end of his life. One of his biographers, Charles Watson, spoke about this tragedy in Kelvin’s scientific career in 1969: “In the first half of his career, Thomson seemed incapable of being wrong, in the second, incapable of being right.” Someone said: “There are two kinds of fortune tellers. Some don't know what they're talking about. Others don't know that they don't know what they're talking about." Kelvin clearly belonged to the second group of predictors; he did not have time to learn what refuted his judgments. And to paraphrase Pushkin, we can say that brilliant people make brilliant mistakes. Brilliant mistakes can lead to brilliant discoveries.
