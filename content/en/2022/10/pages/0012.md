Yes, for a person with a boat, their total center of gravity will retain its location, but only because no external forces act in the direction of movement of the person and the boat (and the system is isolated in its projection onto the axis along which the person walks and the boat moves). But for a balloon, the opposite is true - the system is not isolated, since it is in the vertical direction that very significant external forces act on all its elements. This is, firstly, the force of gravity, and secondly, the buoyant force of the air. They determine the movement of the ball. Yes, as a matter of fact, the very presence of a ball with a person in the air in a state of equilibrium is determined primarily by these external forces, and not by any movement of the person along the stairs. But it is quite acceptable to consider that the basket forms an inextricable whole with the ball itself, so that the mass of the basket is included in the mass of the ball. The basket is located directly under the very bottom of the ball - almost right next to it. And since the buoyant force plays an important (even, perhaps, primary) role in our problem, we note at the same time that the own volume of the basket and the shell of the ball can be completely neglected, and the buoyant force acting on them can be considered absent. With regard to the stairs, we will be even more severe - we will neglect both its mass and volume. Thus, of the objects under consideration, two remain: a gas-filled ball and a person. We take the mass of the shell of the ball (including the basket) equal to $m_{ш} 200$ kg, the mass of a person – $m_{ч} 80$ kg. We assume that the ball is located quite high - at an altitude of H 1000 m above sea level, and the length of the stairs will be considered equal to L 100 m. The density of air, as is known, decreases with height (and, by the way, it is this decrease that allows balloons to “hover” at a certain height - otherwise the ball would rise up to the very end of the atmosphere).
