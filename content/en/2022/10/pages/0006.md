Using a construction similar to that considered in the third case, prove that for the first two cases the line along which the cutting plane and the plane containing the points of contact of the inscribed sphere with the cone intersect is the directrix of the conic. Remark 1. In his work [1], Dandelin did not prove the statement of Exercise 4. This was done by the Irish mathematician Piers Morton in 1829. The Irish mathematician and bishop Hugh Hamilton in 1758, in his treatise on conic sections, made constructions similar to Dandelen's construction. And although Hamilton did not fit spheres into a cone, he considered the tangent circles of such spheres and the cone. He defined such a circle as a circle on a cone lying at the same distance along the generatrix from the top of the conic at which its focus is located. For such a circle, he proved that for any point of a conic section the length of the generatrix to the circle is equal to the distance to the focus and the plane of the circle intersects the plane of the conic along its directrix.

Note that if the original plane passes through the vertex of the cone, then it intersects the cone either along a point, or along a pair of straight lines, or along one straight line. Sometimes such sections are called degenerate conics. Thus, we have described all conic sections.
