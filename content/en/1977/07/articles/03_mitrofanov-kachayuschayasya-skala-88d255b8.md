Rocking Rock

“...It must be said that in these places it is not uncommon to come across the so-called “swinging rock” - a very beloved phenomenon, the essence of which is that a separate piece of rock, from time immemorial, acquires stability of equilibrium. It usually stands on a stone platform and, if you swing it, it, like an insert, returns to its original position. Such rocks sometimes weigh thousands of tons, but are obedient to the movement of the hand of a person of average strength. Such a rock cannot fall, unless, of course, it is blown up..."

These are lines from Alexander Green’s story “The Rocking Rock” - a sad story about a poor hunter who was offered, for three million, to overturn a huge pillar swinging near its equilibrium position. The hunter, despite all his efforts, failed to cope with the task and went crazy (but never left his side and kept trying to push the stone).

Let's try to understand why the “rocking rock” is stable.
We know that in order to be in an equilibrium position, two conditions must be met:

vector sum of all forces,
acting on the body
equal to zero; (*)
algebraic sum
moments of all strength
relative to an arbitrary point
is equal to zero. (**)

Not every equilibrium position is stable. For example, a needle, on which only the force of gravity and the reaction of the support acts, does not stand freely on a smooth table, although if the needle is placed strictly vertically, conditions (*) and (**) will be satisfied. But at the slightest deviation of the needle from the vertical, moments of force arise that overturn the needle. At the same time, the brick stands steadily on any edge. And no matter how we reduce the brick, maintaining its shape, it will still stand stably on the table. But the same brick is very difficult to “balance,” for example, on a soccer ball (we will keep the ball motionless).

From the above we can conclude that the stability of the body’s equilibrium depends on the shape of the body (more precisely, its base) and the surface of the support. To derive a stability criterion, let us turn again to a rocking rock and consider the case when the stone and the support in the area of ​​contact are spherical. We will assume that the stone and the block on which it stands have been worn down or weathered and become smooth, without chips or protrusions, so that the area of ​​contact between the stone and the support is small and can be taken as a point. figure 1 shows the contour of the stone and the support by a vertical plane passing through the point of their contact (point C). O and O' are the centers of the spherical surface of the stone and the support in the contact area, r and R are the corresponding radii.

For the stone to be balanced, it is necessary first of all that the center of gravity (point P) lies on the vertical OO'. In this case, conditions (*) and (**) will be satisfied. Let's see what a slight deviation of the stone from its original position will lead to.

Let, as a result of deflection, the position of the stone on the support become the same as at figure 2. If in this case the center of gravity of the stone turns out to be equal to the vertical AA', then the moment of gravity relative to the point of support A will contribute to further deflection, and the stone will not return to its original position. If point P turns out to be

So, if CP < CQ (see Fig. 2), then the equilibrium is stable. Let's see how CP R and r are related to each other. In triangle OAQ

$$\angle OAQ = \alpha, \angle AOQ = \beta = \frac{\widehat{CA}}{r} = \frac{\widehat{C'A}}{r} = \alpha \frac{R}{r} \text{ (так как } \alpha \text{ мало).}$$

By the law of sines we have:

$$\frac{OQ}{\sin \alpha} = \frac{r}{\sin[\pi - (\alpha + \beta)]} = \frac{r}{\sin(\alpha + \alpha \frac{R}{r})}.$$

The one of interest shows small deviations of the stone from its equilibrium position. By "small deflection" we mean that the distance "traversed" by the point of contact on the surface of the support, i.e. arc C'A (and therefore arc C'A = C'A), is small compared to the radii r and R of the surface of the stone and support. And this means that the angles α and β are small, i.e. α << 1 and β = α \frac{R}{r} << 1.

For small angles, as is known, the sine of the angle is equal to the angle itself with good accuracy. Therefore the expression

(1) can be written as follows:

$$\frac{OQ}{r} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{R}{r}}.$$

Hence $OQ = \frac{r^2}{R + r}$. Since $CQ = r - OQ = \frac{Rr}{R + r}$, the condition for stable equilibrium of the stone (i.e., condition $CP < CQ$) is written as the inequality

$$CP < \frac{Rr}{R + r}$$

(2) If the surface has a wavy shape with radius $R$ (the shape of the inner surface of a sphere of radius $R$), then the condition for stable equilibrium of the stone on the support looks like this:

$$CP < \frac{Rr}{R - r}$$

(CP is still the distance from the point of contact to the center of gravity in the equilibrium position). Try to derive this formula yourself.

Let us now note the following important circumstance. Let us assume that the balance of the stone on the support is stable. When the stone deviates from the equilibrium position, a moment of force will arise that prevails over this deviation: a shoulder appears in the gravitational force relative to the new fulcrum (see Fig. 2). To keep the stone motionless in a new position, it is necessary to apply an external force such that its moment relative to the new fulcrum is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the moment of gravity. (The magnitude and direction of this force are determined by the conditions (*)). This means that even for a slight deviation of the body from a stable equilibrium position, it is necessary to do work against the force of gravity. This work goes to increase the potential energy of the body. This means that in a position of stable equilibrium, the potential energy of the body has a minimum value, or, what is the same, the center of gravity of the body occupies the minimum position. Therefore, conditions (2) can be derived differently by looking at what happens to the center of gravity of the stone when it is slightly deflected (see Exercise 1). These two different approaches to solving the problem of sustainability are essentially completely equivalent.

If a stone is slightly deviated from a stable equilibrium position and is not kept in a new position, then it begins to return back, “leaks” (by inertia) the equilibrium position, returns to it again, etc. That is, the stone will oscillate around the stable equilibrium position.

If a small deviation of the body from the equilibrium position does not change the height of the body's center of gravity above the fulcrum. This situation is called indifferent equilibrium. For example, a single-pod ball on a horizontal plane is in indifferent equilibrium.

Now we understand what a “swaying rock” is: it is a vertical building stone with a low center of gravity or a large radius of curvature at the base. The deflection of the stone (albeit within certain limits; see exercise 2) leads to its oscillation around the equilibrium position. The swinging rock is a pendulum stone.

Of course, the manual cannot be applied for the purpose of building a stone like that. The point is not only that the swaying rock has a large mass and in order to impart acceleration to it, a very large force must be applied. Due to the deformation of the support under the influence of the weight of the stone, reaction forces may arise that prevent the rock from deflecting from the vertical. And yet, a rocking rock essentially doesn't require something like this to be built in?

In conclusion, let's look at examples that do not require a trip to the mountains, they can be studied on a table, but by their nature they are also the same as a rocking rock.
