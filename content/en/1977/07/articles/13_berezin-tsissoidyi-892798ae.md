Cissondas

Cissonda Dioclescus *) is perhaps the most ancient of the remarkable curves. It can be defined like this. Consider an arbitrary circle (O, r) and a point P on it. Let PQ be the diameter of the circle, and l be the tangent to it passing through the point Q (Fig. 1). Let us choose an arbitrary point L ∈ l. Let [PL] ∩ (O, r) = N. Let us construct a point M on the segment PL for which |PM| = |LN|. The set of points M, constructed for all possible points L ∈ l, is the Dioclescus cissonde.

The position of point M can be described by a pair of numbers ⟨φ, ρ⟩, where φ = LPQ, ρ = |PM|. The pair ⟨φ, ρ⟩ is called polar coordinates **(points M, φ is its polar angle, ρ is its polar radius). Point P is called a pole (analogous to the origin of coordinates), vector PM is called the radius vector of the cissonde. In the Dioclescus cissonde - π/2 < φ < π/2.

You can write an equation relating the polar coordinates of an arbitrary point on the Dioclescus cissonde:

ρ = 2r/cos φ - 2r cos φ is the polar equation of the cissonde (derive it).

Let us assume that PQ is the symmetry axis of the Dioclescus cissonde. As φ tends to π/2 (or κ - π/2), point M tends to L; Thus, we assume that l is an asymptote of the cissonde.

*) Dioclescus lived somewhere between 250 and 100 AD. BC h.

** The polar coordinate system (to describe various spirals) was apparently first introduced in the 70s of the 17th century by I. Newton.

At the end of the 17th century, the concept of a cissonde was generalized: let f₁, f₂ be two arbitrary (flat) curves, P an arbitrary point: Otherwise, the construction is arbitrary in the old way: we take an arbitrary point L ∈ f₂ and plot on PL the segment PM for which |PM| = |LN| (here N = f₁ ∩ |PL|). The resulting set of point M is called a cissonde.

Report, for example, what cissonde will be obtained if f₁ = (O, u), f₂ = (O, s) and P = O.

The second page of the cover shows a family of cissondes obtained with a fixed curve f₁ = (O, r) and a pole P ∈ (O, r), if f₂ changes: what f₂ is obtained by parallel translation 2c of the tangent l (“red line” on the cover). (Write the polar equation of these cissondes—see Fig. 2. It will, of course, depend on the parameter |c|).

The cissonda Dioclescus (“red cissonda” on the cover) has a point at the pole. If the straight line f₂ is located outside the circle f₁, then at the point most distant from f₂ the cissonde has a rounding. If f₂ ∩ f₁ ≠ ∅, then the cissonde has a loop at the pole. In particular, if f₂ passes through the center of the circle f₁, then the cissonde turns into a strophonde («Kvant», 1977, No. 2).

Let us present a method for constructing other cissondes (from the family of cissondes shown on the cover), which belongs to Newoton. Let us define a point R and a straight line l in the plane. Take an arbitrary triangle ABC (B = 90°), for which |AB| = |RT| (T is the base of the perpendicular dropped from R to l). We will mix the square (Fig. 3) so that the vertex A slides along l, and the roll BC “passes” through R. Then the fixed point M of the leg AB falls along the cissonde (in particular, the point B is an arc. strophonds).

How to show this? Let us draw through M a straight line parallel to AR. Let this line intersect with RT at point Pₘ. From the constructibility of the triangles ABR and ATR it follows that the point Pₘ occupies a position on the segment RT that does not depend on the position of the triangle ABC. By calculating |MPₘ|, we can verify that point M describes a cissonde. We present this to the reader (we recommend using the polar equation).

Using Newoton's construction, it is easy to determine the position of the tangent of the cissondes at point M. Let us draw a perpendicular to BC through point R and a perpendicular to l through A. Let these perpendiculars intersect at point H (Fig. 3). Then the perpendicular to HM drawn at point M will be the desired tangent.
