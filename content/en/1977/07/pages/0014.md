⟦folio 12⟧

(1) can be written as follows:

$$\frac{OQ}{r} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{R}{r}}.$$

Hence $OQ = \frac{r^2}{R + r}$. Since $CQ = r - OQ = \frac{Rr}{R + r}$, the condition for stable equilibrium of the stone (i.e., condition $CP < CQ$) is written as the inequality

$$CP < \frac{Rr}{R + r}$$

(2) If the surface has a wavy shape with radius $R$ (the shape of the inner surface of a sphere of radius $R$), then the condition for stable equilibrium of the stone on the support looks like this:

$$CP < \frac{Rr}{R - r}$$

(CP is still the distance from the point of contact to the center of gravity in the equilibrium position). Try to derive this formula yourself.

Let us now note the following important circumstance. Let us assume that the balance of the stone on the support is stable. When the stone deviates from the equilibrium position, a moment of force will arise that prevails over this deviation: a shoulder appears in the gravitational force relative to the new fulcrum (see Fig. 2). To keep the stone motionless in a new position, it is necessary to apply an external force such that its moment relative to the new fulcrum is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the moment of gravity. (The magnitude and direction of this force are determined by the conditions (*)). This means that even for a slight deviation of the body from a stable equilibrium position, it is necessary to do work against the force of gravity. This work goes to increase the potential energy of the body. This means that in a position of stable equilibrium, the potential energy of the body has a minimum value, or, what is the same, the center of gravity of the body occupies the minimum position. Therefore, conditions (2) can be derived differently by looking at what happens to the center of gravity of the stone when it is slightly deflected (see Exercise 1). These two different approaches to solving the problem of sustainability are essentially completely equivalent.

If a stone is slightly deviated from a stable equilibrium position and is not kept in a new position, then it begins to return back, “leaks” (by inertia) the equilibrium position, returns to it again, etc. That is, the stone will oscillate around the stable equilibrium position.

If a small deviation of the body from the equilibrium position does not change the height of the body's center of gravity above the fulcrum. This situation is called indifferent equilibrium. For example, a single-pod ball on a horizontal plane is in indifferent equilibrium.

Now we understand what a “swaying rock” is: it is a vertical building stone with a low center of gravity or a large radius of curvature at the base. The deflection of the stone (albeit within certain limits; see exercise 2) leads to its oscillation around the equilibrium position. The swinging rock is a pendulum stone.

Of course, the manual cannot be applied for the purpose of building a stone like that. The point is not only that the swaying rock has a large mass and in order to impart acceleration to it, a very large force must be applied. Due to the deformation of the support under the influence of the weight of the stone, reaction forces may arise that prevent the rock from deflecting from the vertical. And yet, a rocking rock essentially doesn't require something like this to be built in?

In conclusion, let's look at examples that do not require a trip to the mountains, they can be studied on a table, but by their nature they are also the same as a rocking rock.
