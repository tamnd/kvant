Rocking Rock

“...It must be said that in these places it is not uncommon to come across the so-called “swinging rock” - a very beloved phenomenon, the essence of which is that a separate piece of rock, from time immemorial, acquires stability of equilibrium. It usually stands on a stone platform and, if you swing it, it, like an insert, returns to its original position. Such rocks sometimes weigh thousands of tons, but are obedient to the movement of the hand of a person of average strength. Such a rock cannot fall, unless, of course, it is blown up..."

These are lines from Alexander Green’s story “The Rocking Rock” - a sad story about a poor hunter who was offered, for three million, to overturn a huge pillar swinging near its equilibrium position. The hunter, despite all his efforts, failed to cope with the task and went crazy (but never left his side and kept trying to push the stone).

Let's try to understand why the “rocking rock” is stable.
We know that in order to be in an equilibrium position, two conditions must be met:

vector sum of all forces,
acting on the body
equal to zero; (*)
algebraic sum
moments of all strength
relative to an arbitrary point
is equal to zero. (**)

Not every equilibrium position is stable. For example, a needle, on which only the force of gravity and the reaction of the support acts, does not stand freely on a smooth table, although if the needle is placed strictly vertically, conditions (*) and (**) will be satisfied. But at the slightest deviation of the needle from the vertical, moments of force arise that overturn the needle. At the same time, the brick stands steadily on any edge. And no matter how we reduce the brick, maintaining its shape, it will still stand stably on the table. But the same brick is very difficult to “balance,” for example, on a soccer ball (we will keep the ball motionless).
