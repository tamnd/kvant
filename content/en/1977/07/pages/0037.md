Let us prove our statement by indication of the number of areas. If there are two squares, then we will leave two streets connecting the first square with the second and second, and close the rest.

Let there be more than two areas. Let's assume that we managed to indicate a closed route along the city streets, starting and ending in square A and passing through at least three squares. (How circular routes are built is shown in figure 2: we take a route connecting area A with some area B, then a route connecting B with A, and select the route we need - the red “ring” on figure.) Let's change the city plan: we'll declare this entire circular route a new square, and we'll declare all the streets that are not included in our circular route, keeping the direction of movement on them. At the same time, we may have streets starting and ending on a new square. Let’s check that the resulting “city” satisfies all the conditions of the problem. To do this, you need to make sure that at least three streets open onto the new square. This ensures our assumption that there are at least three squares in the circular route (see Fig. 3). It is no less clear that it is still possible to drive from any square to any other - our “redevelopment” could only shorten the corresponding routes. Thus, we have obtained a city that satisfies the conditions of the problem and has fewer areas. According to the indication assumption, it contains an “extra” street. It corresponds to a certain street in the old city. We will ban traffic on it. It is easy to verify that travel is still possible. Indeed, consider squares $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$ and a route connecting $\Pi_1$ to $\Pi_2$ in a new city and not passing through a closed street. Cross this route to the old town. If this route did not go through the new square, then it connects $\Pi_1$ to $\Pi_2$. Otherwise, it will break into two parts: before the “ring” and after the “ring”. By adding the corresponding part of the ring to it, we get the desired route connecting $\Pi_1$ with $\Pi_2$ in the old city and without passing through a closed street.

It remains to consider cities that do not have long circular routes. Let us take some area A of such a city. If you can drive from it to square B along one street, then from square B you can drive to it along one street - otherwise the distance would be to indicate a larger ring. Let us now proceed as follows: consider area A and the adjacent area B1. Area B1 is connected to area A (see Fig. 4). In addition, there is another street facing A. Let its other end go to square B2. But then there is another street connecting A and B2 - with the opposite direction of travel. Therefore, we will declare ring $AB_1A$ a new square, the conditions of the problem will be met: at least three streets will open onto the new square.
