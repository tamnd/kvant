Place a glass tube with a connection with a diameter of about 1 mm at the end of a rubber hose, the other end of which is lowered into a vessel with water located at a height of 0.5-1 m above the table surface. Place the glass tube on the table at an arbitrary angle to the horizontal. Using your mouth, pull the air out of the hose and get a stream.

In experiments with a jet self-generator, we recommended connecting the hose directly to the water tap. Here, this method of producing a jet is undesirable because various sounds travel well through the iron pipes of the water supply system and, if they somehow affect the jet, they will interfere with what is being observed.

The stream coming out of the glass tube is inexpensive. Near the answer, it is solid, then it becomes cloudy and already in the upper part of the trajectory it breaks into completely isolated drops, which fall so quickly that they create an error for the whole again of continuous jets (Fig. 3, a). Now place a speaker on the table next to the glass tube and connect it to the sound generator. Turn on the power and gradually change the sound frequency with a variable resistor. You will notice that at a certain frequency the solid (transparent) section of the jet sharply contracts, and the sheaf of jets merges, forming one seemingly perfect continuous jet (Fig. 3, b)! This is so amazing that everyone who sees the described phenomenon for the first time comes to study it. It turns out that the water jet is extremely sensitive to sound. You can place the speaker anywhere on the table, put it on another table - all the same, if the frequency of the sound is similar, the jet responds to the sound. By changing the frequency of sound, it is possible to obtain from a sheaf of jets two jets that are approximately equal in thickness (Fig. 3, c), and three jets, and two jets, one of which is much thinner than the other and is somehow to the side (Fig. 3, d), etc.

If you do not dare to assemble a sound generator for experiments, you can replace it with any other sound source, the frequency of which can be changed. The most accessible of them is your voice. Above only means that the sound at which the jet reacts should be quite low. It is best if its frequency lies in the range of 200-500 Hz. It is not necessary to shout loudly to the stream: it is quite obedient if you reduce the stream in a language that it understands. Another possible sound source is, for example, a guitar. Bring it to the table surface and pluck the strings. You can easily select a sound of a frequency at which the smeared stream flows together. By taking simultaneous sounds of slightly different frequencies, you can get sound beats, and then the jet will move in time with the changes in sound.
