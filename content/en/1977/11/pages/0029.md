Achievements of Soviet physicists

A brief chronicle of outstanding Soviet physicists includes mainly “name” discoveries. Of course, not every major discovery necessarily subsequently acquires the names of the scientists who made it. Therefore, the list given here does not cover even the largest discoveries made by our scientists in the field of physics. A detailed account of the achievements of Soviet physicists, published for the 50th anniversary of the October Revolution, was hardly reduced to two large volumes. But we hope that even a short list of “name” discoveries will help our readers feel how great the contribution of Soviet scientists to the treasury of world science is. The chronicle was prepared by V. A. Leshkovtsev.

It is known that the strength of a solid body, determined experimentally, is many times less than its value calculated theoretically. Academician Abram Fedorovich Yuffe was the first to reveal the reason for this difference. It turns out that various defects in the crystal structure, for example, microsopper cracks on the surface of the crystals, are to blame for the premature destruction of materials. By immersing a crystal of rock salt in water and dissolving the surface layer, Yuffe achieves an increase in strength hundreds of times. The strengthening of crystals during dissolution of the surface layer is called the “Juffe effect.”

In 1919-1920, academician Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam theoretically showed that microsopper inhomogeneities in the density of matter, resulting from thermal vibrations of molecules, change the spectrum of scattered light, adding new spectral lines to it, which are very difficult to observe. (These lines were first discovered by corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Evgeny Fedorovich Gross.) The phenomenon as a result of which they arise was called the “Mandel effect.”

In 1922, Professor Aleksandrovich Friedman obtained two new solutions to the basic equation of the general theory of the relative length of the Universe. Both of them described not a Stationary Universe, like Einstein’s, but a Universe that is continuously evolving. Subsequently, “Friedmann’s model of the expanding Universe” received experimental confirmation and is now generally accepted.

Already in 1926, he generalized the basic equation of this theory to the case of the presence of a magnetic field and obtained the relativistic equation of quantum mechanics, known to physicists as the “Fock-Klein equation.” Subsequently, Academician V. A. Fok, simultaneously with the English physicist Hartree, created a method for calculating the spectra of atoms containing a large number of electrons, and even the spectra of complex molecules. This method went down in the history of physics under the name of the Fock-Hartree method. Before the creation of this method, the calculation of complex atoms was a task inaccessible to quantum mechanics.
