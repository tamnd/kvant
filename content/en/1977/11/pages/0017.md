Calculations have shown that convection currents should be carried out in Gelini II with the necessary ease, without friction. It could be assumed that Gelini II is a superfluid liquid, that is, a liquid that has no liquid.

To test this assumption, Pyotr Leonidovich conducted such an experiment in 1938. He threaded Gelini II through a gap between two flat, polished glass plates pressed against each other. The heads were polished so carefully that the gap between them was no more than half a micron. And through such a gap, under the influence of its own gravity, gelini II flowed at a speed that is only possible in the almost complete absence of anger!

This is how the phenomenon of superfluidity in Gelini II was discovered.

Another mystery remained unresolved, related to the change in the thickness of helinium II. The fact is that arrogance can be defined in two ways. One of them is a change in the speed of liquid flow from a capillary vessel (or through a narrow slit, as was the case in the experiments of P. L. Kapitsa). For a given pressure difference at the ends of the capillary, the less liquid it is, the greater the amount of liquid flowing through it in a given time. The second way to determine hardness is to study the damping of circular vibrations of a solid in a liquid. A thin disk is lowered into the liquid on a thread so that its plane is horizontal, and its own circular oscillations around an axis passing through the thread are observed. Naturally, the higher the liquid thickness, the faster the vibrations die out. For all liquids, the thickness values ​​modified by these two methods were the same. But for Gelini II these values ​​were different. The first method gave a vanishingly small value of density, demonstrating the superfluidity of helium II, and the second led to small, but completely changed values.

L. D. Landdu

The answer to all these questions is for the hydrodynamic theory of superfluidity, created in 1941 by academician Lev Davidovich Landdu. He showed that the general classical approach to explaining the properties of matter at columnar temperatures is incorrect. Landd managed to develop the quantum theory of He II superfluidity. She explained the results of Kapitsa's experiments and predicted a number of new phenomena, which were soon discovered by experiments.

The superfluid nature of liquid helium has not yet found wide application. But the theory of superfluidity created by Landdu had a great influence on the development of theoretical physics. It was the key to creating the theory of another remarkable phenomenon that occurs at low temperatures - superconductivity.
