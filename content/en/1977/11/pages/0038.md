A number of results were obtained, the strongest of which was the result of the young Soviet mathematician A. G. Vitushkin, which would indirectly confirm D. Hilbert’s hypothesis.

All the more unexpected was the result obtained in 1954 by academician A. N. Kolmogorov and V. I. Arnold, then a student at the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics at Moscow University, later a professor and Lenin Prize laureate. They tested Hilbert's conjecture by showing that every nonprime function of three variables is the sum of nine functions, each of which is a one-time superposition of functions of two variables.

Hilbert's second problem was to prove the consistency of arithmetic. At the same time, Hilbert greatly limited the means of proof to the framework of the approach that later received the name “Hilbert’s feintism.” Hilbert himself, together with his students, spent a lot of effort on solving this problem and even at one time felt confident that he had obtained such a proof. However, the results of K. Gödel in 1931 put an end to this confidence - they found out that such a proof within the framework of “Hilbert’s feintism” cannot in principle be obtained. The search began for such a proof without the need for restrictions imposed by “Hilbert’s feintism.” However, the most remarkable evidence of this kind was obtained in 1943 by Academician P. S. Novikov.

The essence of the tenth problem, finally solved by Yu. V. Matiyasevich, is as follows: an arbitrary Diophantine equation with integer rational coefficients is given; indicate a method by which it is possible, after a finite number of operations, to determine whether a given equation is solvable in rational integers.

For example, integer solutions to the equation

$$x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0,$$

arising in connection with the meaning of the Pythagorean theorem are the sets ($\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}$)

$$x = \alpha^2 - 1, y = 2\alpha, z = \alpha^2 + 1.$$

These formulas were attributed to Pythagoras, but they do not cover all integer solutions (Pythagorean points) of a given equation. General solution

$$x = \xi^2 - \eta^2, y = 2\xi\eta, z = \xi^2 + \eta^2$$

($\xi, \eta \in \mathbb{Z}$) we find in Diophantus, although it was undoubtedly known long before him. In the work of Diophantus, the theory of such equations received significant development, so they were subsequently named after him.

The study of Diophantine equations, or Diophantine analysis, as they say today, was carried out by P. Fermat, L. Euler, J. Lagrange and C. F. Gauss. The last two attempts to solve solved second degree equations with two unknowns. One can name a number of other outstanding mathematicians of the 19th century who spent their time in Diophantine analysis, but by 1900, the time Hilbert presented this problem, successes in solving higher-order equations were rather modest. By posing the above problem, Hilbert intends to solve, apparently, to attract the attention of mathematicians to the development of the problem of Diophantine analysis.
