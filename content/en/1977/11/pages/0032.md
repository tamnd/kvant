Some metals and alloys, when cooled to very low temperatures, completely lose resistance to the current flowing through them. This is called superconductivity. In 1950, academicians Vitaly Lazarevich Ginzburg and Lev Davidovich Landdu built a theory of superconductivity, taking into account quantum efficiencies in superconductors. The user of the theory, corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences Aleksey Alekseevich Abrikovov and Lev Petrovich Gorkov created a theory of superconducting alloys, which is of great importance. This theory is known throughout the world as the “GLAG theory” (Ginsburg - Landdu - Abrikov - Gorkov).

The Microsoppech theory of superconductivity, which explains the mechanism of this mysterious phenomenon, was created in 1957 by academician Nikolai Nikolaevich Bogolyubov and American physicists Bardeen, Cooper and Schriefer. It is called the “Bogolyubov-Bardeen-Cooper-Schriefer theory of superconductivity.”

In the early 50s, academicians Aleksandr Mikhailovich Prosorov and Nikolai Gennadievich Basov developed the foundations of a new field of physics—quantum electrical peaking—and created the first quantum molecular generator using ammonia molecules. For these outstanding works, in 1964 they, together with the American physicist Townes, were awarded the Nobel Prize.

Complex molecules of the organization of substances have spectra consisting of wide continuous spectral bands. In 1952, Professor Eduard Vladimirovich Shpolsky showed that by dissolving organizational substances in specially selected solvents and cooling the resulting solutions to very low temperatures, it is possible to obtain absorption spectra consisting of very low spectral lines that are uniquely related to the structure and properties of the molecules. Semiconductors, like other substances, can absorb light whose wavelengths fall within certain limits. They are transparent for light with long wavelengths. Thus, there is a limit to the wavelength at which a semiconductor will absorb light. In 1958, academician Leonid Veniaminovich Kelysh theoretically predicted that under the influence of an external electric field this boundary should shift towards longer wavelengths. A similar prediction was made by the German physicist Franz. In 1960, Professor Viktor Sergeevich Vavilov received experimental confirmation of this phenomenon, now known as the “Kelysh-Franz effect.”

In 1966, the most powerful charged particle accelerator for that time, the synchrophasotron, came into operation in the USSR, accelerating protons to an energy of 70 billion electron volts. It was built down the city of Serpukhov. With the help of this accelerator, it was shown that the probability of collision of charged particles at an energy greater than 20 billion electron volts does not decrease and does not remain constant with increasing energy, as the theory predicted, but continues to decay. This necessary phenomenon was called the “Serpukhov effect”.
