⟦folio 28⟧

Samuilovich Landsberg, while studying the scattering of light in quartz crystals, discovered additional spectral lines in the spectrum of scattered light that did not belong to the light incident on the crystal. These spectral lines arise from the interaction of incident light with the individual molecules that form the crystal lattice. Somewhat later, a similar phenomenon was discovered by the Indian physicist Raman while studying the scattering of light in liquid benzene. It is called the “Mandelshtam-Landsberg-Raman effect” or “raman scattering of light.” Raman scattering has become a powerful tool for studying the structure of molecules.

In 1928-1929, Academician Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa, using the original installation he designed to create super-strong magnetic fields, established that in such fields the change in the electrical resistance of metals does not grow in proportion to the square of the magnetic induction, as expected based on the then existing theories, but is a linear function of magnetic induction. This unexpected phenomenon, which could not be explained for about 30 years, is called “Kapitsa’s law.”

In the early 30s, Professor Lev Vasilyevich Shubnikov, working in the laboratory of the Dutch physicist De Haas, discovered that the electrical resistance of a height placed in a magnetic field, as the magnetic induction of the field increases, either returns or kills (as they say, the resistance of a height oscillates). This “Shubnikov-De-Haas effect” is widely used to study the energy spectrum of electrons in a metal.

In 1930, academician Lev Davidovich Landu theoretically showed that free electrons in a metal under the influence of an external magnetic field can only move along certain (as physicists say - quantum) trajectories. This means that special energy levels arise in a magnetic field, called “Landou levels”.

From the same theory it followed that the action of a magnetic field on free electrons in a metal leads to the appearance of an additional magnetic moment directed in the direction opposite to the external magnetic field. Such a moment is called diamagnetic, and the described phenomenon is called “Landou diamagnetism.” Note that the same theory also implied the Shubnikov-De-Haas effect.

In 1962, L. D. Land was awarded the Nobel Prize for his outstanding achievements in the field of theoretical physics.

Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Yakov Ilyich Frenkel was the first in 1931 to introduce a quasi-particle, which is now called the “Frenkel expeton,” into use among physicists. He drew attention to the fact that in a crystal lattice there can be a special influence of the state of electrons. Having arisen in any cell of the crystal, this emergence moves throughout the crystal, like a free shaped particle. The existence of extons was first proven in the experiments of Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Evgeniy Fedorovich Gross. Expetons play a large role in solid state physics and modern chemistry.

In 1933, academician Igor Evgenievich Tamp theoretically predicted the existence of free energy states (“loves”) on the surface of crystals. They entered into a science called "Tampa Surface Levels."
