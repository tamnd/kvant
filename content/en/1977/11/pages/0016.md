⟦folio 14⟧

Of great interest was the study of the properties of various substances under conditions where strong magnetic fields are combined with low temperatures. And Kapitsa began to consider the question of ways to obtain low temperatures close to absolute zero. It is known that the lowest temperature liquid is liquid helium. At normal pressure, the boiling point of liquid helium is 4.2 K.

Liquid helium was first obtained by the Dutch scientist Kamerlingh Onnes in 1908. The installation he built in his laboratory in Leiden produced small amounts of liquid helium. Such installations were very expensive. P. L. Kapitsa developed a more productive and economical installation, in which he could obtain a fairly large amount of liquid helium.

By that time, physicists became interested in the properties of liquid helium itself. The fact is that liquid helium is the only substance that, as they say, undergoes a phase transformation while in a liquid state: at a temperature of 2.19 K its physical properties change sharply. Above this temperature, helium behaves like an animated liquid, and at $T \leq 2,19$ K it has a number of completely unusual properties: its thermal conductivity increases sharply, and its toughness drops sharply. It is customary to call helium at temperatures above 2.19 K helium I, and at $T \leq 2,19$ K - helium II.

Helium II was first discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes. Then Keez studied it at the Leiden laboratory. He discovered that the thermal conductivity of helium II is many times greater than that of the most thermally conductive metals. Therefore, Kees called helium II a superconducting substance.
