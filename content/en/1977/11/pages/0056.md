Apparently, problem 137 means that the “major diagonal section” is the diagonal section that passes through the major diagonal.

In this case, solving the problem is not difficult. The height of the paramid is the same as the height of the triangle, which is $\frac{c}{2}$, and the length of the side of the hexagon is also $\frac{c}{2}$. Hence the area of ​​the base of the paramid is $\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^2$, and the volume is $\frac{c^3\sqrt{3}}{16}$. This is the answer given in the textbook.

Well, what if you understand the size of the triangle and its area? And in this case the problem is also not too difficult to solve. First, let us replace that the diagonal sections of the paral paramid are triangles with two congested sides, which are the lateral edges of the paramid. If we denote the length of the side edge by $l$, then the area of ​​the triangle under consideration is equal to $\frac{1}{2}l^2\sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the indicated sides, and that of the sections is large, which has a large sine angle.

Secondly, let us replace that if the indicating angle is a right angle, then from what was said above it follows that such a section is the most important.

The case in which the largest section passes through the largest diagonal has already been discussed. It remains to consider the case when it passes through another diagonal. In this case, the length of this diagonal is $c$, the length of the larger diagonal is $\frac{2c\sqrt{3}}{3}$, and the sides of the hexagon are $\frac{c\sqrt{3}}{3}$. The length of the lateral edge is $\frac{c\sqrt{2}}{2}$, and the height of the paramid is $h = \sqrt{\frac{c^2}{2} - \frac{c^2}{3}} = c\sqrt{\frac{1}{6}}$. The final result is that the area of ​​the base is $\frac{c^2\sqrt{3}}{2}$, and the volume of the paramid is $\frac{c^3\sqrt{2}}{12}$.

What is the purpose of this note? Of course, this is not to point out an unsuccessful problem in the textbook, but to once give you the opportunity to be convinced that mathematics is a science in which all concepts and relationships between them must be strictly defined (of course, with the exception of basic, undefined concepts, such as a point, a plane, a straight line).

I would also like to note the fact that the same term can play different roles in different books. Naturally, according to the book or article, this term is strictly defined.
