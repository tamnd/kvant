Diophantine analysis: equation $P(x, y) = c$, where $c$ is an integer, $a$ $P(x, y)$ is an irreducible polynomial in which all terms have the same degree, not less than three, can have only finite numerical solutions (a polynomial is called irreducible if it does not decompose into the product of two polynomials with integer coefficients).

But neither Thue's method nor subsequent methods provided an algorithm for finding solutions. The results obtained for Diophantine equations of degree above two (the work of the Soviet mathematician B. N. Delone, the German mathematician K. Siegel, etc.) related to the search for algorithms for individual classes of Diophantine equations. And although success in the search for a general method for arbitrary Diophantine equations was extremely insignificant, mathematicians nevertheless believed that sooner or later it would be found.

However, in the mid-thirties, a clear concept of “algorithm” was formed in mathematics and the idea of ​​problems for which there is no algorithm that specifies their solutions continues—the algorithm of unsolvable problems. Examples of such problems were given in the 30-40s by A. Church, A. Tyuring, E. Post, A. A. Markov. In 1952, P. S. Novikov proved the algorithmic undecidability of one important problem in group theory - the problem of action points of words. Examples of algorithmic unsolvable problems, on the one hand, and difficulties associated with attempts to find the original algorithm, on the other, gave rise to doubt: maybe an algorithm that solves ten problems does not exist at all? The results of American mathematicians (M. Davis and others), obtained in the 50-60s, gave indirect confirmation of this assumption. They were able to show that for a certain more general class of equations, the so-called “exponential-Diophantine equations with integer coefficients,” such an algorithm does not exist. But this is for exponential-Diophantine equations, and for a more special case - Diophantine equations - such an algorithm could exist. So, Yu. V. Matiyasevich showed that for the class of such equations there is no required algorithm.

We have talked here only about the most striking achievements of Soviet mathematicians in solving Hilbert's problems. A large and detailed story is the topic of not an article, but a book. Such a book, telling about the achievements of scientists around the world in solving Hilbert's problems, written by major specialists in the relevant field of mathematics, was published in our country in 1969. True, when talking about this book, it should be borne in mind that, firstly, reading the book requires significant mathematical preparation - even knowledge of a university course is not enough to understand its individual sections, and, secondly, during the time that has elapsed since its publication, the state of affairs with the study of Gilbert's problems has changed greatly. Mathematics is now in a stage of rapid development; it constantly poses new and new problems for scientists. And many old ones (including some of Gilbert's problems) have not yet found their solution. We can be sure that Soviet mathematicians will continue to delight us with remarkable successes in solving the most important mathematics.
