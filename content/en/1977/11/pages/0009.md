toelectrics (the so-called dielectrics with the same anomalous properties as Rochelle salt) behave in many ways similar to the behavior of ferromagnets. For example, the dependence of the electric induction (the so-called electric field of the internal pole) in ferroelectrics on the magnitude of the external electric field is similar to the dependence of the magnetic induction in ferromagnets on the external magnetic field. In crystalline ferroelectrics, as well as in ferromagnets, sharp anisotropic properties are observed: their electrical characteristics significantly depend on the orientation of the external field relative to various axes of the crystal. When heating ferroelectrics, it is observed that when they pass the “critical” temperature (for Rochelle salt, as we have already said, this is ~30°C), their electrical properties change abruptly - the anomaly of dielectric capacity disappears and in the future they behave like general dielectrics. This phenomenon is completely similar to the behavior of ferromagnets when they pass the Curie point - when they go from a ferromagnetic state to a parametric state. Investigating the transition of ferroelectrics through the Curie point (as the critical temperature for ferroelectrics is called after the anomaly), Kurchatov showed that, as in the case of ferromagnets, this transition is accompanied by the release of heat. This effect is called the electrocaloric effect (the anomaly effect for ferromagnets is called magnetocaloric). The similarity between ferroelectric and ferromagnetism is so great that foreign scientists call the phenomenon ferroelectricity.

I.V. Kurchatov collected the results of his research in a separate book, which was recognized everywhere as a fundamental study of ferroelectricity.
