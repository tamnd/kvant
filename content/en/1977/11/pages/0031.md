⟦folio 29⟧

In 1934, Academician Isaac Konstantinovich Kikoni discovered the photoelectromagnetic effect, showing in experiments that when a semiconductor is illuminated in a magnetic field, an electromotive force arises in it. Light quanta create a current carrier - free electrons and holes, which diffuse along the direction of incidence of light, and the magnetic field separates them in opposite directions. This phenomenon has long been called the “Kikon effect.”

Academician Pavel Alekseevich Cherenkov, together with his teacher, Academician Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov, discovered the “superluminal electron” effect in 1934. It turned out that in liquid and solid media electrons can move at a speed greater than the speed of light in these media (but, of course, not greater than the speed of light in emptiness!). Electrons can burn the light emitted by the immi. This effect, called the “Cherenkov effect,” has found a number of valuable practical applications, in particular for the detection of fast charged particles (the so-called Cherenkov particles). The theory explaining the Cherenkov effect was created by academicians Igor Evgenievich Tam and Ilya Mikhailovich Frank. For the discovery and explanation of this phenomenon, P. A. Cherenkov, I. E. Tam and I. M. Frank were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1958.

In 1936, Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Yakov Ilyich Frenkel developed the electrocapillary theory of atomic nuclei, in which nuclei are considered as drops of a charged liquid. Following Frenkel, the same ideas were developed by the Danish physicist Niels Bohr. They went down in the history of physics under the name “droplet model of the Bohr-Frenkel nucleus.”

Another phenomenon discovered by him in 1938 is associated with the name of Academician Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsya. While studying the properties of the liquid gel, he discovered that at temperatures below 2.19 K the gel flows through the narrow mouth with virtually no hesitation. This phenomenon was later called superfluidity. The theory of this phenomenon is created by the “Vlasov equation”.

Professor of Moscow University Anatoly Aleksaidrovich Blasov in 1938 was the first to derive an equation that describes the behavior of rarefied plasma in which no new generation of charged particles occurs. It entered science under the name “Vlasov equation”.

In 1944, academician Evgeniy Konstantinovich Zavoisky discovered electronic parametric resonance, marking the beginning of a new field of solid state physics. The essence of this phenomenon is that substances located in a constant magnetic field selectively absorb the energy of a high-frequency magnetic field perpendicular to it.

In 1944, academician Vladimir Iosifovich Veksler discovered the principle of aptophasization of particles accelerated in cyclic accelerators that bears his name. The work of all modern cyclic accelerators is based on it. Somewhat later, independently of Wexer, the same principle was discovered by the American physicist McMinlan. For this discovery they were awarded an international scientific prize.

In 1947, academician Lev Davidovich Landu showed that even in the absence of new plasma particles propagating in it, electromagnetic waves experienced attenuation. This phenomenon plays a significant role in plasma enhancement. Physicists around the world call it “Land damping.”
