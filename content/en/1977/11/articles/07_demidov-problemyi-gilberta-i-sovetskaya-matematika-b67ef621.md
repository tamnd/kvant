Gilberti's problems and Soviet mathematics

The number one event at the International Congress of Mathematicians, held in August 1970 in the world famous French resort town of Niszcz, was the famous solution of Gilberti's decimal problem. The hero of the day was the twenty-year-old Soviet mathematician Yu. V. Matiyase in Chi, whose report was included in the agenda of the congress above and beyond the compiled program. Thus ended the 70-year history of this problem by Gilberti.

The solution to each of Gilberti's twenty-year problems, even each partial success in solving them, is accepted by the entire mathematical world as a major mathematical achievement. What is the secret of such popularity of Gilbert's problems, the significance that their solution gives? After all, the number of unsolved problems posed in mathematics

Russian literature is enormous, and only a few of them (such as Fermat’s problema) become widely known. And here is not one, but twenty-three problems, some of which are not just problems in the narrow sense of the word, but plans for the development of entire mathematical directions! To answer this question, we will have to step back to 1900 and find ourselves in Paris at the meeting of the Second International Congress of Mathematicians, at which on August 8 D. Hilbert gave a report “Mathematical Problems”.

David Gilbert was born in 1862 in Königsberg. In this city he graduated from school and university, here he began his path in science, where he was successful from the very first steps. The results he obtained became widely known. In 1895, the famous geometer F. Klein invited him to Gottingen to take the position of ordinary professor at the local university, with which all of Gilberti’s further activities were connected. By the time of his speech at the 1900 congress, Gilberti had become famous for his remarkable results on the theory of invariants and the theory of algebraic numbers. In 1899, his work “Foundations of Geometry” was published, which constituted an era in the foundations of mathematics. These works fully revealed the amazing versatility and generalizing powers of his talent, allowing him to easily navigate the most diverse areas of mathematics, the necessary strength of his mathematical intuition. Gilberti, along with A. Poincaré, is supported by the words of the greatest mathematician of his time. Understood that's why he's exceptional

the interest with which the congress participants greeted his report with the promising title “Mathematical Problems.”

“Which of us would not want to lift the veil behind which our future is hidden, in order to penetrate at least with one glance into the upcoming conditions of our knowledge and the secrets of its development to the next degree? What new methods and new facts will be discovered in the new state on the wide and rich field of mathematical thought? - with these words D. Gilbert began his report. Then he continued. — History teaches that the development of science proceeds continuously. We know that every age has its own problems, which the subsequent era either solves or is pushed aside as free in order to replace them with new ones. In order to imagine the possible nature of the development of mathematics in the near future, we must work through in our imagination the questions that still remain open, survey the problems that modern science poses, and the solutions of which we expect from the future. Such an overview of the problem seems to me today, at the turn of the new century, to be especially timely.” And Hilbert offers the attention of listeners twenty-three problems from various areas of mathematics, “the study of which can significantly stimulate the further development of science.”

The first six problems of Hilbert's report relate to the foundation of various mathematical disciplines, the next nine - to more special questions of algebra, algebraic geometry and number theory, the remaining eight - to the theory of functions, differential equations and expressions and expressed calculus.

It should be noted that some of these problems were posed before Hilbert. Thus, the first on the list - the problem of contirum - was posed by G. Kant in 1878; questions related to the third problem were discussed by K. Gauss in his correspondence with Hermann. As for the questions that make up the content of the eight problems, one of them - the only one about the zeros of the zema function - was posed by B. Riemann in 1859, the other, called the only one about the Holbach zeros, back in 1742 in the latter’s letter to L. Euler, and finally, the 21st problem - the problem put forward by B. Riemann in 1857. The remaining problems, the author of which was Hilbert himself, constitute only part of the problems posed by him by that time. These circumstances support the special nature of the choice of problems contained in the document - here are only those most important, in Hilbert’s opinion, tasks that were then facing mathematics, reflection on which could help “to imagine the possibility of the development of mathematical knowledge in the near future.”

The further course of events showed that the choice of problem made by Hilbert was in the main: the development of ideas related to their content constituted a significant part of the mathematics of the 20th century. Many talented mathematicians from different countries of the world took part in solving these problems, including Hilbert himself and his many students. A remarkable place among them belongs to accounting mathematicians. At that time, Russia was not yet a powerful mathematical power like Germany or France, although it already had recognized mathematical schools and a number of outstanding mathematicians for the world, among them, among them, the leading mathematical genius - N. I. Lobachevsky, P. L. Chebyshev. However, the golden age of accounting mathematics was still to come. At the congress in Paris, the Russian delegation was relatively small - 9 people (compare: France - 90, Germany - 25) and made only one report “On the disappearance (we would say about zeros - S.D.) of the function H of several variables,” which was made by Kharkov professor M.A. Tikhomandrikiy.

In what follows, we will mainly adhere to the chronological principle.

The first work in Russia devoted to the development of Glober's problems was studied by V. F. Kagan in 1903, who significantly shortened and simplified M. Dehn's proof, which appeared shortly before, which gave a positive solution to the third Glober problem, relative to the degree of B. 1904. S. N. Bernishtein, a young scientist from Russia, later an academician, became famous for his outstanding results in the theory of differential equations, the theory of functions and the theory of probability, gave solution of the nineteenth Globert problem - a difficult problem in the theory of differential equations with partial arbitrary ones. This decision formed the content of Bernstein's doctoral dissertation, which he completed in Paris that same year. In his works of 1908-1909, he also obtained important results related to the twentieth problem, also related to the theory of partial differential equations.

Subsequent developments related to the development of these problems became one of the main ones for gaining worldwide fame for the Soviet school of the theory of differential equations, the origins of which we find in the work of V. A. Steklov and A. M. Lyapunov. Brilliant results on the nineteenth problem were obtained in 1937 by I. G. Petrovsky, later an academician, one of the largest specialists in the field of the theory of differential equations.

I. G. Petrovsky also deals with the sixteenth problem of Globert, which included a number of problems in the topology of algebraic curves and surfaces. In 1933, he solved one of these problems, and in a 1949 paper (together with O. A. Oleinik) he generalized his result.

In 1960, Leningrad mathematicians O. A. Ladyzhenskaya and N. N. Uraltseva obtained a “convergence” of results on the nineteenth and twentieth problems of Glober. Research on them turned out to be in the theory of connection with the calculus of variations, recognizing the development of which constitutes the content of Globert. The answer to this confession was the research of Soviet authors (S. N. Bernshtein, L. A. Lyusternik, L. G. Shnirelman, L. S. Pontryagin, O. A. Ladyzhenskaya, etc.).

The sixth problem of Globert is introduced as follows: “Mathematical presentation of the axioms of physics” (this also meant the theory of probability). It is interesting to note that for Globert, as for many mathematicians of his time, the theory of probability seemed to be a branch of physics (similar to mechanics), in which mathematical methods play a prominent role. This point of view was largely explained by the weak development of the axiomatic function of probability theory, which sometimes entailed a weak extraction of its actual mathematical content. The axiomatic construction of probability theory allows it to be rightly formulated by Globert in the form of one of the main problems facing mathematics.

This axiomatization was first given by S. N. Bernstein in a work that appeared in the year of the Great October Socialist Revolution. In 1936, the outstanding mathematician of our time A. N. Kolmogov, now an academician, proposed another axiomatic system of probability theory, based on the concepts of measure theory. This system, associated with a generally accepted one, marked the beginning of a new stage in the history of probability theory and appeared as the focus of the idea, on the one hand, of the Moscow school of the theory of functions of a real variable, founded by D. F. Egorov and N. N. Luzin, on the other hand, of the St. Petersburg school of P. L. Chebyshev, which is leading in the field of probability theory

They tested Hilbert's conjecture by showing that every nonprime function of three variables is the sum of nine functions, each of which is a one-time superposition of functions of two variables.

Hilbert's second problem was to prove the consistency of arithmetic. At the same time, Hilbert greatly limited the means of proof to the framework of the approach that later received the name “Hilbert’s feintism.” Hilbert himself, together with his students, spent a lot of effort on solving this problem and even at one time felt confident that he had obtained such a proof. However, the results of K. Gödel in 1931 put an end to this confidence - they found out that such a proof within the framework of “Hilbert’s feintism” cannot in principle be obtained. The search began for such a proof without the need for restrictions imposed by “Hilbert’s feintism.” However, the most remarkable evidence of this kind was obtained in 1943 by Academician P. S. Novikov.

The essence of the tenth problem, finally solved by Yu. V. Matiyasevich, is as follows: an arbitrary Diophantine equation with integer rational coefficients is given; indicate a method by which it is possible, after a finite number of operations, to determine whether a given equation is solvable in rational integers.

For example, integer solutions to the equation

$$x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0,$$

arising in connection with the meaning of the Pythagorean theorem are the sets ($\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}$)

$$x = \alpha^2 - 1, y = 2\alpha, z = \alpha^2 + 1.$$

These formulas were attributed to Pythagoras, but they do not cover all integer solutions (Pythagorean points) of a given equation. General solution

$$x = \xi^2 - \eta^2, y = 2\xi\eta, z = \xi^2 + \eta^2$$

($\xi, \eta \in \mathbb{Z}$) we find in Diophantus, although it was undoubtedly known long before him. In the work of Diophantus, the theory of such equations received significant development, so they were subsequently named after him.

The study of Diophantine equations, or Diophantine analysis, as they say today, was carried out by P. Fermat, L. Euler, J. Lagrange and C. F. Gauss. The last two attempts to solve solved second degree equations with two unknowns. One can name a number of other outstanding mathematicians of the 19th century who spent their time in Diophantine analysis, but by 1900, the time Hilbert presented this problem, successes in solving higher-order equations were rather modest. By posing the above problem, Hilbert intends to solve, apparently, to attract the attention of mathematicians to the development of the problem of Diophantine analysis.

Diophantine analysis: equation $P(x, y) = c$, where $c$ is an integer, $a$ $P(x, y)$ is an irreducible polynomial in which all terms have the same degree, not less than three, can have only finite numerical solutions (a polynomial is called irreducible if it does not decompose into the product of two polynomials with integer coefficients).

But neither Thue's method nor subsequent methods provided an algorithm for finding solutions. The results obtained for Diophantine equations of degree above two (the work of the Soviet mathematician B. N. Delone, the German mathematician K. Siegel, etc.) related to the search for algorithms for individual classes of Diophantine equations. And although success in the search for a general method for arbitrary Diophantine equations was extremely insignificant, mathematicians nevertheless believed that sooner or later it would be found.

However, in the mid-thirties, a clear concept of “algorithm” was formed in mathematics and the idea of ​​problems for which there is no algorithm that specifies their solutions continues—the algorithm of unsolvable problems. Examples of such problems were given in the 30-40s by A. Church, A. Tyuring, E. Post, A. A. Markov. In 1952, P. S. Novikov proved the algorithmic undecidability of one important problem in group theory - the problem of action points of words. Examples of algorithmic unsolvable problems, on the one hand, and difficulties associated with attempts to find the original algorithm, on the other, gave rise to doubt: maybe an algorithm that solves ten problems does not exist at all? The results of American mathematicians (M. Davis and others), obtained in the 50-60s, gave indirect confirmation of this assumption. They were able to show that for a certain more general class of equations, the so-called “exponential-Diophantine equations with integer coefficients,” such an algorithm does not exist. But this is for exponential-Diophantine equations, and for a more special case - Diophantine equations - such an algorithm could exist. So, Yu. V. Matiyasevich showed that for the class of such equations there is no required algorithm.

We have talked here only about the most striking achievements of Soviet mathematicians in solving Hilbert's problems. A large and detailed story is the topic of not an article, but a book. Such a book, telling about the achievements of scientists around the world in solving Hilbert's problems, written by major specialists in the relevant field of mathematics, was published in our country in 1969. True, when talking about this book, it should be borne in mind that, firstly, reading the book requires significant mathematical preparation - even knowledge of a university course is not enough to understand its individual sections, and, secondly, during the time that has elapsed since its publication, the state of affairs with the study of Gilbert's problems has changed greatly. Mathematics is now in a stage of rapid development; it constantly poses new and new problems for scientists. And many old ones (including some of Gilbert's problems) have not yet found their solution. We can be sure that Soviet mathematicians will continue to delight us with remarkable successes in solving the most important mathematics.
