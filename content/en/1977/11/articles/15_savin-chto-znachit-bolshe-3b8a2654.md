WHAT DOES “MORE” MEAN?

For any two numbers we can always tell which one is greater; To do this, it is enough to compare their decimal notations. The question “Which of the two segments is larger?” does not cause misunderstandings either. Obviously the one with the longer length. We can compare the lengths of segments without calculating them, but simply by moving them so that they are on the same ray, and one of the ends of each segment coincides with the beginning of the ray. So in a dispute between two guys - “Who is taller” - the matter, as a rule, ends with them standing with their backs to each other and someone cutting off this dispute with the help of their palm.

But how do we understand this statement: “The guys in our class are higher than in yours!”? It can be understood this way:

“The tallest student in our class is taller than the tallest student in your class,” or “The shortest student in our class is taller than the shortest student in your class,” or “Any student in our class is taller than any student in your class.” Several other methods of comparison can be proposed, but it is very difficult to give preference to any of them.

As we will see, a similar situation arises when trying to solve problem No. 137 from a geometry textbook for grade 10. Let us recall its formulation.

137. In a regular six-cell paramid, the larger diagonal section is an equilateral right triangle with zipomenus c. Find the volume of the paramid.

To understand the condition of the problem, you should remember what a diagonal section is. In the conditions of problem No. 89 the following definition was given:

“The section of a paramid by a plane passing through two lateral edges that do not lie on the same face is called a diagonal section.”

Unfortunately, the textbook did not contain a definition of a section of a paramidus (and a body in general) by a plane, but from the way this word is used, we can conclude that

“The section of a body by a plane is a geometric figure consisting of all points that simultaneously belong to both the chalk and the plane” (the intersection of a body and a plane).

It remains to understand what “big” means. Since the diagonal section of the paramid is obviously a triangle, it remains to find out what it means that one triangle is larger than the other.

There are a lot of possible answers that can be proposed. For example, “The one with the larger perimeter”, or “The one with the larger area”, or “The one with the larger circumcircle area”, or “The one with the larger circumcircle area”, or “The one with the larger side”...

It should be noted that in real situations the “size” of a triangle can be characterized by which of the numbers listed above is associated with

Apparently, problem 137 means that the “major diagonal section” is the diagonal section that passes through the major diagonal.

In this case, solving the problem is not difficult. The height of the paramid is the same as the height of the triangle, which is $\frac{c}{2}$, and the length of the side of the hexagon is also $\frac{c}{2}$. Hence the area of ​​the base of the paramid is $\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^2$, and the volume is $\frac{c^3\sqrt{3}}{16}$. This is the answer given in the textbook.

Well, what if you understand the size of the triangle and its area? And in this case the problem is also not too difficult to solve. First, let us replace that the diagonal sections of the paral paramid are triangles with two congested sides, which are the lateral edges of the paramid. If we denote the length of the side edge by $l$, then the area of ​​the triangle under consideration is equal to $\frac{1}{2}l^2\sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the indicated sides, and that of the sections is large, which has a large sine angle.

Secondly, let us replace that if the indicating angle is a right angle, then from what was said above it follows that such a section is the most important.

The case in which the largest section passes through the largest diagonal has already been discussed. It remains to consider the case when it passes through another diagonal. In this case, the length of this diagonal is $c$, the length of the larger diagonal is $\frac{2c\sqrt{3}}{3}$, and the sides of the hexagon are $\frac{c\sqrt{3}}{3}$. The length of the lateral edge is $\frac{c\sqrt{2}}{2}$, and the height of the paramid is $h = \sqrt{\frac{c^2}{2} - \frac{c^2}{3}} = c\sqrt{\frac{1}{6}}$. The final result is that the area of ​​the base is $\frac{c^2\sqrt{3}}{2}$, and the volume of the paramid is $\frac{c^3\sqrt{2}}{12}$.

What is the purpose of this note? Of course, this is not to point out an unsuccessful problem in the textbook, but to once give you the opportunity to be convinced that mathematics is a science in which all concepts and relationships between them must be strictly defined (of course, with the exception of basic, undefined concepts, such as a point, a plane, a straight line).

I would also like to note the fact that the same term can play different roles in different books. Naturally, according to the book or article, this term is strictly defined.
