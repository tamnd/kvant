The basis of the dynamics of a material point is Newton's three laws. They allow you to choose the most convenient reporting system for describing the motion, connect the acceleration of a body with the forces acting on this body, and establish some important patterns of interaction of the body. For example these laws.

Newton's first law: there is a maximum system patronymic relative to which a moving body maintains its speed constant if other bodies do not act on them or other acting bodies are compensated *).

These reporting systems are called inertial. There are also reporting systems in which the statement made in Newton's first law is not fulfilled. Such reporting systems are called non-inertial.

After the choice of any inertial reporting system has been made, you can begin to study and describe the interaction of the body and the nature of their movement.

Numerous experiments have shown that the only principle for changing the speed of a given body in inertial reporting systems is its interaction with other bodies (or with a field), that is, the action of a force on the body.

Newton's second law expresses the relationship between force and the acceleration of a body, which is communicated by this force - the force acting on a body is equal to the product of the mass of the body by the acceleration imparted by this force:

$$\vec{F} = ma.$$

(1)

Newton's second law can be formulated in another way - the change in the momentum of a body is equal to the momentum of the force acting on this body:

$$\Delta(mv) = \vec{F}\Delta t$$

(2)

(here $\Delta(mv)$ is the change in the momentum of the body over a period of time $\Delta t$).

Note that the impulse corresponds both due to changes in speed and due to changes in body mass*).

Newton's third law states that bodies act on each other with forces directed along the same straight line, equal in abscise magnitude and arbitrary in direction.

For example, when two bodies collide (Fig. 1), two elastic forces arise, related by the relation:

$$\vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21},$$

(3)

where $\vec{F}_{12}$ is the force acting on the first body from the second, and $\vec{F}_{21}$ is the force with which the first body acts on the second. Forces always appear in pairs.

Solving problems in dynamics must begin with an analysis of the forces acting on a given body, and it is necessary to imagine what, the action of which other body characterizes each force. Then the direction of each force, the direction of motion (velocity) and the acceleration of the body in question should be determined.

Let, for example, a small body rest on an inclined plane (Fig. 2). How many forces are acting on it? The body interacts with the Earth - this action is characterized by the gravitational force $mg$, directed vertically downwards - and with the surface of the inclined plane - this action is characterized by the reaction force $\vec{R}$. Since the body is in equilibrium, the vector sum of forces must be equal to zero, therefore the force $\vec{R}$ is equal in absolute value to $|mg|$, and is directed vertically upward. Sometimes the action of the surface of an inclined plane on a body is described by two forces:

elastic force $\vec{N}$, directed normal to the surface (normal reaction force), and friction force $\vec{F}_{tp}$, directed along the surface. The vector sum of these forces is equal to the support reaction force (see Fig. 2):

$$\vec{N} + \vec{F}_{tp} = \vec{R}.$$

The key to solving most dynamics problems is Newton's second law, which is written mathematically in the form of vector equations (1) or (2). However, in most cases it turns out to be more convenient to write this law in projections onto coordinate axes. For example, vector equation (1) in the general case can be replaced by triple equations written in projections onto three coordinate axes:

$$F_x = ma_x, F_y = ma_y, F_z = ma_z.$$

Let us also note that when analyzing the conditions of a problem, it is necessary to clearly understand the meaning of the control assumptions contained, as a rule, in the text of the problem. So, if a system of bodies connected by a thread is considered, then one of these additional conditions is the general “weightlessness” of the thread. The term “weightlessness” in this case is not entirely correct; it would be more accurate to say that the mass of the thread is negligible. What does this simplification achieve? It allows us to assume that the thread tension in all sections is the same. If the thread had mass, then the tension would change along the thread. For example, let's look at this problem.
