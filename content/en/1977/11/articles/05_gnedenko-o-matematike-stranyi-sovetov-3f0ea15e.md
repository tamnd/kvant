About mathematics of the Land of Soviets

Pre-revolutionary Russia gave the world a number of outstanding mathematicians who enriched our science with first-class ideas and results. It is enough to remember the names of N. I. Loachevsky, M. V. Ostroredsky, P. L. Chebyshev, A. A. Markov, A. M. Lyapuov, N. E. Zhukovsky in order to understand how much valuable our country has contributed to the development of mathematics. And this was done in an era when not only higher and secondary education, but even primary education was rare. For the progress of science, it is necessary not only the presence of potential talents, but also a high level of culture of large masses of the population, a well-organized school, and the presence of a developed economy and industry. Pre-revolutionary Russia was deprived of all this. In general there were only talented people, who, as a rule, did not find a place for themselves in life.

The Great October Revolution aroused enormous scientific enthusiasm among young people, a desire for the same decisive changes in science that occurred in social life, faith in success and the need for scientific research. This was facilitated by the fact that science and scientific discoveries were perceived to be only the personal matter of the scientist, of national significance, a necessity for the progress of the entire life of the people and the country.

In the very first years of the existence of Soviet power, during the years of devastation, civil war, intervention, famine, and epidemics, the government provided effective assistance to universities and took care of the creation of new universities and specialized research institutes and laboratories.

So, in 1918, a decree was issued on the organization of TsAGI - the Central Aeronautical Institute and N. E. Zhukovsky was appointed its director. In the same year, the Nizhny Novgorod Radio Laboratory was created. Both of these institutions had a huge influence on the development of domestic self-responsibility and radio engineering, but also on attracting the interests of Soviet mathematicians to new applied problems of great public importance.

In 1921 - at the height of devastation - on the initiative of V. A. Steklav, the Physics and Mathematics Institute of the Academy of Sciences was organized. For the first time in the history of our country, a special scientific institution began to develop theoretical and applied mathematics. This alone emphasized the attitude of the new state system to science, not only in its applied, but also in its theoretical aspect. In 1934, the Institute of Physics and Mathematics was divided into a number of independent scientific institutions, among which the Mathematical Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences, named after its founder, V. A. Steklav, is of particular interest to us. Over the more than half-century period of its existence, the Steklav Institute has made a great contribution to the development of theoretical mathematics.

Later, as the Republic of Academies of Sciences was organized, specialized mathematical institutes began to work in each union republic. The creation of a wide network of research institutions in our country was a new form of organization

scientific research. Talented researchers, passionate about scientific problems, were given the opportunity to completely devote themselves to their favorite work.

By what I have just said, I would not like to give readers the false impression that in our day science can be fully developed only within the walls of academic institutions. In fact, major scientific discoveries can be made in factory laboratories, college departments, and research institutes of applied processes. It's all about the person, his talent, passion, and the atmosphere around him. If he is interested in science, it is in the foreground, and the problems of his personal career do not bother him; if he is a generator of new ideas and is able to find approaches to solving them; if he is able to think critically about his results and listen to the suggestions of others; If institutions have sound scientific content and mutual assistance, then success is assured.

In connection with the mathematization of science and strengthened scientific and technological progress, fundamental scientific problems arise today in institutions of the applied process.

It is important not to neglect practice problems, since they give rise to formulations of mathematical problems of great importance. In order for individual practical problems to be balanced against general problems, it is necessary to build mathematical models of real phenomena in the essence of these problems and their basis. It is also important to see behind general mathematical constructions the possibility of reflecting real phenomena of the world around us - biological, physical, economic, engineering, organizational. In our time, when mathematical methods have produced literally all sciences, when many thousands of mathematicians work in scientific institutions of the applied process, it is especially important that the emergence of mathematics be visible to a sufficiently wide audience both by mathematicians themselves and by representatives of other fields of knowledge and activity.

We have already said that the Great October Revolution, being a powerful impetus for the progress of mathematics in our country, created exceptionally favorable conditions for the development of its abstract directions and the application of mathematical methods in natural science, engineering, and production organization. This success caused a huge influx of capable young people into universities, their desire to test their strength in scientific research and make their contribution to the progress of mathematics. Therefore, during the years of Soviet power, many hundreds of major mathematical researchers were trained, who obtained significant results in various fields of mathematical science, continued their own promising paths in our science, and created new areas of scientific research.

Further, a brief description of what Soviet mathematicians have done over the past sixty years would require a multi-volume essay and the work of a large group of specialists. That is why here we are forced to limit ourselves to only a cursory overview of a small proportion of important areas of scientific research. In doing so, we will be forced to mention only a small number of names, although many of those who will not be mentioned here deserve the highest praise for their scientific discoveries.

Already the first admissions of students to Moscow University after 1917 gave rise to the second generation of active students of Luzin. Luzin organized a seminar at which each participant was asked to design a definition for a monograph; then establish a discussion of the abstract with the formulation of questions, identifying opportunities for managing and summarizing the evidence. Such discussions perfectly educated scientific mathematicians and taught them to think independently.

In the twenties, Luzin’s students called their group “Lusitans.” This ascent was emphasized by his teacher, his scientific ideas, and lectures. Luzin's students were proud of their involvement in the group developing his ideas, and strived to give the maximum of what they were capable of in science. As P. S. In analytic number theory, remarkable results were obtained by I. M. Vinogradov M. V. In 1924, he showed that many problems of analytic number theory can be reduced to estimates of “trigonometric sums,” that is, sums of the form

$$\sum e^{2\pi i f(x)},$$

where $f$ is some function, and $x$ runs through one or another sequence of integers. In 1934, I.M. Vinogradov succeeded in this way in almost completely solving Goldbach’s value problem: every odd natural number can be represented as a sum of no more than three prime numbers.

New universities, formed in a number of cities in the country in the first years of the revolution, quickly turned into significant mathematical centers. First of all, we must name here Tbilisi and Tashkent, which were famous for their research in mathematical physics (Tbilisi), nonlinear differential equations and mathematical statistics (Tashkent). The organizers of the Georgian Mathematical School were N. I. Muskhelshvili, G. N. Nikoladze, A. M. Razmadze and A. K. Kharadze*). The founder of the Tashkent mathematical school was V.I. Romanovsky.

By the beginning of the Great Patriotic War, Soviet mathematics had gained enormous scientific prestige throughout the world. Her research covered practically all areas of mathematical thought. Many mathematicians took part in solving problems of natural science, technology and organization of production. The results of mathematicians were especially significant in connection with the problems raised by the development of authorization. The problem of flying in a skimming fluid prompted the construction of a theory of quasiconformal mappings by M. A. Lavrentiev. A major success on the eve of the Great Patriotic War was achieved in the construction of a mathematical theory of “shimmy” and “corkscrew”, which were the cause of the death of many aircraft. As a rule, having got into a “spin” or “shimmy”, the plane died along with the occupant.

The Great Patriotic War did not pass many Soviet mathematicians: thousands of them went to the front, many switched to solving problems necessary for victory, the rest did not stop working at their posts, believing in victory and creating new scientific values ​​for the future. Such prominent scientists as Yu. V. Linnik, A. A. Lyapunov, M. V. Bebutov fought at the fronts. Unfortunately, not everyone returned from the war fields, and Soviet mathematics lost many talented scientists. Some young mathematicians, having served in the Army throughout the war years, forever connected their lives with it. Many major military specialists who made significant contributions to Soviet military science began their career as mathematicians before the war.

Already the first days of the war showed that Soviet science could be an effective weapon in the fight. Tables were created for bombing from low-speed aircraft (“maize bombers”). A. N. Kolmogorov created the theory of artificial dispersion to increase the likelihood of hitting cells. This theory found application in mine attacks on naval vessels, as well as in anti-aircraft artillery fire.

M.A. Lavrentyev created a theory of the action of a shaped charge, the idea of ​​which was known to the hot workers and was used in the blasting of rocks. During the war it was used both by us and by other warring states. After the war, this theory was widely used in peaceful practice in the construction of dams, canals, and various types of embankments.

Problems of enormous importance in mathematics arose in connection with ensuring the quality of mass industrial products. Individual quality control of each manufactured product required a huge number of inspectors. It was impossible to do this, since there was already a catastrophic lack of force work. In addition, in some cases, checking each product is unacceptable, since it leads to irreparable damage. For example, to test a fuse, you need to detonate it. It was at this time that the idea of ​​constructing a theory of statistical product quality control arose. A. N. Kolmogorov took an active part in its creation. Currently, this theory is vigorously developing along two paths: 1) checking the quality of already manufactured batches (acceptance control) and 2) managing the quality of the product during the manufacturing process (technical or operational control).

At that time, many excellent studies were carried out in the fields of algebra, topology, probability theory, functional analysis, function theory and geometry. The post-war years are characterized by a rapid process of mathematization of meanings and practical activities. Mathematicians were required to solve problems of organizing production and to study biological processes, to advance in the field of physics and to create technical systems, to study economic issues and to rationally locate industrial enterprises.
