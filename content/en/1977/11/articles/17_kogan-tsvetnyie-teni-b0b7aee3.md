B.KOGAN COLOR SHADOWS

This post describes two simple experiments in which an interesting color change is observed. Of course, you can continue these experiences yourself. To carry them out, very little is required - only a few colored light sources. For example, a red light bulb (you can buy it in photo stores) and a shining light bulb (such light bulbs are sold in electrical stores along with regular ones).

green shadow

In a room lit with ordinary white light, light a table lamp after screwing a red bulb into its socket. Place a sheet of white paper on the table and place a small object, such as a pencil, between it and the lamp. His shadow will appear on a sheet of paper, but it will be completely unexpected in color - not black or gray, but... green.

This effect is apparently associated not only and not so much with physics, but with physiology and psychology. The shadow of an object appears green to us due to the contrast with the surrounding background, which, being in fact reddish, is felt by us as white, or we know that the paper is white. Apparently, our consciousness perceives the absence of red color in an area occupied by shadow as the presence of green color in this area. But why green?

The fact is that red and green are complementary colors.

This is the name for colors that complement each other to white. What does this mean? Back in the 17th century, Newton discovered that white sunlight is complex, a combination of simple colors - violet, blue, blue, green, yellow, orange and red. This can be verified, for example, using a glass prism. If sunlight is passed through a narrow slit and directed at the face, a multi-colored image of this slit will be obtained. Newton also conducted other experiments - he “collected” colors together (for example, using a lens) and as a result again obtained white color (Fig. 1). It turns out that if you “hold back” some color, say green, then the image of the slit becomes colored, and red at that (Fig. 2). Or, if you “hold back” the yellow color, the image of the slit will turn out to be blue, etc. It is in this sense that green and red, yellow and blue, etc., are called complementary colors (Fig. 3).

This interesting experiment can be carried out with light bulbs of other colors. So, if the light bulb is green, the shadow turns red. If the light bulb is blue, the shadow will be yellow, and if the light bulb is yellow, the shadow will be blue. In general, the color of the shadow always turns out to be complementary to the color of the light bulb.

Description of the phenomenon can often be observed in winter near newspaper city signs. When there is snow on the ground, shadows are clearly visible on it, the color of which is complementary to the color of the illuminated sign.

red leaves

Turn off the light in the room and light the lamp, after placing a blue light bulb in it. Look at the leaves of plants in the light of this light bulb: green leaves, illuminated by the light, seem not green or blue, but... red.
What's the matter? It turns out that the glass of a blue light bulb transmits not only blue, but also partially red light.

At the same time, plant leaves reflect not only green light, but also partly red light, absorbing other colors. Therefore, when light from a blue bulb falls on the leaves, they reflect a little red light and, therefore, appear red to us.

The described effect can be obtained in another way: look at the leaves of plants through blue glasses or through a blue filter. Talking about his experience with such glasses, the famous Soviet scientist K. A. Timiryazev wrote: “You just have to put them on, and the whole world appears in a “rosy light.” Under a clear blue sky, a fantastic landscape with caral-red meadows and forests develops...”
