How Soviet physics was created

We continue the publication of the memoirs of the Hero of Socialist Labor, Academician I.K. Kikon, scientific in issue 10.

At the end of the 30s, Academician Abram Fedorovich Ioffe set a new task for his employees: to study a class of substances with a special electrical property - semiconductors. As you know, semiconductors are substances whose conductivity is too low to be considered metals, but too small to be classified as dielectrics.

At that time, semiconductors were not widely used. And yet, Ioffe, intuitively understanding that in the future they would acquire great practical importance, from the very beginning paid much attention to work on studying their properties. And these studies were not easy. And here's why. Different samples of the same semiconductor chemical composition had completely different physical characteristics. For example, for different samples of cuprous oxide (Cu₂O), the conductivity turned out to be different, as well as the dependence of conductivity on temperature. The samples obtained by different methods behaved some as dielectrics, others as conductors. It is clear that it is very difficult to identify any general patterns when examining the table of “capricious” substances. And this is due to the fact that, as it turned out, the properties of semiconductors are extremely dependent on the tiny amounts of impurities present in the samples. For example, if in a sample of cuprous oxide there are so many fractions of a percent more oxygen atoms than in “pure” cuprous oxide (that is, with the exact chemical state of copper and oxygen), then it behaves as a conductor, although pure cuprous oxide is a dielectric.

This feature of semiconductors, in a sense, predetermined the direction of research. First of all, it was necessary to establish how the properties of semiconductors depend on the amount of impurities they contain. And for this it was necessary to have samples with a precisely known chemical composition and amount of impurities.

Boris Vasilievich Kurchatov (brother of I.V. Kurchatov) was very actively involved in these works. He found a way to obtain samples of cuprous oxide with a given excess amount of oxygen. Having created several such samples, he studied how the conductivity of cuprous oxide changes depending on the admixture of oxygen. It turned out that conductivity increases sharply with increasing amount of impurity. This classic work began the creation of semiconductors with a given property by introducing a certain amount of a particular impurity. Now they find huge practical applications.

After Abram Fedorovich Ioffe switched our attention to semiconductors, I decided to study the so-called internal photoeffect. The phenomenon of this is that under the influence of light, additional electron properties appear in the semiconductor and the conductivity of the sample increases. I wanted to find out whether these extra electrons had the same mobility as dark electrons (that is, free electrons).

One of the main areas of scientific work at the Physico-Technical Institute was also the study of the electrical properties of dielectrics. Among the employees involved in this issue was Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov. The laboratory, which he then headed, began studying the properties of the so-called Rochelle soli (chemical formula NaKC₄H₄O₆·4H₂O). Rochelle soli crystals are very beautiful. They reach enormous sizes. I watched with satisfaction as Igor Vasilievich grew them from rasters in large glass vessels.

Rochelle salt is a typical representative of dielectrics with anomalous dielectric constant. (Recall that the dielectric constant is a number indicating how many times the electric field inside the dielectric is less than the external field in which the dielectric is located.) So, in ordinary dielectrics the permittivity is of the order of several units (for glass - 3 ÷ 5, for mica - 7 ÷ 8); The dielectric constant of water, equal to 80, was considered abnormally high. And for Rochelle soli, the value of this value can reach several tens of thousands of units! And one more anomaly is the untrained “behavior” of this permeability when the external electric field changes: in strong fields its value is not large, but in weak fields it reaches enormous values. Naturally, the study of the properties of Rochelle soli is of great interest. Using it as a filler, it was possible to create small capacitors with a very large capacity. True, the anomalous properties of Rochelle soli appear in a rather narrow temperature range (from approximately -30°C to +30°C).

In 1934, the famous physicist Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa returned to the Soviet Union from England, from a long scientific trip. While working in England, he became famous for investigating a number of properties of substances in strong magnetic fields. The general magnetic fields that were created in laboratories at that time were of the order of 1-2 T. To obtain stronger fields, it was necessary to use large electromagnetic fields, which were very expensive. It is, of course, possible to create strong magnetic fields inside the solenoid without iron. But for this it is necessary to feed the solenoid with a very strong current, which is associated with a large release of heat and overheating of the solenoid winding.

Kapitsa reasoned like this. It is not necessary to have a constant large field for a long time. Phenomena occurring in a substance placed in a magnetic field are atomic in nature. And such phenomena take place very quickly - in millionths and even billions of seconds. Therefore, it is enough to create a strong magnetic field that exists for a short period of time, and during this time the necessary research is carried out in it. Kapitsa implemented this idea in the following way. A magnetic field was created in a solenoid through which a large current from an alternator was passed for one polerinad (0.01 second).

The armature of the alternator is untwisted by an electric motor. In this case, the generator terminals were open. When the armature unwinded to the calculated (nominal) speed, the engine was turned off, and the armature continued to rotate by inertia. For example, at the open terminals of the winding (emf) it changed according to a sinusoidal law. At the moment when it “passed” through zero, using a special switch, the generator terminals were shorted to the solenoid winding. And after half the period, that is, after 0.01 seconds, when the direction dropped to zero again, the target was unlocked. The circuit must be opened at the moment when the current passes through zero to avoid the occurrence of an electric arc. During production, a high current flowed through the winding (and therefore, a large magnetic field existed), and the coil did not have time to heat up very much. But another difficulty arose. When current flows through the solenoid, radial forces act on each turn of it, tending to break it. When the current reaches a high level, these forces can exceed the strength limit of the winding material, and it will collapse. (Peter Leonidovich says that when he first carried out this experiment in a laboratory in England, the coil ruptured, its fragments scattered in different directions. And on the same day, the laboratory staff hastened to insure their lives.) So it arose that obtaining strong magnetic fields in this way is limited to reading the materials from which the winding is made. Kapitsa made the winding from special high-conductivity bronze. The coil was small - its diameter was 25 mm, length - about 100 mm. A current was passed through this coil for a hundredth of a second, which made it possible to obtain a field of about 30 T.

In such fields, Kapitsa investigated a number of physical phenomena. In particular, he studied in great detail the effect of a magnetic field on the resistance of conductors. At low fields, the change in the resistance of conductors in a magnetic field increases approximately in proportion to the square of the magnetic induction. It was assumed that in strong fields there would also be the same dependence. But it turned out that in strong fields the square dependence of the change in resistance on the magnitude of magnetic induction is violated. A coincidence was found between the conductor resistance value and the magnetic field induction value.

Of great interest was the study of the properties of various substances under conditions where strong magnetic fields are combined with low temperatures. And Kapitsa began to consider the question of ways to obtain low temperatures close to absolute zero. It is known that the lowest temperature liquid is liquid helium. At normal pressure, the boiling point of liquid helium is 4.2 K.

Liquid helium was first obtained by the Dutch scientist Kamerlingh Onnes in 1908. The installation he built in his laboratory in Leiden produced small amounts of liquid helium. Such installations were very expensive. P. L. Kapitsa developed a more productive and economical installation, in which he could obtain a fairly large amount of liquid helium.

By that time, physicists became interested in the properties of liquid helium itself. The fact is that liquid helium is the only substance that, as they say, undergoes a phase transformation while in a liquid state: at a temperature of 2.19 K its physical properties change sharply. Above this temperature, helium behaves like an animated liquid, and at $T \leq 2,19$ K it has a number of completely unusual properties: its thermal conductivity increases sharply, and its toughness drops sharply. It is customary to call helium at temperatures above 2.19 K helium I, and at $T \leq 2,19$ K - helium II.

Helium II was first discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes. Then Keez studied it at the Leiden laboratory. He discovered that the thermal conductivity of helium II is many times greater than that of the most thermally conductive metals. Therefore, Kees called helium II a superconducting substance.

The answer to all these questions is for the hydrodynamic theory of superfluidity, created in 1941 by academician Lev Davidovich Landdu. He showed that the general classical approach to explaining the properties of matter at columnar temperatures is incorrect. Landd managed to develop the quantum theory of He II superfluidity. She explained the results of Kapitsa's experiments and predicted a number of new phenomena, which were soon discovered by experiments.

The superfluid nature of liquid helium has not yet found wide application. But the theory of superfluidity created by Landdu had a great influence on the development of theoretical physics. It was the key to creating the theory of another remarkable phenomenon that occurs at low temperatures - superconductivity. Let me remind you that the phenomenon of superconductivity was discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911. He discovered that at a temperature of 4.15 K the electrical resistance of mercury completely disappears, and called this phenomenon superconductivity. After this, it was discovered that many metals and alloys at low temperatures lose electrical resistance and become superconducting. Of course, the existence of conductors without resistance promised tempting practical prospects, and physicists became very active in the study of superconductivity. However, it was not possible to create a general theory of the phenomenon.

After the creation of quantum mechanics, the microcoptic theory of solids was constructed, that is, a theory that considered the properties of solids from the point of view of the behavior of electrons and atoms. It fully qualitatively determined all the properties of metals, dielectrics, and semiconductors under various conditions. But superconductivity remained a mysterious phenomenon. And only in 1957, that is, 46 years after the discovery of this phenomenon, it received its explanation in the microcoptic theory created by the Soviet scientist academician Nikolai Nikolaevich Bogolyubov and, independently of him, by the American physicists Bardeen, Cooper and Schriefer (BCS, as this group is called).

By that time, many properties of superconductors had been studied experimentally. It was found that under the influence of a magnetic field, the superconductivity of metals is destroyed - they become ordinary conductors. The value of the magnetic field induction at which superconductivity disappears is called the critical field (critical induction). This value is different for different metals. the critical field depends on the temperature of the superconductor. The higher the temperature, the smaller the critical field. At the critical temperature the critical field is zero.

Thus, in 1937, L. D. Landdu, analyzing the experimental data available at that time, theoretically predicted a new phenomenon related to the process of transition of a metal from a superconducting state to a normal one in a magnetic field. According to his theory, this transition occurs gradually. Starting from a certain magnetic field indication value that has not yet reached the critical value, the field partially penetrates into the superconductor. At the same time, an alternating layer is formed in it - superconducting and non-superconducting. When the critical value of the external field indication is reached, the superconducting state is completely destroyed.

Experiments that were soon carried out at the Kharkov Institute of Physics and Technology by Lev Vasilyevich Shubnikov and at the Moscow Institute of Physical Problems by corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Alexander Iosifovich Shalnikov, the lectures confirmed the pre-descriptions of Landdu’s theory. I had the opportunity to observe these experiments both in Moscow and Kharkov. They clearly demonstrated the structure of the “complex” intermediate state, so to speak, the topography of alternating superconducting and normal layers of the inner superconducting ball. This state of a superconductor is usually called the “Shubnikov phase” abroad.

In 1950-1952, a group of Soviet physicists - academicians Vitaly Lazarevich Ginzburg and Lev Davidovich Landdu, corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences Alexei Alekseevich Abrikovov and Lev Petrovich Gorkov - developed a theory from which it followed that superconductors with the necessary properties should exist. Now it is called the GLAG theory (Ginsburg, Landdu, Abrikovov, Gorkov). Such superconductors are called type II superconductors, in contrast to ordinary superconductors. For them, the values ​​of the critical field must be significantly larger.

Very soon experiments were carried out that fully justified these pre-tales. As it turns out, metallic nobium and a whole series of alloys belong to type II superconductors. The values ​​of the critical fields for such superconductors actually turned out to be very large. For example, for an alloy of nobium with ovum it reaches 20-30 T. The large value of the critical field allows the use of type II superconductors to create strong magnetic fields. Moreover, despite the high costs associated with producing a liquid gel, the use of superconductors turns out to be much more profitable than the use of conventional electromagnets. There are already generators and electric motors with superconducting objects with a power of about 10,000 kilowatts. Currently, the problem of transmitting electricity over long distances via “superconducting” transmission lines is being considered.

Thus, the theory of Soviet physicists played a huge role in the creation of new technology.
