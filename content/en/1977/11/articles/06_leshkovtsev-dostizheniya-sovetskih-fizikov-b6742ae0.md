Achievements of Soviet physicists

A brief chronicle of outstanding Soviet physicists includes mainly “name” discoveries. Of course, not every major discovery necessarily subsequently acquires the names of the scientists who made it. Therefore, the list given here does not cover even the largest discoveries made by our scientists in the field of physics. A detailed account of the achievements of Soviet physicists, published for the 50th anniversary of the October Revolution, was hardly reduced to two large volumes. But we hope that even a short list of “name” discoveries will help our readers feel how great the contribution of Soviet scientists to the treasury of world science is. The chronicle was prepared by V. A. Leshkovtsev.

It is known that the strength of a solid body, determined experimentally, is many times less than its value calculated theoretically. Academician Abram Fedorovich Yuffe was the first to reveal the reason for this difference. It turns out that various defects in the crystal structure, for example, microsopper cracks on the surface of the crystals, are to blame for the premature destruction of materials. By immersing a crystal of rock salt in water and dissolving the surface layer, Yuffe achieves an increase in strength hundreds of times. The strengthening of crystals during dissolution of the surface layer is called the “Juffe effect.”

In 1919-1920, academician Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam theoretically showed that microsopper inhomogeneities in the density of matter, resulting from thermal vibrations of molecules, change the spectrum of scattered light, adding new spectral lines to it, which are very difficult to observe. (These lines were first discovered by corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Evgeny Fedorovich Gross.) The phenomenon as a result of which they arise was called the “Mandel effect.”

In 1922, Professor Aleksandrovich Friedman obtained two new solutions to the basic equation of the general theory of the relative length of the Universe. Both of them described not a Stationary Universe, like Einstein’s, but a Universe that is continuously evolving. Subsequently, “Friedmann’s model of the expanding Universe” received experimental confirmation and is now generally accepted.

Samuilovich Landsberg, while studying the scattering of light in quartz crystals, discovered additional spectral lines in the spectrum of scattered light that did not belong to the light incident on the crystal. These spectral lines arise from the interaction of incident light with the individual molecules that form the crystal lattice. Somewhat later, a similar phenomenon was discovered by the Indian physicist Raman while studying the scattering of light in liquid benzene. It is called the “Mandelshtam-Landsberg-Raman effect” or “raman scattering of light.” Raman scattering has become a powerful tool for studying the structure of molecules.

In 1928-1929, Academician Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa, using the original installation he designed to create super-strong magnetic fields, established that in such fields the change in the electrical resistance of metals does not grow in proportion to the square of the magnetic induction, as expected based on the then existing theories, but is a linear function of magnetic induction. This unexpected phenomenon, which could not be explained for about 30 years, is called “Kapitsa’s law.”

In the early 30s, Professor Lev Vasilyevich Shubnikov, working in the laboratory of the Dutch physicist De Haas, discovered that the electrical resistance of a height placed in a magnetic field, as the magnetic induction of the field increases, either returns or kills (as they say, the resistance of a height oscillates). This “Shubnikov-De-Haas effect” is widely used to study the energy spectrum of electrons in a metal.

In 1930, academician Lev Davidovich Landu theoretically showed that free electrons in a metal under the influence of an external magnetic field can only move along certain (as physicists say - quantum) trajectories. This means that special energy levels arise in a magnetic field, called “Landou levels”.

From the same theory it followed that the action of a magnetic field on free electrons in a metal leads to the appearance of an additional magnetic moment directed in the direction opposite to the external magnetic field. Such a moment is called diamagnetic, and the described phenomenon is called “Landou diamagnetism.” Note that the same theory also implied the Shubnikov-De-Haas effect.

In 1962, L. D. Land was awarded the Nobel Prize for his outstanding achievements in the field of theoretical physics.

Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Yakov Ilyich Frenkel was the first in 1931 to introduce a quasi-particle, which is now called the “Frenkel expeton,” into use among physicists. He drew attention to the fact that in a crystal lattice there can be a special influence of the state of electrons. Having arisen in any cell of the crystal, this emergence moves throughout the crystal, like a free shaped particle. The existence of extons was first proven in the experiments of Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Evgeniy Fedorovich Gross. Expetons play a large role in solid state physics and modern chemistry.

In 1933, academician Igor Evgenievich Tamp theoretically predicted the existence of free energy states (“loves”) on the surface of crystals. They entered into a science called "Tampa Surface Levels."

In 1934, Academician Isaac Konstantinovich Kikoni discovered the photoelectromagnetic effect, showing in experiments that when a semiconductor is illuminated in a magnetic field, an electromotive force arises in it. Light quanta create a current carrier - free electrons and holes, which diffuse along the direction of incidence of light, and the magnetic field separates them in opposite directions. This phenomenon has long been called the “Kikon effect.”

Academician Pavel Alekseevich Cherenkov, together with his teacher, Academician Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov, discovered the “superluminal electron” effect in 1934. It turned out that in liquid and solid media electrons can move at a speed greater than the speed of light in these media (but, of course, not greater than the speed of light in emptiness!). Electrons can burn the light emitted by the immi. This effect, called the “Cherenkov effect,” has found a number of valuable practical applications, in particular for the detection of fast charged particles (the so-called Cherenkov particles). The theory explaining the Cherenkov effect was created by academicians Igor Evgenievich Tam and Ilya Mikhailovich Frank. For the discovery and explanation of this phenomenon, P. A. Cherenkov, I. E. Tam and I. M. Frank were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1958.

In 1936, Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Yakov Ilyich Frenkel developed the electrocapillary theory of atomic nuclei, in which nuclei are considered as drops of a charged liquid. Following Frenkel, the same ideas were developed by the Danish physicist Niels Bohr. They went down in the history of physics under the name “droplet model of the Bohr-Frenkel nucleus.”

Another phenomenon discovered by him in 1938 is associated with the name of Academician Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsya. While studying the properties of the liquid gel, he discovered that at temperatures below 2.19 K the gel flows through the narrow mouth with virtually no hesitation. This phenomenon was later called superfluidity. The theory of this phenomenon is created by the “Vlasov equation”.

Professor of Moscow University Anatoly Aleksaidrovich Blasov in 1938 was the first to derive an equation that describes the behavior of rarefied plasma in which no new generation of charged particles occurs. It entered science under the name “Vlasov equation”.

In 1944, academician Evgeniy Konstantinovich Zavoisky discovered electronic parametric resonance, marking the beginning of a new field of solid state physics. The essence of this phenomenon is that substances located in a constant magnetic field selectively absorb the energy of a high-frequency magnetic field perpendicular to it.

In 1944, academician Vladimir Iosifovich Veksler discovered the principle of aptophasization of particles accelerated in cyclic accelerators that bears his name. The work of all modern cyclic accelerators is based on it. Somewhat later, independently of Wexer, the same principle was discovered by the American physicist McMinlan. For this discovery they were awarded an international scientific prize.

In 1947, academician Lev Davidovich Landu showed that even in the absence of new plasma particles propagating in it, electromagnetic waves experienced attenuation. This phenomenon plays a significant role in plasma enhancement. Physicists around the world call it “Land damping.”

Some metals and alloys, when cooled to very low temperatures, completely lose resistance to the current flowing through them. This is called superconductivity. In 1950, academicians Vitaly Lazarevich Ginzburg and Lev Davidovich Landdu built a theory of superconductivity, taking into account quantum efficiencies in superconductors. The user of the theory, corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences Aleksey Alekseevich Abrikovov and Lev Petrovich Gorkov created a theory of superconducting alloys, which is of great importance. This theory is known throughout the world as the “GLAG theory” (Ginsburg - Landdu - Abrikov - Gorkov).

The Microsoppech theory of superconductivity, which explains the mechanism of this mysterious phenomenon, was created in 1957 by academician Nikolai Nikolaevich Bogolyubov and American physicists Bardeen, Cooper and Schriefer. It is called the “Bogolyubov-Bardeen-Cooper-Schriefer theory of superconductivity.”

In the early 50s, academicians Aleksandr Mikhailovich Prosorov and Nikolai Gennadievich Basov developed the foundations of a new field of physics—quantum electrical peaking—and created the first quantum molecular generator using ammonia molecules. For these outstanding works, in 1964 they, together with the American physicist Townes, were awarded the Nobel Prize.

Complex molecules of the organization of substances have spectra consisting of wide continuous spectral bands. In 1952, Professor Eduard Vladimirovich Shpolsky showed that by dissolving organizational substances in specially selected solvents and cooling the resulting solutions to very low temperatures, it is possible to obtain absorption spectra consisting of very low spectral lines that are uniquely related to the structure and properties of the molecules. Thus a new field of molecular research was created.

Semiconductors, like other substances, can absorb light whose wavelengths fall within certain limits. They are transparent for light with long wavelengths. Thus, there is a limit to the wavelength at which a semiconductor will absorb light. In 1958, academician Leonid Veniaminovich Kelysh theoretically predicted that under the influence of an external electric field this boundary should shift towards longer wavelengths. A similar prediction was made by the German physicist Franz. In 1960, Professor Viktor Sergeevich Vavilov received experimental confirmation of this phenomenon, now known as the “Kelysh-Franz effect.”

In 1966, the most powerful charged particle accelerator for that time, the synchrophasotron, came into operation in the USSR, accelerating protons to an energy of 70 billion electron volts. It was built down the city of Serpukhov. With the help of this accelerator, it was shown that the probability of collision of charged particles at an energy greater than 20 billion electron volts does not decrease and does not remain constant with increasing energy, as the theory predicted, but continues to decay. This necessary phenomenon was called the “Serpukhov effect”.
