Kappa

The curve, called *Kanna* (see the third page of the cover) has a center of symmetry $O$ and two mutually perpendicular axes of symmetry. Kappa also has two horizontal asymptotes. A straight line drawn in the plane of the kappa intersects it at most at four points.

The idea behind the construction of Sluz, the discoverer of the kappa, is as follows. Distance (Fig. 1) are congestive circles whose centers lie on the line $l$ and do not contain the point $O$ inside. To each such circle from point $O$ we draw a pair of tangents. The set $\{M\}$ of these points of contact is represented by a kappa. This method is essentially equivalent to method $I$. Nyyuton (the robl of radii of congested circles is used by Nyyuton to use the side of the square $MB$).

Method $I$. Newton has, however, the advantage that it can be used to graphically construct a tangent to the kappa at any point. To do this, it is enough to construct a normal (perpendicular to the tangent). We'll show you how to do it.

The motion of the square at each moment of time can be represented as rotation around a certain point $N$. How to find the position of this point for an arbitrary moment in time? Points $B$ and $O$ of the square (Fig. 2), together with its other points, rotate about $N$, and we know the directions of velocity at these points ($B$ moves along $l$, and $O$ moves along $AM$). Therefore, $N$ is at the intersection of lines perpendicular to $AM$ and $l$. Then $MN$ is the normal to the kappa at point $M$.

Using figure 3 it is easy to derive the polar kappa equation $\rho=a$ sttg $\varphi$ (a is the radius of congested circles).

We will show the method of constructing a mouthguard, belonging to I. Barrow. Let us draw a “vertical” ray from point $O$; through the point $A$ on it we draw a line $m$ perpendicular to $OA$ (Fig. 4). The distance is an arbitrary ray $OA_1$ intersecting with $m$. Let us put aside the segment $OM_1 \cong AA_1$ at $OA_1$. The set $\{M\}$ of such points forms the two upper branches of the kappa. (You can see that this is really a kappa by looking at its polar equation.)

We present another method of constructing a mouth guard, which belongs to I. Bernoulli, without a drawing. Let's draw a circle $(L, |LO|)$ with center $L \in l$. Let us plot two congested arcs $OP < \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $PQ$ on this circle. Let's connect the points $O$ and $Q$ with a chord. At the intersection of this chord or its continuation with the line passing through the point $P$ and parallel to $l$, we obtain the point $M$. The set of such points $\{M\}$ for all possible positions of the point $P$ is also represented by two branches of the kappa. Prove it.
