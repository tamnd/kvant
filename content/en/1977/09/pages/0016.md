If reflectors were much superior to refractors in terms of optical parameters, they were much inferior to them in terms of ease of observation and versatility of applications. Larger and less bulky refractors have long since become first a special mount, in which one of the two mutually perpendicular axes is installed parallel to the axis of the world. Uniform rotation around this axis at a speed of one revolution per day allows you to monitor the visible movement of the starry sky and keep the observed object in the field of view all the time. The other axis serves to direct the telescope to any point in the sky.

As for the reflectors, due to their enormous size and weight, they were mounted much more simply and could be used for observations only near the meridian, turning in the most practical way with the help of blocks and ropes; the observed one had to be piled up in extremely inconvenient places. In addition, the disadvantage of reflectors was that the metal mirrors would wear out due to weathering, which required repeated difficult polishing.

Thus, reflectors did not give up their positions and were used where it was necessary to make precise angular measurements - in meridian instruments for determining the spherical coordinates of stars, in micrometric measurements, for example, of double stars, and later - for photographing the starry sky.

Reflectors received new opportunities in the sixties, when the German chemist Liebig found a way to coat glass with a thin layer of shiny silver. At the same time, the French physicist Foucault began making reflector mirrors from glass, followed by silvering the reflecting surface. Such mirrors were much lighter than metal ones, which made it possible to use a special mount for them. In case of tarnishing, the silver layer was easily washed off with nitric acid, and the mirror was again silvered chemically without additional polishing.
