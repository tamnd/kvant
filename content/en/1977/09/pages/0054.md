So far we have talked about coordinates on a sphere or plane, defined by two numbers. There are also coordinates specified by a single number. These are the coordinates on a straight line. I think each of you knows that it is enough to set one number - the distance from the point to the beginning of the cutoff - to indicate the position of this point on a straight line. In life, we very often come across such coordinates. An example is a railway with kilometer columns along it. Another example is the numbers of houses on the street. What about time? Although we denote each moment in time with a whole bunch of numbers: year, month, day, hour, minute and second, it is clear that we can get around it with a single number, expressed, for example, in seconds. One number specifies the position of a point on a circle. When we say “Vanya was born on September 14,” we are essentially dealing with coordinates on a circle (along what?).

Well, how many coordinates will determine the position of a point in space? Naturally, three. These three numbers can be obtained, for example, like this. Let us connect the center of the Earth and our point with a beam and consider the latitude and longitude of the intersection of the beam with the Earth and the distance from our point to the center of the Earth. Such a coordinate system is called spherical. You can do it differently for your friend. Let's choose a certain plane and introduce a Cartesian coordinate system on it, and compare to our point the coordinates of its projection onto this plane and the distance from it to the plane, taken with the plane sign for one half of the space and with the minus sign for the other; this way we get a Cartesian coordinate system in space.

The spherical coordinate system is usually used on airfields. A radar is installed next to the lake. This device has certain distances to the aircraft, the angle at which the aircraft is visible above the horizon, and the angle between the direction towards the aircraft and the direction to the north.

In conclusion, let's discuss one business proposal. Each point in space can be specified by numbers. Moreover, you can set the position of my or your apartment using numbers. So why write long addresses on envelopes? It would be enough to agree on a coordinate system (for example, long, hiss and floor number) and the letters would perfectly reach the addressee.

Now it’s time to remember Captain Grant’s letter. After all, if you make a mistake in one of the numbers in such a short address or this number cannot be read (you never know what will happen in a long distance), the letter will not reach the addressee. The primary address is more stable. If you make a minor mistake, the letter will still get there. The address contains, as they say, redundant information that Captain Grant also included in his letter. He wrote his letter in three languages ​​and included not only the coordinates of the crash site, but also the continent on which it occurred. But this was not enough. So please don’t switch to a simplified address.
