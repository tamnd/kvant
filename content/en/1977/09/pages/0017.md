Such large mirrors, despite their significant thickness (reaching $1/8$ fraction of the diameter), noticeably signal under the influence of their own weight at different inclinations to the horizon. To create this harmful phenomenon, a so-called unloading was invented, consisting of numerous levers with counterweights that support the rear part of the mirror with a force that depends on the tilt of the mirror.

By this time, the scope of application of large reflectors was finally determined. These are, on the one hand, photographic observations of the faintest and most distant objects - nebulae (mainly extragalactic, located at distances of many tens and hundreds of millions of light years), and on the other hand, photographic spectra for the study of the physical properties and chemical state and their atmospheres. This also includes the discovery and study of spectroscopic double stars and flaring novae. For all these purposes, you need to collect as much light as possible, which can really be done with the help of large mirrors. In addition, for spectral observations, the complete achromaticity of the reflectors is important.

The great successes achieved with the help of the mentioned reflectors raised the question of creating even more powerful tools. So, in 1917, a 100-inch (254 cm) reflector came into operation, also on the basis of which an innovation was applied: the polar axis was flipped by two large floats, half immersed in a semi-syndrical mixture with mercury, which achieved very easy and smooth rotation (Fig. 6).

In the early thirties, in the Californian city of Pasadena, preparatory work began on the design of a reflector with a diameter twice as large - 200 inches (508 cm) and in 1946 its installation was completed on Mount Palomar (200 km from Los Angeles, California) at an altitude of 1750 m above sea level. The reflector (Fig. 7) is installed in a round tower under a dome with a diameter of 42 m, which has a wide hatch, which is directed to the desired direction of the sky by turning the dome (Fig. 8).
