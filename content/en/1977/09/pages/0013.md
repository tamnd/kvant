Six meter telephone

In 1609, Galileo for the first time directed a homemade telescope, made up of two spectacle glasses, to the new sky and obtained an enlarged image of celestial objects. This spotting scope provided only three times magnification. The very next year, Galileo made two more telecopes, then several more. The best of the telecopes magnified 30 times. With this instrument, Galileo made a number of solid discoveries: he saw the four-moon Jupiter, discovered the phases of Venus (resembling the phases of the Moon), noticed mountains on the Moon, and “decomposed” the Milky Way into many faint stars invisible to the naked eye.

What is phone magnification and what does it depend on? The Galileo telescope consists of two main parts: the lens (facing towards the object) and the eyepiece (facing towards the eye). The objective was a converging lens, and the eyepiece was a diverging lens, and these lenses were positioned so that their rear foci coincided. The path of rays in such a telephone (now usually called a Galilean tube) is shown in figure 1. A parallel beam of rays from a distant source, after refraction in the lens, becomes converging, and upon exiting the eyepiece it turns out to be parallel again. Then falling into the eye of the observer, the rays converge on the retina and give a real image of the source. The angle α′ at which the rays exit the telescope is greater than the angle α that the rays making with the axis fall on the lens (or are observed directly into the eye). In other words, the spotting scope increases the angle at which the source is seen. This increase is equal to (see Fig. 1).

$$K = \frac{tg \alpha'}{tg \alpha} = \frac{F_1}{F_2},$$

where $F_1$ is the focal length of the lens, and $F_2$ is the eyepiece.

Galileo's contemporary Kepler replaced the diverging eyepiece with a short-focus collecting lens, which plays the role of rays when viewing the image of an enlarged source created by the lens. The path of rays in the Kepler tube is shown in figure 2. The advantage of this tube in comparison with the Galilean tube is that it gives a real image in the focal plane of the lens, where a measuring scale or photographic plastic can be placed. The magnification of the Kepler tube, like the Galilean tube, is equal to the ratio of the focal lengths of the lens and eyepiece.
