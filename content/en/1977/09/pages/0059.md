an integral part of the All-Russian Olympids, and the tasks presented in the book are distinguished by a high scientific level and are well thought out from a methodological point of view.

The book begins with a story about the history of the Olympics. The authors of the book directly took part in holding “big” Olympiads, and they have something to tell readers. This chapter, written vividly and experientially, is a good starter for the book.

The second and third chapters contain formulations of problems corresponding to mathematics and physics, offered at the MIPT Olympiads. The collected problems are only part of the many problems that have been offered at the Olympiads over 15 years.

It should be emphasized that most of the tasks are not standard and are generally quite difficult.

Many problems are formulated in a very intricate and artificial way: not only different animals, insects and fish participate in the formulations, but even ... ghosts. But the planner's formulation hides very meaningful and difficult mathematical problems. So one very small task is connected with Ali Baba and... herrings. In itself, it is very difficult - you need to come up with a strategy, following which you can bring all the herrings to the same position for a fixed number of moves (problem 98).

Following the division of the 1975 International Olympiad Borya Yusin (he is a student at Moscow State University) together with his younger brother tried for quite a long time to generalize this problem when there are $n$ “herrings”, and only $k$ of them are allowed to look and change. In the end, they even came up with two different solutions. Here is how B. Yusin formulated the problem in general terms:

Petya and Vasya are playing the following game. They have a plate in the shape of a regular $n$-gon, in the corners of which there are $n$ switches covered with caps. Petya wants all switches to be either on or off at the same time. To do this, he can open any $k$ caps and put each of these $k$ switches in any position of his choice, after which he must respond, while Vasya, meanwhile, closes the caps and can rotate the plate to any angle. Then Petya can do the same again, etc. Petya's initial position is unknown. If he adds his own, Vasya tells him this as soon as he answers. At what $k$ and $n$ does Petya have a winning strategy?

Try to solve this problem too. The answer turns out to be:

$$\pi k = \frac{8\pi n}{3}.$$ From it we obtain the equation $3k = 8n$, which has a solution $k = 8t, n = 3t$. So, the answer to the problem looks like this:

$$\{8\pi t \mid t \in \mathbb{Z}\}.$$
