Brust's interference experiment

Experimental setup

In 1817, the English physicist, member of the Korpel Society of London D. Brewster (1781-1868) discovered that if two flat-parallel glass plates of the same thickness are placed almost parallel to each other, then, looking through them, one can observe an interference pattern.

The optical scheme of Brust's experiment is depicted in figure 1. Light falling from an accumulation lamp S onto a sheet of white paper B is scattered on it and, passing through plane-parallel plates P₁ and P₂, enters the eye Γ observed. The interference pattern is visible on the black background of the opaque screen E.

To repeat Brust's experiment, you must first select a suitable glass: the plates P₁ and P₂ must be plane-parallel and installed parallel to each other. Glass with a thickness of about 7 mm, which is often used to make mirrors and shop windows, is the most affordable and is often fully suitable for conducting subtle interference experiments. Having taken out a piece of such glass, place it in front of your eye so that some dark object with clear boundaries is reflected in it against a light background (for example, the frame of a window frame is reflected against the background of the daytime sky). Focus (accommodate) your eye on the surface of the glass so that the border of the image of the dark object is visible. If, at the same time, the smooth edges of the object are visible slightly wavy or “blurred” in some places, then it means that the surfaces of the glass are not smooth and such glass is not suitable for experiments. It is usually possible to select glass whose surfaces are so smooth that the boundary between the image of a dark object and a light background appears completely sharp. This is the glass that should be used in experiments.

From the displayed glass, use a craft or diamond glass cutter to cut a piece approximately 80 × 70 mm in size and cut it in half, so that you get two plates P₁ and P₂ measuring 40 × 70 mm. These plates, very accurately, have the same thickness near the cut line with high precision. Also prepare a third glass plate C of the same thickness as the first two, measuring 30 × 40 mm.

To avoid injury from the sharp edges of the plates when working, sand them (wet them with water and work them on an abrasive block or wheel). Be careful not to scratch the surfaces of the records. Wash the treated glass plates with water, wipe dry with a clean soft cloth and remove any remaining dust and lint from their surfaces.

A little theory

Let a light ray (Fig. 4) fall on a flat glass plate of thickness $d$ at a certain angle $\varphi$. As a result of multiple reflections and refractions of light on the surface of the plate, many parallel and equally spaced rays will emerge from it. It is clear that the intensity of each subsequent beam emerging from the plate is less than the intensity of the previous one and quickly decreases with increasing serial number of the beam. Rays 1 and 2, which indicate the direction of propagation of light waves, were obtained from one beam incident on the plate. Therefore, they are coherent. This means that light waves traveling in the directions of these rays, being illuminated together, must interfere.

Let us calculate the difference in path between beams 1 and 2, which occurs after passing the first plate (Fig. 4). Let us draw a segment AB from point B, perpendicular to these rays. The difference in the path traveled by coherent waves from the point of their formation D to points B and A (waves going further to infinity do not acquire an additional path difference) is equal to the geometric path difference

$$\delta = DC + CB - AD = 2DC - AD.$$

If a glass plate has a transmissibility index $n$, then the light wave travels the DCB path in the glass at a speed $n$ times less than the speed of light in air, moving in which it would cover a distance $2n \cdot DC$ in the same time. In addition, when light is reflected at point D from an optically denser medium, a “half-wave loss” will occur (a change in the phase of the reflected wave by π). Therefore, the true, or, as they say, optical path difference between coherent waves is equal to

$$\Delta = 2n \cdot DC - AD + \frac{\lambda}{2}.$$ (1)

From geometric messages

$$DC = \frac{d}{\cos \psi}, BD = 2DC \cdot \sin \psi = 2d \cdot \frac{\sin \psi}{\cos \psi},$$

$$AD = BD \cdot \sin \varphi = 2d \cdot \frac{\sin \varphi}{\cos \varphi} \cdot \sin \varphi.$$

According to the law of predominance of light

$$\sin \psi = \frac{1}{n} \sin \varphi.$$

Substituting the found values ​​of DC and AD into formula (1) and taking into account the law of pre-sufficiency, we obtain

$$\Delta = 2n \frac{d}{\cos \psi} - 2d \frac{\sin \psi}{\cos \psi} \cdot \sin \varphi + \frac{\lambda}{2} = 2d \cdot \sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 \varphi} + \frac{\lambda}{2}.$$ (2)

The light intensity maxima (light fringe interference) will occur if the optical path difference is equal to an integer number of light wavelengths:

$$2d \sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 \varphi} + \frac{\lambda}{2} = k\lambda, k = 1, 2, \dots$$ (3)

Passing the plate $P_2$, rays 1 and 2 acquire an additional path difference, which, as follows from the symmetry of the device, is also determined by formula (2). It is not difficult to report that the difference in the path of light waves passing through both plates and inducing them into the eye is observed (see Fig. 1), expressed by the formula

$$\Delta = \Delta_1 - \Delta_2 = 2d \left( \sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 \varphi_1} - \sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 \varphi_2} \right),$$ (4)

where $\varphi_1$ is the angle of incidence of light from the source on plate $P_1$, and $\varphi_2$ is the angle of incidence of parallel rays 1 and 2 on plate $P_2$.

Using “geometric” calculations, formula (4) can be reduced to the form

$$\Delta = \alpha d \frac{\sin 2\varphi}{\sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 \varphi}},$$

where $\alpha$ are two angles between the plates.

Experiments and observations

Carefully examine the resulting interface picture in the Brewster installation. If you have successfully selected the glass plates and the angle between them, you will see almost straight multi-colored stripes parallel to each other, located symmetrically on both sides of the central white stripe. Next to this white stripe are the two darkest stripes.

The central white stripe corresponds to those angles of light incidence on the plates at which the optical path difference between the interfacing waves is zero. Indeed, in general the interface condition for intensity maxima has the form

$$\Delta = k\lambda, \quad k = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$$

If $k = 0$, then $\Delta = 0$ for any wavelength. This means that at the same angles corresponding to $\Delta = 0$, intensity maxima are given by all monochromatic waves that make up white light. This will cause the central intensity maximum (zero-order maximum) to appear white.

All other interface bands are colored, with the red parts of the bands located further from the central maximum than the phyllo or blue parts.

Insert a strip of black paper 5 mm wide between plates $P_1$ and $P_2$. By mixing it, you can block one of the interface beams and thereby destroy the interface picture.

It's better to do it this way. To the edges of the plates $P_1$ and $P_2$ plate, attach a horizontal or slightly inclined strip of cardboard located between them, separating the bundles from the other. Then, by covering one of the bundles of opaque paper, you can make the picture disappear.

Now, together with strips of paper, insert a strip of stack 1-2 mm thick into one of the interfering beams. The interface picture will disappear forever! Try to explain the result of the experiment yourself.

Place a small spiral made of thin nichrome wire between plates $P_1$ and $P_2$. Having connected the ends of the spiral using stranded conductors to the battery, heat the spiral. In this case, the interface strips will bend and take on the most bizarre shape!

You can perform a simpler version of this experiment if you bring the flame of a burning match under the plates $P_1$ and $P_2$ (the flame should be located far enough from the plates so that only heat from the air flame gets into the gap between them).

What explains the result of the experiment? Obviously, when air is heated, its density changes, and therefore its refractive index. This leads to the fact that an additional path difference arises between the core beams, and the interface picture is mixed. Let us assume that the picture is mixed by $k$ stripes. This means that the path difference has changed by $k$ wavelengths of light. Since the wavelength of the light is very small, very small changes in the path difference and the refractive index of air can be changed in the experiment.

Devices used to measure various physical quantities using light interface are called optical interfaces. They allow arbitrary changes with a very high degree of precision. The device for observing physical Brewster bands described in the article is the simplest interface.
