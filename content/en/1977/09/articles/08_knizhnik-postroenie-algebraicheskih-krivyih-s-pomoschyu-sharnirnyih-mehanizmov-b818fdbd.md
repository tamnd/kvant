Construction of algebraic curves using hinge mechanisms

This note was born from a report made by a Moscow schoolboy, 9th grade student Vadim Knizhnik at a school conference. It tells how, using mechanisms consisting of slotted strips and screws, you can build graphs of various functions.

Mechanisms

We will call a hinge mechanism a system of strips with slots and holes connected by screws. In this case, the slots and holes are located strictly along the axis of the bar. When connected with a screw, strips with holes can only be rotated around the screw, and strips with slots can be rotated and shifted (Fig. 1; connection designations are shown in figure).

These mechanisms work like this. Two perpendicular strips are fixed on the plane, forming a rectangular coordinate system $Oxy$. Mechanisms are attached to these screw bars, which have one “input” - a hole moving along the $Ox$ axis, and one “output” - a hole that is always located on the same vertical as the input, but at a height depending on the position of the input. If you insert a pencil into the “output” and draw the “input” along the $Ox$ axis, then the pencil will draw a curve - a graph of some.

For further purposes, it is useful to maintain another “corner” block, consisting of three planks (with holes) fastened with vintimi. This block, when screwed with the same screws to any two slats with slots, allows them only to move, maintaining a constant angle between themselves (designated at figure - angle).

Examples

figure 2, $a$ shows a mechanism with input $A$ and output $B$, plotting the graph of function $y=x$. It is based on the fact that a rectangle whose diagonal forms an angle $45^\circ$ with the base is a square. figure 2, $b$ shows a mechanism that plots the constant $(y=c)$. We will denote the mechanism that builds the graph of the function $f$ (Fig. 2, $\theta$) on figure by a wavy line connecting the input $A$ and the output $B$, in the middle of the wavy line we will write $f$ in a circle, at the exit we will put an arrow; and in the text we will denote such a mechanism with the letter $f$.

So we already have two functions. Let's learn how to multiply, add, divide functions and extract the root of a function.

Blocks of algebraic operations

Addition block. By definition, let us call an addition block a method of connecting mechanisms $f$ and $g$, in which point $C$—output $g$—writes out the graph of function $y(x) = f(x) + g(x)$. The addition block is depicted at figure Pro. Abscissa axis

Multiplication block. This is a way of connecting mechanisms $f$ and $g$, in which the output $D$ writes out the graph of the function $y(x) = f(x) \cdot g(x)$. The multiplication block is depicted at figure 36. Here $|AM| = 1$, $(PD) \parallel (CM)$, so

$$\frac{|AD|}{|AP|} = \frac{|AC|}{|AM|}.$$

But $|AP| = |AB|$, $|AM| = 1$, hence $|AD| = |AC| \cdot |AB| = f(x) \times g(x)$.

Division block. This block is obtained from the multiplication block if output $f$ is moved to point $D$; then at point $C$ the output of mechanism $f/g$ will be obtained.

Root extraction block. This block is obtained from the multiplication block, if points $B$ and $C$ are combined, and the output of mechanism $f$ is placed at point $D$, then at point $C$ the output of mechanism $\sqrt{f(x)}$ is obtained. Block $\sqrt[n]{f(x)}$ is constructed in the same way.

Control charts

Using the described mechanisms and blocks, you can construct mechanisms for constructing graphs of various functions such as polynomials, algebraic fractions, etc. It is also possible to create a mechanism for constructing a control graph $P(x,y) = 0$, where $P(x,y)$ is some polynomial. To do this, the mechanism shown in figure 4 is first assembled. Points $X$ and $Y$ are considered as outputs for “functions” $y = x$, $y = y$. Then the mechanism $P(x,y)$ is assembled, the inputs of which are $X$ and $Y$, and the output is placed at point $C$ (that is, the position of point $Y$ is such that the output is at point $C$). After this, the point $Y$ can only move in such a way that for its coordinates $x$, $y$ the equality $P(x,y) = 0$ is valid. If you stand at point $Y$ with a pencil, then it can only move according to the control schedule $P(x,y) = 0$.
