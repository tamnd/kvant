Six meter telephone

In 1609, Galileo for the first time directed a homemade telescope, made up of two spectacle glasses, to the new sky and obtained an enlarged image of celestial objects. This spotting scope provided only three times magnification. The very next year, Galileo made two more telecopes, then several more. The best of the telecopes magnified 30 times. With this instrument, Galileo made a number of solid discoveries: he saw the four-moon Jupiter, discovered the phases of Venus (resembling the phases of the Moon), noticed mountains on the Moon, and “decomposed” the Milky Way into many faint stars invisible to the naked eye.

What is phone magnification and what does it depend on? The Galileo telescope consists of two main parts: the lens (facing towards the object) and the eyepiece (facing towards the eye). The objective was a converging lens, and the eyepiece was a diverging lens, and these lenses were positioned so that their rear foci coincided. The path of rays in such a telephone (now usually called a Galilean tube) is shown in figure 1. A parallel beam of rays from a distant source, after refraction in the lens, becomes converging, and upon exiting the eyepiece it turns out to be parallel again. Then falling into the eye of the observer, the rays converge on the retina and give a real image of the source. The angle α′ at which the rays exit the telescope is greater than the angle α that the rays making with the axis fall on the lens (or are observed directly into the eye). In other words, the spotting scope increases the angle at which the source is seen. This increase is equal to (see Fig. 1).

$$K = \frac{tg \alpha'}{tg \alpha} = \frac{F_1}{F_2},$$

where $F_1$ is the focal length of the lens, and $F_2$ is the eyepiece.

Galileo's contemporary Kepler replaced the diverging eyepiece with a short-focus collecting lens, which plays the role of rays when viewing the image of an enlarged source created by the lens. The path of rays in the Kepler tube is shown in figure 2. The advantage of this tube in comparison with the Galilean tube is that it gives a real image in the focal plane of the lens, where a measuring scale or photographic plastic can be placed. The magnification of the Kepler tube, like the Galilean tube, is equal to the ratio of the focal lengths of the lens and eyepiece.

Both Galileo's telephone and Kepler's telephone were subsequently called refractors (from the Latin word frango - I break). The desire to obtain the highest possible magnification led to the construction of very long telescopes, which affected the quality of the image: its brightness decreased. This could be avoided by increasing the diameter of the object, that is, increasing the amount of light passing through the phone *). However, an increase in the size of the object leads to a sharp deterioration in the clarity of the images - they become blurry and with colored stones. This is due to the disadvantages of lenses - primarily spherical and chromatic aberration.

For the device of chromatic aberration, the Englishman Gregory in 1663 proposed using as an object not a lens, but a wave mirror, which was free from chromatism, since, as Newton put it, “the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection for all colors.” Moreover, in order to avoid spherical aberration, the reflecting surface of the mirror must have the shape of a parabolide of rotation, which coincides with one point of rays parallel to the axis of the parabolide. Quite soon they learned how to grind a mirror into the desired shape. However, for a long time they did not know how to cover the surface of glass with a light-reflecting layer of silver, and mirrors were made from a special alloy of copper and tin, which is highly polished and has sufficient reflectivity. It was proposed to use your own lens as an eyepiece.

Gregory failed to make his proposed mirror telexan - a reflector, which received its name from the Latin reflecto - bent backwards. It was made by Newton in 1668, but it was not until 1722 that John Hadley in England and then Short in Edinburgh began making parabolic reflectors that were as good as the largest refractors of the time.

Since then, a debate has begun between two fundamentally different telex designs - between refractors and reflectors. Refractors were more difficult to make: firstly, it was necessary to grind four surfaces of two lenses and, secondly, higher demands were placed on the material - optically homogeneous glass. If reflectors were much superior to refractors in terms of optical parameters, they were much inferior to them in terms of ease of observation and versatility of applications. Larger and less bulky refractors have long since become first a special mount, in which one of the two mutually perpendicular axes is installed parallel to the axis of the world. Uniform rotation around this axis at a speed of one revolution per day allows you to monitor the visible movement of the starry sky and keep the observed object in the field of view all the time. The other axis serves to direct the telescope to any point in the sky.

As for the reflectors, due to their enormous size and weight, they were mounted much more simply and could be used for observations only near the meridian, turning in the most practical way with the help of blocks and ropes; the observed one had to be piled up in extremely inconvenient places. In addition, the disadvantage of reflectors was that the metal mirrors would wear out due to weathering, which required repeated difficult polishing.

Thus, reflectors did not give up their positions and were used where it was necessary to make precise angular measurements - in meridian instruments for determining the spherical coordinates of stars, in micrometric measurements, for example, of double stars, and later - for photographing the starry sky.

Reflectors received new opportunities in the sixties, when the German chemist Liebig found a way to coat glass with a thin layer of shiny silver. At the same time, the French physicist Foucault began making reflector mirrors from glass, followed by silvering the reflecting surface. Such mirrors were much lighter than metal ones, which made it possible to use a special mount for them. In case of tarnishing, the silver layer was easily washed off with nitric acid, and the mirror was again silvered chemically without additional polishing.

As a result, the rapid development of reflectors began at the end of the last century. However, one of the first was installed in 1895, the 36-inch (92 cm) Crosslevsky reflector of the Lick Osservatory in the USA, with which a number of remarkable photographs were obtained of many nebulae, stars,

By this time, the scope of application of large reflectors was finally determined. These are, on the one hand, photographic observations of the faintest and most distant objects - nebulae (mainly extragalactic, located at distances of many tens and hundreds of millions of light years), and on the other hand, photographic spectra for the study of the physical properties and chemical state and their atmospheres. This also includes the discovery and study of spectroscopic double stars and flaring novae. For all these purposes, you need to collect as much light as possible, which can really be done with the help of large mirrors. In addition, for spectral observations, the complete achromaticity of the reflectors is important.

The great successes achieved with the help of the mentioned reflectors raised the question of creating even more powerful tools. So, in 1917, a 100-inch (254 cm) reflector came into operation, also on the basis of which an innovation was applied: the polar axis was flipped by two large floats, half immersed in a semi-syndrical mixture with mercury, which achieved very easy and smooth rotation (Fig. 6).

The main telephoto mirror, 6 m in diameter, was cast and processed at a glass factory near Moscow, for which two special workshops were built there. After being cast from a special grade of low-expansion glass, the red-hot mass was slowly cooled by electronic heating to avoid the development of harmful stresses that would normally cause a glass dish to rupture. This “annealing” process usually lasts for at least a year, only after which it is possible to find out whether the casting has been removed or not.

The huge and complex installation of the telephoto cameras was carried out at the optical-mechanical plant in Leningrad with the participation of a number of other Leningrad heavy engineering plants. The result is an instrument that is comparable in size and weight to the largest metal structures, and in accuracy and subtlety is not inferior to technical microscopes.
