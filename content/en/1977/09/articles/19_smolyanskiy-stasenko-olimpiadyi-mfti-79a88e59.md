MIPT Olympids

Various mathematical competitions in the history of mathematics have been known for a long time. History has brought down to us information about mathematical show jumping conducted back in the 13th century.

The history of school physics and mathematics Olympids starting in 1894, when the first mathematical Olympiad for schoolchildren was held in Hungary, was held in Leningrad in the spring of 1934. The initiators of this Olympiad were Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences B. N. Delaunay (Chairman of the Olympiad Organizing Committee) and Professor V. A. Tartakovsky.

On the initiative of the Moscow Mathematical Society, in the fall of 1935, the first Moscow Mathematical Olympid for schoolchildren was held. Moscow mathematicians greeted her with great enthusiasm. The organizing committee included leading Moscow mathematicians.

Until the Great Patriotic War, mathematical Olympids were held in Moscow every day and very soon gained universal recognition. During the war in Moscow, mathematics Olympids were held in Ashgabat and Kazan. After the Patriotic War, holding mathematical Olympiads became a tradition in many cities of the Soviet Union.

Following the mathematicians, physicists, chemists, astronomers, geographers, biologists, and then linguistics began to conduct their Olympids.

The first Olympids in physics were held by Moscow University back in 1938. Since then, these Olympids have become a tradition for Moscow schoolchildren.

Subsequently, such Olympids began to be held by the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology (MIPT) and other universities in Moscow. Moskvychny’s initiative was picked up in other cities of the country.

In February 1962, the first “big” physics and mathematics Olympid for schoolchildren took place, conducted on the initiative of MIPT. Over 6,500 schoolchildren from 58 cities of the Soviet Union took part in it. It was held in one round during the winter holidays.

The book begins with a story about the history of the Olympics. The authors of the book directly took part in holding “big” Olympiads, and they have something to tell readers. This chapter, written vividly and experientially, is a good starter for the book.

The second and third chapters contain formulations of problems corresponding to mathematics and physics, offered at the MIPT Olympiads. The collected problems are only part of the many problems that have been offered at the Olympiads over 15 years.

It should be emphasized that most of the tasks are not standard and are generally quite difficult.

Many problems are formulated in a very intricate and artificial way: not only different animals, insects and fish participate in the formulations, but even ... ghosts. But the planner's formulation hides very meaningful and difficult mathematical problems. So one very small task is connected with Ali Baba and... herrings. In itself, it is very difficult - you need to come up with a strategy, following which you can bring all the herrings to the same position for a fixed number of moves (problem 98).

Following the division of the 1975 International Olympiad Borya Yusin (he is a student at Moscow State University) together with his younger brother tried for quite a long time to generalize this problem when there are $n$ “herrings”, and only $k$ of them are allowed to look and change. In the end, they even came up with two different solutions. Here is how B. Yusin formulated the problem in general terms:

Petya and Vasya are playing the following game. They have a plate in the shape of a regular $n$-gon, in the corners of which there are $n$ switches covered with caps. Petya wants all switches to be either on or off at the same time. To do this, he can open any $k$ caps and put each of these $k$ switches in any position of his choice, after which he must respond, while Vasya, meanwhile, closes the caps and can rotate the plate to any angle. Then Petya can do the same again, etc. Petya's initial position is unknown. If he adds his own, Vasya tells him this as soon as he answers. At what $k$ and $n$ does Petya have a winning strategy?

Try to solve this problem too. The answer turns out to be:

$$\pi k = \frac{8\pi n}{3}.$$ From it we obtain the equation $3k = 8n$, which has a solution $k = 8t, n = 3t$. So, the answer to the problem looks like this:

$$\{8\pi t \mid t \in \mathbb{Z}\}.$$

**Mathematics**

1. Given an angle on a plane. Find the set of circles circumscribed around the circle of weights of impoverished triangles, whose vertices at the base lie on opposite sides of this angle, and the third vertex lies at a given point A on its bisector.

2. Diagonals are drawn in a convex quadrilateral ABCD. Prove that a quadrilateral with vertices at the intersection points of the medians of triangles ABC, ABD, ACD and BCD is similar to the given one.

3. At the base of a quadrangular straight line there is a rhombus with an angle of 60° at vertex A. The lateral edge emerging from vertex A is equal to the sides of the rhombus. Prove that the remaining lateral edges can be used to form a right triangle.

4. Two pieces are placed on a chessboard of 8x8 cells. Two players take turns making moves for each of their pieces. The first player's piece can only move one square vertically or horizontally for each sede. The second chip is one cell vertically or horizontally, only one cell diagonally. The one who places his fikuku on the parterre fikuku wins. Who wins when played correctly?

5. n players play the following game: there are (2n + 1) checkers, of which 2k are black, the rest are white. The players sit around the table and each of them receives two checkers. By drawing lots, one of the players takes the remaining checker. If he has three checkers of the same color, then he wins, and the game ends; if not, then he keeps the checkers of the same color for himself, and gives the third to his neighbor. If he has three checkers of the same color, then he wins, if not, then he does the same as the first one, etc.

Prove that for k not equal to n, the game will end in a finite number of checker transfers (moves). Find the maximum number of moves in this game.

8. Estimate the minimum permissible daily productivity for a cryta with mass M and radius R.

9. If the internal combustion engine is not properly adjusted, sometimes instead of the relatively slow combustion of the fuel mixture, so-called “detonation” occurs, when the mixture burns quickly with an explosion. Why does the efficiency drop? movements?
