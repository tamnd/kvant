At first we didn’t even dare to ask because we didn’t understand anything. But understanding came quite quickly, apparently because we got used to the style of presentation and read a lot ourselves.

At our faculty, of course, we had educational student seminars. One of them was led by Professor Rozhansky, and the other by Professor Frenkel. At these seminars, the most modern issues in physics were discussed, and we quickly learned what problems physicists were concerned about. In particular, in 1927, at the seminar of Professor Rozhansky, the just published work of Davisson and Germer was discussed, in which it was for the first time experimentally shown that when electric currents of crystals are reflected, a diffraction pattern is observed, as in the scattering of ordinary light itself on a diffraction grating. We knew that light diffraction occurs because light is a wave. But the electron was considered a normal particle, subject to the law of mechanics. And at first it seemed simply impossible to understand why the reflection of electric currents occurs as if not a beam of particles, but a beam of light falls on the surface of the crystal. We discussed this question at Rozhansky’s seminar, where the experience of Davisson and Germer was discussed in detail (reported by students) and its interpretation was discussed. This experiment was the first convincing evidence of the validity of the proposal made in 1924 by the French physicist de Broglie that moving electrons exhibit the properties not only of particles (corpuscles), but also of waves, just as electromagnetic radiation, for example, light, has the properties of both waves and particles. And de Broglie also proposed that the correspondence between corpuscular characteristics and wave characteristics should be the same for electric currents and radiation. The experiment of Davisson and Germer showed that electrons actually behave like waves when reflected from the surface of a crystal, and that the wavelength λ corresponding to a moving electron is equal to h/mv, where h is Planck’s constant, m is the mass of the electron, v is its speed. Nowadays this has become trivial.

At Frenkel's seminar, a question that worried the physicist of time was also discussed. The question concerned not individual atoms and not individual electrons, but the structure of matter. We already knew that atoms attract each other as long as the distance between them is approximately equal to the size of the atoms. And with further convergence of atoms, repulsive forces begin to act between them. But it was not clear why atoms are arranged in a solid, forming a regular crystal lattice.

At that time, there was quite a bit of research into solid state theories. Einstein started the creation of the modern solid state theory.
