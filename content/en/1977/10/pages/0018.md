ZERMELO'S AXIOM is a statement used by the Italian mathematician Zermelo that for any collection of pairwise disjoint sets, a new set can be constructed by selecting one element from each set. Despite its educational value, this statement (also called the axim of arbitrary choice) leads to very paradoxical results (for example, to the statement that a sphere can be divided into 4 sets that are congruent with each other so that from two sets one can obtain a sphere and rotate them relative to the center). A number of studies of the Moscow mathematical scale were devoted to finding out how a scientist works with an 18-year-old student who is just beginning to interact with mathematics. After listening to me, Luzin, through skillfully posed questions, very quickly understood the nature of my mathematical inclinations and immediately, in a form accessible to me, outlined the main directions that he could offer me for further studies; very carefully, he himself persuaded me to choose one of these directions, and all this was done subtly, without any pressure and, as I can now say, correctly. At that time I became Luzin’s student.

My acquaintance with Luzin happened quite precisely in the middle of the decade in which he obtained his most significant results. He then lived completely alone in furnished rooms (Kokorevskoe farmstead on Balchug), lived only by science. I remember one phrase of his, said during one of our many meetings: “I think day and night about Zermelo’s axiom (the axiom of arbitrary choice in set theory). If only someone knew what this thing is!”

Seeing Luzin during these years, I really saw what can be called an inspired attitude towards science, and I not only learned mathematics from him, but also received a lesson in what a real scientist is, as well as what a university professor should be.

Then I realized that science and the acquisition of new young people to it are two sides of the same activity - the activity of a scientist. All this remains with me for the rest of my life. Pedagogical activities at Moscow University were carried out by the Egorov-Luzin school in several directions. D. F. Egorov taught mandatory courses in differential geometry, differential equations and calculus of variations. In addition, he taught a number of special courses, which, although not mandatory, were very popular and were attended by a large number of students. D. F. Egorov’s courses could also serve as the first introduction to functional analysis, which was just emerging at that time.

N.N. Luzin read every day one or another course on the theory of functions of a real (sometimes complex) variable, changing its special topics, but each time bringing the presentation to questions that directly interested him and that constitute the day of science in this area today.
