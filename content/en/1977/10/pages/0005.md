To train such specialists, Ioffe created the Faculty of Physics and Mechanics at the Leningrad Polytechnic Institute. I entered this faculty in 1925.

It was difficult to study at the faculty, because physico-mathematical disciplines were taught at the university level, and at the same time students must study engineering disciplines. Upon entering the institute, I immediately immersed myself in the atmosphere of real science.

We, students, knew that our dean was a scientist of signs, whose effect he discovered was named. The Ioffe effect is that when the surface of a rock coli (NaCl) crystal is wetted with water, its strength increases several hundred times. The results of the incredibly easy-to-use experiment conducted by Ioffe were extremely important. And here's why.

In 1915, the remarkable German physicist Max Born created the theory of crystals. This theory explains the optical, electrical and other properties of various crystals. Numerous experimental data available at that time confirmed the validity of the theory. And only in one area did the theory sharply diverge from experiment—in assessing the strength of crystals. It was known, for example, that a rock coli crystal, when stretched, collapses when the directions resulting from deformation in it are approximately 4.5 10⁶ H/m². (Stress σ is called the relative elastic force |Fγуп| to the area S of the cross section of the sample, perpendicular to the direction of deformation: σ = |Fγуп|/S. The value of σ at which the sample fails is called the tensile strength.) But the theoretical calculation gave a different estimate to the performance -

A.F. Ioffe proposed that the model of crystal destruction considered in theory differs from what happens as a result of the crystal during its deformation. Theoretical calculations of the read limit were made within the limits that the crystal is destroyed instantly over the entire cross-section of the sample. Ioffe proposed that the destruction of the crystal occurs gradually. It starts in some “weakest” place, and then spreads along the cross section of the sample. Gradual destruction is due to the fact that there are microscopic cracks on the surface of the crystal. From such a crack, destruction begins in directions, of course, less than the theoretical values ​​of the ultimate strength. The crack gradually develops and leads to rupture of the entire crystal.
