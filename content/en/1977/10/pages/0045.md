Here is one of the shortest solutions sent by readers. Let p ≠ 1. (The case p=1 is trivial: equation [x] + p {x} = q
(1) turns into x = q.) Then (1) is equivalent

$$[x] + px - p[x] = q \Leftrightarrow [x] = \frac{px - q}{p - 1}.$$

Taking y = (px - q)/(p - 1) intersects the edges of the strip bounded by the lines y = x and y = x - 1 and conclude the graph y = [x], at points A₀(q; q) and A₁(q - p + 1; q - p) (figure 2 and 3; to find the abscissas x₀, x₁ of these points, you need just solve the equation px₀ - q = x₀(p - 1) and px₁ - q = (x₁ - 1)(p - 1)). It is clear that the number of solutions to equation (2), that is, the number of horizontal lines y = h (h is an integer), intersected by the segment [A₀A₁] [(without point A₁), is equal to the number of integers enclosing between q and q - p (including q, if it is an integer, but not including q - p). Now it is not difficult to give the answer in problem a): the segment [1978; 1978 + 1977] contains 1977 integer points, - and prove the statement of problem 6): on any segment of length |p| number line lies [|p|] or [|p|] + 1 integer points.

You can solve this problem by leaving x and {x} (the “fractional part” of x) in the equation. Some readers who have gone this route have made mistakes in defining {x} for x < 0 (note that {-0.1} = 0.9; {-√2} = -2 + √2, etc.).
