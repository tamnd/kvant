However, this mixing is not observed in the experiment. It follows that if there are forces that orient “elementary magnets” in individual domains, then they are not magnetic, because they have practically no effect on a moving electron.

And only in 1928, Ya. I. Frenkel at the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology and almost simultaneously with him the German physicist Heisenberg created the quantum mechanical theory of ferromagnetism. They showed that in fact there is no molecular field. The forces that orient “elementary magnets” within domains are electrical forces of a specific nature. They are now called exchange forces. A short article about this was published by Frenkel in 1928 in the Ionian issue of the German physical journal Zeitshrift für Physik, and a detailed article by Heisenberg appeared in the Iolian issue of the same journal. So, independently of each other, they created the theory of ferromagnetism. (Later, Yakov Ilyich Frenkel told me that he took up ferromagnetism because we, experimenters, were constantly “magnetizing” him).

After the creation of the theory of ferromagnetism, many works appeared on the issues of magnetism in metals. In particular, it was shown that, as follows from the theory, exchange forces can arise only in crystals. There cannot be liquid ferromagnetic materials. It was also shown that the exchange forces are different along different directions inside the crystal. However, it has been shown that there is a pronounced anisotropy of the magnetic properties of ferromagnetic crystals. This is still of great importance in the creation of new magnetic materials, on which all electrical engineering is based. Transformers, generators, and electric motors are all made of ferromagnetic materials, and their characteristics significantly depend on the properties of the ferromagnetic material itself. Somewhat later, the theory of ferromagnetism was developed by our outstanding theoretical Lev Davydovich Landdu and his student Evgeniy Mikhailovich Lifshits. Teller's modern theory of ferromagnetism is entirely based on the work of Frenkel, Heisenberg, Landdu and Lifshitz.

As I already said, we had educational seminars at the Faculty of Physics and Mechanics. However, such seminars were organized for 4th-5th year students. This seminar was called calculation, and it was led by our professor, academician Vladimir Aleksadrovich Fok. The seminar was not entirely straightforward. Fok would come up with some tricky problem, the solution to which he himself did not know. And right there at the doxy, he and we (or rather, we together with him) began to solve it. So we could observe, so to speak, the “creative kitchen” of such a wonderful theorist as V. A. Fok.

And so we saw how he approached solving the problem: he tried one way - it didn’t work; he washed it with doxie, tried another method - it didn’t work either. In the end, sooner or later, a solution was found. Sometimes in the same lesson, sometimes in the next. We saw not just a ready-made solution, but how the paths to this solution were gradually found.
