In A. Fok worked on various problems of theoretical physics. But with this in mind, he did not hesitate to solve applied problems, purely practical ones. In particular, he was attracted by geophysicists to solve a number of problems about the distribution of the electric field inside the Earth. This decision was very important for geological exploration. Fock solved this problem together with V.K. Fredericks. In general, it was characteristic of the entire style of work of the Leningrad Physical Technical Institute that, with the development of theoretical and experimental problems in physics, more attention was paid to solving these applied problems related to the needs of technology, industry and agriculture.

In 1930, when I had just graduated from college and received the title of engineer-physicist, on the recommendation of A.F. Ioffe, I was sent on a business trip to Germany to get acquainted with the physics laboratories of the West. I visited about three places in Germany and was able to get acquainted with the work of physics laboratories in Leipzig, Monchen, and Hamburg. And I must say that I was very pleased when I became convinced that the level of our physicists, in particular my own level, was in no way lower than the level of physicists I met in laboratories abroad. True, they had some advantages over us: their laboratories were very well equipped with instruments. We in the Soviet Union did not yet have domestic instrument making; we often had to make instruments ourselves or buy them abroad, and this was difficult in those days when more funds were spent on the development of the national economy. But in terms of the level of knowledge, the level of understanding, even the technical level of the experiment, we were, as I was convinced, no lower than the largest university laboratories in Germany.

While on a business trip, I worked for about a month at the laboratorium in Monchen, in the former laboratorium of Roentgen. University doctoral students also worked there (that’s what they called students graduating from university who were preparing their dissertations). However, I noticed that doctoral students, preparing for final exams, read Ya. I. Frenkel’s book “Course in Electrodynamics,” published in German. I asked: “Who will you give the exams to?” They answered: “Sommerfeld.” That same Sommerfeld, the world's greatest theoretician, about whom, when we were students, there was a saying “there is no Bohr but Bohr, and Sommerfeld is his vice.” I knew that there was a fifth course in physics by Sommerfeld himself, and I asked why doctoral students teach electrodynamics not according to Sommerfeld, but according to Frenkel. Therefore, they replied, Sommerfeld said that he would take the exam only on Frenkel’s course, since there is no better course in the world now. When I said that I personally knew Frenkel, I felt that my authority in their eyes increased sharply. And I felt a feeling of true pride in our Soviet physicists, who have earned wide recognition among the world's leading theoretical scientists.
