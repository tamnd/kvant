When a current is passed through the liquid metal in a magnetic field, forces begin to act on the metal from the magnetic field, which lead to the mixing of individual layers of liquid metal relative to each other. (In solid metal, of course, this mixing of layers does not occur.) As a result, output currents will arise that “lubricate” the Hall effect in the liquid metal. So it is necessary, if not to eliminate it, then to minimize these “parasitic” currents. To do this, we took a sample in the form of a thin layer of liquid metal. Careful measurements revealed the presence of the Hall effect. A year and a half later, Sommerfeld’s work was published, which described experiments confirming the modern theory of metals. Sommerfeld also referred to our experience. To tell the truth, I was unusually proud - such an authority as Sommerfeld appreciated my work.

There was another problem in the physics of metals, which then greatly worried theoretical physicists - the issue of ferromagnetism. It was well known that of all the metals that exist in nature, only three metals are ferromagnetic - iron, nickel and cobalt. (Now much more of them are known.) They are distinguished by the fact that their magnetization, which appears in a very weak magnetic field, is quite large, several hundred thousand times greater than that of ordinary metals. For example, in order to obtain in an ordinary metal the kind of magnetization that appears in an iron sample in a field of ~0.01 T, it is necessary to have an external magnetic field of ~1000 T. To explain this incomprehensible phenomenon, the French physicist Weiss created a theory according to which in ferromagnetic metals - in iron, nickel and cobalt - there are regions in which the magnetic fields of individual atoms are oriented exactly parallel to each other. So the total magnetic field of such a region - it is called a domain (from the French word “domain” - region) - is quite large. The dimensions of these regions are very small - on the order of fractions of a micron, but in each of them many hundreds of thousands of oriented “elementary magnets” are reduced. The domains themselves are perfectly distributed throughout the metal, so that in the general state (in the absence of an external magnetic field) the magnetization of the ferromagnetic sample is zero.

When such a sample is placed in a magnetic field, the magnetic fields of individual domains are oriented along the external field, and the sample as a whole is magnetized. And it is easier to orient entire domains than a huge number of individual “elementary magnets”.

This is, roughly speaking, what the theory of ferromagnetism proposed by Weiss looked like. In order to explain the presence of domains - the presence of a region with oriented "elementary magnets" - Weiss proposed the existence inside the metal of a special magnetic field that orients the magnetic fields inside the domains. This field - Weiss calls it a molecular magnetic field - according to Weiss's calculations should be on the order of 1000 T. However, the arrival of this field was not understood.

To discover this huge hypothetical field, Professor Yakov Grigorievich Dorfman, in whose laboratory I then worked at the Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute, continued this experiment. He placed thin iron foil in a magnetic field so that the plane of the foil sheet was parallel to the field induction. The foil became very magnetized. Fast electrons emitted by a radioactive source were passed through the foil, perpendicular to its surface, and therefore perpendicular to the external magnetic field, “cut out” by a slit installed in front of the foil (figure 2).
