The logical difficulties that arose could have been immediately avoided if we had looked at equation (1) carefully from the very beginning. Indeed, by substituting any number into its left side, for example, $x=2$ or $x=1/2$, we get an expression that contains an infinite number of exponentiations and then has no meaning. Try, for example, to answer the question, what is

$$2^2 \text{ или } \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)$$

Meanwhile, examples containing an infinite number of operations are found in some construction problems and, unfortunately, even in entrance exams.

In mathematics, we often encounter expressions containing an infinite number of operations. But such expressions are always given clear definitions in advance. The new school curriculum makes it possible in some cases to attribute meaning to the sum of an infinite number of terms (infinite series)
$$x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n + \dots$$
(see the textbook “Algebra and the beginnings of analysis 9”, pp. 67-68), as well as many other infinite expressions.

Using the concept of a limit, one can, for example, naturally give meaning to the expression

$$A = \sqrt{3\sqrt{3\sqrt{3}}}$$

(7)

Namely, consider the sequence $\{x_n\}$

$$x_1 = \sqrt{3}, x_2 = \sqrt{3\sqrt{3}},$$

$$x_3 = \sqrt{3\sqrt{3\sqrt{3}}}, \dots$$

and we will understand expression (7) as the limit of this sequence:

$$A = \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n$$

(if, of course, this limit exists). Let us show that it really exists. Since

$$x_n = 3^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot 3^{\frac{1}{4}} \cdot \dots \cdot 3^{\frac{1}{2^n}} =$$

$$= 3^{\frac{1}{2}} + \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n}$$

And

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n}\right) =$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} / (1 - \frac{1}{2}) = 1,$$

due to the nonprime nature of the exponential function $y=3^x$

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} 3^{\frac{1}{2}} + \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n} =$$

$$= 3^{\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n}\right)} = 3.$$

Therefore, expression (7) makes sense and is equal to 3.

Let us now try to give meaning to the expression

$$\sqrt{2} \sqrt{2} \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2} \sqrt{2} \sqrt{2}, \dots$$

(9)

In the article by A. Egorov “Equations and Limits” (p. 34) it is proven that the equation

$$(\sqrt{2})^x = x$$

(10)

has exactly two roots (since $1 < \sqrt{2} < 1, 42 < e^{\frac{1}{e}}$) and sequence (9) converges to the smaller of these roots. The roots of equation (10) can be easily found by selection: $x_1=2$, $x_2=4$. Consequently, the limit of sequence (9) is equal to 2, and if expression (8) gives the above meaning, then equality (2) is true, and (4) is false.
