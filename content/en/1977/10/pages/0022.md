FUNCTIONS OF A COMPLEX VARIABLE.
Functions that, in the neighborhood of any point $x_0$, where they are defined, are sums of series of the form
$$a_0 + a_1(x-x_0) + a_2(x-x_0)^2 + \dots + a_n(x-x_0)^n + \dots$$
(when the fixants of such a series are expressed through arbitrary original functions at the point $x_0$) are called analytic functions.
These include rational, exponential, logarithmic and trigonometric functions studied in school, as well as all functions found in applications of mathematics.
Discontinuous functions and functions whose graphs have breaks (for example, $y = |x|$ or $y = [x]$) are not analytic.
It is remarkable that two analytic functions that coincide on an extremely small interval are equal to each other by definition. The theory of analytic functions is revealed in its full beauty if it is considered not only for action values, but also for complete values ​​of the argument.
Therefore, this theory is also called the theory of complex variables. It is closely related to the theory of conformal mappings.
In a complex domain, it is possible to express the values ​​of an analytical function inside a certain domain in terms of its values ​​at the boundary of the domain. However, these boundary values ​​may turn out to be not only non-analytical, but even different.

I especially want to remember this now, in the year of the sixtieth anniversary of the Great October Revolution.

An essential element of N.N. Luzin’s communication with his students were meetings and conversations that N.N. Luzin had with very small groups of them (about 1-3 people, focusing on some topic). In addition, there was a weekly general training day for all Nikolai Nikolaevich’s students. However, these were Thursdays, then Wednesdays.

In addition to N.N. Luzin’s unrepresented students, V.V. Stepanov and sometimes I.I. Privalov often attended these weekly meetings at N.N. Luzin’s home. These evenings at N. N. Luzin’s were made up of two parts: first there was a mathematical part in Nikolai Nikolaevich’s office, a very cozy room in which, as, indeed, in N. N. Luzin’s entire apartment at that time (Arbat, 25, third floor), there was (at the owner’s request) kerosene lighting. I will never forget those conversations, rich in the most lively mathematics, that took place then. These conversations sometimes dragged on for a long time, but whenever they ended, they were followed by tea and the invariable very tasty cake. Beyond this - no longer in the office, but in the Luzins' dining room - the conversations took on a different, non-mathematical character and touched on a variety of issues of cultural and social life. Sometimes something was read dry. V.V. Stepanov read Blok’s poems with great enthusiasm, but less heartfelt things were read more often. So, for example, N.N. Luzin with great skill and artistry read the works of Korney Chukovsky, intended specifically for children. I remember once reading Anatole France’s story “The Miracle of St. Nicholas.”

The meetings with N.N. Luzin ended in dismay. After their completion, the participants of the large group went out onto the Arbat and its alleys and gradually, in various sequential combinations, seeing each other off, finally dispersed - usually by morning - to their homes.
