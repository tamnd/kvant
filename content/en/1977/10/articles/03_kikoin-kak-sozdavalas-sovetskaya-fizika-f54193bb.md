How Soviet physics was created

The editors turned to the Hero of Social Labor, Academician I. K. Kikoniu, with a request to tell about the main stages of the formation of Soviet physics. Isaac Konstantinovich Kikoni began studying physics in 1927 - the year of the tenth anniversary of Soviet power. He was fortunate to work with many wonderful scientists who can rightfully be considered the founders of Soviet physics. Therefore, his story is the story of an eyewitness and participant in the events described.

In this issue we will begin publishing the memoirs of academician I.K. Kikonia. The publication was prepared by T. Petrova and I. Slobodetsky.

From the very first years of its existence, the young Soviet state paid great attention to the development of domestic science. On the initiative and with the active support of Vladimir Ilyich Lenin, many new scientific centers were created in the country. One of them was the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology, founded in 1918 by academician Abram Fedorovich Ioffe. It was at this institute that he began to study physics.

Do revoilionn hayka in Russia developed mainly in universities and was divorced not only from practical life, but even from the present of modern science. Abram Fedorovich Ioffe says that when he began to study physics, the following point of view on the work of a physicist prevailed in Russian universities: the job of a scientist is to repeat experiments that were carried out abroad, and new ideas and independent work were considered unthinkable, because, they say, we do not have sufficiently trained scientists.

Back in 1918, through the smoke of the fire of the civil war, A.F. Ioffe was able to see the future outline of the socialist country and understand that the future was technically and industrially

We, students, knew that our dean was a scientist of signs, whose effect he discovered was named. The Ioffe effect is that when the surface of a rock coli (NaCl) crystal is wetted with water, its strength increases several hundred times. The results of the incredibly easy-to-use experiment conducted by Ioffe were extremely important. And here's why.

In 1915, the remarkable German physicist Max Born created the theory of crystals. This theory explains the optical, electrical and other properties of various crystals. Numerous experimental data available at that time confirmed the validity of the theory. And only in one area did the theory sharply diverge from experiment—in assessing the strength of crystals. It was known, for example, that a rock coli crystal, when stretched, collapses when the directions resulting from deformation in it are approximately 4.5 10⁶ H/m². (Stress σ is called the relative elastic force |Fγуп| to the area S of the cross section of the sample, perpendicular to the direction of deformation: σ = |Fγуп|/S. The value of σ at which the sample fails is called the tensile strength.) But the theoretical calculation gave a different estimate to the performance -

A.F. Ioffe proposed that the model of crystal destruction considered in theory differs from what happens as a result of the crystal during its deformation. Theoretical calculations of the read limit were made within the limits that the crystal is destroyed instantly over the entire cross-section of the sample. Ioffe proposed that the destruction of the crystal occurs gradually. It starts in some “weakest” place, and then spreads along the cross section of the sample. Gradual destruction is due to the fact that there are microscopic cracks on the surface of the crystal. From such a crack, destruction begins in directions, of course, less than the theoretical values ​​of the ultimate strength. The crack gradually develops and leads to rupture of the entire crystal.

The idea of ​​the experiment that A.F. Ioffe set up to test his proposal was incredibly simple. A thin single-crystal sample of rock coli was immersed

Many physicists believed that the model of crystal destruction proposed by Iofe did not correspond to reality. But repeated convincing experiments confirmed her rule. And the phenomenon of increasing the strength of crystals when their surface was smoothed, discovered by Iofe, was given the name “Iofe effect.”

At one of his popular lectures for first-year students, A.F. Iofe used his idea with the following simple experiments. He took a straight strip of paper and tried to tear it, directly stretching it with his hands. To do this, he had to make a significant effort. But I wanted to tear this strip a little across (that is, to create a “crack” in it), when it was torn apart with an insignificant amount of force. (The reader can easily repeat this experiment himself.) Another experiment was carried out with glass rods. Abram Fedorovich prepared glass rods with a diameter of 2-3 mm for the lecture, which were immersed in a vessel with distilled water for several hours. The same, but dry chopsticks lay nearby on the table. An attempt to bend dry sticks into an “arc” invariably ended in failure - the sticks broke. The sticks, just taken out of the water, easily bent into a hoop! This is explained by the fact that, although much slower than salt, glass also dissolves in water, and the water “washes away” the cracks from the surface of the stick.

When a body is heated, the amplitude of these vibrations changes, and therefore the energy of the vibrating atoms also changes. The change in the energy of a body when it is heated by one degree is the heat of the body. Einstein's formula for the heat of a solid correctly described the nature of the change in heat with temperature, but at low temperatures the heat values ​​calculated using this formula did not coincide with the experimental results.

Einstein's theory was improved by Debye in 1912. He proposed that atoms in a solid do not vibrate independently; they are interconnected and form a single oscillatory system. Calculations based on this proposal gave excellent agreement between the theoretical heat values ​​and the experimental data. In particular, they were consistent with a long-known fact - the so-called law of Dulong and Petit, according to which the heat of one mole of any solid substance is the same and equal to 25 J/(mol K).

But in the developed theory of heat there was one “dark place” - the heat of metals. A metal, as we know, consists of ionized atoms forming a crystal lattice and free electrons. These free electrons behave like a general ideal gas (called an electron gas). It would seem that the heat of 1 mole of metal should be the sum of the heat of the lattice (25 J/(mol K)) and the heat of the electron gas. And the latter is not at all difficult to calculate - as the heat of a monatomic ideal gas: the energy E of one mole of gas is equal to $^3/2RT$ (R is the universal gas constant), and the heat of

$$C = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta T} = \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{R \Delta T}{\Delta T} = \frac{3}{2}$$

$R \approx 12,5$ J/(mol K).

So, the heat of a mole of metal should be approximately 37.5 J/(mol K). And the changes show that it is equal to 25 J/(mol K). It was truly unexplained, what physicists call the “heat catastrophe” paradox.

It became possible to explain this fact only with the advent of quantum mechanics. The quantum mechanical theory of electron gas in metal was developed by Sommerfeld in Germany, and in the Soviet Union by Professor Yakov Ilyich Frenkel. According to the theory, the properties of an electron gas differ significantly from the properties of a monatomic gas. In particular, at ordinary (not very high) temperatures, the energy of free electrons in a metal is practically independent of its temperature, while the kinetic energy of gas atoms at propionational temperature. In other words, when a metal is heated, its internal energy changes only due to a change in the vibrational energy of atoms (more precisely, ions) of the crystal lattice; the energy of the electron gas remains unchanged. When a current is passed through the liquid metal in a magnetic field, forces begin to act on the metal from the magnetic field, which lead to the mixing of individual layers of liquid metal relative to each other. Sommerfeld also referred to our experience. To tell the truth, I was unusually proud - such an authority as Sommerfeld appreciated my work.

There was another problem in the physics of metals, which then greatly worried theoretical physicists - the issue of ferromagnetism. It was well known that of all the metals that exist in nature, only three metals are ferromagnetic - iron, nickel and cobalt. (Now much more of them are known.) They are distinguished by the fact that their magnetization, which appears in a very weak magnetic field, is quite large, several hundred thousand times greater than that of ordinary metals. For example, in order to obtain in an ordinary metal the kind of magnetization that appears in an iron sample in a field of ~0.01 T, it is necessary to have an external magnetic field of ~1000 T. To explain this incomprehensible phenomenon, the French physicist Weiss created a theory according to which in ferromagnetic metals - in iron, nickel and cobalt - there are regions in which the magnetic fields of individual atoms are oriented exactly parallel to each other. So the total magnetic field of such a region - it is called a domain (from the French word “domain” - region) - is quite large. The dimensions of these regions are very small - on the order of fractions of a micron, but in each of them many hundreds of thousands of oriented “elementary magnets” are reduced. The domains themselves are perfectly distributed throughout the metal, so that in the general state (in the absence of an external magnetic field) the magnetization of the ferromagnetic sample is zero.

When such a sample is placed in a magnetic field, the magnetic fields of individual domains are oriented along the external field, and the sample as a whole is magnetized. And it is easier to orient entire domains than a huge number of individual “elementary magnets”.

This is, roughly speaking, what the theory of ferromagnetism proposed by Weiss looked like. In order to explain the presence of domains - the presence of a region with oriented "elementary magnets" - Weiss proposed the existence inside the metal of a special magnetic field that orients the magnetic fields inside the domains. This field - Weiss calls it a molecular magnetic field - according to Weiss's calculations should be on the order of 1000 T. However, the arrival of this field was not understood.

To discover this huge hypothetical field, Professor Yakov Grigorievich Dorfman, in whose laboratory I then worked at the Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute, continued this experiment. He placed thin iron foil in a magnetic field so that the plane of the foil sheet was parallel to the field induction. The foil became very magnetized. Fast electrons emitted by a radioactive source were passed through the foil, perpendicular to its surface, and therefore perpendicular to the external magnetic field, “cut out” by a slit installed in front of the foil (figure 2). Frenkel at the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology and almost simultaneously with him the German physicist Heisenberg created the quantum mechanical theory of ferromagnetism. They showed that in fact there is no molecular field. The forces that orient “elementary magnets” within domains are electrical forces of a specific nature. They are now called exchange forces. A short article about this was published by Frenkel in 1928 in the Ionian issue of the German physical journal Zeitshrift für Physik, and a detailed article by Heisenberg appeared in the Iolian issue of the same journal. So, independently of each other, they created the theory of ferromagnetism. (Later, Yakov Ilyich Frenkel told me that he took up ferromagnetism because we, experimenters, were constantly “magnetizing” him).

After the creation of the theory of ferromagnetism, many works appeared on the issues of magnetism in metals. In particular, it was shown that, as follows from the theory, exchange forces can arise only in crystals. There cannot be liquid ferromagnetic materials. It was also shown that the exchange forces are different along different directions inside the crystal. However, it has been shown that there is a pronounced anisotropy of the magnetic properties of ferromagnetic crystals. This is still of great importance in the creation of new magnetic materials, on which all electrical engineering is based. Transformers, generators, and electric motors are all made of ferromagnetic materials, and their characteristics significantly depend on the properties of the ferromagnetic material itself. Somewhat later, the theory of ferromagnetism was developed by our outstanding theoretical Lev Davydovich Landdu and his student Evgeniy Mikhailovich Lifshits. Teller's modern theory of ferromagnetism is entirely based on the work of Frenkel, Heisenberg, Landdu and Lifshitz.

In 1930, when I had just graduated from college and received the title of engineer-physicist, on the recommendation of A.F. Ioffe, I was sent on a business trip to Germany to get acquainted with the physics laboratories of the West. I visited about three places in Germany and was able to get acquainted with the work of physics laboratories in Leipzig, Monchen, and Hamburg. And I must say that I was very pleased when I became convinced that the level of our physicists, in particular my own level, was in no way lower than the level of physicists I met in laboratories abroad. True, they had some advantages over us: their laboratories were very well equipped with instruments. We in the Soviet Union did not yet have domestic instrument making; we often had to make instruments ourselves or buy them abroad, and this was difficult in those days when more funds were spent on the development of the national economy. But in terms of the level of knowledge, the level of understanding, even the technical level of the experiment, we were, as I was convinced, no lower than the largest university laboratories in Germany.

While on a business trip, I worked for about a month at the laboratorium in Monchen, in the former laboratorium of Roentgen. University doctoral students also worked there (that’s what they called students graduating from university who were preparing their dissertations). However, I noticed that doctoral students, preparing for final exams, read Ya. I. Frenkel’s book “Course in Electrodynamics,” published in German. I asked: “Who will you give the exams to?” They answered: “Sommerfeld.” That same Sommerfeld, the world's greatest theoretician, about whom, when we were students, there was a saying “there is no Bohr but Bohr, and Sommerfeld is his vice.” I knew that there was a fifth course in physics by Sommerfeld himself, and I asked why doctoral students teach electrodynamics not according to Sommerfeld, but according to Frenkel. Therefore, they replied, Sommerfeld said that he would take the exam only on Frenkel’s course, since there is no better course in the world now. When I said that I personally knew Frenkel, I felt that my authority in their eyes increased sharply. And I felt a feeling of true pride in our Soviet physicists, who have earned wide recognition among the world's leading theoretical scientists.
