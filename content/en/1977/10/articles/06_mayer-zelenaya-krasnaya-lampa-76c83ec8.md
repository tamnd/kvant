⟦folio 32⟧

In the wonderful book by W. Bragg “World of Light. The World of Sound"*) describes a red experiment demonstrating one curious property of the eye. This experience is so simple that each of you can repeat it at home without much difficulty.

The device for performing the experiment (Fig. 1) can be assembled from parts of a mechanical construction set. On an aluminum base (1) measuring 15 × 60 × 110 mm, secure the microelectric motor (2) with an aluminum iron bracket. In response to two drains (3) approximately 60 mm high, insert a shaft (4) with a pulley (5) with an internal diameter of 15-25 mm. A cardboard disk (6) with a diameter of 100-140 mm must first be attached to the end of the shaft (if the shaft is threaded, the disk can be secured with two nuts and washers). On the base of the device, place an aluminum stand (7) with a light bulb (8). Place the rubber ring (9) on the microelectric motor shaft and pulley. Connect the motor to one or two series-connected flashlight batteries. According to Bragg's recommendation, the disk should rotate at a speed of 2-3 rps. The speed of rotation of the disk can be adjusted within certain limits by adjusting the pulley with your finger.

*) W. Bragg. World of light. World of Sound, 1967.

After the preliminary test, prepare the device for experiment. In a cardboard disk, cut out a sector on the other approximately 45° and cover one half of the remaining sector with white paper and the other with black paper (Fig. 2). Cover the glass bulb of a flashlight bulb, designed for a voltage of 3.5 V, with red nitrospaint (you can try using nail polish). When the paint is dry, screw the light bulb into the hole in the device stand and connect the conductors to its electrons. Place a table lamp in front of the disk at a distance of 20-50 cm from it so that the light from it shines on the disk. Connect the light bulb to the battery and turn on the microelectric motor.

If the direction of rotation of the disk is such that with each rotation of the light bulb the black part of the disk first overlaps, then at any illumination and speed of rotation the light bulb looks red. If, by changing the polarity of the power source, you change the direction of rotation, that is, make the disk spin so that the light bulb first overlaps its white part, then the light bulb will be... green or blue-green! If the experimental conditions (the speed of rotation of the disk, the paint that the light bulb is coated with) are not selected well enough, the light bulb does not look blue-green, but light blue with a whitish tint.

Let's try to bind the result of experience. The disk rotates quickly, and when the slot in it is in front of the red light bulb, the retina of the eye momentarily receives a red image of the light bulb. If, with further rotation of the disk, the light bulb is blocked by its white half, then the diffused white light of the table lamp falls on the retina of the eye. White color affects the retina for a longer period of time than red color. After the black half of the disk spins in front of the eye, the process is repeated. In this case, the light bulb is perceived as blue-green.
