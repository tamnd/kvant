F438. A homogeneous rod lies inside a rough spherical cavity. The length of the stick is equal to the radius of the sphere. The time factor is $\mu$. What is the best point with a horizon that can be made up of a stick?

Obviously, the stick will make the greatest angle $\alpha$ with the horizon when the plane of the great circle drawn through the points of contact of the stick with the sphere is vertical.

In this plane, the rod is acted upon by gravitational forces $Mq$, normal reaction forces of the sphere $\vec{N}_1$ and $\vec{N}_2$, and friction forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ (Fig. 7).

The stick is at rest if the sum of the projections of these forces in any direction and the sum of the moments of forces relative to any fixed axis is zero. Let us write down the condition for the equilibrium of the stick in the projection onto its direction:

$$|\vec{N}_1| \cos \varphi - |\vec{N}_2| \cos \varphi + |\vec{F}_1| \sin \varphi + |\vec{F}_2| \sin \varphi - |\vec{Mg}| \sin \alpha = 0.$$

Projecting the forces onto the direction, redistribution to the stick, we get

$$|\vec{N}_1| \sin \varphi + |\vec{N}_2| \sin \varphi - |\vec{F}_1| \cos \varphi + |\vec{F}_2| \cos \varphi - |\vec{Mg}| \cos \alpha = 0.$$

It is convenient to write the moment equation with respect to the axis, redistribution to the drawing plane and the sphere passing through the center:

$$|\vec{F}_1| R + |\vec{F}_2| R - |\vec{Mg}| R \sin \varphi \sin \alpha = 0.$$

Here $\varphi = \pi/3$, $R$ is the length of the stick, equal to the radius of the sphere. With the closest point to the horizon, you can make a stick in projection to its direction:

$$|\vec{F}_1| = \mu |\vec{N}_1| \text{ и } |\vec{F}_2| = \mu |\vec{N}_2|.$$

Having solved the resulting heel system of the equation, we find

$$tg \alpha_{\text{max}} = \frac{\mu}{\sin^2 \varphi - \mu^2 \cos^2 \varphi} = \frac{4\mu}{3 - \mu^2},$$

$$\alpha_{\text{max}} = \arctg \frac{4\mu}{3 - \mu^2}.$$

The problem can be solved in the same way using the so-called “three force theorem”: if a body is in equilibrium under the action of three non-parallel forces applied to it, then the lines of action of these forces intersect at one point.

In our case, the three forces applied to the stick are the gravitational force $\vec{Mg}$ and the resulting reaction forces $\vec{R}_1 = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{N}_1$ and $\vec{R}_2 = \vec{F}_2 + \vec{N}_2$ (Fig. 8). Since the force tremya at the largest angle $\alpha_{\text{max}}$ of the head of the stick is maximum, the force $\vec{R}_1$ and $\vec{R}_2$ with the radii of the sphere drawn at the points of contact make up the same angle $\beta = \arctg \mu$ (angle tremya).

Let us consider the triangle $ACB$, in which the stick is the base, and the point $C$ of intersection of the lines of action of the forces is the opposite vertex. The linear action of gravity divides the mentioned triangle into two, having a common side of length $l$ (median of the triangle $ACB$) and concreting bases of length $R/2$. Applying the sine theorem to each triangle, we get

$$\frac{\sin(\pi/2 + \alpha - \varphi - \beta)}{R/2} = -\frac{\sin(\varphi + \beta)}{l}$$

And

$$\frac{\sin(\pi/2 - \alpha - \varphi + \beta)}{R/2} = \frac{\sin(\varphi - \beta)}{l},$$

where $tg \beta = \mu$ and $\varphi = \pi/3$.

Dividing these two equations by one another, we get

$$\frac{\cos(\alpha - \varphi - \beta)}{\cos(\alpha + \varphi - \beta)} = \frac{\sin(\varphi + \beta)}{\sin(\varphi - \beta)},$$

from which we will find

$$tg \alpha = \frac{(1 + tg^2 \varphi) tg \beta}{tg^2 \varphi - tg^2 \beta} = \frac{4\mu}{3 - \mu^2}.$$

F439. The neon light (NL) lights up when the direction on it reaches the value $U_1$. In this case, the resistance of the light bulb becomes negligible. When we push the direction on the lamp to the value $U_2$, the lamp will jam and its resistance becomes infinitely large. This lamp was connected to the circuit as shown in figure 9. Counting $\gg U_1 > U_2$, construct an approximate graph of the direction on the capacitor versus time after closing the switch $K$.

After closing the key $K$, current will flow in the circuit, and the direction to the capacitor begins to increase until it reaches the value $U_1$, at which the neon light bulb lights up. Since $\gg U_1$, we can assume that during the entire charging time of the capacitor, the current $I = \mathcal{E}/R$ and direction $U_C$ to the capacitor grows according to a linear law (Fig. 10).

At time $t_1$, when the voltage across the capacitor becomes equal to $U_1$, the light bulb will light up. Time $t_1$ can be found from the condition

$$It_1 = Q = CU_1, \text{ и } t_1 = \frac{CU_1R}{\mathcal{E}}.$$

F440. Estimate the approximation at what minimum radius it will form a contained atmosphere, consisting mainly of classor and nitrogen, if the surface temperature is $T = 300$ K. The average density of the substance is equal to $\rho = 4 \cdot 10^3$ kg/m³.

Let's consider a molecule that is part of the atmosphere. This molecule will be held in close proximity to the surface of the plans if its kinetic energy does not exceed the potential energy of the molecule in the gravitational field of the plans:

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} \leqslant \gamma \frac{mM}{r}.$$

Here $m$ is the mass of the molecule, $v^2$ is the mean square of its speed, $\gamma$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ is the plan mass and $r$ is its radius. An estimate of the minimum radius of plans can be checked using kinetic and potential energies in order of magnitude:

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} \approx \gamma \frac{mM}{r_{\min}}.$$

The mean square velocity of a gas molecule is related to the temperature $T$ of the gas by the correspondence

$$\overline{v^2} = \frac{3kT}{m} = \frac{3RT}{\mu},$$

where $k$ is Boltzmann’s constant, $R$ is the universal gas constant, $\mu$ is the molar mass of the gas. The mass of the plans can be expressed through the average value $\rho$ of the density of the matter of the plans and its minimum radius:

$$M = \frac{4}{3} \pi r_{\min}^3 \rho.$$

Then correspondence (1) can be rewritten as follows:

$$\frac{3RT}{2\mu} \approx \frac{4}{3} \gamma \pi r_{\min}^2 \rho,$$

where

$$r_{\min} \approx \sqrt{\frac{9RT}{8\gamma \pi \mu}}.$$

From formula (2) it follows that $r_{\min}$ depends (other things being equal) on the molar mass $\mu$: the larger $\mu$, the smaller $r_{\min}$. According to the conditions of the problem, the atmosphere consists mainly of classor and nitrogen, for which the molar masses are equal to the corresponding $\mu_k = 32 \cdot 10^{-3}$ kg/mol and $\mu_a = 28 \cdot 10^{-3}$ kg/mol. Obviously, to estimate the minimum radius of the plans, it is enough to find $r_{\min}$, assuming that the atmosphere consists only of nitrogen.

So,

$$r_{\min} \approx \sqrt{\frac{9RT}{8\gamma \pi \mu_a}} \approx 3 \cdot 10^5 \text{ м} = 300 \text{ км}.$$

F441. Between the plates of a closed flat capacitor there is a powerful charge q. The area of ​​the plates is large, the distance between them is equal to d. The initial charge is located at a distance $\frac{1}{3} d$ from the left plate.

What charge will pass through the conductor connecting the capacitor plates when moving charge q to a new position at which it will be at a distance $\frac{1}{3} d$ from the right plate?

Fig. 11.

Since the capacitor plates are large, the charges induced on them will not change when the charge q moves parallel to the planes of the plates (we neglect edge effects). This means that the charges of the plates will not change even if the charge q is evenly “smeared” along a plane parallel to the plates of the capacitor and initially located at a distance $\frac{1}{3} d$ from the left plate.

Let us denote by $q_1$ the charge of the left plate of the capacitor. Then the charge of the right plate (according to the law of conservation of charge) is equal to $-q_1$. Charges $q_1$ and $-q_1$ of the capacitor plates create an electrostatic field between the plates with intensity $|\vec{E}_1| = \frac{q_1}{\varepsilon_0 S}$ ($S$ is the area of ​​each plate), and the plate with charge $q$ located inside the capacitor creates a field with intensity $|\vec{E}| = \frac{q}{2\varepsilon_0 S}$ (Fig. 11). On one side of the plate the directions of the directed fields coincide, and on the other side they are opposite to each other. Therefore, the potential difference $\Delta\varphi$ between the plates of the capacitor is equal to

$$\Delta\varphi = \left( |\vec{E}_1| - |\vec{E}| \right) \frac{1}{3} d + \left( |\vec{E}_1| + |\vec{E}| \right) \frac{2}{3} d.$$

On the other hand, since the capacitor plates are closed, the direction between them is zero: $\Delta\varphi = 0$. Hence,

$$\frac{1}{3} d \left( \frac{q_1}{\varepsilon_0 S} - \frac{q}{2\varepsilon_0 S} \right) + \frac{2}{3} d \left( \frac{q_1}{\varepsilon_0 S} + \frac{q}{2\varepsilon_0 S} \right) = 0.$$

From this equation we find the charge $q_1$:

$$q_1 = -\frac{1}{6} q.$$

Similarly, you can find the charge $q'_1$ of the left plate of the capacitor when the middle plate (charge q) is located at a distance $\frac{2}{3} d$ from it:

$$q'_1 = \frac{1}{6} q.$$

Thus, when the internal capacitor moves a charge q (or charged plate) along the conductor connecting the capacitor plates, a charge passes

$$\Delta q = q'_1 - q_1 = \frac{1}{3} q.$$
