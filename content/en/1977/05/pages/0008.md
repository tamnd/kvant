Let $S(x)$ be the area of ​​that part of the figure $F$ that is located to the left of the line drawn through the point $x$.

It is not difficult to prove that the function $S$ is antiderivative for $f$ on the interval $[a, b]$, i.e. $S'(x) = f(x)$ for $x \in ]a, b[$. Indeed, let $\varepsilon$ be an arbitrary positive number less than $f(x)$. On a straight line parallel to the ordinat axis and passing through the point $x$, we take points $M, N, P, Q$, spaced $\varepsilon/2$ from the ends of the segment along which the straight line is taken and intersects the figure $F$ (Fig. 10). Points $M, Q$ are located outside the figure $F$, and $N, P$ are internal points of this figure. Consequently, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that segments of length 2$\delta$, parallel to the abscissa axis and having midpoints at points $M, N, P, Q$, are located: the first and last - outside the figure $F$, and the second and third - inside it. Now let $\Delta x$ be a positive number less than $\delta$. The difference $\Delta S(x) = S(x + \Delta x) - S(x)$ is equal to the area of ​​the figure protected by figure 10. This area will enclose between the areas of rectangles $NLTP$ and $MKRQ$, i.e.

$$(f(x) - \varepsilon) \Delta x < \Delta S(x) < (f(x) + \varepsilon) \Delta x.$$

From here

$$f(x) - \varepsilon < \frac{\Delta S(x)}{\Delta x} < f(x) + \varepsilon$$

at $0 < \Delta x < \delta$.
