Archimedes' law and... solutions to equations

Last year, in the ninth issue of our magazine, we discussed how to solve cubic equations using the Cardan formula and graphical methods. Now we want to introduce you to another way to solve third-degree equations - using... some physics.

Rewriting the pages of the old but very interesting journal “Bulletin of Experimental Physics and Elementary Mathematics” (1903, No. 348), we discovered a description of Meslin’s ingenious machine for solving algebraic equations, which had long been clogged with weight. Meslin proposed finding the roots of the equation
$$a_n y^n + a_{n-1} y^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 y + a_0 = 0,$$
(1) determining the depth of immersion in water of specially made weights suspended from the yoke of the scale (see figure).

What are these cargoes? It turns out that the weights must be of such a shape that the volume of water they displace is numerically equal to the immersion depth raised in accordance with the power (1, 2, 3, ..., n). In other words, each term in equation (1) must correspond to a certain load.

It is most convenient to use bodies of rotation as weights*). For the term $a_1 y$ the corresponding body of rotation is a cylinder with area

*) See, for example, chapter IV “Geometry 10”.

base equal to one. The volume of water displaced by such a cylinder will be numerically equal to the immersion depth (y). For the term $a_2 y^2$ it is necessary to make a body called a parabolun of rotation. It is formed by the rotation of a parabola (more precisely, the arcs of a parabola) around its axis. If a parabola is described by the equation $y = \frac{\pi}{2} x^2$, then the volume of the parabola of revolution is $V = \frac{1}{2} S y = \frac{1}{2} (\pi x^2) y = y^2$.

When this body is immersed to a depth of $y$, the volume of displaced water will be equal to $y^2$—the square of the depth of immersion. The term $a_3 y^3$ corresponds to a cone, the generatrix of which is described by the equation $y = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}} x$. Such a cone displaces a volume of water equal to $y^3$—the cube of the depth of immersion (check it out!).

It can be shown that in the general case, if for the term $a_h y^h$ we make a corresponding body by rotating the curve (more precisely, the arcs of the curve) $y = \sqrt[k-1]{\frac{\pi x^2}{k}}$ around the ordinate axis, then the volume of water displaced by this body will be numerically equal to $y^k$.

How does Meslin's machine actually work? Let's consider a specific example - let's experimentally find the roots of the cubic equation
$$y^3 - 16 y^2 + 83 y - 140 = 0.$$ (2)
To work, you will need lever scales, three communicating vessels with water (or one large transparent vessel) and three weights - a cylinder, a paraboloid of revolution and a cone.
