The event $C$ “rolling the sum of points, no more than 5” is favored by 10 outcomes (indicate them as you please), therefore $P(C) = \frac{10}{36} = \frac{5}{18}$.

The event $D$ “rolling a total of points greater than 5” is favored by 26 outcomes (all not included in $C$), therefore $P(D) = \frac{26}{36} = \frac{13}{18}$.

The event $E$ “rolling an even number of points on the red die and a multiple of trem on the blue” is favored by 6 outcomes, so $P(E) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.

Thus, when determining the probability of some event $A$, three actions must be checked:

(a) determine the total number of (equally credible) outcomes of the experiment;
(b) determine the number of outcomes favoring event $A$;
(c) divide the second number by the first.

Let us emphasize that from the very beginning we believe (“by definition”) that the outcomes of the experiment are equally probable. This corresponds to practical results if the experiment has some symmetry (the bone has the shape of a hexadron - a cube, and not, say, an oblique parallelepid and is not filled with pork on one side, the coin is new, not worn, that is, it has the shape of a cylinder, etc.). Because of this, it is impossible, say, to assume that in the experiment “determining the sum on two dice” there are only 11 outcomes (sums from 2 to 12) - they are unequal, the probability of each of them is not 1/11. Determining such “good” outcomes of an experiment is an essential part of part (a) of the probability problem, and the manual expresses the probability addition theorem that if one event $C$ splits into a number of other events $(C_1, C_2, C_3, C_4)$, then its probability $P(C)$ is equal to the sum of the probabilities of the component events.
