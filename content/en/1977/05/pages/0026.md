⟦folio 24⟧

Each of the numbers 1, 2, ..., N appears once each.
a) Give an example of a universal sequence of $N^2-N+1$ terms.
b) Prove that any universal sequence consists of at least $\frac{N(N+1)}{2}$ terms.
c) Prove that for $N=4$ the short universal sequence itself consists of 12 terms.
d) Try to find the length of the given $N$ as short as possible of the universal sequence.

Let $q = (b_1, b_2, \dots, b_N)$ be an arbitrary permutation of the numbers 1, 2, ..., $N$. If this permutation is of the form $q = (N, N-1, \dots, 1)$, then in our sequence in the first group we cross out all numbers except $N$, in the second - all numbers except $N-1$, etc. If $q$ is not of this form, then for some $k$ we will have: $b_k < b_{k+1}$. In this case, if $i < k$, then cross out all numbers except $b_i$ in the $i$-th group of our sequence; in the $k$th group, cross out all numbers except $b_k$ and $b_{k+1}$, and if $j > k$, then cross out all numbers in the $j$ group, except $b_j+1$. And finally, by crossing out the very last number 1, we get the permutation $q = (b_1, b_2, \dots, b_N)$.

To solve point e) we need some generalization of this construction*. Namely: let $1 \leq k \leq N$. Let us write out in a row $k-1$ the group of numbers 1, 2, ..., $N$, followed by the shortened $k$ group of the form 1, 2, ..., $N-k+1$ (for example, for $N=5$, $k=3$ we get 1234512345123); we obtain a sequence of length $kN-k+1$. In a completely analogous way, it is proved that from this sequence, if we cross out parts of the terms, we can obtain any sequence of length $k$, in which the numbers 1, 2, ..., $N$ appear - each no more than once. We will call this property $(N, k)$-universality.

Let us now construct a $N$-universal sequence of length $N^2-2N+4$ (this is an improvement of the previous estimate, starting from $N=4$). Let's write down $N-2$ groups of numbers 1, 2, ..., $N-1$ in a row. After it we will write a shortened $(N-1)$ group, consisting of only two numbers: 1 and 2. Then we will insert $N$ copies of the number $N$ into this sequence according to the following rule: we will write the first copy at the very beginning, the second - in the first group, after the first occurrence of the number $N-1$, the third copy - in the second group, after the second occurrence of the number in our sequence $N-2$, etc. The last, $N$th copy of the number $N$ is inserted into the last shortened group after the last occurrence of the number 1 (for example, with $N=5$ we get the sequence 5123451235412534152). We obtain a sequence of length $(N-1)(N-2)+2+N=N^2-2N+4$; let's denote it by $P$. Let us prove that the sequence $P$ is $N$-universal. Indeed, let $q$ be an arbitrary permutation of the numbers 1, 2, ..., $N$, and let the number $N$ occur in the sequence $q$ in the $k$ place. The number $N$ splits the sequence $q$ into two parts: the left one of length $k-1$ and the right one of length $N-k$. We need to remove some terms from $P$ so that we get the sequence $q$. First cross out all instances of the number $N$, except for $k$. To the left of the uncrossed out $N$ there remains, obviously, a $(N-1, k-1)$-universal sequence, from which we can cross out the sequence $q$, which stands from $N$ on the right. This means that the constructed sequence $P$ (of length $N^2-2N+4$) is indeed $N$-universal.

Let's now move on to estimates. We will simultaneously solve parts c) and d) by proving a stronger statement and e: for $N \geq 2$ the length of any $N$-universal sequence is at least $\frac{N^2+3N-4}{2} = \frac{N(N+1)}{2} + N-2$.

*) An elegant solution to problem d) was added by A. Kaschinuk from Nikolaev and A. Moshonkin from Leningrad.
