Pascal's snail

Pascal's snail received its name *) not in honor of the great Blaise Pascal, but in honor of his father Etienne Pascal (1588-1651), a provincial French judge and a great lover of mathematics. E. Pascal built a snail like this.

Let's draw a circle (O, r) on the plane, given a certain length d, select an arbitrary point P on this circle and fix it - this will be Pascal's snail strip. For each point K of the circle different from P, draw a straight line KB and plot the segments M1K and KM2 on it in opposite directions from the point K, so that |M1K|=|KM2|=d (Fig. 1). The set of such points M1 and M2 forms a continuous closed curve, which is called Pascal's snail. The P stripes may or may not belong to the cochlea, ensuring continuity of the curve.

Pascal's snail has a fundamental difference from the curves that were shown on the first or second pages of the cover of Quiet for this year - its shape is variable.

Its shape depends on the value of d (Fig. 2): for d=0 the snail coincides with (O, r), for 0<d<2r it has a point

Let us present the idea on which the second ability to construct a snail (Fig. 3) is based. Let us again consider a circle (O, r), on which there are stripes P and a fixed point Q, as well as a circle (Q, R) of some radius R. Let

PQ=2α. For each point V∈(O, r), we draw a straight line PV and find points M1 and M2 on it, so that if we draw tangents to (Q, R) from them and then rotate them by angle α clockwise, they will end up on PV. The set of such points M1 and M2 is Pascal's snail, and d=R/sin α.

At figure 4 another construction of Pascal's snail is substantiated. The movable circle (O′, ρ) touches the fixed (“support”) circle (O, ρ) from the outside. Let us show that when rolling (O′, ρ), a point M rigidly connected to it is described by Pascal’s snail.

Let the length of the segment MO be minimal (M=M1). Let us choose a point P on the line OO′1 for which |OP|=|O′1M1|, and construct a circle (O, |OP|). If the movable circle is moved through an angle φ from the initial position, then points O′ and M fall, respectively, to positions O′2 and M2. Let us draw the line M2P and find the point of its intersection N with the circle (O, |OP|). We get |ON|=|OP|=|M1O′1|=|M2O′2|. The quadrilateral ONM2O′2 (with the location of point N as at figure 4) is the union of the isosceles trapezoid OPM2O′2 (POO′2=OO′2M2=φ) and the isosceles triangle PON. It is clear that this quadrilateral is a parallelogram, and the lengths of its sides do not depend on the angle φ. Since the segment NM maintains a constant length for any position of the point M, then in the trajectory of the point M we recognize Pascal’s snail, at the point P - its stripes, and in (O, |OP|) - the circle (O, r).
