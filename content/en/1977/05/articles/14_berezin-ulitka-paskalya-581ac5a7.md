Pascal's snail

Pascal's snail received its name *) not in honor of the great Blaise Pascal, but in honor of his father Etienne Pascal (1588-1651), a provincial French judge and a great lover of mathematics. E. Pascal built a snail like this.

Let's draw a circle (O, r) on the plane, given a certain length d, select an arbitrary point P on this circle and fix it - this will be Pascal's snail strip. For each point K of the circle different from P, draw a straight line KB and plot the segments M1K and KM2 on it in opposite directions from the point K, so that |M1K|=|KM2|=d (Fig. 1). The set of such points M1 and M2 forms a continuous closed curve, which is called Pascal's snail. The P stripes may or may not belong to the cochlea, ensuring continuity of the curve.

Pascal's snail has a fundamental difference from the curves that were shown on the first or second pages of the cover of Quiet for this year - its shape is variable.

Its shape depends on the value of d (Fig. 2): for d=0 the snail coincides with (O, r), for 0<d<2r it has a point

Probability
You see that rolling 2 and 6 on one dice are equally likely, but rolling 2 and 7 on two dice are not equally likely. In such cases, the sum 7 is said to be favored by six outcomes out of a total of 36 equally likely outcomes, while the sum 2 is favored by only one outcome.

And in general, counting the number of favorable outcomes out of their total number is the most important action for determining the probability of an event. In this case, notations are also used. The event itself (for example, the appearance of the sum 7) is denoted by the letter A, and the probability of this event is P(A). So,

$$P(A) = \frac{m}{n},$$

where $n$ is the total number of outcomes of a given experiment, and $m$ is the number of outcomes favoring event $A$.

Let's try to calculate the probability of some events.

Table 4 has a total of 36 cells. That's 36 possible outcomes when rolling two dice. A score of 7 (event $A$) is favored by 6 outcomes. Using formula (*) we get: $P(A) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.

A score of 2 (event $B$) is favored by 1 outcome. According to the formula (*) $P(B) = \frac{1}{36}$.

The event $C$ “rolling the sum of points, no more than 5” is favored by 10 outcomes (indicate them as you please), therefore $P(C) = \frac{10}{36} = \frac{5}{18}$.

The event $D$ “rolling a total of points greater than 5” is favored by 26 outcomes (all not included in $C$), therefore $P(D) = \frac{26}{36} = \frac{13}{18}$.

The event $E$ “rolling an even number of points on the red die and a multiple of trem on the blue” is favored by 6 outcomes, so $P(E) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.

Thus, when determining the probability of some event $A$, three actions must be checked:

(a) determine the total number of (equally credible) outcomes of the experiment;
(b) determine the number of outcomes favoring event $A$;
(c) divide the second number by the first.

Let us emphasize that from the very beginning we believe (“by definition”) that the outcomes of the experiment are equally probable. This corresponds to practical results if the experiment has some symmetry (the bone has the shape of a hexadron - a cube, and not, say, an oblique parallelepid and is not filled with pork on one side, the coin is new, not worn, that is, it has the shape of a cylinder, etc.). Because of this, it is impossible, say, to assume that in the experiment “determining the sum on two dice” there are only 11 outcomes (sums from 2 to 12) - they are unequal, the probability of each of them is not 1/11.
