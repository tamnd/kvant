⟦folio 9⟧

As in the case of areas, the existence and uniqueness theorem holds.

Without dwelling on other methods, let's consider the calculation of volumes using integration and Cavalieri's principle for volumes.

Let a coordinate system be given in space and some figure $F$ be taken, the projection of which onto the abscissa axis is represented by a certain segment $[a, b]$. By $S(x)$ we denote the area of ​​the figure cut out from $F$ by a plane that is perpendicular to the abscissa axis and passes through the point $x$ of the segment $[a, b]$. Next, by $V(x)$ we denote the volume of that part of the figure $F$, which is located to the left of the drawn plane (Fig. 15). Then the equality $V'(x) = S(x)$ is true, i.e. $V$ is an antiderivative for the function $S$. The proof of this equality is the same as in the case of areas.

Of course, for the proof to pass, some assumptions must be made. The figure $F$ must be cubic, its sections must be square.

In the X-grade textbook, for example, the equality $V'(x) = S(x)$ is valid and justified for the case when $F$ is a rotation figure of a special type. We don't stop there. From the equality $V'(x) = S(x)$ it follows that for the volume of the figure $F$ the formula is valid

$$V(F) = \int_a^b S(x) dx.$$

Finally, from this formula follows the Cavalieri principle for obtems: if any plane parallel to a fixed plane intersects the spatial figures $F$ and $H$ along figures having the same area (Fig. 16), then the figures $F$ and $H$ are equal: $V(F) = V(H)$. An elegant example of the application of the Cavalieri principle to the calculation of volumes is given in the note published in this issue by M. Mamikon.
