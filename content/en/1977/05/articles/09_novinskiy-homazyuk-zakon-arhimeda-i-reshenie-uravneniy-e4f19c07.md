Archimedes' law and... solutions to equations

Last year, in the ninth issue of our magazine, we discussed how to solve cubic equations using the Cardan formula and graphical methods. Now we want to introduce you to another way to solve third-degree equations - using... some physics.

Rewriting the pages of the old but very interesting journal “Bulletin of Experimental Physics and Elementary Mathematics” (1903, No. 348), we discovered a description of Meslin’s ingenious machine for solving algebraic equations, which had long been clogged with weight. Meslin proposed finding the roots of the equation
$$a_n y^n + a_{n-1} y^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 y + a_0 = 0,$$
(1) determining the depth of immersion in water of specially made weights suspended from the yoke of the scale (see figure).

What are these cargoes? It turns out that the weights must be of such a shape that the volume of water they displace is numerically equal to the immersion depth raised in accordance with the power (1, 2, 3, ..., n). In other words, each term in equation (1) must correspond to a certain load.

It is most convenient to use bodies of rotation as weights*). For the term $a_1 y$ the corresponding body of rotation is a cylinder with area

*) See, for example, chapter IV “Geometry 10”.

base equal to one. The volume of water displaced by such a cylinder will be numerically equal to the immersion depth (y). For the term $a_2 y^2$ it is necessary to make a body called a parabolun of rotation. It is formed by the rotation of a parabola (more precisely, the arcs of a parabola) around its axis. If a parabola is described by the equation $y = \frac{\pi}{2} x^2$, then the volume of the parabola of revolution is $V = \frac{1}{2} S y = \frac{1}{2} (\pi x^2) y = y^2$.

When this body is immersed to a depth of $y$, the volume of displaced water will be equal to $y^2$—the square of the depth of immersion. The term $a_3 y^3$ corresponds to a cone, the generatrix of which is described by the equation $y = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}} x$. Such a cone displaces a volume of water equal to $y^3$—the cube of the depth of immersion (check it out!).

It can be shown that in the general case, if for the term $a_h y^h$ we make a corresponding body by rotating the curve (more precisely, the arcs of the curve) $y = \sqrt[k-1]{\frac{\pi x^2}{k}}$ around the ordinate axis, then the volume of water displaced by this body will be numerically equal to $y^k$.

How does Meslin's machine actually work? Let's consider a specific example - let's experimentally find the roots of the cubic equation
$$y^3 - 16 y^2 + 83 y - 140 = 0.$$ (2)
To work, you will need lever scales, three communicating vessels with water (or one large transparent vessel) and three weights - a cylinder, a paraboloid of revolution and a cone. It is better not to attach the yoke of the scale to the stand (as shown in figure), but, like a pharmacy scale, to hang it on

threads On the right and left shoulders, we will make marks in millimeters for 20 cm. Weights can be made, for example, from aluminum. The height of all empty weights will be the same and equal to 10 cm. Let’s take the area of ​​the base of the cylinder equal to 1 cm², then its volume will be equal to 10 cm³, and its mass will be 27 g. However, it is reasonable to reduce the mass of the cylinder to 10 g (i.e., make it numerically equal to the volume): the necessary calculations will be significantly simplified. To do this, you can drill a corresponding hole from the inside, along the axis of the cylinder (but not to the bottom!). Let's make other bodies of revolution in the same way. The parabolon of rotation will have a volume of 100 cm³ and a mass of 100 g (and in this case, part of the metal from the inside will have to be removed). But it is better to make the cone 100 times smaller in volume and mass than Meslin proposed. Otherwise, with a cone height of 10 cm, its diameter at the top will be about 20 cm! Thus, the generatrix of our cone is described by the equation $y = 10 \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}} x$, the volume of the cone (with a height of 10 cm) is 10 cm³, and the mass (including the internal hole) is 10 g. Moreover, the diameter of the upper part of the cone is ≈ 2 cm, so no “technical” difficulties will arise.

Now let’s be present unpredictably to the experiment. For convenience, we rewrite equation (2) differently, crossing terms with negative coefficients to the right:

$$y^3 + 83y = 16y^2 + 140.$$

(3) We hang the weights on the scales and bring the scales into balance. We hang the cone corresponding to the member $y^3$ from the left shoulder of the feeder. Since the coefficient of $y^3$ in equation (3) is equal to 1, it would be reasonable to attach the cone at a distance of 1 mm (we will measure distances in mm) from the axis of rotation of the feed. But our cone mass is 100 times less than it should be. To copy this, hang the cone at the 100 mm mark. We also hang the cylinder corresponding to member 83y from the left shoulder at a distance of 83 mm from its middle. We hang the parabolon of rotation on the right shoulder at the 16 mm mark. It is clear that the scales will not be in equilibrium: the sum of the moments of forces that rotate the feed counterclockwise is not equal to the sum of the moments of forces causing the rotation clockwise. However, balance can be easily achieved in a variety of ways. For example, let’s attach the necessary additional weight to the 100 mm division on the right shoulder. From the condition of equality of the moments of force, it is easy to find the mass of this weight - it is equal to 2.3 g.

Then we will begin to gradually fill the communicating vessel with water. As soon as the water reaches the suspended weights (note that their lower peaks must be at the same level), the equilibrium will be disrupted. This is understandable: when diving to the same depth, different bodies displace different volumes of water, i.e., different pushing forces arise. And the additional weight does not sink into the water at all.

Is it possible to restore the disturbed balance? Yes, if the total moments of the pushing forces acting on the feeding scales on the left and right become the same. Let the immersion depth of the bodies be equal to $y$. then the moment of the ejecting force acting on the cone is proportional to $y^3$, on the cylinder - 83 $y$, and on the parabolon of rotation - 16 $y^2$ (the proportional coefficient is the acceleration of free fall). But all these quantities are included in equation (3)! Only the free member turns out to be “not in the aisle.” Let's put it to work too. To represent the pushing out moment corresponding to the free term of our cubic equation, let’s create a moment on the left arm of the feed that is proportional to the value of this free term (140). To do this, you can, for example, hang another additional weight weighing 1.4 g at the 100 mm mark on the left shoulder.

Thus, the equation for the moments of the pushing forces will look like this:

$$y^3 + 83y = 16y^2 + 140.$$
