⟦folio 19⟧

Now, let's find the roots of our original equation. For the first time, the equilibrium is restored at an immersion depth of 4 cm, for the second time - at a depth of 5 cm and for the third time - 7 cm. Please note that each time the equilibrium position is passed, the slope of the stern changes to the opposite. Up to a depth of 4 cm, the yoke is tilted to the left, from 4 to 5 cm - to the right, etc.

In principle, negative roots can also be found using this method. To do this, just put $y = x - \frac{a}{3}$.

Let us consider a special case of the last equation, when $p \geq 0$ and $q < 0$. Let's make one more change: let $x = y\sqrt{p}$. We obtain an equation of the form

$$y^3 + y = a,$$

where $a = \frac{-q}{p\sqrt{p}}$ ($a > 0$). Let's find the positive root of this equation.

Let's take two vessels of the same height $h$ (see figure): one is cylindrical with a base plane of 1 cm$^2$, the other is finite with a volume $h^3\left(\frac{h}{r} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}}\right)$. Let's connect them with a thin tube to a tap. Let's pour $a$ cm$^3$ water into one of the vessels and open the tap. Water will flow into the second vessel until the water levels in both vessels are the same. Let us denote by $y_0$ the height of the water level. In this case, the volume of water in a cylindrical vessel will be numerically equal to $y_0$, in a Konch vessel - $y_0^3$, and the total volume of water is equal to $a$. Hence,

$$y_0^3 + y_0 = a$$

(we neglect the volume of water in the connecting tube).

This means that $y_0$ is one of the roots of equation (*).
