At the end of his short article “Letter from Professor Möbius to the publisher of the journal” (Astronomic News, 1825, vol. 3), Möbius writes about the problem he proposed:

“It is not enough to talk about a pillar problem, which, considered in itself, is simply a curiosity; it deserves some attention only insofar as it represents an example of the following theorem, which seems new to me: if each two points of any system of n points located in a plane are connected by a straight line, and if we consider given the areas (independent of each other) of any 2n-5 polygons arising from the intersection of these straight lines, then through them we can express the area of ​​the remaining polygons.”

The proof of this theorem can be the subject of independent research by the reader (Möbius also published it in his book “Barycentric Calculus”).

Let us propose one more problem - it is a kind of continuation of the first one (and just like it, it seems to be an example of a theorem like the Möbius theorem).

In ABCDE, the areas p, q, r, s, t of triangles ACD, BDE, CEA, DAB and EBC are given.

*) The reader will find a detailed presentation of the theory of changes in the “areas of oriented figures” together with applications of this theory (in geometry and geodesy) in the brochure by A. M. Lopshitz - issue 20 of the series “Popular Lectures on Mathematics”, M., 1956.

The area of a p-site λ-conjugate to a given polysite $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ is equal to $P\lambda^2 + Q\lambda + R$, where the numbers P, Q, R are determined by the area of the polysite $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ and the area of the polysite $S_1S_2 \dots S_n$, $1/2$-conjugate to the polysite $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$ (its vertices are the midpoints of successive sides of the polygon $A_1A_2 \dots A_n$: $S_1$ is the midpoint of $A_1A_2$, $S_2$ is the midpoint of $A_2A_3$, etc.).
