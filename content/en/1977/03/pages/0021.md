Deltona

The curve depicted on the second cover is often called a deltona because of its resemblance to the letter Δ of the Greek alphabet. Its properties were first studied by Leonhard Euler in the 18th century and Jacob Steiner in the 19th century. Deltona is defined here as a curve tangent to each of the lines of the family, which is constructed like this. The projections of any point on a circle circumscribed about an arbitrary triangle onto its sides (or their extensions) lie on the same straight line (proof). This is Simson's straight line*). The family consists of Simson lines constructed for the “red” triangle.

Let's look at another definition of deltona. If a circle rolls without sliding inside another circle, then the curve described by a fixed point of the moving circle is called a hypocyclode. Deltona is a hypocyclode, for which the ratio of the radii of these circles is 1:3.

Let first the point M of the changing circle, whose trajectory we will follow, be located at the larger point V (Fig. 1). Then the movable circle rolled along the stationary arc VU; let's put VOU=φ. Let's show that the tangent to the deltona at point M passes through the point P of intersection of OU with the moving circle, and let's look at what angle of the surface the tangent reaches from the initial position OV.

The speed v of point M is equal to v₁ + v₂, where v₁⊥ OU, v₂⊥ MO' and |v₁|=|v₂|. The parallelogram of velocities is a rhombus, and v is its diagonal, MO'U=3φ and MPU=3φ/2. Let P' be the point of intersection of the tangent to the delta with the line OU. It's not difficult to calculate: MP'U=3φ/2=MPU. Points P and P' coincide, the tangent passes through P and the angle OWP=3φ/2.

Let us outline by proving the equivalence of the given definitions. Euler established that nine points of an arbitrary triangle: the bases of its altitudes, medians and midpoints of segments from the vertices to the intersection of the altitudes (Fig. 2) lie on the same circle (prove). Its center is the midpoint O of the segment HN, where H is the point of intersection of the heights, N is the center of the circumscribed circle, and the radius is half the radius of the circumscribed circle (prove this too). Therefore, the circumcircle and Euler circle are prepared with center H and availability factor 2.

Let L be a point of the circumcircle (Fig. 3). Let us show that its Simson straight line passes through the middle of the segment HL, which belongs to the Euler circle. To do this, we lower the perpendicular from point L to the nearest side of the triangle and continue it until it meets the circle at point D. The Simson straight line is parallel to BD (on the one hand, LCL₃=LL₂L₃, on the other, LCL₃=BAL=BEL=DBE). By construction, the segment LL₂ is congruent and parallel to FG and IH (the point of intersection of the heights and the point E is symmetrical with respect to AC). Consequently, point P lies at the intersection of diagonals HL and IL₂ of the parallelogram LL₂HI, and |HP|=|PL|.

Since the circumcircle and Euler circle are prepared relative to point H with a readiness factor of 2, point P lies on the Euler circle.

If we construct an Euler circle on figure 3, then we can recognize the circle (O, OP) with figure 1 in it, and the tangent WP in the Simson line. How Simson's straight line turns when point L moves along a circumscribed circle is left to the reader to figure out.
