Rutherford's model of experience

Rutherford's landmark experiments on the scattering of alpha particles on heavy nuclei (1906-1911) played an exceptional role in the development of ideas about the structure of the atom. In these experiments, alpha particles emitted by a radioactive source were passed through thin films of gold and other metals. When passing through the films, α-particles deviated from the initial direction at different angles. Scattering α-particles hit a screen coated with zinc sulfide and caused flashes (scinitillations). By observing this scinitillation in a microscope, it was possible to determine how much the trajectory of the part changes when passing through the metal layer.

Most α-particles deviated from the initial direction of motion at very small angles. But some parts deviated quite significantly. And, what was amazing, some alpha particles (about 1 in 20,000) changed the direction of movement almost to the opposite. (These parts were recorded by spots on a screen located so that particles “reflected” from the metal could fall on it.)

It was “obvious” that the scattering of α-particles occurs as a result of their interaction with the positive charge of the atom (the mass of the electron is approximately 8000 times less than the mass of the α-particle, so the electrons could not see the nature of the motion of the α-particle). The atomic model that existed in those years, according to which the positive charge was distributed uniformly throughout the entire volume of the atom (this model was proposed by J. Thomson), was not able to support the results of experiments on the scattering of α particles. The sizes of atoms had already been estimated by that time (~10⁻⁸ cm). And the calculation based on the Thomson model, carried out for α-particles scattering at angles of ~180°, led either to an incredibly large value of the atomic radius, or to an impossible large value of positive charges in the atom *).

To require scattering at large angles, it was necessary to assume that the positive charge averaged in a very small (compared to the size of the atom) region inside the atom. This positive charge—the nucleus of the atom—creates a strong electrostatic field, and an alpha particle entering the field of the nucleus is subject to the Coulomb repulsive force.

Based on this idea of ​​the structure of the atom, Rutherford made a theoretical calculation of the quantitative distribution of the number of parts at different scattering angles. His calculations are in excellent agreement with the experimental results.

So, we briefly recalled the history of the creation of the nuclear model of the atom.
