The Möbius problem and its continuation

A century and a half ago (in 1823), a young thirty-year-old astronomer at the Leippite Observatory, August Möbius, drew the attention of mathematicians to the fact that although Euler had already considered not only simple geometric transformations (movements and subdivisions), but also more general ones, which he called affine*). they are all insufficiently used by geometers.

Möbius points out that multi-geometric problems, the content of which does not use either the concept of length or the measurement of angles, were solved after Euler by means foreign to their content, and expressed his conviction**) that the problems should also be presented as fully as possible, systematically arrange them and strike an independent geometric direction that would not use either trigonometrics or Magister Matheseos (as the Pythagorean theorem was called in the 17th century). At the end of his short article “Letter from Professor Möbius to the publisher of the journal” (Astronomic News, 1825, vol. 3), Möbius writes about the problem he proposed:

“It is not enough to talk about a pillar problem, which, considered in itself, is simply a curiosity; it deserves some attention only insofar as it represents an example of the following theorem, which seems new to me: if each two points of any system of n points located in a plane are connected by a straight line, and if we consider given the areas (independent of each other) of any 2n-5 polygons arising from the intersection of these straight lines, then through them we can express the area of ​​the remaining polygons.”

The proof of this theorem can be the subject of independent research by the reader (Möbius also published it in his book “Barycentric Calculus”).
