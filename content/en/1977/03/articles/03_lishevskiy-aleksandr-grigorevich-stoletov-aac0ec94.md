THE OUTSTANDING RUSSIAN PHYSICIST A. G. STOLETOV WAS BORN ON JOL 29 (August 10), 1839, into the family of a poor Vladimir merchant. His father, Grigory Mikhailovich, owned a small grocery store and a leather workshop. The mother, Alexandra Vasilievna, was also of merchant rank, but the STOLETOV family was not at all like typical merchant families, the gloomy world of which was so masterfully portrayed in his plays by A. N. Ostrovsky. Alexandra Vasilievna was an educated woman and she herself taught her children (before they entered the gymnasium) Russian language and arithmetic.

The house had a good library, and Sasha, having learned to read at the age of four, began to use it early. At the age of five he was already reading completely freely.

In 1849, Alexander STOLETOV entered the Vladimir Gymnasium, from which he graduated in 1856. The graduation certificate states that he “... was recognized as having completed the Gymnasium course with the right to enter the University without a secondary exam and was awarded for excellent success in the sciences... with a gold medal.”

In recent years, the students at the gymnasium have clearly identified Alexander's inclinations. His favorite subjects are mathematics and physics. He indulges in it in class, and at home he makes homemade physical instruments and performs various experiments. It's decided. He will be a physicist!

In the autumn of the same 1856, A.G. STOLETOV was assigned to the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Moscow University as a “state-paid” student (that is, receiving a state stipendio). At that time there were many mediocre professors at the university, but Alexander was lucky with his teachers. He listened to applied mathematics from Nikolai Dmitrievich Brashman, who educated the great P. L. Chebyshev, astronomy was taught to him by Fedor Aleksadrovich Bredikhin, and lectures on analytical geometry, differential and integral calculus, higher algebra and varnation calculus

Sleepless nights, excessive work and herbal stress will affect the health of the young scientist. He falls ill and spends about a year in various treatments. He is forbidden to read, write, or engage in any mental activity. This was the most painful period in the life of A. G. Stoletov. Finally, a council of professors allows him to attend classes with students. And immediately all the doctors’ instructions to take care of your health are forgotten, Alexander Grigorievich once again completely devotes himself to pedagogical and scientific activities.

At that time, Moscow University, like other higher educational institutions in Russia, did not have a physics laboratory. So that scientific research, Russian scientists were forced to study abroad, A. G. Stoletov set himself the goal of creating such a laboratory. He writes letters, petitions, knocks on the thresholds of bureaucratic offices, proves that the university cannot but have its own laboratory. After all, physics is an experimental science. A physicist is not a mathematician; he cannot create science only at his desk.

The entire year 1870 was spent in efforts to build the first physical laboratory in Russia. In the same year, students Aleksandra Grigorievich and his workmate began to gather at Stoletov’s apartment to service various physical problems. A physics circle emerges, which laid the foundation for the Stoletov physics school, which, in turn, gave rise to the school of Russian physicists.

Using electric juice connected to an uncharged body, Galvax showed that as a result of the discharge of the negative electrode when illuminated with ultraviolet light, the charge is transferred to the surrounding body.

Having begun the study of the phenomenon discovered by Hertz (this phenomenon was called the photoelectric effect), Stoletov, repeating the experiments of Hertz, Wiedemann, Ebert and Galvask, subsequently developed a new method that made it possible to construct a quantitative theory of the photoelectric effect.

On figure 2 there is a diagram of the setup with which Stoletov carried out his experiments (this figure Stoletov cited in his manuscripts). The main part of the installation is a device that Stoletov called a mesh capacitor. It consists of a metal mesh - the anode and a flat metal disk - kadada (the mesh capacitor was the prototype of the photo effect). This device (C) is connected in series with a galvanometer (G) in a circuit with a battery (B). When the qadas was illuminated with the strong light of a voltaic arc (A), the galvanometer recorded the presence of current in the circuit.

Using this setup, Stoletov studies various aspects of the photo effect. Based on the results of his experiments, he pretends that a necessary condition for the photoeffect is the absorption of light by cadada materials; that each element of the surface of the cadada participates in the phenomenon independently of the others; that the phenomenon of the photoeffect is practically inertia-free (“... the current appears and disappears simultaneously with illumination, and therefore, during the first illumination, the current is also intermittent with the same period”). By changing the voltage on the electrodes, Stoletov obtains the current-voltage characteristic of the photoelectric effect (mesh capacitor): the photocurrent returns with increasing voltage between the electrodes, and small currents are proportional to the voltage; starting from a certain voltage value, the photocurrent practically does not change as the voltage increases, that is, the photocurrent tends to saturation.

Having placed the device in a glass cylinder from which air could be opened, Stoletov discovered that as the air pressure decreases, the photocurrent returns, reaches the maximum and then kills.

Being increased in the fact that the magnitude of the photocurrent is definitely related to illumination, Stoletov conducts a whole series of experiments in order to establish this dependence. By changing the intensity of the light source, he found that the magnitude of the photocurrent is said to be proportional to the light flux incident on the cadad.

Stoletov's research made it possible to identify the main principles of the photoeffect and showed enormous results.
