2. Internal geometry of surfaces

Obviously, the systematic study of geometry begins with planemetry, that is, the study of figures on a plane. You can study shapes on some other surface, for example, on the surface of a cylinder, sphere, or ellipsoid.

When we say “surface,” we usually mean some kind of “film” somehow located in space. However, when we say “flatness,” we are not trying to somehow embed it in the surrounding space. Occupying the “internal geometry” of the plane, we study the properties of the plane, abstracting from everything that is outside it; for us the plane becomes, as it were, an independent world; in this sense, it itself can be called “space” (of two dimensions).

Similarly, when studying the geometry of a certain surface, it is useful not to try to embed it in space, that is, not to distinguish, say, a plane, a cylindrical surface built on a parabola, and a dihedral angle (Fig. 1) - they are obtained from each other by bending, which does not change the “internal properties” of these surfaces.

To better understand this definition, let's imagine a two-dimensional existing "Blob" living in a surface. Everything that the “Blob” can learn about this surface, changing the length of the lines on the surface with a flexible cord, changing the angles with a protractor, etc., constitutes the internal geometry of the surface. For “The Blob” the whole world is related to the surface in which it lives, and for it there is nothing real for it other than this surface!
