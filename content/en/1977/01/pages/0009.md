It is quite easy to study geodesics on a sphere. It turns out that geodesics on the sphere are great circles.

Let's prove it. Suppose that points A and B on a sphere of radius R were connected by a linear M of length l and l = AB/k (k>1), where AB is the arc of a great circle. Let us divide the curve M into n parts of equal length (Fig. 5), and choose n so that the length l/n of each resulting part is small enough, namely: so that in a circle of radius R for any chord PQ of length less than l/n and the smaller arc subtended by it, the correspondence PQ<|PQ|·k holds (that this is possible for k>1 follows from the definition of long-circle). Let's connect adjacent division points with chords and smaller arcs of large circles. Let the lengths of these arcs be c1, c2, ..., cn, and the lengths of the chords d1, d2, ..., dn. Let us note that c1 + c2 + ... + cn ≥ AB = lk (prove this yourself). It follows that for at least one arc, for example, for arc number i, l/n ≤ c_i/k.

But c_i/k < d_i/k = d_i, that is, l/n < d_i, which is impossible, since a straight segment is the shortest of the lines connecting two points.

Just as on a plane, straight lines come out from a point in all directions, so on a sphere, from any of its points in any “direction” exactly one geodesic comes out. For strips of such geodesics there will be terdines.

It is easy to prove that even a small piece of a spherical surface cannot be bent (without stretching and rolling, even if very small) into a piece of plane.
