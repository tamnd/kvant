From the first law of thermodynamics
$$Q = \Delta U + A^*$$
it follows that in an adiabatic process
$$\Delta U = -A.$$

When the gas expands, the force from which it acts on the piston does positive work ($A > 0$), therefore the internal energy of the gas decreases ($\Delta U < 0$).

We see that during adiabatic expansion of a gas, its internal energy decreases precisely by the amount of work done, that is, the gas does maximum work equal to the decrease in its internal energy. In this case, the temperature position occurs. It is easy to prove that when a gas is adiabatic, its internal energy increases due to the work done on the gas by other bodies.

So, when the gas is adiabatic, it heats up, and when it expands, it cools. Remember, for example, the experiment with the ignition of cotton wool, soaked with ether, during a sharp release of ether vapor in a cylinder. The cooling of air and water vapor contained in it during adiabatic expansion explains the formation of clouds. Heating near the Earth's surface

*) An adiabatic process can also occur in the case when the gas is not in a heat-conducting shell, but expands or contracts so quickly that heat transfer does not have time to occur.

**) Let us recall that $Q$ is the amount of heat transfer received by the system, $\Delta U$ is the change in internal energy, $A$ is the work done by the system on external bodies.
