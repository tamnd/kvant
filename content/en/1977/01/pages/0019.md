The restrictions imposed on the full use of internal energy to perform mechanical work in heat engines do not follow from the first law of thermodynamics. This law “prohibits” only obtaining more work than the energy expended. But if the energy of the heater decreased, for example, by 100 joules, and at the same time the heat engine performed 100 joules of work, then this would not contradict the first law of thermodynamics. However, as it was found out, such a cyclic process is impossible in which the entire amount of heat received from the heater is used to perform work. Inevitably some of this amount of heat is transferred to other bodies at a lower temperature.

The above restrictions on the performance of continuous work in cyclic processes due to internal energy constitute the content of another important law of nature - the second law of thermodynamics.

Especially the use of internal energy makes it possible to understand why the unknown sources of energy around us are useless. Obviously, the energy of such bodies whose temperature is equal to the ambient temperature cannot be used.

How tempting it would seem to use the almost limitless supply of internal energy contained in the atmosphere and ocean waters! However, to obtain work from this energy, it is necessary to have a giant refrigerator that would accept part of this huge amount of heat without heating itself to the temperature of the ocean. That is why, as already indicated, the energy of the oceans is not considered among the energy reserves on the globe.

However, proposals are currently being made about the possibility of using temperature differences in the deep and surface layers of seas and oceans, although this is associated with enormous technical difficulties.
