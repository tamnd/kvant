As in planometry, on a sphere the solution to a problem usually comes down to the distance of a series of triangles. A spherical triangle is made up of three vertices connected in pairs by shortest arcs - these are its sides. But on the sphere there are also double poles, for example, two poles connected by two meridians.

Let us prove that the sum of the angles of a spherical triangle is always greater than π*). To do this, we calculate the area S_{ABC} of the spherical triangle ABC, if the values ​​of its angles Å, B, C (in radians) are known.

For clarity, we will construct the drawing so that point A falls into the verified strip of the sphere (Fig. 7). Let us first calculate the area S_{AA'} of the bigon, which will be obtained if we extend the sides AB and AC to mutual intersection at point A', diametrically opposite to point A. Let the area of ​​the entire spherical surface be equal to S. It is clear that S_{AA'} = S·Å/2π (since the area of ​​a bigon with an angle of 2π, very/no, is equal to S - this is the entire sphere). In the same way, continuing the sides of angle B, we obtain a diagon with area S_{BB'} = S·К/2π, and angle C will lead us to a diagon with area S_{CC'} = S·К/2π. Continuing the sides of the same angles in opposite directions, we obtain additional diagons of the same areas. What part of the sphere will be covered by these pairs of bigons? It is clear that they will cover the entire sphere, and triangles ABC and A'B'C' will be covered three times (for clarity, do this construction for triangle ABC on the plane!). So,

*) The great angle (or simply the angle) between two intersecting curves is the angle between the tangents to these curves drawn at the point of intersection of the curves.

$$\frac{2S}{2\pi} \hat{A} + \frac{2S}{2\pi} \hat{B} + \frac{2S}{2\pi} \hat{C} = S + 4S_{ABC};$$

from here

$$S_{ABC} = \frac{S}{4\pi} (\hat{A} + \hat{B} + \hat{C} - \pi).$$

The area of ​​the triangle turns out to be proportional to the so-called “spherical excess”

$$\varepsilon = \hat{A} + \hat{B} + \hat{C} - \pi.$$

Note that the numbers S and S_{ABC} are positive, which means that the excess of ε is positive, in other words, the sum of the angles of a spherical triangle is always greater than π.

It is interesting that on the Lobachevsky plane the sum of the angles of a triangle is always less than π, and its area is proportional to the “defect” δ = π—(Å + B + C).

In geometry on a sphere, the same signs of equality of triangles are valid as in polymetry (on a side and two angles, on two sides and the angle between them, on those sides), but there is one more: on three angles - there are no similar triangles on the sphere! (The evidence is the same as in ploimetry.)
