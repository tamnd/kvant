To avoid this, Watt added pantographic extensions to the three-link, obtaining a complete parallelogram (Fig. 3; $|O_1A_1| = |O_2A_2| = |B_2A_2| = |B_1A_1|$, $|B_1B_2| = |A_1A_2|$, $|MA_1| = |MA_2|$, the rod $O_2B_2$ at point $A_2$ does not bend), after which the mechanism itself began to be called *Watm's parallelogram*. Due to the similarity of triangles $O_2A_2M$ and $O_2B_2B_1$, points $O_2$, $M$ and $B_1$ always lie on the same straight line. On the same basis, $|O_2B_1|$ is twice as large as $|O_2M|$; therefore, point $B_1$ describes the trajectory, the preparatory trajectory of point $M$, to a satisfactory scale. In practice, the improvement was that the piston was attached not at point $M$ of the three-link $O_1A_1A_2O_2$, but at point $B_1$ - the top of the parallelogram $A_1B_1B_2A_2$; Thus, Watt managed to completely coordinate the dimensions of the mechanism with the dimensions of the general engine room.

figure 3 shows a closed curve, which is described by point $M$ (if the movement of the three-link is not limited); only arc $MN$ of this curve can be used in the machine.

The remarkable Russian scientist Pafun Lvovich Chebyshev (1821-1894) worked extensively and fruitfully on hinged mechanisms. He shows a special inclination towards them from an early age. He never stopped working on them throughout his life. Chebyshev himself made many different articulated mechanisms, among them a boat with a rowing apparatus, a walking man (“a hundred-walking machine”) and other wonderful things.

P. L. Chebyshev was mainly interested in those mechanisms that could most accurately reproduce rectilinear motion.
