We bring the cylinder into contact with the heated gas, allowing it to expand and do work. The gas receives from the heated amount of heat $Q$. This process of graphically depicting manufacturing is called manufacturing (curve $ab$ on figure 3, a).

Next, the gas should be sloped, but, as has already been found out, at a lower temperature, that is, the slope isotherm should be lower than the expansion isotherm. After all, only in this case the work of the skatia will be less than the work of the expansion. But we remember that the gas should not be cooled by a resistance with a colder body in order to eliminate heat transfer without doing work. Sadi Carnot wrote: “In bodies used to develop the driving force of heat, there should not be a single change in temperature that does not occur from a change in volume.” In other words, the temperature of the working body should not change without the heat doing work. This means that the only option left is to cool the gas, giving it the opportunity to expand adiabatically.

However, the manufacturing process of expansion is not completed until the end of the piston stroke in the cylinder. When the gas volume becomes equal to a certain value $V_1'$,do the cylinder is isolated from being heated; after this, the gas expands adiabatically to a volume $V_2$, corresponding to the maximum possible stroke of the piston in the cylinder (adiabat $bc$ at figure 3, b). In this case, the gas is cooled to a temperature of $T_2$.

Now the cooled gas can be produced by producing scatium at a temperature of $T_2$. To do this, it must be brought into contact with a body having the same temperature $T_2$ (refrigerator), and the gas must be rolled up by an external force. However, in this process the gas will not return to its original state - its temperature $T_2$ will always be less than $T_1$.
