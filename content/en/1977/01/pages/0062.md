⟦folio 60⟧

Any map on a plane can be colored in four colors

This is the name of a note that appeared in September 1976 in the journal of the American Mathematical Society, Bulletin of American Mathematical Society. This message attracted the attention of professional mathematicians and mathematics lovers of his world: the replacement problem of four colors of solution!

For more than a hundred years, this problem has captivated the imagination of mathematicians with its formulation - no less simple than the formulation of Fermat's Great Theorem, and at the same time more profound; work on this problem stimulated the creation of graph theory, which is now so popular and important for applications.

Our magazine has repeatedly talked about the problem of four colors*).

Here are a few episodes from the hundred-year history of this problem.

1840 The German astronomer and geometer A. Mobius proved that “it is impossible to measure five regions on a plane so that each of them has a common boundary.” This simple theorem is a prelude to the four-color problem.

1852 The English mathematician De Morgan writes to his friend and colleague W. Hamilton about a problem invented by one of his students: “Any map on a sphere can be colored in no more than four colors so that any two neighboring countries are colored in different colors.” I didn’t remember this problem for a quarter of a century.

1878 From the rostrum of the British Geographical Society, the English mathematician A. Cayley again formulates the problem of four colors (and adds that he himself has not been able to solve it for several days - this happened almost a hundred years ago!).

Since then, this problem (or hypothesis*) has become widely and to some extent inexpensively known. “The four-color hypothesis can rightfully be called the “four-color disease,” since it is in many ways similar to a disease...”**) Thousands of amateur mathematicians, including famous libra (O. Veblen, F. Franklin, etc.), were “sick” of it.

*) See articles: for younger schoolchildren - “Topological experiments with your own hands”, 1974, No. 2, p. 58; for schoolchildren in grades 7-9 - “Maps and Coloring Books”, 1972, No. 4, p. 33 and for tenth graders - a rather complex article “Arithmetic on a Geographical Map”, 1974, No. 4, p. 23.

*) The hypothesis is that the map can be cut, the problem is to decide whether the hypothesis is true.

**) F. Harari, Graph Theory. Transl. from English, publishing house "Mir", M., 1973, p. 151.
