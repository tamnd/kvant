⟦folio 14⟧

In a thermal process, the internal energy of the gas does not change ($\Delta U = 0$). Then from the first law of thermodynamics it follows that

$$Q = A,$$

that is, during isothermal expansion, the amount of heat received by the gas from the energy source is equal to the work done.

In addition to those considered, a variety of gas expansion processes are possible. But in addabate and isothermal processes it is most useful to use internal energy to do work. In these processes, the gas gives energy to the chalk when doing work. In the addabate process, the gas does work at the expense of its internal energy, and this energy decreases. In an isothermal process, a gas does work due to the internal energy of another body (energy source), from which it receives a certain amount of heat.

Cyclic processes

Do six pores consider only one-time gas expansion processes, which stop when the gas pressure becomes equal to the external one. But for continuous operation it is necessary that the process of gas expansion be repeated periodically. And for this, it is necessary that after the gas expands there is a slope again, so that the piston returns to its original position and the gas can expand again. Processes in which the body's work periodically returns to its initial state are called cyclic. So the continuous work of thermal motion consists of repeating cycles.

Should a cycle consist of such processes in order for the efficiency of movement to be maximum? Obviously, it must consist of processes that make it possible to perform maximum work due to the amount of heat received from the energy source and the internal energy of the body’s work. Such processes, as it was found out, are isothermal and addabatic. Therefore, during the expansion and collapse of gas, these processes must be used, since only they make it possible to exclude contact of a hot body with a cold one, that is, to exclude a decrease in energy without doing work.

For the first time, such a more advanced cyclic process was proposed by the remarkable French physicist and engineer Sadi Carnot in 1824.

Carnot lived a short life - only 36 years, but left a bright mark on science. In his work “Reflections on the driving force of fire and on machines capable of developing this force,” which provided his name, he laid the foundations for the theory of heat engines. Essentially, he was the founder of the science of thermal phenomena - thermodynamics.

Sadi Carnot was looking for ways to solve a problem that was relevant for his time - to establish the principle of the imperfection of heat engines and find their most effective use.

His works are the most striking example in the history of physics of the mutual influence of science and technology.
