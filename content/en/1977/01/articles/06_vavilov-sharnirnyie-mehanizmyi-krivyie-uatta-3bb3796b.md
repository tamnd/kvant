A common misconception is that a significant part of the activity of a mathematician (or theoretical physicist) is experimental. Before proving anything, the mathematician spends a lot of time, considering various examples and special cases, to free himself with new objects and find patterns that can then be presented in the form of a “correct problem” or “theorem.” Only after a lot of work, having covered mountains of paper with specific calculations or covered it with graphs, does a mathematician begin solving an abstract mathematical problem or proving a theorem. Many results of mathematical science that are important for practical applications are also not abstract theorems, but tables for calculations, graphs, diagrams, and programs for calculations.

Our new section “Mathematical Workshop” is dedicated to this “experimental” part of mathematics. By completing the tasks of the “Mathematical Workshop”, you will learn to find general patterns through several specific observations, and you will be able, if you understand the topic of the task, to understand it in depth and, perhaps, make your first independent discoveries. As a rule, our tasks for a mathematical workshop will consist of two or several steps: the first is to carry out an “experiment” (write several curves or figures, solve a series of equations, etc.), the next is to formulate a general problem, write a general equation of the phenomenon or process being studied, in a word, check some research.

In each of our assignments, we provide a specific example to follow and describe the work plan. We ask that letters with completed assignments be written to the editor with the note “Mat. workshop." Complete graphs and drawings on standard sheets of paper. We ask that you do not roll the sheets into a tube when shipping. Please include a self-addressed envelope with your letter for your reply.

*) James Watt (1736-1819) - an outstanding English inventor. While working as a mechanic at the University of Glasgow, he studied steam engines.

called abbreviated Watt's parallelogram: it consists of two rods $O_1A_1$ and $O_2A_2$ of the same length (rocker arm and outlet radius), connected to each other using hinges of the rods $A_1A_2$ (connecting rod). Rods $O_1A_1$ and $O_2A_2$ rotate around fixed points $O_1$ and $O_2$, and, if the lengths of the rods allow, a whole series of different “cruciform” figures p (Fig. 2, a, b) can be obtained.

Having carefully examined the movement of the point $M$ of the shatur $A_1A_2$, Watt was convinced that the middle point of the shatur $A_1A_2$ slightly deviates from the straight line when all the systems as a whole begin to move (Fig. 3).

The three-bar $O_1A_1A_2O_2$ has one drawback: the distance between points $O_1$ and $O_2$ (between the axes of rotation of the rocker arm and the outlet radius) must be quite significant so that the suitable part of the trajectory (that is, the part of the curve that differs little from a straight line) of the movement of the middle point of the shatur is extended; this led to an unacceptable increase in the size of the ma-

To avoid this, Watt added pantographic extensions to the three-link, obtaining a complete parallelogram (Fig. 3; $|O_1A_1| = |O_2A_2| = |B_2A_2| = |B_1A_1|$, $|B_1B_2| = |A_1A_2|$, $|MA_1| = |MA_2|$, the rod $O_2B_2$ at point $A_2$ does not bend), after which the mechanism itself began to be called *Watm's parallelogram*. Due to the similarity of triangles $O_2A_2M$ and $O_2B_2B_1$, points $O_2$, $M$ and $B_1$ always lie on the same straight line. On the same basis, $|O_2B_1|$ is twice as large as $|O_2M|$; therefore, point $B_1$ describes the trajectory, the preparatory trajectory of point $M$, to a satisfactory scale. In practice, the improvement was that the piston was attached not at point $M$ of the three-link $O_1A_1A_2O_2$, but at point $B_1$ - the top of the parallelogram $A_1B_1B_2A_2$; Thus, Watt managed to completely coordinate the dimensions of the mechanism with the dimensions of the general engine room.

The remarkable Russian scientist Pafun Lvovich Chebyshev (1821-1894) worked extensively and fruitfully on hinged mechanisms. He shows a special inclination towards them from an early age. He never stopped working on them throughout his life. Chebyshev himself made many different articulated mechanisms, among them a boat with a rowing apparatus, a walking man (“a hundred-walking machine”) and other wonderful things.

P. L. Chebyshev was mainly interested in those mechanisms that could most accurately reproduce rectilinear motion. In particular, he found that in the case of Watt's parallelogram, the “best” curves are obtained when the correspondence $(2|O_1A_1|)^2 + |A_1A_2|^2 = |O_1O_2|^2$ is satisfied, which means that there must be such a state of the mechanism,
