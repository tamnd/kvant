SPACE AND INTERNAL GEOMETRY OF SURFACES

1. Our space - what is it like?

The geometry we study at school is Eclidian geometry. But is it true that the space in which we live is structured this way? Is it Euclidean? Are Euclid's axioms true in our real space?

How could it be otherwise? After all, the axioms are so very strong: “Only one straight line passes through two points”; “Every straight line is infinite in both directions”; “A perpendicular and an oblique line to the same line are not intersecting,” etc.

It seems that all this is so obvious that it cannot be otherwise! This is what the famous German philosopher I. Kant said: “It is impossible to think otherwise!”, That is, there are truths that we know without any experience - “a priori.” And in support of the existence of such “a priori” truths, Kant cited precisely geometric axioms, as self-evident facts, possessing undoubted merit, independent of any experience. But our great compatriot N.I. Lobachevsky showed that it is perfectly possible to think differently!

He constructed a geometry in which all of Euclid's axioms are satisfied, except one - the third of the above *). It turned out that this geometry is not only no worse than Euclidean, but in some respects even more perfect and rich.

Is it possible to refuse other axioms? Is it possible to imagine that a straight line has a finite length? Isn't it clear that it is infinite? But, let’s face it, has anyone ever gone so far in a straight line that they became convinced of its infinity? Perhaps, if we go very, very far in a straight line, then suddenly we end up at the starting point of the journey? “What a ridiculous proposal!” - Believe it or not, some readers thought. Meanwhile, this is exactly what would happen to a person who, believing that the Earth is flat, would set off in a certain direction, trying all the time to walk along the shortest line (on a sphere this is the circumference of a “great circle”); he would think that he was walking in a straight line, and suddenly he would come to the starting point of the journey.

Perhaps we just think that a straight line is infinite, but one day it will turn out to be a closed linear one, like a circle, only of a very large length? You never know there were extremely clear things that later turned out to be simply wrong. After all, it was clear (until people knew that the Earth is a sphere) that the verticals are strictly parallel, and infinitely, that they intersect in the center of the Earth. It seemed to Magellan's companion that if they carefully made entries in the ship's log every day, then, having traveled around the world (that is, around the Earth) and returned home, they would not be mistaken in counting the days. But it turned out that one day was missing.

There is every reason to suggest that our space is more complex than Euclid thought. Lobachevsky himself showed very well how it is possible to study a space other than the “usual” one (the expression of N. I. Lobachevsky), having constructed his geometry, therefore, for the first acquaintance with “non-Euclidean” spaces, Lobachevsky’s geometry is often chosen. We will talk about more interesting things.

2. Internal geometry of surfaces

Obviously, the systematic study of geometry begins with planemetry, that is, the study of figures on a plane. You can study shapes on some other surface, for example, on the surface of a cylinder, sphere, or ellipsoid.

When we say “surface,” we usually mean some kind of “film” somehow located in space. However, when we say “flatness,” we are not trying to somehow embed it in the surrounding space. Occupying the “internal geometry” of the plane, we study the properties of the plane, abstracting from everything that is outside it; for us the plane becomes, as it were, an independent world; in this sense, it itself can be called “space” (of two dimensions).

Similarly, when studying the geometry of a certain surface, it is useful not to try to embed it in space, that is, not to distinguish, say, a plane, a cylindrical surface built on a parabola, and a dihedral angle (Fig. 1) - they are obtained from each other by bending, which does not change the “internal properties” of these surfaces.

To better understand this definition, let's imagine a two-dimensional existing "Blob" living in a surface. Everything that the “Blob” can learn about this surface, changing the length of the lines on the surface with a flexible cord, changing the angles with a protractor, etc., constitutes the internal geometry of the surface. For “The Blob” the whole world is related to the surface in which it lives, and for it there is nothing real for it other than this surface!

It is most convenient to construct geometry on “homogeneous” and “isotropic” surfaces.

At school you studied plane movements. Recall that moving a plane is a mapping of the plane onto itself that preserves distances, that is, the lengths of segments connecting two points. Similarly, moving a surface is a mapping of this surface onto itself that preserves the lengths of all lines on it. Such mappings are also called isometries. We emphasize that we do not define the concept of “line length” here; you just need to imagine the lines on the surface as a thread that can be separated from the surface, applied to the “meter” and changed its length.

A surface is called homogeneous if, for any two of its points A and B, it is possible to indicate the isometry of the surface, in which point A is mapped to point B. This means that the surface is “equally arranged around any of its points.” This is, for example, the surface of an infinite circular cylinder (Fig. 2).

A surface is called an isometry at some point A if, no matter what two “directions” Am and An emanating from A are taken on it (Fig. 3), there is a movement of the surface at which point A remains in place, and Am goes over to An. For example, any bracing surface intersecting the bracing axis at point A is isotopic at A.

A surface is called isometric if it is isotropic at every point. Interestingly, an isotropic surface is always homogeneous.

One example of an isotropic surface is a plane; Below we will look at another one - the sphere.

3. Geodetic lines

Let us consider between points A and B on the surface of the natural name the length of the shortest line connecting these points and lying entirely on the surface. Therefore, in the internal geometry of any surface, geodesic lines are especially important: they play the role of straight lines. These lines are defined as follows: for any two points A, B of such a line that are not too far from each other, its piece AB is shorter than any other line connecting A with B and lying on the surface.

It is quite easy to study geodesics on a sphere. It turns out that geodesics on the sphere are great circles.

Let's prove it. Suppose that points A and B on a sphere of radius R were connected by a linear M of length l and l = AB/k (k>1), where AB is the arc of a great circle. Let us divide the curve M into n parts of equal length (Fig. 5), and choose n so that the length l/n of each resulting part is small enough, namely: so that in a circle of radius R for any chord PQ of length less than l/n and the smaller arc subtended by it, the correspondence PQ<|PQ|·k holds (that this is possible for k>1 follows from the definition of long-circle). Let's connect adjacent division points with chords and smaller arcs of large circles. Let the lengths of these arcs be c1, c2, ..., cn, and the lengths of the chords d1, d2, ..., dn. Let us note that c1 + c2 + ... + cn ≥ AB = lk (prove this yourself). It follows that for at least one arc, for example, for arc number i, l/n ≤ c_i/k.

But c_i/k < d_i/k = d_i, that is, l/n < d_i, which is impossible, since a straight segment is the shortest of the lines connecting two points.

Just as on a plane, straight lines come out from a point in all directions, so on a sphere, from any of its points in any “direction” exactly one geodesic comes out. For strips of such geodesics there will be terdines.

Find the shortest path along its surface between points A and A', located symmetrically relative to the center of the paralleliped on its square faces on the middle vertical ones (for clarity, we consider the 30x12 faces to be the floor and the ceiling), point A - at a height of 1 above the floor, A' - at a height of 1 below the floor. Many, it is believed, will shout: “The length of the shortest path is 42!” However, this is not true - there is a noticeably shorter path! Find him.
