Since ancient times, people have used the internal energy possessed by the seas and oceans*. The mass of water in the world's oceans is about $10^{21}$ kk. Cooling it by only 1 degree would lead to the release of energy on the order of $10^{24}$ J. This is 100,000,000 times more than all the energy generated on the globe in a year!

It would seem that the energy reserves on the globe are almost limitless. However, the internal energy of the atmosphere and oceans is not considered energy reserves. This is due to the peculiarities of using this type of energy.

To use the internal energy of the body, you need to “take it away” from it. This can be done in two ways - during the process of heat transfer, when the body gives off a certain amount of heat, or when it performs work.

Heat reduction is used, for example, in a number of technological processes associated with heating the body, as well as for heating rooms using the internal energy of hot steam or water.

But, as already noted, the most important thing is the use of the internal energy of the body to perform mechanical work.

Mechanical work is performed only when the ordering of movement occurs - the movement of the body. Internal energy is the energy of randomly (chaotic) moving molecules. Consequently, in order for work to be done due to internal energy, it is necessary to find a way to “transform” the chaotic movement of molecules into the ordered movement of any macrosopic body. And this is not an easy task! Any ordered movement of a body in real conditions (with

Such conditions can be created, for example, by using a cylinder with a piston. The random movement of gas molecules in the cylinder causes the piston to move. The piston cylinder forms the main part of the firearm. A firearm is thus a kind of heat engine. But this is not a continuous engine, which is necessary to drive machine tools, generators at power plants, vehicles, etc.

In order for the engine to operate continuously, it is necessary that the piston, after expanding the gas, returns to its original position each time, and the gas can expand again. And for this, an external force must do work on the gas flow in the cylinder. If the downward work done by the external force is equal to the work done by the gas during expansion, then the efficiency of the engine is obviously zero. It is necessary that the work be less than the work of gas expansion.

As is known, the elementary work performed when the volume of gas changes from the value $V_i$ to the value $V_i + \Delta V_i$ is equal to

$$A_i = p_i \Delta V_i.$$

It is clear to Pozny that in order for the work done by the external force during the descent of gas to be less than the work done by the gas during expansion, it is necessary that each value of the volume of gas during descent corresponds to less pressure than during expansion. As is known, the lower the temperature of a gas at the same volume, the lower it is. This means that the gas before the downfall must be cooled.

It would seem that cooling the gas is a simple task. For example, you can do this by bringing it into contact with bodies of lower temperature. But in this case, the energy of the chaotic movement of gas molecules is converted into the energy of the chaotic movement of other bodies, and it will be required to perform work. That is why the first thermal movements that use this method of cooling steam before it escapes have an extremely low efficiency - up to 5%. Obviously, it was necessary to find another way to cool the gas.

Thus, technical needs set the task for science to study the operation of heat engines and find ways to increase the efficiency of their operation.

Gas expansion process

So, internal energy, released from a heated body when it comes into contact with a colder body, is completely useless for doing work. It only increases the energy of the xaotic movement of the molecules of a cold body.

Therefore, if we want to obtain maximum work during gas expansion, it is necessary that the gas in this process does not collide with bodies that have a lower temperature.

There are two ways to exclude the contact of the body with colder bodies: placing the gas in a vessel whose walls are made of heat-conducting material, or maintaining constant contact of the gas with bodies that have the same temperature as itself. Let's consider both methods.

Adiabatic process

Let's imagine that the slope gas is in a cylinder with a movable piston (Fig. 1). The cylinder and piston are made of ideal heat-conducting material. Under these conditions, the gas will expand and do some work $A$ without transferring heat to the environment ($Q = 0$). This is a process in which heat

transmission is excluded, called adiabatic *).

From the first law of thermodynamics
$$Q = \Delta U + A^*$$
it follows that in an adiabatic process
$$\Delta U = -A.$$

When the gas expands, the force from which it acts on the piston does positive work ($A > 0$), therefore the internal energy of the gas decreases ($\Delta U < 0$).

We see that during adiabatic expansion of a gas, its internal energy decreases precisely by the amount of work done, that is, the gas does maximum work equal to the decrease in its internal energy. In this case, the temperature position occurs. It is easy to prove that when a gas is adiabatic, its internal energy increases due to the work done on the gas by other bodies.

So, when the gas is adiabatic, it heats up, and when it expands, it cools. Remember, for example, the experiment with the ignition of cotton wool, soaked with ether, during a sharp release of ether vapor in a cylinder. In a thermal process, the internal energy of the gas does not change ($\Delta U = 0$). Then from the first law of thermodynamics it follows that

$$Q = A,$$

that is, during isothermal expansion, the amount of heat received by the gas from the energy source is equal to the work done.

In addition to those considered, a variety of gas expansion processes are possible. But in addabate and isothermal processes it is most useful to use internal energy to do work. In these processes, the gas gives energy to the chalk when doing work. In the addabate process, the gas does work at the expense of its internal energy, and this energy decreases. In an isothermal process, a gas does work due to the internal energy of another body (energy source), from which it receives a certain amount of heat.

Cyclic processes

Do six pores consider only one-time gas expansion processes, which stop when the gas pressure becomes equal to the external one. But for continuous operation it is necessary that the process of gas expansion be repeated periodically. And for this, it is necessary that after the gas expands there is a slope again, so that the piston returns to its original position and the gas can expand again. Processes in which the body's work periodically returns to its initial state are called cyclic. So the continuous work of thermal motion consists of repeating cycles.

Should a cycle consist of such processes in order for the efficiency of movement to be maximum? Obviously, it must consist of processes that make it possible to perform maximum work due to the amount of heat received from the energy source and the internal energy of the body’s work. Such processes, as it was found out, are isothermal and addabatic. Therefore, during the expansion and collapse of gas, these processes must be used, since only they make it possible to exclude contact of a hot body with a cold one, that is, to exclude a decrease in energy without doing work.

For the first time, such a more advanced cyclic process was proposed by the remarkable French physicist and engineer Sadi Carnot in 1824.

Carnot lived a short life - only 36 years, but left a bright mark on science. In his work “Reflections on the driving force of fire and on machines capable of developing this force,” which provided his name, he laid the foundations for the theory of heat engines. Essentially, he was the founder of the science of thermal phenomena - thermodynamics.

Sadi Carnot was looking for ways to solve a problem that was relevant for his time - to establish the principle of the imperfection of heat engines and find their most effective use.

His works are the most striking example in the history of physics of the mutual influence of science and technology.

So, in section $abc$ the gas does work ($A > 0$), and in section $cda$ work is done on the gas ($A < 0$). In sections $bc$ and $da$, work is performed only due to a change in the internal energy of the gas. Since $\Delta U_{bc} = -\Delta U_{da}$, so is $A_{bc} = -A_{da}$. So the total work done per cycle is determined by the difference in work done in sections $ab$ and $cd$. Numerically, this work is equal to the area of ​​the figure bounded by the cycle curve $abcd$ (Fig. 3, e).

In the section $ab$ the gas receives a certain amount of heat $Q_1$ from the heater with a temperature $T_1$, and in the section $cd$ the gas invariably transfers an amount of heat $Q_2$ to the refrigerator with a temperature $T_2$ when falling. This means that only part of the amount of heat $Q_1$ received from the heater, equal to $Q_1 - Q_2$, is actually converted into useful work. Therefore, the efficiency of the cycle is equal to

$$\eta = \frac{Q_1 - Q_2}{Q_1}.$$

If the gas is not cooled before rolling down and, therefore, does not transfer a certain amount of heat to a colder body, then the useful work per cycle will be zero. In this state, it is especially true that mechanical work is performed by heat engines: it is impossible to completely convert the entire amount of heat received from the heater into work during a cyclic process. It is inevitable that some part of this amount of heat must be separated to a third body with a lower temperature. This means that for heat engines to operate, it is not enough to have only an energy source (heater) and a working fluid. A third body with a lower temperature (refrigerator) is needed. The surrounding atmosphere often serves as such a refrigerator. For continuous operation of any heat engine, you must have a cutter, a work place and a refrigerator.

So, the Carnot cycle was carried out at all stages in such a way that there was no contact of the body with different temperatures anywhere. This excludes the possibility of heat transfer without doing work, that is, it excludes the transition of the random movement of molecules of one body into the random movement of molecules of other bodies.

As Sadi Carnot showed, the efficiency of an ideal cycle can be expressed in terms of the temperatures of the heater ($T_1$) and refrigerator ($T_2$):

$$\eta = \frac{Q_1 - Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{T_1},$$

or

$$\eta = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1}.$$

As a result of the movement, it was not possible to implement a cycle consisting of ideal isotherms and adnabate. The fact is that the processes in engines occur quite quickly, and the isothermal nature of the processes is violated—a decrease in temperature as a result of elementary expansion “does not have time” to cope with an increase in temperature due to contact with the heater. The materials from which the cylinder walls and piston are made cannot be ideal insulators, as is necessary for the adnabate process. Therefore, the efficiency of the cycle carried out as a result of movement is always less than the efficiency of the Carnot cycle (at the same temperatures of the heater and refrigerators).

At the same time, consideration of the ideal Carnot cycle is of great importance, since it indicates ways to increase the efficiency of heat engines. From formula (*) it is clear that the higher the temperature of the heater and the lower the temperature of the refrigerator, the greater the efficiency of the engine.

The restrictions imposed on the full use of internal energy to perform mechanical work in heat engines do not follow from the first law of thermodynamics. This law “prohibits” only obtaining more work than the energy expended. But if the energy of the heater decreased, for example, by 100 joules, and at the same time the heat engine performed 100 joules of work, then this would not contradict the first law of thermodynamics. However, as it was found out, such a cyclic process is impossible in which the entire amount of heat received from the heater is used to perform work. Inevitably some of this amount of heat is transferred to other bodies at a lower temperature.

The above restrictions on the performance of continuous work in cyclic processes due to internal energy constitute the content of another important law of nature - the second law of thermodynamics.

Especially the use of internal energy makes it possible to understand why the unknown sources of energy around us are useless. Obviously, the energy of such bodies whose temperature is equal to the ambient temperature cannot be used.

How tempting it would seem to use the almost limitless supply of internal energy contained in the atmosphere and ocean waters! However, to obtain work from this energy, it is necessary to have a giant refrigerator that would accept part of this huge amount of heat without heating itself to the temperature of the ocean. That is why, as already indicated, the energy of the oceans is not considered among the energy reserves on the globe.

However, proposals are currently being made about the possibility of using temperature differences in the deep and surface layers of seas and oceans, although this is associated with enormous technical difficulties.
