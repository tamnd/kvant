Mathematical principles of natural philosophy

Preface *)

“...Mechanics is the doctrine of motion produced by any movement, and of the movement required to produce any movement, precisely stated and proven.

The ancients developed this part of mechanics only in the form of the doctrine of five machines **) used in crafts; at the same time, even weight (since this is not a strengthening produced by the hands) was considered precisely not as force, but only as loads moved by the said machines. The whole difficulty of physics, as will be seen, consists in recognizing the forces of nature from the phenomena of motion, and then using these forces to explain other phenomena. The general proposals set forth in books one and two are intended for this purpose. In the third book, we give an example of the above-mentioned application, explaining the system of the world, or here, from celestial phenomena, using assumptions proven in previous books, mathematical forces are derived from the gravitational forces of bodies towards the Sun and individual planets. Then, from these forces, also with the help of mathematical propositions, the movements of the planets, comets, Lunya and the sea are deduced. It would be desirable to derive from the principles of mechanics the rest of the phenomena of nature, distinguishing them in a similar way, or many things force me to assume that all these phenomena are determined by certain forces from which the particles of bodies, as a result of reasons as yet unknown, either tend to each other and adhere to regular figures, or mutually repel each other. Since these forces are unknown, until now the attempts of philosophers to explain natural phenomena have remained fruitless. I hope that either this method of alienation, or another, more correct one, the reasons outlined here will receive unrevealed illumination.

During the publication of this work, Edmund Halley, the most witty man in all areas of learning, contributed to the content, who not only corrected the typographical proofs and became familiar with the production of figure, but even at his insistence, he began the publication itself. Having received from me evidence of the type of orbits of celestial bodies, he constantly insisted that I report them to the Royal Society, which then, with its kind attention and care, made me think about releasing them into the world...”
