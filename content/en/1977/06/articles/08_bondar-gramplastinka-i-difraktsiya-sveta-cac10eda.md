The gramophone record and the diffraction of light

A. Bondar

The phenomenon of light diffraction is studied in the 10th grade, so only 10th graders will probably be able to fully understand all the theoretical calculations carried out in this article. And we advise everyone to continue the experience, which is very simple and beautiful.

As is known, one of the most accurate methods for determining the spectral composition of the radiation under study is based on the phenomenon of diffraction. A good spectral device is a diffraction grating. It turns out that usually a gramophone record can also be used to observe diffraction and, in particular, to measure the wavelength of visible light.

During sound recording, grooves are applied to the surface of the record at equal distances from each other. These grooves scatter light, and the spaces between them reflect it. Thus, a gramophone record is similar to a reflective diffraction grating. If the width of the reflecting stripes is $a$, and the width of the scattering grooves is $b$, then the value $d = a + b$ is the grating period.

Let a plane monochromatic wave of length $\lambda$ fall on a reflective grating with a period $d$ at an angle $\theta$ to the grating (Fig. 1). (The case of normal incidence of rays is similarly discussed in a textbook for grade 10 (see § 110).) According to the Huygen-Fresnel principle, each point of the reflective surface of the grating becomes an independent precise source, sending light in all possible directions. Let us consider a wave propagating at an angle $\varphi$ to the grating (see Fig. 1). With the help of a collecting lens (for example, the lens of an eye), these waves can be concentrated into one point. Let's find a condition when, when added, the waves will reinforce each other.

The difference in the path of rays 1 and 2, induced from the corresponding points $A$ and $B$ of two adjacent reflective sections of the grating (Fig. 2), is equal to

$$|AK| - |NB| = d \sin \varphi - d \sin \theta = d (\sin \varphi - \sin \theta)$$

(KB is the front of the reflected wave in the direction at an angle $\varphi$, AN is the front of the incident wave). If once

The length of the stroke is a multiple of the wavelength, the phases of the oscillations coming from points A and B will be the same, and therefore the oscillations will reinforce each other. All other reflective sections of the grating behave similarly. Consequently, the conditions for the formation of main maxima can be written as follows:

$$d | \sin \varphi - \sin \theta | = k \lambda,$$

where $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$. From this we can determine the wavelength $\lambda$. To do this, you need to know the grating period $d$, the angle $\theta$ of wave incidence on the grating and the direction to the corresponding maximum - angle $\varphi$. Typically, the grating period is much longer than the wavelength ($d \gg \lambda$), so the angles $\varphi$ are small. This means that the main maxima are located very close to each other, and the diffraction pattern is very unclear. However, the greater the angle of incidence of the rays on the grating (angle $\theta$), the larger the angles $\varphi$ and, therefore, the more convenient it is to make the necessary changes. That is why it is best to use not normal, but oblique incidence of the rays on the grating.

Until now we have been talking about multichromatic light. What if white light, complex in its spectral composition, falls on the grating? From equation (*) it unexpectedly follows that the position of each main maximum depends on the wavelength. The shorter the wavelength, the smaller the value of the angle $\varphi$ corresponds to the maximum. Thus, all maxima (except for zero) grow into a spectrum, the purple end of which faces the center of the diffraction pattern, and the red end faces the outside.
