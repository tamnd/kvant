In this article we will discuss in detail Newton's mathematical achievements.

As Newton's rough notes show, in the field of mathematics he was self-taught - in college he taught only a course in elementary mathematics. The young man's interest in mathematics was aroused by an accidental obstruction: in the summer of 1663, he bought a book on astrology at a fair. To understand it, he needed familiarity with trionometry, which, in turn, required knowledge of Euclidian geometry. So Newton began studying Euclid’s Elements, from which he moved on to the works of Vieta and Descartes on algebra and analytical geometry, then became acquainted with the works of Valis on the infinitesimal calculus. Notebooks show that it took Newotn about a year to master contemporary mathematics and begin independent research. It was at this time that he wrote down the famous formula for the series expansion of $(1+x)^r$, where $r$ is any rational number (we will discuss it below). Already in 1664-1667, Newton began his research on analytical geometry and infinitesimal calculus.

Analytical geometry and the study of triangular order curves

Curves of the second order (ellipse, hyperbola and parabola) were studied by Apollo (3rd century BC). At the time of Apollo there was no letter algebra, so Apollonius wrote down the equations of geometric curves using the equality of certain areas.

Analytical geometry, based on letter algebra, was created only in modern times by Pierre Fermat (1636) and René Descartes (1637), the latter giving letter calculus its modern form. When introducing a coordinate system, both mathematicians drew one coordinate axis (horizontal) with the beginning of the report placed, for the second axis only the direction was specified. Much more important is the fact that neither Descartes nor Fermat derived the properties of curves from the definition of their equations. They were content to reduce the equation to one of the canonical forms found in Apollo, and then simply use his results. Thus, the specifics of the method of analytical geometry remained undisclosed.

Young Newton immediately began to draw on the drawing about the coordinate axes (at an arbitrary angle to each other). All four squares were equally familiar to him, whereas previous research studies had drawn up the curve so that the study was carried out mainly in the first and fourth squares. Newton answered the central point of coordinate transformation and wrote out the formulas for parallel change and rotation.

Finally, Newton began a study of triangle curves, only isolated examples of which were known. He carried out this research with the same exhaustiveness.

Fluxion method

At the same time, Newton began to create a new calculus.

In the 17th century, in connection with the successes of the mechanics of terrestrial and celestial bodies (recall that at that time the laws of falling bodies were discovered by Galileo and the laws of planetary motion by Kepler), scientists began to study the works of Archimedes, trying to extract from them general methods for determining tangents, extrema, velocities, centers of gravity, areas and volumes.

Newton was the first to understand that this entire set of problems falls into two classes of mutually imaginative problems that can be formed in a general form, and a single algorithm can be given to solve them. To do this, Newton introduces, first of all, the concept of fluent (from the Latin word fluere - to flow) - a quantity that changes with a change in time or another quantity (which he called generalized time). Thus, fluenta is the first name for the function. Newton designated fluents with the last letters of the Latin alphabet: x, y, z. He called the rate of change of fluent, i.e. derivative, fluxey and designated, respectively, x, y, z. (In physics, these notations have been retained to this day.) After this, Newton formed two main problems:

I. The correspondence between fluents is given: $f(x, y, z) = 0$. Find a correspondence between their fluxions: $\Phi(\dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{z}, x, y, z) = 0$.

II. The correspondence between fluxes is given: $f(x, y, z, x, y, z) = 0$.
Find correspondence between fluxes: $\Psi(x, y, z) = 0$.

Special cases of the first problem are finding the speed of movement along a path specified as a function of time, finding extrema and tangents. Newton gives an algorithm for solving this problem for the case when the correspondence between fluxes is specified using the polynomial $F(x, y)$ or $F(x, y, z)$.

Newton's rule is as follows: arrange the polynomial in powers of each ubud of fluents, for example $x$; multiply each term by $x/x$ and by the corresponding power $x$; do the same in relation to the other fluente; take the sum of the received terms and equate it to zero.

Let us apply, for example, this rule to the polynomial $y - x^n$. We get $y \cdot \frac{y}{y} - x^n \cdot n \cdot \frac{x}{x} = 0$ or $y = nx^{n-1}x$.

Newton justified his rule as follows: we increase time by an “indefinitely small part” - the moment $o$, then the fluents will increase by $ox$ and $oy$; Let's substitute the new fluent values ​​into the original expression and reduce all terms by $o$. He sets out the central point of his justification as follows: “Not since we assumed $o$ to be an infinitesimal value..., then those terms that are multiplied by it can be considered nothing compared to others. That’s why I neglect them.”

Newton himself was not satisfied with the justification presented and is trying to provide a more solid basis for his calculation. This was done about 150 years later in the works of O. Cauchy (1789-1857) and some other mathematicians of the last century.

Power series

Initially, the fluxion method was applied to polynomials. What about other functions? How, for example, to find fluxiometric functions
$$\frac{x}{a^2+x^2}, \sqrt{ax+b}, \sin x$$ How to solve equation $\dot{y}=\sin x, \dot{y}=\sqrt{ax+b}$?
And so Newton came to the idea that all the functions known to him can be written in a completely uniform manner using power series
$$a_0 + a_1x + a_2x^2 + \dots + a_nx^n + \dots,$$
which are like infinite polynomials. An example of such a notation is (for $|x|<1$) the equality
$$\frac{1}{1-x} = 1+x+x^2+\dots+x^n+\dots.$$
Newton writes that he came to his idea, guided by an analogy with the representation of decimal numbers by fractions: “... the doctrine of literal expressions is in the same relation to algebra as the doctrine of decimal fractions is to ordinary arithmetic... And just as decimal fractions have the advantage that the common fractions and roots expressed in them acquire to some extent the properties of whole numbers, so do alphabetic ones infinite series bring the benefit that soon complex expressions (fractions with a composite denominator, roots of compound quantities or implicit equations, etc.) can be reduced with their help to a series of simple quantities...”

Newoton obtained a power series expansion for the functions $a^x$, $\log_a(1+x)$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $\arcsin x$, $\arccos x$, as well as for fractional and irrational functions. It was for the decomposition of the latter that Newoton generalized the binomial formula known for the positive integer $n$
$$(a+x)^n = a^n + na^{n-1}x + \frac{n(n-1)}{1.2}a^{n-2}x^2 + \dots + x^n$$
for the case when the exponent is any rational number $r$:
$$(a+x)^r = a^r + ra^{r-1}x + \frac{r(r-1)}{1.2}a^{r-2}x^2 + \dots$$
