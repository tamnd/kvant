Aftermath
Since Nikolai Nosov wrote the wonderful poem “Vitya Maleev at school and at home,” the teaching of mathematics at school has changed a lot. Now the subject is called mathematics, and not argumentation, and its content has also changed. The current third or fourth grader will no longer agonize over the problem of nuts. He will denote the number of nuts the girl has by “x”, and then the boy will have “2x” nuts. Thus, there were “3x” nuts in total, and it is known that there were 120 of them. This means that 3x = 120, hence x = 40, 2x = 80. Solution problem.

As for the problem of “saws and axes,” the almighty “x” can come to the rescue here too. Let us recall the final form of the problem, which it took after simplification:
“12 axes and 3 saws cost 84 rubles, 12 axes and 5 saws cost 100 rubles.” Let us denote the cost of the saw by “x”, then from the first condition 12 axes cost 84 - 3x rubles, and one ax (84 - 3x): 12 rubles. Let us now write the second condition:
$$12 \cdot (84 - 3x) : 12 + 5x = 100$$
or
$$84 - 3x + 5x = 100,$$
whence x = 8 (rubles). Again, the standard method comes to the rescue with SEL, which leads to the goal without much thought.

However, don’t you think that Vanya Pakhomov’s solution to the city is more beautiful? She captivates us with the simplicity and elegance of logical reasoning, leading to the solution of a problem. In a hermetically sealed container, the temperature of which is maintained constant, the liquid can be stored for as long as desired without changing its volume. Does this mean that evaporation has stopped? Of course not. As before, the molecules leave the surface of the liquid “fast”, but at the same time condensation of the vapor formed above the liquid occurs. And when the number of molecules leaving the liquid in a given time becomes equal to the number of molecules returning back in the same time, then the masses of both the liquid and the vapor will remain unchanged. Equilibrium occurs between liquid and vapor. Vapor that is in equilibrium with its liquid is called saturated vapor.

The liquid in equilibrium and the vapor colliding with it continuously exchange their molecules. What is the number of molecules exchanged in a given time? How many molecules leave the liquid and return back in a given time?

Let on average the same number of molecules $z_0$ fall through a unit of surface area per unit of time, and $z$ molecules enter it. Equilibrium occurs between liquid and vapor when $z_0 = z$.

It is difficult to calculate the value of $z_0$, if only because we do not know exactly the value of the attractive forces between the molecules of vapor and liquid. But calculating the value of $z$ is quite simple. Indeed, every vapor molecule that reaches the surface of the liquid penetrates inside the liquid; therefore, $z$ is equal to the number of molecules falling per unit time per unit surface area of ​​the liquid. Let's find this number.

Let us direct the $X$ axis perpendicular to the free surface of the liquid in the vessel (Fig. 1). We will assume that the projections of the velocities of all vapor molecules onto the $X$ axis are identical in absolute value. Then half of all molecules have a velocity projection on the $X$ axis

Fig. 1.

is positive and equal to $|v_x|$, and half is negative and equal to $|v_y|$.

For a unit of time, those vapor molecules that are located at a distance $l \leqslant |v_x|$ from it and have a velocity projection onto the $X$ axis equal to $|v_y|$ will fall onto a unit surface area. In other words, per unit time per unit surface area, the area of ​​the vapor molecules located in the volume $V_1 = |v_x|$ applied to the surface. If the volume occupied by steam in a vessel is $V$ and there are $N$ vapor molecules in this volume, then in volume $V_1$ the number of vapor molecules is $N_1 = \frac{N}{V} V_1 = \frac{N |v_x|}{V}$.

Thus, the number of vapor molecules falling per unit time per unit surface area of ​​the liquid is equal to

$$z = \frac{1}{2} \frac{N}{V} |v_x|.$$

We assumed that the projections of molecular velocities onto the $X$ axis are identical in absolute value. However, this is not so, and it would be more accurate to use the non-value $|v_x|$, but the average value of this value. That's why

$$z = \frac{1}{2} \frac{N}{V} |\bar{v}_x|.$$

If the number $z_0$ of molecules shown per unit surface area per unit time is equal to this value $z$, then equilibrium is established between the liquid and the vapor.

So, the number of molecules returning from vapor to liquid depends

not only on temperature (from $|\bar{v}_x|$), but also on vapor density ($\frac{N}{V}$). Consequently, vapor above the liquid becomes saturated when just enough molecules $N$ accumulate in the volume $V$ it occupies so that the conditions are satisfied

$$\frac{1}{2} \frac{N}{V} |\bar{v}_x| = z_0. \tag{1}$$

Recall that the ratio $\frac{N}{V}$ determines the pressure $p$ of steam at a given temperature:

$$p = \frac{N}{V} kT. \tag{2}$$

If the steam is saturated, then, according to (1), $\frac{N}{V} = \frac{2z_0}{|\bar{v}_x|}$, and the steam pressure is equal to

$$p_H = \frac{2z_0 k T}{|\bar{v}_x|}. \tag{3}$$

Thus, we come to the conclusion that the actual pressure of saturated vapor does not depend on the volume it occupies and at a given temperature is fully defined as a value characteristic of a given liquid.

It may seem that the independence of saturated vapor pressure from volume contradicts the gas state equation (2), from which it follows that at a constant temperature $T$ the gas pressure is inversely proportional to the volume $V$ it occupies. Maybe the gas equation of state is not applicable for saturated steam?

In reality, there is no contradiction, and equation (2) does not apply.

From equation (2) it follows that the pressure $p$ of a gas is inversely proportional to its volume, provided that $N$ is constant, that is, when the mass of the gas does not change. But for saturated steam this condition is not met. When the volume of saturated steam changes, its mass also changes. Indeed, if you change saturated steam in such a way that its volume decreases from a value $V$ to some value $V_1$, the ratio $N/V_1$ will immediately decrease. Then, as can be seen from formula (1), the number $z$ of molecules returning from vapor to liquid will decrease, and it will exceed the number $z_0$

Fig. 2.

"evaporating" molecules. As a result, the number $N$ of vapor molecules begins to decrease. When it decreases to such a value $N_1$ at which

$$\frac{N_1}{V_1} = \frac{N}{V},$$

balance will be restored. But then, in accordance with equation (2), the pressure $p$ of the steam will also be restored. This is how it happens experimentally.

When the volume of saturated steam decreases, part of it condenses and the mass of the steam decreases as many times as its volume decreases. Therefore, saturated vapor pressure does not depend on volume. (It is not difficult to prove that when the volume occupied by saturated vapor increases from the value $V$ to the value $V_1$ everything will happen in the reverse order: the number of “condensing” molecules becomes greater than the number of evaporating ones, as a result of this the number of molecules in the vapor will decrease to such a value $N_1$ at which

$$\frac{N_1}{V_1} = \frac{N}{V},$$

and the steam pressure remains the same.)

So, we have found out that the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature has a fully defined value characteristic of a given liquid. We kept “at a given temperature” because experience shows that the pressure of saturated vapor above a liquid depends on temperature.

It is not difficult to obtain this dependence from experience. To do this, you first need to place a closed vessel with liquid into a heater using a pressure gauge and change the vapor pressure at different temperatures. figure 2 shows a typical graph of saturated vapor pressure versus temperature for water. This dependence is not at all similar to the familiar linear dependence of gas or unsaturated vapor pressure on temperature, which is shown as a dotted line on the same graph. From the graph, for example, it is clear that when the temperature increases from 373 °C (100 °C) to 473 °C (200 °C), the saturated vapor pressure of water increases 16 times, from one atmosphere to 16 atmospheres. The pressure of the gas or unsaturated vapor returns by only 27%, as follows from the equation of state of the gas.

What explains such a strong difference?

Let us turn once again to the equation of state of the gas

$$p = \frac{N}{V} kT.$$

From this equation it is clear that when the density of molecules $N/V$ does not change with temperature, then the vapor pressure is proportional to the temperature $T$. The “steeper” dependence of saturated vapor pressure $p$ on temperature observed in the experiment can only be explained by the fact that with increasing temperature the calculation and ratio $\frac{N}{V}$, that is, the calculation of vapor density.

Why does an increase in temperature cause an increase in saturated vapor density? Because with increasing temperature of the liquid, the proportion of its molecules whose kinetic energy is greater than the work of evaporation sharply returns. This means that with increasing temperature the number of $z_0$ molecules evaporated from the liquid increases sharply. And this, in turn, leads to an increase in the density of vapor molecules $\frac{N}{V}$ and its pressure $p$.

True, as the temperature increases, the speed of the vapor molecules also increases, that is, the value $|\bar{v}_x|$ increases, but this increase is small compared to the increase in density $\frac{N}{V}$.

Saturated vapor pressure increases with increasing temperature mainly due to an increase in the amount of liquid turning into steam.

It is clear that if the liquid in a closed vessel evaporates entirely, the vapor becomes unsaturated and with further heating its pressure $p$ increases in proportion to the temperature $T$. Saturated steam can be converted to saturated steam. To do this, it must be cooled. As the temperature rises, the vapor pressure will drop in proportion to the temperature until condensation begins - small droplets of liquid in the form of dew will appear on the walls of the vessel. This means the steam becomes saturated. The temperature at which it becomes saturated is called the dew point.

Example 4. Solve a system of equations
$$\begin{cases} ax^2 + 2by = a, \\ by^2 + 2ax = b. \end{cases}$$

It is clear that if $a = b = 0$, then any pair of numbers $(x, y)$ satisfies the system. If $b = 0$ but $a \neq 0$ or if $a = 0$ but $b \neq 0$, then the system has no solution. Let, finally, $a \neq 0$ and $b \neq 0$. Then, expressing $y$ from the first equation of the system and substituting it into the second, we obtain an equation of the fourth degree relative to $x$ with two parameters $a$ and $b$, which can be considered as a quadratic relative $b$:
$$4b^2 - 2b \cdot 4ax - a^2(1 - x^2)^2 = 0.$$
From here we find two equations:
$$b = \frac{a(1 + x)^2}{2}, \quad b = -\frac{a(1 - x)^2}{2}.$$

In conclusion, let us turn to one good well-known problem that appeared in the entrance exams at the university.

Example 11. Given a knot HAU, unfold the smaller one, and point O is inside it. A straight line l is drawn through this point, intersecting the sides of the angle at points B and C. Find the maximum area of ​​the straight line l for which triangle ABC has the smallest area.

The solution to this problem, based on calculating the area of ​​triangle ABC, is given, for example, in the book by V. B. Liidsky et al. 《Problems in elementary mathematics》（M., “Nauka”, 1967, p. 67, 293). In his letter to the editor, V.V. Vanin (Moscow) indicates two purely geometric solutions to the problem that do not use any calculations. Let us introduce the magazine reader to one of these solutions.

Let l be some position of the secant. Reflect the cut triangle ABC relative to point O, and obtain triangle A'B'C'. If the secant l₀ is such that its segment B₀C₀ is bisected by the point O (Fig. 4), then the union of triangles AB₀C₀ and A'B'₀C'₀ forms a parallelogram AB₀A'C₀, the area of which is twice the area of the triangle AB₁C₁ and and A'B'₁C'₁ forms a polygon, the area of which is also twice the area of the triangle AB₁C₁, but the mentioned parallelogram is contained in this polygon (proof!), and therefore its area is less. Therefore, the area of ​​triangle ABC is smallest when |A'B| || [AY], [A'C] || [AX].
