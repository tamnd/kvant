Law of Gravity

In modern physics there are constants that are called fundamental. Their average speed of light is $c$, electron charge $e$, electron mass $m$, gravitational constant $\gamma$, Planck constant $h$. These constants are included in formulas that describe physical processes in the microcosm and in outer space. The fundamental thing about these constants is that their values ​​do not change with time or location in space. Wherever we change these constants, we will always get the same values. Physicists know that these constants have not changed appreciably over the billions of years of evolution of the Universe.

The first fundamental statement - the gravitational statement $\gamma$ - was introduced into physics by Isaac Newton. It appeared with the discovery of the law of universal gravitation.

There are many surprising and interesting things in the history of this discovery. A funny legend is about how Newton discovered this law while sitting under an apple tree when a thickening cloud fell on his head. It is interesting how Newton was able to prove the validity of this law by studying the movement of the Moon. It's amazing how accurate this law turned out to be.

But the most amazing thing was the courage with which Newton announced that the law of gravity is a universal law that determines the interaction of any bodies in the Universe.

From the equality of the acceleration of all falling bodies, he will conclude that the force acting on a falling body is proportional to the mass of this body; The force with which the Moon is attached to the Earth must be proportional to both the mass of the Moon and the mass of the Earth, since one can just as well assume that the Earth is attached to the Moon.

This was the main thing in Newton's discovery. But Newton also understood that, although the acceleration should be the same for different bodies, it should decrease with increasing distance between the adjacent bodies.

In our essay “Mathematical Principles of Natural Physiology”, we went to England in 1686,

Newton summed up his many years of research in mechanics *); he sets out how he found the dependence of the forces of attraction between bodies on the distance between them.

The easiest way would be to derive this law for motion in a circle (we will do this below); but planets move in ellipses, and it was necessary to prove that an elliptical trajectory can be obtained from the same law.

In his book, Newton formulates four rules that should guide the natural master. He calls these rules “rules of philosophizing.” We will present them in full:

“Rule I. One must not accept in natural principles beyond those that are true and available to explain phenomena.

Rule III. Such properties of the body, which can be neither successful nor weakened and which turn out to be inherent in all bodies on which experimentation is possible, should be considered as the properties of all bodies in general.

Rule IV. In experimental physics, propositions derived from ongoing phenomena assisted by guidance, despite the possibility of assumptions contrary to them, should be considered true either exactly or approximately, until such phenomena are discovered that they are further refined or are already subject to exception.”

Rule III allows Newton to draw the following conclusion: “...It is established by everyday life and astronomical observations that all bodies in the vicinity of the Earth gravitate towards the Earth.”

Newton's book was the first book where mechanics was presented as an exact science; this book should be considered the first book on theoretical physics and the year the book was published - 1686 - should be considered the year of the birth of theoretical physics. The influence of this book on science was enormous. After it, it was impossible to limit ourselves only to reasoning and verbal descriptions of natural phenomena - it was necessary to create their theory. And the correctness of a theory can only be established by experimental testing: if the predictions of the theory coincide with experimental and observational data, the theory is correct. The creation of mathematical analysis by Newton and Leibniz provided the means to carry out this program.

The ancient Greeks created the first theoretical movement of the planets. This theory was expounded by Ptolemy in his treatise “Almagest”. But it did not touch at all on the question of why planets move. Such a question never arose. Following the teachings of Aristotle, the Greeks

They believed that planets tend to move in a circle, just as heavy bodies tend to fall, and light ones, like smoke, rise up. Even in the Middle Ages, discussing the principles of planetary motion meant mixing astronomy with physics, which was not permissible in the opinion of that time; the laws of heaven and earth belonged to different areas and one should not look for anything in common in them.

Nevertheless, Copernicus in 1543 said that gravity is a common property of bodies, “...the tendency, thanks to which they, closing in the shape of a ball, form a single whole. And it should be assumed that this desire is also inherent in the Sun, Lunts and other planets...”

Kepler (and before Kepler also Hilbert) compared gravity with magnetic action. At the same time, Kepler was the first who declared the problem of the movement of planets a physical problem, and the first who clearly posed the question of why the planets move, while all other astronomers only came up with geometric models that describe how the planets move. Physicists of the times of Galileo and Keplar solved physical problems by drawing up proportions. Thus, it was said about the fall of bodies on Earth that if time intervals are arranged in the form of an arithmetic pro-
regression, then the segments of the path traversed by the body over successive periods of time will be related as the squares of successive numbers. Galileo knew that all bodies on Earth fall with the same acceleration; he could well explain why bodies abandoned on Earth fly in a parabola. Describing the flight of a projectile, he said that this movement consists of a uniformly accelerated fall and uniform rectilinear motion by inertia, and did not ask the question of why the projectile falls uniformly accelerated. It was very difficult to come to the conclusion that acceleration depends on altitude. Galileo believed that even the Moon (if its forward motion was stopped) would fall to the Earth with the same acceleration with which a stone falls on Earth.

True, Galileo believed that the Moon should also fulfill the “earthly” law of the independence of the acceleration of its fall from mass. So he got “half” of the problem right! But Galileo could not accept the idea that the Earth was supporting a stone. The fall of a stone (the Moon) to the Earth is simply a manifestation of the desire of all bodies to converge close to one center, to form a non-spherical “heap”. At that time it was considered obvious that one body can act on another only by contacting it. Action at a distance seemed simply unthinkable. Therefore, Galileo answered Keplar that the ebb and flow of the tides on Earth are associated with the attraction of the ocean waters by the Moon. By Newton's time the situation had begun to change.

Not everyone blindly followed Galileo. Thus, Robert Hooke, based on the analogy of gravity with magnetic attraction, tried in 1666 to experimentally determine how the weight of a body changes with height. He conducted experiments with magnets of different lengths in Westminster Abbey in London. It is clear that he was unable to discover anything significant. Further expressing the assumption that the force of attraction varies inversely with the square of the distance, he was unable to put his thoughts into the strict form of an equation.

Hooke did not yet know how to write equations or solve them, and therefore could not verify the validity of this proposal. Also, another famous mechanic at that time, the Englishman Wren, could not do anything. Ren also spoke about the inverse square law, but with him everything was limited to words.

Only in Newton's hands did everything fall into place. The first, and main, idea came to his mind in 1665, when he was fleeing the plague in his family home. As he said, the whole thing started with an apple that fell from a tree. The 23-year-old scientist was struck by the idea that the reason that makes an apple fall to the Earth must be related to the reason that makes the Moon deviate from its straight path. Now this seems obvious, but in Newton’s time the very idea of ​​​​subordinating cosmic bodies by a simple earthly law was unusually bold. But it’s one thing to believe in a bold hypothesis yourself, and another thing to convince others of its validity. To do this, we must at least find a way to test it, find a way, as we say now, to compare theory with experience.

It was best to start by studying the movement of the Moon, using the existing results of observations. This required effort and time. Newton, in addition, was still busy with optical research; and in general, scientists of that time were usually in no hurry to publish. This is probably why only 20 years later, in April 1686, Newton presented the first volume of “Principles” to the Royal Society (English Academy of Sciences), and the entire work was published by mid-1687. Only then was the theory presented to other physicists.

The acceleration imparted by this force to the planet is

In the Principia, Newton sets out the solution to the inverse problem: he finds what the law of interaction must be for the trajectory to be an ellipse, and shows that this must be the law of “inverse squares”. Moreover, Newton proves that if the law of forces acting between the planet and the Sun were even slightly deviated from the inverse square law, then the points of greatest and least distance of the planet from the Sun would not be motionless in space. And from what has been observed, these points (they are called apses) are almost motionless. This Newtonian proof is one of the most brilliant in astronomy.

For many years, Newton's theory was the basis of celestial mechanics, and although many are trying to discover any amendments to the law of universal gravitation, only Einstein was able to find them. Now we know when the law of universal gravitation becomes inaccurate, and we can calculate corrections to it. The law of gravity is true as long as the distance between the bodies is much greater than the so-called gravitational radius of the tugging body. The gravitational radius of a body of mass $M$ is the quantity

$$R_{\text{rp}} = \frac{2\gamma M}{c^2},$$

where $\gamma M = C$ is the “Kepler constant” already known to us, and $c$ is the speed of light. For the Sun, the gravitational radius is 3 km, and for the Earth - 7 mm. From this we can conclude that the law of universal gravitation on the surface of the Earth may have a relative error equal to approximately $\frac{7 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ м}}{6,4 \cdot 10^6 \text{ м}} \approx 10^{-9}$ (radius of the Earth $\approx 6400 \text{ км}$), and on the surface of the Sun $-\frac{3 \cdot 10^3 \text{ м}}{7 \cdot 10^8 \text{ м}} \approx 0,4 \cdot 10^{-5}$ (radius of the Sun $\approx 700 \text{ 000 \text{ км}}$).

At a distance equal to the distance from the Sun to Mercury (about 50 million km), we can expect an error in the law of universal gravitation of the order of $\frac{3 \cdot 10^3 \text{ м}}{5 \cdot 10^{10} \text{ м}} = 0,6 \cdot 10^{-7}$. Although the error is very small, it leads to the observed effect.

This is due to the property of apses indicated by Newton to “respond” with their movement to the violation of the inverse square law. Due to the small correction, Mercury's elliptical orbit rotates slowly in space. This motion (built-in with the rotation of Mercury in orbit) is superimposed on the motion associated with the possibilities of action of Jupiter, which is explained by Newtonian theory. Over 100 years, an additional mixture $43^{\prime\prime}$ heats up, the explanation of which was given only by the general theory of relativity.

The corrections of the general theory of relativity are not always small. In the depths of space there are areas in which the effectiveness of the general theory of relativity turns out to be fundamental. These are the interiors of heavy stars, the cores of galaxies. When describing the Universe as a physical system, the main “tool” is the general theory of relativity. But Newton opened the way to such research. He was the first to create methods of theoretical physics, and the first to apply these methods to the study of the Universe.
