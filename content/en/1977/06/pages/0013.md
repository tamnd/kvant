Leibniz called this problem the most beautiful and expressed the opinion that only three mathematicians could solve it. And indeed, in addition to I. The third solution was published in the London journal Philosophical Transactions without the author's signature. However, I. Bernulli immediately recognized the unknown author as I. Newton; as he wrote: “ex ignore leopis” (I recognized the lion by his skin). Only after this did scientists draw attention to the fact that in “Mathematical Insolents” a problem of the same kind had already been solved, namely: to find a chalk that, when moving in a liquid along its axis, experiences the least resistance (it is assumed that the resistance of the fluid is proportional to the square of the speed).

This type of problem was used by Euler and Lagrange, who created a calculus for solving it, called vartz calculus.

There are many legends and anecdotes about Newton. He is readily presented as withdrawn, absent-minded, not noticing anything around him. Of course, Newton was really, for the most part, immersed in his thoughts. When asked how he made his great discoveries, he responded by “constantly thinking about them.” Yet he found time to attend to the needs of the university and the Royal Society. They like to say that, having been elected to Parliament, Newton did not say a word there, except for one occasion when he asked to close the window. It is much less known that, as a sharer of Parliament, he helped the University of Cambridge a lot, playing an intermediary between it and the government.

Newton's principle and creative character were evident when in 1687 King James invited the University of Cambridge to grant a master's degree to one Benedictine monk (Alban Francis). The university refused to carry out this test and sent a delegation to London, which included Newton. There was a moment when, under pressure from the king, all members of the delegation wanted to give consent on the condition that such incidents would not happen again. Only Newton's decisive position led to victory: the king was forced to abandon his offer.

Newton spent the last thirty years of his life in London. This was due to the fact that in 1696 he was appointed custodian and then director of the Mint. This position was not just an honorary title. Newton had to check the reminting of all the coins of England. He was also actively involved in the affairs of the Royal Society. His scientific interests shifted to the field of history: he compiled a chronological history of Egyptian, ancient Greek and biblical history. He also dealt with issues of theology.

Newton died at the age of 84, on the night of March 20-21, 1727.

This is what Newton himself said about his work: “I don’t know how the world will look at me, but to myself I seem to be a boy playing on the seashore and coming to the ascent when he sometimes manages to find more eyes than usually a pebble or a beautiful shell; meanwhile, there is a huge ocean of hidden truth in front of a lot of space.”
