The gramophone record and the diffraction of light

A. Bondar

The phenomenon of light diffraction is studied in the 10th grade, so only 10th graders will probably be able to fully understand all the theoretical calculations carried out in this article. And we advise everyone to continue the experience, which is very simple and beautiful.

As is known, one of the most accurate methods for determining the spectral composition of the radiation under study is based on the phenomenon of diffraction. A good spectral device is a diffraction grating. It turns out that usually a gramophone record can also be used to observe diffraction and, in particular, to measure the wavelength of visible light.

During sound recording, grooves are applied to the surface of the record at equal distances from each other. These grooves scatter light, and the spaces between them reflect it. Thus, a gramophone record is similar to a reflective diffraction grating. If the width of the reflecting stripes is $a$, and the width of the scattering grooves is $b$, then the value $d = a + b$ is the grating period.

Let a plane monochromatic wave of length $\lambda$ fall on a reflective grating with a period $d$ at an angle $\theta$ to the grating (Fig. 1). (The case of normal incidence of rays is similarly discussed in a textbook for grade 10 (see § 110).) According to the Huygen-Fresnel principle, each point of the reflective surface of the grating becomes an independent precise source, sending light in all possible directions. Let us consider a wave propagating at an angle $\varphi$ to the grating (see Fig. 1). With the help of a collecting lens (for example, the lens of an eye), these waves can be concentrated into one point. Let's find a condition when, when added, the waves will reinforce each other.

$$|AK| - |NB| = d \sin \varphi - d \sin \theta = d (\sin \varphi - \sin \theta)$$
