⟦folio 70⟧

It is not difficult to obtain this dependence from experience. To do this, you first need to place a closed vessel with liquid into a heater using a pressure gauge and change the vapor pressure at different temperatures. figure 2 shows a typical graph of saturated vapor pressure versus temperature for water. This dependence is not at all similar to the familiar linear dependence of gas or unsaturated vapor pressure on temperature, which is shown as a dotted line on the same graph. From the graph, for example, it is clear that when the temperature increases from 373 °C (100 °C) to 473 °C (200 °C), the saturated vapor pressure of water increases 16 times, from one atmosphere to 16 atmospheres. The pressure of the gas or unsaturated vapor returns by only 27%, as follows from the equation of state of the gas.

What explains such a strong difference?

Let us turn once again to the equation of state of the gas

$$p = \frac{N}{V} kT.$$

From this equation it is clear that when the density of molecules $N/V$ does not change with temperature, then the vapor pressure is proportional to the temperature $T$. The “steeper” dependence of saturated vapor pressure $p$ on temperature observed in the experiment can only be explained by the fact that with increasing temperature the calculation and ratio $\frac{N}{V}$, that is, the calculation of vapor density.

Why does an increase in temperature cause an increase in saturated vapor density? Because with increasing temperature of the liquid, the proportion of its molecules whose kinetic energy is greater than the work of evaporation sharply returns. This means that with increasing temperature the number of $z_0$ molecules evaporated from the liquid increases sharply. And this, in turn, leads to an increase in the density of vapor molecules $\frac{N}{V}$ and its pressure $p$.

True, as the temperature increases, the speed of the vapor molecules also increases, that is, the value $|\bar{v}_x|$ increases, but this increase is small compared to the increase in density $\frac{N}{V}$.

Saturated vapor pressure increases with increasing temperature mainly due to an increase in the amount of liquid turning into steam.

It is clear that if the liquid in a closed vessel evaporates entirely, the vapor becomes unsaturated and with further heating its pressure $p$ increases in proportion to the temperature $T$. Saturated steam can be converted to saturated steam. To do this, it must be cooled. As the temperature rises, the vapor pressure will drop in proportion to the temperature until condensation begins - small droplets of liquid in the form of dew will appear on the walls of the vessel. This means the steam becomes saturated. The temperature at which it becomes saturated is called the dew point.

Exercises

1. Calculate the pressure of saturated water vapor in a vessel at a temperature of 17 °C if the dew point is equal to 5 °C.
2. How long will it take 1 kcal of water in a vessel to evaporate if the space above its surface is evacuated by a pump and the water temperature is maintained constant at 100 °C?

The surface area of ​​the water is 0.01 $m^2$. The saturated vapor pressure of water at 100 °C is 1 bar. Assume that $\bar{v}_x = \sqrt{\frac{v^2}{3}}$.
