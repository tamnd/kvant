$$\frac{1}{2} \frac{N}{V} |\bar{v}_x| = z_0. \tag{1}$$

Recall that the ratio $\frac{N}{V}$ determines the pressure $p$ of steam at a given temperature:

$$p = \frac{N}{V} kT. \tag{2}$$

If the steam is saturated, then, according to (1), $\frac{N}{V} = \frac{2z_0}{|\bar{v}_x|}$, and the steam pressure is equal to

$$p_H = \frac{2z_0 k T}{|\bar{v}_x|}. \tag{3}$$

Thus, we come to the conclusion that the actual pressure of saturated vapor does not depend on the volume it occupies and at a given temperature is fully defined as a value characteristic of a given liquid.

It may seem that the independence of saturated vapor pressure from volume contradicts the gas state equation (2), from which it follows that at a constant temperature $T$ the gas pressure is inversely proportional to the volume $V$ it occupies. Maybe the gas equation of state is not applicable for saturated steam?

In reality, there is no contradiction, and equation (2) does not apply.

From equation (2) it follows that the pressure $p$ of a gas is inversely proportional to its volume, provided that $N$ is constant, that is, when the mass of the gas does not change. But for saturated steam this condition is not met. When the volume of saturated steam changes, its mass also changes. Indeed, if you change saturated steam in such a way that its volume decreases from a value $V$ to some value $V_1$, the ratio $N/V_1$ will immediately decrease. Then, as can be seen from formula (1), the number $z$ of molecules returning from vapor to liquid will decrease, and it will exceed the number $z_0$

"evaporating" molecules. As a result, the number $N$ of vapor molecules begins to decrease. When it decreases to such a value $N_1$ at which

$$\frac{N_1}{V_1} = \frac{N}{V},$$

balance will be restored. But then, in accordance with equation (2), the pressure $p$ of the steam will also be restored. This is how it happens experimentally.

When the volume of saturated steam decreases, part of it condenses and the mass of the steam decreases as many times as its volume decreases. Therefore, saturated vapor pressure does not depend on volume. (It is not difficult to prove that when the volume occupied by saturated vapor increases from the value $V$ to the value $V_1$ everything will happen in the reverse order: the number of “condensing” molecules becomes greater than the number of evaporating ones, as a result of this the number of molecules in the vapor will decrease to such a value $N_1$ at which

$$\frac{N_1}{V_1} = \frac{N}{V},$$

and the steam pressure remains the same.)

So, we have found out that the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature has a fully defined value characteristic of a given liquid. We kept “at a given temperature” because experience shows that the pressure of saturated vapor above a liquid depends on temperature.
