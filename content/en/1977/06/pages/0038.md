The length of the stroke is a multiple of the wavelength, the phases of the oscillations coming from points A and B will be the same, and therefore the oscillations will reinforce each other. All other reflective sections of the grating behave similarly. Consequently, the conditions for the formation of main maxima can be written as follows:

$$d | \sin \varphi - \sin \theta | = k \lambda,$$

where $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$. From this we can determine the wavelength $\lambda$. To do this, you need to know the grating period $d$, the angle $\theta$ of wave incidence on the grating and the direction to the corresponding maximum - angle $\varphi$. Typically, the grating period is much longer than the wavelength ($d \gg \lambda$), so the angles $\varphi$ are small. This means that the main maxima are located very close to each other, and the diffraction pattern is very unclear. However, the greater the angle of incidence of the rays on the grating (angle $\theta$), the larger the angles $\varphi$ and, therefore, the more convenient it is to make the necessary changes. That is why it is best to use not normal, but oblique incidence of the rays on the grating.

Until now we have been talking about multichromatic light. What if white light, complex in its spectral composition, falls on the grating? From equation (*) it unexpectedly follows that the position of each main maximum depends on the wavelength. The shorter the wavelength, the smaller the value of the angle $\varphi$ corresponds to the maximum. Thus, all maxima (except for zero) grow into a spectrum, the purple end of which faces the center of the diffraction pattern, and the red end faces the outside.
