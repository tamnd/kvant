Newton also came up with a way to expand into a series the function $y$, given implicitly using the equation $F(x, y) = 0$, where $F(x, y)$ is a polynomial. This method is called *Newton's parallelogram*). It was the basis for the work of Pünze and other mathematicians over the centuries who studied algebraic functions.

Newton perfectly understood the role of series in the calculus he created. He emphasized this in the very title of his works: “The method of fluctuation and infinite series” and “Analysis using an equation with an infinite number of terms.” Both of these works were widely known to English and continental mathematicians from the very beginning of the 70s of the 17th century, but their publication was constantly postponed according to various principles. The second of them was published only in 1711, and the first in 1736, 9 years after the death of the author and 60 years after it was written.

**Algebra**

Newton's interest in algebra awoke very early. His first independent result was recorded by him back in 1664-1666. It related to the expression of the sums of powers of the roots of an algebraic equation through its coefficients. Even François Viète (1540-1603) established a connection between the coefficients and the roots of an algebraic equation: if $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ are the roots of the equation $x^n + a_1x^{n-1} + \dots + a_{n-1}x + a_n = 0$, then

$$\begin{cases} x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n = -a_1, \\ x_1x_2 + x_1x_3 + \dots + x_{n-1}x_n = a_2, \\ \dots \\ x_1x_2 \cdot \dots \cdot x_n = (-1)^n a_n. \end{cases}$$

(*) For more information about Newton’s parallelogram, see p. 19.

The functions specified by the left sides of the equalities (*) do not change if the arguments are rearranged. They are called *elementary symmetric functions*.

Albert Girard (1595-1632) was able to express sums of powers of roots

$$S_m = x_1^m + x_2^m + \dots + x_n^m$$

for $m=2, 3, 4$ (by Vieta’s theorem $S_1 = -a_1$) through the coefficients of the equation. Newton found a general recurrent formula, which in modern notation (by definition of $a_i=0$ for $i>n$) has the form

$$S_m + a_1S_{m-1} + a_2S_{m-2} + \dots + a_{m-1}S_1 + a_m \cdot m = 0.$$

It allows you to find $S_m$ for any $m$.

From 1673 to 1683, Newton lectured at Cambridge on algebra, which formed the basis of his book General Argumentation, published in 1707. In it, Newton focuses on the question of finding the roots of an equation, as well as how, without solving the equation, one can find out within what limits all its roots lie, how many of them are positive, negative or minimal.

We do not mention here many other mathematical results of Newton, the understanding of which would require the reader to specialize.

We must remember, however, that Newton was a physicist no less than a mathematician. Already in 1665-1667, he discovered the law of universal gravitation and began to deduce with its help the laws of planetary motion. However, he gradually investigated the problems of optics. Newton discovered the dispersion of light and designed a reflecting telexcope. He presented this telexcope to the Royal Society (as the Academy of Sciences is called in England), which appreciated Newton’s invention so highly that it elected him as a member in 1672. In 1703, Newton became president of the Royal Society and retained this post until the end of his life.
