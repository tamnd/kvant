II. The correspondence between fluxes is given: $f(x, y, z, x, y, z) = 0$.
Find correspondence between fluxes: $\Psi(x, y, z) = 0$.

Special cases of the first problem are finding the speed of movement along a path specified as a function of time, finding extrema and tangents. Newton gives an algorithm for solving this problem for the case when the correspondence between fluxes is specified using the polynomial $F(x, y)$ or $F(x, y, z)$.

Newton's rule is as follows: arrange the polynomial in powers of each ubud of fluents, for example $x$; multiply each term by $x/x$ and by the corresponding power $x$; do the same in relation to the other fluente; take the sum of the received terms and equate it to zero.

Let us apply, for example, this rule to the polynomial $y - x^n$. We get $y \cdot \frac{y}{y} - x^n \cdot n \cdot \frac{x}{x} = 0$ or $y = nx^{n-1}x$.

Newton justified his rule as follows: we increase time by an “indefinitely small part” - the moment $o$, then the fluents will increase by $ox$ and $oy$; Let's substitute the new fluent values ​​into the original expression and reduce all terms by $o$. He sets out the central point of his justification as follows: “Not since we assumed $o$ to be an infinitesimal value..., then those terms that are multiplied by it can be considered nothing compared to others. That’s why I neglect them.”

To consider an example we have

$$y + oy = (x + ox)^n,$$

$$y + oy = x^n + nx^{n-1}ox +$$

$$+ \frac{n(n-1)}{1 \cdot 2} x^{n-2}o^2 \dot{x}^2 + \dots,$$

$$\dot{y} = nx^{n-1}ox +$$

$$+ \frac{n(n-1)}{1 \cdot 2} x^{n-2}o^2 \dot{x}^2 + \dots,$$

$$\dot{y} = nx^{n-1}x +$$

$$+ \frac{n(n-1)}{1 \cdot 2} x^{n-2}o^2 \dot{x}^2 + \dots,$$

$$\dot{y} = nx^{n-1}x.$$

The answer is correct! However, its justification is very unconvincing.

Newton himself was not satisfied with the justification presented and is trying to provide a more solid basis for his calculation. In the introduction to “The Mathematical Principle of Natural Fluosophy” (1687), Newton outlined the theory of limits (he called it “the theory of first and last relations”), but he did not apply it to justify and systematically construct differential calculus. This was done about 150 years later in the works of O. Cauchy (1789-1857) and some other mathematicians of the last century.

The second problem in general is the problem of integrating differential equations, that is, equations connecting certain functions and their arbitrary ones. In a particular case - for the equation $y = f(x)$ - this problem comes down to finding the first figurative.

Newton shows that the problema “Determine the area bounded by any given curve” has properties to the definition of fluxion, since the fluxion of the (arbitrary) area $z = ADB$ is equal to ordinate $y = BD$ (see Fig. 1; see also equality (2) in paragraph 100 of the textbook “Algebra and the beginnings of analysis 10”).

In the general case, to solve the second problem, Newton used a new analytical apparatus, which allows him to extend his algorithm to a very wide class of functions. This was a fundamental step. Let's look at it in more detail.
