Points in these zones, spaced from each other at a distance of $3/2 d$, are directed; points $A_1$ and $B$ will be called respectively. All waves emanating from the corresponding points will have the same location. From triangle $A_1BD$ this path difference is equal to

$$|A_1D| = 3/2 d \sin \varphi.$$ (3)

From correspondence (2) and (3) it follows that $|A_1D| = 3/2 \lambda$, that is, the path difference is equal to an odd number of half-waves. Such waves cancel each other out. Consequently, all waves from zones $AA_1$ and $BB_1$, if condition (2) is satisfied, are damped at point $K$.

Similar calculations can be carried out for any pair of subsequent symmetrically located zones and show that the waves from all zones cancel each other out and a minimum is actually observed at point $K$.

In the same way, it can be shown that the second minimum is observed when the condition $d \sin \varphi = 2\lambda$ is satisfied. In this case, the wave front must be divided into zones of width $d/4$ and the corresponding calculations must be carried out.

Formula (1) allows us to determine the light wavelength:

$$\lambda = \frac{d \sin \varphi}{k}.$$

However, changes are greatly simplified if a simple changing device called an eriometer is used. To make an eriometer, take a square piece of cardboard with sides 10-15 cm and draw a circle in its middle with a radius of $r = 20—30$ mm. A hole with a diameter of 2-3 mm is pierced in the center of the circle, and 6-8 holes of varying diameter are pierced along the circle.

When changing the eriometer $A$ (see Fig. 1, a) is installed directly in front of the incandescent light bulb. Moving away from the eriometer at a distance of 1-2 m, find a position in which rays enter the eye through hole $O$, induce directly from one of the sections of the hot thread. Then a thread of wiring is placed in front of the eye and a good vision of the external eyes is added by moving the stream in the direction perpedicular to the rays. By changing the distance between the eriometer and the eye, a position is found in which the circumference of the eriometer with holes coincides with the middle of the dark ring with the number $k$ (at figure 1 the case is shown when $k=2$).

As can be seen from figure 1, the tangent of the diffraction angle $\varphi$ for a dark ring is calculated by the formula $tg \varphi = r/l$, where $r$ is the radius of the eriometer circle, $l$ is the distance from the eriometer to the wiring flow. At small diffraction angles, from which it comes precisely to such changes, the following relation is valid:

$$\sin \varphi = tg \varphi = \frac{r}{l}.$$

Substituting the value of sin $\varphi$ into expression (1), we obtain a formula for determining the wavelength:

$$\lambda = \frac{dr}{kl}.$$ (4)

The radius of the eriometer circle $r$ is known to us in advance. The distance $l$ is easy to change. The number of the dark ring $k$ is determined by observing the outside. The diameter of the wiring $d$, if it is not known, is changed with a micrometer.

If changes are made in white light, then using formula (4) we find the effective wavelength of light to which our eye is most sensitive. It is approximately equal to 0.56 microns. Light waves also correspond to the green part of the spectrum.
