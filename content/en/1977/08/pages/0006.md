⟦folio 4⟧

Favorite science
greatness of mathematicians

This is one of the many epithets that Gauss gave to argumentation (number theory). By that time, arguments from a set of isolated observations and statements had already turned into science.

Gauss would later write: “It is mainly to the later researchers, admittedly few in number, but who have won lasting fame, such as Fermat, Euler, Laherange, Legendre, that we owe the fact that they found access to the treasure trove of this boxed science and showed what riches it is filled with.” But all this is not yet known to the boy from Brunswick, who with amazing speed rediscovered what had taken his great predecessors many years to achieve. Here are a few topics that occupied him.

Gauss notices early on that the remainders when dividing the squares of integers by the primes $p$ cannot be arbitrary. For example, when divided by 3, the remainder can be either 0 or 1; when divided by 5 - 0.1 or 4. But you can look at the situation from the other side: for which $p$ are there numbers $n^2$ that, when divided by $p$, give the given remainder $q$? With $q=1$ any $p(n=p+1)$ are suitable, but the remainder $p-1$ is $p$ for some, but not for some: for $p=3$ there is no remainder 2 when dividing the square, but for $p=5$ the remainder 4 is $(n=3)$, for $p=7, 11, 19$ there is no remainder $p-1$ does not exist, but with $p=13, 17$ it does. A hypothesis arises: if $p=4k-1$, then no $n^2$ when divided by $p$ gives a remainder $p-1$, but for $p=4k+1$ such a $n^2$ can be indicated. Gauss did not know that Fermat had formulated this hypothesis before him and that it was rightly established by Euler.

“I accidentally came across one amazing argumentative truth, and as it not only seemed to me perfectly in itself, but also gave me the idea that it was connected with other types of truth-telling by facts, I looked at it with all my energy to find out the reasons on which it is based and to build a proof of it. After this desire was finally realized, the pursuit of these studies made me smile so much that I no longer wanted to leave them.”

Gauss is interested in what $p$ numbers $n^2$ can give remainders $q=2$, 3, etc. For $q=2$ he agrees that the whole point is in the remainder of the division of $p$ by 8 (Euler could not prove this, but Lagrange proved it), for $q=3$ everything is determined by the remainder of the division $p$ by 12. The general statement is that all prime $p$ that have the same remainder when divided by $4q$ can either simultaneously give a remainder $q$ when dividing some $n^2$ by $p$, or cannot. Gauss calls this statement the “golden theorem”; Nowadays it is usually called the square law of reciprocity *). The Golden Theorem does not support young Gauss. “This theorem tormented me for a whole year and did not yield to his efforts.” But this was the point at which he “caught up” with the mathematics he had perfected: the numbers of major mathematicians trying to prove the quadratic reciprocity law were inconclusive.

Here is another topic of Gauss's thoughts. Little Gauss is pleased that if you divide 1 by $p$, then after a while the decimal places begin to repeat - you get an infinite periodic decimal fraction. Proving periodicity is not difficult, but how to calculate the length of the period? Gauss, without rushing to conclusions, goes through prime numbers in a row, not being lazy to write down the periods accurately. This may seem like a boring task (for example, for 97 the period contains 96 signs), but Gauss went through all the $p<1000$. He established that the length of a period is always a divisor of the number $p-1$. (This easily follows from Fermat's Little Theorem**, which Gauss reproved.) Gauss is interested in those $p$ for which the period is exactly equal to $p-1$. To do this, it is enough that among the remainders from division by $p$ of the numbers $10, 10^2, \dots, 10^{p-1}$ there are all non-zero remainders. Whether the number of such $p$ is infinite or finite is not known to this day.

*) See the article “The Golden Theorem” («Kvant», 1973, No. 1).
**) See the article “Fermat’s Little Theorem” («Kvant», 1972, No. 10).
