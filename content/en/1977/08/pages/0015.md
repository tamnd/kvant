He warns Hermin, who was only going to mention that what he did about parallels may turn out to be wrong: “It is not the wasps, from which you destroy, that will rise above your ox.” The decision to record the results, but not publish them, gradually matures: “It will probably not be long before I can process my extensive research on this issue so that they can be published. In May 1831, Gauss began to systematically write: “For several weeks now, I have begun to sort out in writing some of the results of my own reflections on this subject, some of which are already 40 years old, but have never been written down much, as a result of which I had to renew all the work in my field 3 or 4 times. I would not like, oddako, for this to happen with me” (letter to Schumacher).

However, in 1832 he received from Farkas Boyan a small work by his son Janos, “The Appendix” (the name is due to the fact that it was published as an appendix to his father’s larger book). “My son bets more on that narrowing than on the narrowing of all Europe.” The content of the book amazed Gauss: it completely and systematically constructed non-Euclidean geometry. These were not fragmentary agreements and agreements between Schweikart and Taurinus. Gauss himself was going to receive such a presentation in the near future. He writes to Hermin: “... I found all my own ideas and results, developed with great grace, although a consequence of the conciseness of the presentation, in a form difficult to understand for someone to whom this area is alien... I believe that this young geometer Boya is a genius of the first magnitude.” And here is what was written to the father: “... the entire content of this work, the path that your son took, and the results that he received, almost completely coincide with mine, which I partially received 30-35 years ago. I am truly extremely amazed by this. I had the intention about my own work, some of which I have now put on paper, not to publish anything during my lifetime... I had the intention... so that these thoughts, at least, would not come close to me. Therefore, I am extremely amazed by what exists - it frees me from this necessity; and I am very glad that it was the son of my old friend who anticipated me in such a wonderful way.” Janos did not receive any public assessment or support from Gauss. Apparently, at the same time, Gauss was making systematic notes on non-Euclidean geometry, although occasional notes were preserved, dating back to the 40s.

In 1841, Gauss became acquainted with the German edition of Lobachevsky's work (Lobachevsky's first publications date back to 1829). True to himself, Gauss is interested in other publications of the author, limiting himself to speaking about him in correspondence with close correspondents. However, at the suggestion of Gauss, in 1842 Lobachevsky, “as one of the most excellent mathematicians of the Russian state,” was elected a corresponding member of the Goettigen Scientific Royal Society. Gauss personally extracted Lobachevsky's information. However, neither Gauss's presentation nor the diploma awarded to Lobachevsky mentioned non-Euclidean geometry.

Gauss's work on non-Euclidean geometry became known only with the publication of a posthumous archive. Thus, Gauss provided himself with the opportunity to work calmly by refusing to reveal his great discovery, causing ongoing debate to this day about the admissibility of the position he took.

It should be noted that Gauss is interested not only in the purely logical question of provability of the statements made about parallels. He is interested in geometry in the natural sciences, the question of the true geometry of our physical world (see above statement of 1817). He carries out the possibility of astronomical verification, responding with interest to Lobachevsky’s messages on this matter. While studying geodesy, Gauss could not resist changing the sum of the angles of a triangle with the vertices of High Hagen, Brocken, Inselberg. Deposition from 2l did not exceed 0.2′.

Electrodynamics and terrestrial magnetism

By the end of the 20s, Gauss, who had passed the 50-year mark, began to search for new areas of scientific activity. This is evidenced by two publications in 1829 and 1830. The first of them bears the stamp of reflections on the general principles of mechanics (here the “principal name of narrowing” of Gauss is built); the other is devoted to the study of capillary phenomena.
