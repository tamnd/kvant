On the authenticity of scientific jokes

Popular science literature often contains anecdotes about various scientists. Some of them are repeated repeatedly, although it is difficult to say whether they are true. In this regard, a small historical study carried out in 1946 by physicist Bernard Kozn regarding a letter from one of the readers of the English magazine Nature is of interest. Translation and preparation of the publication by V. Berezin.

Letter to the editor

“In the Memoirs of Baron de Grimm, in the place where the launch of the first hot air balloon is described, which takes place in an atmosphere of general enthusiasm in Paris on the Champ de Mars on June 27, 1783, we read: “Among the enthusiastic witnesses of the event, there were many people who were in a cold mood and did not miss the opportunity to repeat: “What is the point of all this? Does this invention deserve so much fuss about it? But the wise Franquin (who was in Paris at that time) answered these skeptics with his characteristic simplicity and expressiveness: “Why do you need a newborn?”

As far as I know, this saying is generally attributed to Faraday, not Franklin. So, in one popular science book it says (without any reference to the source) that after an experiment performed by Faraday during a lecture, a certain lady asked him a question: “Listen, professor, even if this phenomenon actually occurs, what toll will it do?” She received a worthy answer: “Madam, can you tell me what kind of crawling a newborn does?”

Klement Uzbeks

Editor's response

“There are two famous jokes about Faraday and the benefits of scientific discoveries. These anecdotes were found in one form or another by various authors of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries in works on the history of science. They are usually told in connection with Faraday's discoveries in the field of electromagnetic phenomena. Their essence is as follows. An important official (most often the Prime Minister himself) pays Faraday a visit and, finding him demonstrating the induction current, asks: “What benefit can be derived from this discovery?” According to one anecdote, Faraday replied: “What good is a newborn baby?” According to another: “The time is not far distant when you will be able to impose a new tax.”

The first of the two answers is also attributed to one of Faraday's predecessors in the study of the nature of electricity - Benjamin Franklin. This answer only expresses the feelings of people engaged in science, that it seems appropriate to shed light on the actual circumstances under which it was uttered by Faraday.
