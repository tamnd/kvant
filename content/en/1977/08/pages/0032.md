- Tell me, Rol, how will the enemy act in these conditions? Does he have an optimal strategy?

- Yes, I have. The enemy does not know our plans, but he knows the consequences of an attack on Verda and Kalimna - his intelligence usually does not fail; therefore, for his part, he must begin to inflict the greatest damage on us. If we denote our damage by $w$, and the probabilities of an enemy attack on Kalimna and Verda by $q_1$ and $q_2$, then $q_1 + q_2 = 1$, $6q_1 + 3q_2 \geq w$, $q_1 + 5q_2 \geq w$, $y_1 = q_1/w$, $y_2 = q_2/w$, $M = 1/w$, and the enemy will come to the next

$$\begin{cases} 6y_1 + 3y_2 \geq 1, \\ y_1 + 5y_2 \geq 1, \\ y_1 \geq 0, \\ y_2 \geq 0, \\ M(y_1, y_2) = y_1 + y_2, \end{cases}$$

and we need to look for the smallest value $M$. When the opponent succeeds in solving this linear opponents problem, he will receive: $y_1 = 2/27$, $y_2 = 5/27$, $M = 7/27$, from where $q_1 = 2/7$, $q_2 = 5/7$, $w = 3^6/7$ (Fig. 2).

That is, in order to achieve the highest level of naim in two cases out of seven, the enemy must set up on Verda, and in the heels - on Kalimna. And to achieve it will be equal to the same $3^6/7$ drocker. Of course, he must establish it in such a way that we do not know for sure what kind of adjustment lies ahead.

“Given the work of our intelligence,” Lud couldn’t resist, “it’s not difficult.”

“Tell me, Rol,” Dor asked, what will happen if the enemy does not follow his existing strategy?

“I would definitely agree with you, Rol, if we had to deal with the purple ones a few times.” But there will be only one battle. What decision should we make?

- Regardless of the number of battles, we must cast a kind of lot. If the optimal strategy had the form $p_1 = 1/2$, $p_2 = 1/2$, then it would be enough to throw a coin, wishing for kuhu and bark.
