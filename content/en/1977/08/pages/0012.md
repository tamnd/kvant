⟦folio 10⟧

During this time, Gauss got married (“life seems to me like spring with always new bright flowers”). In 1806, the Duke, to whom Gauss was apparently sincerely attached, dies of his wounds. Now nothing is holding him back in Brunswick.

Gauss's life in Göttigen was not sweet. In 1809, after the birth of his son, his wife died, and then the child himself. In addition, Napoleon imposed a heavy indemnity on Goettigen. Gauss himself had to pay an exorbitant tax of 2,000 francs. Olbers and, right in Paris, Laplace tried to introduce money for him. Both times Gauss proudly refused. However, another benefactor was found, this time anonymous, and there was no one to return the money (much later they learned that it was the Elector of Main, a friend of Goethe). “Death is less than my life,” writes Gauss between notes on the theory of elliptic functions. Those around him did not appreciate his work and considered him, to say the least, an eccentric. Olbers reassures Gauss, saying that one should not count on people’s understanding: “they must be desired and served.”

In 1809, the famous “Theory of the motion of celestial bodies revolving around the Sun, completed in 1807,” was published. The delay occurred due to the product's fears that the book in German would not find demand, and Gauss, from patronage reports, refused to print the book in French. The compromise was to publish the book in Latin. This is Gauss's only book on astronomy (in addition to this, he published several articles).

Gauss outlines his methods for calculating orbits. To be convinced of the power of his method, he repeats the calculation of the orbit of the comet in 1769, which Euler, who then lost his sight, calculated in three days of directed calculation. It took Gauss an hour to do this. The book describes the least squares method, which remains one of the most common methods for processing the results of overlays.

Gauss indicated that he had known this method since 1794, and since 1802 he had been using it systematically. (Two years before the publication of Gauss's Theory of Motion, the method of least squares was published by Legendre.)

In 1810, there were more honors: Gauss received the Prize of the Paris Academy of Sciences and the Gold Medal of the Royal Society of London, and was elected to several academies.

In 1804, the Paris Academy chose Pallas's perturbation theory as the topic for a major prize (a gold medal weighing 1 kg). The deadline was postponed twice (to 1816) in the hope that Gauss would submit the work. Gauss was helped in his calculations by his student Nikolan (“a young man tireless in calculations”), and yet the calculations were not completed. Gauss passed them while in severe depression.

Regular studies in astronomy continued almost until his death. The famous comet of 1812 (which “foreshadowed” the fire of Moscow!) was observed in court, using Gauss’s calculations. On August 28, 1851, Gauss observed a solar eclipse. Gauss had many astronomer students (Schumacher, Herming, Nikolan, Struve). The greatest German geometers Möbnitz and Staudt studied from him not geometry, but astronomy. He was in active correspondence with many astronomers, regularly read articles and books on astronomy, and published solutions. From letters to astronomers we learned a lot about mathematics. How does the appearance of Gauss the astronomer contrast with the idea of ​​an inaccessible hermit that existed among mathematicians!

Geodesy

At the beginning of the century, he tried to use the results of measurements of other meridians undertaken by French surveyors to establish a standard length (meter) in order to find the true roll of the Earth.
