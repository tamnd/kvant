This is one of the many epithets that Gauss gave to argumentation (number theory). By that time, arguments from a set of isolated observations and statements had already turned into science.

Gauss would later write: “It is mainly to the later researchers, admittedly few in number, but who have won lasting fame, such as Fermat, Euler, Laherange, Legendre, that we owe the fact that they found access to the treasure trove of this boxed science and showed what riches it is filled with.” But all this is not yet known to the boy from Brunswick, who with amazing speed rediscovered what had taken his great predecessors many years to achieve. Here are a few topics that occupied him.

Gauss notices early on that the remainders when dividing the squares of integers by the primes $p$ cannot be arbitrary. For example, when divided by 3, the remainder can be either 0 or 1; when divided by 5 - 0.1 or 4. But you can look at the situation from the other side: for which $p$ are there numbers $n^2$ that, when divided by $p$, give the given remainder $q$? With $q=1$ any $p(n=p+1)$ are suitable, but the remainder $p-1$ is $p$ for some, but not for some: for $p=3$ there is no remainder 2 when dividing the square, but for $p=5$ the remainder 4 is $(n=3)$, for $p=7, 11, 19$ there is no remainder $p-1$ does not exist, but with $p=13, 17$ it does. A hypothesis arises: if $p=4k-1$, then no $n^2$ when divided by $p$ gives a remainder $p-1$, but for $p=4k+1$ such a $n^2$ can be indicated. Gauss did not know that Fermat had formulated this hypothesis before him and that it was rightly established by Euler.

“I accidentally came across one amazing argumentative truth, and as it not only seemed to me perfectly in itself, but also gave me the idea that it was connected with other types of truth-telling by facts, I looked at it with all my energy to find out the reasons on which it is based and to build a proof of it. After this desire was finally realized, the pursuit of these studies made me smile so much that I no longer wanted to leave them.”

Gauss is interested in what $p$ numbers $n^2$ can give remainders $q=2$, 3, etc. For $q=2$ he agrees that the whole point is in the remainder of the division of $p$ by 8 (Euler could not prove this, but Lagrange proved it), for $q=3$ everything is determined by the remainder of the division $p$ by 12. The general statement is that all prime $p$ that have the same remainder when divided by $4q$ can either simultaneously give a remainder $q$ when dividing some $n^2$ by $p$, or cannot. Gauss calls this statement the “golden theorem”; Nowadays it is usually called the square law of reciprocity *). The Golden Theorem does not support young Gauss. “This theorem tormented me for a whole year and did not yield to his efforts.” But this was the point at which he “caught up” with the mathematics he had perfected: the numbers of major mathematicians trying to prove the quadratic reciprocity law were inconclusive.

Here is another topic of Gauss's thoughts. Little Gauss is pleased that if you divide 1 by $p$, then after a while the decimal places begin to repeat - you get an infinite periodic decimal fraction. Proving periodicity is not difficult, but how to calculate the length of the period? Gauss, without rushing to conclusions, goes through prime numbers in a row, not being lazy to write down the periods accurately. This may seem like a boring task (for example, for 97 the period contains 96 signs), but Gauss went through all the $p<1000$. He established that the length of a period is always a divisor of the number $p-1$. (This easily follows from Fermat's Little Theorem**, which Gauss reproved.) Gauss is interested in those $p$ for which the period is exactly equal to $p-1$. To do this, it is enough that among the remainders from division by $p$ of the numbers $10, 10^2, \dots, 10^{p-1}$ there are all non-zero remainders. Whether the number of such $p$ is infinite or finite is not known to this day.

Gauss noticed that when dividing the numbers 3, 9, 27, ..., 3$^{16}$ by 17, all possible non-polistic remainders are found: 1, 2, ..., 16. This observation only served to lead to Gauss’s first great discovery - the construction of a regular 17-gon *).

Gauss knew that with a compass and a ruler it was possible to construct regular $n$-gons (or, what is the same, to divide a circle into $n$ equal parts) for $n = 3, 4, 5$. He, of course, understood that such constructions are also possible for $n = 2^k, 2^k \cdot 3, 2^k \cdot 5, 15\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{2}{15}\right)$. Apparently, Gauss knew that the ancients could not complete the construction for any other $n$.

New times have brought the opportunity to translate the problem of constructing a regular $n$-gon into algebraic language. The possibility of constructing such a polygon with a compass and straightedge comes down to representing the roots **)** of the equation $z^n - 1 = 0$ or (if we discard the root $z = 1$) $z^{n-1} + z^{n-2} + \dots + z + 1 = 0$ in the form of square irrationals (i.e., expressing them through rational numbers using arithmetic operations and extracting square roots).

This reduction makes it possible to uniformly consider the cases available to the ancients, while the ancients themselves sought a special method for each case.

Gauss thought about the division equations (that is the name of the above equation), while simultaneously thinking about the divisibility of numbers. On March 30, 1796, prompted by the unexpected, he saw connections between these tasks.

Gauss later solved the problem of dividing a circle completely. The problem is reduced to the case of primes $n$ of the form $2^{2k} + 1$ (these are the so-called prime Fermat numbers), the construction follows the same scheme as for $n = 17$. The next $n$ of this type is equal to 257. There is evidence that Gauss was not too lazy to carry out constructions for this case as well. For simple $n$ that do not have this form, the construction, as proven (but not published) by Gauss, is impossible (in part, for $n = 7, 11$).

Now Gauss's fate was decided: there was no longer any talk of philology. He will be a Mathematician! In his later years, Gauss remembered how ideas were seething in his head at that time. He managed to make sketchy notes about what he was doing. Gauss begins to keep a diary. The first entry in it is dated March 30, 1796. It deals with the construction of a regular 17-gon. The second (dated April 8) indicates that he managed to prove the square law of reciprocity. From the diary we learned that the great mathematician continued to study, precisely through the action of a “student” exercise.

Gelstadt dissertation

In Braunschweig, Gauss did not have the literature necessary to work on his Arithmetic Studies. Therefore, he often traveled to neighboring Gelstadt, where there was a good library. Here, in 1798, Gauss prepared a dissertation devoted to the proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra - the statement that every polynomial with complex (in particular, with real) coefficients has a complex root (if you want to stay in the field of real numbers, then the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra can be formulated as follows: every polynomial with real coefficients is decomposed into the product of polynomials of the first and second degrees). Gauss critically examines all the attempts at proof and carries out D'Alembert's idea with great care. An impeccable proof still did not work out, since there was a lack of a strict theory of continuity. Subsequently, Gauss came up with three more proofs of the Fundamental Theorem (the last time in 1848).

Lemniscate and arithmetic-geometric mean

Let's talk about another line in Gauss's work, which began in childhood.

In 1791, when Gauss was 14 years old, he was preoccupied with the following game. He took two numbers $a_0, b_0$ and constructed for them the arithmetic mean $a_1 = \frac{a_0 + b_0}{2}$ and the geometric mean $b_1 = \sqrt{a_0 b_0}$. He then calculated the averages of $a_1, b_1$: $a_2 = \frac{a_1 + b_1}{2}, b_2 = \sqrt{a_1 b_1}$, etc. Gauss calculated both sequences with a large number of signs. Very soon he could no longer distinguish between $a_n$ and $b_n$ - all the calculated signs coincided. In other words, both sequences quickly converged toward a common limit $M(a_0, b_0)$ (called the arithmetic-geometric mean).

In those same years, Gauss tinkered a lot with a curve called a lemniscus (or Bernoulli's lemniscus) - a set of points, the product of the distances of each of them to two fixed points $O_1, O_2$ (foci) constructed and equal to $\left(\frac{1}{2} \left| O_1 O_2 \right|\right)^2$ (Fig. 1). Gauss began to systematically study the lemniscate in 1797. He tries for a long time to find the lemniscate until he realizes that it is equal to $\frac{2\pi}{M(\sqrt{2}, 2)} \left| O_1 O_2 \right|$.

We do not know how Gauss reported this, but we know that it was May 30, 1799 and that, without first having proof, he calculated both quantities with the same (!) decimal places. First of all, we must keep in mind that, starting with the work of Kepler, Galileo and Newton, astronomy has been the most prominent application of mathematics. This tradition was continued in the works of Euler, d'Alembert, Clairaut, Lagrange, and Laplace. Predicting and explaining celestial phenomena, mathematicians felt as if admitted to the secrets of the universe. Gauss, with his early interest in concrete calculations, could not, of course, help but try his hand at this traditional field.

However, there were also rosonic reasons. Gauss occupied the modest position of privatdozent in Braunschweig, receiving 6 thalers a month. A pension of 400 thalers from the patron duke did not insist on improving his position so that he could support his family, but he was thinking about turning yellow. It was not easy to get a chair in mathematics somewhere, and Gauss was not very keen on active teaching. The expanding network of the observatory made the astronomer's career more accessible.

Gauss began to become interested in astronomy while still in Göttingen. He carried out some observations in Brunswick, and he spent part of the ducal pension on the purchase of sextrate. It does a decent computational task, solving small problems for now. Thus, he publishes a simple method for calculating the time of Easter and other cyclical times together with extremely useful sieves; which have been used before. The idea of ​​the present problem appeared in 1801 under the following circumstances.

On January 1, 1801, astronomer Piashi, compiling a star catalog, discovered an unknown star of 8th magnitude. After observing it for 40 days, Piashi turned to major astronomers with a request to continue observations. Due to various principles, his request was not fulfilled. In June, this information reached Tsakh, who published the only astronomical magazine at that time. Tsach hypothesized that the furnace is “about a long-suspected new large planet between Mars and Uniter.” Tsach’s hypothesis seemed plausible, and it was urgent to look for the “lost” planet. And for this it was necessary to calculate its trajectory. Determining an elliptical path along an arc of 9°, which Piashi knew, was beyond the computational capabilities of astronomers. In September 1801, leaving all our affairs, we calculated the orbits of Gauss. In November the calculations were completed. They were published in the December issue of Tsach's journal, and on the night of December 31 to January 1—exactly a year after the observation of Piasha—the famous German astronomer Olbers, based on the trajectory calculated by Gauss, found a planet (it was called Zirera). It was signed!

March 25, 1802 Olbers discovers another planet - Pallas. Gauss quickly calculates its orbit, showing that it too is located between Mars and Juniter. The effectiveness of Gausast's computational methods for astronomers is undeniable.

Recognition comes to Gauss. One of the signs of this was his election as a corresponding member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences. Soon he was invited to take the place of director of the St. Petersburg General School. Gauss writes that he is flattered to receive an invitation to the city where Euler worked, and is seriously thinking about moving. In his letters, Gauss writes that the weather in St. Petersburg is often bad, and therefore he will not be too busy with observations, and there will be time for studies. He also writes that the 1000 rubles he will receive is more than the 400 thalers he has now, but life in St. Petersburg is more expensive.

At the same time, Olbers is making successful efforts to save Gaus for Germany. Back in 1802, he proposed to the curator of the University of Göttingen to invite Gaus to the post of director of the newly organized general school. Olbers writes at the same time that Gauss “has a positive meaning for the department of mathematics.” According to dado, the move took place only at the end of 1807.

During this time, Gauss got married (“life seems to me like spring with always new bright flowers”). In 1806, the Duke, to whom Gauss was apparently sincerely attached, dies of his wounds. Now nothing is holding him back in Brunswick.

Gauss's life in Göttigen was not sweet. In 1809, after the birth of his son, his wife died, and then the child himself. In addition, Napoleon imposed a heavy indemnity on Goettigen. Gauss himself had to pay an exorbitant tax of 2,000 francs. Olbers and, right in Paris, Laplace tried to introduce money for him. Both times Gauss proudly refused. However, another benefactor was found, this time anonymous, and there was no one to return the money (much later they learned that it was the Elector of Main, a friend of Goethe). “Death is less than my life,” writes Gauss between notes on the theory of elliptic functions. Those around him did not appreciate his work and considered him, to say the least, an eccentric. Olbers reassures Gauss, saying that one should not count on people’s understanding: “they must be desired and served.”

In 1809, the famous “Theory of the motion of celestial bodies revolving around the Sun, completed in 1807,” was published. The delay occurred due to the product's fears that the book in German would not find demand, and Gauss, from patronage reports, refused to print the book in French. The compromise was to publish the book in Latin. This is Gauss's only book on astronomy (in addition to this, he published several articles).

Gauss outlines his methods for calculating orbits. To be convinced of the power of his method, he repeats the calculation of the orbit of the comet in 1769, which Euler, who then lost his sight, calculated in three days of directed calculation. It took Gauss an hour to do this. The book describes the least squares method, which remains one of the most common methods for processing the results of overlays.

Gauss indicated that he had known this method since 1794, and since 1802 he had been using it systematically. (Two years before the publication of Gauss's Theory of Motion, the method of least squares was published by Legendre.)

In 1810, there were more honors: Gauss received the Prize of the Paris Academy of Sciences and the Gold Medal of the Royal Society of London, and was elected to several academies.

In 1804, the Paris Academy chose Pallas's perturbation theory as the topic for a major prize (a gold medal weighing 1 kg). The deadline was postponed twice (to 1816) in the hope that Gauss would submit the work. Gauss was helped in his calculations by his student Nikolan (“a young man tireless in calculations”), and yet the calculations were not completed. Gauss passed them while in severe depression.

Regular studies in astronomy continued almost until his death. The famous comet of 1812 (which “foreshadowed” the fire of Moscow!) was observed in court, using Gauss’s calculations. On August 28, 1851, Gauss observed a solar eclipse. Gauss had many astronomer students (Schumacher, Herming, Nikolan, Struve). The greatest German geometers Möbnitz and Staudt studied from him not geometry, but astronomy. He was in active correspondence with many astronomers, regularly read articles and books on astronomy, and published solutions. From letters to astronomers we learned a lot about mathematics. How does the appearance of Gauss the astronomer contrast with the idea of ​​an inaccessible hermit that existed among mathematicians!

In 1841, Gauss became acquainted with the German edition of Lobachevsky's work (Lobachevsky's first publications date back to 1829). True to himself, Gauss is interested in other publications of the author, limiting himself to speaking about him in correspondence with close correspondents. Electrodynamics and terrestrial magnetism

By the end of the 20s, Gauss, who had passed the 50-year mark, began to search for new areas of scientific activity. This is evidenced by two publications in 1829 and 1830. The first of them bears the stamp of reflections on the general principles of mechanics (here the “principal name of narrowing” of Gauss is built); the other is devoted to the study of capillary phenomena. Gauss decides to study physics, but his interests have not yet been decided. In 1831 he tried to study crystallography. This is a very difficult year

Gauss met him in 1828 at Humboldt's house. Gauss was 54 years old; his reticence was legendary, and yet in Weber he found a companionship in science that he had never had before.

The interests of Gauss and Weber lay in the field of electrodynamics and terrestrial magnetism. Their activities had not only theoretical, but also practical results. In 1833 they invent the electromagnetic telephone (this event is depicted in their common monument). The first telephone called the observatory and the physical institute. Due to financial principles, it was not possible to bring the telephone into life.

In the process of studying magnetism, they relied both on observations at the magnetic observatory created in Göttingen, and on materials that were collected in different countries by the “Union for the Observation of Terrestrial Magnetism,” created by Humboldt after returning from South America. At the same time, Gauss created one of the most important chapters of mathematical physics - potential theory.

The joint studies of Gauss and Weber were transformed in 1843, when Weber, along with six other prominent professors, was expelled from Göttingen for signing a letter to the king, which pointed out the latter's violations of the structure (Gauss did not sign the letter). Weber returned to Gottingen only in 1849, when Gauss was already 72 years old.
