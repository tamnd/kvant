Codes and statistics
We looked at various ciphers related to arithmetic operations on letter numbers. All these ciphers have one significant drawback - each letter transforms into the same character, no matter where this letter appears in the letter. To decipher such codes, methods of mathematical statistics can be used. Each letter of the Russian alphabet and space character corresponds to a certain frequency with which they (on average) occur in long texts:

| "space" | 0,175 | R | 0,040 | I | 0,018 | X | 0,009 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ABOUT | 0,090 | IN | 0,038 |Y | 0,016 |AND | 0,007 |
| HER | 0,072 | L | 0,035 |Z | 0,016 |Yu | 0,006 |
| A | 0,062 | TO | 0,028 |b,b0.014 |Sh | 0,006 |
| AND | 0,062 | M | 0,026 |B | 0,014 |C | 0,004 |
| T | 0,053 | D | 0,025 |G | 0,013 |SCH | 0,003 |
| N | 0,053 | P | 0,023 |H | 0,012 |E | 0,003 |
| WITH | 0,045 | U | 0,021 |Y | 0,010 |F | 0,002 |

Therefore, you can take the message being deciphered, count for each sign the frequency with which it occurs, and on this basis judge what this sign means. For example, the sign that comes across most often has many chances to turn out to be
white Well, if not a space, then perhaps “O” (this letter comes in second place). You can also study combinations of neighboring characters - most of the entire row with a vowel letter has a consonant. Further, in a short text, you can easily separate the signs for consonants from the signs for vowels. Such messages make it easier to solve codes based on simply replacing letters with signs.

Ciphers based on rearranging the letters of the message according to one rule or another are also relatively easy to solve.

In order for the code to be “according to the book,” the code numbers mean the numbers of lines and letters on a specific page of a certain book, and the page number can be changed depending on the date the message was written. In this case, without knowing which book formed the basis for the encryption, it is very difficult to reveal the secret of the code.

Nowadays, electronic computers are used to compose and solve ciphers. During the Second World War, when there were no computers yet, mechanical encryption machines were used for encoding and decoding. The outstanding English mathematician and logician Alan Thjoring, one of the creators of the so-called theory of algorithms, played a greater role in unraveling the code then used by fascist cryptographicists.
