Is there any food left in the refrigerator?

More precisely, the question would have to be formulated as follows: “How much does food usually dry out faster in the refrigerator than in the open air?” Let's try to figure this out.

Let's start with two facts that are certainly known to you:

1) Cold air is a soft menu (please remember why?).
2) The shallower the air, the more water in the form of steam can be present in it. How can this be explained?

The air, as a rule, comes into contact with some open bodies of water. In water at any temperature it will find molecules that can fly out from the surface of the water and form water vapor. Simultaneously with the evaporation process, the reverse transition of molecules from vapor to liquid (condensation) also occurs. Obviously, the higher the vapor density, the more active the condensation process is. If the evaporation of water occurs in a limited volume (for example, in a closed vessel), then there will certainly come a moment when the number of molecules leaving the liquid is equal to the number of molecules returning back. In this case, they say that equilibrium occurs between the liquid and its vapor (the amounts of liquid and vapor no longer change). Steam in this state is called saturated, thereby emphasizing that under constant conditions, further evaporation is no longer possible.

It is clear that the higher the temperature, the more intense the water evaporates and the greater, therefore, the density of water vapor must be for equilibrium to occur. In other words, the higher the temperature, the more water vapor can be contained in a given volume of air. For example, at 20°C in a room with an area of ​​12 m² and a height of 3 m, about 600 g of water can be in the form of steam, and at 100°C - about 20 kg!

The degree of air humidity (the content of a certain amount of water vapor in the air) is usually characterized using a special physical quantity - relative humidity. Relative air humidity is the ratio of the mass of water vapor contained in 1 m³ of air to the maximum mass of water vapor that is in this volume at a given temperature. This ratio is usually expressed as a percentage. If the amount of water vapor in the air does not change, and the air temperature rises, then the relative humidity will decrease. And on-

turnover: when the air cools, its relative humidity increases. As soon as it becomes equal to 100%, water vapor begins to condense, the “extra” steam will turn into dew or something else.

Now let’s see how the air inside the refrigerator behaves. All refrigerators have a freezer at the top. The cooled air chamber of the air goes down. When it comes into contact with the walls of the refrigerator and the food, it heats up. At the same time, its relative humidity decreases, and the ability to absorb water increases. Having heated up and taken away some of the water from the food, the air rises to the freezer. Here it is cooled to the initial temperature, but its humidity turns out to be higher than the original (due to water taken from the products). After a certain number of cycles, the air humidity increases so much that, upon approaching the freezer, he will be forced to leave some of the water on the chamber in the form of “sediment” - water droplets or ice crystals.

So, with the help of air, water “migrates” from warmer bodies to colder ones - from food to the freezer. In this case, a very effective transfer of heat occurs: during evaporation, a certain amount of heat from the products is transferred to the air, and during condensation, it is taken from the air and transferred to the freezer. Of course, water will be easily taken from hotter bodies. For example, to evaporate 12 water at a temperature of 0℃, the amount of heat required is about 2.59·10³ JS, and at 50°C - 2.38·10³ JS. (The same amount of heat is released during condensation.)

A natural question may arise: how are “dry” objects cooled? After all, in this case there is no transfer of water and the associated transfer of energy! This is indeed true. But there remains one more retrofitting process - when cooling and heating the circulating air. True, “dry” objects cool hotly more slowly than “wet” ones.

Now try to answer these questions yourself:

1. When you breathe in the cold, fog and other things appear. Why?
2. Why do my glasses fog up when entering the house from the cold? Why do glasses “sweat” in the cold?
3. Why is it common for showers to leak from the ceiling and from cold pipes, while hot water pipes are left dry?
