It doesn’t matter how such a temperature difference is created: whether the liquid is heated up or cooled from above - it is only important that the temperature decreases upward. The phenomenon can manifest itself on a variety of scales. The Frenchman Henri Benard, who first noticed it in 1900, used a microscope for observation*). However, this phenomenon can be seen in the area above the earth, and on the surface of water in the seas, and even in the structure of the photosphere of the Sun.

On the surface of hot coffee, Benard cells are clearly visible due to the following phenomenon. The intense flow of water vapor molecules rising from the hot surface of the coffee acts on the colder atmosphere immediately adjacent to the surface of the liquid with an upward force. Most of the water droplets that condense in moisture-saturated air either sink back into the liquid or rise into the atmosphere and evaporate there. However, these are precisely the droplets that are too large for the upper layers of air to rise, and at the same time too small for their gravity to overcome the pressure of those rising in the air above the surface of hot coffee. At the boundaries of the Benard cells there is a downward flow of steam, the droplets settle, and here the black surface of the coffee is thirsty. The fact that the number and size of droplets is determined by the origin of condensation centers can be proven with the help of a stale match held below the edge of the cup. The flame of a match, as is known, creates additional condensation centers*), as a result of which the concentration of droplets sharply decreases, their sizes become noticeably smaller, and they localize close to the surface of the hot liquid.

If the surface of a liquid is illuminated with a bright horizontal parallel beam of light (for example, the rays of the rising Sun, a light beam from a projector for diapasons, or from a bright flashlight) and observation is carried out in the direction of illumination and close to the axis of the light beam, then spectrally decomposed light can be seen above the surface of the dark liquid. The phenomenon of diffraction of light by small droplets in high layers of the atmosphere sometimes leads to the appearance of colored crowns around the Sun or Moon when they are covered with clouds consisting of almost uniform droplets**).

A skilled and true experimenter may also notice a characteristic and rather curious electrical phenomenon. If you bring a designated object (for example, an ebonite comb) to the surface of the liquid, then the droplets of moisture suspended in the air disappear.
