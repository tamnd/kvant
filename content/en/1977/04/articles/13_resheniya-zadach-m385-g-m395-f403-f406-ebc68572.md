M385. f) Prove that for any two convex multipoints $F$ and $G$ with vertices at the bridles, the expression $N(nF+mG)$ is a multipoint of $n$ and $m$ (here we mean the Minkowski sum).

In Kvanit No. 5 last year, the not-so-ordinary problem M385 was published. It was written earlier that it was a topic for real mathematical research. Therefore, decisions can be sent within six months.

In this issue of the journal we publish an article by the author of problem M385 A. Kushinirenko, in which a small theory has been developed that allows solving all points of the problem, except point d) *).

*) The number of points falling on the boundary of the polygon (polyhedron) $F$, which in the conditions of problem M385 is denoted by $N(\overline{F})$, in the article by A. Kushinirenko is denoted by $B(F)$.

Before receiving the letters from the editors, two solutions to problem d were known: a $n$-dimensional generalization of this problem was discussed by McMullen at the International Mathematical Conferences in 1974 and - with another solution - published by D. N. Bernishtein in the journal "Functional Analysis and Its Applications", No. 3 for 1976.

Readers A. Kasyanchuk from Nikolaev and M. Narodtsky from Kuibyshev added a solution to this problem, different from the known solutions (see below).

E. Turkevich from Chernivtsi added an eyewitness proof of formulas for the three-dimensional case. It is worth noting that most of the scientific article by J. Ritz “On the volume of solve polydras”, published in the journal “Proceedings of London Mathematical Society” 7, No. 7, 1957, is associated with the proof of one of these formulas.

V. Medved from Molodechno and E. Turkevich added several new formulas for the three-dimensional case (the reader will find one of them on p. 19 in exercises 8), and V. Smovbe from Moscow and M. Narodtsky added a generalization of problem M385 to the $n$-dimensional case.

Let us present a plan for solving problem d) sent by Kasyanchuk and Narodtsky.

Step 1. If $F$ and $G$ are convex polygons and $m$ and $n$ are natural numbers, then

$$S(nF + mG) = n^2S(F) + m^2S(G) + cnm,$$

(*) where $c$ is some constant, independent of $n$ and $m$. (This statement is problem 2,a) from the article by N. Vasiliev “Addition of figures”, «Kvant», 1976, No. 4).

Step 2. If $F_1$ and $F_2$ are integer polygons, then

$$N(F_1 + F_2) = N(F_1) + N(F_2).$$

(**)

Step 3. Using Peak's formula: $S(nF + mG) = N(nF + mG) - \frac{1}{2}N(\overline{nF} + mG) - 1$. Applying formulas (*) and (**), we obtain: $n^2S(F) + m^2S(G) + cnm = N(nF + mG) - \frac{1}{2}N(\overline{nF}) - \frac{1}{2}N(\overline{mG}) - 1$.

Since $N(\overline{nF}) = nN(\overline{F})$, we see that $N(nF + mG) = n^2S(F) + m^2S(G) + cnm + \frac{n}{2}N(\overline{F}) + \frac{m}{2}N(\overline{G}) + 1$ is a polynomial in $m$ and $n$.

L. Gavrilov

M395. The numbers (+1) and (-1) are placed at the vertices of a regular $n$-line with the center at point O. In one step, it is cut to change the sign of all numbers standing at the vertices of any regular $k$-line with center O (at the same time, we also allow 2-line, catching under the 2-line a segment with the middle at point O). Prove that in case $a), b), c)$ there is an arrangement called the arrangement (+1) and (-1) such that from it in no number of steps can one obtain a set of only (+1):

a) $n=15$,
b) $n=30$,

Note that the final arrangement depends on the initial arrangement and which polygons were selected, but does not depend on the order in which this was done. Indeed, each vertex contains the same number as in the initial arrangement if this vertex is in an even number of polygons, and a number opposite in sign if it is in an odd number; the order is therefore immaterial. In addition, it is clear that it makes no sense to replace signs more than once in the same polygon.

We call two arrangements equivalent if they can be obtained from each other (if arrangement $N$ can be obtained from arrangement $M$, then vice versa, $M$ can be obtained from $N$). The set of all arrangements equivalent to some arrangement is called an equivalence class; they are equivalent (since one can go from any of them to any one through an arrangement by ordering this class), but arrangements from different classes are not equivalent.

F405. A homogeneous monoxtomic sphere of radius $R$ was cut into two parts and fastened, as shown in figure 7. At what height is the center of the softness of the resulting glass, if the height of its stem is $h$?

Let us divide the glass into separate flat strips of height $\Delta h \ll R$ as shown in figure 8. Obviously, the center of gravity of the entire glass is, in essence, the center of gravity of the system of such strips.

But the center of gravity of each such strip is located at the center of a circle of radius $r_i$, where $r_i$ is the average radius of the strip. So we need to find the position of the center of gravity of the system of material points located on a vertical line passing through the center of the sphere. The mass of each point is the mass of the corresponding strip. Let's see what it depends on.

Let $\rho$ be the density of surface units of materials of the sphere. The surface area of ​​each strip is equal to (see Fig. 8)

$$s_i = 2\pi r_i l = 2\pi R \sin \alpha \frac{\Delta h}{\sin \alpha} = 2\pi R \Delta h,$$

and the mass—

$$m_i = \rho 2\pi R \Delta h.$$

We see that the masses of all stripes are the same and depend only on their height $\Delta h$ (given $\rho$ and $R$).

Thus, the center of gravity of a glass is the center of gravity of a system of materials of points of equal masses uniformly distributed along a vertical segment of length $2R$ ($\Sigma \Delta h_i = 2R$).

Consequently, the center of gravity of the glass is at the middle of its height, i.e. at a height $R$ from the “base” of the glass.

I. Slobodechkiy

F406. The satellite moves in a circular orbit at a distance from the earth's surface equal to its radius $R$. At some moments, a wetted nersia chalk of mass $m$ is launched from the satellite into the field of myotene chalk of mass $M$ equal to $U = -\gamma \frac{mM}{r}$.

From the conditions of the problem it is clear that the station’s starting point is the most distant point from the earth in the orbit of the “remnant” satellite. At this point, the speed of the satellite $u$ and the speed of the “remnant” $w$ are directed along one straight line perpendicular to the radius vector drawn from the center of the earth (Fig. 9). From the law of conservation of momentum

$$\vec{Mu} = \mu \vec{w} + m \vec{v}$$

(1)

($M$ is the mass of the satellite, $\mu$ is the retreat mass, $m$ is the mass of the station, $\vec{v}$ is its speed immediately after the launch) it follows that the speed of the station $v$ immediately after the launch is directed along the same straight line.

Obviously, a higher case is when the speed of the station immediately after the launch is directed in the same direction as the speed of the satellite immediately before the launch, and the speed of the “remainder” is directed in the opposite direction (see Fig. 9).

Then

$$|\vec{mv}| = |Mu| + |\mu \vec{w}|.$$

(2)

We find the relation $m : M$ from (2), taking into account that $m + \mu = M$:

$$\frac{m}{M} = \frac{|\vec{u}| + |\vec{w}|}{|\vec{v}| + |\vec{w}|}.$$

⟦folio 37⟧

Let's define the values ​​$|\vec{u}|$, $|\vec{v}|$, $|\vec{w}|$.

The speed of the satellite is determined from the condition of motion in a circular orbit. Centripetal acceleration is imparted to the satellite by the force of gravity towards the Earth:

$$\frac{M u^2}{2 R} = \gamma \frac{M_0 M}{4 R^2}$$

($M_0$ is the mass of the Earth). From here

$$|\vec{u}| = \sqrt{\gamma \frac{M_0}{2 R}}.$$

The total mechanical energy of the station at the moment of start is equal to $\frac{m v^2}{2} - \gamma \frac{M_0 m}{2 R}$. As you move away from the Earth, the potential energy of the station increases, and further from the Earth (at infinity) it is zero. The minimum speed that the station has at the moment of launch must be such that the decrease in the kinetic energy of the station during the flight is equal to its potential energy.

Then at infinity the kinetic energy of the station will be zero. Consequently, at the moment of start, the total mechanical energy of the station must be equal to zero, i.e.

$$\frac{m v^2}{2} - \gamma \frac{M_0 m}{2 R} = 0.$$

From here

$$|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{\gamma \frac{M_0}{R}}.$$

Let's determine the value of $|\vec{w}|$. According to Kepler's second law, the radius vector of the “remnant” moving along an elliptical orbit will notice equal areas in equal times.

If in perigree the speed of the remainder is $\vec{w}'$ (Fig. 10), then in a short period of time $\Delta t$

$$2 R |\vec{w}| \Delta t = R |\vec{w}'| \Delta t$$

(the time $\Delta t$ is small enough to assume that $|\vec{w}|$, $|\vec{w}'|$ and the lengths of the radius vectors remain constant).

According to the law of conservation of energy

$$\frac{\mu w^2}{2} - \gamma \frac{M_0 \mu}{2 R} = \frac{\mu (w')^2}{2} - \gamma \frac{M_0 \mu}{R}$$

From (4) and (5) we find $|\vec{w}|$:

$$|\vec{w}| = \sqrt{\gamma \frac{M_0}{3 R}}.$$

Substituting the found values ​​$|\vec{u}|$, $|\vec{v}|$ and $|\vec{w}|$ into expression (3), we finally obtain

$$\frac{m}{M} \approx 0,8.$$

V. Belonuchkin
