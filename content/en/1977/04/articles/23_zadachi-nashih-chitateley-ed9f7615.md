To the note “March drops”
(see “Quant” No. 3, p. 15)
1) For an ice stalactite to form, two conditions must be met: the air temperature must be slightly below 0°C, and there must be a source of flowing water. Water is formed from snow lying on the roof of a building when it is illuminated by sunlight. As is known, the illumination of a surface depends on the angle of incidence of the rays. Given the appropriate height of the Sun above the horizon and a suitable slope of the roof, the energy absorbed by the snow may be enough for the snow to begin to melt. But since the air temperature is below 0°C, the small drops of water that flow down freeze and form icicles. 2) At first, the ice icicle is a cylindrical column; water slowly flows down it and rises as a drop at its end. As the length of the column grows, individual drops of melt water do not have time to drain from it and freeze, collapsing the stalactite in its upper part. 3) The length of the ice cone, other things being equal, depends on some optimal speed of water flow to it: both higher and lower speeds do not contribute to the growth of the ice icicle. 4) Icicles can change under the influence of wind, convection air currents and other random factors. 5) An ice icicle is a crystal that is optically almost uniform and therefore transparent. Snow has a polycrystalline structure. Due to multiple reflections and refractions on the grains of individual snow crystals, the light in it is diffusely scattered. Therefore, the snow is not transparent.
