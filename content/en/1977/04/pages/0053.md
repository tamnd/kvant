Everywhere in what follows we will deal only with paraxyl rays *). In addition, we will limit ourselves to considering only thin lenses, that is, lenses whose focal lengths are significantly greater than their thickness.

If a thin lens is made of materials with a refractive index n, next to the lens there is a medium with a refractive index n₁, and to the right - with a refractive index n₂, then the following relations hold:

$$\frac{n_2}{F_2} = \frac{n - n_1}{R_1} + \frac{n - n_2}{R_2},$$

$$\frac{n_1}{F_1} = \frac{n - n_1}{R_1} + \frac{n - n_2}{R_2}.$$

Here F₁ and F₂ are the front and rear focal lengths of the lens, R₁ and R₂ are the radii of curvature, respectively, of the front and rear surfaces of the lens. These relationships can be obtained (do it yourself!) by considering the course of rays, indix from infinite remote calculus.

*) It is possible, in principle, to come up with refractive surfaces for which the condition of ray paraxity is not mandatory. However, spherical surfaces are the easiest to manufacture.

It is generally accepted that if a surface with its convex side faces a medium with a lower refractive index, then its radius of curvature R is positive (R>0), otherwise R<0. Lenses with a positive focal length (F>0) are called positive or converging, while lenses with F<0 are called negative or divergent.
