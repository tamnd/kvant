Before receiving the letters from the editors, two solutions to problem d were known: a $n$-dimensional generalization of this problem was discussed by McMullen at the International Mathematical Conferences in 1974 and - with another solution - published by D. N. Bernishtein in the journal "Functional Analysis and Its Applications", No. 3 for 1976.

Readers A. Kasyanchuk from Nikolaev and M. Narodtsky from Kuibyshev added a solution to this problem, different from the known solutions (see below).

E. Turkevich from Chernivtsi added an eyewitness proof of formulas for the three-dimensional case. It is worth noting that most of the scientific article by J. Ritz “On the volume of solve polydras”, published in the journal “Proceedings of London Mathematical Society” 7, No. 7, 1957, is associated with the proof of one of these formulas.

V. Medved from Molodechno and E. Turkevich added several new formulas for the three-dimensional case (the reader will find one of them on p. 19 in exercises 8), and V. Smovbe from Moscow and M. Narodtsky added a generalization of problem M385 to the $n$-dimensional case.

Let us present a plan for solving problem d) sent by Kasyanchuk and Narodtsky.

Step 1. If $F$ and $G$ are convex polygons and $m$ and $n$ are natural numbers, then

$$S(nF + mG) = n^2S(F) + m^2S(G) + cnm,$$

(*) where $c$ is some constant, independent of $n$ and $m$. (This statement is problem 2,a) from the article by N. Vasiliev “Addition of figures”, «Kvant», 1976, No. 4).

Step 2. If $F_1$ and $F_2$ are integer polygons, then

$$N(F_1 + F_2) = N(F_1) + N(F_2).$$

(**)

Step 3. Using Peak's formula: $S(nF + mG) = N(nF + mG) - \frac{1}{2}N(\overline{nF} + mG) - 1$. Applying formulas (*) and (**), we obtain: $n^2S(F) + m^2S(G) + cnm = N(nF + mG) - \frac{1}{2}N(\overline{nF}) - \frac{1}{2}N(\overline{mG}) - 1$.

Since $N(\overline{nF}) = nN(\overline{F})$, we see that $N(nF + mG) = n^2S(F) + m^2S(G) + cnm + \frac{n}{2}N(\overline{F}) + \frac{m}{2}N(\overline{G}) + 1$ is a polynomial in $m$ and $n$.

L. Gavrilov

◆

M395. The numbers (+1) and (-1) are placed at the vertices of a regular $n$-line with the center at point O. In one step, it is cut to change the sign of all numbers standing at the vertices of any regular $k$-line with center O (at the same time, we also allow 2-line, catching under the 2-line a segment with the middle at point O). Prove that in case $a), b), c)$ there is an arrangement called the arrangement (+1) and (-1) such that from it in no number of steps can one obtain a set of only (+1):

a) $n=15$,
b) $n=30$,

Note that the final arrangement depends on the initial arrangement and which polygons were selected, but does not depend on the order in which this was done. Indeed, each vertex contains the same number as in the initial arrangement if this vertex is in an even number of polygons, and a number opposite in sign if it is in an odd number; the order is therefore immaterial. In addition, it is clear that it makes no sense to replace signs more than once in the same polygon.

We call two arrangements equivalent if they can be obtained from each other (if arrangement $N$ can be obtained from arrangement $M$, then vice versa, $M$ can be obtained from $N$). The set of all arrangements equivalent to some arrangement is called an equivalence class; they are equivalent (since one can go from any of them to any one through an arrangement by ordering this class), but arrangements from different classes are not equivalent.
