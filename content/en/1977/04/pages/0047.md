Now let’s see how the air inside the refrigerator behaves. All refrigerators have a freezer at the top. The cooled air chamber of the air goes down. When it comes into contact with the walls of the refrigerator and the food, it heats up. At the same time, its relative humidity decreases, and the ability to absorb water increases. Having heated up and taken away some of the water from the food, the air rises to the freezer. Here it is cooled to the initial temperature, but its humidity turns out to be higher than the original (due to water taken from the products). After a certain number of cycles, the air humidity increases so much that, upon approaching the freezer, he will be forced to leave some of the water on the chamber in the form of “sediment” - water droplets or ice crystals.

So, with the help of air, water “migrates” from warmer bodies to colder ones - from food to the freezer. In this case, a very effective transfer of heat occurs: during evaporation, a certain amount of heat from the products is transferred to the air, and during condensation, it is taken from the air and transferred to the freezer. Of course, water will be easily taken from hotter bodies. For example, to evaporate 12 water at a temperature of 0℃, the amount of heat required is about 2.59·10³ JS, and at 50°C - 2.38·10³ JS. (The same amount of heat is released during condensation.)

A natural question may arise: how are “dry” objects cooled? After all, in this case there is no transfer of water and the associated transfer of energy! This is indeed true. But there remains one more retrofitting process - when cooling and heating the circulating air. True, “dry” objects cool hotly more slowly than “wet” ones.
