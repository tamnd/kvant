Cyclic accelerators

High-energy linear accelerators turn out to be very expensive. As an example, we point out that the linear electron accelerator in Stepford (USA) with an energy of 20,000 MeV has a length of more than 3.5 km. Throughout this continuous stretch there are uniquely powerful and very expensive high-frequency stations.

It turns out to be more economical to roll the trajectory of accelerated particles into a spiral or a ring, continuously returning them to the same accelerating stations. Such accelerators are called cyclic. To eliminate the trajectory, magnetic fields are used. No matter how expensive magnets are, no matter how high the cost of electricity required to power them, these costs are more than offset by savings on high-frequency devices.

Let's write some simple formulas. The curvature of the trajectory is caused by the Lorentz force acting on the particle from the magnetic field. This force is known to be equal to

$$F = evB,$$

where $e$ is the charge of the particle, $v$ is its speed, and $B$ is the magnetic field induction (it is assumed that the direction $v$ is perpendicular to $\vec{B}$). The Lorentz force is perpendicular to both the direction of the magnetic field and the direction of the particle velocity. It imparts to the particle a construction acceleration of $a = \frac{v^2}{R}$, so that the motion passes in a circle. The magnitude of the force $F$ and the radius of the circle are related, as is known, by the formula

$$F = \frac{mv^2}{R}.$$

Equating the rules of parts of formulas (5) and (6), we find

$$evB = \frac{mv^2}{R},$$

or

$$p = mv = eBR,$$

where $p = mv$ is the momentum of the particle. If we understand by mass the relativistic mass of the particle, determined by the formula

$$m = m_0 \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}},$$

then correspondence (7) turns out to be valid at relativistic energies.

Formula (7) shows that the induction of the magnetic field and the size of the accelerator are related not to the particle energy that is familiar to us, but to its momentum. Momentum and kinetic energy in relativistic mechanics are related by a rather complex formula

$$p = \frac{1}{c} \sqrt{K(K + 2m_0c^2)}.$$

Under the root of this expression there is a quantity that has the dimension of the square of energy. If we multiply this expression by $c$, we get

$$pc = \sqrt{K(K + 2m_0c^2)}.$$

This formula shows that together with the momentum $p$ it is convenient to consider the quantity $pc$, proportional to it, which has the dimension of energy ($pc$ is measured in electron volts). At $K \ll m_0c^2$ (non-relativistic case) this expression transforms into the familiar formula $p = \sqrt{2m_0K}$, and at $K \gg m_0c^2$ (high energies) we simply have $pc = K$. In these units, formula (7) takes on a form familiar to all physicists.

$$pc (\theta) = 3 \cdot 10^8 BR (m \cdot m).$$

Therefore, it was not possible to obtain stronger fields in the accelerator circuit using magnetic circuits.
