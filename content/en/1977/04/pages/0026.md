Observations over a morning cup of coffee

V. Schaefer

We borrowed this article from the journal “Uspekhi Fizicheskikh Nauk” (1972, volume 108, issue 3).
The author of the article, Vincent J. Schaefer, is the director of the Atmospheric Research Center at the State University of New York at Albany, USA.
The article is printed with certain abbreviations.

Careful observations of everyday phenomena reveal many extremely interesting chemical and physical phenomena when a cup of very hot black coffee is illuminated by a strong beam of light parallel to the surface of the liquid. The first time I observed these phenomena was with my eyes along the edge of the boiling springs in winter in Yellowstone Park *). A few years later I discovered them over my morning cup of coffee, shortly after the sun had risen. This time it all happened in the dry air of Northern Arizona.

If you fill a cup to the brim with black coffee (instant or regular), close to boiling, and look at it in suitable lighting, the first thing that will catch your eye is the bizarre cells that form on the surface of the coffee as the steam rises (see figure). Cells with a transverse size of 1 to 3 cm have the shape of irregular polygons and look like dusty light spots bounded by narrow dark lines. The spots mark the places where liquid soon appears on the surface. Then these flows spread over the surface, cool slightly, and in those places marked by dark lines, the output is immersed inside the coffee, forming a structure known as Benard output cells.
