Equiangular spiral

The second page of the cover shows two families of congruent equiangular spirals (more precisely, broken lines of chords inscribed in them).

An equiangular spiral can be defined as the trajectory of a point M, which moves along a ray uniformly rotating around a point O (the pole of the spiral), and its total speed at each moment forms the same angle α (Fig. 1) with the ray. This spiral has an infinite number of turns, not only when untwisting, but also when twisting.

Along this spiral, new bottles fly towards the candle flame. Due to the complex structure of the eyes, they are directed towards the target in such a way that their speed makes a constant angle with the direction towards the candle flame. The mollusk guides the section of its shell along this spiral (Fig. 2).

The equiangular spiral is used in a variety of technical devices. For example, rotating legs in cutting machines often have a profile cut in an equiangular spiral - at a constant angle to the surface being cut, due to which the blade of the leg grows together evenly (Fig. 3).

Returning to figure on the cover, you will notice that the spirals of the same family on it can be combined with each other by rotating relative to the center of the entire figure (aka the stripes of each of the spirals) at an angle $k \frac{\pi}{6} (k = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$. More

*) An equiangular spiral is also called matrix.

Once you meet at the same figure, you will notice quadrilaterals transforming into each other as a result of homothety with respect to a common center (each of them is the outline of two triangles - an equilateral and an isosceles rectangular). An equiangular spiral, when homothetic with respect to a pole, also moves away from itself.

These two properties of an equiangular spiral (the fact that it changes from itself during rotation and homothety) so delighted J. Bernoulli*) that he ordered to engrave its image on his tombstone. The features of this remarkable curve have attracted many outstanding mathematicians. The first of them were E. Torricelli** and R. Descartes (1596-1650)***.

E. Torricelli, by the way, found out amazing facts. It turns out that, despite the infinite number of turns, the length of other logarithmic spirals from its arbitrary point A to the pole O is finite.

Indeed, let us turn to figure 4. Let the equiangular spiral roll without slipping along its tangent AB. The stripe of the spiral O will then move at right angles to AO and will eventually hit point B. And the arc AO will roll along AB without sliding. Therefore the length of AO is $\frac{|OA|}{\cos \alpha}$.

And now the task: you need to find stripes of an equiangular spiral if the directions of the tangents at three known points are known.

V. Berezin
