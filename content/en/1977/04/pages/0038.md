F405. A homogeneous monoxtomic sphere of radius $R$ was cut into two parts and fastened, as shown in figure 7. At what height is the center of the softness of the resulting glass, if the height of its stem is $h$?

Let us divide the glass into separate flat strips of height $\Delta h \ll R$ as shown in figure 8. Obviously, the center of gravity of the entire glass is, in essence, the center of gravity of the system of such strips.

But the center of gravity of each such strip is located at the center of a circle of radius $r_i$, where $r_i$ is the average radius of the strip. So we need to find the position of the center of gravity of the system of material points located on a vertical line passing through the center of the sphere. The mass of each point is the mass of the corresponding strip. Let's see what it depends on.

Let $\rho$ be the density of surface units of materials of the sphere. The surface area of ​​each strip is equal to (see Fig. 8)

$$s_i = 2\pi r_i l = 2\pi R \sin \alpha \frac{\Delta h}{\sin \alpha} = 2\pi R \Delta h,$$

and the mass—

$$m_i = \rho 2\pi R \Delta h.$$

We see that the masses of all stripes are the same and depend only on their height $\Delta h$ (given $\rho$ and $R$).

Thus, the center of gravity of a glass is the center of gravity of a system of materials of points of equal masses uniformly distributed along a vertical segment of length $2R$ ($\Sigma \Delta h_i = 2R$).

Consequently, the center of gravity of the glass is at the middle of its height, i.e. at a height $R$ from the “base” of the glass.

F406. The satellite moves in a circular orbit at a distance from the earth's surface equal to its radius $R$. At some moments, a wetted nersia chalk of mass $m$ is launched from the satellite into the field of myotene chalk of mass $M$ equal to $U = -\gamma \frac{mM}{r}$.

From the conditions of the problem it is clear that the station’s starting point is the most distant point from the earth in the orbit of the “remnant” satellite. At this point, the speed of the satellite $u$ and the speed of the “remnant” $w$ are directed along one straight line perpendicular to the radius vector drawn from the center of the earth (Fig. 9). From the law of conservation of momentum

$$\vec{Mu} = \mu \vec{w} + m \vec{v}$$

(1)

($M$ is the mass of the satellite, $\mu$ is the retreat mass, $m$ is the mass of the station, $\vec{v}$ is its speed immediately after the launch) it follows that the speed of the station $v$ immediately after the launch is directed along the same straight line.

Obviously, a higher case is when the speed of the station immediately after the launch is directed in the same direction as the speed of the satellite immediately before the launch, and the speed of the “remainder” is directed in the opposite direction (see Fig. 9).

Then

$$|\vec{mv}| = |Mu| + |\mu \vec{w}|.$$

(2)

We find the relation $m : M$ from (2), taking into account that $m + \mu = M$:

$$\frac{m}{M} = \frac{|\vec{u}| + |\vec{w}|}{|\vec{v}| + |\vec{w}|}.$$
