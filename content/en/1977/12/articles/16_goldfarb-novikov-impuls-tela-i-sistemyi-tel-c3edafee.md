Body impulse and body systems

The concept of momentum (quantitative motion) was first introduced into mechanics by Newton. Let us recall that the impulse of material power (chalk) is understood as a vector quantity $p$, equal to the product of the body’s mass and its speed $*$:

$$p = mv.$$

Along with the concept of body impulse, the concept of force impulse is used. The impulse of force does not have a special designation. In the particular case when the force acting on the body is constant, the impulse of the force is by definition equal to the product of the force and the time of its action: $\vec{F} \Delta t$. In general, when a force changes with time $(\vec{F} = \vec{F}(t))$, the momentum of the force is defined as $\int_0^{\Delta t} \vec{F}(t) dt$.

 Using the concept of body momentum and force impulse, Newton's first and second laws can be formulated as follows.

 Newton's first law: the essence of the cut-off system, in which the momentum of a body remains unchanged if other bodies do not act on it or other acting bodies are compensated.

 Newton's second law: in inertial systems the cutoff changes

*) However, the development of physics in the 20th century showed that momentum is an “independent” concept and cannot always be represented as simply the product of a body’s mass and its speed.

In contrast to the traditional Galilean form of the second law: $ma = F$, the “impulse” form of this law allows it to be applied to problems associated with the movement of a body of variable mass (for example, rockets) and with movements in the region of near-light speeds (when the mass of a body depends on its speed).

We emphasize that the impulse acquired by a body depends not only on the force acting on the body, but also on the duration of its action. This can be done on a machine, on an experiment with a selected sheet of paper from under the bottle - we will leave it standing practically motionless if we make it a fish (Fig. 1). The sliding friction force acting on the bottle for a very short period of time, that is, a small impulse of force, causes a corresponding small change in the momentum of the bottle.

Newton's second law (in “impulse” form) makes it possible to determine by changing the momentum of a body the impulse of the force acting on a given body and the average value of the force during its action. As an example, consider the following problem.

When we move on to a system of interacting bodies (particles), it turns out that the total momentum of the system - the geometric sum of the momenta of the interacting bodies - has the replaced property of being conserved in time. This law of conservation of momentum is a direct consequence of Newton's second and third laws. In the textbook “Physics 8”, this law was derived for the case of two interacting bodies forming a closed system (these bodies do not interact with any other bodies). It is easy to generalize this conclusion to a closed system consisting of an arbitrary number $n$ of bodies. Let's show it.

According to Newton’s second law, the change in the momentum of the $i$-th body of the system over a short period of time $\Delta t$ is equal to the sum of the impulses of the forces of its interaction with all other bodies of the system:

$$\Delta p_i = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \vec{F}_{ik} \Delta t.$$

The change in the total momentum of the system $\Delta p$ is the sum of the changes in the impulses that make up the system of bodies: $\Delta p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta p_i$ and, according to Newton’s second law, is equal to the sum of the impulses of all internal forces of the system:

$$\Delta p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \vec{F}_{ik} \Delta t.$$

In accordance with Newton's third law, the forces of interaction between the bodies of the system are pairwise identical in absolute magnitude and oppositely positive in direction: $\vec{F}_{ik} = -\vec{F}_{ki}$. Therefore, the sum of all internal forces is equal to zero, and therefore $\Delta p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta p_i = 0$.

Not if the change of a certain quantity over an arbitrary short period of time $\Delta t$ is equal to zero, then this quantity itself is constant in time:

$$\vec{p} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{p}_i = \text{const.}$$

Thus, any change in momentum of any of the elements that make up a closed system is compensated by an opposite change in other parts of the system. In other words, the impulses of a closed system can be satisfied, but their sum remains constant in time.

If the system is not closed, that is, not only internal but also external forces act on the bodies of the system, then, reasoning in a similar way, we will come to the conclusion that the increment in the total momentum of the system over the time interval $\Delta t$ will be equal to the sum of the impulses of external forces over the same period of time:

$$\Delta p = \sum F_{\text{внешш}} \Delta t.$$

The impulse of the system can be changed by external forces.

If $\sum F_{\text{внешш}} = 0$, then the open system behaves like a closed one, and the law of conservation of momentum applies to it.

Momentum is a vector quantity, so the law of conservation of momentum can be applied to each of its projections on the coordinate axes. In other words, if $\vec{p}$ is preserved, then $p_x$, $p_y$ and $p_z$ are preserved independently (if the problem is three-dimensional).

In the case when the sum of external forces is not equal to zero, but the projection of this sum to a certain direction is zero, the projection of the total impulse to the same direction remains unchanged. For example, when a system moves in a field of gravity, the projection of its momentum onto any horizontal direction is conserved.

Task 4. A horizontal lightning bullet hits a wooden block suspended on a very long cord, and gets stuck in the block, giving it a speed of $|\vec{u}|=0,5$ m/s. Determine the speed of the bullet before impact. The mass of the bullet is $m=15$ e, the mass of the block is $M=6$ ke.

Braking a bullet in a block is a complex process, but there is no need to go into detail to solve the problem. Since there are no external forces acting in the direction of the speed of the bullet before impact and the speed of the block after the bullet gets stuck (the suspension is very long, so the speed of the block is horizontal), the law of conservation of momentum can be applied:

$$mv=(m+M)u.$$

Hence the speed of the bullet

$$\vec{v}=\frac{(M+m)\vec{u}}{m}; |\vec{v}| \approx 200 \text{ m/c}.$$

As a result of the condition - under the conditions of the Earth's gravity - there are no closed systems unless the Earth is included in them. However, if the interaction between the bodies of the system is much stronger than their interaction with the Earth, then the law of conservation of momentum can be applied with great accuracy. This can be done, for example, in all short-term processes: explosions, collisions, etc. (see, for example, task 1).

Task 6. Products are ejected from a rocket with mass $M$, sorted in portions of the same mass $m$ at a speed $u$ relative to the rocket. Neglecting the effect of gravity, let us determine the speed of the rocket that it reaches after the departure of the $n$th portion.

Let $\vec{v}_1$ be the speed of the rocket relative to the Earth after the release of the 1st portion of gas. According to the law of conservation of momentum
$$\left(M - m\right)\vec{v}_1 + m\left(u + \vec{v}_1\right) = 0,$$
where $\vec{u} + \vec{v}_1$ is the speed of the first portion of gas relative to the Earth at the moment of separation of the rocket-gas system, when the rocket has already reached a speed of $\vec{v}_1$. From here
$$\vec{v}_1 = -\frac{m}{M}\vec{u}.$$
Let us now find the speed $\vec{v}_2$ of the rocket after the departure of the second portion. In the reporting system, moving at a speed of $\vec{v}_1$, the packet is motionless before the departure of the second portion, and after the release it acquires a speed of $\vec{v}_2$. Using the previous formula and making the replacement $M \rightarrow M - m$, $\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2$ in it, we get
$$\vec{v}_2' = -\frac{mu}{M - m}.$$
Then $\vec{v}_2$ will be equal to
$$\vec{v}_2 = \vec{v}_2' + \vec{v}_1 = -mu \left(\frac{1}{M} + \frac{1}{M - m}\right).$$
Continuing this process further, it is not difficult to obtain
$$\vec{v}_n = -mu \left(\frac{1}{M} + \frac{1}{M - m} + \dots + \frac{1}{M - (n - 1)m}\right).$$
The law of conservation of momentum can be given another form, which simplifies the solution of many problems, if we introduce the concept of the center of mass (center of inertia) of the system. The coordinates of the center of mass (points $c$), by definition, related to the masses and coordinates of the particles that make up the system, follows the relations:

$$x_c = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2 + \dots + m_nx_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \dots + m_n} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i x_i$$
$$y_c = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i y_i$$
$$z_c = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i z_i.$$

It should be noted that the center of mass of the system in a uniform gravity field coincides with the center of gravity.

To clarify the physical meaning of the center of mass, let’s calculate its speed $\vec{v}_c$, or rather, the projection of this speed. By definition
$$v_{cx} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x_c}{\Delta t}.$$
In this formula
$$\Delta x_c = \frac{\sum m_i \Delta x_i}{m} \frac{\Delta x_c}{\Delta t} = \frac{\sum m_i \frac{\Delta x_i}{\Delta t}}{m},$$

$$v_{cx} = \frac{\sum m_i \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x_i}{\Delta t}}{m} = \frac{\sum m_i v_{xi}}{m} = \frac{p_x}{m}.$$
