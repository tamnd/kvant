Pierre-Simon Laplace

The Chancellor of the Imperial Senate, who received more than 100 thousand livres of annual annuity, with no less zeal than a simple academician, Laplace sought to respect all the irregularities and disturbances in the movement of the luminaries with an eye to the universal order, to extend the method of mathematical analysis to the phenomena of earthly physics and to subordinate to his formulas the phenomena of social life in which the average person sees a mystery or a blind clue.

Laplace, along with Lagrange, Monge, Lavuzier, was recruited to work on the Metric Commission, whose goal was to create a unified system of measures. Celestial mechanics

The beginning of Laplace's scientific activity came at a difficult time. A major stage in the construction of infinitesimal analysis has been completed. There were no problems around which the efforts of the greatest mathematicians could be concentrated. It seemed to many that the days of pure mathematics were numbered. Further, the versatile Lagrange, whose algebraic works were ahead of his time, at some point stopped studying mathematics. “Doesn’t it seem to you that high geometry is nearing decline? It is supported by milk from you and Euler,” he wrote to d’Alembert in 1772.

Under these conditions, the center of interest shifted towards applied mathematics, where the indisputable problem was to construct a theory of the motion of celestial bodies based on the law of universal gravitation.

The background to this problem is as follows. At the beginning of the 18th century, Kepler, thinking through the scrupulous observations of Tycho de Brahe from the point of view of the Copernican theory, formulated three laws that govern the movement of planets around the Sun. The desire to take into account the influence of at least one more object leads to the three-body problem, which in the general situation has not been solved to this day.

"Common Sense Refined"
This is what Laplace figuratively called the theory of probability. This is Laplace's second scientific love, which he remained throughout his entire scientific career, starting with his first works in 1774.

Laplace's style in this area is different from that which was characteristic of the author of Celestial Mechanics. There is no one big problem here and a lot of time is spent understanding what has been done before, starting with the problem of dividing bets, which stood at the origins of probability theory.

The focus is on J. Bernoulli's law of large numbers, which states that with a large number of trials, the frequency of an event in some sense approaches its probability. Starting from Moivre's result, Laplace obtains an estimate of the probability that this deviation is large. This is one of the central theories of probability—the Moivre-Laplace metorem. Its proof uses the means of mathematical analysis, which was new to probability theory.

Laplace appreciated and made available to science the results of the English saint Bayes on estimating the probability of competing hypotheses if the results of their tests are known.

The results of Laplace’s work were summarized in his “Analytical Theory of Probability,” which was published in three editions during his lifetime (the first in 1812). Here a lot of space is devoted to the creation of an apparatus, first of all, the method of arbitrary functions, which are applied far beyond the limits of probability theory. From Laplace comes the “classical definition” of probability, in which events are defined as a set of equally probable cases: “The theory of probability consists in reducing all events of the same kind to a certain number of equally probable cases, that is, cases, regarding the implementation of the components we are equally not settled, and in a certain number of cases that are favorable for the event, the probability of which we assume.”

Along with a book for “experts,” Laplace writes a book for the general public. This is his "Oltphilosophy of the Theory of Probability", which grew out of lectures given at the Ecole Normale in 1795, and was included in the 2nd edition of "The Analytical Theory of Probability" (1814).

Laplace's thoughts on the theory of probability were largely stimulated by his preoccupation with astronomy and cosmogony. But he was also concerned about the role of chance in public life. Most often, his statements on this matter do not contain specific calculations. There was one issue that Laplace relied on the formatting of - the application of probability theory to legal proceedings. The starting point is that the decision is absolutely reliable if necessary, and you only need to care that the decision is correct from the most correct side. It dates back to Condorcet and is closely related to the practice of judicial proceedings in decisions. Laplace’s position is more cautious, and yet he believes that it is necessary to calculate the probability “of mine that the decision of the court, which can only be carried out with a given majority, will be fair, that is, we will correspond to the true solution of the question posed,” and since “the majority of our existence is based on the probability of testimony, it is very important to subordinate their calculation.” It is supposed to include in the assessments the political sympathies of the judges, the degree of complexity of the case, the intellectual characteristics of the judges, etc. Life has shown the fallacy and social danger of such calculations.
