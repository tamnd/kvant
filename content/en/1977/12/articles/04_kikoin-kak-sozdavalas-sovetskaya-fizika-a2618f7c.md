How Soviet physics was created

We are finishing the publication of the memoirs of Academician I.K. Kikon.
Beginning see «Kvant» No. 10, 11.

Until the end of the twenties of our century, the basic conditions of world physics, including Soviet ones, were aimed at solving the problem of the structure of atoms, its electron shell and the physics of metals.

As for the problem of the structure of the atomic nucleus, few physicists have bothered with it. These were mainly physicists from the school of Rutherfort in England and Marie Curie in France. In the Soviet Union, Leningrad scientists L.V. Mysovsky at the State Radium Institute, D.V. Skobeltsyn at the Leningrad Polytechnic and Leningrad Physics and Technology Institutes worked on nuclear physics.

At that time we were taught, and then we ourselves were taught, that the nucleus consists of protons and electrons, that the number of protons is equal to the atomic weight of the element, and the number of electrons in the nucleus is equal to the difference between the atomic weight and the atomic number of the element.

In the early 1930s, nuclear physics experienced an information explosion—within two or three years, a stream of new fundamental discoveries literally hit us. In 1931, in Rutherforth's laboratory, Cockroft and Walton first calculated lithium nuclei by bombarding them with protons with energies of several hundred keV. In this case, α-particles with an energy of about 8 MeV were emitted from the lithium. The message about these experiences was expectedly discussed by us in the laboratories, in the corridors and even at lunch. This was the first time that the energy of the resulting particles significantly exceeded the energy of the bombard particles.

Somewhat later, these experiments were repeated in many laboratories around the world and, in particular, in our country by Kharkov physicists under the leadership of K. D. Sinelnikov.

In 1932, the English physicist Chadwick (a student of Rutherfort) discovered the neutron - a particle whose mass is close to the mass of a proton, but has no electric charge. It was a real sensation. Moreover, similar experiments had previously been carried out independently by German and French physicists, who, like Chadwick, observed penetrating radiation emitted by light elements (for example, beryllium) when bombarded with α-particles. This radiation easily penetrated a thick layer of lead and, when it hit the paraffin, knocked protons out of it. These physicists believed that the radiation penetrating through lead were gamma rays, but did not change their energy. Chadwick carried out such measurements and became convinced that γ-rays could not have such energies. He convincingly showed that uncharged particles, which turned out to be neutrons, penetrate through lead. For us, this was an additional illustration of how important quantitative experiments are in physics.

In the same 1932, a message appeared about the discovery in cosmic rays of a new elementary particle - the positron, which was a positive elementary electron.

All these messages were published in short notes, without details, in popular science magazines.

To quickly bring Soviet scientists into the mainstream of modern ideas of atomic nuclear physics, A.F. Ioffe decided to organize the All-Union Conference on the physics of the atomic nucleus with the participation of leading foreign scientists. This conference took place at the end of September 1933. It took place in the assembly hall of the Academy of Sciences in Leningrad. In terms of the composition of participants and the wide range of programmers, the conference had an international character. It was attended by the English physicist Paulli, French physicists Perrin, the Jollu-Curie spouses and other world-famous scientists.

Already after the first experiments on expanding the lithium nucleus, A.F. Ioffe realized that the physics of the atomic nucleus was entering a new era, and was involved in organizing research in this area at our Physico-Technical Institute. At first, a permanent seminar on nuclear physics was organized (separate from the general physics traditional seminar, which I have already talked about).

At this seminar (as far as I remember, it met on Wednesdays) all the new works in nuclear physics were discussed in detail. Although I did not intend to become a “nuclear scientist” because I was passionate about my research into semiconductors and metals, I carefully attended these seminars to keep abreast of new developments in physics. When the next sensational message was discussed at the seminar, the hall was overcrowded. Pretty soon there were two groups of “nuclear scientists” at the organization’s institute. One under the leadership of I.V. Kurchatov, the other under the leadership of A.I. Alikhanov. Both of these groups set out to create experimental technology that was new to them. It was necessary to acquire a source of α-particles, β-particles (electrons), γ-particles and neutrons. Such sources were products of the radioactive decay of radium, the reserves of which were concentrated at the Leningrad Radio Institute. The source of neutrons were ampoules containing gaseous radon (a decay product of radium), emitting α-particles, and beryl powder (beryl, irradiated by α-particles, emits neutrons). At the same time, instruments for detecting particles were being prepared - counters, cloud chambers, ionization chambers and much other types of technology, without which it is impossible to carry out experiments in nuclear physics. All this was developed relatively quickly, made with our own hands, and experimental work began to boil.

Soon after the discovery of the positron in cosmic rays, many physicists began to look for the possibility of producing positrons in laboratory conditions. These attempts were not only successful, but also led to the discovery of artificial radioactivity. Irène and Frédéric Jollu-Curie discovered that when light elements such as aluminum or magnesium are irradiated with alpha particles, they emit positrons. Investigating this phenomenon, they unexpectedly processed its experimental intensity. So the Curies made a double discovery. They processed artificial radioactivity for the first time and discovered a new type of radioactive decay - positron decay.

The report of these experiments was no less sensational for us than the discovery of the neutron. Artificial radioactivity during irradiation with α-particles is observed only in light elements. For heavy elements it was processed. This was explained by the fact that the probability of arbitrary charged particles (for example, α-particles) being internal is small. Since the beginning of the 50s, our institute began an intensive development of problems related to the peaceful use of atomic energy. The first result of this work was the launch in 1954 of the world's first nuclear power plant in the city of Obninsk near Moscow. It has a small power, only 5000 kW. But today in our country there are nuclear power plants of various types and medium-sized ones - the nuclear energy giant Leningrad Nuclear Power Plant with a capacity of 2,000,000 kW. In the 10th five-year period, the capacity of Soviet nuclear power plants will increase significantly.

The world's first nuclear-powered icebreaker, Lenin, built under the scientific guidance of physicists from our institute, has been successfully operating for more than 10 years, and has greatly contributed to the development of navigation in the polar seas. The recently built nuclear icebreaker Arktika has already sailed to the North Pole. The even more powerful nuclear icebreaker Siberia is preparing to enter service.

In the early 50s, physicists came up with the idea of ​​using the energy released in so-called thermonuclear reactions. Even before the war, the theory of the German physicist Boethe and other physicists appeared, according to which the enormous energy emitted by the Sun provides a thermonuclear reaction that proceeds as follows. When high temperatures are met, light nuclei, which have enormous kinetic energy, are able to overcome the Coulomb repulsive forces and can merge into heavier nuclei. This process is accompanied by the release of enormous energy, significantly exceeding the energy of merging nuclei.

Under “terrestrial” conditions, the thermonuclear reaction was used for the first time to produce an explosion of enormous power—the thermonuclear bomb was created. After this, the question arose about the possibility of using thermonuclear energy for peaceful purposes. Natural reserves

The reserves of the iron element as action (heavy hydrogen $^3\text{H}$), which can be used for thermonuclear reactions, are enormous - action is part of heavy water, which makes up 1/6000 of all the water available on the globe. And when two nuclei merge, the energy is about 13 MeV, which is much higher than the initial energy of the fusion nuclei. So this class energy is practically unexplored in this direction.

But in order to use this energy for peaceful purposes, it is necessary to prematurely re-initialize the energy of the nuclei. So this class energy is practically unexplored in this direction.

First of all, it was necessary to learn how to heat hydrogen to a high temperature at which the nuclei are capable of entering into a thermonuclear reaction. The simplest thing is to heat the gas with electric current. To do this, a discharge is created in the gas, and at high current values, the gas is heated due to Joule heat. In this case, the gas is completely ionized and turns into plasma. At very high temperatures, hydrogen plasma is a “mixture” of electrons and nuclei that can enter into a thermonuclear reaction. However, high-temperature plasma is very unstable - with huge currents flowing through the discharge, the one-dimensionally charged layer of plasma pushes apart, hitting the walls of the gas-discharge tube and quickly cooling.

The problem of plasma “containment” is one of the most difficult problems that must be solved on the way to controlling a thermonuclear reaction. And Soviet physicists made an invaluable contribution to solving this problem. Under the leadership of academicians L.A. Artsimovich and M.A. Leontovich, an installation in which a specially selected and oriented magnetic field makes it possible to retain high-temperature plasma was constructed in Istichit atomic energy. This is the so-called TOKAMAK - “toroidal chamber in a magnetic field.” Nowadays, all over the world, experiments on plasma confinement are carried out mainly in installations of this type. And they are called TOKAMAK all over the world.

Currently, research on the problem of controlling a thermonuclear reaction is at such a level that engineers and designers are already beginning to consider purely engineering, technical issues related to the implementation of a prototype of a future thermonuclear station. One can hope that by the end of the century or so a prototype will be built and tested, and then it will be possible to resolve the issue of the industrial use of thermonuclear energy.

Of course, I cannot give a comprehensive picture of the development of Soviet physics. I talk mainly only about those jobs that I, one way or another, had to deal with. Therefore, I did not talk about many outstanding achievements of Soviet physicists - such as the birth of quantum electrons in the work of Nobel Prize winners Academicians N. G. Basov and A. M. Prokhorov, the discovery of electronic parametric resonance by Academician E. K. Zavoisky, the discovery of the principle of aWTO charged particles by Academician V. I. Veksler and much more. However, I hope that my story helped readers «Kvant» imagine what a huge contribution Soviet physicists made to the development of science and technology over the 60 years of Soviet power.
