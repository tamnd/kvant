Drop on a hot surface

Looking at the world, one cannot help but be surprised.
K. Prutkov

Having placed it horizontally, drop a little water on it. If the temperature is around 100 °C (a little over 100 °C), then nothing special will happen. The droplet grows on the surface of the duck and quickly, in a few seconds, evaporates. If the temperature is significantly more than 100 °C (350-500 °C), the picture will be different. A droplet, falling on the duck, will bounce off it like a ball from the floor (low, to a height of 1-5 mm), and then move without touching the heated surface. The stability of this state depends, first of all, on the surface temperature - the hotter the surface is, the more capable the drop behaves. In addition, the time it takes for a drop to be interrupted until complete evaporation increases by 100–200 times. Moreover, the rate of droplet evaporation depends on its size: larger droplets quickly decrease in size to 3-5 mm, and small ones “live” for quite a long time without noticeable changes. In one of our experiments, a droplet with a diameter of 3 mm lasted until complete evaporation for about 5 minutes (300 seconds).

What is the reason for such a strange surface of the drop? Let's go back to the beginning of the experiment - a drop of water falls on a hot surface. At the initial moment its temperature is about 20 °C. Then, literally in a split second, the lower layer heats up to 100 °C, and such intense evaporation begins that the pressure force of the water vapor formed becomes greater than the force of the gravity of the drop. The drop jumps, then falls again on the utog. In just a few jumps, all the water in the drop manages to warm up to boiling point. Then, when the temperature reaches the surface, the drop quickly calms down and begins to move at a certain height above this surface. Obviously, in this case, the pressure force of water vapor balances the gravity force of the drop. In steady state, the drop is quite stable and “lives” for a very long time.
