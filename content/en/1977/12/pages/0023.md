Laplace's point of view is that distance probabilities are needed only where some information is unknown: “... we must truly consider the state of the Universe as a consequence of its previous state and as a cause of the subsequent one. A mind that would know for any given moment all the forces that animate nature, and the relative position of all its constituent parts, if in addition it were sufficiently general to subject these data to analysis, would embrace in one formula the movements of the magnitudes of chalk. The Universe is on a par with the movement of the smallest atoms: there would be nothing left that would be untrue for him, and the future, as well as the past, would be predicted before his eyes. The human mind in the perfection that it was able to impart to astronomy gives us an idea of ​​a weak sketch of a possible mind...” The hypothetical creature referred to in the quotation is now called Laplace’s demon.

Laplace's thoughts on the theory of probability were largely stimulated by his preoccupation with astronomy and cosmogony. But he was also concerned about the role of chance in public life. Most often, his statements on this matter do not contain specific calculations. Here is an example: “Let us not say that free and often dangerous misrepresentation is an inevitable consequence of the decision process, but we will only be extremely careful in changing our attitudes and communities to which we have long been applied. We know well from past experience the inconveniences which they present, but we do not know how great it will be that their change may cause. With such an unknown method of probability, we prescribe to avoid any change; We should especially avoid making changes, which in the correct order, as well as in the physical one, never take place without great help from animals.”

There was one issue that Laplace relied on the formatting of - the application of probability theory to legal proceedings. The starting point is that the decision is absolutely reliable if necessary, and you only need to care that the decision is correct from the most correct side. It dates back to Condorcet and is closely related to the practice of judicial proceedings in decisions. Laplace’s position is more cautious, and yet he believes that it is necessary to calculate the probability “of mine that the decision of the court, which can only be carried out with a given majority, will be fair, that is, we will correspond to the true solution of the question posed,” and since “the majority of our existence is based on the probability of testimony, it is very important to subordinate their calculation.” It is supposed to include in the assessments the political sympathies of the judges, the degree of complexity of the case, the intellectual characteristics of the judges, etc. Life has shown the fallacy and social danger of such calculations.
