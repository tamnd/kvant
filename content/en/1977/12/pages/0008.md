It is known that electrons emitted during β-decay have different kinetic energies, the values ​​of which range from zero to a certain maximum value that is characteristic of each nucleus. Meanwhile, it is obvious that during the radioactive transformation of a nucleus it passes from a certain initial state to a final state, the same is done definitely. In this case, a certain energy must be released, which is transferred to the electron emitted from the nucleus. Why do the emitted electrons have different energies? For example, $^{210}_{83}$Bi nuclei turn into $^{210}_{84}$Po nuclei as a result of β-decay. The maximum energy of electrons emitted for this reaction is $1,05 \cdot 10^6$ EV. In other words, the energy of each $^{210}_{83}$Bi nucleus decreases by $1,05 \cdot 10^6$ EV. The average energy received by each emitted electron turns out to be equal to $0,39 \cdot 10^6$ EV. Consequently, some of the emitted electrons have less energy than the energy “lost” by the nucleus. Where does the rest of the energy go?

This question was one of the most pressing at the beginning of the thirties. Niels Bohr even decided to propose that the law of conservation of energy may be violated in elementary nuclear processes.

A “way out” of this situation was proposed by the Swiss physicist Pauli. He proposed that during the decay of a nucleus, along with the electron, another part of an insignificantly small mass flies out, having no charge, which “carries away” with it “insufficient” energy. This particle is assigned to a neutrino (in Russian “neutrishka”). However, all attempts to detect neutrinos in experiments were unsuccessful. the neutrino remained “a secret particle, without a figure,” as we jokingly called it, rephrasing the corresponding passage from Tynyanov’s story “Lieutenant Kizhe.”
