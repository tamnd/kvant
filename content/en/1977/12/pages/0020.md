Halley's riddle about “great inequalities” was put to rest by 1784. “When I discovered these inequalities and determined with more attention than had been done hitherto, which had already been calculated, I was convinced that all observations, ancient and modern, were represented by my theory in all their power. Previously they seemed inexplicable using the law of universal shadowing; now they serve as one of his most striking exposures. Such is the fate of this brilliant discovery: every difficulty that arose turned into his merging, and this is the surest sign of his compliance with the spinal system of nature.”

Much success was required from Euler, D'Alembert, and Clairaut to construct a theory of the Moon's motion that was consistent with observations. The main effect that needed to be explained was the rapid (41° per year) movement of the elliptical orbit. The calculations of all three resulted in a displacement not exceeding 20°. Only in 1849 did Clairaut manage to clarify the calculations of the present, so that the required displacement was obtained (and they always thought about the correction terms in Newton’s law!). However, there remained one more “trifle”, replaced by the same Halley in 1693. Analyzing Polemaeus's "Almagest" and medieval information about eclipses, he reliably showed that the Moon's movement is accelerating; later calculations showed that the acceleration is 11″ per 100 years!

Laplace solved this riddle in 1787. The rooting turned out to be due to a previously discovered additional existential*) of the Earth's orbit: when the eccentricity decreases (the orbit becomes more circular), the average speed of the Moon increases. Another possibility that seemed “age-old” turned out to be additional!

Laplace does not miss a single mystery of astronomy. He had the right to say: “The posterity will probably see with blame that the latest geometry has not conveyed to him a single astronomical phenomenon, not the ultimate laws and reasons.” He shows that the rings of Saturn cannot be continuous, and the planet itself is strongly suggestive (Herschel confirms this with observations during Laplace’s lifetime). Laplace refines the theory of applications, shows with the help of perturbation theory how observations of the Moon can be used to determine the astronomical unit (the distance from the Earth to the Sun), to clarify the shape of the Earth.

Of course, Laplace did not go through much of the task about the satellites of Jupter, which has been traditional for all great astronomers since these satellites were discovered by Halley. In 1774, this problem was chosen by the Academy of Sciences as the topic for a prize. In 1789, Laplace built a theory of the motion of Jupter's satellites, taking into account the influence of the Sun and their interactions. In particular, it shows that the orbital time of the first satellite is equal to the same as the third orbital time of the second.

The main problem that worried Lagrange and Laplace in 1773-1784 was the problem of the stability of the Solar system as a whole. Perturbations for all the planets were systematically examined, and although no strict proof of stability was obtained, the agreement of all apparent anomalies with the theory of gravity was indisputable. Confidence in the perturbation theory was such that when inexplicable deviations in the movement of Uranus were discovered, Le Verrier decided to attribute them to the existence of a new planet.
