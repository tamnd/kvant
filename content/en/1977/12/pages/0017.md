General Bonaparte maintained contacts with the Institute in every possible way and took an active part in the work of the geometry department. During the Egyptian campaign, he signed his proclamation: “Bonaparte, Commander-in-Chief, Member of the Institute.”

In 1799, the first two volumes of Celestial Mechanics were published and Laplace - literally a few days before the coup of the 18th Briomere (November 12) - presented volume I to Napoleon. The general’s response said: “With the gratitude of principle, citizen, I have sent you a copy of Your Excellent.mpruda. In the first six months that I will have the opportunity, I will almost be able to ask yours for a wonderful work.”

After establishing the consulate, Napoleon decides to give the post of Minister of the Interior to the scientist. The choice fell on Laplace, most likely due to his great fame and personal acquaintance with Napoleon. However, Laplace's activities as minister were unsuccessful. Unlike his colleagues in the cabinet Talleyrand and Fouche, Laplace was unable to figure out in time where the thoughts of the consul, who was protecting the sciences, were directed. Not without naivety, he pursues royalism and religion: “Do not miss a single opportunity to prove with your content that there is no more royalism than the changes that traced the 18th century” (from the circular of Minister Laplace). Not many more months passed, and Napoleon replaced Laplace with his brother Lucien. Memoirs of Napoleon, written on the island of St. Elena, said: “The first-class geomer soon declared himself to be a more than mediocre administrator; His first steps in this field convinced us that we had been deceived in him. It is remarkable that not one of the questions of practical life was presented to Laplace in his true estimate. He looked everywhere for small things, little things; his ideas were distinguished by their extremeness; finally, he was completely imbued with the spirit of “infinitesimals,” which he also brought into the administration.”

Nevertheless, the exchange of courtesy between Napoller and Laplace did not stop. Having become First Consul, Napoleon appoints Laplace a lifelong member of the Protective Senate. (However, this Senate did not play any role in political life.) Since 1803, Laplace has been a candidate for the Senate. Among the few acts of the Senate was the abolition - according to Laplace's report - of the revolutionary calendar. The Order of the Legion of Honor is established, and Laplace is among its first recipients. In 1808 he became Count of the Empire.

Meanwhile, Laplace continues to work on Celestial Mechanics. In 1802, the third volume was published, dedicated to Napoleon - “I am trying to pacify Europe, to whom France owes its wining, its greatness and the most brilliant eloha of its weakness.” Napoleon's response says: “It is truly regrettable that the force of circumstances removed me from the learned priest.” A little later, already an emperor, Napoleon would write: “It seems to me that Celestial Mechanics has restored the splendor of our century.” On August 12, 1812, while near Smolensk, Napoleon received the “Analytical Theory of Probability” and again wrote: “At another time, having more leisure, I would read with interest your “Theory of Probability”... And further: “The dissemination, assimilation and mastery of mathematical sciences is the place of connection with the state’s freedom.”

Napoleon actively interferes in the activities of the Institute. In 1801, a mandatory uniform was introduced for members of the Institute. Members of the Institute spoke after months in sheeregu in the drawing room of the Tulle for presentation to the emperor. At this time, it was possible to transfer scientific works to the emperor and receive his “domestic” present. He patronized the exact sciences, but distrusted the humanities.
