⟦folio 32⟧

How are two sets of estimates different if for some $i : 1 \leq i \leq n$ the number of $i$ places in one set is not equal to the number of $i$ places in the other set.

Version 5. Prove that if the sum of a skater’s places does not exceed 20, then he ranks no lower than four.

Version 6. For any $k : 4 \leq k < n$, prove that if the sum of a skater’s places does not exceed $k + 16$ and his set of marks includes places greater than $k$, then he ranks no lower than $k$.

Let us prove by mathematical induction that for $k : 4 \leq k < n$ the sum of places does not exceed $k + 16$ guarantees the skater a place no lower than $k$. From here and from (1) it follows $\alpha_k = k + 16$.

Basis of induction. $k = 4$ - follows from Exercise 5.

Induction step. Let us assume that for $l : 4 \leq l < n - 1$ the sum of places does not exceed $l + 16$ guarantees the skater a place not lower than $l$. Let us prove that then the sum of places does not exceed $l + 17$, guarantees a place not lower than $(l + 1)$. Let's assume the opposite: the skater $A$ has a sum of places that does not exceed $l + 17$, and a place below $(l + 1)$. From Exercise 6 it follows that in the set of skater scores $A$ all places do not exceed $l + 1$. But then in the set of scores for a skater $B$, who has $(l + 1)$ place, all places do not exceed $l + 1$. From our rules and the conditions of the problem it follows that the sum of the places of the skater $B$ is less than the sum of the places of the skater $A$. This means that it does not exceed $l + 16$. And this already contradicts the assumption of induction.

Our statement has been proven. Thus, we have completely solved our problem.

In our problem, let’s omit the condition that no two skaters received the same amount of places. Then several skaters can have the same sets of scores. In this case, let's assume that all these skaters occupy fourth place, then we will assume that they have fourth place, and the rest of the skaters are claiming not fifth, but sixth place.

Version 7. Prove that no matter how we supplement the Rule for Determining Places, for any $4 \leq k < n$ the skater who received the sum of places $k + 16$ can take a place lower than $k$.

Let us assume that the sum of places of the skater who took first place does not coincide with any of the sums of places received by the other skaters.

Try to determine whether it is possible to supplement the Rule for Determining Places in such a way that all the results of our problem remain unchanged.

Try also solving our problem if the number of referees in the competition is 2.7, $p$.
