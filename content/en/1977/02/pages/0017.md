Experiments to study jet propulsion

Last school year, Evgeniy Ponomarev, an 8th grade student at secondary school No. 82 in the village of Chernogolovka, Moscow Region, under the guidance of physics teacher V.A. Orlov, did very interesting work on the experimental study of jet propulsion. In the published article, the author talks about the work done.

Reactive movement is usually called movement caused by the reaction of a leaking liquid or gas. This is how rockets move, for example. Hot gases formed when fuel is deposited into the rocket engine escape at high speed through the hole (nozzle) in the tail of the rocket. The reaction force of the escaping gases imparts acceleration to the rocket. As the rocket's mass gradually decreases (fuel burns), the rocket's acceleration pattern changes over time. How, then, can we calculate the speed of the rocket?

The famous Soviet scientist K. E. Tsiolkovsky theoretically derived a formula (it is now called the Tsiolkovsky formula) by which the speed of a rocket can be determined at any moment when its engine is operating. In particular, by the time the fuel is completely drained, the speed model $\vec{v}_1$ of the rocket is calculated by the formula

$$\vec{v}_1 = \vec{v}_2 \ln \left( 1 + \frac{m_2}{m_1} \right).$$

Here $\vec{v}_2$ is the speed of outflow of disintegration products relative to the rocket, $m_1$ is the mass of the rocket itself (without fuel), $m_2$ is the mass of the accelerated fuel, $\ln$ is the natural logarithm *).

This formula can be experimentally verified using a device for demonstrating jet propulsion. It is a model rocket that is sold in stores as a toy and is found in school physics classrooms. In addition, for the experiments you will need all laboratory levers and household equipment, a stopwatch, a beaker and a bicycle pump (Fig. 1).

The experimental task is to determine $|\vec{v}_1|$ in the experiment and compare it with the value calculated using the Tsiolkovsky formula.

First of all, a certain amount of water is poured into the rocket body, and a launching device with a bracket holding the rocket is inserted into the rocket nozzle. Then, air is pumped into the vertical rocket through the launcher. With the next clamp, the compressed air releases water, and the rocket, separating from the launcher, takes off.
