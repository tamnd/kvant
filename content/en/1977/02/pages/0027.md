⟦folio 25⟧

The knife must meet more than one size. We have obtained a necessary condition for the solvability of the problem: the number $q^n+1$ must be representable in the form $M^2N$, $M > 1$.

2) Let us now prove that this condition is also a sufficient mime.

Let's assume that $q^n+1 = M^2N$, $M > 1$. If the number $N$ is already $n$-valued, that is, if $q^{n-1} \leq N < q^n$, then everything is in order: $N$ itself is suitable as $A$: then $\overline{AA} = N(q^n+1) = M^2N^2$ is an exact square. But for now we can only say that $q^{n-i-1} \leq N < q^{n-i}$, $i \geq 0$ (i.e. $N$ may turn out to be less than $n$-digit). Let's take $r > 1$ such that $r^2 \leq q$ (this can always be done if $q \geq 4$; for example, put $r = 2$), and consider the geometric progression $\{N \cdot r^{2k}\}$, $k = 0, 1, \dots$. Since the first term of this progression is less than $q^n$ and the denominator $r^2 \leq q$, it is clear that it contains the term $B$, which belongs to the interval $[q^{n-1}, q^n]$. Since $B$ has the form $N \cdot r^{2i}$, then, putting $A = B$, we obtain that $\overline{AA}_q = N \cdot r^{2i}(q^n+1) = M^2N^2 \cdot r^{2i}$ is an exact square.

Our reasoning holds for all $q \geq 4$. This leaves the case $q = 2$ and $q = 3$. In the case of $q = 3$ we take $A = 1$ since $\overline{AA}_3 = 11_3 = 1 \cdot 3 + 1 = 4$ is a perfect square. In the case of $q = 2$, putting $n = 3$, we get $2^3 + 1 = 3^2 \cdot 1$, i.e. $N = 1 < 2$, and we need $2^2 \leq N < 2^3$. Multiplying $N = 1$ by $2^2 = 100_2$, we get a three-digit number $A = 100_2$. The number $\overline{AA}_2 = 100 \cdot 100_2 = 36$ is a perfect square.

3) Let us prove the existence of a solution for any $q$, i.e. we prove that for every $q$ there also exist $n$ and $M > 1$, which means $q^n+1 = M^2N$.

If $q - \text{число вида } rs^2 - 1$, $s > 1$, then $n = 1$, $M = s$, and the solution to the problem is the number $A = r$ (written as one digit):

$$\overline{rr}_q = r(rs^2 - 1) + r = (rs)^2.$$

In particular, such numbers are $q$ of the form $2^k - 1$, $k \geq 2$ (for $s = 2$, $r = 2^{k-2}$).

Here are the first few $q$ of the form $rs^2 - 1$:

3, 7, 8, 11, 15, 17, 19, 23.

Consider the number $q+1$. If it does not contain odd factors, then it is of the form $2^k$, i.e. $q = 2^k - 1$, and this is the case already considered. If $q+1$ contains an odd factor $p$, then

$$q^p+1 = [(q+1)-1]^p+1 = (q+1)^p-p(q+1)^{p-1}+\dots+p(q+1),$$

whence it follows that $q^p+1$ is divisible by $p^2$ (since $q+1$ is divisible by $p$).

In particular, for $q = 10$ we get $n = 11$, $M = 11$, $N = 826446281$, $A = 4^2 \cdot 826446281 - 13223140496$, so $1322314049613223140496 = (4 \cdot 11 \cdot 826446281)^2$.

4) So, the number $10^n+1$ is represented as $M^2N$, $M > 1$ with $n = 11$. Is $n = 11$ the smallest possible? To clarify this issue, we compiled a computer program and obtained the following prime factorizations:

$$10^1+1=11; \quad 10^8+1=17 \cdot 5882353;$$

$$10^2+1=101; \quad 10^9+1=7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 \cdot 19 \cdot 52579;$$

$$10^3+1=7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13; \quad 10^{10}+1=101 \cdot 3541 \cdot 27961;$$

$$10^4+1=73 \cdot 137; \quad 10^{11}+1=11^2 \cdot 23 \cdot 4093 \cdot 8779;$$

$$10^5+1=11 \cdot 9091;$$

$$10^6+1=101 \cdot 9901;$$

$$10^7+1=11 \cdot 909091;$$

so $n = 11$ is indeed the smallest.
