⟦folio 29⟧

Is it possible to “shake off” the thermometer almost immediately after changing the temperature?

F396. In a vessel filled with water, a vessel of varying diameter is attached upwards, motionlessly attached to a larger vessel and partly filled with water (Fig. 11). A kuskold floats on the surface of the water inside a changing vessel. What happens to the water levels in the vessels when the ice melts? How will the volume change if the changing vessel is not attached to the larger one and floats on the surface of the water?

The pressure in the liquid at its surface inside a small vessel at equilibrium is equal to the pressure of the air in the small vessel. In order for the air pressure not to change, it is necessary that it remains constant (we neglect the change in temperature when the ice melts).

The water formed when ice melts takes up the volume that was the part of the ice that was initially under water. If the water level in a small vessel did not change, the volume of water in the vessel would increase and the air pressure would decrease. So, as the ice melts, the water level in the small vessel rises, and in the large vessel, accordingly, it falls.

If a small vessel is not attached to a large one, but floats on the surface of the water (Fig. 12), then the pressure on the bottom of the vessel cannot change when the ice melts. Indeed, this pressure is equal to the weight of the contents of the vessel divided by the area of ​​the bottom of the vessel. Since none of these quantities changes, the pressure at the bottom of the vessel does not change.

But the pressure at the bottom of the vessel is equal to ρ|g|h, where ρ is the density of water, |g| is the acceleration of free pressure and h is the height of the column of water. Consequently, h does not change when ice melts. As for the small vessel, it will be submerged somewhat in water so that the volume of air in it remains the same.
