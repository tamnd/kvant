Squareword and werewolf words

The entertaining mathematical puzzle “squareword”, invented by L. Motsalov, should be a treat for fans of multi-step logic problems. The analysis of this game leads to very interesting results.

For example, how many letters need to be left in a square so that the remaining terminals of the square can be uniquely filled? Consider the 5x5 square shown at figure 1. It adds 12 letters to the first row, but there are two different fills for the remaining cells (Fig. 2, 3).

On the other hand, adding to the letters of the first line only two more letters at the beginning and at the end of the second line (Fig. 4), we obtain a set in which the filling is unique. It is not difficult to verify this; it is enough to find all the different fillings of a 5x5 square given the first row (there are only 8 of them).

What is the minimum number of filled terminals that completely determines the further arrangement of letters in the n×n square? Where are these terminals located?

It is easy to check that for a 4x4 square there are only two different fillings for a given first word (check!), and in each cell of a non-first row, the letter placed during the first filling differs from the letter that is placed there during the second filling. Therefore, by specifying only one additional letter, the filling of the square is determined uniquely, if it is possible at all.

Analysis of games on n×n squares for n>5 is much more complicated. It is more convenient to carry it out not with letters, but with numbers 1, 2, 3, ..., n. For example, for a 6x6 square, given the first row, 128 different fillings are possible, that is, again a power of two (2¹ for 4x4 squares, 2³ for 5x5 squares and 2⁷ for 6x6 squares). Will it always be like this? If you are interested in this topic, try to figure out 7x7 squares.

However, the letter filling of the square is called the idea of ​​another type of puzzle. In the Russian language, there are groups of words that differ only in the rearrangement of the letter (“resin” and “oil” or “author”, “product”, “brand”, “decoction”). These are werewolf words. Figures 5, 6 show two fillings of the square, in which the words “product” and “author” are simultaneously present. Is it possible to place the words “resin” and “oil” in a square at the same time? Or three of the set at once: “author”, “product”, “brand”, “decoction”? What about all four? How do other groups of shape-shifting words behave in this sense?
