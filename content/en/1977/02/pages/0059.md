10. The plane-parallel plate consists of two glass wedges with small angles $\alpha=1^\circ$ and refractive indices $n_1=1,5$ and $n_2=1,6$ (Fig. 11). A parallel beam of light falls on this plate, normal to its surface. Behind the plate there is a line of its own with a focal length of $F=180$ cm. The screen is located in the focal plane of the line. How far will the light point be displaced onto the screen if the glass plate is removed from the light beam?

11. A point light source $S$ is located on the main optical axis of a thin collecting line at a distance of $a=20$ cm from it (Fig. 12). At a distance of $a_1=30$ cm, on the same side of the line, there is a flat mirror, which is inclined at an angle of $\alpha=45^\circ$ to the optical axis. The line gives two images of the source $S$. Determine what the distance between these images is if the focal length of the line is $F=5$ cm.

P. Bulkin, N. Gorev, S. Kromov

Pi
and novel
"War and Peace"

The first page of the magazine cover shows two hundred and eighty consecutive digits of the number $\pi$ (in decimal). Each number from 0 to 9 is represented by a colored hexagon: 0 is brown, 1 is light red, 2 is orange, 3 is yellow, 4 is white, 5 is light green, 6 is green-lipped, 7 is blue, 8 is dark blue and 9 is purple. Then, moving from left to right along the decimal notation of the number $\pi$, we assign a colored hexagon to each digit in this expansion and place it next to the previous one, starting in the center and

“unwinding” further in a clockwise spiral (3.141592...).

This figure makes some interesting observations. It is easy to check that the number of polygons of each color is approximately $280/10 = 28$. Thus, in a given segment of the decimal notation of the number $\pi$, different digits appear approximately equally often.

There is an assumption that a more general and stronger statement is true: for any $l$ there is a piece of decimal notation of the number $\pi$ in which every combination of digits of length $l$ appears approximately equally often.

From this assumption it follows, in particular, that in the decimal notation of the number $\pi$ there is either a rearranged combination of digits.

And from this, in turn, one can make one paradoxical choice. Let's number all the letters of the alphabet and all the punctuation marks, as well as the space sign, in pairs of numbers from 00 to 99 (in fact, a lot of menish pairs will be required). Then we’ll take some kind of code and encode it: together with each character (letter, punctuation mark, space) we’ll put its number. Then, together with the books, we will get a sequence of numbers from which the original book can be uniquely restored.

If the above assumption is correct, then in the decimal notation of the number $\pi$ somewhere the code for the novel “War and Peace” (as, indeed, any other book, not even written yet!) appears.

On figure two single-bar hexagons occur many times in a row, and even three single-bar hexagons are decomposed several times. Quite recently, 100,000 digits of the number $\pi$ were calculated, using a computer, of course. The averages of them were discovered not only by the triples 000, 111, ..., 999, but also by several four identical numbers and even six nines in a row!

V. Vasilov
