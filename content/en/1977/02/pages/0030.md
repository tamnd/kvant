The medical thermometer is a so-called “maximum” thermometer. Its readings correspond to the maximum temperature for the entire measurement time. This happens due to “constriction”. When the root in the can is heated, it expands. The urgencies that arise in this case exceed the surface tension forces acting on the handle bar at the constriction point, and the handle is “pushed” into the capillary. When the thermometer cools down, the root begins to skim, the resulting forces compress the handle at the point of constriction, and the handle corresponding to the maximum temperature remains in the capillary. There are pairs of root between the stem in the capillary and the ruth in the cylinder.

Let us tell the thermometer an acceleration directed to the right (Fig. 9). At the first moment, the rod, due to inertia, will remain at rest and “enter” into the constriction, a convex bag will be formed. The pressure inside the rod under the meniscus will be higher than the pressure of the saturated vapor of the rod in the constriction by $\frac{2\sigma}{r}$, where $r$ is the radius of the meniscus. At the same time, the pressure at the right end of the handlebar in the capillary is higher than the pressure on the face $\frac{2\sigma}{R}$, where $R$ is the radius of the right handle. It can be considered equal to the radius of the capillary tube.

Let us consider the acceleration of the handlebar, shaded at figure, with a cross-sectional area $s = \pi r^2$. The pressure at its left end is $p + \frac{2\sigma}{r}$, and at the right - $p + \frac{2\sigma}{R}$ (p is the vapor pressure of the root). Therefore, the force $|\vec{F}_1| = \left( p + \frac{2\sigma}{r} \right) s$ follows, and on the right - the force $|\vec{F}_2| = \left( p + \frac{2\sigma}{R} \right) s$.

The difference between these forces imparts acceleration to the steward (of mass $m$)

$$|\vec{a}| = \frac{|\vec{F}_1| - |\vec{F}_2|}{m} = \left( \frac{2\sigma}{r} - \frac{2\sigma}{R} \right) \frac{s}{m}.$$

If the acceleration imparted to the thermometer is greater than this value $|\vec{a}|$, then the rod will “lag behind” and be “pushed” through the waist. In this case, the radius $r$ of the change in the constriction will decrease. Its minimum value is equal to the radius $r_0$ of the waist. Consequently, the maximum acceleration that would be imparted to the handler is equal to

$$|\vec{a}_0| = \frac{2\sigma s}{m} \left( \frac{1}{r_0} - \frac{1}{R} \right).$$

Since $r_0 \ll R$, then $\frac{1}{r_0} \gg \frac{1}{R}$, and we can assume that

$$|\vec{a}_0| \approx \frac{2\sigma s}{r_0 m}.$$

This value $|\vec{a}_0|$ is the minimum acceleration that must be imparted to the thermometer in order to “shake it off”. Let us estimate the values ​​of $|\vec{a}_0|$.

The mass of the handle above the waist is equal to $\rho V$, where $\rho = 13,6 \times 10^3 \text{ kg/m}^3$ is the density of the handle and $V$ is the volume of the handle above the waist. It can be estimated by assuming that the length of the table is approximately 4 cm, and the diameter is ~6·10$^{-5}$ m. Using these data and the value $\sigma = 0,5 \text{ h/m}$, we find that

$$|\vec{a}_0| \approx 80 \text{ m/cm}^2.$$
