If the package took off at a certain angle to the horizon and landed at a distance $s$ from the launch site, then

$$\vec{v}_1 = \sqrt{(|\vec{g}| t/2)^2 + (s/t)^2}.$$

Note that the height of the package in our experiments reached 30 m, so the launch should be done in an open space.

Now the problem essentially comes down to determining the speed $\vec{v}_2$ of water flow relative to the bag.

To do this, the force $\vec{F}$ with which the water jet from the nozzle of the bag hits the substituted spring balance is measured. Upon impact, water transfers all its momentum to the scales, therefore $\vec{F} |\Delta t = m_2 |\vec{v}_2|$, where $\Delta t$ is the time of water ejection, and the mass of ejected water is $m_2 = \rho S |\vec{v}_2| \Delta t$ (ρ is the density of water, S is the area of ​​the nozzle section). Thus,

$$\vec{v}_2 = \sqrt{|\vec{F}| / (\rho S)}.$$

To measure force, it is convenient to place an additional arrow on the scale, which does not return to its original position after the impact of water and records the maximum force of the impact.

Since measurements $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ cannot be carried out in the same sample, it is important to ensure that the air pressure in the bag is always the same. The easiest way to achieve uniform pressure is by weighing the rocket before pumping it up, so that the speed of the package increases with increasing mass of water (“spent fuel”). In experiment, it turns out that, starting from a certain value $m_2$, the speed of the packet decreases with increasing mass of water (Fig. 2). This contradiction can be explained by the fact that the speed of water outflow ($|\vec{v}_2|$) depends on its mass. Thus, with a large amount of water, the volume of the slope impact in the bag increases noticeably as the water is released. This leads to a decrease in the pressure on the water, and hence the rate of water flow. The optimal value $m_2$ can be found experimentally. In our experiments, the volume of water is 30 cm³ with a bag volume of 140 cm³.

In conclusion, we present the results of one of the experiments: mass of the packets $m_1 = 53,15$ e, mass of water $m_2 = 30$ e, mass of the injected impact on the water $m_B = 0,53$ e, cross-section of the nozzle packets $S = 3,1 \cdot 10^{-5}$ m², impact force of the scab $\vec{F} = 29$ n, experimentally measured speed of the packets $\vec{v}_{13} = 14,7$ m/sec, and calculated by the formula Tsiolkovsky $\vec{v}_{1p} = 13,9$ m/sec. From here

$$\frac{|\vec{v}_{13}| - |\vec{v}_{1p}|}{|\vec{v}_{13}|} \cdot 100\% \approx 5,4\%$$

that is, the Tsiolkovsky formula has been tested with a completely acceptable measurement error.
