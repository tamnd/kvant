Tasks
1. Construct an algorithm that finds word equivalence for the associative calculus in the alphabet $\{a\}$ with a single substitution
$$a a \leftrightarrow \Lambda.$$
2. Same problem for calculus in alphabet $\{a, b\}$ with substitutions
$$\begin{cases} aaaa \leftrightarrow \Lambda, \\ bb \leftrightarrow \Lambda, \\ abab \leftrightarrow \Lambda. \end{cases}$$
3. Same problem for calculus in alphabet $\{a, b\}$ with substitutions
$$\begin{cases} aaaaa \leftrightarrow \Lambda, \\ bb \leftrightarrow \Lambda, \\ abab \leftrightarrow \Lambda. \end{cases}$$
4. Same problem for calculus in alphabet $\{a, b, c\}$ with substitutions
$$\begin{cases} aa \leftrightarrow \Lambda, \\ bb \leftrightarrow \Lambda, \\ cc \leftrightarrow \Lambda, \\ ab \leftrightarrow c, \\ ac \leftrightarrow b, \\ ba \leftrightarrow c, \\ ca \leftrightarrow b, \\ cb \leftrightarrow a. \end{cases}$$
5. Construct an associative calculus with an undecidable equivalence problem with 7 substitutions in a two-letter alphabet. e are also indicated. It is allowed to take advantage of the fact that for the Tseytin calculus (p. 8) the equivalence problem is undecidable.
6. There is a number $n$, which does not contain a zero. If there are two identical numbers or two identical two-digit numbers next to each other, then they can be crossed out. On the contrary, you are allowed to put two identical numbers or two identical two-digit numbers in any place. Prove that by combining these operations one can obtain a number less than 10$^9$. (This problem, invented by G. Galperny, was proposed at the XXXIX Moscow Mathematical Olympid.)

M. Karajyan

Using expression (4), it is not difficult to show that for any $\alpha_1$ such that $0 < \alpha_1 < \pi$ (we restrict ourselves to considering only the upper half-plane),

$$\cos \alpha_2 < \cos \alpha_1, \text{ и } \alpha_2 > \alpha_1.$$

One could continue to consider the path of the ray $AM$ and find expressions for the corresponding angles $\alpha_3$, $\alpha_4$, ..., $\alpha_n$. However, we will leave it to the reader to continue this on their own. We only note that in the general case, with multiple reflections, the correspondence is valid

$$\alpha_1 < \alpha_2 < \alpha_3 < \dots < \alpha_n.$$

With increasing expentricity $\varepsilon$ the “convergence” of the rays accelerates.

The table below shows, as an example, the calculation results for angles $\alpha_2$, $\alpha_3$ and $\alpha_4$ depending on angle $\alpha_1$. Ellipse $\varepsilon = 0,5$. The table shows that after the third cycle of reflections, almost all the rays “line up” along the major axis of the ellipse.

Similar reasoning can be carried out for a mirror ellipson. More complex problems can also be considered, for example, when the light source is located at an arbitrary point inside the ellipson or when each point inside the ellipson surface is an emitter.

At the beginning, we called the property of the mirror ellipson under consideration an interesting geometric property. But the fact that almost all the rays "line up" along the major axis of the ellipson means that the light energy is almost all concentrated in one direction. Perhaps this can be used practically?

Note, however, that the conditions necessary for this—a perfectly reflective mirror surface of the ellipson and an “instantaneous” flash—are practically unrealistic. The second condition is especially difficult to fulfill. Indeed, the flash time t should be less than the light travel time $AMB: \tau < \frac{r_1 + r_2}{c_0} = \frac{2a}{c_0}$. Since the speed of light in vacuum is $c_0 = 3 \cdot 10^8 \text{ m/сек}$, then with reasonable dimensions of the ellipson ($a \sim 1 \text{ m}$) $\tau < 10^{-8} \text{ сек}$. So the problem considered is of purely theoretical interest for now.
