⟦folio 58⟧

Ask - we answer

The editor received a letter from tenth grader B. Ivachin. In it he writes:

“Looking through old issues «Kvant», I noticed a note at the end of the solution to problem M115 “... this problem first appeared in one of the works of the famous modern algebraist A. I. Shirshov. He needed it to build a server-based mathematical theory. Integrano find out how she solves it? Is mathematics used for this calculation, or is the decision made by the will of the element? After all, if it is elementary, my cycles can be written down by the will of server-side mathematical research.”

We asked Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences A.I. Shirshov to answer these questions.

Dear Borya!

You don't have to look through old «Kvant» numbers to come across interesting math ideas. They are all around us.

You are interested in problem M115. Let me remind you of its conditions:

The MRI of the vessel is filled with a whole number of liters of water. Any vessel is cut to pour as much water as it already contains from any other vessel. To prove that several maxims overflow, one of the vessels can be released. (The vessels are large enough: which one can take all the water together).

At one time I found the solution outlined below.

Let $(n, p, q)$ be some initial state of the vessels, and $\gamma$ be the most natural number such that at least one of the numbers $n+p$, $n+q$, $p+q$ is divisible by $2^\gamma$. Obviously $\gamma > 0$. Let $n+p=2^\gamma (2s+1)$, $n=2^\alpha (2t+1)$, $p=2^\beta (2r+1)$. Let's assume $\alpha=\beta$. Then $\alpha<\gamma$. If $n>p$, then we will make a transfusion $(n, p, q) \Leftrightarrow (n-p, 2p, q)$; if $n<p$, then transfusion $(n, p, q) \Leftrightarrow (2n, p-n, q)$. Since $n-p=2^{\alpha+1}(t-r)$ and $2p=2^{\alpha+1}(2r+1)$, at some step shown when twos become different, or the corresponding numbers become equal. Therefore, we can assume that $\alpha>\beta=\gamma$. If $n<p$, then we will make several transfusions $(n, p, q) \Rightarrow (2n, p-n, q) \Rightarrow \dots$ until $n_i$ becomes larger than $p_i$ (β will not change, but $\alpha$ will increase). So, $n>p$, $\alpha>\beta=\gamma$. If $p<q$, $(n, p, q) \Leftrightarrow (n, 2p, q-p)$ and $n+2p=2^{\gamma+1}m$, i.e. the number γ increases. If $p>q$, $(n, p, q) \Leftrightarrow (n-p+q, 2(p-q), 2q)$ and $2(p-q)+2q=2p$. $\gamma$ continues to increase. Since $\gamma$ cannot increase indefinitely, at some step an empty vessel will be obtained.

It’s easy to understand that if I had decided I would have been the same. All vessels can be emptied, except perhaps two.

Of course, in my work (Mathematical collection 45:2, 1958), there was no talk about vessels.
