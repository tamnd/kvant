Cones in frames

When solving problems involving combinations of cones, it is sometimes difficult to imagine their location in space. If we enclose the distance of the cones in “frames” (figures formed by the edges of polyhedra), then the spatial perception of the cones is obligated. At the same time, the object of the problem becomes more “tangible” and the solution can be found easier and faster, which is especially important for the applicant. First, let's prove one theorem.

Theorem. Near a cone, the value of which is less than π/2 in the axial section, one can describe a parameter whose base is an equilateral triangle, and the side edge passing through the vertex of this triangle is perpendicular to the opposite side face.

Proof. At the end, we will carry out an arbitrary development of the ASB section (Fig. 1). In the plane of the base of the cone, we draw a tangent m to the base of the cone at point B. In the development plane ASB, we construct a perpendicular to the image SB at point S until it intersects with the extension [BA] at point C. Since $\hat{ASB} < \pi/2$, point C is outside the cone (at the extension [BA]). Now from point C we draw tangents to the base of the cone until they intersect with line m at points D and E (M, N are the points of tangency).

Through the intersecting lines CE and CS, CD and CS, SB and DE we draw planes and obtain the parameter SCDE described around the cone *). Since in triangle CDE the altitude CB is also a bisector, then this triangle is equilateral. It is easy to show that the face SDE is also an equilateral triangle ($|SE| = |SD|$).

Thus, the SCDE parameter we constructed is the one we are looking for. Let us agree to call this parameter the frame parameter for a given cone. It is easy to show that the frame parameters of structural cones are structural.

Now it will go to the given ones.

Example 4 (Kyiv Polytechnic Institute, 1970). Six congruous cones have a common vertex, and each of the cones has one common tangent with four others. The name of the sum of objects of cones to the volume of a ball, touching the plane of the bases of all cones.

To solve this problem, we choose a cube as a frame, in the center of which there is a common vertex of the cones, the bases of the cones are inscribed in the faces of the cube (Fig. 5; only one of the cones is shown). In this case, each of the six cones will have one common tangent with the four friends, and the ball touching the base of the cones will be inscribed in the cube. Let the edge of the cube be equal to $a$. Then the volume of one cone

$$V = \frac{1}{3} \pi \frac{a^2}{4} \cdot \frac{a}{2} = \frac{\pi a^3}{24},$$

and six cones $\pi a^3/4$. The volume of the sphere is $\pi a^3/6$, from where the ratio is found; it is equal to 3:2.
