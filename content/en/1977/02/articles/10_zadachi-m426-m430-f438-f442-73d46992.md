It is known that the frequency of radiation of atoms moving at a speed of $v$ in the direction of the observable surface changes by values ​​of $\Delta f = \frac{v}{c}f_0$, where $c$ is the speed of light, $f_0$ is the frequency of radiation along the atom’s axis (this phenomenon is called the Doppler phenomenon). As a result, due to the broom motion of atoms, the spectral lines of atoms turn out to be broadened. Estimate the temperature of Ne atoms, knowing that in its emission spectrum a red line is detected: atoms $f_0 = 4,8 \cdot 10^{14}$ еч, width $\Delta f = 1,6 \cdot 10^9$ еч.

The average kinetic energy of thermal motion of a Ne atom is $\bar{E}_K = \frac{3}{2}kT$. On the other hand, $\bar{E}_K = \frac{m\bar{v}^2}{2}$, where $m$ is the mass of the atom, $\bar{v}^2$ is the mean square of the speed of its thermal motion. From equality $\frac{m\bar{v}^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2}kT$ we find the root-mean-square velocity of the Ne atom:
$$\sqrt{\bar{v}^2} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{\mu}},$$
where $\mu$ is the atomic mass Ne.

The change in the frequency of the radiation received is observed to depend on the speed $v_x$ of the source moving in the direction of the observatory. To estimate the magnitude of the coefficient, we can assume that the velocity component $v_x$ for different neon atoms ranges from $-\sqrt{\bar{v}_x^2} + \sqrt{\bar{v}_x^2}$, where $\sqrt{\bar{v}_x^2}$ is the root-mean-square value of the velocity $v_x$.

Using this somewhat simplified model, we obtain for the linewidth in the emission spectrum of the source
$$\Delta f = \frac{2\sqrt{\bar{v}_x^2}}{c}f_0.$$

Since $\bar{v}_x^2 = \frac{1}{3}\bar{v}^2 = \sqrt{\frac{RT}{\mu}}$, the final can be written
$$\Delta f = \frac{2/0}{c}\sqrt{\frac{RT}{\mu}},$$

whence follows
$$T = \frac{\mu c^2}{4R}\left(\frac{\Delta f}{f_0}\right)^2 \approx 700 \text{ °K}.$$

When the root in the can is heated, it expands. The urgencies that arise in this case exceed the surface tension forces acting on the handle bar at the constriction point, and the handle is “pushed” into the capillary. When the thermometer cools down, the root begins to skim, the resulting forces compress the handle at the point of constriction, and the handle corresponding to the maximum temperature remains in the capillary. $$|\vec{a}| = \frac{|\vec{F}_1| - |\vec{F}_2|}{m} = \left( \frac{2\sigma}{r} - \frac{2\sigma}{R} \right) \frac{s}{m}.$$

F396. In a vessel filled with water, a vessel of varying diameter is attached upwards, motionlessly attached to a larger vessel and partly filled with water (Fig. 11). A kuskold floats on the surface of the water inside a changing vessel. What happens to the water levels in the vessels when the ice melts? How will the volume change if the changing vessel is not attached to the larger one and floats on the surface of the water?

The pressure in the liquid at its surface inside a small vessel at equilibrium is equal to the pressure of the air in the small vessel. In order for the air pressure not to change, it is necessary that it remains constant (we neglect the change in temperature when the ice melts).

The water formed when ice melts takes up the volume that was the part of the ice that was initially under water. If the water level in a small vessel did not change, the volume of water in the vessel would increase and the air pressure would decrease. So, as the ice melts, the water level in the small vessel rises, and in the large vessel, accordingly, it falls.

If a small vessel is not attached to a large one, but floats on the surface of the water (Fig. 12), then the pressure on the bottom of the vessel cannot change when the ice melts. Indeed, this pressure is equal to the weight of the contents of the vessel divided by the area of ​​the bottom of the vessel. Since none of these quantities changes, the pressure at the bottom of the vessel does not change.

But the pressure at the bottom of the vessel is equal to ρ|g|h, where ρ is the density of water, |g| is the acceleration of free pressure and h is the height of the column of water. Consequently, h does not change when ice melts. As for the small vessel, it will be submerged somewhat in water so that the volume of air in it remains the same.
