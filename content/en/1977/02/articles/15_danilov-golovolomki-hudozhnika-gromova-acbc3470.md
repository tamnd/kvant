Take a look at the figures shown on p. 40-41. To transform any of them into any other (for example, to make an elephant out of a hippopotamus, a rhinoceros out of an ostrich, or a hunted animal out of a hunter), you don’t need to be a swearer or a wizard or turn to Hasan Abdurahman Ibn-Khottab, also called Hottab, for help. All these figures are made from parts of a non-educational construction set - parts of a square, cut as shown in Fig. 1. (Each part, if necessary, is allowed to be turned upside down.)

The artist Aleksadr Illarionovich Gromov came up with this method of cutting out a square and expressive figures that can be built from “pieces”. In 1930, the State Publishing House published 5 of his small puzzle books: “Indian”, “Steam Locomotive”, “Factory”, “Rooster” and “House”. Published by a small group of people, these fascinating game books have long since sold out and become a bibliographic rarity. In this issue we introduce the reader to figures from A. Gromov’s book “The Indian”.

The puzzles of A. Gromov are close relatives of the famous game “tangram”, who devoted many wonderful pages of his works to the classics occupied by mathematicians Sam Loyd and Henry Duden. A little science

A special branch of the theory, combinatorial geometry, studies the various arrangements of figures and, in particular, the transformations of equally composed figures.

An important theory of the combinatorial theory of the plane, proven in the thirties of the 19th century by the Hungarian mathematician Farkas Bolyany (father of one of the creators of non-Euclidean geometry - Janos Bolyany) and, independently of him, by the German mathematician Gerwin, states that any two equal-length polygons (having the same area) are equal, i.e. one of them allows division into parts from which another polygon can be formed. It is curious that the analytical theory is not true for space: as the German mathematician Dehn proved at the beginning of the 20th century, there are equal-sized polyhedra (having equal volumes), but not equal-composed polyhedra. The elementary proof of the theorem of Boyani - Gervin and Dehn is devoted to the brochure of V. G. Boltyansky “Equivalent Figures and Equal Figures”, published in 1956 by the State Technical Institute in the series “Popular Lectures on Mathematics”.

The Bojani-Gerwin theorem is one of the so-called pure existence theorems: it says nothing about how to find a partition of one of the equal-area polygons into parts from which another polygon can be composed. For a long time there were no general methods that would allow one to find the necessary partitions, and the search for them was carried out not so much by geometers as by “mathematical strategists”, who sometimes managed to find the concept of “nuggets” of amazing beauty. For example, Henry Dueden sums to cut a square into four parts, from which an equilateral triangle can be formed (Fig. 2).

The now living expert of the Australian Patent Office, Harry Lindgren, having developed general methods for solving wide classes of cutting problems, has transformed cutting geometry from a chaotic set of individual facts into a science. Harry Lindgren's book “Entertaining Cutting Problems” will soon be published by Mir Publishing House.

12. Given eight integers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_8$, with $0 < a_1 < \dots < a_8 < 16$. Prove that for some $k$ from the given eight integers one can choose at least three pairs $(a_i, a_j)$ related to the relations $a_i - a_j = k$.

13. Prove that whatever the natural numbers $a, m$, the remainders when dividing the numbers $a, a^2, a^3, \dots, a^n$, … by $m$ are periodic and repeat.

14. There is a set of 4 $n$ positive numbers. It is known that any four pairwise distinct numbers in this set can be used to form a geometric progression. Prove that there are $n$ identical numbers in the set.

15. Prove that from 990 different natural numbers not exceeding 1977, it is possible to choose three numbers, the sum of two of which is equal to the third. Is it possible to reduce the number 990 here?

16. The upper parts of a convex 65-gon contain different natural numbers, each of which does not exceed 1977. Prove that there are two diagonals for which the differences of the numbers written at their ends are the same.

17. Let $a_1, \dots, a_n$ be natural numbers, and $a_1 < \dots < a_n \leq 2n$. Prove that if the least common multiple of any of these two numbers is greater than 2$n$, then $a_1 > \frac{2n}{3}$.
