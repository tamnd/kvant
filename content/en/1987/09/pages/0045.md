It turns out that equilibrium stability and oscillation are closely related to each other. Whenever in physics we are talking about the behavior or properties of a system in relation to its equilibrium position, the problem of oscillations arises, or, as it is often called, the problem of an oscillator (from the Latin oscillo - I swing). There are a large number of problems in modern physical science regarding the properties of the oscillator.

Stable equilibrium is characterized by the fact that the potential energy of the system in this position is minimal. It is due to this that oscillations arise. Indeed, by deflecting the system from its equilibrium position, we thereby increased its potential energy. In this case, forces arise in the system tending to return it back (to reduce the potential energy). If you imagine such a system to itself, then it begins to “accelerate”, “rolling” to its equilibrium position (like a ball in a hole). Excess potential energy is converted into energy associated with movement, that is, kinetic. When passing through the equilibrium position, the “speed” of the system is maximum (all the potential energy of the transition is in kinetic energy), and the system passes through this position. Subsequently, the “speed” begins to decrease, the kinetic energy again turns into potential, etc. Oscillations arise.

It is no coincidence that we put the words “accelerate” and “speed” in quality. The fact is that the quantity $v = \Delta x/\Delta t$ (Δx is the “displacement” of the body from the equilibrium position), which plays a role in speed, sometimes coincides with the real speed, and sometimes not (we will explain this with an example below). But regardless of this, kinetics and honest energy are always proportional to the square of this quantity, i.e.

$$W_k \sim \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \right)^2.$$

Since for a moving body $W_k = mv^2/2$, then in a more general case the kinetic energy is usually written in the form

$$W_k = \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \right)^2.$$

The value $m$ here often coincides with the mass, but in principle it can differ from it.

The potential energy of an arbitrary system in relation to a stable equilibrium position can be written in the form

$$W_p = \frac{k}{2} \Delta x^2.$$

The factor 1/2 is introduced here simply for convenience (for symmetry with the expression $W_k = mv^2/2$), the coefficient $k$ is different in each specific case, and the value $\Delta x$, as already mentioned, is a small “displacement” of the body from the equilibrium position.

When describing any physical phenomenon, energy plays perhaps the most fundamental role. If only because the law of its conservation greatly limits the possibility of types of movement. It is extremely important in our case as well. Let's make some general statement:

If the total energy of the system, equal to the sum of potential and kinetic energy, at an arbitrary moment of time is written in the form

$$W = \frac{k \Delta x^2}{2} + \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \right)^2,$$

then this means, firstly, that the system is oscillating and, secondly, that the square of the oscillation frequency is equal to the ratio of the coefficients that determine the potential and kinetic energies, i.e. $\omega^2 = k/m$.
