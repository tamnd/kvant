⟦folio 16⟧

In his travels, observations, experiments, and forays, he saw the path that would lead him to discover the secrets of nature. Descartes later wrote:

“I perfected my book studies and decided to seek only that science that I could find in myself or in the great book of the world, and I used the rest of my youth to travel, see doors and armies, meet people of different nations, gather a variety of experience, test myself in the meetings that fate would send, and everywhere reflect on the objects encountered so as to extract some benefit from such studies.”

After graduating from college, Descartes spent some time with his father’s family, and then went to Paris, where at first he led the social life that was promised for a young and famous nobleman. Soon a turning point came - Descartes isolated himself in the subject of Paris Saint-Germain and the subject of scientific studies. In 1616 he received a bachelor's degree in law in Poitiers.

In 1618, Descartes volunteered to join the army that fought against the common enemy of France and Holland - the Spanish-Austrian troops. From 1619 he carried out military service in the army of the Duke of Bavaria. In 1620 Descartes was in Bohemia, the next year in Hungary. At this time, he decided to leave military service and returned to France, visiting Germany and Holland.

After visiting his father, Descartes continued his pages. He visited Switzerland and Italy, thus fully realizing the educational travel plan he had conceived in his youth. From 1625 Descartes settled in Paris. All these years he constantly met with scientists, mainly mathematicians and philosophers, as well as with practitioners who made optical instruments.

In Paris, Descartes developed the foundations of his scientific method. The young scientist's fame grew quickly. They spoke of it as a creative physiosphical system. Descartes felt the need for complete solitude to complete his research. He decided to “move away from all places where he could have acquaintances.”

In 1628, Descartes moved to Holland, which was then experiencing a time of economic and cultural prosperity. Its sailors made trips. Seaports and large cities grew in the country, crafts developed, and universities arose. The best scientific works were printed in Dutch printing houses, including the works of Copernicus and Halley's Dialogues. And most importantly, the country was distinguished by the religious tolerance necessary at that time and significant freedom from censorship.
