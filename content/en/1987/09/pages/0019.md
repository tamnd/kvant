Descartes presented the results of his philosophical ideas and natural science experiments in a number of works that brought him wide fame. The novelty and boldness of scientific ideas nevertheless brought Descartes to a collision with Protestant wealth. He was forced to leave Holland.

In 1649, Descartes accepted the offer of the Swedish Queen Christina, who wanted to gather two outstanding scientists from Europe. In one of his letters, he talked about how difficult it is for a person “born in the gardens of Touraine” to move to Sweden - “the country of bears, snow and ice.” Soon after the move, Descartes suffered a illness and contracted pneumonia. On February 11, 1650 he died. In 1663, Descartes' works were included in the list of books prohibited by the cataloging church. Despite this, Descartes' scientific ideas quickly spread throughout Europe, having a profound impact on the minds of his contemporaries.

Founder of the new scientific method

In his Discourse on Method, Descartes formulated the “main rules of method” and noted: “I have made a firm decision to strictly observe them without a single deviation.” These are the rules.

First: not to accept anything as true until you have recognized it as undoubtedly true, that is, try to avoid conjecture and prejudice and include in your being only that which appears to my mind so clearly and distinctly that in no way can give rise to doubt.

Second: divide each of the many difficulties being solved into a column of parts, as many as necessary, in order to better resolve them.

Third: guide the course of your thoughts, starting with the simplest and easily knowable objects, and ascending

Descartes emphasized that a scientific theory should be based on clear and simple principles.
