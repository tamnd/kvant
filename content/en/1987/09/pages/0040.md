Is it easy to describe movement?

Many concepts of cinematography - a branch of mechanics devoted to the study of the movement of bodies without finding out their cause - seem to us very simple, even elementary. However, it took centuries for science to develop effective methods for describing motion. Let us dwell in some detail on the history of the formation of the idea of ​​two cinematic quantities - speed and acceleration.

The movement of any body is associated with a change in its position in space over time. This idea, which is familiar to us, is not only unambiguous. Indeed, what, for example, should be taken as an independent variable when describing movement - path or time? Scientists of the early 17th century were not unanimous in answering this question. And if this difference in views did not lead to serious discrepancies in the study of uniform rectilinear motion, then in the case of uneven motion it turned out to be significant. Try, say, to establish the law of motion by assuming that speed is proportional to the distance traveled (v～s), and you will see that this is a much more difficult task than describing uniformly accelerated motion, in which speed is proportional to time (v～t).

The merit of systematic consideration of problems of cinematography of rectilinear uniformly accelerated motion belongs to G. Galileo (1564-1642). Having experimentally studied the free fall of bodies and their movement along an inclined plane with little friction, Galileo formulated the law of uniformly accelerated motion, which sounded like this: “If a body, leaving a state of rest, falls uniformly accelerated, then the distances it travels over certain periods of time are related to each other as the squares of time.”
