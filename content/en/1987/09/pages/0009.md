My student years - I studied at Moscow State University - were devastated by war. They are very memorable to me.

At the university we were very good at mathematics. The analysis course was taught to us by Professor I.V. Arnold. He was simply an artist, a real artist, we admired his lectures. We had a wonderful professor in differential geometry - A.P. Norden. He was such a teacher! He started taking notes on the lecture in the upper left corner of the dock, and wrote down the last formula by the end of the second hour in the lower right corner. I read it very well. Lectures on physics VEL by Professor A.V. Molodeevsky, and I didn’t really like them. But the physics workshop was very interesting. It was conducted by V.I. Iveronova and M.P. Shaskolskaya.

My immediate scientific supervisor was Pyotr Georgievich Strelkov. He had two amazing virtues. Firstly, he was a very skillful experimenter, and secondly, he gave us more freedom in our work and encouraged our independence. He inspired us greatly, promoted our first successes, and contributed in every possible way to our self-affirmation in science. This man gave me a lot, and I was very close to him. Six people were assigned to the Institute of Physical Problems. I think it was very useful for me that while still a student I ended up at the P. L. Capizza Institute. Pyotr Leonidovich created a special climate at the Institute of Physical Problems, surprisingly favorable for scientific creativity and especially for the education of young people. It was a climate in which the love of science—the desire for scientific knowledge—was paramount in life. We all attended an extraordinary seminar led by Alexander Iosifovich Shalnikov. The reports at the seminar were methodical in nature and sometimes lasted for a month or even more. Along the way, various questions arose, and we all sorted them out very carefully, discussed them... And Alexander Iosifovich constantly asked tricky questions, forced us to quickly explain all the intricacies of the installation that the speaker was talking about, and come up with designs for new devices. Thanks to his temperature, all discussions were very heated.

Then I worked at the Moscow State Institute of Measures and Measuring Instruments (MGIMIP), and this, I thought, was my use in choosing the direction of research, in creating a laboratory, I started everything myself from the beginning. And I “returned” to the IFP in 1956.

— What are your hobbies in life besides physics?

— I really like to travel. Previously, it was traveling with a backpack - 40 kg on our back and two weeks of tent life... The Urals, Pampir, Altai, Caucasus - we damaged a lot. We traveled a lot with my wife on a kayak.

I got carried away with joy and soldered four TVs; I do photography.

— What path do you think is most effective for a young man who has decided to devote himself to physics?

- The main thing - based on my experience - is that there should be more good popular science literature. Since I was in school, I remember Roberta Millikan's book about measuring the charge of an electron. Its cost was to reprint. In general, it would be wonderful if leading scientists wrote popular literature, so that science would reach the curious, as they say, first hand. But everyone is in a bad time right now. Now, if only the modern Ya. I. Perelman appeared.

I consider “management” to be a very important thing soon - gladly, gliders, carpentry, photos...

It would undoubtedly be useful if, in some cases, schoolchildren were given lectures by leading scientists at least once a month.

But still, popular science literature should play the main role in attracting young people to science. In this sense, «Kvant» is a very useful thing. Previously, when my son was in school, I read it often. And now I’m reading it and I think that the magazine is good, very necessary. There is no better one in this series, please.
