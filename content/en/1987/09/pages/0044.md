Does the gas pressure on the wall depend on its temperature? At first glance, it should depend. If, for example, there is no thermal equilibrium, then molecules should bounce off a cold wall with less energy than from a hot one.

However, even if one wall is kept cold using a refrigeration unit, the pressure on it still cannot be less than the pressure on the opposite hot wall. After all, then the vessel would move quickly without external forces, and this contradicts the law of mechanics: by freeing a fixed vessel with walls of different temperatures, we will not cause its displacement. The point here is that for a given nonequilibrium state of gas in a vessel, the concentration of molecules near the cold wall is compensated by an increase in the concentration of molecules and vice versa. As a result, the pressure on the cold and hot walls is the same.

Let's consider another version of the experiment. Let's cool one of the walls very quickly. At the first moment, the pressure on it will decrease, and the vessel will move a little; then the pressures will equalize and the vessel will stop*). But during this movement, the center of mass of the system remains in place due to the fact that the gas density at the cold wall will be slightly greater than at the hot one.

It should be noted that in fact the pressure does not remain a strictly fixed value. It experiences fluctuations, and therefore the vessel slightly “quivers” in place. But the amplitude of the vessel trembling is extremely small.

So, we finally came to the conclusion that the gas pressure on the walls of the vessel does not depend either on the quality of the walls’ processing or on their temperature.

G. Ya. Makshtsev

Harmonic vibrations and equilibrium

What do we study by considering the vibrations of a weight suspended by a string or externally? Two specific movements or something more?

It turns out that behind the “elementary” movements of the weight there is a huge, inexhaustible world of vibrations. Everything in nature fluctuates. Electrons vibrate inside.atoma,.atomy inside molecules, molecules inside a crystal. Houses, mostly and any other mechanical structures, sway. The number of shukas and crucian carp in reservoirs, wolves and hares in forests fluctuates. And this could go on ad infinitum.

Each oscillation is characterized by its own frequency $\omega$ (or period $T=2\pi/\omega$), and this frequency is the most important characteristic of any oscillating system. For example, the vibration frequencies of atoms in crystals determine the physical properties of the crystals: their heat capacity, thermal conductivity, electrical resistance, etc. The vibration frequencies of mechanical structures are associated with their strength and resistance to all kinds of mechanical influences. Some electrical systems (generators, antennas, etc.), in fact, are specially created in order to obtain oscillations of certain frequencies we need. It is clear, therefore, how important it is to understand the frequencies of oscillations and the occurrence of errors in various systems. But before we tell you how this is done, let’s discuss why vibrations are so common in nature.

We are accustomed to the fact that the world around us is quite stable. Houses and places do not collapse, ships do not capsize, wolves and hares coexist steadily with each other. It is completely obvious that a body lying at the bottom of a pit will never spontaneously “crawl out” of it, while a body lying at the top of a slide may well simply roll down.
