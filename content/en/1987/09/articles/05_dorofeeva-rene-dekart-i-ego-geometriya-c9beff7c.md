RENE DESCARTES
AND ITS “GEOMETRY”

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. V. DOROFEEVA

On June 8, 1637, in the Dutch city of Leiden, in the titography of Jean Maire, a book was published with the length of its time called “Discourse on the method of directing the mind and finding truth in the sciences.” Also, Dioptics, Meteors and Geometry, which are applications of this method.” The author of this book was Rene Descartes. He was then in his forty-second year and was rightfully considered one of the greatest mathematicians in Europe; however, “Discourse on Method” was his first published work. He had another thirteen years of life ahead of him, amazing works, but even one small part of the first printed work - geometry - was enough to immortalize his name. Together with Descartes' Geometry, the coordinate method entered science.

Life path

Descartes was born in 1596 in the province of Touraine in the south of France into a family belonging to a famous but impoverished family. In 1597 his mother died. When Rene was eight years old, the report sent him to the College of LaFlèche, shortly before the opening of the catalog order of the Nesuits for the enlightenment of the privileged duality.

During the first five years at the college they taught Latin and Greek, history, literature, and then for three years - philosophy, logic, mathematics and physics. Recalling his years of study, Descartes wrote in his Discourse on Method:

“I studied there everything that others had studied, and, not being satisfied with the information taught, I ran through all the books that were put under my hands, which treated the most rare and most interesting sciences... I especially liked mathematics, the fidelity and very dignity of my reasoning.”

He began early to demonstrate the necessary independence of judgment.

“I confess that I was born with such a mind that the main pleasure for me in scientific pursuits lay in the fact that I listened to other people’s opinions, but in the fact that I always strived to create my own.”

While diligently studying everything that the school offered, reading the scientific books that came to him on his own, the late Descartes at the same time experienced deep disappointment. He doubted the reliability of the knowledge received. Education at school and universities at that time was specialized. It presupposed faith in church teaching, the heritage of antiquity, scientific authorities, names, and treaties.

After graduating from college in 1614, the young man pondered a plan for his future life.

In his travels, observations, experiments, and forays, he saw the path that would lead him to discover the secrets of nature. Descartes later wrote:

“I perfected my book studies and decided to seek only that science that I could find in myself or in the great book of the world, and I used the rest of my youth to travel, see doors and armies, meet people of different nations, gather a variety of experience, test myself in the meetings that fate would send, and everywhere reflect on the objects encountered so as to extract some benefit from such studies.”

After graduating from college, Descartes spent some time with his father’s family, and then went to Paris, where at first he led the social life that was promised for a young and famous nobleman. Soon a turning point came - Descartes isolated himself in the subject of Paris Saint-Germain and the subject of scientific studies. In 1616 he received a bachelor's degree in law in Poitiers.

In 1618, Descartes volunteered to join the army that fought against the common enemy of France and Holland - the Spanish-Austrian troops. From 1619 he carried out military service in the army of the Duke of Bavaria. In 1620 Descartes was in Bohemia, the next year in Hungary. At this time, he decided to leave military service and returned to France, visiting Germany and Holland.

After visiting his father, Descartes continued his pages. He visited Switzerland and Italy, thus fully realizing the educational travel plan he had conceived in his youth. From 1625 Descartes settled in Paris. All these years he constantly met with scientists, mainly mathematicians and philosophers, as well as with practitioners who made optical instruments.

In Paris, Descartes developed the foundations of his scientific method. The young scientist's fame grew quickly. They spoke of it as a creative physiosphical system. Descartes felt the need for complete solitude to complete his research. He decided to “move away from all places where he could have acquaintances.”

In 1628, Descartes moved to Holland, which was then experiencing a time of economic and cultural prosperity. Its sailors made trips. Seaports and large cities grew in the country, crafts developed, and universities arose. The best scientific works were printed in Dutch printing houses, including the works of Copernicus and Halley's Dialogues. And most importantly, the country was distinguished by the religious tolerance necessary at that time and significant freedom from censorship.

Descartes lived in Holland for two years filled with continuous work. His time was distributed as follows: in the morning - long reflections and solving mathematical

Despite this, Descartes' scientific ideas quickly spread throughout Europe, having a profound impact on the minds of his contemporaries.

Founder of the new scientific method

In his Discourse on Method, Descartes formulated the “main rules of method” and noted: “I have made a firm decision to strictly observe them without a single deviation.” These are the rules.

First: not to accept anything as true until you have recognized it as undoubtedly true, that is, try to avoid conjecture and prejudice and include in your being only that which appears to my mind so clearly and distinctly that in no way can give rise to doubt.

Second: divide each of the many difficulties being solved into a column of parts, as many as necessary, in order to better resolve them.

Third: guide the course of your thoughts, starting with the simplest and easily knowable objects, and ascending

Descartes emphasized that a scientific theory should be based on clear and simple principles. It is necessary to study, describe, classify natural phenomena, conduct experiments and mathematical calculations. When studying nature, you need to rely only on your own strengths, and not wait for help from above, a divine revelation. These views of the scientist were incompatible with theology and scholasticism.

Descartes discovered the same law, apparently independently of Snell, published and substantiated it in his Dioptrics. Using it, he created the radiga theory.

"Geometry" by Descartes
This third appendix to the “Discourse on Method” disseminated the name of the scientist to a greater extent than his other discoveries. Descartes created the coordinate method, with the help of which technologies for connecting geometry and algebra are established. His method of solving algebraic problems using geometry is, on the contrary, using the theory of algebraic equations to solve geometric problems.

The main element of “Geometry” is a segment. If we add the segment $b$ to the segment $a$, we get a new segment $a+b$ - the sum of the segments $a$ and $b$ (Fig. 2). How to determine the product of segments $ab$? For the ancient Greeks, $ab$ is the area of ​​a rectangle with sides $a$ and $b$ (Fig. 3). Descartes broke this centuries-old tradition, which led to many inconveniences. His $ab$ is also a segment; it is found from the proportion $x/a=b/1$. figure 4 shows how Descartes constructed the segment $x=ab$. Similarly, from the proportion $x/1=a/b$ one can see how the segment $a$ can be divided into the segment $b$.

Descartes introduced the abscissa axis - the straight line on which the point $O$, which is the beginning of the report, is offset, and chose a certain segment as a unit (Fig. 5). With its help you can change the length of any segment. This means that for Descartes, each segment corresponds to a positive number - its length. He carries out operations with segments, and in essence, these are operations with positive real numbers: the lengths of segments are added, variable, the length of one segment is divided by the length of another.

The presence of the x-axis allows you to establish a connection between lines and equations. Let $F(x,y)=0$ be the algebraic equation $*$), for example, $3y-2x-6=0$. Let us plot on the abscissa axis a segment whose length is equal to $x$. From equation $F(x,y)=0$ we find the corresponding value $y$ and plot it on a straight line perpendicular to the axis. We get point $M$ (Fig. 6). The numbers $x$ and $y$ are called the coordinates of the point $M$.

If the values ​​of $x$ change, then the corresponding values ​​of $y$ change, and the point $M$, when moving, describes a certain line $L$.

Descartes' conclusion: the algebraic equation $F(x,y)=0$ corresponds to a certain line $L$ on the plane.

For example, our equation $3y-2x-6=0$ corresponds to a straight line, and our equation $xy-1=0$ corresponds to a hyperbola.

Before Descartes' research, negative numbers were encountered in mathematics only when solving algebraic equations. They were not equal to positive numbers.

The coordinate method led Descartes to a geometric interpretation of negative numbers.

Consider the equation $y^2=x$. Each positive number $x$ here corresponds to two values ​​$y$. The corresponding line is depicted at figure 7. The segments $AK$ and $KB$ lie on opposite sides of the abscissa axis. They clearly convince us that negative coordinates have the same right to exist as positive ones.

The basic idea of ​​Descartes' geometry is that the geometric properties of lines can be determined by the product of algebraic transformations of their equations. For example, to find the intersection points of the circle $(x-2)^2+y^2=1$ and parabolas $y^2=2x-3$, you need to solve the system
$$\begin{cases} (x-2)^2+y^2=1, \\ y^2=2x-3. \end{cases}$$

Using algebraic techniques, we obtain $(x-2)^2+2x-3=1$. Hence $x_1=0$, $x_2=2$. Then we find $y^2=-3$ or $y^2=1$.

The equation $y^2=1$ has two roots: $y_1=1$ and $y_2=-1$. In Descartes' terminology, $y_1=1$ is a “true” root and $y_2=-1$ is a “false” root. But, as figure 8 shows, from the point of view of geometry, both roots are completely equal.

The equation $y^2=-3$, in Descartes' terminology, has two “imaginary” roots. This is what he called complex numbers (see the article about them by L. S. Pontryagin in «Kvant» No. 2, 1985).
