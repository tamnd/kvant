We solved this problem easily:

$$s = v_0 t + \frac{gt^2}{2}, \quad v_t = v_0 + gt,$$

where

$$v_t = \frac{s + gt^2}{t} = \frac{30 \text{ m} + (10 \text{ m/c}^2)(0.5 \text{ c})^2}{0.5 \text{ c}} = 62.5 \text{ m/c},$$

but no connection with the previous task was found. However, as soon as the teacher wrote the final formula differently:

$$s = v_t t - \frac{gt^2}{2},$$

we immediately saw the reversibility of the movement. (It’s like in a movie when they play a scene in reverse order.)

Now our task can be formulated as follows:

During the first second of uniformly accelerated motion without initial velocity, the body travels 5 cm. Find the path during the warm second.

We quickly found several solutions. The teacher stopped at one of them, in which an expression was obtained for the path traveled in any second:

$$s_n = (2n - 1) \frac{a\tau^2}{2},$$

where $\tau = 1$ s, $n$ is the number of the second. Drawing our attention to the set (2n-1) - the recurrent formula of an odd number, the teacher wrote down:

$$s_1: s_2: \dots: s_n = 1: 3: \dots:(2n-1).$$

And then it became clear to everyone that our problem could really be solved orally:

$$s_3 = 5s_1 = 25 \text{ см.}$$

Gas pressure in the vessel

Does the gas pressure on the wall of a vessel depend on the wall materials and its temperature? Let's try to answer this question.

When deriving the basic equation of the molecular-kinetic theory of an ideal gas in the textbook “Physics 9” (§7), it is assumed that the wall is absolutely smooth and collisions of molecules with the wall occur according to the law of absolutely elastic impact. In other words, the kinetic energy of the molecules does not change upon impact, and the angle of incidence of the molecules is equal to the angle of reflection. Is this assumption justified and necessary?

Briefly put this way: the assumption is justified, but not necessary.

At first glance, it seems that the wall cannot be considered absolutely smooth in any case - the wall itself consists of molecules and, therefore, cannot be smooth. Because of this, the angle of incidence cannot be equal to the angle of reflection for any collision. In addition, the wall molecules perform chaotic flask-like equilibrium (participate in random tell motion). Therefore, when a gas molecule collides with any wall molecule, it can transfer part of the energy to the wall or, conversely, increase its kinetic energy at the expense of the wall.

Nevertheless, the assumption about the absolutely elastic nature of the collision of gas molecules with the wall is justified. The fact is that when calculating pressure, in the final case, the average values ​​of the corresponding quantities are important. Under the condition of Tellian equilibrium between the gas and the wall of the container, the kinetic energy of the gas molecules on average remains unchanged, i.e., collisions with the wall do not change the average energy of the gas molecules. If it were not so, then the tell equilibrium would be spontaneously disrupted. And this is impossible according to the second thermodynamics. The same may apply to the reflection of molecules in any justified direction - otherwise the vessel with the gas would begin to move, which contradicts the law of conservation of momentum. This means that the average number of molecules falling on the wall at a certain angle is equal to the average number of molecules flying away from the wall at the same angle. The assumption of specular reflection from the wall of each individual molecule corresponds to this condition.
