Does the gas pressure on the wall depend on its temperature? At first glance, it should depend. If, for example, there is no thermal equilibrium, then molecules should bounce off a cold wall with less energy than from a hot one.

However, even if one wall is kept cold using a refrigeration unit, the pressure on it still cannot be less than the pressure on the opposite hot wall. After all, then the vessel would move quickly without external forces, and this contradicts the law of mechanics: by freeing a fixed vessel with walls of different temperatures, we will not cause its displacement. The point here is that for a given nonequilibrium state of gas in a vessel, the concentration of molecules near the cold wall is compensated by an increase in the concentration of molecules and vice versa. As a result, the pressure on the cold and hot walls is the same.

Let's consider another version of the experiment. Let's cool one of the walls very quickly. At the first moment, the pressure on it will decrease, and the vessel will move a little; then the pressures will equalize and the vessel will stop*). But during this movement, the center of mass of the system remains in place due to the fact that the gas density at the cold wall will be slightly greater than at the hot one.

It should be noted that in fact the pressure does not remain a strictly fixed value. It experiences fluctuations, and therefore the vessel slightly “quivers” in place. But the amplitude of the vessel trembling is extremely small.

So, we finally came to the conclusion that the gas pressure on the walls of the vessel does not depend either on the quality of the walls’ processing or on their temperature.

Harmonic vibrations and equilibrium

What do we study by considering the vibrations of a weight suspended by a string or externally? Two specific movements or something more?

It turns out that behind the “elementary” movements of the weight there is a huge, inexhaustible world of vibrations. Everything in nature fluctuates. Electrons vibrate inside.atoma,.atomy inside molecules, molecules inside a crystal. Houses, mostly and any other mechanical structures, sway. The number of shukas and crucian carp in reservoirs, wolves and hares in forests fluctuates. And this could go on ad infinitum.

Each oscillation is characterized by its own frequency $\omega$ (or period $T=2\pi/\omega$), and this frequency is the most important characteristic of any oscillating system. For example, the vibration frequencies of atoms in crystals determine the physical properties of the crystals: their heat capacity, thermal conductivity, electrical resistance, etc. The vibration frequencies of mechanical structures are associated with their strength and resistance to all kinds of mechanical influences. Some electrical systems (generators, antennas, etc.), in fact, are specially created in order to obtain oscillations of certain frequencies we need. It is clear, therefore, how important it is to understand the frequencies of oscillations and the occurrence of errors in various systems. But before we tell you how this is done, let’s discuss why vibrations are so common in nature.

We are accustomed to the fact that the world around us is quite stable. Houses and places do not collapse, ships do not capsize, wolves and hares coexist steadily with each other. It is completely obvious that a body lying at the bottom of a pit will never spontaneously “crawl out” of it, while a body lying at the top of a slide may well simply roll down. So, in a certain sense, the house, the place, and the boat on the water are extremely similar

It turns out that equilibrium stability and oscillation are closely related to each other. Whenever in physics we are talking about the behavior or properties of a system in relation to its equilibrium position, the problem of oscillations arises, or, as it is often called, the problem of an oscillator (from the Latin oscillo - I swing). There are a large number of problems in modern physical science regarding the properties of the oscillator.

Stable equilibrium is characterized by the fact that the potential energy of the system in this position is minimal. It is due to this that oscillations arise. Indeed, by deflecting the system from its equilibrium position, we thereby increased its potential energy. In this case, forces arise in the system tending to return it back (to reduce the potential energy). If you imagine such a system to itself, then it begins to “accelerate”, “rolling” to its equilibrium position (like a ball in a hole). Excess potential energy is converted into energy associated with movement, that is, kinetic. When passing through the equilibrium position, the “speed” of the system is maximum (all the potential energy of the transition is in kinetic energy), and the system passes through this position. Subsequently, the “speed” begins to decrease, the kinetic energy again turns into potential, etc. Oscillations arise.

It is no coincidence that we put the words “accelerate” and “speed” in quality. The fact is that the quantity $v = \Delta x/\Delta t$ (Δx is the “displacement” of the body from the equilibrium position), which plays a role in speed, sometimes coincides with the real speed, and sometimes not (we will explain this with an example below). But regardless of this, kinetics and honest energy are always proportional to the square of this quantity, i.e.

$$W_k \sim \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \right)^2.$$

Since for a moving body $W_k = mv^2/2$, then in a more general case the kinetic energy is usually written in the form

$$W_k = \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \right)^2.$$

The value $m$ here often coincides with the mass, but in principle it can differ from it.

The potential energy of an arbitrary system in relation to a stable equilibrium position can be written in the form

$$W_p = \frac{k}{2} \Delta x^2.$$

The factor 1/2 is introduced here simply for convenience (for symmetry with the expression $W_k = mv^2/2$), the coefficient $k$ is different in each specific case, and the value $\Delta x$, as already mentioned, is a small “displacement” of the body from the equilibrium position.

When describing any physical phenomenon, energy plays perhaps the most fundamental role. If only because the law of its conservation greatly limits the possibility of types of movement. It is extremely important in our case as well. Let's make some general statement:

If the total energy of the system, equal to the sum of potential and kinetic energy, at an arbitrary moment of time is written in the form

$$W = \frac{k \Delta x^2}{2} + \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \right)^2,$$

then this means, firstly, that the system is oscillating and, secondly, that the square of the oscillation frequency is equal to the ratio of the coefficients that determine the potential and kinetic energies, i.e. $\omega^2 = k/m$.

Similarly, the frequencies of any vibration springs are found: molecules in a crystal, the Ostankino TV tower or a place across the bay in San Francisco, etc.

The second example (a little more complicated) is a mathematical matrix (Fig. 2). When writing the potential energy of a material point in a gravitational field, it is necessary to take into account that in the expression $W_p = mgh$ the values ​​are $h = \Delta x^2/2l$ (this follows from the similarity of triangles ABD and ABC on figure 2). $$W = \frac{mg}{2l} \Delta x^2 + \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{\Delta x}{\Lambda t} \right)^2, \quad \omega^2 = \frac{g}{l},$$

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}.$$

The vibration frequencies of a ball in a hole, a ship on water, etc. are found similarly.

Example three (electrical) - an oscillatory circuit (Fig. 3). At equilibrium, there is neither charge on the capacitor (C) nor current through the coils (L). When deviating from equilibrium, a charge $\Delta Q$ appears on the capacitor, and current $I = \Delta Q/\Delta t$ flows through the coils. The role of potential energy is played by the energy of a charged capacitor: $W_p = (\Delta Q)^2/(2C)$ (after all, it is precisely this energy that is associated with the mutual arrangement of charges). The role of kinetic energy is taken by the energy stored in the coils: $W_k = LI^2/2 = (L/2)(\Delta Q/\Delta t)^2$ (it is associated with current, i.e., with the movement of charges; $I = \Delta Q/\Delta t$ plays the role of speed). So,

$$W = \frac{1}{2C} (\Delta Q)^2 + \frac{L}{2} \left( \frac{\Delta Q}{\Lambda t} \right)^2,$$

$$\omega^2 = \frac{1}{LC},$$

In conclusion, I would like to say that oscillations are not one of the types of movement in a series of polygons, but represent a fundamental phenomenon, an element of that shadowy “alphabet” with the help of which nature creates everything that surrounds us.
