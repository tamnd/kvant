Did tachycardia and fibrillation occur? What is the mechanism of disruption of the regulatory wave pattern in the heart? These questions are answered by the mathematical theory of waves in the emerging heart.

We will try to qualitatively explain the mechanism of occurrence of described cardiac arrhythmias.

Excitation waves

Have you seen how the fuse cord burns? A wave of combustion along the cord, without dying out. The “shape” of such a wave can be represented as the dependence of temperature on time at various points along the propagation path. As the combustion front passes, a sharp, stepwise increase in temperature occurs, and after the fuel supply in a given place is used up, the temperature returns to the initial value. It is clear that when two combustion waves meet, they extinguish each other.

Waves in living organisms fill the combustion wave. A good example of such a biological wave is an excitation impulse that spreads along a nerve fiber. This is an electrical impulse without attenuation and distortion along a nerve fiber with a diameter of less than 0.025 mm and a length of up to 1.5 m at a constant speed of ten meters per second. A clear analogue of the author’s process is the propagation of a combustion wave in a medium that is capable of subsequent restoration of its properties. The simplest example of such a phenomenon is the burning of the steppe. Each time a wave of flame passes, all the grass burns, but then it slowly grows back, so that the steppe can be set on fire again. The steppe as an “excitable environment” has the property of recovery.

Individual muscle fibers of the heart are similar in electrical characteristics to nerve fibers. The muscle tissue of the heart consists of tightly woven fibers that are electrically connected to each other in areas of contact. The excitation impulse can pass from one fiber to another, so that excitation waves can propagate through the cardiac tissue in any direction. In this sense, cardiac tissue is an excitable environment.

How are these states realized?
In the absence of external influences, the element must remain in a state of rest indefinitely. By external influence it can be transferred from this state to a state of excitation. Trial determination of time $\tau_{\text{воэб}}$ in this state, the element passes to a state of refractoriness, in which it is inexcitable. The refractory state lasts for a time $\tau_{\text{peф}}$; After the procession of this time, the ability to excite is restored - the element goes into a state of rest. (All these transitions are shown schematically in figure 2.)

An additional proposal of the model is that excitation can be transferred from excited elements to neighboring ones that are at rest. As a result, the excitation wave propagates through the medium (figure 3). The speed $c$ of propagation of these waves is plotted.

The Wiener-Rosenbluth model defines the behavior of excitation waves in a medium.

1. There is a minimum time interval for the repetition of excitation waves. This period $T_{\text{min}}$ is equal to the sum of the long-term state of excitation and refractoriness: $T_{\text{min}} = \tau_{\text{воэб}} + \tau_{\text{peф}}$. If the waves follow with a minimum period, i.e., with the maximum possible frequency, then all elements of the medium are in two states: one is excited, the others are in a state of refractoriness (figure 4). The minimum spatial interval of wave repetition is $L_{\text{min}} = cT_{\text{min}} = c(\tau_{\text{воэб}} + \tau_{\text{peф}})$.

2. When a collision occurs, the waves cancel each other out. This is easy to understand - after all, during a collision, the excited elements find themselves “sandwiched” between two impending areas of refractoriness.

3. Excitation waves are not reflected from the boundaries. This statement is proven similarly to the previous one.

4. “Fast” wave sources suppress the activities of all “slower” sources. (“Fast” are sources that emit waves partly at short intervals of time.) Let’s make sure of this.

Let there be two periods of active sources in the medium, which emit waves at time intervals $T_1$ and $T_2$. figure 5 shows a time evolution of the wave pattern from these two precise sources. The left source emits excitation waves more frequently than the right one (i.e. $T_1 < T_2$). Over time, the point where the two fronts meet and cancel will blend toward the low-frequency right-hand source until it is suppressed. Thus, after a procession of quite a long time, only the “fastest” of the sources “survives” in the environment.

5. The excitation wave can circulate indefinitely along the ring or around the response in an excitable environment.

Let's take a narrow strip of excitation of the medium and roll it into a ring. If the length of the strip is $l > L_{\text{min}}$, then an excitation wave can circulate along the ring for an unlimited time (figure 6,a). Its circulation period is $T = l/c$.

Indeed, the front of the excitation wave cannot be a straight radial line rotating around the response with some angular velocity $\omega$. Indeed, in this case, at a distance $r$ from the center of the response, the front speed would be equal to $c=\omega r$ and increase indefinitely with increasing $r$. However, the speed of propagation of waves $c$ is constant. This means that distant parts of the front will begin to lag behind, and over time it will take the shape of a spiral.

Thus, a spiral wave can rotate around the response in an excitable medium for any length of time without attenuation. The period of its rotation is the time spent on the passage of the excitation pulse throughout the entire perimeter $l$ of the response, i.e. $T=l/c$. If the answer is round and its radius is $R$, then $T=2\pi R/c$, and the rotation frequency is $\omega=2\pi/T=c/R$.

So, we got acquainted with the Wiener-Rosenbluth model. How can it be used to explain the occurrence of cardiac arrhythmias?

Spiral waves and cardiac arrhythmias

The layers of the atrium are quite thin, so the heart tissue here can be considered a two-dimensional excitable medium. As a result of impaired blood supply after myocardial infarction, small areas of cardiac tissue may die, losing the ability to conduct excitation waves. These areas play the role of responses in the excitable environment of cardiac tissue.

What will the circulation of a wave of excitation around one of these responses lead to? The answer depends on how the circulation period relates to the frequency of the waves emitted by the sinus node. If the rotation frequency of the spiral wave is higher than the frequency of wave generation by the sinus node, then the spiral wave is a faster source and suppresses the activity of the sinus node. Spiral waves can arise from discontinuities in the front of excitation waves. Let us assume that in the medium there is a region $D$, the elements of which have an increased duration of the refractory state. Let two excitation waves move through the medium one after another, separated by a small time interval (figure 7,a). After the first wave passes through region $D$, the elements located in it remain in a state of refractoriness for a long time. Excitation from the second wave cannot penetrate this area - the wave front breaks (figure 7, b). After some time, the state of refractoriness in area $D$ will end and the elements located in this area will once again acquire the ability to excite. If by this time the second wave has not yet passed the region $D$, then the excitation from the front of this wave begins to spread inside the region $D$ (figure 7, b). The wave will then twist around the discontinuity area (figure 7, c), and in the meantime, the spiral wave shown at figure 7, d is formed.

Cardiac tissue is heterogeneous in its properties, including the duration of the refractory state. Heterogeneity further increases as a result of disruption of normal cell nutrition after a heart attack. In normal cardiac operation, the interval between successive waves of excitation is 1 second, and the duration of the refractory state is about 0.1 s. Therefore, the increase in refractoriness values, characteristic of living tissue, does not lead under normal conditions to a break in the front of excitation waves. With some lesions of the cardiac tissue, however, an additional wave is generated in it by the sinus node, separated from the previous one by a very short time interval. If this time interval is short enough (less than the sum of the maximum duration of the refractory state and the long excitation state), the front of such a wave will break at the boundary of the region with increased refractoriness, and a spiral wave will be born. figure 8 shows the formation of a spiral wave from a front discontinuity in an experiment with a piece of tissue taken from the heart of a rabbit.

The lifetime of a spiral wave in an inhomogeneous medium is finite. Due to the heterogeneity of the medium, the centers of the wave shift over time and spread across the medium. Sooner or later the wave reaches the boundary of the medium and disappears. With the disappearance of the spiral wave, the attack of increased heartbeat ends.

However, another outcome is also possible. At the front of the excitation wave - on the arm of the spiral wave - secondary breaks can form when passing through an area with increased refractoriness. As a result, the spiral waves will soften. If the speed of wave relaxation exceeds the speed of their disappearance at the boundaries, the number of spiral waves will increase with time until the entire medium is frozen.
