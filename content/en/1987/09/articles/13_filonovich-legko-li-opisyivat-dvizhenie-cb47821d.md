Is it easy to describe movement?

Many concepts of cinematography - a branch of mechanics devoted to the study of the movement of bodies without finding out their cause - seem to us very simple, even elementary. However, it took centuries for science to develop effective methods for describing motion. Let us dwell in some detail on the history of the formation of the idea of ​​two cinematic quantities - speed and acceleration.

The movement of any body is associated with a change in its position in space over time. This idea, which is familiar to us, is not only unambiguous. Indeed, what, for example, should be taken as an independent variable when describing movement - path or time? Scientists of the early 17th century were not unanimous in answering this question. And if this difference in views did not lead to serious discrepancies in the study of uniform rectilinear motion, then in the case of uneven motion it turned out to be significant. Try, say, to establish the law of motion by assuming that speed is proportional to the distance traveled (v～s), and you will see that this is a much more difficult task than describing uniformly accelerated motion, in which speed is proportional to time (v～t).

The merit of systematic consideration of problems of cinematography of rectilinear uniformly accelerated motion belongs to G. Galileo (1564-1642). Having experimentally studied the free fall of bodies and their movement along an inclined plane with little friction, Galileo formulated the law of uniformly accelerated motion, which sounded like this: “If a body, leaving a state of rest, falls uniformly accelerated, then the distances it travels over certain periods of time are related to each other as the squares of time.”

Galileo's idea that free fall occurs uniformly accelerated was difficult for his contemporaries to accept. One of the heroes of Galileo’s book “Proceedings and Mathematical Proofs” gives a contrary objection that seems convincing to him: “If a heavily falling body leaves a state of rest in such a way that its speed increases in proportion to the time elapsed from the beginning of the movement, then it follows that the body must move with any degree of speed or any greater degree of slowness, leaving a state of rest ... - is which is very difficult to imagine when our senses show that a heavily falling body immediately acquires greater speed." Galileo considers each colloquial “revealed color” and theory with the help of a witty experiment (think about which one).

Galileo also came up with the idea of ​​representing complex motion as the result of the addition of relative simple motions. The “touchstone” for testing it was the problem of the movement of a body thrown at an angle to the horizon, which (being important in military terms) attracted the attention of scientists when published. Even Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519) generally correctly represented the type of trajectory of such a body (Fig. 1).

However, even during the time of Galileo, in treatises on military affairs one could find an erroneous description of the trajectory of a projectile as a combination of two straight segments and part of a circle (Fig. 2). Galileo's proof of the theorem that “in complex motion, weakly derived from uniform horizontal and natural accelerated motion, a thrown body describes a semi-parabula” was a major scientific achievement.

It should be noted that Galileo in his reasoning never used the concept of “speed” and the corresponding expression $v=s/t$. All of his theorems are devoted to the analysis of relations between homogeneous (i.e., having the same dimension) quantities. (Remember, for example, the formulation of a statement concerning the free fall of a body.) This method of presentation is determined by a tradition dating back to the era of Antiquity. It is interesting that I. Newton (1643-1727) did not use the expression $v=s/t$ in his famous “Principles”. The first who dared to introduce speed in this way was L. Euler (1707-1783). In his essay “The Theory of the Motion of Rigid Bodies” (1765), he gave it the following definition: “With uniform motion, the ratio of the path to the time intervals during which they pass.”
