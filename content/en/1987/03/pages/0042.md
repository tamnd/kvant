The field of induced charges also exists outside the conductor, in particular, it acts on the charge +q - the charge is attracted to the ball, although the total charge of the ball is zero (Fig. 1, a). If the ball were replaced, the attraction would be stronger, since an excess negative charge would appear on the ball (Fig. 1, b). This charge would flow to the conductor from the ground under the influence of the field charge +q.*)

How to calculate the field of induced charges? Let's look at an example. Let the conductor occupy the entire right half-space (Fig. 2). Then, induced negative charges will accumulate on its flat boundary OO′, which will destroy the field in the conductor. The obvious fact that the field of induced charges is symmetrical relative to the OO′ plane will help us calculate the field outside the conductor (to the left of OO′). Look at figure 2 - it clearly shows what the field of induced charges to the right of OO′ (in the conductor) is equal to. Since this field compensates the field of the charge +q, then, obviously, it coincides with the field of an imaginary charge -q placed at the same point as the charge +q. It is now clear what kind of field is created by the induced charges to the left of OO′ (outside the conductor). It is exactly equal to the field of an imaginary charge -q, but placed on the other side of the OO′ plane, symmetrical with respect to the charge +q. The charge -q is called the image of the charge +q.

*) The field outside the conductor can be determined if either the total charge of the conductor is known (as in the case of the image on figure 1, a) or its potential (as on figure 1, b). The first layer corresponds to an insulated conductor, the second to a conductor connected to a remote, very large conductor of known potential (for example, to ground). We will mainly consider the second type of problems. The potential of an infinite conductor will be considered equal to the potential at infinity, i.e., zero.

So, the flat surface of a conductor attracts the exact charge +q, removed from it at a distance d, with the same force with which it would be attracted by a charge -q, removed at a distance 2d:

$$F = kq^2/(2d)^2.$$

We got an amazing result: the field created by the charge and the conductor (Fig. 3, a) in space in a non-prodnikka area coincides with the field of only two exact charges (Fig. 3, b). Why is such a substitution possible? Recall that the surface of a conductor is an equivalent surface, and in our example the potential of the conductor is zero. The field of two charges +q and -q has the following property: the equivalent surface φ=0 coincides with the plane of symmetry OO′, that is, it exactly repeats the shape of the surface of the conductor in question. This is where the reason for the coincidence of the fields shown in figure 3, a and b is hidden. And in other cases, one must strive to arrange the charge charges inside the conductor so that the surface of the conductor coincides with the surface of a constant potential equal to the potential of the conductor.*)
