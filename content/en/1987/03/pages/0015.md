The arsenal of scientific hydroacoustic equipment increasingly includes the so-called actic autonomous bottom stations (ADS). Such a station, equipped with sound receivers (hydrophones), specialized computers and tape recorders, can sink to the bottom of the Ocean and work there. If a research vessel, having installed the ADS, moves away from it, the station will record the noise of the ship's engines. In accordance with the laws of sound propagation in the Ocean and in the thickness of sedimentary rocks, the signal received by the ADS comes along a water beam and an additional beam refracted in the bottom of the Ocean (figure 3). In addition, there is a relatively weak background of signals scattered by the surface and surfaces of the Ocean floor, as well as the Ocean’s own noise. As the vessel moves, the “trajectories” of the water and water beams change smoothly. In this case, the relative delay in the arrival time of the noise signal along these rays also changes. Having divided the conditionally sedimentary rocks of the Ocean, bordering the water, into a layer with as yet unknown values ​​of the speed of sound, it is possible to create the same system of equations for these velocities, as was done earlier in the problem of tomography of the ocean thickness. In this case, $\Delta t_m$ will be the delays of noise signal arrivals modified by the station for various distances between the ship and the station. By solving the system of equations, we will restore the sound speed profile in sedimentary layers. Once the changes are completed, the station is called to the surface, and the experiment can be repeated in any other location.

Active methods are widely used for studying small-scale areas of the ocean floor. Thus, distortions of the sound signal scattered by the house carry information about the unevenness of the water-soil boundary and about various densities in the sedimentary layers. In the future, along this path, it is proposed to carry out Aktic visualization of the bottom.

To carry out such research and analyze the results, theoretical calculations are carried out, specialized experimental equipment and data processing methods are developed, focused on the use of the most modern computing tools. These and many other problems are dealt with by students and graduates of the Department of Hydrocosm Physics at MIPT, including on sea expeditions on research vessels.
