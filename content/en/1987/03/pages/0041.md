⟦folio 39⟧

In the case of resistance to the movement of a solid body in a liquid or gas, we are talking about the transfer of momentum (amount of motion) of the molecules of a layer, which moves in some direction at a higher speed than the neighboring layer, to the molecules of this neighboring layer.*) And vice versa, the molecules of this slow layer, having in addition to their chaotic movement also a slow direction of movement along the boundary of the layer, crush the “fast” layer, thereby slowing it down. It is generally assumed that the layer immediately adjacent to the body has the same speed as the body. Consequently, it experiences the same frictional force and, in turn, slows down or accelerates the layer located a little further away. In this case, what plays a role is how quickly the speed increases (or kills) as you move from one layer to another, or, as they say, how much the speed gradient is.

Research by various scientists has shown that at low speeds the force of friction is proportional to the relative speed of motion of a solid body and a liquid or gas. It depends on the nature of the liquid and on the shape and size of the body. Such a resistance force is experienced, for example, by particles of fog and smoke settling in the air, small solid balls falling in a viscous liquid (for example, glycerin), etc.

With any type of movement friction, another extremely important phenomenon occurs. This is the transformation of the energy of mechanical motion into internal energy (thermal motion of molecules) in accordance with the law of conservation of energy. Coulomb also studied the damping of circular oscillations of a cylinder in a liquid. In this experiment, the mechanical energy of the cylinder dissipates when oscillating, turns into heat, and the liquid along with the cylinder heats up. And if the liquid was ideal, that is, without viscosity, then the cylinder could oscillate on an elastic suspension forever.

So, strong training leads to a loss of mechanical energy, to resistance to the movement of the body in the liquid. But does this mean that the density of a liquid or gas must be reduced in all cases, and that an airplane, for example, will fly faster in this case? Not at all! An airplane in air devoid of internal friction (in an ideal “fluid”) will not take off at all, since the lifting force of its wing will be zero, as will the traction force of the propeller. The point here is the special property of the flow of an ideal fluid, devoid of vzzos. It turns out that such a flow can act on a body of any shape only with forces whose sum is equal to zero. In this state, the so-called d'Alembert-Euler paradox is essential. But discussion of this issue, unfortunately, is beyond the scope of this note.

V. V. Bukhovtsev

Power plant image method

In your school physics course, you became acquainted with the phenomenon of power plant induction. Let's remember what it is.

Let us bring the exact charge +q to some conductor, for example, to an uncharged metal ball. In a very short time, the free charges on the ball will be redistributed so that the voltage of the resulting field inside it becomes zero. After this, the movement of charges will stop. The field of the external charge turns out to be compensated by the field of charges that “appeared” on the surface of the ball. We will call them induced charges.
