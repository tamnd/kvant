Like atoms, excited nuclei are released from excess energy, emitting quanta of electromagnetic radiation. These quanta are called gamma rays (or gamma rays). A discrete set of energy states of the atomic nucleus corresponds to a discrete spectrum of frequencies of gamma quanta emitted by them. Gamma rays are transverse electromagnetic waves, the same as radio waves, visible light, or RENTEN rays. They are the shortest-wavelength type of electromagnetic radiation known, and their corresponding wavelengths range from approximately 10⁻¹¹ m to 10⁻¹³ m.

The energy states of atomic nuclei and transitions of nuclei from one state to another with the absorption or release of energy are usually described using energy diagrams similar to the energy diagrams of atoms (“Physics 10”, § 94). figure shows the energy diagram of the nucleus of the iron isotope - 58/26 Fe, obtained on the basis of proton bombardment experiments. Note that while the energy diagrams of atoms and nuclei are qualitatively similar, there are significant quantitative differences between them. If energy of several electron volts is required to transfer an atom from the ground state to the excited state, then energy of the order of hundreds of thousands or millions of electron volts is required to excite the atomic nucleus. This difference is due to the fact that the nuclear forces acting between nucleons in the nucleus largely exceed the forces of the Coulomb interaction of electrons with the nucleus.

The ability of atomic nuclei to spontaneously transition from a state with a large supply of energy to a state with less energy explains the origin of not only gamma radiation, but also the radioactive decay of nuclei.
