However, as the depth increased (the maximum depth in the Sea of ​​Japan is 3700 m), the hydrostatic pressure increased significantly. This led to an increase in the speed of sound. As a result, a peculiar dependence of the speed of sound on depth was formed. An example of such a dependence (sound speed profile) is shown in figure 1. The sound speed minimum is located at a depth of 300 m. Above and below this level, the speed increases. What does this sound speed profile lead to?

figure 2 shows sound rays leaving the emitter (I), placed at a depth of 100 m, and falling into the receiver (R), which is located at a depth of 300 m at a distance of 184 km from the emitter. Due to “refraction” the rays are bent, and again and again they return to the horizontal axis corresponding to the minimum speed of sound. In this case, a whole family of rays (some of them are shown in figure 2) do not reach the bottom, where sound signals can be absorbed, and do not reach the surface, on the unevenness of which they can be scattered. As a result, the sound arrives at the receiver through pure water rays, or, as they say, through an underwater sound channel -

PZK - almost without attenuation. This allows the recording of sound signals many thousands of kilometers from their source.*)

The presence of the PZK explains the phenomenon of “ultra-long-range” sound propagation, observed in 1946 in the Sea of ​​Japan. It turns out that the PZK is available in any sea and any ocean, provided it reaches their depth.

The emitted sound signal, propagating along various beams, arrives at the receiving point at different times. Any, even small changes in the sound speed profile lead to a shift in the moment of arrival of the sound signal along a separate beam. The magnitudes of these mixings, or fluctuations, are different for each of the rays. The entire sequence of pleasant signals is completely determined by the properties of the environment and is an extremely sensitive indicator of these properties.
