Let us now prove that the stereographic projection of any circle $\omega$ on the sphere will also be a circle if $\omega$ does not contain the center of the projection $P$ (if $\omega$ contains the point $P$, then the projection $\omega$, as is easy to see, will be a straight line). Let $Y_0$ and $Y$ be the projections of arbitrary points $X_0$ and $X$ of the circle $\omega$. The plane $PX_0X$ intersects the sphere along a certain circle, and the plane $\alpha$ intersects along the straight line $Y_0Y$, which is parallel to the tangent MP to this circle at point $P$ (Fig. 2, 3). It's clear that

$$\angle PY_0Y = \angle MPY_0 = \frac{1}{2} \cup PX_0 = \angle PXX_0.$$

Let us now draw another, auxiliary sphere containing the circle $\omega$ and the point $Y_0$; let the straight line $PX$ intersect it at the point $Y'$. Considering the section of this sphere by the plane $PX_0X$ (Fig. 4), we are convinced that

$$\angle PY_0Y' = 180^\circ - \angle X_0XY' = \angle PXX_0,$$

i.e. $\angle PY_0Y' = \angle PY_0Y$, therefore $Y = Y'$. Thus, the projections of all points of the circle $\omega$ lie simultaneously in the plane $\alpha$ and on our auxiliary sphere, i.e. they form a circle.

If the point $P$ is taken on a straight line containing the center of the dopekadra with the center of its face, then the stereographic projection gives the configuration shown in figure 5. The strict dopekadr is essential to this configuration and can be easily verified without going into space (we present this to the reader). And although by itself it does not eliminate us (one of its circles contains the rest), with inversion with a center, say, at point $O$ (see Fig. 5) it will produce what we need. The reader who is not familiar with the inversion transformation and its properties can refer to the article by V. M. Uroev “Inversion” (Kvant, 1984, No. 5, p. 26); It also talks about stereographic projection.

6) Let us assume that the desired arrangement of circles exists, and not all of them are equal to each other. Let's consider all the circles of the smallest radius (denote it by $r$) and select from them a circle that touches at least one circle of another radius $r_1$ ($r_1 > r$). Let $O$ and $O_1$ be the centers of these circles, $O_2$, ..., $O_6$ the centers of the remaining five circles touching the circle $O$ (the centers are lined clockwise, Fig. 6). In triangle $OO_1O_2$, sides $O_1O_2$, $O_2O$ and $OO_1$ are equal to $r_1 + r_2$, $r_2 + r$ and $r + r_1$ respectively, where $r_2$ is the radius of circle $O_2$ ($r_2 \ge r$), i.e. $O_1O_2$ is the largest side, which means $\angle O_1OO_2 > 60^\circ$ (the inequality is strict, since $O_1O_2 > OO_1$). Similarly, it is proved that the angles $O_2OO_3$, $O_3OO_4$, ..., $O_6OO_1$ are not less than $60^\circ$. But then the sum of all six angles $O_1OO_2$, ..., $O_6OO_1$ turns out to be greater than $360^\circ$, which, of course, is impossible.

So, all circles must have the same radius $r$. In this case, each circle must be the circle of the others, like the circle $O$ on figure 6, which is only possible for an infinite number of circles. (If $O_1$ and $O_2$ are the centers of two neighboring circles, then all points of the straight line $O_1O_2$ located from $O_1$ at a distance $O_1O_2$, $2O_1O_2$, $3O_1O_2$, ..., must be the centers of the circles of our configuration.)
