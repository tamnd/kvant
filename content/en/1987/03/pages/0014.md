Another area of ​​application of acoustic tomography is the study of the ocean floor. This is not easy when you consider that the researcher is usually separated from the subject of study by a column of water several kilometers high. Currently available seismic freezing methods

$$\Delta t_m = \sum_{n=1}^{N} E_{mn} \Delta c_n.$$

By going through all possible rays, we obtain a system of $M$ equations for $\Delta c_n$ ($n=1, 2, ..., N$). The values ​​of the coefficients $E_{mn}$ are calculated if the trajectory of the $m$ reference ray and the geometric shape of the $n$ cell are known. In cells not “affected” by the $m$th ray, $E_{mn} = 0$.

By measuring fluctuations in the arrival times of sound signals $\Delta t_m$ and solving the system of linear equations described above, we obtain the values ​​$\Delta c_n$ in each cell. This is exactly what is done in practice, although along the way one has to overcome many technical and mathematical difficulties.

If the distribution of the speed of sound $c_n(x, y, z, t)$ was able to be restored, then, using the correspondences known in the physics of sea water, it is possible to approximately restore the temperature, salinity and density of water. Knowing the temperature over a large area allows us to determine the heat content of the Ocean, which is important for weather forecasting. But this is not the only information obtained by acoustic tomography. From the difference in the time of signal distribution back and forth between two corresponding points, one can estimate the average projection of the speed of ocean currents along the ray onto the direction of the sound path. This is associated with hope for a change in large-scale ocean circulation, which is inaccessible to traditional methods. There are projects covering vast areas of the World Ocean with a tomographic network, which allows for a new approach to the problems of interaction between the Ocean and the atmosphere and the formation of the Earth’s climate.
