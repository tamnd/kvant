Studying the powerful trade wind current in the Atlantic, which was considered exclusively constant, scientists discovered that in the current zone there are giant water vortices hundreds of kilometers in size, similar to the atmospheric cycle and anticycle (further research showed that such vortex movements cover the practical scales of the Ocean). The center of the vortex moves at a speed of up to 300 m/h, but the water particles, participating in the rotational movement of the vortex, move 10 times faster.

The passage of an intense vortex sometimes leads to such changes in the environment that can completely disrupt acoustic communication and, for example, can, like mirages in empty spaces, make previously inaudible things audible.

Several years ago, the idea arose to take advantage of the high sensitivity of sound signals to changes in the aquatic environment to organize long-term, about a year, observations of large-scale ocean variability over an area of ​​up to 10⁶ km². To do this, it is necessary to place systems of moored acoustic emitters and receivers along the perimeter of the water area at different depths and change the fluctuations in the arrival time of signals along all possible “purely water” beams. These signals, as we have already said, carry information about all the low-cost opportunities encountered along the way. In this way, it is possible to “examine” the entire thickness of the Ocean, making it like the “last sim” of the water area. No fleet of scientific vessels is able to practically instantly make changes in a column of volume. However, such efficiency comes at the price of complexity in processing experimental data. Based on the time sequence of pleasant signals, it is necessary to reconstruct the sound speed field - find the values ​​of the speed of sound in different places in the water area; analysis of these results informs the outputs. The described method is called acoustic tomography of the Ocean (from the Greek totsog - layer, aches).
