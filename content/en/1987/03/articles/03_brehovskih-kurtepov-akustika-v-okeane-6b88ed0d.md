ACOUSTICS IN THE OCEAN

Academician L. M. BREKHOVSKIKH,
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences V. M. KURTEPOV

A source of food resources, a class of minerals, the “kitchen” of the weather, the main road of the Earth... These words can generally characterize the role of the World Ocean in our lives. And every year it becomes clearer and clearer how important this role is, how enormous the influence of the Ocean is on all spheres of human activity, how important research work in the Ocean and the development of technical means for the development of its resources are.

Man is keenly interested in knowing as much as possible about the state of the Ocean, the movement of its waters, and the structure of the ocean day. This is necessary for surface and underwater navigation, to determine the area with the most biological productivity, to take into account the influence of the Ocean on the weather and climate of our planet, and for many other purposes.

Numerous research ships from different countries are constantly in the Ocean and in the seas attached to the continents. They record the state of water masses at different depths, the state of the atmosphere over the Ocean, and study the characteristics of the day. The results of the research flow into World Data Centers, the main of which are located in Moscow and Washington. This information is very valuable. However, in terms of its volume, it is too small to fully describe the current state of the entire World Ocean and the seas adjacent to it. For this purpose, the flow of information must be increased thousands of times. This is where the means of exploring the Ocean from space come to the rescue. Instruments installed on artificial Earth satellites and space stations quickly and immediately “collect” data on many characteristics of the Ocean from a large area. Measuring your own study of the Ocean in the information or centimeter range allows you to record its surface. By scattering electromagnetic waves in the centimeter range on the surface of the Ocean, one can determine the characteristic

Sticks of surface waves, surface current speed and surface wind. Based on the travel time of the electromagnetic pulse from the space station to the surface of the Ocean and back, it is possible to determine changes in the Ocean level over large distances. This is how a “trough” was first discovered on the surface of the Ocean, about 23 meters deep above the Puerto Rican platinum on the ocean floor.

However, all this data obtained from space refers to the surface of the Ocean. And it’s clear why - electromagnetic waves cannot penetrate deep into sea water to any significant extent. In the water column, the light fades at a distance of several tens of meters; Even for the most powerful laser beam, the limit is several hundred meters.

Aktika provides a tool for “enlightening” the depths of the Ocean. Only sound waves can travel a considerable distance in water. Thus, the sound of a relatively small research explosion in marine experiments was recorded at a distance of 22,000 kilometers.

Here we need to turn to discoveries that were discovered forty years ago. In 1946, Soviet scientists discovered a very interesting phenomenon in the Sea of ​​Japan. The sounds of explosions (anti-submarine bombs were detonated at a depth of 100 meters) spread without noticeable attenuation over very long distances - many hundreds of kilometers. Detailed processing and analysis of experimental materials showed that this occurs due to the free dependence of the speed of sound in the Ocean on depth. The speed of sound in sea water, generally speaking, changes with changes in temperature, salinity and hydrostatic pressure. During work in the Sea of ​​Japan, salinity changes slightly with depth, and its influence does not affect. When moving from the surface to a depth of approximately 300 meters, the speed of sound

However, as the depth increased (the maximum depth in the Sea of ​​Japan is 3700 m), the hydrostatic pressure increased significantly. This led to an increase in the speed of sound. As a result, a peculiar dependence of the speed of sound on depth was formed. An example of such a dependence (sound speed profile) is shown in figure 1. figure 2 shows sound rays leaving the emitter (I), placed at a depth of 100 m, and falling into the receiver (R), which is located at a depth of 300 m at a distance of 184 km from the emitter. Due to “refraction” the rays are bent, and again and again they return to the horizontal axis corresponding to the minimum speed of sound. In this case, a whole family of rays (some of them are shown in figure 2) do not reach the bottom, where sound signals can be absorbed, and do not reach the surface, on the unevenness of which they can be scattered. As a result, the sound arrives at the receiver through pure water rays, or, as they say, through an underwater sound channel -

PZK - almost without attenuation. This allows the recording of sound signals many thousands of kilometers from their source.*)

The presence of the PZK explains the phenomenon of “ultra-long-range” sound propagation, observed in 1946 in the Sea of ​​Japan. It turns out that the PZK is available in any sea and any ocean, provided it reaches their depth.

The emitted sound signal, propagating along various beams, arrives at the receiving point at different times. Any, even small changes in the sound speed profile lead to a shift in the moment of arrival of the sound signal along a separate beam. The magnitudes of these mixings, or fluctuations, are different for each of the rays. The entire sequence of pleasant signals is completely determined by the properties of the environment and is an extremely sensitive indicator of these properties.

Studying the powerful trade wind current in the Atlantic, which was considered exclusively constant, scientists discovered that in the current zone there are giant water vortices hundreds of kilometers in size, similar to the atmospheric cycle and anticycle (further research showed that such vortex movements cover the practical scales of the Ocean). The passage of an intense vortex sometimes leads to such changes in the environment that can completely disrupt acoustic communication and, for example, can, like mirages in empty spaces, make previously inaudible things audible.

Several years ago, the idea arose to take advantage of the high sensitivity of sound signals to changes in the aquatic environment to organize long-term, about a year, observations of large-scale ocean variability over an area of ​​up to 10⁶ km². To do this, it is necessary to place systems of moored acoustic emitters and receivers along the perimeter of the water area at different depths and change the fluctuations in the arrival time of signals along all possible “purely water” beams. These signals, as we have already said, carry information about all the low-cost opportunities encountered along the way. In this way, it is possible to “examine” the entire thickness of the Ocean, making it like the “last sim” of the water area. No fleet of scientific vessels is able to practically instantly make changes in a column of volume. However, such efficiency comes at the price of complexity in processing experimental data. Based on the time sequence of pleasant signals, it is necessary to reconstruct the sound speed field - find the values ​​of the speed of sound in different places in the water area; analysis of these results informs the outputs. The described method is called acoustic tomography of the Ocean (from the Greek totsog - layer, aches).

Another area of ​​application of acoustic tomography is the study of the ocean floor. This is not easy when you consider that the researcher is usually separated from the subject of study by a column of water several kilometers high. Currently available seismic freezing methods

$$\Delta t_m = \sum_{n=1}^{N} E_{mn} \Delta c_n.$$

By going through all possible rays, we obtain a system of $M$ equations for $\Delta c_n$ ($n=1, 2, ..., N$). The values ​​of the coefficients $E_{mn}$ are calculated if the trajectory of the $m$ reference ray and the geometric shape of the $n$ cell are known. In cells not “affected” by the $m$th ray, $E_{mn} = 0$.

By measuring fluctuations in the arrival times of sound signals $\Delta t_m$ and solving the system of linear equations described above, we obtain the values ​​$\Delta c_n$ in each cell. This is exactly what is done in practice, although along the way one has to overcome many technical and mathematical difficulties.

If the distribution of the speed of sound $c_n(x, y, z, t)$ was able to be restored, then, using the correspondences known in the physics of sea water, it is possible to approximately restore the temperature, salinity and density of water. Knowing the temperature over a large area allows us to determine the heat content of the Ocean, which is important for weather forecasting. But this is not the only information obtained by acoustic tomography. From the difference in the time of signal distribution back and forth between two corresponding points, one can estimate the average projection of the speed of ocean currents along the ray onto the direction of the sound path. This is associated with hope for a change in large-scale ocean circulation, which is inaccessible to traditional methods. There are projects covering vast areas of the World Ocean with a tomographic network, which allows for a new approach to the problems of interaction between the Ocean and the atmosphere and the formation of the Earth’s climate.

The arsenal of scientific hydroacoustic equipment increasingly includes the so-called actic autonomous bottom stations (ADS). Such a station, equipped with sound receivers (hydrophones), specialized computers and tape recorders, can sink to the bottom of the Ocean and work there. If a research vessel, having installed the ADS, moves away from it, the station will record the noise of the ship's engines. In accordance with the laws of sound propagation in the Ocean and in the thickness of sedimentary rocks, the signal received by the ADS comes along a water beam and an additional beam refracted in the bottom of the Ocean (figure 3). In addition, there is a relatively weak background of signals scattered by the surface and surfaces of the Ocean floor, as well as the Ocean’s own noise. As the vessel moves, the “trajectories” of the water and water beams change smoothly. In this case, the relative delay in the arrival time of the noise signal along these rays also changes. Having divided the conditionally sedimentary rocks of the Ocean, bordering the water, into a layer with as yet unknown values ​​of the speed of sound, it is possible to create the same system of equations for these velocities, as was done earlier in the problem of tomography of the ocean thickness. In this case, $\Delta t_m$ will be the delays of noise signal arrivals modified by the station for various distances between the ship and the station. By solving the system of equations, we will restore the sound speed profile in sedimentary layers. Once the changes are completed, the station is called to the surface, and the experiment can be repeated in any other location.

Active methods are widely used for studying small-scale areas of the ocean floor. Thus, distortions of the sound signal scattered by the house carry information about the unevenness of the water-soil boundary and about various densities in the sedimentary layers. In the future, along this path, it is proposed to carry out Aktic visualization of the bottom.

To carry out such research and analyze the results, theoretical calculations are carried out, specialized experimental equipment and data processing methods are developed, focused on the use of the most modern computing tools. These and many other problems are dealt with by students and graduates of the Department of Hydrocosm Physics at MIPT, including on sea expeditions on research vessels.
