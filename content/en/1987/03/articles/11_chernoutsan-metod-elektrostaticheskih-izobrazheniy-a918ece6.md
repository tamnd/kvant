In the case of resistance to the movement of a solid body in a liquid or gas, we are talking about the transfer of momentum (amount of motion) of the molecules of a layer, which moves in some direction at a higher speed than the neighboring layer, to the molecules of this neighboring layer.*) And vice versa, the molecules of this slow layer, having in addition to their chaotic movement also a slow direction of movement along the boundary of the layer, crush the “fast” layer, thereby slowing it down. It is generally assumed that the layer immediately adjacent to the body has the same speed as the body. Consequently, it experiences the same frictional force and, in turn, slows down or accelerates the layer located a little further away. In this case, what plays a role is how quickly the speed increases (or kills) as you move from one layer to another, or, as they say, how much the speed gradient is.

Research by various scientists has shown that at low speeds the force of friction is proportional to the relative speed of motion of a solid body and a liquid or gas. It depends on the nature of the liquid and on the shape and size of the body. Such a resistance force is experienced, for example, by particles of fog and smoke settling in the air, small solid balls falling in a viscous liquid (for example, glycerin), etc.

With any type of movement friction, another extremely important phenomenon occurs. This is the transformation of the energy of mechanical motion into internal energy (thermal motion of molecules) in accordance with the law of conservation of energy. Coulomb also studied the damping of circular oscillations of a cylinder in a liquid. In this experiment, the mechanical energy of the cylinder dissipates when oscillating, turns into heat, and the liquid along with the cylinder heats up. And if the liquid was ideal, that is, without viscosity, then the cylinder could oscillate on an elastic suspension forever.

So, strong training leads to a loss of mechanical energy, to resistance to the movement of the body in the liquid. But does this mean that the density of a liquid or gas must be reduced in all cases, and that an airplane, for example, will fly faster in this case? Not at all! An airplane in air devoid of internal friction (in an ideal “fluid”) will not take off at all, since the lifting force of its wing will be zero, as will the traction force of the propeller. The point here is the special property of the flow of an ideal fluid, devoid of vzzos. It turns out that such a flow can act on a body of any shape only with forces whose sum is equal to zero. In this state, the so-called d'Alembert-Euler paradox is essential. But discussion of this issue, unfortunately, is beyond the scope of this note.

V. V. Bukhovtsev

Power plant image method

In your school physics course, you became acquainted with the phenomenon of power plant induction. Let's remember what it is.

Let us bring the exact charge +q to some conductor, for example, to an uncharged metal ball. In a very short time, the free charges on the ball will be redistributed so that the voltage of the resulting field inside it becomes zero. After this, the movement of charges will stop. The field of the external charge turns out to be compensated by the field of charges that “appeared” on the surface of the ball. We will call them induced charges.

The field of induced charges also exists outside the conductor, in particular, it acts on the charge +q - the charge is attracted to the ball, although the total charge of the ball is zero (Fig. 1, a). If the ball were replaced, the attraction would be stronger, since an excess negative charge would appear on the ball (Fig. 1, b). This charge would flow to the conductor from the ground under the influence of the field charge +q.*)

How to calculate the field of induced charges? Let's look at an example. Let the conductor occupy the entire right half-space (Fig. 2). Then, induced negative charges will accumulate on its flat boundary OO′, which will destroy the field in the conductor. The obvious fact that the field of induced charges is symmetrical relative to the OO′ plane will help us calculate the field outside the conductor (to the left of OO′). Look at figure 2 - it clearly shows what the field of induced charges to the right of OO′ (in the conductor) is equal to. Since this field compensates the field of the charge +q, then, obviously, it coincides with the field of an imaginary charge -q placed at the same point as the charge +q. It is now clear what kind of field is created by the induced charges to the left of OO′ (outside the conductor). It is exactly equal to the field of an imaginary charge -q, but placed on the other side of the OO′ plane, symmetrical with respect to the charge +q. The charge -q is called the image of the charge +q.

*) The field outside the conductor can be determined if either the total charge of the conductor is known (as in the case of the image on figure 1, a) or its potential (as on figure 1, b). The first layer corresponds to an insulated conductor, the second to a conductor connected to a remote, very large conductor of known potential (for example, to ground). We will mainly consider the second type of problems. The potential of an infinite conductor will be considered equal to the potential at infinity, i.e., zero.

So, the flat surface of a conductor attracts the exact charge +q, removed from it at a distance d, with the same force with which it would be attracted by a charge -q, removed at a distance 2d:

$$F = kq^2/(2d)^2.$$

We got an amazing result: the field created by the charge and the conductor (Fig. 3, a) in space in a non-prodnikka area coincides with the field of only two exact charges (Fig. 3, b). Why is such a substitution possible? Recall that the surface of a conductor is an equivalent surface, and in our example the potential of the conductor is zero. The field of two charges +q and -q has the following property: the equivalent surface φ=0 coincides with the plane of symmetry OO′, that is, it exactly repeats the shape of the surface of the conductor in question. This is where the reason for the coincidence of the fields shown in figure 3, a and b is hidden. And in other cases, one must strive to arrange the charge charges inside the conductor so that the surface of the conductor coincides with the surface of a constant potential equal to the potential of the conductor.*)

There is no cut-off, and usually you have to act, as they say, “from the end” - from the charges to the conductor.

Let's take several exact charges, calculate their field, find any equipotential surface $\varphi = \varphi_0$ and fill the space inside this surface with a conductor with potential $\varphi_0$. Then the field that we have already calculated appears to be a ready-made solution for obtaining the current conductor and those charges that end up in it! The charges that “died” inside the conductor play the role of image charges. In this way, you can build many “ready-made” solutions, but there is no guarantee that among them there will be a conductor of a predetermined shape.

Let's look at an example. Let's take some equipotential surface for the same charges $+q$ and $-q$, for example with $\varphi = -1$ B (Fig. 4, a). The field of these two charges outside this surface coincides with the field of the charge $+q$ and the conductor having a fixed potential $\varphi = -1$ V (Fig. 4, b).

Another example. The field of four charges $+q$, $+q$, $-q$ and $-q$, located at the vertices of a rectangle (Fig. 5, a), has an equipotential surface $\varphi = 0$ in the form of two mutually perpendicular planes. This means that part of this field contained in the first square coincides with the field of the charge $+q$ placed in the dihedral angle (Fig. 5, b). For other charges, it is an image of the charge $+q$. Try to find your own solution for a charge placed in a triangular corner - for this you will have to use seven additional charges.

Let us now return to the example with the metal ball, which was discussed at the beginning of the article. Let's take charges $+q_1$ and $-q_2$ ($q_1 > q_2$), located at a distance of $l$ from each other. It turns out that the equipotential surface $\varphi = 0$ is represented by

sphere (Fig. 6, a).*) To determine the radius of this sphere $R$ and the distance $L$ from its center to the charge $+q_1$, we can equate to zero the potentials of points $A$ and $B$ (Fig. 6, b):

$$k \frac{q_1}{L-R} - k \frac{q_2}{R-(L-l)} = 0,$$

(*)

$k \frac{q_1}{L+R} - k \frac{q_2}{R+(L-l)} = 0.$

The field of these two charges in space outside the sphere coincides exactly with the field that arises if the charge $+q_1$ is placed at a distance $L$ from the center of a grounded metal ball with radius $R$ (Fig. 6, b). In this case, when the position of the ball is given, we know $R$ and $L$, and the position of the imprinted charge-image ($l$) and its magnitude ($q_2$) can be found from the same system (*):

$$l = L - R^2/L, \quad q_2 = q_1R/L.$$

The force with which the charge $+q_1$ is applied to the ball is equal to

$$F = k \frac{q_1q_2}{l^2}.$$
