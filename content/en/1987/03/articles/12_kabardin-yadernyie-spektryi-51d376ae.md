Nuclear spectra

As you know, atomic nuclei consist of nucleons - protons and neutrons, between which nuclear attractive forces and Coulomb repulsive forces act. What can happen to a nucleus when it collides with another nucleus, parts or gamma quantum? Experiments by E. Rutherford, carried out in 1919, showed, for example, that under the influence of an alpha particle a proton can be knocked out of the nucleus. In experiments conducted by D. Chadwick in 1932, it was found that alpha particles can knock out neutrons from atomic nuclei (“Physics 10”, § 106). But does the birth process always end this way? Couldn't an atomic nucleus absorb the energy received during a collision and redistribute it between its constituent nucleons, thereby changing its internal energy? What will happen next with such a core?

The answers to these questions are based on experiments to study the interaction of protons with atomic nuclei. Their results are very similar to the results of experiments by Frank and Hertz on the study of so-new electrons with atoms (“Physics 10”, § 96). It turns out that with a gradual increase in the energy of protons, first used with atomic nuclei, the kinetic energy is not converted into other types of energy, but is only redistributed between the proton and the atomic nucleus as one particle. However, starting from a certain value of the proton energy, inelastic collisions can also occur, in which the proton is absorbed by the nucleus and completely transfers its energy to it. The nucleus of each isotope is characterized by a strictly defined set of “portions” of energy that it can accept.

These experiments prove that nuclei have discrete spectra of possible energy states. Thus, quantization of energy and a number of other parameters is a property not only of atoms, but also of atomic nuclei. The state of the atomic nucleus with a minimum amount of energy is called the ground, or normal state; the state with a higher energy point (compared to the ground state) is called excited.

Like atoms, excited nuclei are released from excess energy, emitting quanta of electromagnetic radiation. These quanta are called gamma rays (or gamma rays). A discrete set of energy states of the atomic nucleus corresponds to a discrete spectrum of frequencies of gamma quanta emitted by them. Gamma rays are transverse electromagnetic waves, the same as radio waves, visible light, or RENTEN rays. They are the shortest-wavelength type of electromagnetic radiation known, and their corresponding wavelengths range from approximately 10⁻¹¹ m to 10⁻¹³ m.

The energy states of atomic nuclei and transitions of nuclei from one state to another with the absorption or release of energy are usually described using energy diagrams similar to the energy diagrams of atoms (“Physics 10”, § 94). figure shows the energy diagram of the nucleus of the iron isotope - 58/26 Fe, obtained on the basis of proton bombardment experiments. Note that while the energy diagrams of atoms and nuclei are qualitatively similar, there are significant quantitative differences between them. If energy of several electron volts is required to transfer an atom from the ground state to the excited state, then energy of the order of hundreds of thousands or millions of electron volts is required to excite the atomic nucleus. This difference is due to the fact that the nuclear forces acting between nucleons in the nucleus largely exceed the forces of the Coulomb interaction of electrons with the nucleus.

The ability of atomic nuclei to spontaneously transition from a state with a large supply of energy to a state with less energy explains the origin of not only gamma radiation, but also the radioactive decay of nuclei.
