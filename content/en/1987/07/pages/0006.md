Curves of the third degree
Staying true to the geometric approach discussed above, we will focus our attention not on solving the third-degree equation, but on the following equivalent question: what are the rational points of the curve in the plane given by the third-degree equation

$$f(x, y) = ax^3 + bx^2y + \dots + hx + iy + j = 0$$
with integer coefficients?

All such curves can be divided into two large classes. The first class includes curves that have sharp points (like the point (0; 0) of the curve $y^2 = x^3$), self-intersections (Fig. 3), as well as curves for which $f(x, y)$ is represented as $f(x, y) = f_1(x, y) \cdot f_2(x, y)$,

where $f_1(x, y), f_2(x, y)$ are polynomials of lower degrees (Fig. 4). Let us say that such curves are degenerate. The second class is formed by non-degenerate curves of the third degree with integer coefficients - such curves are called elliptic. It is this (most general) class that will interest us. We will consider elliptic curves given in the so-called canonical form, i.e. the equations

$$y^2 = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c$$
(6)

with integer coefficients $a, b$ and $c$, in which the polynomial $P(x) = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c$ has no multiple roots.

This does not violate generality: any non-singular curve $f(x, y) = 0$ of the third degree can be reduced to the form (6) by some transformation; Moreover, if the coefficients $f(x, y)$ were integers, the problem of finding rational points on the curve $f(x, y) = 0$ can be reduced to a similar problem for curve (6) with integers $a, b$ and $c$.
