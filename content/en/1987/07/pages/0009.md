⟦folio 7⟧

The given equation is $y^2 = x^3 - 2$ and $P = (3; 5)$. Then $2P = \left(\frac{129}{100}; -\frac{383}{1000}\right)$ is a new rational point. Now we can calculate $3P, 4P$, etc. Note that the volume of calculations grows rapidly with each step. If we denote by $u_n$ the first coordinate of the point $nP$, then

$$u_1 = 3, \quad u_2 = \frac{129}{100}, \quad u_3 = \frac{164323}{29241},$$

$$u_4 = \frac{2340922881}{58675600}, \quad u_5 = \frac{307326105747363}{160280942564521}.$$

Further even faster. For example, $u_{11}$ has 71 characters.

There is currently no known lower general procedure for finding all rational solutions to the equation $y = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c$. In the analyzed example $y^2 = x^3 - 2$ we simply selected one solution (3; 5); in the general case, there is no known method that would allow one to find this first solution. Finding a rational solution to an elliptic equation using an efficient procedure is one of the biggest problems in number theory. However, if there is one solution, you can find others using formulas (8) and (9).

Point order
on an elliptic curve
When obtaining points $nP$ from a given point $P$, two cases are possible. In the first case, at step $n$ we get zero, i.e. there is a number $n$ such that $nP = E$. If for all $m < n$ $mP \neq E$, then the point $P$ is said to have finite order $n$. For example, on the curve $y^2 = x^3 + 4$ the point $P = (0; 2)$ has order 3, on the curve $y^2 = x^3 + 1$ the point $P = (2; 3)$ has order 6, on the curve $y^2 = x^3 - 43x + 166$ the point $P = (3; 8)$ has order 7. We can pose the question: how many rational points of finite order are there and what are these orders?

In 1976, the American mathematician B. Mazur obtained an outstanding result by showing that if $P$ is a rational point of order $n$, then $n \leqslant 10$ or $n = 12$; on the other hand, there are at most 16 rational points of finite order on an elliptic curve.

The second case is when all points $2P, 3P, 4P$ etc. are different. In 1901, the French mathematician A. Poincaré (1854-1912) hypothesized that it is always possible to find such a finite number of rational points $P_1, \dots, P_r$ that every rational point $P$ is expressed through them, i.e., represented in the form

$$P = n_1P_1 + \dots + n_rP_r + Q,$$

where $n_1, \dots, n_r$ are integers uniquely determined by the point $P$, and $Q$ is a point of finite order. The points themselves $P_1, \dots, P_r$ are not expressed through each other. The number $r$ is called the ranzem of the curve.

The Poincaré conjecture was proved by the English L. Mordell in 1922, but his proof does not provide any way to calculate the rank. It is still unclear whether there are elliptic curves of any higher rank. It is only known that the rank is estimated through the coefficients $a, b, c$ of the equation $y^2 = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c$, therefore curves of higher rank should have larger coefficients. For example, among the curves of rank $r \geqslant 8$ is the recently found curve for which

$$a = -3^2 \cdot 1487 \cdot 1873,$$

$$b = 2^5 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 151 \cdot 14551 \cdot 33353,$$

$$c = 2^8 \cdot 3^4 \cdot 5^2 \cdot 7 \cdot 151^2 \cdot 193 \cdot 273 \cdot 156307.$$

Conclusion: curves of arbitrary degrees
Here we limited ourselves to curves (and therefore diffant equations) of degrees 2 and 3. But what about the degrees $n \geqslant 4$? In this case, it is also natural to distinguish a class of “unexpressed” curves of degree $n$ (a typical representative is the curve $x^n + y^n = 1$). At $n > 3$ the picture changes dramatically. Back in 1931, the English mathematician L. Mordell put forward a hypothesis - on such curves the number of rational points is always finite. Mordell's conjecture is more useful in the spotlight of leading mathematicians around the world. A significant contribution to its solution was made by Soviet mathematicians I. P. Shafarevich, Yu. I. Manin, S. Yu. Arakelov, A. N. Parshin, Yu. G. Zarkhin, and the honor of solving it in 1983 fell to a young scientist from Germany, Gerd Faltigs. For this, in 1986, at the Congress of Mathematicians in Berkeley (USA), he was awarded the Fields Medal, the highest award for mathematicians.
