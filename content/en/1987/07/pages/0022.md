UNITS:
FROM SYSTEM TO SYSTEM

S. I. VALYANSKY

On December 15, 1832, the work of the world famous mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss was presented to the Göttingen Scientific Society for discussion. It was called “The Intensification of the Earth's Magnetic Force Reduced to Absolute Measure” and was short, only 37 pages. However, this modest work played a huge role in the development of natural science. To feel this role, we will have to turn to history, remember some moments of the “biography” of the magnet.

The ancients learned about the existence of a “stone” that accepts iron, and attempts to explain the accepting power of this stone were made more than 2000 years ago. The first practical use of a magnet was the compass. There is no clear opinion regarding the place and time of invention of the compass. But at least since the 13th century, the compass has been known in Europe. With its appearance, discoveries began related to the nature of terrestrial magnetism. It was immediately discovered that the magnetic needle does not indicate a strict direction towards light; that the deviation of the arrow from this direction (magnetic declination) varies depending on the location of observation; that the arrow is not installed horizontally, and the angle it makes with the vertical also depends on the observation location.

What explains the orientation of magnetic needles? What are magnetic forces? The first to try to answer these questions was the English physicist William Gilbert (1544-1603). Being a keen experimenter, Hilbert laid the foundations on magnetism and, in particular, on terrestrial magnetism. He suggested that the Earth is a large magnet, and in this sense it is like a ball of magnetic material. To confirm this hypothesis, the scientist made a small model of the Earth - an iron ball - and showed that when moving along the surface of such a ball, the magnetic needle is oriented exactly as it is in reality.

The problem of navigation posed a new task for the natural scientist: it became clear that in order to successfully use a compass (the compass was one of the main navigational tools) it was necessary to have a map of magnetic declinations.
