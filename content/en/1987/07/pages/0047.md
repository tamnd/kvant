It is curious, but for this it is enough to prove that any polygon can be cut into parts, from which squares of equal size (and, therefore, identical) can be added. Let us draw on one such square the boundaries of the pieces of the first polygon laid on it, and then the boundaries of the pieces of the second polygon (for example, as on figure 2). By cutting the square along all the resulting lines, it will be possible to form both the first and the second polygon from the resulting pieces.
