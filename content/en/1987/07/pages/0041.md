M1034. A rectangular piece of chocolate is divided into 50 square sections by longitudinal and transverse grooves. Two from the height of such a zett. The beginner spreads the chocolate along a certain depression into two rectangular parts. Then, in turn, cut one of the resulting parts into two along a certain depression. The one who breaks off a square slice (without a hole) with a feather, a) breaks through, b) wins. Which of the players can secure winnings for themselves - the beginner or his partner?

Answer: In both versions of the game, the beginner wins. This is also true for any chocolate of mn pieces (size m×n), where mn is even (except for the case of 2×n chocolate with odd n in option 6 - here the answer depends on n.) We will immediately consider the general case. Interestingly, the winning strategies in the “opposite” options a) and 6) are almost the same.

a) The strategy that ensures a winner for the beginner is as follows. At least one of the numbers m and n is even - let it be m(m=2k). With the first move, the beginner breaks the chocolate into two equal halves (n×k pieces each). Then every second move he plays on the other half of the chocolate bar. Thus, after each turn of the first game, both halves will be broken in exactly the same way. It is clear that in this case the first one will not break off a 1 × 1 slice equal to that of the second one.

6) Here, with even m>2 and n>1, the beginner can use the same “symmetrical” strategy until the second one breaks off a strip with 1; the first one immediately breaks off a 1x1 piece of it and wins it out.

When publishing the problem, due to a misunderstanding, we did not place next to the conditions a picture depicting chocolate made from 5 × 10 slices; Therefore, strictly speaking, we also need to consider the case of a 2x25 chocolate bar (although, apparently, there were no such things in real life). In variant 6) of the game, the “symmetrical” strategy for a 2×n chocolate bar is not suitable - it is impossible to distinguish a strip with a stripe of 1 (this immediately leads to a hole), so the chocolate can only be broken “across”. A game of “partitioning” arises. The number n is written on the board at the beginning; then, with each move, one of the numbers is erased and the neoctors write two natural numbers, the sum of which is erased. The one who is forced to write 1 loses (that is, the opening position is a set of numbers 2 and 3). The question of who wins in such games is far from simple; an interesting theory that allows you to find the answer without much searching will be discussed in one of the issues
