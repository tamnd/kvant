For example, atoms inside a crystal with a cubic lattice (such as in figure 1) have six neighbors, but atoms on the surface have only five. Consequently, some of the possible connections at the surface of the atoms remain unused, and the energy of such atoms is greater than the atoms living inside the crystal. The existence of an energy surface is disadvantageous, since it increases the energy of the crystal as a whole. Surface atoms tend to go inside the crystal, surround themselves with their own atoms, use all possible connections and thereby reduce their energy. However, any real crystal occupies a limited area of ​​space, the surface is essential and someone needs to be on it. Moreover, atoms end up on the surface not for any “misdeeds”, but by accident. As in the famous dog: “Some may be lucky, some may not.”

Thus, we see that the formation of a surface is associated with certain energy costs, and the main characteristic of a surface is surface energy - the energy required to create a surface of a unit area.

It is known that the mechanical, electrical and other properties of crystals can depend on direction. This phenomenon is called anisotropic and is a consequence of the ordering of the crystal structure. surface energy - anisotropic properties. As you can see from figure 2, surfaces can be created in many ways. For example, by splitting a crystal along planes 1–1, 2–2, or 3–3, we obtain surfaces with significantly different structures—the number of atoms per unit area on surface 1–1 is greater than on surface 2–2. Different numbers of atoms mean different surface energy. The surface energy values ​​per unit area for different surfaces of the same crystal can differ very much, by several tens of percent.

Dadorbtion

The gas mask example with which we started the conversation serves as a clear illustration of a phenomenon called adsorption. Adsorption (from the Latin words ad - “on” and sorbeo - “to absorb”) is the absorption of a substance from a gas or liquid surface medium through the decomposition (adsorbing) of environmental particles, and they become associated with the surface. Of course, oxygen, nitrogen and other air components are adsorbed on coal. The surface of the coal is saturated with these gases even during the cooking process - after all, coal is always in contact with the atmosphere, and the molecules of the gases that make up the air are already supplied to occupy all the places available for them on the surface.
