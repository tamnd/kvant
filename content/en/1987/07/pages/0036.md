Van der Waals and his equation
(on the 150th anniversary of the scientist’s birth)

On November 23, 1837, the first of nine children was born into the family of Leiden carpenter Jacobus Van der Waals - Johannes Diederik Van der Waals, the future famous Dutch scientist, one of the founders of modern molecular physics.

The modest income of a family of one hundred people did not allow even to dream of higher education, so after primary school, Van der Waals went through the entire long and difficult path to science on his own, as they would say now, without interruption from work, or only at the age of 40 did he part with a professional school teacher. True, he still had the opportunity, albeit for many years for the first time, to attend ten lecture courses in mathematics, astronomy and physics at the ancient University of Leiden.

But on June 14, 1873, Van der Waals - at that time he was still teaching physics at one of Gaga's real schools - came to Leiden University not to expand his knowledge with another lecture. His doctoral dissertation “On the continuity of the liquid and gaseous state” was scheduled for this day. The defense was successful, but the Gaz teacher’s dissertation did not cause any particular enthusiasm among the members of the academic council. Ten years passed, and European scientists began to realize that this work - note, this was Van der Waals's first independent scientific research, his first scientific publication! — its author included his name among the classics of science. In 1910, Van der Waals was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics.

What is the origin of this classic work of the Dutch scientist? In formula language, the answer to this question looked like this:

before van der Waals, the equation of state of an ideal gas was known
$$pV=RT,$$
(1)
in “Continuity” the equation of state of a reactive gas first appeared
$$\left(p+\frac{a}{V^2}\right)(V-b)=RT,$$
(2)
where $V$ is the volume of a mole of gas at temperature $T$ and pressure $p$, $R$ is the universal gas constant, $a$ and $b$ are experimental constants characterizing the correspondence between molecular acceptance and the intrinsic volume of molecules.

An ideal gas is, although extremely useful in physics, an extremely simplified theoretical model. It corresponds to a gas whose density is so low that the interaction between molecules and the size of the molecules play practically no role. The van der Waals equation (the so-called equation of state of a reactive gas) corresponds to a much more realistic gas model: molecules are absolutely solid balls of a very small but finite range.

Van der Waals described the transition from equation (1) to equation (2) as “introduction of corrections.” And this is really true. Firstly, it takes into account that in addition to the external pressure $p$ there is an “internal” pressure $a/V^2$, which arose due to intermolecular pressure and “tends” to pull the molecules rushing about in unprecedented thermal motion into one text cluster. Secondly, we take into account the fact that not the entire volume $V$ is available for the thermal movement of molecules, but only the space remaining after subtracting the molecules’ own volume. (In fact, van der Waals discovered, to his considerable surprise, that the constant $b$ is, to a first approximation, equal to four times the total intrinsic volume of the molecules contained in one molecule).

The general word “amendment” is associated with something not too important, at least not fundamental, not related to a fundamental change. However, in physics this habitual association is very often not justified. And a clear example of this is the van der Waals transformation, bringing to the studied equation (1), valid for a theoretical object that does not exist in nature called “ideal gas,” into equation (2), which describes the behavior of a reactive substance in the range of densities, space from values ​​​​corresponding to rarefaction of a gas, to values ​​​​corresponding to the liquid state.

Moreover, from the very expressions for the van der Waals cumulative corrections, it is possible to determine both the molecular forces and the size of the molecules - perfect real parameters that are not even discussed in the ideal gas model.
