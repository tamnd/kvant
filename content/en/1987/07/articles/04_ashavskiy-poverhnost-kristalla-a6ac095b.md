SURFACE
CRYSTAL

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. S. ASHAVSKY

1915 The First World War has been going on for a year now. Germany was the first of the warring powers to use chemical warfare agents. When this idea became known, the outstanding scientist Nikolai Dmitrievich Zelinsky developed a special device that protects people from chemical warfare agents. This device - a carbon gas mask - saved the lives of thousands of Russian soldiers.

The device developed by Zelensky is a prototype of a modern gas mask. The main part of the gas mask is a box filled with coal powder. Let's try to understand what the effect of such a powder is based on and why it can protect against the effects of poisonous gases.

Little giants

Imagine that in a gas mask box, along with coal powder, there is simply a piece of coal of the same mass. What if you get into a gas attack zone with such a gas mask? Will it protect against toxic gases? It turns out not. It's all about the powder.

What distinguishes powder from a solid piece of coal?

The gas mask uses specially prepared carbon, which is called activated carbon. This type of coal differs from regular coal in that it has a much larger surface area per unit mass. Its particles are riddled with pores and somewhat resemble worm-eaten wood. The surface area of ​​activated carbon per unit mass, called the specific surface area, turns out to be millions of times greater than the specific surface area of ​​a solid piece. One gram of activated carbon has a surface area exceeding 1000 m². Think about it: a small sticky piece, specially prepared and ground into powder, acquires a gigantic surface! In such a powder, a significant part of the molecules and atoms appear on the surface. And this fact determines the protective effect of the gas mask: it is the surfaces of the atoms of coal that “detain” the atoms of poisonous gases, clearing them from the air, passing into the respiratory mass.

Why can atoms on the surface absorb poisonous gas, but not in the bulk? How does the life of atoms on the surface differ from its life in the bulk? Let's try to figure it out.

Why are atoms uncomfortable on the surface?

It is well known that a crystal has an ordered and symmetrical lattice, in which each atom occupies a strictly defined place. Atoms of a crystal interact with each other, and each atom, as a result of such interaction, forms stable “connections” with its neighbors. The number of nearest neighbors of atoms in a given crystal lattice is called the coordinate number and is a characteristic of the crystal. No matter where inside the crystal we choose an atom, it will always have a number of neighbors equal to the coordinate number. What if the atom is on the surface?

Let's imagine a crystal located in a vacuum. The surface does not have atoms on all sides -

For example, atoms inside a crystal with a cubic lattice (such as in figure 1) have six neighbors, but atoms on the surface have only five. Consequently, some of the possible connections at the surface of the atoms remain unused, and the energy of such atoms is greater than the atoms living inside the crystal. The existence of an energy surface is disadvantageous, since it increases the energy of the crystal as a whole. Surface atoms tend to go inside the crystal, surround themselves with their own atoms, use all possible connections and thereby reduce their energy. However, any real crystal occupies a limited area of ​​space, the surface is essential and someone needs to be on it. Moreover, atoms end up on the surface not for any “misdeeds”, but by accident. As in the famous dog: “Some may be lucky, some may not.”

Thus, we see that the formation of a surface is associated with certain energy costs, and the main characteristic of a surface is surface energy - the energy required to create a surface of a unit area.

It is known that the mechanical, electrical and other properties of crystals can depend on direction. This phenomenon is called anisotropic and is a consequence of the ordering of the crystal structure. surface energy - anisotropic properties. As you can see from figure 2, surfaces can be created in many ways. For example, by splitting a crystal along planes 1–1, 2–2, or 3–3, we obtain surfaces with significantly different structures—the number of atoms per unit area on surface 1–1 is greater than on surface 2–2. Different numbers of atoms mean different surface energy. The surface energy values ​​per unit area for different surfaces of the same crystal can differ very much, by several tens of percent.

Dadorbtion

The gas mask example with which we started the conversation serves as a clear illustration of a phenomenon called adsorption. Adsorption (from the Latin words ad - “on” and sorbeo - “to absorb”) is the absorption of a substance from a gas or liquid surface medium through the decomposition (adsorbing) of environmental particles, and they become associated with the surface. Of course, oxygen, nitrogen and other air components are adsorbed on coal. The surface of the coal is saturated with these gases even during the cooking process - after all, coal is always in contact with the atmosphere, and the molecules of the gases that make up the air are already supplied to occupy all the places available for them on the surface.

Some bind tightly, others, at the slightest impact, can no longer hold on and leave their places, giving way to more “friendly” molecules. Fortunately, many poisonous gases are so strongly bound by the damage of coal that the role of exposure is not taken into account - so small a quantity is adsorbed by coal. Molecules of the toxic substance remain on the surface of the coal, and molecules of oxygen pass through, allowing breathing.

Langmuir formula and activated carbon

Significant progress in the study of adsorption was achieved thanks to the work of the American physicist-chemist I. Langmuir (1881-1957). In 1932, he was awarded the Nobel Prize for his work on damage phenomena. Langmore obtained a formula that allows one to calculate the amount of an adsorbent substance depending on its pressure in the environment. Obtaining this formula is not difficult at all.

Microphotographs of activated carbon damage.

When deriving the Langmuir formula, we will assume that all atoms of damage are the same and each of them can adsorb a strictly defined number of molecules (or atoms) of the environment and that there is no interaction between the adsorbed molecules, i.e., the adsorbed molecule does not affect the adsorption of the others. In order for a gas molecule to be adsorbed on a defect, two independent events must occur: the gas molecule must fall on the defect of the adsorbent and there must be a free bond at this point of damage. The probability $w_1$ of the first event is proportional to the number of impacts of gas molecules on the damaged adsorbent per unit time, and this number is proportional to the gas pressure $p$; so $w_1 \sim p$. The probability $w_2$ of the second event is proportional to the number of independent events on the damage of the adsorbent. If at a given moment $\theta$ of all free bonds is occupied by adsorbed molecules, then $w_2 \sim (1 - \theta)$. The probability $W$ of a complex event - adsorption - is equal to the product of the probabilities of these two independent events, i.e. $W \sim p(1 - \theta)$. If we denote the number of parts adsorbing on damage per unit time by $N$, then

$$N \downarrow = \alpha p(1 - \theta),$$

where $\alpha$ is a constant that depends on temperature.

The molecule flies away from the damage when its energy is sufficient to tear it off. The probability of such other event is proportional to the total number of molecules adsorbed on the damage. Consequently, the number of molecules escaping from the damage per unit time is

$$N_{\uparrow} = \beta \theta,$$

where $\beta$ is a constant, also depending on temperature.

At equilibrium, the number of molecules arriving at the surface and leaving it per unit time should be equal to $(N_1 = N_2)$:

$$a p(1 - \theta) = \beta \theta.$$

From here there are bonds on the surface occupied by adsorbed molecules:

$$\theta = \frac{b p}{1 + b p},$$

where the coefficient $b = \alpha / \beta$ depends on temperature (since $\alpha$ and $\beta$ depend on $T$). If the number of free places on the surface of the adsorbent is $z$ (usually the number of free bonds per unit mass of the adsorbent is taken), then the number of adsorbed molecules

$$\Gamma = z \frac{b p}{1 + b p}.$$

This is Langmuir's formula.

The constant $z$ is a quantity characterizing the surface. It is in $z$ that the high adsorption ability of activated carbon lies - after all, the larger the specific surface, the greater $z$. For an adsorbent with a specific surface area of ​​$1000 \text{ m}^2/\text{г}$, the value of $z$ is approximately $10^{22} \text{ g}^{-1}$.

figure 3 shows graphs of $\Gamma$ versus $p$ for two different adsorbents. These are the so-called Langmuir adsorption isotherms (isotherms - because the adsorption process is assumed to occur at a constant temperature). At relatively low pressures ($b p \ll 1$) $\Gamma$ is directly proportional to $p$; at high pressures ($b p \ll 1$), saturation occurs - all the bonds on the surface that were free are occupied, and there is no room left for new atoms and molecules of the environment - after all, the surface is not infinite. Obviously, substance 1 is a better adsorbent than substance 2.

Ubiquitous surface
In many processes, the surface plays a decisive role. However, the most striking examples of this are the processes of catalysis (from the Greek κατάλυσις - “destruction”).

Catalysts are substances that can influence the rate of a chemical reaction without participating in it themselves. The activity of solid catalysts is primarily determined by the properties of their surface. In this regard, I would like to mention one interesting phenomenon.

Atoms that absorb energy from the surface can come to the surface not only from the environment, but also from the internal region of the crystal. Let's imagine a crystal consisting of two types of atoms (two components). The conditions for the existence of atoms on the surface and in the bulk, as we already know, are different. Therefore, it is entirely possible that the relative concentrations of atoms on the surface and in the bulk are also different. Atoms that reduce surface energy tend to come to the surface, and their relative number on the surface increases. This process is called sergeration (from the English segregation - “separation”). The concentration of atoms of one of the components on the surface can be thousands of times higher than its concentration in the volume. For example, in an alloy consisting of Fe and Bi atoms, the concentration of Bi on the surface is ten percent, while its concentration in the volume is only 0.1%. Sergeration must be taken into account during the catalysis process, since the rate of chemical reaction on the surface of a solid catalyst very much depends on the surface concentrations of substances.

The surface is the authorized representative and protector of materials in their interaction with the environment. After all, the surface is primarily supported by the environment. The worst enemy of a metal surface is corrosion. The first task of the surface is to resist the onslaught of this enemy. Minor amounts of impurities, hundreds of times less than what is necessary to cover the surface with a monogated layer, can significantly increase the corrosion resistance of the metal surface.

“The surface was invented by the devil,” the outstanding physicist, one of the creators of quantum mechanics, W. Pauli (1900-1958), once said. These words are apparently associated with the enormous difficulties of surface research.

Methods for obtaining information about a surface—its structure, chemical composition, etc.—are very complex, and basic advances in this area have only been made in the last decade. Let's give a simple example: it is necessary to determine the concentration of a substance in a crystal. To carry out such a measurement using traditional chemical methods, a minimum of 0.001 g of the substance is required. If the surface area of ​​the analyzed sample is 1 cm² (it is practically impossible to obtain a larger area without destroying the sample), then it is possible to determine the average concentration of the substance in a layer about 1000 international distances thick.

Modern methods make it possible to study a layer of surface licking with a thickness of just one mechanical distance! They make it possible to see atoms, study their relative position, and interaction with each other. Using these methods, it is easy to establish that during sergeration, all the difference between the surface concentration and the volume concentration is concentrated in a very thin layer of surface licks - only a few mechanical distances thick.

Progress in surface research over the past decade is primarily associated with the success of microsectors and the creation of new devices. The answer to the most important question: what elements does the surface consist of? - it was impossible to obtain just a few years ago, but today, with the help of a tunnel microsector, it is possible to distinguish individual atoms on the surface, located at a distance of only a few angstroms from each other.

“The surfaces are very interesting, but there are so few of them,” said the Great Intai physicist Enrico Fermi (1901-1954). Indeed, only a very small part of the substance belongs to the surface. But its significance in the life of matter is enormous.
