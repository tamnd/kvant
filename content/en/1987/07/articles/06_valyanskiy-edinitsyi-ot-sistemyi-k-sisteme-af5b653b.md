UNITS:
FROM SYSTEM TO SYSTEM

S. I. VALYANSKY

On December 15, 1832, the work of the world famous mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss was presented to the Göttingen Scientific Society for discussion. It was called “The Intensification of the Earth's Magnetic Force Reduced to Absolute Measure” and was short, only 37 pages. However, this modest work played a huge role in the development of natural science. To feel this role, we will have to turn to history, remember some moments of the “biography” of the magnet.

The ancients learned about the existence of a “stone” that accepts iron, and attempts to explain the accepting power of this stone were made more than 2000 years ago. The first practical use of a magnet was the compass. There is no clear opinion regarding the place and time of invention of the compass. But at least since the 13th century, the compass has been known in Europe. With its appearance, discoveries began related to the nature of terrestrial magnetism. It was immediately discovered that the magnetic needle does not indicate a strict direction towards light; that the deviation of the arrow from this direction (magnetic declination) varies depending on the location of observation; that the arrow is not installed horizontally, and the angle it makes with the vertical also depends on the observation location.

What explains the orientation of magnetic needles? What are magnetic forces? The first to try to answer these questions was the English physicist William Gilbert (1544-1603). Being a keen experimenter, Hilbert laid the foundations on magnetism and, in particular, on terrestrial magnetism. He suggested that the Earth is a large magnet, and in this sense it is like a ball of magnetic material. To confirm this hypothesis, the scientist made a small model of the Earth - an iron ball - and showed that when moving along the surface of such a ball, the magnetic needle is oriented exactly as it is in reality.

The problem of navigation posed a new task for the natural scientist: it became clear that in order to successfully use a compass (the compass was one of the main navigational tools) it was necessary to have a map of magnetic declinations. To compile such a map, the Royal Society of London (England Academy of Sciences) in 1698 equipped

How did events develop further? Understanding the importance of magnetic measurements for the development of science, travel, and military affairs, three Academies of Sciences - St. Petersburg, Paris and Berlin - are announcing a competition for the best work explaining the phenomenon of terrestrial magnetism.
With the discovery of Coulomb's law, a new stage in the study of terrestrial magnetism began. In the wake of the famous German scientist - geologist, geographer, traveler - Alexander Humboldt, since 1800, almost all expeditions included the measurement of the magnetic field at various points on the Earth as one of the program items. By the way, the famous Russian seamen also took up such measurements. This was in the middle of the 18th century. Many types of educational postcards are called by the Academies. A number of fundamental works on the study of terrestrial magnetism were carried out precisely at this time. And among them is the work of the French physicist and engineer Charles Auguste Coulomb on the precise measurement of electrical and magnetic forces.
After conducting a series of amazingly accurate experiments for that time, Coulomb proved that the force of interaction between charged bodies is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them and directly proportional to their charges. By analogy with electric charges, to characterize the magnetic properties of matter, Coulomb used the concept of magnetic charges and showed that the force of interaction between magnetized bodies depends on the distance between them and on the magnitude of magnetic charges in the same way as the force of power interaction in the case of electrically charged bodies.*) Thus, thanks to Coulomb’s experiments, it became possible to measure the absolute value of magnetic force.

In 1828, Gauss met with A. Humboldt. A long conversation between the two greatest scientists of their time ended with Gauss becoming interested in the phenomena of terrestrial magnetism and deciding to try to create a theory of this phenomenon. But before starting to solve the problem, it was necessary to develop precise scientific definitions of the characteristics of terrestrial magnetism - declination, inclination and directionality. And for this it is necessary to develop the basis of magnetic changes. Gauss was able to begin his intended work only in the fall of 1831, when the 27-year-old physicist from Halle Wilhelm Weber was invited to the University of Göttingen. Going through various triplets of quantities that can claim to be fundamental, he established that in mechanics it is most convenient to use length, mass and time as the main quantities. In addition, there were stages and simple measurement methods for these quantities.

Next, it was necessary to construct a device, by measuring the mechanical parameters of which it was possible to determine the magnetic characteristics of magnetized objects. To do this, Gauss took advantage of the following circumstance. The period of oscillation of a magnetized needle in the Earth's magnetic field depends on the strength of the earth's magnetism, as well as on the magnetic and mechanical properties of the needle. Therefore, by measuring and comparing the periods of oscillation of different hands with known mechanical properties, it is possible, knowing the law of interaction of magnetic charges, to determine the strength of earthly magnetism. So, the process of measuring magnetic quantities leads to the measurement of mechanical quantities, which, in turn, can be expressed through the basic ones - length, mass and time.

And one more thing: it was this authoritative Commission that proposed the unit of force - dyne and the unit of energy - erg. In fact, the well-known system of GHS units was developed. For electrical and magnetic units, Gauss's idea of ​​​​connecting mechanical and electromagnetic units through measurement procedures (SGSE and SGSM systems) was used.

This is why the decision of the British Commission was at the center of the service of the First Electrical Congress in Paris in 1881. Since the Congress was international, the decisions it made had international significance. And these decisions were based on the proposals of the British Association for the Advancement of Science, i.e., the ideas of Gauss.
