⟦folio 14⟧

FIGURES
RETURNING HOME

Once we already talked about puzzles in which all the white pieces that arrive on the board occupy their original places in the initial position («Kvant», 1985, No. 4). Please, it is worth returning to this fascinating topic once again.

G. Ibulyaev, 1986
a) White: Kre1, Cf1, Kg1; black: Kph2.
b) White: Kre1, Cf1, Kg1; black: Kph1.
Checkmate in 3 moves.
a) 1. Kpf2 Kph1 2. Cg2+ Kph2 3. Kf3×.
b) 1. Kpf2 Kph2 2. Kf3+ Kph1 3. Cg2×.
More subtle positions with two minor pieces belong to the well-known Soviet problem.
R. Tavarnina, 1986
a) White: Kre1, Kb1, Cf1; black: Kpg1.
b) White: Kre1, Cc1, Cf1; black: Kpg1.
Checkmate in 4 moves.
a) 1. Kd2 Kph1 2. Kpf2 Kph2 3. Kf3+ Kph1 4. Cg2×, 1...Kph2 2. Kpf2 Kph1 3. Cg2+ Kph2 4. Kf3×.
b) 1. Kpe2 Kph1 2. Kpf2 Kph2 3. Cf4+ Kph1 4. Cg2×, 1...Kph2 2. Kpf2 Kph1 3. Cg2+ Kph2 4. Cf4×. Unfortunately, the opening move in the second problem is not the only one.

Let us recall the famous puzzle by S. Lloyd. All White's pieces are in their places, Black has one king on h4. White puts mat in 3 moves. 1. d4 Kph5 2. Qd3 and 3. Qh3×, 1...Kpg4 2. e4+ Kph4 3. g3×. It is curious that the h4 square is the only one on which the lone black king gets mated so quickly. The “safest” place is on e8, then the king cannot be captured before the sixth move. If the king himself helps, then mat is placed twice as fast - the same speed as in Loyd's record.

A. Korotaev, 1986. White has all the pieces and pockets as at the beginning of the game, Black has one king on the e8 square. Cooperative mat in 3 moves.
1. Kpf7! (in “cooperatives” black starts) d3 (d4)
2. Kpg6! Qd2! 3. Kph5 Фg5×.
And now for more unusual tasks. Once again you are faced with two twin problems on two half-boards.

R. Tavarnina, 1986
a) In what (name) number of moves can the white king get from b5 to d8?
6) The white king is moved from b5 to h5 and the board is rotated 180°. In what number of moves can the white king get from a4 to e1?
a) Eight moves are enough: 1. Kpc5 h5 2. Kpd5 c5! 3. Kpe5 Qa5 4. Kpf5 f6! 5. Kpg6 Kpd8 6. Kpf7 Kpc7 7. Kpe8 Kpb6 8. Kpd8!
6) This journey is four moves longer: 1. Kpb3 Kf3 2. Kpc4 g1K! 3. Kpb4 Ka3! 4. Kpc3 Cg2 5. Kpd3 b1Ф 6. Kpe3 Cb2 7. Kpe4 Kpc1 8. Kpf4 d1C! 9. Kpg4 Qc3 10. Kpg3 f1C! 11. Kpf2 Kd2 12. Kpe1!
To quickly let the white king through to the goal, Black carried out a weak promotion three times!
L. Sokolov, 1986 White: Kre1, Cf1, p. h2; black: Kpb8, Rh1, Kg1. Cooperative mat in 6 moves.
Here Black's rook and knight also occupy their usual places, albeit in the opponent's camp. 1. Kpc7! h4 2. Kpd6 h5 3. Kpe5 h6 4. Kpf4 h7 5. Kpg3 h8C!! Letting the king through to the intended route point. 6. Kph2 Ce5×.

“Oh pawn! You know, she’s strong, since she turns into an elephant!” — this is how the author commented on this problem.
Until now, white troops occupied the starting lines even before completing a combat mission, but here is a unique example of the opposite kind - all white pieces (pawns do not count) return home during the decision process.

T. Cabo, 1963. Reverse mat in 7 moves.
In contrast to cooperative problems, where black is placed in a problem on the reverse mat, white forces the mat to be questioned according to their wishes. 1. Kb1 ab 2. Ra1 b5 3. f5 gf 4. Kg1 f4 5. Cf1 f3 6. Qd1+ Rc2 7. Rh1!
All the first horizontal has acquired a familiar appearance, and Black is forced to put mat - 7...f2×.
