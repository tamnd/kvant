While thinking about this, Federsen remembered an article he had once read about the Leyden jar discharge. This article was written in 1853 by the English scientist William Thomson. Thomson did not do any experiments with the Leyden jar, and he did not do any experiments at all. But he was a very skilled mathematician. He knew the physical laws that govern electric current and knew how to derive mathematical consequences from these laws. And so Thomson tried, using calculations, to establish what actually happens to the electric charge when a Leyden jar is charged.

Thomson's calculations showed: an electric charge, having been added along the wires from the cup of the Leyden jar to the spark gap, makes a jump from ball to ball and rushes along the wire again into the jar, but into another cup. Thus, both charges, positive and negative, change places.

The negative charge, which was initially, say, in the inner cup of the jar, will fly from the opening to the outer one, and the positive one from the outer one to the inner one. Thus, the bank will not be discharged, but will only be charged anew, and the electric current will again rush to the spark gap, but in the opposite direction. The electric charge will run back and forth, jumping from one ball to another, again from the second to the first and again from the first to the second.

“So that’s what it’s all about!” thought Federsen. That is why the device noted not one spark in the image, but eight. This means that electric current passed from ball to ball eight times, first in one direction, then in the other. And every time a brilliant flash appears in the spark gap. The flash ended, and immediately another appeared - this was again the electric current breaking through, but in the opposite direction. Flash after flash sparkled in the narrow space between the balls while the life of the spark continued, and with each flash the direction of the current changed. The current ran in one direction - a flash, ran back - a new flash.

Federsen's rotating mirror confirmed Thomson's agreement: an electric spark is a small alternating electric current. After minute intervals, every few millionths of a second, the current changes its direction.

So the rotating mirror helped Federsen study the nature of the electric spark.
