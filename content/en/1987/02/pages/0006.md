It is known that any finite group is a subgroup of one of the permutation groups $S_n$, $n=1, 2, \dots$ (see p. 9 of this issue). Therefore, the search for simple finite groups can begin with the study of subgroups of the group $S_n$.

But what is a simple group? Let us call a gorodophysim $\alpha$: $G \rightarrow H$ simplifying if its pattern $\alpha(G) \subset H$ has more than one element, but less than in $G$. A finite group $G$ is said to be simple if it has no simplifying gorodophisms.

The most obvious example of a simple group is the cyclic group $Z_p$ of any prime order $p$:

$$Z_p = \begin{cases} z, z^2, \dots, z^p = e \\ z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & \dots & (p-1) & p \\ 1 & 2 & \dots & p & 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{cases} \subset S_n.$$

Effectively, if there were a simplifying gorodophysim $\alpha$: $Z_p \rightarrow H$, then one of the unequal $e$ elements $w \in Z_p$ would go to one, but then any element from $Z_p$ would go to one (think why) and $\alpha(Z_p) = \{e\}$ - a contradiction! The simplicity of $p$ is essential here, or the group $Z_m$ with the composite $m$ can be mapped homomorphically onto $Z_k$, where $k$ is a divisor of $m$.
