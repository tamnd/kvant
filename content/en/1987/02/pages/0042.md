From table No. 43, we learned that each point of the Earth on the spine of Moscow moves due to the rotation of the Earth at a speed of 259 m/s. At points of the equator this speed reaches 465 m/s. Why don't we notice this movement? Or are there still phenomena that allow us to notice it, just as we notice the movement of a train while in it?

Are there trees that drown in water? Such trees exist, these are bakut (“iron tree”) and ebony - the density of their wood is greater than the density of water. This information is provided by tables No. 49 and No. 55. The same tables report that of all types of wood, large wood has the lowest density: it floats “best” in water, and, as indicated in the footnote to table No. 55, the Kon-Tiki raft was made from it. That is why the participants of the voyage on the Kon-Tiki are looking for a ball tree in South America, overcoming enormous difficulties: during the jan season, they moved through the Andes to the jungle area and took from there twelve ball trees, nine of which were used to build a raft (an entire chapter in the famous book by T. Heyerdahl is devoted to the description of this journey).

And if a raft of the same volume as the Kon-Tiki is made not from balla, but from pine, then how many times less will its carrying capacity be (i.e., the mass of the cargo that the raft can hold until completely submerged)? Table No. 55 will help you get the answer to this question.

What distances do you think the air molecules in the room travel from one collision to another? Is it possible to deflect such a distance on paper using a sharpened pencil? It turns out that under normal conditions it is impossible for the average free collision of air molecules to be 60 nanometers (and the number of collisions of molecules per second is 7.5 billion). But at an altitude of 80 km above the Earth, the air is so rarefied that the distance between molecules is on average 4.4 mm (Table No. 105); such a segment can be put down with a pencil on paper.
