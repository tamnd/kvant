Federsen covered these drains with gray hair - only on one side. The result was two shiny, volgutile mirrors. Federsen fixed them on his screw so that one looked in one direction, the other in the other. When the weight fell and the screw began to move, both mirrors attached to the screw spun evenly and quickly with it.

The mechanism of the newly invented watch was ready. But that's not all. After all, a watch requires not only a clock mechanism that makes the hand rotate, but also a dial to change the path traveled by the hand.

If the hand is made of light, what should the dial be made of?

Federsen thought for a long time, he searched for a long time for suitable material for this matter. Finally, I found it: a photographic plate, a photographic plate sensitive to light, will be the dial of an extraordinary watch. On this dial the electric spark itself, with its own rays, will mark the beginning and end of its short life. The rays of the spark will fall on the rotating mirror, the swift bunny will slide across the photographic plate and leave its mark on it. The longer the spark burns, the longer the bunny's trail will be. And based on the length of the trail, knowing the speed of the bunny, it will not be difficult to calculate how long he ran along the plastic - how long the electric spark burned.

Federsen's device was completed. The steel screw was installed in the frame, the rotating mirror was working properly, and the photographic plate was at the ready. A Lezhensky jar, a source of electron sparks, was also at the ready. This jar consists of three cups placed inside each other: the outer one is metal, the middle one is glass, and the inside is again metal.

Federsen charged the jar: the outer metal cup is positive and the electric one is negative. Then he connected the jar with wires to two metal balls placed opposite each other. These balls are called spark gaps. Federsen connected the inner cup of the jar to one spark gap ball, and the outer cup to the other. Now all that remained was to press the button that closed and opened the electrical circuit, so that a charge of electricity rushed out of the can through the wires. The positive charge will run towards the negative one, and a thin spark will flare up between the balls of the spark gap.

Federsen began experiments with his extraordinary watch. He released the weight that set the gears and screw in motion. Now the screw and the mirror began to turn so quickly that Federsen’s eyes began to flash. The max wheel attached to the screw rotated with a thin whistle. Listening to this whistle, Federsen was convinced that his device was working properly: the sound was the same pitch all the time, which means that the axis rotates evenly, without replacing or speeding up its movement.

Then Federsen turned off the light and slightly opened the cassette in which the photographic plate was prepared, and then pressed the button that closed the electrical circuit.
