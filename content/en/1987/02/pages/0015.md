Classic Hall effect

In 1879, American physicist Edwin Hall discovered that if a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field perpendicular to the current, then an electrostatic field will arise in the conductor, directed perpendicular to the magnetic field and current (figure 1).

We can calculate the voltage of this field. The current in a conductor is the direction of movement of the electrons. The magnitude of the current and the average speed of this movement are related by the relation $v = I/(nes)$, where $n$ is the concentration of electrons in the conductor, $e$ is the electron charge, $s$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the conductor. Let us place a rectangular sample through which current $I$ flows in a magnetic field with induction $B$ (see figure 1). The Lorentz force $F = evB$, directed perpendicularly $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$, acts on moving electrons from the magnetic field.

As a result of the action of this force, electrons will mix towards the side face of the sample, so that an excess of electrons will appear on one of the faces, and a deficiency on the other. This leads to the appearance of an electrostatic field perpendicular to the current - it is called the Hall field. The movement of electrons across the current will stop as soon as the force acting on the electron from the Hall field becomes equal to the Lorentz force: $E_X e = evB$, whence $E_X = vB$, or

$$E_X = \frac{IB}{nes}.$$

The Hall field creates a potential difference on the conductor faces equal to

$$U_X = E_X b = \frac{IBb}{nes} = \frac{IB}{nea},$$

where $b$ is the width of the conductor, $a$ is its thickness. This potential difference on the faces can be directly changed in experiments, and also changes to very important information about the number of electrons per unit volume of the conductor, since the ratio of the Hall voltage $U_X$ to the current $I$ passed through the conductor, as follows from the above calculations, depends on $n$. This relationship is explained by the direction perpendicular to the magnetic field and current (figure 1).
