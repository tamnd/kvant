Groups of symmetric geometric functions

With each geometric function F one can associate, in full definition, a group S(F), called the self-sample group or the group symmetric to this function; by definition, its action set consists of all movements that combine the function F with itself. For example, S(□) consists of 8 actions: four rotations of the square (relative to its center, including 0°) and four reflections (relative to two diagonals and two “middle lines” of the square).

In the group S(△) the self-sample of a regular triangle is 6 actions, in the group S(□) of a right square is 4. The multiplication table of the group S(□) is shown on figure 4.

If you compare the multiplication table for group S(□) with the multiplication table for group H, you will notice that these tables differ only in the designation of actions. If the action variable is like this:
$$\begin{array}{c}
O \rightarrow R_0, \quad \Pi \rightarrow R_1, \quad B \rightarrow S_1, \quad \Pi' \rightarrow S_2, \\
\text{то одна таблица превращится в другую.}
\end{array}$$

Groups with coincidences (with a suitable change of actions) with multiplication tables are called isomorphic. We have now established that the groups S(□) and H are isomorphic (they are usually denoted by the letter K in honor of F. Klein), and earlier we noticed that these groups are not isomorphic to the group R(□) of actions that are additive.

The reader may have agreed why we denoted the group of actions composed R(□): it is isomorphic to the group of rotations of a square relative to its center through angles 2kπ/4, k=0, 1, 2, 3. This group is a special case (for n=4) of the group of rotations of a regular n-knot (relative to its center), which is also called a cyclic group of n-zo order and is usually denoted by Zn.

In algebra, groups are studied “up to isomorphism,” that is, isomorphic groups are not distinguished: an algebraist is not interested in the name of the group and its actions; it is important for him to know the structure of the group’s multiplication table.

Permutation groups and their subgroups

Consider a finite set of objects—say, five. Let's denote the objects by numbers, and the weight of the set by N5={1, 2, 3, 4, 5}. A permutation i∈S5 of these objects is any one-to-one mapping i: N5→N5, i.e., simply put, a variable of objects. We will denote the new number i(k) of the kth item by ik. For clarity, the permutation i is usually presented in table form:

$$i = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ i_1 & i_2 & i_3 & i_4 & i_5 \end{pmatrix}.$$

This makes it easy to find the product of permutations i and j. For example, if

$$i = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 4 & 3 & 5 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad j = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 3 & 5 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$

then k(3)=(i∘j)(3)=i(j(3))=i(5)=2, so

$$i∘j = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 2 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$$

(note that when k=i∘j, j is executed first, and then i, and it is not the same: i∘j≠j∘i - check!)

It is also easy to find reverse permutations (“reading from bottom to top”):

$$i^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 4 & 3 & 5 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 4 & 5 & 2 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

It is easy to check that S5 forms a group consisting of 5!=120 permutations. This group is called the permutation group for objects or the symmetric group of five stenni. The symmetric group Sn of n-h walls is defined in exactly the same way for any natural number n.
