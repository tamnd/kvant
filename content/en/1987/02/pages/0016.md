The presence of a magnetic field also affects the total resistance of the conductor (the resistance to the flow of current) - it, as a rule, increases.

This can be done qualitatively in the following way. The cause of electrical resistance is the scattering of electrons by obstacles encountered along their path - on impurities, on defects in the crystal lattice, etc. When encountering obstacles, the directions of electron velocities can change in a derivative way. However, in the intervals between collisions, the electrons will be accelerated by the electric field - the projection of their velocities along the calculated field. Thus, a non-zero average speed of electron drift along the field is established in the conductor—current flows through the conductor. In the presence of a magnetic field by tractors, the movements of electrons between collisions are bent, and as a result, the speed of their drift decreases significantly. Consequently, at the same potential difference, the current decreases, i.e., the calculation of resistance. It should be noted, however, that the cold field partially weakens the described effect (and sometimes compensates for it completely).

During the years that have passed since the discovery of the Hall effect, much has been done in its study. It was found out how the cold resistance is affected by the presence of impurities in the sample material, the presence of carriers of a different sign, temperature changes, etc. However, formula (1) all this time, basically, did not cause any problems for physicists.

And in 1980, K. von Klitsing discovered that in special systems called two-dimensional electronic layers, the dependence of cold resistance on the magnetic field induction and electron concentration is not at all the same as prescribed by formula (1). And the resistance to current in a two-dimensional electron layer behaves in a special way in the presence of a magnetic field.

The reasons for these “unlearning errors” lie in the properties of the object itself on which they are observed.

conductor - for example silicon - is covered with a layer of dielectric (silicon oxide), on which a so-called gate is sprayed on top - a film of metal (usually aluminum; after the first letters of the words metal - oxide - a semi-video device, it is called a MOS transistor). If a positive voltage is applied to the gate relative to the semiconductor, then the electrons present in the semiconductor are transferred to the gate. But they cannot pass through the oxide and form a thin layer near it (its thickness is 20-30 Å, i.e. ~10⁻⁹ m). This layer can be called two-dimensional - in the sense that in two dimensions along the layer electrons can move freely, while the electrostatic field of the gate does not allow them to travel deep into the semiconductor.

The gate and layer form a flat capacitor, the capacitance of which can be considered with sufficient accuracy equal to $C = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon S/d$, where $\varepsilon$ is the dielectric food oxide, $d$ is its thickness, $S$ is the area of ​​the layer. The charge of the layer can be determined by the formula $Q = CU_3$, where $U_3$ is the voltage between the gate and the semiconductor. Thus, the number of electrons in the layer

$$N = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{CU_3}{e} = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon \frac{S}{de} U_3$$
