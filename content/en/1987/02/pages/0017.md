The semiconductor will become infinite, since its own current carriers will disappear - the semiconductor will become an insulator. Now nothing prevents us from studying the properties of a two-dimensional layer by measuring the voltage drop between contacts to the layer. In this case, current is passed through the sample between contacts called, for obvious reasons, source and drain (see figure 2). If we are interested in cold resistance $R_X$, then it can be determined by measuring the voltage $U_X$ between contacts 1 and 2 or 3 and 4 and dividing the result by the current values ​​$I$. Information about the resistance to the flowing current $R$ can be obtained by measuring the voltage between contacts 1 and 3 or 2 and 4. To measure the cold resistance of such a two-dimensional layer, it is necessary, of course, to place it in a magnetic field directed perpendicular to the plane of the layer. But in a sufficiently strong field, under the influence of the Lorentz force, the electrodes will move in a circle with the same rotation frequency

$$\omega = Be/m,$$

(3)

where $m$ is the mass of the electrode.

Thus, we have obtained an electronic system in which the movement of the electrodes is limited in any direction - in the plane of the layer they are twisted by a magnetic field, and the electric field of the gate prevents them from leaving this plane.

From the point of view of classical physics, a two-dimensional conductor is not qualitatively different from a three-dimensional one. So through such a conductor it is still connected to the drift of electrons (along the layer). The cold resistance should be determined by formula (1), with the difference, however, that by $n$ one must remember the number of electrons coming per unit area, and the denominator, thus, will no longer include the thickness of the conductor:

$$R_X = \frac{B}{ne}.$$

(4)

Nothing unusual should happen to the resistance of the current flowing through the layer - it should increase with increasing $B$. However...

Quantum reasons for the two-dimensional layer

From the point of view of quantum mechanics, however, such a system of electrons should have fundamentally new properties. You probably know that the energy of a microparticle that moves in a limited region of space, according to quantum theory, cannot be arbitrary. For example, when electrons move in an atom, the energy of electrons can only take certain values ​​$W_0$, $W_1$, $W_2$, ... and cannot take intermediate ones. In this case, they say that allowed energy levels $W_0$, $W_1$, $W_2$, .... The same happens with electrons rotating in a magnetic field. It turns out that their energy can only be equal to a discrete value

$$W_i = \left( i + \frac{1}{2} \right) \frac{\omega}{2\pi},$$

(5)

where $i$ is an integer, and $\omega$ is the rotation frequency, determined by formula (3). These permitted energy levels are commonly referred to as Landdu.

According to the laws of quantum mechanics, electrons located in the same place in space must want to be different in some way - have different energies, different spins, etc. Let's try to calculate how many electrons with energy $W_0$ ($i=0$) - this is the lower Landdu level - can fit per unit area. Let the electron rotate in a circle of radius $r$ with frequency $\omega$, determined by formula (3). Its kinetic energy is $m\omega^2r^2/2$. Let's equate this energy $W_0$:

$$\frac{h\omega}{4\pi} = \frac{m\omega^2r^2}{2},$$

or $$\frac{h}{4\pi} \frac{Be}{m} = \frac{m}{2} \frac{B^2e^2}{m^2} r^2.$$

From here there is one electron with energy $W_0$: $\pi r^2 = h/(2Be)$. The same area can accommodate another electron with the same energy $W_0$, but with an oppositely directed spin - this is what the Pauli principle says (the same Pauli principle that is involved in the “dispersal” of electrons in an atom). Other electrons with energy $W_0$ can be placed nearby, so that the “orbits” of the electrons do not overlap. Thus, a $n = \frac{2}{\pi r^2} = \frac{4Be}{h}$ electron with an energy of $W_0$ can be placed per unit area.
