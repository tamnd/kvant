Inventors of the radiotelegraph
(Chapters from the book)

M. P. BRONSHTEIN

Who and when?
Who invented radio and when?

Some people answer this question: Aleksandr Stepanovich Popov invented it, and that was forty years ago. Others say: the radio was invented by the Pultanian Guglielmo Marconi.

And indeed: forty years ago, both Popov and Marconi simultaneously built the world's first radio stations and began sending the world's first radio telegrams.

But the history of radio began much earlier than the last radio telegram. The scientists who began the history of radio with their discoveries and experiments did not send or receive any radio telegrams. They did not strive to transmit any signals or music or human sounds over a distance. How surprised these first inventors of the radiotelegraph would have been if someone had told them that they were inventing the radiotelegraph!

The transmission of sounds, signals, images did not occupy them at all. They were interested in something else.

Have you ever seen electric sparks flying from electrified objects? Dazzling electric sparks that flare up for an instant and then immediately fade away again?

It was with these sparks that the history of radio began.

For many decades, physicists observed an electric spark, did experiments with it, and studied its properties. Finally, they wanted to know: how long does it take from the birth of a spark to its death? How long does an electric spark last?

The question was difficult. They usually answered it like this: it flares up and immediately fades away, it lives only for one moment. But what is a moment? A hundredth of a second or a thousandth or a millionth? How to find out, how to measure?

Every person feels the passage of time. We all distinguish a minute from two minutes, seconds from two seconds, and even ten hours of seconds from a whole.

But everything that is less than one tenth, one fifteenth of a second - all this is already indistinguishable to us, all this - the hundredth, and the thousandth, and the millionth of a second - each is absolutely the same to us. Our sense organs are not so fast, accurate, or sophisticated. In any period of time that is less than one fifteenth of a second, we do not perceive any duration. Therefore, in our hearing there is a fraction of a second with a thousandth, a thousandth with a millionth. A moment - and that’s it.

Well, what about the clock? After all, they were made to change time. Can't a clock change the duration of one moment?

Let's go to a factory that produces precision instruments. We will see there wall clocks, base clocks, and pocket clocks. We will find there chronometers that sailors take with them when going on long voyages, and ultra-precise watches for astronomical observations, and electric chronographs, and stopwatches. But we won’t find clocks that measure millionths of a second in the factory.

And yet such watches exist. Seventy-five years ago, it was invented and built by the German physicist Wilhelm Federsen. He invented them specifically to change how long an electric spark lasts.

Little did he know that by creating this watch, he was starting a story for the sake of it.

Federsen watch

The watches built by Federsen still exist today. They are kept in a museum in the German city of Munich.

They are nothing like our ordinary watches. They don't have an hour hand, a minute hand, or even a second hand. What kind of arrows can we talk about when we need to measure millionths of a second? Where can I find an arrow that can make a million visible steps per second? And these steps should be noticeable - after all, this is Federsen’s task.

And so, after much thought, Federsen announced which hand his watch needed. He made it not from broンズ, not from steel, but from materials that no watchmaker had used before.

He built it from rays of light.

Let's take a small pocket mirror and take it outside into the sunlight. The rays of the sun are reflected from the mirror and will bounce off it as a shiny bright bunny.

Let's turn the mirror. How quickly the bunny runs, how he starts and jumps, reflecting from the mirror that is in my hand! Just now he was very close, but then the mirror turned a little, and the bunny had already run across to the other side of the street and was jumping on the walls, on the eaves, on the balconies of the houses.

The bunny moves quickly - a hundred, a thousand times faster than the second hand of the clock. Is it possible to somehow make it spin not a hundred, but a million times faster?

Can. He just groans and turns the mirror tighter. It’s better to turn it not with your hands, but with a machine - after all, a machine is more conductive than human hands, and moreover, it’s more accurate: whatever speed you order it, it will work at that speed.

In the machine built by Federsen, a heavy multi-pound weight, lowering, pulled a rope behind it. The rope turned the shaft on which the gear was mounted. This wheel, turning, clings with its teeth to another gear, another to a third, and a third to slicing a large steel wine. Gradually moving from wheel to wheel, the movement became faster and faster: the first wheel did not turn very quickly - it made only a few revolutions per second, the second rotated faster, the third even faster. And the fastest thing was a steel propeller: every second it managed to make as many as a hundred revolutions around its axis.

The mechanism of the newly invented watch was ready. But that's not all. After all, a watch requires not only a clock mechanism that makes the hand rotate, but also a dial to change the path traveled by the hand.

If the hand is made of light, what should the dial be made of?

Federsen thought for a long time, he searched for a long time for suitable material for this matter. Finally, I found it: a photographic plate, a photographic plate sensitive to light, will be the dial of an extraordinary watch. On this dial the electric spark itself, with its own rays, will mark the beginning and end of its short life. The rays of the spark will fall on the rotating mirror, the swift bunny will slide across the photographic plate and leave its mark on it. The longer the spark burns, the longer the bunny's trail will be. And based on the length of the trail, knowing the speed of the bunny, it will not be difficult to calculate how long he ran along the plastic - how long the electric spark burned.

Federsen's device was completed. The steel screw was installed in the frame, the rotating mirror was working properly, and the photographic plate was at the ready. A Lezhensky jar, a source of electron sparks, was also at the ready. This jar consists of three cups placed inside each other: the outer one is metal, the middle one is glass, and the inside is again metal.

Federsen charged the jar: the outer metal cup is positive and the electric one is negative. Then he connected the jar with wires to two metal balls placed opposite each other. These balls are called spark gaps. Federsen connected the inner cup of the jar to one spark gap ball, and the outer cup to the other. Now all that remained was to press the button that closed and opened the electrical circuit, so that a charge of electricity rushed out of the can through the wires. The positive charge will run towards the negative one, and a thin spark will flare up between the balls of the spark gap.

History of art

Federsen began experiments with his extraordinary watch. He released the weight that set the gears and screw in motion. Now the screw and the mirror began to turn so quickly that Federsen’s eyes began to flash. The max wheel attached to the screw rotated with a thin whistle. Listening to this whistle, Federsen was convinced that his device was working properly: the sound was the same pitch all the time, which means that the axis rotates evenly, without replacing or speeding up its movement.

Then Federsen turned off the light and slightly opened the cassette in which the photographic plate was prepared, and then pressed the button that closed the electrical circuit. And now, in the darkness, a spark leaked between the balls of the discharge: this electric charge rushed from one metal cup of the legendary jar to another. In an instant

The spark burned for only a moment, but during this elusive world, a quick reflection from the mirror managed to fall on the photographic plate. He ran across the plate with the speed of an artillery shell and traced his path on it.

Right there on the spot, without leaving the device, Federaren developed the plate and printed a photographic SIM card. A narrow stripe was clearly visible on the SIM card - a mark left by a bunny.

Federaren measured the length of the strip - one and a half centimeters. He knew the speed of the bunny - 60,000 centimeters per second. How long does it take for someone to click on a record? The length of the strip - 1.5 must be divided by the speed of the bunny - 60,000, it turns out 0.000025. So, that means twenty-five millionths of a second—that’s how long it took for the record to click without a trace, and that’s how long the electric spark lived.

The Federaren watch performed its assigned task perfectly. Continuous sparks were measured. The task that Federaren set for himself was solved.

But, peering into his upward snare, Federaren became convinced that his watch had made another discovery. They not only measured the length of the spark's life, but also found out what the purpose of this short life was and compiled a detailed biography of the spark.

The trail drawn by the bunny on the SIM card turned out to be not continuous, but intermittent. It consisted of several light spots separated from each other by dark spaces.

This means that the electric spark that leaked between the balls of the spark gap did not burn with a uniform brilliance. During its entire life, which lasted only 25 millionths of a second, it flared up and went out several times. Short flashes came one after another so quickly that the human eye could not follow it - several flashes seemed to the eye of one. And only the wonderful Federaren watch was able to dissect the moment, decompose the spark into several separate flashes.

Federaren counted the number of towers on his symbol. There were eight of them, and each subsequent one was a little weaker than the previous one. Eight flashes in twenty-five millionths of a second! This means that the spark consisted of individual sparks that lit up and died out every three millionths of a second.

So, from the note made by the bunny, Federaren read the story of the electric spark, a story that lasted only one moment from beginning to end.

Experiments continue

Federaren repeated his experience more than once. He took either a single Leyden jar or a whole battery of ten, fifteen and even twenty Leyden jars. When I saw the balls almost closely, I moved them apart by a whole centimeter or one and a half. He changed and washed the balls - he took iron, copper, lead, and gold. In one experiment both balls were made of the same metal, in others they were made of different ones. Federaren took the wires that went from the Leyden jars to the spark gap balls, either short and thick, or long and thin. And each time he photographed the reflection of the spark in the rotating mirror.

Using photographic SIM cards, he measured how long the spark lasted, learned how it flashes, burns and goes out.

The sparks came out of different fury, different lengths, different durations, but each state was not from just one flash, but from many. The flashes followed each other every few millionths of a second and became weaker and weaker until the spark died out.

Why does the electric charge make its way between the spark gap balls in more than one jump?

While thinking about this, Federsen remembered an article he had once read about the Leyden jar discharge. This article was written in 1853 by the English scientist William Thomson. Thomson did not do any experiments with the Leyden jar, and he did not do any experiments at all. But he was a very skilled mathematician. He knew the physical laws that govern electric current and knew how to derive mathematical consequences from these laws. And so Thomson tried, using calculations, to establish what actually happens to the electric charge when a Leyden jar is charged.

Thomson's calculations showed: an electric charge, having been added along the wires from the cup of the Leyden jar to the spark gap, makes a jump from ball to ball and rushes along the wire again into the jar, but into another cup. Thus, both charges, positive and negative, change places.

The negative charge, which was initially, say, in the inner cup of the jar, will fly from the opening to the outer one, and the positive one from the outer one to the inner one. Thus, the bank will not be discharged, but will only be charged anew, and the electric current will again rush to the spark gap, but in the opposite direction. The electric charge will run back and forth, jumping from one ball to another, again from the second to the first and again from the first to the second.

“So that’s what it’s all about!” thought Federsen. That is why the device noted not one spark in the image, but eight. This means that electric current passed from ball to ball eight times, first in one direction, then in the other. And every time a brilliant flash appears in the spark gap. The flash ended, and immediately another appeared - this was again the electric current breaking through, but in the opposite direction. Flash after flash sparkled in the narrow space between the balls while the life of the spark continued, and with each flash the direction of the current changed. The current ran in one direction - a flash, ran back - a new flash.

Federsen's rotating mirror confirmed Thomson's agreement: an electric spark is a small alternating electric current. After minute intervals, every few millionths of a second, the current changes its direction.

So the rotating mirror helped Federsen study the nature of the electric spark.
