The Nobel Prize in Physics in 1985 was awarded to a scientist from Germany, Professor Claus von Klititzn, for a series of works on semiconductor physics. The main result of these works was the discovery of the quantum Hall effect - an effect in which the classical Hall effect, known for many years, was “involved”, the quantum laws of behavior of electronic systems, the formation in the middle of the 20th century, the untrained properties of “two-dimensional” structures, first discovered in 1966, and MOS transducers, the creation of which dates back to the 1900s. The discovery of K. von Klititzg interested physicists of various specialties, since it opened up new possibilities for measuring fundamental constants and creating a new resistance standard.

What is the essence of this discovery? To understand this, we will teach you from the discovery of the hundred-day period.

Classic Hall effect

In 1879, American physicist Edwin Hall discovered that if a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field perpendicular to the current, then an electrostatic field will arise in the conductor, directed perpendicular to the magnetic field and current (figure 1).

We can calculate the voltage of this field. The current in a conductor is the direction of movement of the electrons. The magnitude of the current and the average speed of this movement are related by the relation $v = I/(nes)$, where $n$ is the concentration of electrons in the conductor, $e$ is the electron charge, $s$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the conductor. Let us place a rectangular sample through which current $I$ flows in a magnetic field with induction $B$ (see figure 1). The Lorentz force $F = evB$, directed perpendicularly $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$, acts on moving electrons from the magnetic field.

As a result of the action of this force, electrons will mix towards the side face of the sample, so that an excess of electrons will appear on one of the faces, and a deficiency on the other. This leads to the appearance of an electrostatic field perpendicular to the current - it is called the Hall field. The movement of electrons across the current will stop as soon as the force acting on the electron from the Hall field becomes equal to the Lorentz force: $E_X e = evB$, whence $E_X = vB$, or

$$E_X = \frac{IB}{nes}.$$

The Hall field creates a potential difference on the conductor faces equal to

$$U_X = E_X b = \frac{IBb}{nes} = \frac{IB}{nea},$$

where $b$ is the width of the conductor, $a$ is its thickness. This potential difference on the faces can be directly changed in experiments, and also changes to very important information about the number of electrons per unit volume of the conductor, since the ratio of the Hall voltage $U_X$ to the current $I$ passed through the conductor, as follows from the above calculations, depends on $n$. This relationship is explained by the direction perpendicular to the magnetic field and current (figure 1).

As can be seen from formula (1), $R_X$ is proportional to the magnetic induction

The presence of a magnetic field also affects the total resistance of the conductor (the resistance to the flow of current) - it, as a rule, increases.

This can be done qualitatively in the following way. The cause of electrical resistance is the scattering of electrons by obstacles encountered along their path - on impurities, on defects in the crystal lattice, etc. When encountering obstacles, the directions of electron velocities can change in a derivative way. However, in the intervals between collisions, the electrons will be accelerated by the electric field - the projection of their velocities along the calculated field. Thus, a non-zero average speed of electron drift along the field is established in the conductor—current flows through the conductor. In the presence of a magnetic field by tractors, the movements of electrons between collisions are bent, and as a result, the speed of their drift decreases significantly. Consequently, at the same potential difference, the current decreases, i.e., the calculation of resistance. It should be noted, however, that the cold field partially weakens the described effect (and sometimes compensates for it completely).

During the years that have passed since the discovery of the Hall effect, much has been done in its study. It was found out how the cold resistance is affected by the presence of impurities in the sample material, the presence of carriers of a different sign, temperature changes, etc. However, formula (1) all this time, basically, did not cause any problems for physicists.

And in 1980, K. von Klitsing discovered that in special systems called two-dimensional electronic layers, the dependence of cold resistance on the magnetic field induction and electron concentration is not at all the same as prescribed by formula (1). And the resistance to current in a two-dimensional electron layer behaves in a special way in the presence of a magnetic field.

The reasons for these “unlearning errors” lie in the properties of the object itself on which they are observed.

Quantum reasons for the two-dimensional layer

From the point of view of quantum mechanics, however, such a system of electrons should have fundamentally new properties. You probably know that the energy of a microparticle that moves in a limited region of space, according to quantum theory, cannot be arbitrary. For example, when electrons move in an atom, the energy of electrons can only take certain values ​​$W_0$, $W_1$, $W_2$, ... and cannot take intermediate ones. In this case, they say that allowed energy levels $W_0$, $W_1$, $W_2$, .... The same happens with electrons rotating in a magnetic field. It turns out that their energy can only be equal to a discrete value

$$W_i = \left( i + \frac{1}{2} \right) \frac{\omega}{2\pi},$$

(5)

where $i$ is an integer, and $\omega$ is the rotation frequency, determined by formula (3). These permitted energy levels are commonly referred to as Landdu.

According to the laws of quantum mechanics, electrons located in the same place in space must want to be different in some way - have different energies, different spins, etc. Let's try to calculate how many electrons with energy $W_0$ ($i=0$) - this is the lower Landdu level - can fit per unit area. Let the electron rotate in a circle of radius $r$ with frequency $\omega$, determined by formula (3). Its kinetic energy is $m\omega^2r^2/2$. Let's equate this energy $W_0$:

$$\frac{h\omega}{4\pi} = \frac{m\omega^2r^2}{2},$$

or $$\frac{h}{4\pi} \frac{Be}{m} = \frac{m}{2} \frac{B^2e^2}{m^2} r^2.$$

From here there is one electron with energy $W_0$: $\pi r^2 = h/(2Be)$. The same area can accommodate another electron with the same energy $W_0$, but with an oppositely directed spin - this is what the Pauli principle says (the same Pauli principle that is involved in the “dispersal” of electrons in an atom). Other electrons with energy $W_0$ can be placed nearby, so that the “orbits” of the electrons do not overlap. Thus, a $n = \frac{2}{\pi r^2} = \frac{4Be}{h}$ electron with an energy of $W_0$ can be placed per unit area.
