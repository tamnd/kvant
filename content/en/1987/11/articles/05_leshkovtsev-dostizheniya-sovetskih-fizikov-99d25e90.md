ACHIEVEMENTS
SOVIET PHYSICISTS

Soviet physics is very young. But the successes achieved in such a short period of time convincingly testify to her enormous scientific maturity and ability to solve complex problems of the modern natural sciences.

Currently in our country there are more than a hundred special physical institutes of the USSR Academy of Sciences, republican academies, as well as non-specialist departments. The middle ones are the world-famous physical institutes named after I. N. Lebedev, the Institute of Physics and Technology named after A. F. Ioffe, the Institute of Physical Problems named after S. I. Vavilov, the Institute of Atomic Energy named after I. V. Kurchatov, the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna. Soviet science has outstanding achievements in many areas of modern physics. These include, for example, nuclear physics, plasma physics and controlled thermonuclear reactions, semiconductor physics, low temperature physics, quantum electronics, high pressure physics. Our scientists successfully work in numerous program areas of modern science. Chemical and biological physics, physics of the Earth, atmosphere, outer space, physics of the World Ocean and science are represented in our country by many world-famous scientists.

Soviet physicists carried out so much fundamental scientific research that any attempt to even briefly talk about each of these works would inevitably lead to a dry list of a huge number of individual names and facts that are obscure to a wide range of readers. Therefore, we will tell here only about some of the largest achievements of our physical scientists.

A brief chronicle of the outstanding discoveries of modern physics includes mainly “nominal” discoveries. Of course, not every major discovery is subsequently acquired by the scientists who made it. Therefore, the list given here does not cover even the largest discoveries made by our scientists in the field of physics. But we hope that a short list of “name” discoveries allows our readers to feel how great the contribution of modern scientists to the treasury of world science is.

The general theory of relativity, created by Einstein, contains equations that characterize the state of the Universe. Einstein found a static (independent of time) solution that describes a closed, non-developing Universe. Soon, Professor Alexander Alexandrovich Fridman received two new solutions to the basic equation of general relativity. Both of them describe a Universe that is continuously expanding. Subsequently, the “Friedmann model of the expansion of the Universe” received experimental confirmation and is now generally accepted.

It is known that the strength of a solid body, determined experimentally, is many times less than its value calculated theoretically. The reason for this difference was first revealed by academician Abram Fedorovich Ioffe. It turned out that various defects in the crystal structure that were not taken into account theoretically, for example, microsoptic requirements on the surface of crystals, are to blame for the premature destruction of materials. By immersing a rock salt crystal in water and dissolving the surface layer, Ioffe will achieve an increase in strength hundreds of times. The simplification of crystals upon dissolution of the surface layer is called the “Ioffe effect.”

Academician Vladimir Aleksandrovich Fock has been working on its development since the advent of quantum mechanics. He generalized the basic equation of this theory to the case of a magnetic field and obtained the relativistic equation of quantum mechanics, known to physicists as the “Fock-Klein equation.” Subsequently, Academician V. A. Fok, independently of the English physicist Hartree, created a method for calculating the spectra of atoms containing a large number of electrons, and even the spectra of complex molecules. This method went down in the history of physics under the name “Hartree-Fock method”. Before the creation of this method, the calculation of complex atoms was a completely inaccessible task for quantum mechanics.

Academician Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam theoretically showed that microcoptic inhomogeneities in the density of matter, arising as a result of the thermal movement of molecules, change the spectrum of scattered light, adding new spectral lines to it, which are very difficult to observe. (These lines were first discovered by corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Evgeniy Fedorovich Gross.) The phenomenon as a result of which they arise was called the “Mandelshtam-Brillouzon effect,” since a similar prediction was made somewhat later by the French physicist Brillouzon.

Investigating the scattering of light in quartz crystals, academicians Leonid

Isaakovich Mandelstam and Grigory Samuilovich Landsberg discovered additional spectral lines in the spectrum of scattered light that did not belong to the light incident on the crystal. These spectral lines arose as a result of the interaction of incident light with individual molecules forming a crystal lattice. Somewhat later, a similar phenomenon was discovered by the Indian physicist Raman while studying the scattering of light in liquid benzene. It is called the “Mandelshtam-Landsberg-Raman effect” or “raman scattering of light.” Raman scattering has become a powerful tool for studying the structure of molecules.

Academician Pyotr Leonidovich Kapits, using the original installation he designed to create super-strong magnetic fields of the installation, that in such fields the change in the electrical resistance of metals does not grow proportionally to the square of the magnetic induction, as expected based on the then existing theories, but is a linear function of magnetic induction. This necessary phenomenon, which has not been explained for about 30 years, is called “Kapitsa’s law.”

Another phenomenon discovered by him is associated with the name of Academician P. L. Kapitsa. While studying the properties of the liquid gel, he discovered that at temperatures below 2.19 K, the gel flows through a narrow neck practically without any pressure.

Professor Lev Vasilievich Shubnikov, working in the laboratory of the Dutch physicist de Haas, discovered that the electrical resistance of a vysut placed in a magnetic field at the temperature of a liquid gel, as the magnetic induction of the field increases, it either returns or kills (as they say, the resistance of the vysut oscillates). This “Shubnikov-de Haas effect” is widely used to study the energy spectrum of electrons in metals.

Professor of Moscow University Anatoly Aleksadrovich Blasov was the first to derive an equation describing the behavior of a rarefied inoculated gas (plasma), in which new loaded particles do not appear. It entered science under the name “Blasov’s equation.”

Academician Lev Davidovich Landdu theoretically showed that free electrons in a metal under the influence of an external magnetic field can only move along strictly defined (as physicists say - quantum) trajectories. This means that special energy levels arise in a magnetic field, called “Landdu levels”.

From the same theory it followed that the action of a magnetic field on free electrons in a metal leads to the appearance of an additional magnetic moment directed in the direction opposite to the external magnetic field. Such a moment is called diamagnetic, and the described phenomenon is called “Landdu diamagnetism.” Note that the same theory explained the Shubnikov-de Haas effect.

USSR Alexey Alekseevich Abrikovov and Lev Petrovich Gorkov created the theory of superconducting alloys. This theory is known throughout the world as the “GLAG theory” (Ginsburg-Landu-Abrikovov-Gorkov).

The Microsoppech theory of superconductivity, which describes in detail the mechanism of this mysterious phenomenon, was created by American physicists Bardeen, Cooper and Schriefer and, independently of them, academician Nikolai Nikolaevets Bogolyubov. It is called the “Bardeen-Cooper-Schriefer-Bogolyubov theory of superconductivity.”

Academician Isaac Konstantinovich Kion, together with M. M. Noskov, discovered the fotoelectromagnetic effect, showing in experiments that when a semiconductor is illuminated in a magnetic field, an electromotive force arises in it. Light quanta lead to the appearance of current carriers - free electrons and holes, which diffuse along the direction of incidence of light, and the magnetic field separates them in opposite directions. This phenomenon has long been called the “Kionne-Noskov effect.”

Academician Igor Evgenievich Tump gave a strict choice of formula that describes light on free electrons. She played an important role in the development of quantum electrodynamics. It went down in the history of physics under the name “Tamm-Klein-Nishina formulas”.

I. E. Tamp developed a method for calculating the interaction of particles moving at speeds close to the speed of light (relativistic particles), which entered theoretical physics as the “Tamm-Dankov” method.

Academician Igor Evgenievich Tamp theoretically predicted the existence of free energy states (“traps”) on the surface of semiconductor crystals. They entered into a science called “surface levels of Tamm.”

Academician Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov and Pavel Alekseevich Cherenkov discovered the “superluminal electron” effect. It turned out that in liquid and solid media electrons can move at a speed greater than the speed of light in these media (but, of course, not greater than the speed of light in emptiness!). Electrons can burn the light they emit. This effect, called the “Vavilov-Cherenkov effect,” has found a number of valuable practical applications, in particular for recording fast loaded particles (so-called Cherenkov particle counters). The theory obliging the Vavilov-Cherenkov effect was created by academicians Igor Evgenievich Tump and Ilya Mikhailovich Frank. For the discovery and discovery of this phenomenon, P. A. Cherenkov, I. E. Tampa and I. M. Frank were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1958.

Academician Vladimir Iosifovich Veksler discovered the principle of autophasing of particles accelerated in cyclic accelerators, which bears his name. The work of all modern cyclic accelerators - synchrotrons, phasotrons and synchrophasotrons - is based on it. Somewhat later, independently of Wexler, the same principle was discovered by the American physicist MacMillan. For this discovery, both of them were awarded an international scientific prize.

Complex molecules of the organization of substances have spectra consisting of wide continuous spectral bands. Professor Eduard Vladimirovich Shpolsky showed that by dissolving organic substances in specially selected solvents and cooling the resulting solutions to very low temperatures, it is possible to obtain absorption spectra consisting of very fine spectral lines that are uniquely related to the structure and properties of the molecules. Thus the new field of molecular optics was created. This phenomenon is widely called the “Shpolsky effect” in the scientific literature.

Semiconductors, like other substances, can absorb light whose wavelengths are within certain limits. For light with longer wavelengths they are transparent. Thus, there is a limit to the wavelength at which the semiconductor stops absorbing light. Academician Leonid Veniaminovich Kelysh theoretically said that under the influence of an external electric field this boundary should mix towards longer wavelengths. A similar prediction was made by the German physicist Franz. Soon, Professor Viktor Sergeevich Vavilov received experimental confirmation of this phenomenon, now known as the “Kelysh-Franz effect.”

Academician Sergei Nikolaevich Vernov and the American physicist Van Allen, using scientific equipment installed on the first artificial satellites, independently discovered the existence of the Earth's radiation bands. It turned out that the Earth is surrounded by several layers with an increased density of particle pollution. These zones are called “Vernov-Van Allen radiation floors.”

Academicians Alexander Mikhailovich Prokhorov and Nikolai Gennadievich Basov developed the foundations of a new field of physics - quantum electronics and created the first quantum molecular generator using aminac molecules. For these outstanding works, in 1964 they, together with the American physicist Townes, were awarded the Nobel Prize.

Scientists around the world who study physical processes in hot plasma widely use installations called the unusual Russian word “TOKAMAK”. This is the abbreviated name for “toroidal chambers in a magnetic field” - Soviet thermonuclear installations developed and built at the Institute of Atomic Energy named after I.V. Kurchatov under the leadership of Academician Lev Andreevich Artsimovich.
