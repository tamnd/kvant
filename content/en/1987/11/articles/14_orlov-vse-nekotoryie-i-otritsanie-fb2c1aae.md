AND DENIAL

A. I. ORLOV

I recently had an interesting conversation with sixth-grader Alik, center right of our yard’s football team.

- What, Alik, are you running to training? In winter, perhaps, you will change your football to hockey?

- No way in the world! - Alik exclaimed passionately. — In winter, you need to occupy yourself, and not run after the puck. All hockey players study poorly, but football players study much better!

- Why did you decide that?

- And what? Everyone on our team thinks so, I’m not alone.

- Isn’t it true that everyone you are talking about now is like the infamous monkeys from Kipling’s book “Muggly”? Do you remember, they shouted to the whole forest: “We are great! We are free! We are worthy of admiration! We all say that, so it's true! ..."

- Who is this monkey?! - Alik was indignant.

“I didn’t want to offend you, Alik, but you can’t prove anything by citing the opinions of many.” The earth is round, but in ancient times almost everyone thought it was flat. The Indians of the Sinu tribe, as their leader Mato Nazhin writes, even believed that the Earth was square, and this was already in the 19th century... So why are all hockey players poor students?

- Well, here is Seryozha Kukushkin. He plays hockey all the time and gets bad marks all the time.

- You reason like one Frenchman who said: “All Englishmen are short, plump and blackbie,” only because the only person he met looked like that. The name and this Frenchman evoke the laws of logic.

Alik thought about it, and I decided to help him. To begin with, I told this story.

“In one city I saw a sign on a house: “There are no doubles in our house.” There was no such table on the neighboring house. In your opinion, Alik, does this mean that everyone in the neighboring house was double?

“N-no,” Alik muttered uncertainly. Not necessarily. This only means that there are bad students there... At least one bad student... Or maybe everyone there is a bad student. No, I don’t know, I wasn’t there!

— But what does this phrase convey: “It is not true that among the guys there are doubles”?

- It’s like in the first house - everyone studies without failing.

“So it’s one of two things,” I said with satisfaction. “Either everyone is studying without failing grades, or there is at least one failing grade student in the house.” What about puck lovers?

And then it turned out that Alik could, after thinking, reason correctly.

- One of two things is true: either all hockey players study poorly, or not all - there are hockey players who study well.

- But even in the creative class, Alik, I know three excellent students who love to kick the puck. This means that the second is true: there are hockey players who study well.

- Well, of course!

- Then what will you introduce?

So, having tried to reason logically, Alik himself came to the correct conclusion.
We dealt with the doubles and hockey players relatively quickly. Let's try to understand how to reason in more complex cases. The first example is with multi-colored balls. Let's imagine that in urine (a small box) there can be, say, white and red balls. All balls are the same size and are indistinguishable at a glance. Someone dips their hand into the urine and takes out a ball. The ball turns out to be red, for example. What can you say about the color of the balls lying in urine?
Some will say: “All the balls are red.” In fact, this is not true: can one say that some (not necessarily all) balls are red? There may also be white balls. It may also be that the drawn ball is the only red one among the white ones.
A red ball was drawn. This means that the statement “All balls are white” is false. What's true? - “Some (at least one) of the balls are red.” Let's practice. Let each of the following statements be false.
1. All balls in urine are red.
2. Some balls in urine are red.
3. Some balls in urine are white.
4. All equilateral triangles are right triangles.
5. All the students in the class were at the meeting.
6. Some girls were at the meeting.
Formulate true statements.
And now the general scheme for solving all such problems is our “magic solution”. Let us have a set M of such and such objects (this could be several balls, or several books, or all the students of a certain class). Each of the elements of this set may or may not have some property A. For example, the ball may or may not be red; hockey - to be or not to be double.

The law is this: either a statement can be true or its negation. However, only one of two!
(In logic, this law is called the law of excluded middle.) Which statement we are using is either true or false.
Any statement B, or its negation (i.e. the statement: “Statement B is false”). Any hockey players are bad at tevers (possess property A). If this is false, then at least one hockey player is good (does not have property A).
So here's our diagram:
Approval
1. All items from M have property A.
2. Some items from M have the A property.
His denial
1. At least one object from M does not have property A.
2. All objects from M do not have property A.
Note that the statements “some objects have property A” and “at least one object (and perhaps all) have property A” mean the same thing in our scheme.
And now - the tasks.
Construct negations of the following statements:
7. All angles of a given hexagon are obtuse.
8. For any x from set A the inequality x² > 4 holds.
9. Some people are children.
10. For at least one x from set A there will be x² - 2x + 1 = 0.
11. All men are taller than 2 meters.
12. All prime numbers are odd.
13. For any prime p, the number 2ᵉ - 1 is also prime.
Let's return to urine, in which there are balls that are indistinguishable at a time. Balls can be white, red, blue, black.
14. It is known that not all balls in urine are white. Berho, is there a red ball there?
15. It is known that not all balls are the same color. Berho, is there at least one non-white ball in urine?
16. There are 10 white, 8 red, 11 black balls in urine. How many of them must be taken out so that you are sure to get a white ball? Why did you get balls of all three colors?
And one more task - just the last one.
17. There are 5 excellent students, 20 “good” students and 10 “B” students in the class. An excellent student can only get 5 for an answer, a “good” student can get 4 or 5, a poor student can get 3, 4 or 5. There is a new teacher in the class, he doesn’t know any of the students. How many people does he have to call to the board so that at least one A is sure to be given?
