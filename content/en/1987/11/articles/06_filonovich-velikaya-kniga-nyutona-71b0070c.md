The anniversary of Isaac Newton's "Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy" is celebrated all over the world as the most important event in cultural life. Only a few scientific works receive such an honor, and this fact alone speaks of the enormous significance of Newton’s work for the development of not only science, but also the entire civilization.

How was “Principles” created? What is the content of this book? What role did it play in the development of naturalgonium? This question is addressed in the article offered to the reader.

Beginning "Began"

Historians of science very rarely manage to establish exactly when and under what circumstances a scientist became interested in a particular article or book. "Principles" is a happy exception. We know a lot about the creation of this book and can even indicate the event that followed as the starting point in Newton’s work on it.

Time: August 1684. Location: Cambridge, England. Characters: Isaac Newton - member of the Royal Society of London (English Academy of Sciences), professor of mathematics and member of Trinity College, Cambridge University, and Edmund Halley - astronomer and mathematician, one of the secretaires of the Royal Society of London.

In 1684, at the time of Halley's visit, Newton occupied a prominent position in the scientific community of England. He is a recognized authority on issues of optics and mathematics. However, he lived and worked in isolation from other scientists, occasionally exchanging letters with him.

Young Halley (he was 28 years old at that time) came to Newton for advice. He was worried about a problem that many skilled mathematicians could not solve: what trajectory will the planet move if a force is constantly acting on it from the side of the Sun, inversely proportional to the square of the distance? Halley had already discussed this problem with his colleagues at the Royal Society of London, R. Hooke and C. Wren, and Hooke even claimed to know the solution. However, Hooke knew (or rather, had a presentiment) not the solution, but the answer - the trajectory would be an ellipse, but he could not provide the corresponding proof. It is interesting that when preparing the Elements, Newton obtained many of the results that he arrived at using differential and integral calculus (in Newton’s terminology, the method of fluxions), using the so-called “synthetic” method, based on a combination of geometric constructions and diametic communications. For 20th century readers, this form of presentation of the material makes the book very difficult to read. This was done... to facilitate the understanding of its content by contemporaries. Further, many years after the first edition of the Elements, Newton wrote that “in our time, for inexperienced people, the perception of Analiza [i.e. e. the method of fluxions - S.F.], with the help of which these provisions were found, is difficult.”

The same Halley provided great assistance in preparing the “Principles” for publication. He not only eliminated many typos, but also went to great lengths to improve the design of the book. According to the tradition of that time, if a book had a lot of illustrations, they were taken out of the text onto separate sheets - this made it easier to make clichés for printing. Halley insisted that figure be placed directly in the text, which, of course, made it easier to read. Note that this increased the cost of the publication, but Halley had to print “Principles” at his own expense, since the Royal Society was experiencing financial difficulties at that time.

Finally, in mid-1687, Newton's great book, Philosophia naturalis principia mathematica, was published.

Let's take a brief look at its contents.

Through the pages of "Started"

“Principles” opened with the author’s presentation, in which he appeared the goal facing him: the application of mathematics to natural fluxology, i.e., to physics. Next came the Definitions of magnitude and intelligibility, on which the subsequent presentation is based. Let us note that the development of these definitions was not at all easy. So, for example, Definition III reads as follows:

“The innate power of matter is the ability of resistance present in it, by which every individual body, insofar as it is presented to itself, maintains its state of rest or uniform motion around the lightning.”

On the one hand, it is very surprising that here Newton speaks about the most important property of bodies - inertia, and therefore, this definition reflects reality. On the other hand, in this place Newton attributes to all bodies “innate” the power of inERTII, which exists independently of the reporting system. This view of the forces of inertia was not subsequently accepted by physicists. We will present this definition in order to show with what difficulty the basic ideas of mechanics were given out: even the great Newoton made inaccuracies in their formulation.

In the Homily to Definitions, Newton speaks about the concepts of absolute space and absolute time:

“Absolute, true mathematical time in itself and in itself

The clear introduction to absolute space and absolute time was associated with Newton’s general views on the structure of the world: he believed that there was a fixed center of the Universe and even proposed experiments to prove this assumption. At the same time, the use of these understandable concepts allowed, to a first approximation, to solve many problems in mechanics correctly and, importantly, simply. It is worth noting that during the first formulation of a consistent theory of the motion of bodies, such ideas about space and time were inevitable. Only two centuries later, as a result of the development of physics, scientists within the framework of the relative theory came to ideas about space and time that more fully reflect reality.
Next come “Axims or Laws of Motion.” Here's how Newton formulates them:

“LAW I. Every body continues to be maintained in its state of rest or uniform motion and motion until and unless it is able to change this state by applied forces.

LAW II. The change in momentum is proportional to the force applied to the movement and occurs in the direction of the straight line along which this force acts.

LAW III. An action always has an equal and opposite reaction; otherwise: the actions of two bodies on each other are equal and directed in opposite directions.”
This ends the introductory part of the “Beginnings” and begins the presentation of the main material. It is divided into three books (parts).

Book one, entitled “On the Motion of Bodies,” begins with a consideration of mathematical theorems, on the basis of which further exposition is built. Following the mathematical introduction, the solution of many problems related to the movement of bodies under the action of so-called central forces, that is, forces acting in a straight line connecting the body with a certain center, is applied. Most of the problems have the most vague relation to celestial mechanics. The task is considered in strict sequence, so that the results obtained earlier are used to solve the following problems. Because of this, it is difficult to follow Newton's reasoning by choosing a theorem from the middle of the book. This applies, for example, to Problem IX, which Newton discussed with Halley: “Assuming that the centripetal force is inversely proportional to the squares of the distances of places from the center and that the absolute magnitude of this force is known, it is required to find the curve that will follow a body leaving a given place with a given speed.”

So that the reader can get an idea of ​​the method of presentation used by Newton, we will give a proof of Theorem I from Section II “On the determination of centripetal forces,” which is important for justifying Kepler’s second law (law of areas). Its formulation is as follows: “The areas described by radii drawn from the revolving body to the stationary center of forces lie in the same plane and are proportional to the times of their description.”

Proof of the theorem.

“Divide time into equal intervals, and during the first of them let the body, by inertia, describe the straight line AB. If it were not subject to any action, then, continuing to walk in a straight line, it would come to c (no to law I), having passed a path Bc equal to AB, and then described by the radii AS, BS, cS drawn to the center of forces S, the areas ASB and BSc are equal. In reality, when the body came to B, the centripetal force acted on it with one, but forgotten force, the consequence of which only deviates from the straight line Ac and will continue its path along the straight line BC. Let us draw a straight line сС parallel to BS until it meets BC at point C, then by the end of the second time interval the body (by consequence of the first laws)*) will pass to point C, which lies in the same plane as triangle ASB. Let's conduct SC; by the parallelism of SB and Cc, the areas of triangles SBC and SBc will be equal to each other, and therefore they are equal to the area of ​​triangle SAB.

Reasoning in a similar way, we see that if the central force acts sequentially at points C, D, E, etc. and forces the body to describe straight lines CD, DE, EF, etc., then all these straight lines will lie in the same plane, and the areas of triangles SCD and SBC, SDE and SCD, SEF and SDE will be equal to each other. Consequently, equal areas located in a fixed plane are described at equal times. Slavaya, we get that any sums of these areas, like SADS and SAFS, will be related as the times of their description. Then increasing the number of triangles and decreasing their height infinitely, we obtain that in the limit of the perimeter ADF there will be a curved line and the central force, which the body deviates all the time from the tangent to this curve, acts incessantly, while the areas SADS and SAFS, described by the radius, remaining constantly proportional to the times of description of their description, will continue to be proportional to these times.”

In this style, task by task is considered. The last section of the first book presents elements of corpuscular optics, in particular, the law of light refraction is discussed on the basis of the laws of mechanics.
