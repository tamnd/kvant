M1052. Prove that out of n quadrilaterals cut off from a convex n-gon by diagonals, no more than n/2 can be circumscribed circles. Give an example of an octagon that has 4 such quadrilaterals.

If it were possible to count more than n/2 of the described quadrilaterals, then among them there would be two neighboring ones - having two common sides. Let's denote them ABCD and BCDE (Fig. 1). For each of them, the sums of opposite sides are equal:
$$AB + CD = BC + AD, \quad BC + DE = CD + BE.$$
From here we get that
$$AB + DE = AD + BE.$$ (*)

The original n-gon is convex, so its diagonals AD and BE intersect at some point P. By

triangle inequality

$$AD + BE = AP + BP + PD + PE > AB + DE,$$

which contradicts (*).

To construct the required octagon, we describe an equilateral trapezoid $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ around a circle with angles of 45° at the base $A_1A_4$, and then build it up to an octagon $A_1A_2...A_8$ as shown in figure 2. Analogously, an n-gon is constructed, from which we can count the diagonals [n/2] of the described quadrangles.

M1053. Prove that in the sequence of Fibonacci numbers 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, ..., where each number is equal to the sum of the two previous ones, at $m \geq 2$ there will be at least 4 and at most 5 m-digit numbers.

$$u_n = \frac{\tau^{n+1} - (-1/\tau)^{n+1}}{\sqrt{5}},$$

where $\tau = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$.

Let $u_n$ be the Fibonacci sequence: $u_0 = 1, u_1 = 1,$

$$u_{n+1} = u_n + u_{n-1}.$$

(1)

We come across the following simple estimate for the ratio $r_n = u_{n+1}/u_n$ of neighboring Fibonacci numbers: starting from $u_3/u_2 = 3/2$, the ratio $r_n$ is no less than $3/2 = 1,5$ and no more than $5/3 = 1,66... < 1,7$.

It is easy to prove using the method of mathematical induction. By virtue of (1)

$$r_n = \frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n} = 1 + \frac{u_{n-1}}{u_n} = 1 + \frac{1}{r_{n-1}}.$$

Therefore, if

$$\frac{3}{2} \leq r_{n-1} \leq \frac{5}{3},$$

(2)

i.e. $2/3 \geq 1/r_{n-1} \geq 3/5$, then $r_n$ is estimated from above by the number $1+2/3=5/3$, and from below by the number $1+3/5=8/5>3/2$. And since estimate (2) is true for $n=3$, it is also true for all $n \geq 3$.

Let $u_k$ be the smallest $m$-digit Fibonacci number, $m \geq 2$. Then $u_k \geq 10^{m-1}, u_{k+1} \geq 1,5u_k,$

$$u_{k+2} = u_{k+1} + u_k \geq 2,5u_k,$$

$$u_{k+3} \geq (2,5+1,5)u_k = 4u_k,$$

$$u_{k+4} \geq (4+2,5)u_k = 6,5u_k,$$

and then $u_{k+5} \geq 10,5u_k > 10^m$ is no less than a $(m+1)$-digit number. Thus, there are no more than 5 $m$-digit Fibonacci numbers.

On the other hand, $u_{k-1} < 10^{m-1}, u_k < 1,7u_{k-1}$ and similarly to the previous one we get that $u_{k+3} < 7,1u_{k-1} < 10^m$. Therefore, there are at least 4 $m$-digit Fibonacci numbers.

You can solve this problem differently, use the explicit formula for Fibonacci numbers *) given in the margin.

F1063. A pedestrian needs to get from field point $A$ to field point $B$ in the shortest possible time, the distance between which is 1300 m. The field is crossed by a straight road so that point $A$ is located at a distance of 600 m from it, and point $B$ is at a distance of 100 m. The speed of movement of a pedestrian across the field is 3 km/h, and along the road - 6 km/h. Which path should the pedestrian take?

What is the minimum time? The distance occurs when points $A$ and $B$ lie on the same side of the road and when they lie on opposite sides of the road.

To solve the problem, we will construct the front of the reachability region - the boundary of the region at any point of which one can be in a given period of time.*)

We will construct this boundary for the moment in time when it passes through the point $B$ (the distance is the case when points $A$ and $B$ lie on opposite sides of the road). For this case, we draw through the point $B$ (see figure) a straight line forming an angle $\varphi$ with the road such that $\sin \varphi = v/u$, where $v$ is the speed of movement along the field, $u$ is the speed of movement along the road. From the data of the problem it follows that $\sin \varphi = 1/2$, $\varphi = \pi/6$. Let this straight line intersect the road at point $D$. From point $A$ we draw a circle tangent to line $BD$. From the data of the problem it follows that point $B$ lies between the point of tangency $C$ and point $D$.

So, at the moment the front passes the point $B$ of interest to us, the front section is represented by a circle with radius $AC$ and a tangent segment $CD$. **) Theler can easily understand that the optimal trajectory from $A$ to $B$ will be the broken line $AFGB$, and $CFD = BGD = \pi/2 - \varphi = \pi/3$.

Similarly, if points $A$ and $B(B')$ lie on the same side of the road, then the optimal trajectory will be the broken line $AFKB'$, with $B'KD = \pi/2 - \varphi = \pi/3$. Note that with a different mutual arrangement of points $A$, $B$ and the road (at other distances), the optimal trajectory can be a straight segment without road sections (in this case, point $B$ lies on a section of the front, which is part of a circle with radius $AC$).

Teler will find the minimum time of movement from point $A$ to point $B$. If the points lie on opposite sides of the road -

$$t_{\min} = \frac{AF}{v} + \frac{FG}{u} + \frac{GB}{v} \approx 23,5 \text{ мин.}$$

If points $A$ and $B(B')$ lie on the same side of the road -

$$t_{\min} = \frac{AF}{v} + \frac{FK}{u} + \frac{KB'}{v} \approx 24,6 \text{ мин.}$$

S. S. Krotov

F1064. The thread is attached to and wound around the rim of a heavy object. If the other end of the thread is attached to the ceiling and the object is released, then it will move downward, healing and unwinding the thread. Let three such objects be connected “in series,” that is, the end of the thread of the next obus is attached to the axis of the previous one (Fig. 1). All objects were simultaneously released, and they began to move. Determine with what acceleration the upper volume moves.

With what acceleration will the upper object move if the number of objects connected in series is very large?

Assume that the mass of the hoop is evenly distributed along its rim, the knitting needles are not weightless, the threads are not weightless and inextensible, there is no trench.

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

Let the system contain $N$ sequentially connected objects, as shown in figure 2. Let’s process them from top to bottom. The hoop numbered $k$ is acted upward by the tension force of the $k$ thread, and downward by the force of gravity and the tension force of the $(k+1)$ thread. Let us write down the equation of motion for the $k$-th object:

$$mg + T_{k+1} - T_k = ma_k,$$

(1)

where $a_k$ is the projection of the acceleration of the center of mass of the object onto the direction of movement, $m$ is the mass of the object, $T_k$ and $T_{k+1}$ are the tension forces of the thread.

A similar equation can be written for any volume. In this case, $T_{N+1} = 0$, since there is simply no thread $(N+1)$. Thus, equations (1) written for $k=1\div N$ represent a system of $N$ equations with $2N$ unknowns $a_k$ and $T_k$.

Another $N$ equations can be obtained using the law of conservation of energy.

During the time $t$ after the start of movement $k$, the object with acceleration $a_k$, dropped to a distance $s_k$, acquiring a translational speed $v_k$ and an angular velocity of rotation around the object $\omega_k$. These quantities are related by the relations

$$s_k = a_k t^2 / 2, \quad v_k = a_k t, \quad \omega_k R = v_k - v_{k-1},$$

(2)

where $R$ is the radius of the object. Let's calculate the kinetic energy of the volume that it has arrived at at time $t$. To do this, we select two small pieces of the same mass $\Delta m$, located on the same diameter (Fig. 3). The speed of each piece is the sum of the speed $v_k$ of its translational movement and the linear speed of rotational movement around the center of the object $(\bar{u} \text{ и } -\bar{u}, u = \omega_k R)$. The total kinetic energy of these pieces is equal to

$$\Delta m (\bar{v}_k + \bar{u})^2 / 2 + \Delta m (\bar{v}_k - \bar{u})^2 / 2 = 2 (\Delta m v_k^2 / 2 + \Delta mu^2 / 2).$$

The kinetic energy of the entire volume is equal to the sum of the energies of all such pairs, i.e., it is equal to

$$mv_k^2 / 2 + m (\omega_k R)^2 / 2.$$

(3)

This energy is equal to the work of all forces acting on the object. The work of forces $mg$ and $T_{k+1}$ is positive and equal to $(mg + T_{k+1}) s_k$. The work of forces $T_k$ is negative and equal to $(-T_k) s_{k-1}$ (since the point of application of the force moved downward by a distance $s_{k-1}$). That's why

$$mv_k^2 / 2 + m (\omega_k R)^2 / 2 = (mg + T_{k-1}) s_k - T_k s_{k-1}.$$

From this equation, taking into account (1) and (2), after transformation we obtain:

$$T_k = m (a_k - a_{k-1}), \quad k = 1 \div N.$$

(4)

Equation (3), expressing the tension force of the thread through acceleration, is valid for any thread, and therefore, (3), like (1), is a system of $N$ equations with $2N$ unknowns. Substituting from this system expressions for tension forces in the system of equation (1), the final result is:

$$-a_{k-1} + 3a_k - a_{k+1} = g, \quad k = 1 \div N.$$

In this system, the equation should be considered $a_0 = 0$, since this is the acceleration of the suspension point of the first volume, $a_{N+1} = a_N$, since $T_{N+1} = 0$ (($N+1$) there is no thread).

Let us now write in expanded form the system of equation (4) for three objects ($N=3$):

$$\begin{align*}
3a_1 - a_2 &= g, \\
-a_1 + 3a_2 - a_3 &= g, \\
-a_2 + 2a_3 &= g.
\end{align*}$$

Solving this system, we get

$$a_1 = \frac{8}{13} g; \quad a_2 = \frac{11}{13} g; \quad a_3 = \frac{12}{13} g.$$

Note that system (4) can be solved for any finite value of $N$. It is clear that as $N$ increases, the “difficulty” of the calculation increases. What if $N \to \infty$? Here we will carry out arguments that are valid specifically for the case $N \to \infty$.

Let's assume that the acceleration of the first hoop is $a_1 = \chi g$, (5)

where $\chi$ is a certain number that we need to determine. Let us now move on to the reporting system, moving with acceleration $a_1$. In this system, describing the movement of all hoops, starting from the second, is a task similar to our original one, with the only difference that the movement occurs in a gravitational field with

$$g' = g - a_1 = g(1 - \chi)$$

and the total number of hoops decreased by one. Since there are still a lot of hoops, it is natural to assume that the acceleration of the upper (second) hoop is equal to

$$a'_1 = \chi g'.$$

Therefore, in a stationary reporting system, the acceleration of the second hoop is equal to

$$a_2 = a'_1 + a_1 = \chi g' + \chi g = \chi(g' + g) = \chi(g(1 - \chi) + g) = g(2 - \chi).$$ (6)

Substituting expressions (5) and (6) for $a_1$ and $a_2$ into the first equation of system (4), we find $\chi$:

$$\chi^2 + \chi - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \chi = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2}.$$

Thus, the acceleration of the first hoop for a sufficiently large $N$ is equal to

$$a_1 = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2} g.$$ (7)

Note that already at $N = 3$ expression (7) is valid with an accuracy of 0.5%, and for $N = 10$ - with an accuracy of $10^{-6}$%.

E. N. Yunosov, I. V. Yaminsky

F1065. A saucepan containing 1 liter of water cannot be brought to a boil using a heated power of 100 W. Determine how long it will take for the water to cool by one degree if you turn off the heated one.

This is a very simple task - from the condition it is clear that the power of the heater is equal to the power leaving the pan of water in the surrounding space (the temperature of the water does not change over time). This means that if the heated one is turned off, the power output is 100 W and the water cools by one degree during the time

$$\tau = \frac{cm \cdot \Delta T}{P} = 4,2 \cdot 10^3 \frac{\text{Дж}}{\text{кг} \cdot \text{К}} \cdot 1 \text{ кг} \cdot \frac{1\text{K}}{100\text{Вт}} = 42\text{с}.$$

A. R. Zilberman

F1066. Let us assume that as a result of strong fires in the upper layers of the atmosphere a layer of sugar will appear, absorbing almost all of the solar radiation that falls on it. What will the average temperature of the Earth become if it is now 300 K?
