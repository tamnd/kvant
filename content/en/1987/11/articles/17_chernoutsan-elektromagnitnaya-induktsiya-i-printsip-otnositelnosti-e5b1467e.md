Farad himself thought it was perfection of nature, that both options are described by the same law. However, a close analysis shows that such a system is far from very good. Let's try to understand this issue in more detail.

When a circuit moves in a magnetic field that does not change with time, the role of an external force generating a current in the circuit is played by the Lorentz force, which acts from the magnetic field on any moving charge. Let us recall an example known to you (“Physics 9”, § 95). Let a rectangular circuit be placed in a uniform magnetic field with induction $B$, the plane of which is perepredcular to the induction line (Fig. 1). On one side, the contour is a jumper with sliding contacts. If you move the jumper at a speed $v$, then an induced emf $E = Bvl$ will appear in it (l is the length of the jumper). Indeed, the free charge $q$ in the moving jumper is acted upon by the Lorentz force $F = qvB$ (the direction of the force is indicated by the arrow); correspond to this side of the EMF force

$$E = \frac{A}{q} = \frac{Fl}{q} = \frac{qvBl}{q} = Bvl$$

where $A$ is the work done on the side of the force along the length of the jumper. Let's compare this result with the rate of change of magnetic flux:

$$\left| \frac{\Phi}{l} \right| = \left| \frac{\Phi(BS)}{l} \right| = \left| \frac{\Phi(Bxl)}{l} \right| = Bvl$$

It is also easy to check that the sign of the resulting EMF corresponds to the Lenz rule.

The analyzed example shows that in the case of the movement of conductors in a constant magnetic field, the occurrence of an induction current does not represent a fundamentally new physical phenomenon.

completely different physical phenomena. In a constant magnetic field, the induced current in a moving circuit is caused by the magnetic field itself, and a changing magnetic field generates an output electric field, which causes charges to move along the stationary circuit. So why is the law of electromagnetic induction the same for these cases? What is this amazing coincidence? It turns out that this “coincidence” reveals a deep connection between the theory of the electromagnetic field and the special theory of relativity, which is based on Einstein’s principle of relativity. (The basic ideas of the special theory of relativity were formulated by Einstein in 1905.) According to this principle, all natural phenomena (not only the laws of mechanics, as follows from Galileo’s principle of relativity) should occur equally in all inertial reporting systems. An important consequence of this nature is, in particular, that it is impossible to say unambiguously, regardless of the reporting system, what fields exist in the surrounding space.

Let us consider as an example the interaction of two electrons from the point of view of two observables (Fig. 2). Observable A states that moving charges create both an electric and a magnetic field around themselves, and in addition to Coulomb repulsion, magnetic acceptance acts between them (as between parallel currents). observed B does not agree with him and claims that there is no magnetic field, since the charges are replaced, and only Coulomb repulsion acts between the electrons. Let us return, however, to Faraday's law and imagine the following experiment. We will refer to a permanent magnet and a closed conducting loop (Fig. 3). A galvanometer connected to the circuit will record the relative current. The deflection of the galvanometer needle will be seen by both observed A, who is standing on the magnet, and observed B, who is sitting on the circuit. “Everything is clear,” the observed A will say, “the circuit moves in the constant field of a powerful magnet, the Lorentz force acts on the magnet in the circuit - so the current flows!” observed “The magnet approaches my motionless contour. The changing magnetic field generates an output electric field - it creates a current in the circuit!” But, according to Einstein, both observables are right. Both of them see the same deflection of the galvanometer needle, i.e., the same result of the manifestation of Faraday’s law. This means that Faraday’s law itself, of course, must be the same for general listening distances.
