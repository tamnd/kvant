We dealt with the doubles and hockey players relatively quickly. Let's try to understand how to reason in more complex cases. The first example is with multi-colored balls. Let's imagine that in urine (a small box) there can be, say, white and red balls. All balls are the same size and are indistinguishable at a glance. Someone dips their hand into the urine and takes out a ball. The ball turns out to be red, for example. What can you say about the color of the balls lying in urine?
Some will say: “All the balls are red.” In fact, this is not true: can one say that some (not necessarily all) balls are red? There may also be white balls. It may also be that the drawn ball is the only red one among the white ones.
A red ball was drawn. This means that the statement “All balls are white” is false. What's true? - “Some (at least one) of the balls are red.” Let's practice. Let each of the following statements be false.
1. All balls in urine are red.
2. Some balls in urine are red.
3. Some balls in urine are white.
4. All equilateral triangles are right triangles.
5. All the students in the class were at the meeting.
6. Some girls were at the meeting.
Formulate true statements.
And now the general scheme for solving all such problems is our “magic solution”. Let us have a set M of such and such objects (this could be several balls, or several books, or all the students of a certain class). Each of the elements of this set may or may not have some property A. For example, the ball may or may not be red; hockey - to be or not to be double.

The law is this: either a statement can be true or its negation. However, only one of two!
(In logic, this law is called the law of excluded middle.) Which statement we are using is either true or false.
Any statement B, or its negation (i.e. the statement: “Statement B is false”). Any hockey players are bad at tevers (possess property A). If this is false, then at least one hockey player is good (does not have property A).
So here's our diagram:
Approval
1. All items from M have property A.
2. Some items from M have the A property.
His denial
1. At least one object from M does not have property A.
2. All objects from M do not have property A.
Note that the statements “some objects have property A” and “at least one object (and perhaps all) have property A” mean the same thing in our scheme.
