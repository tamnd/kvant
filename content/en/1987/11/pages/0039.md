on): in order to change the speed of a body, you need to act on it with a force. In this case, the change in speed per unit time (i.e. acceleration $a$) is subhoropal to the force ($F$) and inversely proportional to the mass ($m$) of the body: $a = F/m$. From the law discovered by Newton, it is clear that the greater the mass of a body, the more rapidly it changes its speed. In this sense, they say that the mass of a body is a measure of its inertia.

Newton's genius lies, in particular, in the fact that, when considering a specific physical phenomenon (for example, the movement of a body under the influence of a force), he always had in mind the whole world as a whole. It is natural, therefore, that Newton tries to apply the laws of dynamics he formulated to describe the motion of the planets. The conclusion is self-directed: no force is needed to push the planets in their orbital motion. On the contrary, you need a force that does not allow the planets to fly away in a straight line (in accordance with the law of inertia) and turns them each time into circular or close to circular orbits. In other words, the planets should be acted upon by a force directed not along, but across the motion. And if so, then there is nothing else left to do but to assume that the source of this force is the Sun.

Then everything was simple. The acceleration of a planet as it moves in a circular orbit is $a = \omega^2 R = 4\pi^2 R/T^2$ (usually centripetal acceleration). Substituting this expression into his own law ($a = F/m$), Newton gets

$$\frac{4\pi^2}{T^2} R = \frac{F}{m}.$$

Here $F$ is the same force that acts on the planet from the Sun, and $m$ is the mass of the planet. Using Kepler's third law, Newton comes to the conclusion that the magnitude $F/m \sim 1/R^2$ and does not depend on the characteristics of the planet, i.e. the force acting on the planet from the Sun,

$$F = \text{const} \cdot \frac{m}{R^2}.$$

Reflecting on what the value of the constants included in this expression could depend on, Newton came to the conclusion that it is determined only by the mass of the Sun ($M$). (How much? Yes, it just seemed natural to him.) This is how the famous and at the same time crowded expression today arose

$$F = G \frac{Mm}{R^2}.$$

The value of the coefficient $G$ does not depend on anything and is a world constant (it is called the boundary constant).

Further analysis of the planet’s motion showed that with such a force, Kepler’s first two laws are automatically satisfied. It was already a great success. Encouraged by him, Newton tries to describe the motion of the Moon around the Earth and, although with some adventures, obtains excellent agreement between his calculations and experiment.

And here Newton decides to counteract his force of generalization. He claims that the force he discovered is in no way connected with the specifics of celestial bodies, but is a universal property of any natural objects. This is how the law of universal gravitation was formulated: the force acting between any two bodies with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$,

$$F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{R^2}.$$

The question of why the very masses that characterize the desire of bodies to maintain their motion (i.e., are a measure of their inertia) simultaneously determine the mutual attraction of bodies to each other, worried many generations of scientists and was subsequently the starting point for Einstein’s construction of the general theory of relativity.

As soon as the law of universal gravitation is applicable to any bodies, it becomes possible to experimentally measure the value of the gravitational constant $G$. It turned out to be incredibly small:

$$G = 6,67 \cdot 10^{-11} \text{H} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{kr}^2.$$

This means that gravitational forces are very weak. That is why we do not at all replace the gravitational attraction between the circles of our bodies. This force becomes noticeable only when at least one of the bodies is very large (such as the Earth, Sun, etc.).
