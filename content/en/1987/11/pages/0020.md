Most of the problems have the most vague relation to celestial mechanics. The task is considered in strict sequence, so that the results obtained earlier are used to solve the following problems. Because of this, it is difficult to follow Newton's reasoning by choosing a theorem from the middle of the book. This applies, for example, to Problem IX, which Newton discussed with Halley: “Assuming that the centripetal force is inversely proportional to the squares of the distances of places from the center and that the absolute magnitude of this force is known, it is required to find the curve that will follow a body leaving a given place with a given speed.”

So that the reader can get an idea of ​​the method of presentation used by Newton, we will give a proof of Theorem I from Section II “On the determination of centripetal forces,” which is important for justifying Kepler’s second law (law of areas). Its formulation is as follows: “The areas described by radii drawn from the revolving body to the stationary center of forces lie in the same plane and are proportional to the times of their description.”

Proof of the theorem.

“Divide time into equal intervals, and during the first of them let the body, by inertia, describe the straight line AB. If it were not subject to any action, then, continuing to walk in a straight line, it would come to c (no to law I), having passed a path Bc equal to AB, and then described by the radii AS, BS, cS drawn to the center of forces S, the areas ASB and BSc are equal. In reality, when the body came to B, the centripetal force acted on it with one, but forgotten force, the consequence of which only deviates from the straight line Ac and will continue its path along the straight line BC. Let us draw a straight line сС parallel to BS until it meets BC at point C, then by the end of the second time interval the body (by consequence of the first laws)*) will pass to point C, which lies in the same plane as triangle ASB. Let's conduct SC; by the parallelism of SB and Cc, the areas of triangles SBC and SBc will be equal to each other, and therefore they are equal to the area of ​​triangle SAB.

Reasoning in a similar way, we see that if the central force acts sequentially at points C, D, E, etc. and forces the body to describe straight lines CD, DE, EF, etc., then all these straight lines will lie in the same plane, and the areas of triangles SCD and SBC, SDE and SCD, SEF and SDE will be equal to each other. Consequently, equal areas located in a fixed plane are described at equal times. Slavaya, we get that any sums of these areas, like SADS and SAFS, will be related as the times of their description. Then increasing the number of triangles and decreasing their height infinitely, we obtain that in the limit of the perimeter ADF there will be a curved line and the central force, which the body deviates all the time from the tangent to this curve, acts incessantly, while the areas SADS and SAFS, described by the radius, remaining constantly proportional to the times of description of their description, will continue to be proportional to these times.”

In this style, task by task is considered. The last section of the first book presents elements of corpuscular optics, in particular, the law of light refraction is discussed on the basis of the laws of mechanics.
