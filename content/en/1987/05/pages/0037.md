$$\frac{5}{21} = \frac{1}{21} + \frac{2}{21} + \frac{2}{21} = \frac{1}{21} + \left( \frac{1}{14} + \frac{1}{42} \right) + \left( \frac{1}{14} + \frac{1}{42} \right) = \frac{1}{21} + \frac{2}{14} + \frac{2}{42} = \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{42}.$$

The Egyptians also knew how to multiply and divide dobi. But when multiplying, you had to multiply fractions by fractions, and then, perhaps, use the table again. The situation with division is even more complicated.

The Vivilonians took a completely different path. The fact is that the number system in Vivilon was hexadecimal - each unit of the next digit was 60 times larger than the previous one. Viviler, the entries 14″ 42′ 38 meant the number 14·60²+42·60+38, i.e. in our entry the number 52958 (only the Vivilonians did not use our numbers, but other designations made up of Klints). That’s why the Vylonians had dobis not in decimal, but in hexadecimal. In fact, we did and now use such a doby in notation of time and angles. For example, the time 3 hours 17 minutes 28 s can be written like this: 3.17′28″ hours (read 3 whole, 17 sixtieth 28 three thousand six hundred hours). Instead of the words “sixtieths”, “three thousand six hundredths” they said in short: “first small fractions”, “second small fractions”. This is where our words “minute” (Latin for “lesser”) and “second” (Latin for “second”) come from. So the Vivilonian way of designating dobei has retained its meaning to this day.
