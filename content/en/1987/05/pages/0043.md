Physics of musical harmony

You know that the sounds that surround us are nothing more than fluctuations in air pressure and density. Among the variety of sounds, some have a strong emotional impact on a person. These are musical sounds. It is impossible to explain their special interaction with the brain only in physical terms. But you can, in the words of Pushkin, “verify harmony with algebra.”

According to legends that have come down from antiquity, the first to try to do this were Pythogoras and his students. They found that equally stretched strings made of the same material produce agreement.

For example, if you take two strings, one of which is half as long as the other, then the sounds extracted from them turn out to be consonants. In this case, musicians say that sounds are distinguished by an octave, and denote them with one note: for example, “do” of the first octave, “do” of the second octave, etc.

Let us see, from a physical point of view, how a string with fixed ends vibrates (Fig. 1). If the string is pulled back and released, it will oscillate, in which the speeds and accelerations of all points are directed equally. *) Such vibrations determine the fundamental tone of the string. It turns out that the frequency $v$ of the fundamental tone of strings made of the same materials and stretched with the same force depends only on their length $l$, and depends inversely: $v \sim 1/l$. Other vibrations of the string are also possible - with a frequency twice, twice, etc. greater than the frequency of the fundamental tone. They are called overtones.
