M1022. The first 8 natural numbers can be arranged in two rows (Fig. 1) so that the sum of the numbers in the top row is equal to the sum of the numbers in the bottom, and the sums of the numbers in the columns are also equal to each other. Is it possible to arrange the first: a) ten, b) twelve natural numbers in a similar way?

c) for what natural numbers $n$ can the numbers from 1 to $2n$ be arranged in this way?

Fig. 1.

| 8 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 7 | 6 | 4 |

Fig. 2.

The sum of the lower numbers is equal to $(1+2+\ldots+2k)+2k\cdot 2k$, i.e. $4k^2$ greater than the sum of the upper numbers. Consequently, we must rearrange the top number down and the bottom number up in several columns so that in the end the top sum increases by $2k^2$ (and the bottom one decreases by a column). Note that the $m$th number on the left in the top row is $2k$ less than the $m$th number on the right on the bottom row for all $m=1, 2, \ldots, 2k$, so if you rearrange the numbers in two columns equidistant from the edges of the table at the same time, the top sum will increase by $4k$.

If $k$ is even, it is sufficient to perform such a rearrangement for $k/2$ symmetric pairs of columns: the upper sum will increase by $4k\cdot k/2=2k^2$. If $k\geqslant 3$ is even, we rearrange the upper numbers $k+1$ and $2k$ with the corresponding lower numbers $3k$ and $2k+1$, as well as the numbers $(k-1)/2$ of symmetric pairs of columns. We will again get the desired increase in the upper amount:

$$3k-(k+1)+(2k+1)-2k+4k\cdot(k-1)/2=2k^2.$$

Finally, for $k=1$ the required arrangement obviously does not exist.

M. I. Shteber

M1023. Is there always at least one triangle out of 100 that can completely cover the other 99?

This sequence is built according to the following principle. First, we define a sequence $a_n$ of the length of the longest side of the triangles $T_n$ so that for any overlap of triangles $T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_{n-1}$ on $T_n$ there remains an uncovered fixed (not dependent on $n$) for the area of the triangle $T_n$ - this can be ensured by choosing $a_n$, sufficiently large compared to $a_1, \ldots, a_{n-1}$. Then you need to select a sequence of areas $S_n$ of triangles $T_n$ so that the total area of ​​triangles $T_{n+1}, T_{n+2}, \ldots$ is less than the area of ​​the uncovered part of triangle $T_n$ - this can be achieved by quickly decreasing the sequence of areas.
