Unfortunately, it turns out that the width of two small intervals - 1.0535 × 1.0535 = 1.110 (since intervals are frequency ratios, to calculate a large interval the small ones included in it must be multiplied and not added) is noticeably different from the large one - 1.125. Therefore, the sounds of the Pythagorean scale are unequal - the scale cannot begin with any of the no. To change the key, the musical instrument must be tuned again. For string instruments this is not difficult to do, but the organ, which consists of many pipes - each for its own sound, is tuned when creating it.

Despite this inconvenience, the Pythagogian scale (along with other types of musical scales) served musicians for more than two thousand years - until the 16th century. Then the so-called diotic scale, which was built on frequencies made up of the ratios of the numbers 2, 3 and 5, became widespread. But it also suffered from a similar drawback.

Only for the further assimilation of instrumental musical structure did physical research begin again. Franz M. Mersenne (1588-1648) established how the frequency of vibration of a structure depends not only on its length, but also on the tension force, the second of the structure and the density of the materials from which it is made. Shortly thereafter, the piano was invented, an instrument in which the structures are tuned to successive musical tones with frequencies ranging from 27.5 to 3520 Hz, spanning 7 octaves.

Around the same time, the so-called good temperament scale was created, that is, a chromatic scale in which all successive intervals are chosen among themselves (Fig. 3). The key to its creation was a mathematical coincidence. It turned out that the powers of the number $^{12}/2$ coincide with the Pythagogian ratios with good accuracy (compare them yourself) - the chromatic quantum, for example, differs from the Pyphagian quantum by only 0.11% - such an error is indistinguishable by ear. Therefore, modern musical instruments can be played in any of 24 keys (12 maximum and 12 minimum). The fact that it is possible to achieve consistent sound regardless of tonality is proven by Bach when he wrote his “Well-Tempered Clavier” - 24 sentences and fugues in all keys. Whether there are physical principles for the difference between maximum and minimum, alas, we still do not know.
