On February 1, 1907, in Paris, an engineer from Munich, Arthur Korn, spoke to journalists, scientists, entrepreneurs and members of the government in a crowded conference room. To the left and right of him on a long table were two large slowly rotating cylinders of unknown purpose to the public.

“Madame and Monsieur,” Korn began his speech, “the problem of transmitting photographs by telefagy solutions! This can be done in the same way as we transmit sound over the telephone.”

There were people in the conference room who were far from science, so Korn went into lengthy and lengthy discussions about the device of the telephone and telephony and various technical details. Today's reader «Kvant» is familiar with these “details,” and we will therefore proceed at once to an account of the yen of the phototelegraph, first realized eighty years ago.

The photo is not transmitted as a whole, but is divided into 100 stripes, and each strip into 100 elements. At the transmitting station, you need to change the tone of each element and turn the current control accordingly. At the receiving station, this change in current will change the light intensity of the lamp, which lights up a section of the photographic film. Now you need to move on to the next element at the transmitting station and accordingly move the film at the receiving station. The idea is good, but if you spend only 5 seconds on transferring one element, then transferring the entire photograph will, alas, take 14 hours of continuous work. Now, if the current regulator worked automatically, instantly changing the tone of each element and communicating it to the receiving station...

Such an automatic regulator can be a solar photocell. The focused light of the lamp, passing through the photograph, is rolled up in the form of a cylinder, and is reflected by a mirror onto a solar photocell, which regulates the current in the telephone wire. The cylinder at the transmitting station rotates, and the cylinder at the receiving station rotates at exactly the same speed. The visual lamp crawls slowly, “viewing” the photographs, and at the same speed the beam detecting the photographic film creeps. And the debris flow sample sends impulses into the communication line that accurately reflect the performance of the next element. Simple, isn't it?

Meanwhile, Korn spent more than three years implementing his idea. And the gap was not selenium at all, no, the semi-driver worked flawlessly.
