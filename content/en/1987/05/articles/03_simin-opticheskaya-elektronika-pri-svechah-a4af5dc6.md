OPTICAL ELECTRONICS BY CANDLELIGHT

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
G. S. SIMIN

Optical electrons (or optoelectronics) is the youngest and perhaps the most promising area of ​​modern semiconductor electronics, a favorite of physicists, developers of ultra-high-speed computers and ultra-long-distance communication systems. It is carried out by the most advanced laboratories in the world, equipped with the most modern equipment. And of course not by candlelight. Meanwhile, we will teach our story about optoelectronics from those distant times when in Russia A. N. Lodygin was still developing the first designs of carbon incandescent lamps, and Europe was illuminated mainly by candles and gas lanterns.

Of the many semiconductors known in the 19th century—substances that were said to be “poor conductors of electricity”—selenium was the material that received special attention from scientists. The fact is that in 1873, the English telephone employee A. May discovered that the resistance of selenium decreases sharply if light falls on it. May may not have been too aware of the new phenomenon, since he discovered it while examining selenium insulating supports for a telephone cable. But May’s supervisor, engineer W. Smith, immediately appreciated the importance of the discovery. He quickly carried out the first studies and reported them in print in the same year of 1873. This message aroused great interest among physicists around the world.

Scientists from Germany, France, England, Russia, and America turned to the study of selenium. Numerous attempts have been made to increase the photosensitivity of selenium. For this purpose, ten methods of obtaining it were used. However, all these searches were carried out practically “from scratch” - after all, physicists of the 19th century had no idea about the incredible sensitivity of semiconductors to impurities, or about methods for their purification. Therefore, the published results contradicted each other; the numerical data given differed by tens and hundreds of times. There were also simply cool (from our today’s point of view) messages. Some authors wrote, for example, that the photosensitivity of selenium increases if rubber or camphor, which previously increased in ozone, is placed next to it. Others have reported that selenium is most photosensitive to neighboring oils. However, it should not be forgotten that at that time semiconductors were not yet recognized as a special, amazing class of materials. So don’t rush to look down on the experiments of a century ago - these were the first steps on the path that led to the creation of optical electrons.

The “selenium boom” attracted the attention of the famous German electrical engineer Werner Siemens. Siemens was found to have rare inventive talent. While still a twenty-year-old young man, while serving a five-year prison sentence for a plot in Duzla, he invented a method of galvanic silvering and gilding and gilded his prison spoon, transferring gold from a coin lying in his pocket onto it. This was in the early forties of the 19th century. In the seventies, Siemens, a major industrial magnet, honorary doctor of the University of Berlin, member of the Berlin Academy of Sciences, author of the ritty resistance standard that immortalized his name, remained the same indomitable inventor as in his youth. He became interested in studying selenium, and this hobby had significant consequences for the future fate of selenium. In 1875, Siemens published

Let's say a few words about photometry and its state in the 19th century so that you can better appreciate Siemens' invention.

It can be considered that photometry originated in the 2nd century AD. e., when Ptolemy began to study the brightness of stars. And all further development of this field of science was associated mainly with astronomical observations. In 1604, Kepler established the laws of illumination. In 1729, the French scientist P. Bouguer formed the principle of photometry and laid the foundation for scientific measurements in this area. Booger's idea is to use the eye's ability to detect differences in light, even if the difference is only 1%. In a Bouguer photometer, two parts of the screen are illuminated by two sources - the one being studied (for example, a star) and the “reference” (for example, light). The illumination of that part of the screen on which the reference source shines can be changed (for example, by bringing the light closer or farther away). If the illumination created by the studied and “artificial” star is equal, the position of the last registration. In this way, indirect measurements can be made.

The Bouguer photometer was improved several times over the course of more than 100 years, but the basic principle of its operation remained unchanged: the equality of illumination was determined by the eye. The astronomer is tired - the accurately measured one falls sharply; with different shades of color of the measured and reference sources, the assessment becomes difficult; a bright object appeared in the field of view - again an error in the measurements. The subjective nature of measurements has become a service disadvantage of photometry. Meanwhile, the need for photometers grew: earthly lamps were added to the heavenly bodies, the brightness of which also needed to be measured.

Having engaged in rural research, Siemens could not miss the opportunity to carry out experiments. He designed a photometer in which, for the first time, subjective judgment by eye was eliminated and light intensity could be expressed in electrical units. The electrical circuit of the Siemens photometer consisted of a galvanic cell, a galvanometer and a piece of selenium with wire electrodes. In the dark, the selenium resistance is so great that the galvanometer needle hardly deviates. When the cell is illuminated, its resistance drops, the current increases, and the galvanometer needle deviates the more strongly, the more intense the light. (This is exactly how the perfect photo maker works!)

Based on a selec photoresistor, Siemens made the world's first optoelectronic toy (of course, no one called it that at that time; the term “optoelectronics” appeared many decades later). The main element of this toy was a model of a human eye, in which a selenium photoresistor was mounted. As soon as a candle was brought close to the eye, it immediately closed, “swirling” from the bright light, just like it was alive.

The invention of the mudflow photometer should only lead to the appearance of a whole series of ingenious devices. An interesting and very bold development of Siemens’ work for that time is the invention of the American engineer J. Carey. In the same 1875, he proposed a design for a device for transmitting images over a distance. The principle of operation of this device now seems very simple. The transmitted image is first projected onto the receiver, which consists of a large number of debris flow cells arranged closely together. The resistance of the cells varies to varying degrees depending on how brightly the cell is illuminated. Reproduction of an image on a “screen”, which is a panel with light bulbs, and the number of light bulbs is equal to the number of seismic cells of the receiver. Each light bulb is connected in series with its corresponding cell and voltage source. When you turn on the voltage of the cell of a particular light bulb, the greater the voltage, the lower the resistance of the seismic element connected to it, i.e., the more illuminated the corresponding cell is. By reaching a greater length of wires connecting the receiver and the screen, the image can be transmitted over a considerable distance.

Carey's invention, however, turned out to be practically unrealizable at that time. After all, if the receiver contains, for example, 100×100 cells, then it is necessary to stretch 10,000 wires to the screen and connect them to 10,000 light bulbs, to 10,000 sources, and so that not a single lamp burns out!

Several of the following selecium photoresistor receivers caused real sensations.

Later in Washington, Alexander Graham Bell read with interest the reports coming from overseas about research on selenium and he himself tried many methods for preparing materials with high photosensitivity. After several successful experiments, Bell came up with the idea of, at first glance, a missel - in the electrical circuit in which the mudflow sample under study was connected, to replace the galvanometer... with a telephone. This idea will not seem so strange if we remember that not long before Bell had just taken out a patent for the world’s first telephone and was now successfully distributing his product throughout the country. Bell's phone made sounds if the current flowing through it changed quickly enough. Therefore, Bell decided that if a selenium photosensitive sample was connected in series with the telephone *), the telephone would sound when the light falling on the selenium was interrupted frequently. On May 17, 1878, while delivering a lecture at the Royal Institution in London, Dr. Bell announced that “it is possible to hear sounds caused by the interruption of light.” Bell received melodic sounds of different heights by changing the speed of rotation of a disk with slots located between a bright source and a mudflow receiver.

Two years later, Bell's idea received a sensational development.

On a sunny summer day in 1880, residents of Washington watched in amazement as a well-dressed gentleman installed a strange structure on the roof of the Franklin School. Many recognized Professor Bell's friend and landowner as Master Tainter. Tainter pointed a small mirror at the sun; the reflected sunlight passed through a small line and, like a bright thin beam, rested directly on the window of the building opposite. This building housed the Bell Research Laboratory. Standing behind the mirror and attaching something like a megaphone to it, Tainter began to speak quietly into the megaphone, while looking at the laboratory window. In response, Master Bell appeared at the window, waving his hat.

On this day, Bell and Tainter successfully implemented wireless optical telephony. The structure on the roof was a transmitting station. Bell Labs housed the receiving station. The beam sent from the roof hit a photodetector - a selecium photoresistor connected in an electrical circuit in series with the telephone. When Tainter spoke into the mouthpiece, the sound vibrations caused the mirror to vibrate slightly. At the same time, due to “defocusing”, the illumination of the photodetector changed...
The phone came to his ear, Bell clearly heard Tainter's voice: "Mr. Bell, if you can hear me, come to the window and wave your hat." Bell's answer indicated the complete success of the experiment.

The sensational report about Bell and Tainter's experiment caused a new wave of increased interest in selenium, and new attempts to increase its photosensitivity. Consistent Bell, his correspondence K. Frits was able to obtain samples whose resistance under the influence of sunlight decreased by more than 300 times (Bell and Siemens achieved only a fifteen-fold change). Frits also suggested another possible use for selenium. If you connect a seismic photoresistor to any electrical device, then this device can be controlled using light. For example, a debris flow element can be included in an electromagnetic relay circuit. When the element is illuminated, its resistance will decrease, the current in the circuit will increase, and the contact relay will close without any direct connection into an electrical circuit. And although optocouplers appeared only in the second half of this century, the idea of ​​optical control using a selec element was very effectively implemented much earlier.

On February 1, 1907, in Paris, an engineer from Munich, Arthur Korn, spoke to journalists, scientists, entrepreneurs and members of the government in a crowded conference room. To the left and right of him on a long table were two large slowly rotating cylinders of unknown purpose to the public.

“Madame and Monsieur,” Korn began his speech, “the problem of transmitting photographs by telefagy solutions! This can be done in the same way as we transmit sound over the telephone.”

There were people in the conference room who were far from science, so Korn went into lengthy and lengthy discussions about the device of the telephone and telephony and various technical details. Today's reader «Kvant» is familiar with these “details,” and we will therefore proceed at once to an account of the yen of the phototelegraph, first realized eighty years ago.

The photo is not transmitted as a whole, but is divided into 100 stripes, and each strip into 100 elements. At the transmitting station, you need to change the tone of each element and turn the current control accordingly. At the receiving station, this change in current will change the light intensity of the lamp, which lights up a section of the photographic film. Now you need to move on to the next element at the transmitting station and accordingly move the film at the receiving station. The idea is good, but if you spend only 5 seconds on transferring one element, then transferring the entire photograph will, alas, take 14 hours of continuous work. Now, if the current regulator worked automatically, instantly changing the tone of each element and communicating it to the receiving station...

Such an automatic regulator can be a solar photocell. The focused light of the lamp, passing through the photograph, is rolled up in the form of a cylinder, and is reflected by a mirror onto a solar photocell, which regulates the current in the telephone wire. The cylinder at the transmitting station rotates, and the cylinder at the receiving station rotates at exactly the same speed. The visual lamp crawls slowly, “viewing” the photographs, and at the same speed the beam detecting the photographic film creeps. And the debris flow sample sends impulses into the communication line that accurately reflect the performance of the next element. Simple, isn't it?

Meanwhile, Korn spent more than three years implementing his idea. And the gap was not selenium at all, no, the semi-driver worked flawlessly.

The main difficulty was achieving complete synchronization of mechanical movements and equality of rotation speeds of the drums.

Finally, by 1907 the problem was solved. And here is a demonstration in Paris. The drums installed to the left and right of Korn are the transmitting and receiving stations. And the wires coming out of the transmitting station are connected to the Paris-Lyon telephone line. (On the second page of the cover there is a photograph from the French magazine L'Illustration, which reported on Korn's sensational experiment.) The signal from the debris flow element travels along one set of wires to Lyon, and along the other back to Paris, only 1000 kilometers. The photograph, placed in the transmitting cylinder, was “viewed” for 12 minutes. The film from the priming cylinder was immediately developed and printed - and a photograph of the President of the French Republic, Follier, was shown to the applause of the audience. Korn, accepting the suppression, said that he had no doubts about success, since a little earlier he had successfully transferred the portrait of President Roosevelt along the Munich-Nunberg-Munich line.

The transmission of images using photocells began to quickly gain recognition. The devices operating according to the principle developed by Korn were so improved that they could transmit small newspaper text over a distance with high clarity.

This was the very first and not very well-known round of the history of semiconductor devices. Originating at the end of the 19th century, they saw the appearance of telephones and transformers for a year, for 20 years - happily, and for almost 50 years - ... themselves, that is, the creation of the class of “semiconductor devices”! Paradoxically, Korn's invention was the last major event at this early stage. We had to wait another ten long years for new devices. This time was needed to create a quantum theory of the electrical properties of semiconductors, develop unique methods for purifying them from random impurities, as well as methods for introducing a strictly controlled amount of the desired impurities, and manufacturing complex sets of equipment for the production of the device. Only after this, at the turn of the 50s of our century, did the “semiconductor revolution” become possible; one of the most advanced units of the crown is modern optical semiconductor electrics.

This interesting science, called optoelectronics for short, studies the laws of converting electric current through a semiconductor into light and the figurative laws by which light falling on a semiconductor causes electrical signals in it. Based on optoelectronics, the creation of a huge device has become firmly established in our lives and has already become familiar. It is worthy of surprise and admiration that more than 100 years ago scientists were able not only to produce the first optoelectronic devices, but also, with amazing intuition, to anticipate the most important areas of their future use and development.

The sensitivity of the Siemens photometer became the prototype of modern semiconductor light detectors - photodetectors. They pick up weak optical signals at a distance of tens of kilometers from the source, “read” optical commands of modern computer-controlled machines, signal the end of tape in tape recorders and perform many other important tasks.

J. Carey's attempt to make a luminous screen for transmitting images over a distance is being continued in the work of researchers in many countries to create a flat television screen, which is considered to replace the bulky film circuit of temporary televisions. Information tables (“luminous newspapers”) installed on the roofs of buildings in Moscow, Leningrad and other cities directly implement a bold idea that was a hundred years old.
