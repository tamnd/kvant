M1021 — M1025; F1033 — F1037

Answer: in five days.

Let $x_n$ denote the highest altitude to which a climber can climb in $n$ days ($n=1, 2, \dots$). If by the end of one day he reaches a height of $h$ meters, the next day he can rise to a height of $h+6\cdot 30=h+180$ meters if he remains insensible on the rock, and to a height of $h+\left(6-\frac{h}{400}\right)40=h+240-h/10$ meters if he goes down insensible. It is clear that the first option is more profitable if $180>240-h/10$, that is, $h>600$, the second - if $h\leq 600$. Thus,

$$x_{n+1}=\begin{cases} x_n+240-x_n/10, & x_n\leq 600, \\ x_n+180, & x_n\geq 600. \end{cases}$$

⟦folio 25⟧

Starting with $x_0=0$, one can define $x_n$ successively for all $n$. Since $x_4<1000$ and $x_5>1000$, the climber will be able to reach the top of the cliff by the end of the 5th day; To do this, he must spend the first two nights below, and the last two on the rock.

The sum of the lower numbers is equal to $(1+2+\ldots+2k)+2k\cdot 2k$, i.e. $4k^2$ greater than the sum of the upper numbers. Consequently, we must rearrange the top number down and the bottom number up in several columns so that in the end the top sum increases by $2k^2$ (and the bottom one decreases by a column). Note that the $m$th number on the left in the top row is $2k$ less than the $m$th number on the right on the bottom row for all $m=1, 2, \ldots, 2k$, so if you rearrange the numbers in two columns equidistant from the edges of the table at the same time, the top sum will increase by $4k$.

If $k$ is even, it is sufficient to perform such a rearrangement for $k/2$ symmetric pairs of columns: the upper sum will increase by $4k\cdot k/2=2k^2$. If $k\geqslant 3$ is even, we rearrange the upper numbers $k+1$ and $2k$ with the corresponding lower numbers $3k$ and $2k+1$, as well as the numbers $(k-1)/2$ of symmetric pairs of columns. We will again get the desired increase in the upper amount:

$$3k-(k+1)+(2k+1)-2k+4k\cdot(k-1)/2=2k^2.$$

Finally, for $k=1$ the required arrangement obviously does not exist.

M1023. Is there always at least one triangle out of 100 that can completely cover the other 99?

This sequence is built according to the following principle. First, we define a sequence $a_n$ of the length of the longest side of the triangles $T_n$ so that for any overlap of triangles $T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_{n-1}$ on $T_n$ there remains an uncovered fixed (not dependent on $n$) for the area of the triangle $T_n$ - this can be ensured by choosing $a_n$, sufficiently large compared to $a_1, \ldots, a_{n-1}$. Then you need to select a sequence of areas $S_n$ of triangles $T_n$ so that the total area of ​​triangles $T_{n+1}, T_{n+2}, \ldots$ is less than the area of ​​the uncovered part of triangle $T_n$ - this can be achieved by quickly decreasing the sequence of areas.

⟦folio 26⟧

Let us give an example of one such sequence of triangles. Let $T_1$ be a regular triangle with sides $a_1=1$; $T_n$ with $n>1$ is an equilateral triangle with base $a_n=4^{n-1}$ and area $S_n=S_1/4^{n-1}$, where $S_1=\sqrt{3}/4$ is the area of ​​triangle $T_1$ (height $h_n$ of triangle $T_n$ is $2S_n/a_n=2\sqrt{3}/4^{2n-1}$). The distance triangles are $T_1$, $T_2$, ..., $T_n$. Each of the triangles $T_i$, $1\leq i\leq n-1$ is placed in a strip of width no more than $a_i$, perpendicular to the base of the triangle $T_n$ (see Fig.ok). The area covered by this area in $T_n$ is less than $a_i \cdot h_n$, so the total area covered by triangles $T_1$, ..., $T_{n-1}$ in $T_n$ is less than $(a_1+a_2+\dots+a_{n-1})h_n$ (this estimate is very rough, but it will be enough for us). Since

$$a_1+a_2+\dots+a_{n-1}=\frac{1}{3}(4^{n-1}-1)<\frac{1}{3}a_n,$$

The area of ​​the triangle $T_n$ less than $a_nh_n/3=2S_n/3$ is covered, which means that the third area $T_n$ remains uncovered. At the same time

$$S_{n+1}+S_{n+2}+\dots=$$

$$=S_1\left(\frac{1}{4^n}+\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}+\dots\right)=\frac{S_1}{4^{n}(1-1/4)}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{S_1}{4^{n-1}}=\frac{1}{3}S_n.$$

Therefore, the triangles $T_1$, ..., $T_{n-1}$, $T_{n+1}$, $T_{n+2}$, ... cannot be covered by the entire triangle $T_n$.

**M1024**. Prove that for any two triangles with angles $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ and $\alpha_1$, $\beta_1$, $\gamma_1$ the inequality

$$\frac{\cos \alpha_1}{\sin \alpha}+\frac{\cos \beta_1}{\sin \beta}+\frac{\cos \gamma_1}{\sin \gamma} \leq$$

$$\leq \text{ctg} \alpha+\text{ctg} \beta+\text{ctg} \gamma.$$

We fix $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$. We must prove that the maximum value of the left-hand side of the inequalities as a function of $\alpha_1$, $\beta_1$, $\gamma_1$ at $\alpha_1+\beta_1+\gamma_1=\pi$ is achieved when $\alpha_1=\alpha$, $\beta_1=\beta$, $\gamma_1=\gamma$. Multiplying the left side by $\sin \alpha \cdot \sin \beta \cdot \sin \gamma$, we give it the form

$$\sin \beta \sin \gamma \cos \alpha_1+\sin \gamma \sin \alpha \cos \beta_1+\sin \alpha \sin \beta \cos \gamma_1=A.$$

This expression can be used as the sum (taken with a minus sign) of three pairwise scalar products of the vectors $a$, $b$ and $c$ of length $a=\sin \alpha$, $b=\sin \beta$, $c=\sin \gamma$, directed along the sides of a triangle with angles $\alpha_1$, $\beta_1$, $\gamma_1$ (see Fig.ok):

$$A=-(\bar{b} \cdot \bar{c}+\bar{c} \cdot \bar{a}+\bar{a} \cdot \bar{b})=$$

$$=\frac{1}{2}(a^2+b^2+c^2-(\bar{a}+\bar{b}+\bar{c})\cdot(\bar{a}+\bar{b}+\bar{c})),$$

since $bc \cos \alpha=-\bar{b} \cdot \bar{c}$, etc. Obviously, this quantity takes on the most significance when $\bar{a}+\bar{b}+\bar{c}=0$, i.e., a triangle can be formed from the vectors $a$, $b$, $c$. The angles of this triangle are $\alpha_1$, $\beta_1$, $\gamma_1$, and the opposite sides are $\sin \alpha$, $\sin \beta$, $\sin \gamma$; by the theorem of sines we obtain that $\alpha=\alpha_1$, $\beta=\beta_1$, $\gamma=\gamma_1$.

F1033. Throw a single-pod flexible rope of mass m and length l over a block of radius R, attached to two hooks on the ceiling located at a distance of 2R (see figure). On the axis of the block there is a load, the mass of which together with the block is M. There are no trenches between the rope and the block. Find the minimum tension force in the rope.

Consider the left half of the rope ABD (see figure). It is clear that the lower the point of the rope in section AB, the less the tension force at this point. Therefore, the point of the rope with minimal tension should be looked for somewhere in the BD section. Let's take an arbitrary point C in this area, located at a distance H from the ceiling.

The section of rope ABC is affected by the direction of the vertical upward force F₁ from the side of the cry, the tension force of the rope F at point C (from the part of the rope located to the right of point C), as well as the pressure force of the block on the rope, distributed over the entire surface of contact of the rope with the block.

Let us mentally move the section of rope ABC by a small distance x so that it takes a new position A₁B₁C₁ (see figure). With such a movement, force F₁ will do work F₁x, and force F will do negative work Fx. The pressure forces of the block on the rope will not do any work (at each point of the rope they are directed perpendicular to the direction of movement of this point). The potential energy of the displaced section of the rope will increase by the same amount as the potential energy of a piece of rope with length x and mass $\frac{m}{l} x$ will increase when crossing it from position CC₁ to position AA₁. According to the law of conservation of energy

$$F_1x - Fx = \frac{m}{l} xgH.$$

From here

$$F = F_1 - \frac{m}{l} xgH. \tag{1}$$

Now it is clear that the minimum value $F$ in section $BD$ will be at the maximum possible value $H$, i.e. at point $D$. Equality (1) for the point $D$ has the form

$$F_{\min} = F_1 - \frac{m}{l} g \left( \frac{l}{2} - \frac{\pi R}{2} + R \right).$$

Since $F_1 = \frac{1}{2}(M+m)g$, which is easy to show, the minimum tension force in the rope is equal to

$$F_{\min} = \frac{Mg}{2} + \frac{mg}{2} \frac{R}{l} (\pi - 2) = \left( M + m(\pi - 2) \frac{R}{l} \right) \frac{g}{2}.$$

F1034. A mole of ideal gas from the initial state 1 with a temperature $T_1 = 100$ K, expanding through a turbine into an empty vessel, does some work and transitions to state 2 (see figure). This transition takes place without the supply or removal of heat. The gas is then compressed in the $2 \rightarrow 3$ process, in which the pressure is a linear fixation of the volume, and finally, in the isochoric $3 \rightarrow 1$ process, the gas returns to its original state. This is the work done by the gas during expansion through the turbine in transition $1 \rightarrow 2$, if in process $2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 1$ $Q = 72$ J of heat is supplied to the gas in this process. It is also known that $T_2 = T_3$, $V_2 = 3V_1$; gas constant $R = 8,31$ J/(mol K).

When a gas expands through a turbine into an empty vessel, it does work using its internal energy (since there is no heat supply). During this transition, individual parts of the gas can have different temperatures and pressures - this is the so-called “nonequilibrium” expansion. However, after the equilibrium temperature $T_2$ and pressure $p_2$ are established in the gas, the work done by the gas is uniquely determined by the law of conservation of energy:

$$A_{1 \rightarrow 2} = c_V(T_1 - T_2),$$ where $c_V = \frac{3}{2} R.$

In the section $2 \rightarrow 3$ the initial internal energy of the gas is equal to the final one, therefore the heat $Q_{2 \rightarrow 3}$ supplied to the gas is determined by the work done by the gas (area of ​​the trapezoid):

$$Q_{2 \rightarrow 3} = A_{2 \rightarrow 3} = \frac{p_2 + p_3}{2} (V_3 - V_2) = RT_2 \frac{1 - \alpha^2}{2\alpha},$$ where $\alpha = \frac{V_2}{V_3} = 3.$

In the section $3 \rightarrow 1$, the heat supplied to the gas is determined by the change in its internal energy, since the isochoric process of work by the gas does not occur:

$$Q_{3 \rightarrow 1} = c_V(T_1 - T_3) = c_V(T_1 - T_2).$$

According to the condition $Q_{2 \rightarrow 3} + Q_{3 \rightarrow 1} = Q \neq 72$ J, or

$$RT_2 \frac{1 - \alpha^2}{2\alpha} + c_1(T_1 - T_2) = Q.$$

The last equality allows us to find the temperature $T_2$, and then the work $A_{1 \rightarrow 2}$:

$$A_{1 \rightarrow 2} = \frac{Q + RT_1 \frac{\alpha^2 - 1}{2\alpha}}{1 + \frac{R}{c_V} \frac{\alpha^2 - 1}{2\alpha}} = 625$$ J.

A. A. Sheronov

An electric diopole of two rigidly connected exact charges $+q$ and $-q$, located at a distance $l$ from each other, flows through a flat capacitor, the layer of which is connected to a source with a constant EMF ($cm. рисунок$). In general, it should be noted that the diopole will be “pulled” into the capacitor - the resulting force acting on it from the plates is directed inside the capacitor, and therefore, the speed of the diopole in the capacitor will return.

Since the field in the capacitor is maintained constant, one can ignore the influence of the diopole field on the charges of the plates, i.e., consider the capacitor field to be external (independent) with respect to the diopole.

delta the speed of the dipole at the center of the capacitor if its speed away from the capacitor is known to be $v_0$. The distance between the plates of the capacitor $d$, the mass of the dipole $m$. Neglect the influence of gravity.

The capacitor field is potential; therefore, in the capacitor, the dipole will also have potential energy. This energy is equal to

$$W_\pi = (+q)\varphi_x + (-q)\varphi_x = q\frac{\varepsilon}{d}(x_+ - x_-) = -ql\frac{\varepsilon}{d},$$

where $x_+$ and $x_- = x_+ + l$ are the coordinates of the charges $+q$ and $-q$, $\varphi_x$ and $\varphi_x$ are the potentials at points with coordinates $x_+$ and $x_-$; coordinate $x$ is missing from the imprinted charged plate (see Fig.ok). With this choice of reference point, the work done to move a unit charge from the imprint plate to the positive one is equal to $\varepsilon$.

We find the required speed of the dipole from the law of conservation of energy:

$$\frac{mv_0^2}{2} = \frac{mv^2}{2} - ql\frac{\varepsilon}{d},$$

where

$$v = \left(v_0^2 + \frac{2ql\varepsilon}{md}\right)^{1/2}.$$

F1036. A body of mass $m = 20$ g lying on the main horizontal floor is attached to two identical elastic threads of stiffness $k = 10^4$ N/m. One thread is attached to the wall, the free end of the second thread begins to be pulled in the horizontal direction at a speed of $v = 10$ m/s. Which thread will break if each thread breaks at an absolute elongation of $\Delta l_{\text{np}} = 5$ cm? Assume that Hooke's law holds true for the threads until they break; there is no training.

It is convenient to monitor the process of stretching the threads; it will move from the stationary system (Fig. 1) to the coordinate system, collapsing to the right with a speed of $v/2$; the body at the first moment of time is at the origin of coordinates ($x = 0$) and moves to the left with a speed of $v/2$.

The elastic force acting on the body does not depend on the movement of the ends of the threads, but depends only on the displacement of the body $x$ from the initial initial position:

$$F_x = -k(\Delta l + x) + k(\Delta l - x) = -2kx,$$

where $\Delta l = (v/2)t$ is the absolute value of the mixed ends of the thread at the moment when the body mixing is equal to $x$. Consequently, the body will perform harmonic oscillations with a frequency of $\omega = \sqrt{2k/m} = 10^3$ Hz around the first initial position. The mixing of the body changes over time according to the law

$$x = -\frac{v}{2\omega}\sin \omega t,$$

and thread elongation -

$$\Delta l_1 = \frac{v}{2}t - \frac{v}{2\omega}\sin \omega t,$$

$$\Delta l_2 = \frac{v}{2}t + \frac{v}{2\omega}\sin \omega t.$$

From this it is clear that during four half-periods of oscillations -

$$2n \frac{T}{2} < t < (2n + 1) \frac{T}{2} \left( T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}, n = 0, 1, 2, \dots \right)$$

F1037. A small hole is made in the hollow sphere, through which a narrow parallel beam of light penetrates inside. The inner surface of the sphere reflects light in all directions equally (diffusely) and does not interfere with it. How will the illumination differ in this case at the point diametrically opposite the hole and at all other points of the sphere?

Since, according to the conditions, the inner surface of the sphere does not absorb the light energy incident on it, after repeated reflections, after some time the installation system is in equilibrium. In this case, each small section of the inner surface of the sphere will dissipate all the light energy that hits it evenly in all directions. As a result of establishing equilibrium, the illumination of all internal points of the sphere, except for the point opposite the hole, will be the same. At equilibrium, the total light energy coming to the hole per unit time from all internal points of the sphere will be equal to the energy entering through the hole into the extracting sphere through the hole into the extracting sphere. Thus, after equilibrium is established (namely, this is the situation that is considered in the problem), the hole will be equivalent to a device in which all the energy coming to it from all internal points of the sphere is sent in a narrow beam to a diametrically opposite point.

It is easy to understand that, due to the smallness of the hole, the total energy coming to it from all the internal surfaces of the sphere is equal to the total energy scattered in all directions by any part of the sphere equal in area to the hole (this follows from the inverse nature of the equilibrium state - if all the scattered rays are “forced” to move in the opposite directions, then nothing will change); this reasoning does not apply only to the point diametrically opposite the hole.

So, due to the difference, the shares of energy sent by all internal points both to the hole and to the point opposite the hole will be the same. In addition, exactly the same energy will come to the point opposite the hole and from the hole. Consequently, the illumination of the internal points of the spherical surface will be exactly half the illumination of the point on the part of the sphere opposite the hole. (The smaller the hole area, the more accurately the 1:2 ratio is met.)
