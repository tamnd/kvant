Physics of musical harmony

You know that the sounds that surround us are nothing more than fluctuations in air pressure and density. Among the variety of sounds, some have a strong emotional impact on a person. These are musical sounds. It is impossible to explain their special interaction with the brain only in physical terms. But you can, in the words of Pushkin, “verify harmony with algebra.”

According to legends that have come down from antiquity, the first to try to do this were Pythogoras and his students. They found that equally stretched strings made of the same material produce agreement.

For example, if you take two strings, one of which is half as long as the other, then the sounds extracted from them turn out to be consonants. In this case, musicians say that sounds are distinguished by an octave, and denote them with one note: for example, “do” of the first octave, “do” of the second octave, etc.

Let us see, from a physical point of view, how a string with fixed ends vibrates (Fig. 1). If the string is pulled back and released, it will oscillate, in which the speeds and accelerations of all points are directed equally. *) Such vibrations determine the fundamental tone of the string. It turns out that the frequency $v$ of the fundamental tone of strings made of the same materials and stretched with the same force depends only on their length $l$, and depends inversely: $v \sim 1/l$. Other vibrations of the string are also possible - with a frequency twice, twice, etc. greater than the frequency of the fundamental tone. They are called overtones.

Therefore, the sounds of the Pythagorean scale are unequal - the scale cannot begin with any of the no. To change the key, the musical instrument must be tuned again. For string instruments this is not difficult to do, but the organ, which consists of many pipes - each for its own sound, is tuned when creating it.

Despite this inconvenience, the Pythagogian scale (along with other types of musical scales) served musicians for more than two thousand years - until the 16th century. Then the so-called diotic scale, which was built on frequencies made up of the ratios of the numbers 2, 3 and 5, became widespread. But it also suffered from a similar drawback.

Only for the further assimilation of instrumental musical structure did physical research begin again. Franz M. Mersenne (1588-1648) established how the frequency of vibration of a structure depends not only on its length, but also on the tension force, the second of the structure and the density of the materials from which it is made. Shortly thereafter, the piano was invented, an instrument in which the structures are tuned to successive musical tones with frequencies ranging from 27.5 to 3520 Hz, spanning 7 octaves.

Around the same time, the so-called good temperament scale was created, that is, a chromatic scale in which all successive intervals are chosen among themselves (Fig. 3). The key to its creation was a mathematical coincidence. It turned out that the powers of the number $^{12}/2$ coincide with the Pythagogian ratios with good accuracy (compare them yourself) - the chromatic quantum, for example, differs from the Pyphagian quantum by only 0.11% - such an error is indistinguishable by ear. Therefore, modern musical instruments can be played in any of 24 keys (12 maximum and 12 minimum). The fact that it is possible to achieve consistent sound regardless of tonality is proven by Bach when he wrote his “Well-Tempered Clavier” - 24 sentences and fugues in all keys. Whether there are physical principles for the difference between maximum and minimum, alas, we still do not know.
