FROM THE HISTORY OF FRACTIONS

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
N. Y. VILENKIN

The first fraction people were introduced to was half. Although the names of all the following fractions are related to the names of their signifiers (tri - “third”, four - “quarter”, etc.), this is not the case for half - its name in all languages ​​has nothing to do with the word “two”. The next fraction was a third. These and some other fractions were found in the oldest mathematical texts that have come down to us, compiled more than 5,000 years ago - ancient Egyptian papirucas and Babylonian clipart tablets. Both the Egyptians and Babylonians had special notations for the fractions 1/3 and 2/3 that were not the same as the notations for other fractions.

The Egyptians tried to write all fractions as sums of shares, i.e. fractions in yes 1/n. The only exception was the fraction 2/3. For example, instead of 8/15 they wrote 1/3+1/5. Otherwise it was convenient. In the papirus, written by the Egyptian scribe Ahmes, there is a task: to divide seven loaves among eight people. If you cut each loaf into 8 pieces, you will have to make 49 cuts. And in Egyptian this problem was solved like this. The fraction 7/8 was written as fractions: 1/2+1/4+1/8. Now it is clear that you need to cut 4 loaves in half, 2 loaves into 4 pieces, and only one loaf into 8 pieces (17 cuts in total).

But adding fractions written as fractions was inconvenient. After all, both terms can contain equal parts, and then upon addition a fraction of the form 2/n will appear. But the Egyptians don’t care for such fractions.

The Egyptians also knew how to multiply and divide dobi. But when multiplying, you had to multiply fractions by fractions, and then, perhaps, use the table again. The situation with division is even more complicated.

The Vivilonians took a completely different path. The fact is that the number system in Vivilon was hexadecimal - each unit of the next digit was 60 times larger than the previous one. Viviler, the entries 14″ 42′ 38 meant the number 14·60²+42·60+38, i.e. in our entry the number 52958 (only the Vivilonians did not use our numbers, but other designations made up of Klints). That’s why the Vylonians had dobis not in decimal, but in hexadecimal. In fact, we did and now use such a doby in notation of time and angles. For example, the time 3 hours 17 minutes 28 s can be written like this: 3.17′28″ hours (read 3 whole, 17 sixtieth 28 three thousand six hundred hours). Instead of the words “sixtieths”, “three thousand six hundredths” they said in short: “first small fractions”, “second small fractions”. This is where our words “minute” (Latin for “lesser”) and “second” (Latin for “second”) come from. So the Vivilonian way of designating dobei has retained its meaning to this day.
