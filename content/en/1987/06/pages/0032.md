When hydrogen interacts with oxygen, something similar to the phenomenon of a stone collapse in the mountains occurs, only quickly, almost instantly. Under normal conditions, hydrogen and oxygen are gases with the chemical formulas H₂ and O₂, that is, which molecule consists of two atoms. This is their natural and stable state. To break a H₂ molecule into two atoms, a certain amount of energy must be expended. The presence of platinum in the gas mixture makes it easier to break the H₂ molecule into atoms—on its surface, the H₂→2H reaction occurs relatively easily.

The atom of hydrogen formed on the surface of platinum is extremely unstable; it strives to quickly get rid of its forced loneliness. The time of his “cold” life is very short. It is not for nothing that in nature atoms of hydrogen, oxygen and polyhydrates of substances are combined into indifferent pairs - molecules. An active atom of hydrogen causes the target to undergo further transformations, just as one small pebble causes the target to collapse in the mountains. When it interacts with an O₂ molecule, the reaction H+O₂=OH+O occurs - two active particles are formed at once: a “lonely” oxygen atom and an OH particle (after all, it lacks one hydrogen atom before the water molecule, to which it tends to quickly attach). Active particles - O and OH - further interact with hydrogen molecules

$$\text{OH} + \text{H}_2 = \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{H},$$
$$\text{O} + \text{H}_2 = \text{OH} + \text{H} \text{ и т. д.}$$

In these reactions, not only stable H₂O molecules are formed, but also new active particles, which again interact with hydrogen and oxygen molecules to form new water molecules and new active particles. All elements of the reaction are not independent of each other, but represent the goal of interaction. In each elementary reaction, not only a reaction product is formed, but also the basis for further interactions is formed - new active particles appear. (Remember how, during a rockfall in the mountains, each new stone involves its neighbors in the movement.) Platinum only begins the goal of transformation (decomposition of the H₂ molecule). The reaction can proceed further without her participation.

Apparently, the following sentence is not always true: “Alone in the field is not a warrior.” In a chain reaction, one “militant” atom can lead ten million of its fellows.

It was chain reactions that led to explosions in the coal mines of England. The resulting “mine” gas instantly ignited when the lamps illuminating the mines were lit. The fire in the lamp helped start a chain reaction in the “mine” gas (like platinum in the “explosive” gas). A special reward was required for avoiding a safe lamp for working in mines. A year later, Humphry Davy and his young employee Michael Faraday, the future discoverer of electromagnetic induction, managed to construct a safe lamp in which dangerous gas was removed from the flame area through tubes. They did this without knowing anything about chain reactions.

Naturally, questions arise: under what conditions can a chain reaction begin? Is there a force that can stop or at least slow down such an avalanche?

These questions were studied by Soviet scientists - Academician N. N. Semenov and his colleagues. In particular, by increasing the concentration of molecules (i.e., their number per unit volume), they discovered that the chain process begins at a very specific—critical—concentration. Below this concentration the reaction does not occur, and above it it has an avalanche character. It’s as if an unknown commander gives the order to attack - and ten million units instantly go into battle.
