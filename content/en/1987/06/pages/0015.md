It is often said that the final stages of stellar evolution may be white dwarfs, neutron stars or black holes. It turns out, however, that even after a “turbulent youth” the stars “do not want to die.” Having exhausted their reserves of nuclear energy, they still retain other sources and reserves of energy, allowing them to radiate for a long time even after they should until their “old age”.

Observing white dwarfs is very difficult, especially if they are cool stars (in astronomy they are called “red” white dwarfs). The low brilliance and complexity of their spectra require very sophisticated telexes. At the same time, several thousand white dwarfs are now known, scattered in a sphere with a radius of 100 parsecs ($1$ parsec $\approx 3 \cdot 10^{16}$ m) around the Solar System. In total, white dwarfs make up from 5 to 10% of all stars.

The state of the matter of white dwarfs, non-existent and unattainable under terrestrial conditions, is very important for us to know for the physics of neutron stars - after all, their outer layers consist of matter with approximately the same parameters as inside white dwarfs.

Application

On the connection between the main parameters of white dwarfs

The main characteristics of any star are, of course, its mass, radius, chemical composition, temperature and luminosity. Of course, not all of these values ​​are independent. It is easy to understand, for example, that luminosity should be related to temperature - the hotter the star, the more it emits. We will give here an example of another relationship - between the mass of a white dwarf $M$, its radius $R$ and its ratio $Z/A$. Parameters $Z$ and $A$ are the number in the Periodic Table of elements and the mass number (i.e., the number of nucleons) of the nuclei of those atoms from which the star is “built.” For simplicity, we will assume that the white dwarf is “built” from identical atoms (this assumption will not spoil the qualitative picture that interests us).

Gravitational substances, the star's skimming matter, create internal gravitational pressure. What “resists” this pressure, which ensures the balance of the star? In the case of a star like our Sun, gravitational compression is opposed by gas pressure (plasma pressure). In white dwarfs, gravitational pressure is balanced by the pressure of the electron fluid inside the star. From the equilibrium condition $p_G = p_e$ we can obtain a formula connecting the parameters $M, R, Z$ and $A$ that interest us. But first we need to find $p_G$ and $p_e$. We will do this using dimensional considerations.

It is natural to assume that the formula for the gravitational pressure at the center of the star should be $p_G$ with the mass of the star $M$, its radius $R$ and the gravitational constant $G$. Let us write down the dimensions of these quantities:

$$[p_G] = \kappa r^1 M^{-1} c^{-2},$$
$$[M] = \kappa r, [R] = m, [G] = \kappa r^{-1} M^3 c^{-2}.$$

We will look for the dependence $p_G(G, M, R)$ that interests us in the form $p_G \sim G^x M^y R^z$. Let us write down the conditions for the equilibrium of the gas dimensions of the left and right parts of the written relation:

$$\kappa r^1 M^{-1} c^{-2} = \kappa r^{-1} M^3 c^{-2} = \kappa r^{-1} + y M^3 x + z c^{-2}.$$

This equality can only be satisfied if

$$1 = -x + y, -1 = 3x + z, -2 = -2x.$$

Solving this system of equations, we obtain: $x = 1, y = 2, z = -4$. Therefore, eliminating the formula is:

$$p_G \sim G \frac{M^2}{R^4}.$$
