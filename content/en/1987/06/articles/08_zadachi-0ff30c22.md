Tasks

1. Replace the letters with numbers so that you get true equalities (see figure). In this case, the same numbers must correspond to the same letters, and different numbers to different ones.

2. They say that Tortila did not give the golden key to Pinocchio as simply as A.N. Tolstoy said, but brought out three boxes: red, blue and green. On the red box it was written “Here lies the golden key”, on the blue one - “The green box is empty”, and on the green one - “Here sits a viper.” Tortila read the inscriptions and said: “Indeed, in one box there is a golden key, in the other there is a viper, and the third is empty, but all the inscriptions are incorrect.”

Where is the golden key?

3. Kolya, Viti and Yura had 12, 14 and 22 nuts. When Kolya gave Vita as many nuts as Vitya had, then Vita gave Yura as many nuts as Yura had, and Yura gave Kolya as many nuts as Kolya had left, all the guys had the same amount of nuts. How many nuts did each have?

4. The dashing captains of the sailing ships transporting cotton from Australia to England were not eager to fully load their ships, although they were interested in transporting more cotton. Why?

5. A convex quadrilateral was cut along two straight lines connecting the midpoints of opposite sides. Show that from the resulting four pieces it is always possible to add a parallelogram.

These tasks were proposed to us by: V. N. Rusanov, A. P. Savin, S. V. Dvoryaninov, L. P. Motsalov, V. V. Proizvolov.

At the beginning of the century, England was alarmed by news of disasters in coal mines - terrible explosions claimed the lives of thousands of miners. They erupted due to the ignition of gas, which the miners called “mine gas.” It was not possible to understand the cause of sudden explosions for almost a century. Only in 1916, the German physicist-chemist Walter Nernst proposed that they would arise as a result of reactions that could be called chain reactions. And it took almost 50 more years to create a theory of this phenomenon.

...Have you ever seen rock falls in the mountains? One small pebble, accidentally falling out of its place, carries with it huge masses of other stones rushing down in an avalanche. Let's try to estimate how many stones are involved in such a rockfall. Let the first stone begin its journey at an altitude of 500 m and at every meter of its path involve another neighbor in its movement. Each new stone, in turn, becomes the initiator of the movement of others, others - the next ones, etc. The movement is, as it were, transmitted along a chain. After one meter there will be two stones in the stream, after the second meter - four, after the third - eight, etc. When the avalanche reaches the foot of the mountain, there should already be 2⁵⁰ stones in it. This is a huge number, more than 150 points! Of course, as a result of the situation, the number of stones turns out to be much smaller. Not all stones involve their neighbors in the movement; some stones encounter obstacles on their way and stop. Nevertheless, the number 2⁵⁰ gives some idea of ​​the scale of the chain process. Some chemical reactions also develop according to a similar pattern.

One of the most common substances in nature is water. To form a water molecule - an H₂O molecule - three reacting particles must meet - two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Therefore, for such a reaction, hydrogen and oxygen must be mixed in a quantitative ratio of 2:1. This mixture is called "explosive" gas. In 1817, the English scientist Humphry Davy discovered an amazing phenomenon: in the presence of platinum, a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen reacts

roars with an explosion. Without platinum, the reaction proceeds slowly and only at very high temperatures.

When hydrogen interacts with oxygen, something similar to the phenomenon of a stone collapse in the mountains occurs, only quickly, almost instantly. Under normal conditions, hydrogen and oxygen are gases with the chemical formulas H₂ and O₂, that is, which molecule consists of two atoms. This is their natural and stable state. To break a H₂ molecule into two atoms, a certain amount of energy must be expended. The presence of platinum in the gas mixture makes it easier to break the H₂ molecule into atoms—on its surface, the H₂→2H reaction occurs relatively easily.

The atom of hydrogen formed on the surface of platinum is extremely unstable; it strives to quickly get rid of its forced loneliness. The time of his “cold” life is very short. It is not for nothing that in nature atoms of hydrogen, oxygen and polyhydrates of substances are combined into indifferent pairs - molecules. An active atom of hydrogen causes the target to undergo further transformations, just as one small pebble causes the target to collapse in the mountains. When it interacts with an O₂ molecule, the reaction H+O₂=OH+O occurs - two active particles are formed at once: a “lonely” oxygen atom and an OH particle (after all, it lacks one hydrogen atom before the water molecule, to which it tends to quickly attach). Active particles - O and OH - further interact with hydrogen molecules

$$\text{OH} + \text{H}_2 = \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{H},$$
$$\text{O} + \text{H}_2 = \text{OH} + \text{H} \text{ и т. д.}$$

In these reactions, not only stable H₂O molecules are formed, but also new active particles, which again interact with hydrogen and oxygen molecules to form new water molecules and new active particles. All elements of the reaction are not independent of each other, but represent the goal of interaction. In each elementary reaction, not only a reaction product is formed, but also the basis for further interactions is formed - new active particles appear. (Remember how, during a rockfall in the mountains, each new stone involves its neighbors in the movement.) Platinum only begins the goal of transformation (decomposition of the H₂ molecule). The reaction can proceed further without her participation.

Apparently, the following sentence is not always true: “Alone in the field is not a warrior.” In a chain reaction, one “militant” atom can lead ten million of its fellows.

It was chain reactions that led to explosions in the coal mines of England. The resulting “mine” gas instantly ignited when the lamps illuminating the mines were lit. The fire in the lamp helped start a chain reaction in the “mine” gas (like platinum in the “explosive” gas). A special reward was required for avoiding a safe lamp for working in mines. A year later, Humphry Davy and his young employee Michael Faraday, the future discoverer of electromagnetic induction, managed to construct a safe lamp in which dangerous gas was removed from the flame area through tubes. They did this without knowing anything about chain reactions.

Naturally, questions arise: under what conditions can a chain reaction begin? Is there a force that can stop or at least slow down such an avalanche?

These questions were studied by Soviet scientists - Academician N. N. Semenov and his colleagues. In particular, by increasing the concentration of molecules (i.e., their number per unit volume), they discovered that the chain process begins at a very specific—critical—concentration. Below this concentration the reaction does not occur, and above it it has an avalanche character. It’s as if an unknown commander gives the order to attack - and ten million units instantly go into battle.

Moreover, it turned out that the critical concentration depends on the shape of the vessel and is inversely proportional to the area of ​​its walls. Here is how N. N. Semenov himself writes about this: “From here it was only one step to the assumption that active particles - say, oxygen atoms - when they reach the walls, they are captured by them, drop out of the game and cannot carry out a reaction. Two such atoms, meeting on the wall, form an inactive molecule again.

lu of oxygen, which easily drains into the volume, cleaning the wall. Along the path of the chain - from the place of its origin inside the vessel to the walls - one or another number of reactions occur, and so many particles arise. The narrower the vessel, the shorter this chain, the fewer elementary reactions it contains, the fewer branches it has time to arise.”

As the concentration increases, the number of active particles increases. As the area of ​​the vessel walls increases, their number decreases, since there are more particles captured by the vessel walls and eliminated from the game. If a vessel is gradually filled with reacting gases, then the chain reaction cannot proceed to a certain concentration - more active particles settle on the walls and calm down than are formed in the volume of the vessel. Continuing to further fill the vessel with gases (in other words, increasing the concentration), we must reach such a critical concentration at which more active particles are formed than die on the walls. The surface of the vessel is no longer able to cope with such an avalanche of active particles. There are too many of them. At this moment there is a transition from an inert state to a violent reaction. The critical concentration is the boundary separating the zone of “calm” reaction from the zone of “explosive” activity. It depends not only on which gases react, but also on the container in which these gases are located. Not one commander controls the chain reaction, but two at once. The first - concentration - brings the atoms into battle, the second - the surface of the vessel - tries to stop them. The judge is their critical concentration. Below is not - the second is stronger, above - the first wins.

If we, maintaining the same concentration, reduce the size of the vessel, then we will do so until such an exchange occurs when most of the chains do not have time to branch at all. then the number of active atoms dying on the wall turns out to be greater than the number of newly formed atoms as a result of the reaction. The avalanche will no longer be able to develop, and the practical reaction will stop.

The walls of the vessel are not the only force capable of calming active particles. This can also be done by foreign molecules of impurities, upon contact with which the parts lose their activity. Small amounts of impurities can greatly influence the speed of chain processes.

The results of the experiments were necessary, and many scientists simply did not believe them. For example, the famous German chemist Max Bodenstein explained the results obtained as an experimental error. Even his close colleagues were incredulous about the results of Semenov’s experiments. “I had to go through some unpleasant hours,” the scientist recalls.

This is today Academician N. N. Semenov - the founder of a new science - chemical physics, the author of the classic work “Chain Reactions”, a real book of polychemical and chemical scientists. And then, more than 50 years ago, it took him a lot of courage not to lose confidence in the correctness of his ideas in a difficult situation.

Chain reactions are the basis of multichemical industrial technologies. This includes combustion, the production of flammable materials from oil, and the production of plastics. Further, the explosion of an atomic bomb is a chain reaction, only the active particles in this case are not atoms, but neutrons. When Semenov's students, academicians Yu. V. Khariton and Ya. V. Zeldovich, created the theory of chain decay of uranium in 1939, they were greatly helped by ideas about chain reactions.

It took the world more than 20 years to fully understand the significance of branched chain reactions. In 1956, Academician N. N. Semenov was the first Soviet scientist to be awarded the Nobel Prize for his study of the mechanisms of chemical reactions. In his speech upon receiving it, he said: “The further task of chemistry is to create the possibility of rational control of the rate and direction of chemical transformation. The theory of chain reactions outlines the initial ways of approaching this issue.”
