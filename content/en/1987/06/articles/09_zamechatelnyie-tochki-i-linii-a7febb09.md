⟦folio 32⟧

Wonderful line and dots

Hyperbole was known by the ancient Greek mathematician. It is defined as a set of points whose distance difference to two given points $F_1$ and $F_2$ is constructed. These points are called the foci of the hyperbola.

Another curve that is less known is the conchoid. It is defined as follows: a straight line $l$ and a point $O$ are fixed on the plane and the number $a$ is given. Through the point $O$ all kinds of straight lines are drawn, on each of which segments of length $a$ are laid out in both directions from the point of intersection with the straight line $l$. The second ends of these segments form a conchoid.

Imagine two round islands in the ocean and a boat sailing along the sea border, i.e. being at the same distance from the shores of these islands at all times. If the sizes of the islands are the same, then the boat’s path will be straight, but if not, then the boat will follow a hyperbola.

The ancient Greek mathematician Nycomedes (3rd century BC) solved the problem of Well's trisculation using a conchoid. Let us draw through the point $A$ a line $l$, parallel to the line $OD$, a circle with center at point $A$ and radius $a = AO$. A conchoid constructed along the line $l$, point $O$ and number $a$ will intersect the circle at point $C$. Angle COD is equal to 1/3 of angle $AOD$.

⟦folio 33⟧

A hyperbola is one of the finite sections: it can be obtained by intersecting a finite surface with a plane that does not pass through its vertex and intersects both of its halves. Other finite sections are parabola and ellipse.

A hyperbola is a graph of inverse proportionality: $y = k/x$. When graph points are removed towards infinity, they are pressed against one of the coordinate axes $x$ or $y$. These clamps are connected by its asymptotes. Any hyperbola has asymptotes.

If you place a light source at one of the focuses of a hyperbolic mirror, then, when reflected in the mirror, the light will move as if it were coming from the second focus of the hyperbola. This is an optical property of a hyperbola. It was used by Garin in A. N. Tolstoy’s novel “Engineer Garin’s Hyperbole.”
