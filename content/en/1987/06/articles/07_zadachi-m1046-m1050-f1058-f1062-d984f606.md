we reflect points D and E relative to the given lines OE and OD (O is the point of their intersection); draw a straight line through their images D$_1$ and E$_1$ until it intersects with DO - at point C and with EO - at point A (if D$_1=E_1$, then draw a straight line through D$_1$ arbitrarily, but so that it intersects the rays DO and EO, Fig. 2); we find the third vertex B of the exclusive triangle as the intersection point of lines AD and CE.

$$\angle DOE = \angle AOC = 180^\circ - \frac{1}{2} (\angle A + \angle C) = 90^\circ + \frac{1}{2} \angle B$$

it follows that the problem is solvable only for $\alpha = \angle DOE > 90^\circ$. But this condition is not yet worthy. $$-2 \cos \alpha < DO : EO < -1/2 \cos \alpha \text{ при } \alpha \neq 120^\circ$$

$$DO = EO, \alpha = 120^\circ$$

F1038. Three identical non-stretchable threads are attached at equal distances from each other to a ring with radius $r_0$ and in a similar way - to a ring with radius $2r_0$. The threads are passed through the third ring with radius $r_0$ (see Fig. 3). Ring 1 is fixed in a horizontal plane, and all systems are in equilibrium. Find the distance between the centers of wheels 2 and 3. All pegs are made of the same wire. Neglect the training.

Since the radius of ring 2 is twice the radius of ring 3, its length is twice the length of ring 3, and since the rings are made of the same wire, the mass of ring 2 is half that of ring 3. If $m$ is the mass of ring 3, then the total weight supported by the threads is $3mg$. Therefore, the tension force of each thread between rings 1 and 3 is

$$F_1 = mg.$$

Since there is no trene, the tension force of each thread is the same along its entire length:

$$F_2 = F_1 = mg$$

(see Fig. 3).

Let us now write down the equilibrium conditions for ring 3:

$$3F_1 - mg - 3F_2 \cos \alpha = 0,$$

where

$$\cos \alpha = \frac{2}{3}.$$

Distance between the centers of wheels 2 and 3 (see Fig. 3):

$$x = r_0 \text{ стg } \alpha = r_0 \frac{\cos \alpha}{\sqrt{1 - \cos^2 \alpha}} = r_0 \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}},$$

$$x = \frac{2r_0}{\sqrt{5}}.$$

V. P. Borodin

F1039. A thread is attached to a block lying on an inclined plane and is thrown through a wire loop; at the other end of the thread there is a weight (see figure). In this case, the block is motionless. The sliding friction coefficient is μ.

In the absence of swings along the inclined plane, the force of static friction (its maximum value is equal to μN=μMg cos α), the tension force of the thread T and the component of the gravity force of the block, equal to Mg sin α, act. Since the block is motionless, then from the equilibrium condition of the weight T=mg≤Mg(μ cos α+sin α) (we neglect the friction of the thread on the loop).

When deflected by a small angle φ0, the weight begins to reach equilibrium, i.e., with increasing speed, the tension of the thread T increases, always exceeding the value mg. Finally, this increasing force exceeds the forces holding the block, and then the block begins to move. This occurs subject to

$$mg + \frac{mv^2}{l} > Mg(\mu \cos \alpha + \sin \alpha),$$

i.e. at $v_1^2 > gl(M(\mu \cos \alpha + \sin \alpha) - m)/m$.

The movement of the block will continue after passing through the equilibrium position, when the speed and, consequently, the tension force begins to decrease. Further passing the speed value of the weight $v_1$, the block will continue to move by inertia for some time, spending the accumulated kinetic energy to work against the friction force and “rolling force”.

After the block stops, when the weight begins to fall after the maximum deviation during oscillation, the process will confirm in the opposite direction. The weight will pick up speed again, and the thread will again “pull” the block like a fish, forcing it to travel a certain distance, less than the previous one. Gradually the process will fade away.

G. V. Meledin

F1041. A conducting rod of length $l$ and mass $m$ is suspended on two non-positioned rods of length $h$ to a horizontal axis (see figure). This frame is in a uniform vertical magnetic field, the induction of which is . A current pulse $I_0$ of low intensity $t$ is passed through the frame. Determine the maximum deviation of the frame from the vertical plane. The frame size for the time $t$ is very small.

With a small displacement of the frame during time $t$ the resulting force acting on the rod is

$$F = I_0 Bl.$$

During this time the rod gains momentum

$$p = mv = F\tau = I_0 Bl\tau,$$

and the kinetic energy of the rod becomes equal

$$E = \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{I_0^2 B^2 l^2 \tau^2}{2m}.$$

Subsequently, when the frame is deflected, the potential energy of the rod increases due to a decrease in its kinetic energy. In the extreme position of the frame at the maximum angle of deflection $\alpha_{\max}$ the potential energy is equal to

$$U = mgh (1 - \cos \alpha_{\max}) = 2mgh \sin^2 \frac{\alpha_{\max}}{2}.$$

From the law of conservation of energy -

$$\frac{I_0^2 B^2 l^2 \tau^2}{2m} = 2mgh \sin^2 \frac{\alpha_{\max}}{2}$$

- located:

$$\sin \frac{\alpha_{\max}}{2} = \frac{I_0 Bl\tau}{2m\sqrt{gh}}.$$

V. V. Pai

F1042. Estimate at what distance the railway rails seem to merge. It is assumed that you, having a good understanding of the physics of the observed phenomenon, can yourself set the numerical values ​​of the required quantities.

To carry out the assessment, you can proceed as follows. Place two points on a piece of paper very close to each other - for example, at a distance of $l \approx 1,5$ mm. When removing the sheet from your eyes, estimate the distance at which the points will merge into one. If you have normal vision, this is a distance of $d \approx 2$ m. Angle $\alpha \sim \frac{l}{d}$ is the minimum angular distance between points at which they are distinguishable by the eye as two objects. This angle characterizes your eye.

The rails will appear to be merged when the angular distance between them is $\alpha$. If the rails are located at a distance of $L \approx 1,5$ m from each other, then they will merge into a point at a distance of $D \sim \frac{L}{a} \sim \frac{L}{l} \approx 2$ km.

P. I. Zubkov
