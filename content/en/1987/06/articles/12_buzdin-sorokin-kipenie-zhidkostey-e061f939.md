Boiling liquid

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. I. BUZDIN,
Candidate of Chemical Sciences
V. V. SOROKIN

The process of boiling liquid is familiar to all of us. We say that water in a kettle is boiling when intense steam formation occurs throughout the entire volume of the liquid. This is the difference between boiling and evaporation, in which the process of vaporization occurs only from the open surface.

Boiling begins when the saturated vapor pressure in the bubbles becomes equal to external atmospheric pressure.*) If the saturated vapor pressure is less than atmospheric pressure, a vapor bubble accidentally formed in the liquid should collapse and disappear.

Borderline boiling of liquid

It is well known that every liquid is characterized by a completely defined boiling point at a certain atmospheric pressure. For example, water at normal atmospheric pressure ($p_0=760$ mmHg) boils at 100 °C, but only ($C_7H_8$) at 111 °C.

And at what temperature will a “mixture” of only water, two boiling waters that do not mix with each other, boil? It would seem that the boiling point should lie between 100 °C and 111 °C. However, if you carefully pour just a little into a test tube with water (it ends up above the water, since its density is less than the density of water) and heat it in a water bath, then it turns out that boiling begins at about 95 ° C! Note that when conducting this experiment it is necessary to use a fairly accurate thermometer.

What's the matter? The answer is suggested by experiment. Upon external observation, it is clear that boiling begins at the boiling point interface - only water, and we are dealing with the so-called boundary boiling of a liquid. In this case, the gas formed at the boundary

Vapor pressure

We have come to the conclusion that to determine the saturated vapor pressure of water at a given temperature, one must use reference tables. Is it possible to try to obtain the temperature dependence of saturated vapor pressure ourselves, i.e., to act as an experimenter - a compiler of the table?

Let's consider one of the simple methods of obtaining such a dependence that you can use. The setup for conducting the experiment consists of a chemical beaker filled with cold water, in which a test tube with divisions marked on its walls is immersed upside down. The test tube and thermometer can be hidden in laboratory pants with paws standing on the table.

Let's partially fill the test tube with water, place the glass with the inverted test tube and thermometer on the electronic hotplate and start heating. As the water temperature increases, we will record data on changes in the volume of gas in the test tube, i.e., record the divisions marked on the walls of the test tube.

The gas in a test tube is a mixture of air and saturated water vapor. The total pressure, naturally, is equal to atmospheric pressure (the pressure of the water column in a glass can be ignored, since it is only 17 mm pt. Art. However, with increasing temperature, water vapor makes an increasingly noticeable contribution.

Using the Mendleev-Clapeyron equation, for air in a test tube we write

$$\frac{p_0 V_0}{T_0} = \frac{p_B V}{T}, \quad p_B = p_0 \frac{V_0}{V} \frac{T}{T_0},$$

where $p_0$ is the initial pressure (equal to atmospheric pressure), $V_0$ and $T_0$ are the initial volume and air temperature, respectively. Now, knowing how volume and temperature change, we can find the saturated vapor pressure at a given temperature:

$$p_H = p_0 \left( 1 - \frac{T}{T_0} \frac{V_0}{V} \right).$$

The experimental dependence of saturated vapor pressure on temperature obtained in this way is good according to reference data. The agreement is especially good at temperatures above 80 °C, with an error of less than 5%. At low temperatures, the accuracy of this method decreases. Think about what this is connected with, what the main sources of errors are and how the accuracy of the proposed method could be improved. Try doing laboratory work on determining the temperature dependence of saturated vapor pressure in your school physics classroom.
