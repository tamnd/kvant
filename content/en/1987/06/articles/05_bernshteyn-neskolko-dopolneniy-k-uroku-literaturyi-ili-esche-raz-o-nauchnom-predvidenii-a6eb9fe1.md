⟦folio 17⟧

So in 1920, robots began their victorious march. And very soon this concept migrated to the works of scientists.

Of course, the merit of Karal Capek is not that he introduced the words, the scientists used them (as happened with the word “quark”, heard in a nightmare by the hero of the English writer James Joyce’s “Finnegans Wake”: physicists called quarks hypothetical fundamental particles, of which many elementary particles are supposed to consist). Strictly speaking, it was not Karal Capek who invented the word “robot”. This is how he describes it himself: “... one fine day... the author came up with the idea of ​​writing... in writing. And while the iron was hot, he ran with a new idea to his brother Joseph, the artist, who at that time was standing at the easel... The author summarized it as briefly as he could... “But I don’t know,” said the author, “what I should call these artificial workers. I would call them labours [apparently from the English word labor - P.B.], but it seems to me that this is too bookish." “So call them robots,” the artist muttered,...continuing to prime the canvas...” In his play, Capek raised the question of the social consequences of technological progress, and considered many philosophical problems of the future of science - cybernetics. In a discussion about the play, he said: “I wanted to write a comedy, partly a comedy of science, partly a comedy of truth... The creation of the homunculus is a medieval idea; in order for it to correspond to the conditions of our century, the process of creation must be organized on the basis of mass production... The plan of human development, in the end, out of the power of human hands, began to live according to its own law.” It seems clear why, having created a mechanical (or, rather, electronic) “man,” scientists remembered not the Golem, not some other homunculus, but the robot Karal Capek.

And one more example. Who developed the theories of multi-stage rocket propulsion? The answer to this question will not be difficult. Of course Konstantin Eduardovich Tsiolkovsky! He expressed many fundamental ideas, derived formulas, and indicated ways for the development of rocket technology. His contribution to science is generally recognized and undeniable. But the idea (only an idea!) of an “engine with several stages” was expressed in the 17th century by the French writer, and therefore the philosopher, Cyrano de Bergerac, whose name, of course, is known to many from the drama by E. Rostand. In the work “Another Light, or the States and Empire of the Moon,” published in 1657, the engine with which the hero gets to our natural satellite is described: “As soon as the flame destroyed one row of rockets—they were arranged in six pieces—thanks to a fuse placed at the end of each row, another row caught fire.”

Indeed, there are no limits to human imagination. But in fairness, it is worth noting that a rich imagination alone would not allow writers to become predictors in science. Deep knowledge is also required.

Boris Nikolaevich Bugaev, who took the literary seed “Andrei Bely” for himself, studied at the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Moscow University.

Frasis Bacon graduated from the University of Cambridge. He possessed much special knowledge and corresponded with outstanding scientists of his time. Karl Marx described him as "the founder of English materialism and all modern experimental science."

Karal Capek studied at the Sorbonne and at the University of Prague and was well-versed in many engineering problems.
