In this article we will try to talk about what cannot be seen either with a microscope or a telescope, but which can be understood using fairly simple and familiar concepts. We will talk mainly about white dwarfs and... capacitors. Of course, all readers «Kvant» know what a capacitor is. Readers have probably also heard about white dwarfs. This is the name for very dense stars with a mass approximately the same as that of our Sun, but with a radius several hundred or even a thousand times less than that of the Sun. Of course, we cannot see how the insides of a white dwarf work. But, having talked about a flat capacitor, we can, using physical analogies, get an idea of ​​how these data and stars that are not very similar to the Sun are structured.

Some simple calculations

The mass of a typical white dwarf is $M \sim 2 \cdot 10^{30}$ kg, its radius is $R \sim 10^{-2} R_\odot$ ($R_\odot = 7 \cdot 10^8$ m is the radius of the Sun). We obtain the average density of the substance that makes up the dwarf by dividing the mass of the star by its volume: $Q$ turns out to be $\sim 10^9$ kg/m$^3$.

If we wanted to “build” a white dwarf from individual atoms, we would have to collect them into a large cloud near the “point” in space where we want to “place” the star, and say this cloud to densities about $10^6$ times greater than the average density of our planet. In outer space, clouds of molecules or atoms are crushed under the influence of gravitational forces.

Real white dwarfs, as follows from the theory of the structure and evolution of stars, contain few “heavy” elements or even do not contain them at all. For estimates, we can assume that the white dwarf is “built” from identical atoms, having approximately ten electrons per atom. In this case, the mass of the $m$ atom can be taken to be approximately 20 times greater than the mass of the nucleon, i.e., considered $m \sim 10^{-26}$ kg. The difference in the masses of the nucleon (proton and neutron), as well as the masses of the electron, can, of course, be neglected in a rough calculation. These are completely reasonable assumptions, and they will not actually affect the conclusions we come to.

The number of $n$ atomic nuclei per unit volume is obtained by dividing $\Omega$ by $m$. For our white dwarf, $n$ turns out to be $\sim 10^{35}$ m$^{-3}$. This means that the average volume of space per nucleus is $\sim 10^{-35}$ m$^3$, and the average distance between nuclei is $\sim 10^{-12}$ m. The dimensions of common atoms are of the order of $10^{-10}$ m. So we come to the conclusion that the average distances between nuclei inside a white dwarf are a hundred times smaller than the sizes of those atoms from which we “started to build” the star. It is clear that at high densities the atom as an “electrons + nucleus” system does not exist.

During the scanning process, all electrons in the interior of the white dwarf are already “torn off” from their nuclei and form an electron liquid. How do the nuclei (which can be called “starved ones” in this case) behave? There are two possibilities for them: they either “swim” in the “electron sea”, resembling suspended particles in a liquid, or they form a crystal lattice in which the electron liquid “lints” - then the system of nuclei and electrons is similar in structure to common metals.

Which of the above implementation possibilities actually exists? Are white dwarfs liquid? or crystalline?

Our goal is to clarify this issue. And, as surprising as it may seem at first glance, a flat capacitor will help us.

Flat Capacitor and Plasma Frequency

The combination of electrons and nuclei inside the white dwarf forms a plasma system, which is similar to the plasma in the interior of the Sun or the plasma formed during a general gas discharge. What these systems have in common is the coexistence of positive and negative particles, the interaction between which is caused by Coulomb forces. Let us consider a volume element occupied by plasma between two flat areas. First, let us discuss the simple case when the positive and negative charges in the plasma are equal in absolute value. Let the area of ​​each site be $S$, and the distance between them $d$. We will consider the particle charges to be equal to $\pm e$, and their concentration to be equal to $n_e$. Let's assume that we were able to separate the charges in this volume and place all the positive charges on one pad, and all the negative ones on the other. Essentially we have created a charged parallel plate capacitor. It is obvious that the absolute value of the charge of the plates of this capacitor is $Q=n_eeSd$, and the direction of the field in it is $E=Q/\varepsilon_0S=n_eed/\varepsilon_0$.

As a result, the relative mixture of the part with opposite charges is $d$. The electric field that appears during mixing can be estimated using the above formula for a capacitor. The force acting on one particle is $eE=n_ee^2d/\varepsilon_0$; dividing this force by the mass of the particle $m$, we obtain its acceleration: $n_ee^2d/\varepsilon_0m$. Please note: the force and acceleration of a particle are proportional to its displacement, and they are directed so as to restore equilibrium. But this means that the particle will perform a harmonious oscillation, and we can immediately say what part of these oscillations should be:

$$\omega_0^2 = \frac{n_ee^2}{\varepsilon_0m}.$$ (*)

Oscillations with a frequency $\omega_0$ are called electrostatic, Langmuir or simply plasma.

The reader is probably already deciding what next step we are going to take in our reasoning. Of course, we must try to understand whether these considerations about plasma oscillations cannot be generalized to electron-nuclear plasma in white dwarfs. The answer to this question also allows a generalization of what is actually possible. In electron-nuclear plasma there should also be oscillations caused by mutual mixtures of charges. But this case differs from the simplest case discussed above in that the charge of the nucleus is not equal to the charge of the electron. They also have different masses. And it is very important that the concentration of part in the plasma of a white dwarf is hundreds of thousands of times greater than, say, in solar plasma. At such concentrations, the plasma must be described by quantum laws (whereas solar plasma can be considered a classical gas of charged particles interacting according to Coulomb’s law). It also turns out that the question of plasma oscillations in white dwarfs is closely related to the question of the possibility of the existence of crystalline structures in them. We will now discuss these issues.

Nuclear crystals and stability of the crystal lattice

One of the most common and familiar phase transformations for us is crystallization. No one is surprised that in winter rivers and lakes are covered with ice. Sometimes even the seas freeze. But isn't it amazing that crystallization can occur in the sea of ​​plasma formed by electrons and atomic nuclei inside white dwarfs?

The bodies of these stars are a liquid mixture of nuclei and electrons. Nuclei in such a “solution,” like particles in ordinary liquids, can be added to significant distances, and their movement could be chaotic. Ideas about the construction and stability of lattices of atomic nuclei immersed in a sea of ​​electrons appeared a little more than a few years ago.

Let us also propose that atomic nuclei with charges Ze actually form a crystal lattice, i.e., they are “tied” to certain places in space. The electrons surrounding them “wave”, of course, “walk” throughout this crystal, but on average there are Z electrons near each nucleus. Lattice destruction would obviously occur if the nuclei could be greatly displaced from their equilibrium positions. Small vibrations of nuclei do not lead to destruction of the lattice.

In ordinary solids, vibrations of the crystal lattice are mainly caused by temperature. For a nuclear crystal, the Coulomb interaction plays a very important role. Plasma-type oscillations will occur at any temperature, including very low ones. Our goal now is to estimate the frequency and energy of these oscillations and compare the latter with the potential energy of the nucleus in the crystal lattice and with the value of kT, which characterizes temperature oscillations. If the potential energy turns out to be much greater than kT and the characteristic energy of plasma oscillations, then we can say that the nucleus is “tied” to “its” node in the crystal lattice.

It is convenient to divide our entire “nuclei + electrons” system into conditional cells that have a spherical shape and such that each of them contains one nucleus with charge Ze and Z electrons. If a unit volume contains nₐ nuclei, then the radius r of the cell can be determined from the condition $\frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 n_\alpha = 1$; hence $r \sim n_\alpha^{-1/3}$. In general, each such cell is electrically neutral. Since the number of electrons Z >> 1, we can assume for estimations that the electrons forming clouds with a negative charge are evenly “smeared” throughout the cell. The oscillations of the nucleus in this negative charged cloud will occur at the plasma frequency

$$\Omega_0^2 \sim \frac{n_\alpha(Ze)^2}{\varepsilon_0 m_\alpha} \sim \frac{Z^2 e^2}{\varepsilon_0 m_\alpha r^3}.$$

This is essentially the same formula (*). But only now we are talking about the oscillations of nuclei with charge Ze and mass mₙ.

We have already mentioned that due to the high concentration of particles, the plasma in a white dwarf is described by quantum laws. Naturally, the oscillations in such a plasma must be pure, so that the total energy of oscillations of the crystal lattice, built from atomic nuclei, consists of individual portions - energy quanta. Given the vibration frequency, it is easy to estimate the energy of such a portion. To do this, frequency $\Omega_0$ must be multiplied by Planck’s constant $\hbar \left(\hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}\right)$.

The value $\hbar \Omega_0$ is the characteristic energy of plasma oscillations, relating to one oscillating particle, in our case, to one nucleus. Here we also say that for typical conditions inside white dwarfs, the energy kT of thermal oscillations (again, per frequency) is much less than $\hbar \Omega_0$. This allows us to “forget” about temperature when talking about the fluctuations of the white dwarf’s crystal lattice. (They can verify the validity of the inequality kT << $\hbar \Omega_0$ by substituting into the formula for $\Omega_0$ the numerical values ​​of the parameters characteristic of white dwarfs and taking for

So, all we have to do is compare $U_0$ and $\hbar \Omega_0$. The potential energy of a nucleus “sitting” at a site of a crystal lattice is $U_0 \sim \frac{Z^2 e^2}{\varepsilon_0 r}$. It is sometimes said that this general “Coulomb” formula determines the “depth of the potential well” in which the oscillating nucleus “fell”. We can also say that $U_0$ determines the binding energy of the nucleus in the system of other particles surrounding it.

Let us now write the relation $\hbar \Omega_0 / U_0$ using the above formulas for $\Omega_0$ and $U_0$:

$$\frac{\hbar \Omega_0}{U_0} \sim \left( \frac{\hbar^2 \varepsilon_0}{Z^2 e^2 r_m} \right)^{1/2} = \left( \frac{r_0}{r} \right)^{1/2}.$$

Here $r_0 = \frac{\hbar^2 \varepsilon_0}{Z^2 e^2 m_\pi}$ is a parameter characterizing the kernel with given $Z$ and $m_\pi$. A typical value of $r_0 / r$ for white dwarfs is on the order of $10^{-5}$. (You can get this number yourself by counting the density of matter in the star $\sim 10^9$ kg/m$^3$, and $Z \sim 10$.)

Thus, we are convinced that $(r_0 / r)^{1/2} \ll 1$, and this means that the energy of the flask nuclei inside the white dwarf is less than the potential energy. In other words, the crystal lattice, if it has formed, should not fall apart on its own!

Crystallization in a white dwarf

For six years now we have discussed a situation where temperature-related efficiencies could be ignored. In this case, the inequality $\hbar \Omega_0 \gg kT$ holds. However, the theory of stellar evolution predicts that when the white dwarf was young, nuclear reactions took place in its depths and it was quite hot. After the nuclear reactions ended, the temperature in the star could still be about $10^7$ K. Naturally, at sufficiently high temperatures the dwarf might not be crystalline. In its early history, there may have been a period when $kT$ was greater than $\hbar \Omega_0$. In this case, the main role was played not by plasma, but by temperature fluctuations.

And for this period, it is $kT$ that needs to be compared with $U_0$ in order to resolve the question of whether the crystal lattice existed then.

No, be that as it may, most of the dwarf’s life is spent in conditions where $kT < \hbar \Omega_0 \ll U_0$. Therefore, as it is left, crystallization must occur. During crystallization, the stellar inner regions always remain hotter than the outer ones, so the crystalline structure - the “crust” - first appears from the outside, and only then “grows” into the depths of the dwarf.

During crystallization, energy is released, which is ultimately radiated by the star. Calculations show that this energy is not so small. It is natural to compare it with the total gravitational energy (this is the name of the energy that would be required to destroy a gravitational ball and “spread” all its constituent particles at sufficiently large distances from each other). The comparison suggests that the energy released during crystallization can reach fractions of a percent or even several percent of the total gravitational energy of a white dwarf. Another source of energy emitted by a dwarf is its thermal energy. Provided that the temperature of the internal crystalline nuclear lattice is close (the latter, according to estimates, can be on the order of $10^7$ K), the thermal energy reserves can be comparable to the crystallization energy. In principle, we can assume that a white dwarf, after the cessation of nuclear reactions in it, is in a state of emitting energy of the order of a percentage of its gravitational energy.

We can now formulate the main choice of our story about white dwarfs. Not very hot stars of this type should be crystalline; they are crystalline; as soon as their temperature drops to approximately $10^6$ K.

Observing white dwarfs is very difficult, especially if they are cool stars (in astronomy they are called “red” white dwarfs). The low brilliance and complexity of their spectra require very sophisticated telexes. At the same time, several thousand white dwarfs are now known, scattered in a sphere with a radius of 100 parsecs ($1$ parsec $\approx 3 \cdot 10^{16}$ m) around the Solar System. In total, white dwarfs make up from 5 to 10% of all stars.

The state of the matter of white dwarfs, non-existent and unattainable under terrestrial conditions, is very important for us to know for the physics of neutron stars - after all, their outer layers consist of matter with approximately the same parameters as inside white dwarfs.

Application

On the connection between the main parameters of white dwarfs

The main characteristics of any star are, of course, its mass, radius, chemical composition, temperature and luminosity. Of course, not all of these values ​​are independent. It is easy to understand, for example, that luminosity should be related to temperature - the hotter the star, the more it emits. We will give here an example of another relationship - between the mass of a white dwarf $M$, its radius $R$ and its ratio $Z/A$. Parameters $Z$ and $A$ are the number in the Periodic Table of elements and the mass number (i.e., the number of nucleons) of the nuclei of those atoms from which the star is “built.” For simplicity, we will assume that the white dwarf is “built” from identical atoms (this assumption will not spoil the qualitative picture that interests us).

Gravitational substances, the star's skimming matter, create internal gravitational pressure. What “resists” this pressure, which ensures the balance of the star? In the case of a star like our Sun, gravitational compression is opposed by gas pressure (plasma pressure). In white dwarfs, gravitational pressure is balanced by the pressure of the electron fluid inside the star. From the equilibrium condition $p_G = p_e$ we can obtain a formula connecting the parameters $M, R, Z$ and $A$ that interest us. But first we need to find $p_G$ and $p_e$. We will do this using dimensional considerations.

It is natural to assume that the formula for the gravitational pressure at the center of the star should be $p_G$ with the mass of the star $M$, its radius $R$ and the gravitational constant $G$. Let us write down the dimensions of these quantities:

$$[p_G] = \kappa r^1 M^{-1} c^{-2},$$
$$[M] = \kappa r, [R] = m, [G] = \kappa r^{-1} M^3 c^{-2}.$$

We will look for the dependence $p_G(G, M, R)$ that interests us in the form $p_G \sim G^x M^y R^z$. Let us write down the conditions for the equilibrium of the gas dimensions of the left and right parts of the written relation:

$$\kappa r^1 M^{-1} c^{-2} = \kappa r^{-1} M^3 c^{-2} = \kappa r^{-1} + y M^3 x + z c^{-2}.$$

This equality can only be satisfied if

$$1 = -x + y, -1 = 3x + z, -2 = -2x.$$

Solving this system of equations, we obtain: $x = 1, y = 2, z = -4$. Therefore, eliminating the formula is:

$$p_G \sim G \frac{M^2}{R^4}.$$
