The specialist is referring to the internal energy of the air that fills our homes, and under typical residential conditions, the air behaves like an ideal gas (more precisely, like a mixture of ideal gases). The energy of an ideal gas is proportional to the absolute temperature $T$ and the amount of matter, i.e., the mass of the gas $m$. Therefore, for the internal energy of air we can write

$$U = amT,$$

where $a$ is some constant.

Now the mistake that the scientist made is becoming very serious. He stated that in this situation the air mass $m$ is a function of temperature. And the reality is that the living space is not isolated from the outside world; when the air is thawed, it heats up, expands and partially comes out through the cracks and pores in the walls. Note that the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation is usually applied in the case when the mass $m$ is fixed. In the problem we are considering, this condition is not satisfied and, according to equation (1), the internal energy of the air, if the product $mT$ is constant, must remain unchanged. The increase in the average kinetic energy of an individual molecule is compensated by a decrease in the number of molecules.

The air temperature in the room will differ from the operating temperature of the human body. Too low a temperature causes severe hypothermia in the body, as a result of which it impedes the process of chemical reactions that ensure metabolism.

Being an astrophysicist, Emden could not help but remember the role of the Sun as its figurative “stove” that warms the Earth with its radiation. Although the Earth radiates almost all the energy received from the Sun back into space, the remaining energy that appears to be released into the environment is sufficient to maintain the temperature necessary for life on the Earth's surface. But let's return to the problems of heating residential premises.

Equations (1) and (2) refer to temperature in kelvins. The absolute temperature range is called Kelvin in honor of the English physicist W. Thomson, who received the title of Lord Kelvin for his great services in the field of science and technology. The title was chosen for the purpose of the river on the banks of which the University of Glasgow stands. Thomson taught physics at this university for fifty years. It is noteworthy that other options for the title were jokingly discussed in the family circle - Lord Cable or Lord Compass. They reflected two of Thomson's achievements: he participated in the creation of the first transatlantic cable from America to England in 1866 and introduced radical conditions in the design of compasses for seagoing ships. Therefore, it is not surprising that, while developing the fundamentals of thermodynamics, Thomson wondered about the rationality of our heating system. In 1852, he published a work in which he showed that the use of so-called heat pumps is several times more profitable than conventional heating devices. In heat pumps, which operate on the principle of a refrigeration machine, energy is expended to transfer heat from colder load air to warmer air *).

Mikhelson, known for his work in the field of thermal radiation theory, made a report “On diapasic heating,” in which he largely developed and supplemented Thomson’s ideas. Mikhelson proposed using the processes of evaporation and condensation of the working substance in heat pumps, which is what is done in modern designs. Over the past decades, there has been a fairly widespread use of heat pumps for heating.
