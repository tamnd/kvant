Can white be black?

V. V. MAYER

Let's start with a very simple observation.

Place pieces of white and black paper side by side and create complete darkness in the room. Then you will not see a single leaf, i.e. both of them will be equally black.

It would seem, not at all! under what conditions white paper cannot be blacker than black. And yet this is not so. Try to come up with and perform an experiment in which white turns out to be more black than black. But first, read the next paragraph.

A body that, at any temperature, completely absorbs the radiation of any particle falling on it is called absolute black.*) It is clear that this is an idealization; in nature there are no absolute black bodies. The bodies that we promise to call black (soot, soot, black velvet and paper, etc.) are actually gray, that is, they partially absorb and sometimes scatter the light that falls on them. Consequently, to solve the problem, it is possible, for example, to make a body from white paper that is closer to absolute black than black paper. Now the solution we are looking for is almost very convenient.

It turns out that a spherical cavity with a non-sky response can serve as a completely good model of an absolute black body. If the diameter of the response does not exceed 1/10 of the diameter of the cavity, then (as the corresponding calculation shows) the beam included in the response properties can exit back only after multiple scatterings or reflections from different points of the cavity wall. But with each “sporic interaction” of the beam with the wall, the light energy is partially absorbed, so that the fraction of radiation that comes back from the response is negligible. Therefore, we can assume that the response cavities practically completely absorb light of any wavelength, just like an absolute black body.

A specific device for experiment can be made, for example, like this. From cardboard, glue a box measuring approximately 100x100x100 mm with an opening lid. Cover the inside of the box with white paper, and cover the inside with black ink, ink, or, even better, cover it with black paper from photo packages. Make responses in the lid with a diameter of no more than 10 mm. You can see a photo of such devices on the screensaver.

Return and death

Having fallen ill, Ramanujan begins to think about returning to his homeland. Only by the beginning of 1919 did his health improve enough to make a long trip by sea. A place was ready for him at the University of Madras - his words reached India. Ramanujan writes a letter of gratitude to the rector, apologizing for the fact that recently he has no longer given the opportunity to work with decent intensity. But he was never able to start working at the university. He managed to do it in his homeland for less than a year. After three months in Madras, Ramanujan moved to Kumbakonam. In January 1920, he sent his last letter to Hardy, where he reported on his work on a new class of theta function. Neither doctors nor relatives can persuade a terminally ill scientist to resume work. On April 26, 1920, Ramanujan died. He was not yet 33 years old.

Memory

The news of Ramanujan's death shocked his friends both in India and England. They felt it was their duty to understand the amazing phenomenon that Ramanujan was. Hardy writes:

“It is possible that the great days of formulas are over and Ramanujan should have been born 100 years earlier; but he was the greatest formula maker of his time.”

More than half a century has passed, and today we clearly see what Hardy and his contemporaries could not have foreseen. Ramanujan's genius turned out to be in tune not only with the past, but also with the future of mathematics. Ramanujan's arithmetic formulas rarely turned out to be key in the new stages of algebraic number theory, and one could only wonder how he could see them without knowing something without which they could not be seen. And then came a revival of interest in specific explicit formulas both within mathematics and in the field of its applications. Modern mathematical and theoretical physics sometimes turns to very abstract branches of mathematics, and very sophisticated explicit formulas play a role. Here are two recent examples related to Ramanujan.

R. Baxter, who became famous for constructing exactly solvable models of statistical mechanics, when using the “rigid hecagon” model, unexpectedly discovered that he was constantly dealing with the Rogers–Ramanujan (Box 4 on p. 19) and Ramanujan identities.
