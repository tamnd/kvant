Letter to Cambridge
At the very beginning of 1913, Cambridge University professor G. G. Hardy received a letter from distant Madras. By the age of 36, Hardy was already one of the leading specialists in analysis and number theory, the author of a number of excellent mathematical works. The sender of the letter, Srinavzaa Ramanujan, worked as a clerk in the accounting department of the Madras Post Office with a more than modest salary of 20 feet per year. He reported about himself that he did not have a university education and after graduating from school he studied mathematics independently, not following the accepted system, but “choosing his own path.” The mathematical content of the letter looks rather clumsy - one can easily mistake the author for a self-confident amateur.

In itself, such a letter could not make much of an impression on Hardy. But a number of formulas were attached to the letter, which were proposed to be published if they were interesting, which the author himself could not do because of his poverty. Viewing the formulas of the real Hardy: he realized that he was dealing with an unnatural phenomenon. He answers Ramanujan with interest, and intense correspondence ensues between them (today it seems surprising how quickly letters went between India and England back then). Hardy gradually collected about 120 different formulas.

Ramanujan's formulas concerned mainly the correspondence between infinite radicals (Box 2), infinite series, products and continued fractions (Boxes 1, 3, 4), and identities between integrals. First of all, it was clear that they went far beyond

Box 1. Example of an infinite sum calculated by Ramanujan

$$1 - 5 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 + 9 \left(\frac{1 \cdot 3}{2 \cdot 4}\right)^3 - 13 \left(\frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5}{2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6}\right)^3 + \dots = \frac{2}{\pi}.$$

This amazing formula is one of the ones Ramanujan appended to Hardy's first letter. How does the sum of the alternating series $a_0 + a_1 + a_2 + \dots$ with a common term

$$a_n = (-1)^n (4n + 1) \left(\frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot \dots \cdot (2n - 1)}{2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot \dots \cdot (2n)}\right)^3$$

may suddenly turn out to be equal to 2/π, Hardy could not understand for a long time. The reader can verify the validity of this formula as an approximate equality using a calculator. Proving an exact equality is not elementary.

Miracle from Kumbakonam

How did the mathematician who so surprised Hardy turn out to be? Srinivaza Ramanujan Iyengor was born on December 22, 1887 in the south of India in the village of Erode. His childhood was spent mainly in the small town of Kumbakonam (260 km from Madras), where his father worked as an accountant in a small textile shop. Ramanujan belonged to the Brahmins, but wealth was no longer the lot of his relatives. His parents, and his mother especially, were deeply replicative. Ramanujan was brought up in the traditions of castes. Childhood spent in a city where every stone is associated with an ancient religion, surrounded by people who constantly felt that they belonged to a higher caste, played a big role in the development of Ramanujan.

From the age of 5, Ramanujan was in school; by the age of 10, he graduated from primary school. He begins to show extraordinary abilities and receives a scholarship that provides education at a high school at half the cost. At the age of 14, a student from Madras gives him a two-volume manual on Loni's trionometry. Soon Ramanujan studied trionometry, and the student had the opportunity to use his advice in solving problems. The first stories and legends date back to this period. It is stated that he himself discovered "Euler's formula for sine and cosines" and was very upset to find this formula in Loney's second volume.

The “little Brahmin” believes that in mathematics, as in other sciences, one should look for the “highest truth” inherent in it, the teacher explains. The elders give vague references to the Pythagorean theorem, and even to calculations with percentages.

Box 3. Numerical exact with infinite sum and whole fraction

$$1 + \frac{1}{1.3} + \frac{1}{1.3.5} + \frac{1}{1.3.5.7} + \frac{1}{1.3.5.7.9} + \dots + \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi e}{2}}.$$

This is perhaps Ramanujan's most beautiful formula, a true work of mathematical art. She unexpectedly connected an infinite series and an infinite whole fraction. It is surprising that neither the series nor the whole fraction can be expressed in terms of the known constants l and e, and their sum inadmissibly turns out to be equal to $\sqrt{\pi e}/2$!

"Synopis of elementary results of pure and applied mathematics"

This two-volume manual by the English mathematician Carara, written in 1880-1886, came to Ramanujan in 1903 - he was then 16 years old. This book played a huge role in the formation of Ramanujan. It collected 6165 theorems and formulas, almost without proof, with minimal explanations. The book is mainly devoted to algebra, trigonometry, analysis, and analytical geometry.

Karar's book stimulated the little one to independently deduce formulas. Those who knew Ramanujan during these years speak about this. The area of ​​its main interests gradually changes: mathematical squares, then the squaring of the circle (it is found with an accuracy that allows one to calculate the length of the equator with an error not exceeding 1-2 m, according to the legend) and, finally, the turn of endless rows comes. This is already the beginning of a true mathematical life!

Karar's book was successful enough to shape Ramanujan's mathematical world. But focusing on this book had other consequences. Since the book did not contain proofs, and at best only suggestive messages, Ramanujan developed a unique method for establishing mathematical truth. Moreover, it is deprived of suitable guidelines in India to carry out rigorous proofs.

“His understanding of the essence of mathematical proof was more than vague; he SEL to all his results, both early and later, both true and false, with the help of a mixture of intuitive proofs, inductive messages and logical reasoning ... "

The Mathematical syllabus of Ramanujan actually completely decided during these years - the direction of scientific research, he never changed the way of thinking. The message that can be conveyed here is that Ramanujan was formed under difficult conditions. Under normal circumstances, he would undoubtedly have become a mathematician with better professional training, but can we be sure that he would have been equally unique? Would Ramanujan have been able to see so much if, from childhood, he had been taught the rules of behavior in mathematics and would have brought his results to publication with external evidence, would he have built his mathematical world on the basis of everything accessible to mankind, and not on a relatively small number of facts?
