Setsu Aiyar and Ramachandra Rao

Letter to Cambridge
At the very beginning of 1913, Cambridge University professor G. G. Hardy received a letter from distant Madras. By the age of 36, Hardy was already one of the leading specialists in analysis and number theory, the author of a number of excellent mathematical works. The sender of the letter, Srinavzaa Ramanujan, worked as a clerk in the accounting department of the Madras Post Office with a more than modest salary of 20 feet per year. He reported about himself that he did not have a university education and after graduating from school he studied mathematics independently, not following the accepted system, but “choosing his own path.” The mathematical content of the letter looks rather clumsy - one can easily mistake the author for a self-confident amateur.

In itself, such a letter could not make much of an impression on Hardy. But a number of formulas were attached to the letter, which were proposed to be published if they were interesting, which the author himself could not do because of his poverty. Viewing the formulas of the real Hardy: he realized that he was dealing with an unnatural phenomenon. He answers Ramanujan with interest, and intense correspondence ensues between them (today it seems surprising how quickly letters went between India and England back then). Hardy gradually collected about 120 different formulas.

Ramanujan's formulas concerned mainly the correspondence between infinite radicals (Box 2), infinite series, products and continued fractions (Boxes 1, 3, 4), and identities between integrals. First of all, it was clear that they went far beyond

Box 1. Example of an infinite sum calculated by Ramanujan

$$1 - 5 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 + 9 \left(\frac{1 \cdot 3}{2 \cdot 4}\right)^3 - 13 \left(\frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5}{2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6}\right)^3 + \dots = \frac{2}{\pi}.$$

This amazing formula is one of the ones Ramanujan appended to Hardy's first letter. How does the sum of the alternating series $a_0 + a_1 + a_2 + \dots$ with a common term

$$a_n = (-1)^n (4n + 1) \left(\frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot \dots \cdot (2n - 1)}{2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot \dots \cdot (2n)}\right)^3$$

may suddenly turn out to be equal to 2/π, Hardy could not understand for a long time. The reader can verify the validity of this formula as an approximate equality using a calculator. Proving an exact equality is not elementary.
