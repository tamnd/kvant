Box 2. Infinitely repeating radicals

$$\sqrt{1+2\sqrt{1+3}\sqrt{1+4}\sqrt{1+...}}=3.$$

Ramanujan received this beautiful formula back in his school years as follows: he wrote a sequence of very significant equalities

$$n(n+2)=n\sqrt{1+(n+1)(n+3)}=n\sqrt{1+(n+1)\sqrt{1+(n+2)(n+4)}}=...,$$

and then substituted $n=1$. Ramanujan was not interested in the question of the lawfulness of the transition to the preposition. By doing the same, the reader can try to obtain the formula on his own!

Miracle from Kumbakonam

How did the mathematician who so surprised Hardy turn out to be? Srinivaza Ramanujan Iyengor was born on December 22, 1887 in the south of India in the village of Erode. His childhood was spent mainly in the small town of Kumbakonam (260 km from Madras), where his father worked as an accountant in a small textile shop. Ramanujan belonged to the Brahmins, but wealth was no longer the lot of his relatives. His parents, and his mother especially, were deeply replicative. Ramanujan was brought up in the traditions of castes. Childhood spent in a city where every stone is associated with an ancient religion, surrounded by people who constantly felt that they belonged to a higher caste, played a big role in the development of Ramanujan.

From the age of 5, Ramanujan was in school; by the age of 10, he graduated from primary school. He begins to show extraordinary abilities and receives a scholarship that provides education at a high school at half the cost. At the age of 14, a student from Madras gives him a two-volume manual on Loni's trionometry. Soon Ramanujan studied trionometry, and the student had the opportunity to use his advice in solving problems. The first stories and legends date back to this period. It is stated that he himself discovered "Euler's formula for sine and cosines" and was very upset to find this formula in Loney's second volume.

The “little Brahmin” believes that in mathematics, as in other sciences, one should look for the “highest truth” inherent in it, the teacher explains. The elders give vague references to the Pythagorean theorem, and even to calculations with percentages.
