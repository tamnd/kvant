This is the idea of ​​the PUMA experiment (scientific supervisors R.Z. Sagdeev (USSR), U. Kissel (Max Planck Society, Germany), J.L. Berto (France)).

A number of instruments were intended to measure the physical characteristics of the dust flow - the number of parts of different masses, their sizes, and densities.

Numerous instruments were also installed on board the stations to study the interaction of cometary ions with the interplanetary medium, which, as is known, is filled with plasma emitted by the solar corona - the solar wind.

What new did we learn during this meeting with the comet?

Analysis of television images transmitted from the stations to Earth showed that the nucleus of Halley's comet is a monolithic body of irregular shape. Its dimensions are 8x8x16 km. This is much more than scientists assume (2-5 km). The volume of the core, according to rough estimates, is approximately 5·10¹¹ m³. The nucleus is rotated around an axis, which is located inside the cone at a distance of ±45° from the normal to the orbital plane. The direction of the doctor coincides with the direction of orbital motion. The reflectivity of the core is very low. The comet's nucleus is one of the darkest bodies in the solar system. Dust emission occurs only on the illuminated part of the core. There are several areas on the surface (approximately 10% of the area), from which dust escapes in relatively narrow streams (jets). Dust, however, leaves the surface and outside the chips. Above the surface there is a thin (about 1 km) layer, inside which the dust is accelerated by a gas flow from zero speed to several hundred meters per second. Features resembling ring craters are visible on the surface.

The photographs taken by the Western European Giotto apparatus added clarification to this picture only in the last question: ring craters are undoubtedly real; they are clearly visible on processed sigma images near the terminator (the boundary of shadow and light), where there is little dust above the surface and lighting conditions are most favorable.

Measurements of the thermal radiation of the core made by the Veni-1 spectrometer made it possible to determine the surface temperature. From preliminary calculations based on heat balance conditions, it followed that on the day side of the comet it should be 200 K. However, measurements for twice the values ​​of $T=380 \pm 30$ K.

This can be explained, believes the Soviet scientist V.I. Moroz, if we assume that the surface of the core is covered with a layer of “weight” (similar to what is often the case in cities with snowdrifts). This layer absorbs solar energy, part of it is re-emitted in the infrared range, and part of it is transferred by thermal conduction down to the inner “clean” surface of the core, where the temperature is about 200 K.

What is the structure of this layer? Its thickness is most likely no more than 0.1 mm, otherwise it greatly weakens the natural flow. At the same time, it must be solid, otherwise the core would not be so black. Apparently, there are many small particles in it, and gas passes freely through the pores. The surface layer in some places cracks from time to time, and then an active region is formed with a particularly powerful outflow of matter.

The formation of such a layer is an interweaving of natural ones, if we remember that cometary ice is transported with particles of refractory substances, and during evaporation after the gas leaves, their concentration on the surface increases.

The temperature of 380 K refers to the warmest point of the core, which is on the dayside at the equator. This point is shifted from the obtained one by approximately one hour due to the thermal inertia of the surface layer. It is much colder on the night side; According to indirect estimates, the temperature here is 180-200 K.

It was possible to estimate the mass and average density of the comet's nucleus. The mass was determined by the reactive effect, which is created by the emission of gas and dust towards the Sun. The calculations were carried out independently by the Swedish scientist G. Rickman and the Soviet scientists R. Z. Sagdeev, P. E. Elyasberg, V. I. Moroz. Results
