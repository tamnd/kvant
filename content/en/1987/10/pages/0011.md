In lazy comets, the remaining “liquid” from our star must again turn into ice, and so on with each subsequent return.

In the 20th century, the idea of ​​the icy nucleus of comet novae was revived by the American astrophysicist F. Whipple. He proposes a model (1950), according to which the cometary nucleus is a cosmic iceberg - a monolithic body of irregular shape, consisting of frozen volatile substances (H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, CO₂, etc.) mixed with particles of meteorite matter. When the cometary nucleus approaches the Sun, volatile substances evaporate from its surface, and gas flows arise that carry along dust particles - forming a coma and tail.

Soviet astronomer B. A. Vorontsov-Velyaminov believed that the comet’s nucleus consists mainly of refractory substances (silicates, metals) and only on the surface there is a layer of frozen gases. The Dutch astrophysicist M. Greenberg generally agreed with Whipple, but believed that complex organizations of matter should be “added” to the hypothetical composition of the nucleus. He proposed that the comet's nucleus is not an iceberg, but rather a kind of snowdrift, and it consists of particles of interterrestrial matter, “compressed” into a complex structure - a cylinder several microns long, silicate inside, a layer of organics on top, and a layer of ice on top of the organics.

There were a large number of hypotheses about the origin of comets. According to one of them, first proposed by Lagrange (1788), and then developed by the Soviet scientist S.K. Vsesvyatsky (1955), cometary nuclei could be ejected from the bowels of giant planets and their satellites during volcanic eruptions. When the American Voyaders discovered volcanic activity on Jupter's moon Io, this assumption gained a kind of “second wind.” Nevertheless, the attitude towards this hypothesis is very skeptical - there are too many “buts”.

Most experts believe that the outstanding Dutch astronomer Jan Oort managed to get the answer to the question of the origin of comets. Of course, at the beginning the correctness of a particular hypothesis is not decided by a majority vote, and only experimental facts provide objective evidence. Nevertheless, Oort's hypothesis (1950) has virtually no flaws.

Oort suggested that the Solar System is surrounded by a giant “cloud” of comets, extending at distances from 20,000 to 200,000 astronomical units (1 AU = 150,000,000 km) from the Sun, i.e. far beyond the planetary system (the orbit of Pluto, the last planet, is about 40 AU).

What makes comets leave their “shelter”? Although the Oort region is estimated to contain 10¹¹÷10¹² comets, their collisions are so rare*) that they cannot be the cause that pushes them out of the cloud. It turns out that the close passage of a star is responsible for this. They change the speed of movement of comets and from time to time they are thrown either outside the Solar System or towards the Sun.

However, the origin of the Oort cloud itself, or, as it is called, the bank or safe of comets, remains unclear. Solving this problem is one of the main goals of the space experiment to Halley's comet. Scientists hoped that observing the comet's nucleus at close range, determining its composition, and examining part of the comet with the “hands” of automata would make it possible to understand how the Oort bank was formed. Is it part of the protoplanetary matter - the gaseous snout nebula, from which the Sun, planets and comets emerged at approximately the same epoch? Or maybe the Oort bank has a galactic origin?
