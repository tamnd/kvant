A few years ago, the inexhaustible Erpö Rubik came up with a tserrushka, in which 11 chips located in the nodes of a square lattice, using three carriages, make “claw decisions” down - up (3 rows of 3 chips at once) and left - right (2 rows of 4 chips separately). As usual, the task is to restore, after several arbitrary shifts, the original order of tiles shown in figure 1. With this puzzle we will once again demonstrate the effectiveness of the techniques that we introduced readers to in «Kvant» No. 5.
First of all, let's look at how chips are represented under the influence of various commutators - operations like “there - here - from there - out”. If “there” is viz, and “siod” is bleiben (“from there” and “otsioda”, naturally, up and to the right), then depending on which of the two horizontal rows is shifted, upper or lower, two operations are obtained - A and B (Fig. 2). Both of them represent cyclic chips: A - along the arrows, B - along the green ones. The natural third option is to move left and right immediately on the horizontal cards. The first operation C represents the chips along the perimeter of the wall counterclockwise. If at first they are in the original order, then after applying C they will move like this:

Fig. 1.

7→8→9→cell→red→3→2→1→4→7. The next step is to consider the commutators of operations A, B and C. However, only one of them can be bypassed - K=AB^{-1} A^{-1} B. It swaps the places of tokens 1 and 7 and simultaneously 5 and 6 (in the original position). Now we will be able to imagine any two tiles standing one after the other along the perimeter of the puzzle (at the same time 5 and 6 are represented at the same time). For example, for chips 1 and 3 this is done like this: we move them using C² to places 7 and 1, perform K and return them back. Operations of this type (C^n K C^{-n}, n=0, ±1,±2,±3) allow you to obtain any permutation of all tokens except 5 and 6; check it out! But doesn't it follow that all these chips will fall into place, but 5 and 6 will be rearranged? It turns out not. Let us explain this by omitting, for lack of space, the most essential thing—evidence. Any permutation can be represented in the following ways. (For example, cycle A will be obtained if you sequentially swap the red chip with chips 6, 5, 4, 1, 2, 3 or chip 6 with 5, 4, 1, ...). In this case, the number of exchanges will either always be even - then the permutation is called even, or odd - then it is odd. If, after several shifts, the cards returned to their original position, then each of them was shifted an even number of times. From this it follows that the resulting permutation will be even, which means that it is impossible to obtain exactly one pairwise exchange.
