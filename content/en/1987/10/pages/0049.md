At the same time, as the scope of computer application expanded, many tasks appeared, as they said then, of a non-executive nature, for which the server difficulty was presented by the original problem of strictly formulating the problem. The language of mathematical analysis and algebraic formulas turned out to be insufficient for these problems. Especially such tasks were their logical nature (such as “wolf-goat-cabbage” problems, “weighing”, “bulls and cows”, etc.), as well as the need to work with complexly organized information. Nevertheless, mathematics came to the rescue here, but not so much its applied areas as deeply theoretical sections related to the justification of mathematics itself: mathematical logic, the theory of recursive functions and the theory of normal algorithms, developed in the 40s by the famous Soviet mathematician Andrei Andreevich Markov.

The first declarative language was the LISP language (from English LIST Processing), developed in the USA in the early 60s. It is based on the theory of recursive functions, generalized to information structures that have the form of perarchine lists, i.e., sequences of members, each of which could have its own structure. An example of structure lists is the contingent of school students: the school community is divided into groups of classes from 1st to 10th, each group is classes A, B, C, etc., each class is a list of units (where they exist), each unit is a group of students, ordered alphabetically. The LISP language is still widely used today.

Some declarative properties were inherent in the imperative language SNOVOL, also developed in the early 60s in the USA for processing text information. Its important property was the pattern operation, which made it possible to single out quite complex combinations of words and characters in the text with “one blow”.

The properties of LISP and SNOWOL were combined in the declarative language REFAL (from RECURSIVE FUNCTIONAL ALGORITHMS), developed in the late 60s in the USSR.

Concluding a comparison of declarative and imperative languages, we see that the declarative property has the formulation of the problem, supports the solution on a computer, and the imperative property brings the recording of the algorithm closer to how it will actually be used on the computer. An attempt to combine these properties in one language has recently led to the emergence of a wide range of languages ​​that allow, through formal transformations of a program, to change the correspondence of its declarative and imperative properties. This family of languages ​​includes the French language PROLOGUE (from the French PROgrammation LOGique), used in projects for the development of 5th generation computers and their software.
