Equations (1) and (2) refer to temperature in kelvins. The absolute temperature range is called Kelvin in honor of the English physicist W. Thomson, who received the title of Lord Kelvin for his great services in the field of science and technology. The title was chosen for the purpose of the river on the banks of which the University of Glasgow stands. Thomson taught physics at this university for fifty years. It is noteworthy that other options for the title were jokingly discussed in the family circle - Lord Cable or Lord Compass. They reflected two of Thomson's achievements: he participated in the creation of the first transatlantic cable from America to England in 1866 and introduced radical conditions in the design of compasses for seagoing ships. Therefore, it is not surprising that, while developing the fundamentals of thermodynamics, Thomson wondered about the rationality of our heating system. In 1852, he published a work in which he showed that the use of so-called heat pumps is several times more profitable than conventional heating devices. In heat pumps, which operate on the principle of a refrigeration machine, energy is expended to transfer heat from colder load air to warmer air *).

Thomson's Eden did not receive development or practical application for many years. In 1920, at the first congress of Soviet physicists, Professor V. A. Mikhelson, known for his work in the field of thermal radiation theory, made a report “On diapasic heating,” in which he largely developed and supplemented Thomson’s ideas. Mikhelson proposed using the processes of evaporation and condensation of the working substance in heat pumps, which is what is done in modern designs. Over the past decades, there has been a fairly widespread use of heat pumps for heating. In particular, in the winter of a year, a heating heat pump unit began operating in the Yalta boarding house “Druzhba”, where heat is extracted from sea water with a temperature of 8 ° C.
