Box 3. Numerical exact with infinite sum and whole fraction

$$1 + \frac{1}{1.3} + \frac{1}{1.3.5} + \frac{1}{1.3.5.7} + \frac{1}{1.3.5.7.9} + \dots + \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi e}{2}}.$$

This is perhaps Ramanujan's most beautiful formula, a true work of mathematical art. She unexpectedly connected an infinite series and an infinite whole fraction. It is surprising that neither the series nor the whole fraction can be expressed in terms of the known constants l and e, and their sum inadmissibly turns out to be equal to $\sqrt{\pi e}/2$!

"Synopis of elementary results of pure and applied mathematics"

This two-volume manual by the English mathematician Carara, written in 1880-1886, came to Ramanujan in 1903 - he was then 16 years old. This book played a huge role in the formation of Ramanujan. It collected 6165 theorems and formulas, almost without proof, with minimal explanations. The book is mainly devoted to algebra, trigonometry, analysis, and analytical geometry.

Karar's book stimulated the little one to independently deduce formulas. Those who knew Ramanujan during these years speak about this. The area of ​​its main interests gradually changes: mathematical squares, then the squaring of the circle (it is found with an accuracy that allows one to calculate the length of the equator with an error not exceeding 1-2 m, according to the legend) and, finally, the turn of endless rows comes. This is already the beginning of a true mathematical life!

Karar's book was successful enough to shape Ramanujan's mathematical world. But focusing on this book had other consequences. Since the book did not contain proofs, and at best only suggestive messages, Ramanujan developed a unique method for establishing mathematical truth. Moreover, it is deprived of suitable guidelines in India to carry out rigorous proofs.

“His understanding of the essence of mathematical proof was more than vague; he SEL to all his results, both early and later, both true and false, with the help of a mixture of intuitive proofs, inductive messages and logical reasoning ... "

The Mathematical syllabus of Ramanujan actually completely decided during these years - the direction of scientific research, he never changed the way of thinking. The message that can be conveyed here is that Ramanujan was formed under difficult conditions. Under normal circumstances, he would undoubtedly have become a mathematician with better professional training, but can we be sure that he would have been equally unique? Would Ramanujan have been able to see so much if, from childhood, he had been taught the rules of behavior in mathematics and would have brought his results to publication with external evidence, would he have built his mathematical world on the basis of everything accessible to mankind, and not on a relatively small number of facts?
