MEETING WITH A COMET
HALLEY HAS BEEN HAPPENED!

Candidate of Physics and Mathematics
T. K. BREUS

Comets are still the least studied objects in the solar system, although several are discovered every year. The reason is that they appear unexpectedly, and the body of the comet itself - its nucleus - is inaccessible to ground-based observations, since it is hidden by the glow of a colossal, hundreds of thousands of times larger than the nucleus itself, head, or “coma” - a gas-dust atmosphere that arose during the evaporation of cometary matter, heated by solar study as the comet approaches the Sun.

Halley's Comet has proven itself to be one of the brightest during its return. For example, in 837 it passed 6 million km from the Earth and was 6.5 times brighter than Sirius, the brightest star in the earth’s sky. In 1910, during the execution of the luminary, the comet found itself between the Earth and the Sun. And since the tails of comets are always directed away from the Sun and stretch for many millions of kilometers, the Earth flew through the comet’s tail for several hours.

At this time, Halley's comet was discovered at a record distance - about 1.6 million km from our planet and the Sun. It was October 16, 1982, when the comet was in the constellation Canis Minor and was 40 million times fainter than the faintest stars that a person can still see with the naked eye. Typically, comets are detected at distances 2-3 times smaller, but this time the most advanced technology was used, which makes it possible to register even individual light quanta. But due to the unfavorable location of the comet’s trajectory (during periods of the trajectory, the Sun found itself between the Earth and the comet), it was impossible to “promise” with it from the Earth properly. This shortcoming of the comet was immediately resolved by the launch of a heel of spacecraft to the comet at once - two Soviet (“Veta-1”, “Veta-2”)*, one Western European (“Giotto”) and two Japanese.

During the previous appearance of Halley's comet in 1910, spectroscopes were aimed mainly at it, with the help of which actromes clarified the composition of the head and tail of the celestial guest. Even earlier, in 1835-1836, the first astrophysical device was used to study Halley's comet - the invention of the outstanding French physicist and astronomer Arago - a polariscope. Then it was proven that the tails of comets shine mainly with light reflected from the Sun, that is, they contain a lot of dust - dispersed stone parts of the nucleus, driven in the form of a tail away from the Sun by some kind of force. It was only in 1910, after the Russian physicist P. N. Lebedev showed that light can exert pressure on particles, that the formation of dusty comet tails was explained.

In 1836, statements about the physical nature of the comet's tinsten nucleus appeared in the scientific literature. The German astronomer Bessel put forward the idea that the substance is volatile, that is, easily ignited by heat. A similar idea was expressed by the late Lapas. In the third edition of his Exposition of the System of the World (1808), he explained the formation of the colossal head and tail of a comet by the rapid evaporation of a “liquid” from the surfaces of the nucleus near the Sun.
