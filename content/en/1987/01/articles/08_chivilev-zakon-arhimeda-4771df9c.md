Archimedes' Law

“...The impact of the rolled air slammed in the pipes, the water in the system began to gurgle, and the depth gauge crawled upward. The boat floated up on a level keel, and the depth gauge showed that the wheelhouse had already left the water,” this is how the submarine’s ascent is described in L. Sobolev’s book “The Soul of the Sea.”

The reason for the ascent is the Archimedes force, also called the buoyancy force, which, after the air moved along with the water, exceeded in magnitude the force of the weights of the boat. When and in what case will the power of Archimedes arise? On what side does it act? Where is it applied, how is it directed and what is it equal to?

The buoyant force is the sum of all pressure forces acting on the side of a liquid or gas on the surface of a body immersed in it (Fig. 1). The real reason for the sinking buoyant force is the presence of different hydrostatic pressure at different fluid levels.

To find the Archimedes force, let us mentally measure the immersed body of liquid in the volume of this body (Fig. 2). The same buoyant force will act on it from the surrounding liquid as on the immersed body. According to Newton's third law, the fluid released in the volume of the body (displaced fluid) will act on the surrounding fluid with the same magnitude, but oppositely directed force. This is the entire volume of liquid displaced. Let us remember that the weight of a body motionless in some reporting system (not necessarily inertial) is called the force with which the body, the effect of its attraction to the Earth, acts on the stand or suspension. So, the buoyant force acting on a body immersed in a liquid is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the weight of the displaced liquid. This is Archimedes' law. Note that in the formulation the law speaks specifically about the entire displaced fluid, and not about the force of gravity. And this is very significant, since the weight of the body (in absolute value) does not always coincide with the force of gravity. For example, a box with mass $m$ in the cabin of a lift $a$ rising with a tilt will hit the floor with a force $m(g+a)$. This means that the weight of the box is $P=m(g+a)$, while the gravitational force acting on the box is $mg$. When the elevator car lowers with the same acceleration, the weight of the box turns out to be equal to $P=m(g-a)$.

From the last expression it is clear that the buoyant force appears when there is no state of weightlessness, that is, any body (including liquid) has weight. If a vessel with a liquid falls freely, then the liquid is truly in a state of weightlessness and does not affect the body of Archimedes’ layer immersed in it. This force does not operate in a spacecraft moving with its engines turned off.

When proving Archimedes' law, we assumed that the body was completely immersed in a liquid and its entire surface was in contact with the liquid. If part of the surface of the body is tightly adjacent to the wall or bottom of the vessel, so that there is no layer of liquid between them, then Archimedes’ law does not apply. A clear illustration of this is the experiment when the smooth lower surface of a wooden cube is rubbed with paraffin and placed tightly against the bottom of the vessel. Then carefully pour water. We just have to learn how to find the weight of the displaced fluid and the personal action of the buoyant force. In the general case (for example, when a body is immersed in a liquid that rotates along with the vessel) this is not so easy to do.

Let's consider the simplest and most common case in practice. Let a vessel with liquid be stationary in some inertial frame of reference. Then, as is known, the weight of any motionless body is equal to the gravitational force acting on the body. Therefore, the buoyant layer is equal in modulus to the gravity acting on the displaced fluid and is directed opposite to it. The line of action of the buoyant force will pass through the center of gravity of the displaced fluid. Let's show it.

Two forces act on the displaced volume of liquid with mass $m$ (Fig. 4) - the gravitational force $mg$, applied at the center of gravity of this volume, and the buoyant layer $F_B$. Since the fluid is in equilibrium, according to the rules of the lever (see § 62 of “Physics 6-7” or § 47 of “Physics 8”) the forces acting on it are inversely proportional to the arm of these forces. The shoulder of gravity relative to the axis passing through the center of gravity is equal to zero. This means that the shoulder of the buoyant force is also zero, i.e. the line of action of the buoyant force passes through the center of gravity of the “displaced” volume of liquid.

Since the point of application of force can be transferred along the line of its action, usually the pushing liquid is placed at the center of gravity of the displaced fluid and this point is called the center of pressure.
