Molecular Forces

One of the greatest modern physicists, Richard Feynman, believes that if it were necessary to convey in one single phase the most important thing that we know about the world today, then this phase should be like this: “... all bodies consist of atoms - small bodies that are in continuous motion, attracted at a short distance, but repelled if one of them is pressed more closely to the other” (Feynman Lectures on Physics, volume 1).

Behind each statement of this “key” phase is a science of its own specialization. What are atoms? How are they structured and how do they interact with each other? The search for answers to these questions leads to quantum physics, and through it to an understanding of the microworld. What is the role of the movement of atoms and their interactions in the formation of the properties of matter? The answer to this question lies in all of macro-specialized physics and related approaches to chemistry, biology and other sciences.

Let us also go a little along this path and try, based on the structure of an individual atom, to try the nature of the interaction between different atoms or molecules.

You know (Physics 10, Chapter 11) that atoms consist of a positively charged nucleus and negative charges - electrons - moving around it in a rather complex way. This pillar of the idea of ​​atoms, which has become common nowadays, led at the moment of its appearance (at the beginning of the 20th century) to a real catastrophe in classical physics. The fact is that, in accordance with classical concepts, an electron, during any uneven movement and, in particular, when rotating around a nucleus, necessarily studies electromagnetic waves. At the same time, its energy decreases, and, ultimately, it must inevitably fall onto the core. This means that within the framework of classical physics, atom cannot exist at all.

It took a radical shift in all our ideas about the world in order to get out of the impasse that created it. As a result, quantum mechanics was created, which, in particular, showed that an electron in an atom can only have discrete energy values. Being in a state with any energy value from this “allowed” set, the electron does not emit electromagnetic waves. Energy is emitted (or absorbed) only during the transition from one stationary state to another. All of the above applies to molecules.

This is how the existence of atoms and molecules was “justified”.

Now it is natural to ask such a question. If atoms and molecules are neutral, then how do they interact with each other?

Let's take a closer look at the molecule. If the centers of distribution of positive and negative charges inside it do not coincide with each other, then such a molecule can be represented as a small dumbbell (“Physics 9”, § 46). There is a positive charge at one end and a negative charge at the other. Such electric dumbbells are called dipoles.*)

When two dipole molecules are placed next to each other, each tends to align itself so that the positive end of one half is toward the negative end of the other, and vice versa. As a result, despite the fact that each molecule is neutral, quite noticeable attraction will arise between the molecules. This interaction is called dipole. It, as we have seen, is due to the fact that in dipole molecules the average distances between different charges are slightly smaller than between similar charges. Obviously, as the molecules move away from each other, this difference in distances decreases. At some point, it becomes insignificant, and the force of interaction between molecules becomes zero.

Well, what about in those cases when the molecules are “symmetrine”, that is, the centers of distribution of positive and negative charges inside them coincide?
