Here we must first clarify what “coincide” means. After all, electrons continuously move around the nucleus. Sometimes they even say that they are “smeared” around the core, forming a cloud. And the center of this imprint cloud coincides with the center of the positive core only on average, and at each individual moment in time they are slightly deviated from each other. It’s just that at one moment the center of the cloud is on one side of the center of the positive charge, and at another moment it is on the other. We can say that in this case the molecule (or atom) appears as a continuously arising and disappearing dipole.

If two such molecules come together at a sufficiently small distance, then these “flickering” dipoles will begin to act on each other so that at each moment of time, opposite charges in different molecules are closer to each other than identical ones. Attractive forces arise, which are now called van der Waals forces. These forces turn out to be significantly weaker than dipole ones.

So, we found that when atoms or molecules come together, silipgeny occurs between them.

What happens if atoms or molecules are compressed more and more? It is understood that there will always come a moment when the electron shells of different atoms begin to overlap. As a result, silipgenia will arise between atoms due to their “deformation.” The silipgenia of the city is larger than the dipole-dipole and even more so the Van der Waals silipgenia, so atoms always appear at short distances.

Attraction at large distances and repulsion at small distances, at which silipgenia and repulsion are compensated. This characteristic size determines the average distance between molecules in liquids and solids (in gases, molecules practically do not interact with each other).

The very existence of various aggregate states of matter (gaseous, liquid and solid) is associated with the interaction described here. True, to characterize the properties of a system of a large number of particles (which is what matter is), it is better to use interaction energies that are understandable, not forces. Thus, if the kinetic energy of a molecule is much greater than the energy of its interaction with its environment, we have a gas; if much less, it is a solid body. In liquids these energies are approximately equal to each other.

Polarization of light

Sailors returning from Spain often brought unusual stones - crystals of the famous spar. These stones, which later began to be called spar, were remarkable in their shape and other properties, but mainly for the properties of the refraction of light. So, for example, if a piece of spar is placed on some inscription, then through it you can see the inscription divided (Fig. 1).

In 1669, the Danish scientist E. Bartholin reported interesting results of his experiments with these crystals. It turned out that when passing through such a crystal, a ray of light is distributed into two - now they are called ordinary and extraordinary rays. The above-mentioned rays are justified: the ordinary ray behaves in a way that corresponds to the known laws of light refraction, and the unusual ray seems to violate these laws. In particular, when the incident ray is perpendicular to the surface of the crystal, the first ray passes through the crystal unrefracted, while the second undergoes refraction (Fig. 2).

Vartolin conducted a study of the phenomenon of double refraction, which he discovered, and additionally discovered that in the crystal there is a non-uniform direction along which the incident beam does not separate.
