⟦folio 4⟧

The impact balls are separated at certain angles $\theta$ and $\beta$ to the direction of the first ball direction $m_1$. At figure 1, the triangle $ABC$ is formed by the momentum vectors $\vec{p}_1 = m_1 \vec{v}_1$, $\vec{p}'_1 = m_1 \vec{v}'_1$ and $\vec{p}'_2 = m_2 \vec{v}'_2$ ($\vec{p}_2 = 0$) - such a geometric representation of the law of conservation of momentum in our problem. Applying the cosine theorem to the triangle $ABC$, we obtain:

$$(m_1 v_1)^2 = (m_2 v_2)^2 - (m_1 v_1)^2 + 2 m_1^2 v_1 v_1 \cos \theta.$$

(1)

The law of conservation of energy in this case can obviously be written in the form

$$\frac{m_1 v_1^2}{2} = \frac{m_1 (v_1')^2}{2} + \frac{m_2 (v_2')^2}{2}.$$

(2).

The system of two equations (1)–(2) includes three unknown quantities: $v_1', v_2'$ and $\theta$, therefore it is impossible to express them independently of each other. However, if it is given by a specific value of the angle $\theta$ - it is called the scattering angle - then through simple transformations it is possible to express the speed $v_1'$ as a function of this angle:

$$v_1' = v_1 \frac{m_1 \cos \theta - \sqrt{m_2^2 - m_1^2 \sin^2 \theta}}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

From here it is not difficult to find the relative change in the kinetic energy of the ball $m_1$:

$$\frac{|\Delta T|}{T_1} = \frac{m_1 v_1^2 - m_1 (v_1')^2}{m_1 v_1^2} = \frac{2}{\left(1 + \frac{m_2}{m_1}\right)^2} \left(\frac{m_2}{m_1} + \sin^2 \theta + \cos \theta \sqrt{\left(\frac{m_2}{m_1}\right)^2 - \sin^2 \theta}\right).$$

(3)

Let us analyze the resulting solution for the simplest case of any collision of balls ($\theta = 0$ or $\theta = \pi$). At $m_1 \ll m_2 \Delta T \rightarrow 0$; this means that the ball $m_1$ bounces off the ball $m_2$ in the direction opposite to the original one ($\theta = \pi$), so the speed is equal to $v_1$. With $m_1 \gg m_2$ also $\Delta T \rightarrow 0$; in this case, the collision with the ball $m_2$ has virtually no effect on the movement of the ball $m_1$ ($\theta = 0$). In all intermediate cases $\Delta T \neq 0$. Consequently, for some ratios $m_2/m_1$ the value $\Delta T$ reaches a maximum. A simple calculation shows that the maximum $\Delta T$ corresponds to the conditions $m_2/m_1 = 1$.
