We have been publishing Kvant's contest problems every month from the very first issue of our magazine. The problems are nonstandard ones, but their solution requires no information outside the scope of the USSR secondary school syllabus. The more difficult problems are marked with a star (*). A. A. Sheropov

F1035. An electric diopole of two rigidly coupled point charges $+q$ and $-q$, located at a distance $l$ from each other, sweeps a flat capacitor, the plates of which are connected to a source with an emf $\epsilon$ (Fig. 3). Determine the speed of the diopole at the center of the capacitor if it is known that its speed away from the capacitor is $v_0$. The distance between the plates of the capacitor is $d$, the mass of the diopole is $m$. Neglect the force of gravity.

A. V. Shelazin

F1036. Two identical elastic threads of stiffness $k=10^4$ N/m are attached to a body of mass $m=20$ g lying on a smooth horizontal floor. One thread is attached to the wall, the free end of the second thread begins to be pulled in the horizontal direction at a speed of $v=10$ m/s. Which thread will break if each thread breaks at an absolute elongation of $\Delta l_{\text{np}}=5$ cm? Assume that Hooke's law holds true for threads until they break; there is no training.

E. P. Sokolov

F1037. A small hole is made in a hollow sphere, through which a hole occurs inside with an absolute elongation of $\Delta l_{\text{np}}=5$ cm? Assume that Hooke's law holds true for threads until they break; there is no training.

Problems
M1021—M1025; P1033—P1037

M1021. A mountain climber intends to go up a sheer rock wall of 1000 m. After spending a warm night in camp near the wall's base, he climbs up, hanging up ropes, with vertical velocity 40 m per hour. After a cold night on the wall itself, his velocity decreases to 30 m per hour. When the ropes are already hanging, he climbs 400 m per hour. How many days will he take to reach the top if he is able to work 6 hours a day on the wall. (The time for climbing down and other operations is negligible).

M1022. The first 8 natural numbers may be written in two rows (see the table on p. 15) so that the sum of numbers in the upper row equals that in the lower one, and the column sums are also equal to each other. Can the first a) ten, b) twelve natural numbers be arranged in this way?

c) For what natural $n$ can the numbers 1 to 2n be arranged this way?

M. I. Shterenberg

M1023. Is it always possible to find one triangle among any 100 which can be covered entirely by the other 99?

G. A. Gurevich
