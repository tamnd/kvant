Therefore, and perhaps also because of very complex connections with the geometric theorem of Pythagoras, equation (1) is called the Pythagorean problem, and the points of natural numbers satisfying this equation are called Pythagorean traces. From the Pythagorean theorem it follows that for every right triangle with integer legs $a, b$ and hypotenuse $c$ there corresponds a certain Pythagorean triple such that $0 < a < b < c$, and vice versa. Thus, the Pythagorean problem has an arbitrary geometric meaning (Fig. 1).

An arithmetic method for solving the Pythagorean problem

It is clear that if the triple of natural numbers $(a, b, c)$ is a pythagorean, then for a positive integer $k$ the triple of numbers $(ka, kb, kc)$ will also be a pythagorean. Therefore, to solve the Pythagorean problem there is no need to enumerate all Pythagorean trikes $(a, b, c)$ - it is enough to enumerate primitive trikes, i.e. such Pythagorean trikes $(a, b, c)$ for which there is not a single integer $k > 1$ dividing all three numbers $a, b, c$ without a remainder.

And the processing of primitive Pythagorean tracks coincides easily. For this there is a simple sieve, known to the Pythogoreans; based on the following statement:

if $p = q$ is a reciprocal prime number of different parities, $p > q$, then the triple of numbers

$$a = p^2 - q^2, b = 2pq, c = p^2 + q^2$$ (*)

will be a primitive Pythagorean triple.

For example, if we take $q = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 42$, $p = 163$, we get the Pythagorean triple (24805, 13692, 28333).
