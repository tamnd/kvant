A few years later, Bartholin’s discovery attracted the attention of the Inland scientist H. Huygen. Having repeated Bartholin's experiments, he goes further, passing both rays emitted from an Inderland spar crystal through a second point of the same crystal. To validate the results of the experiments, Huygens introduced the concept of the optical axis of the crystal (which, in fact, had already been discovered earlier by Bartholin). He calls it the selected direction in the crystal, along which the required and extraordinary rays propagate without separating.

Huygen's experiments showed that in the case when the optical axes of both crystals are parallel and a ray of light falls at a right angle to the front face of the first crystal, then upon entering the second crystal the required and extraordinary rays do not undergo separation into two (Fig. 3). In other cases, both beams that emerged from the first crystal can again split in the second crystal. In this case, the intensity of each of the four rays strongly depends on the angle between the optical axes of the crystals.

Analyzing the results of experiments, Huygens, who adheres to the wave theory of light, came to the conclusion that the required and extraordinary rays correspond to different speeds of propagation in the crystal under study. However, Huygens proceeded from the analogy between sound and light waves and considered the latter, like the former, to be continuous. This delusion does not allow him to explain a certain feature of wire experiments.

More than years later, in 1808, the French physicist E. Ma-

los discovered double refraction when light is reflected. Looking through a crystal of Spanish spar at the reflection of the setting sun in the windows of the Luxembourg Palace in Paris, Malos noticed to his surprise that the two images resulting from double refraction had different brightness. In particular, with some crystals only one image was visible.

Based on this and other experiments and relying on Newton’s corpuscular theory of light, Malos suggested that corpuscles, which, according to Newton, have an external resemblance to magnets with poles, are randomly oriented in sunlight, but after reflection from any surface or after passing through a crystal they acquire a certain orientation. He called such “ordered” light polarized.

We are well aware that visible light is transverse electromagnetic waves. When an electromagnetic wave propagates in space, the electric field direction vector $\vec{E}$ and the magnetic field induction vector $\vec{B}$ oscillate in it. These vectors are always mutually perpendicular and lie in a plane perpendicular to the direction of the wave distance. Light in which the directions of vibrations are ordered in some way is called polarized. If the oscillation of the vector $\vec{E}$ occurs all the time in one plane, then the light is said to be plane-polarized (or linearly polarized), and this plane itself is called the plane of polarization.

Light emitted by one atom is polarized by the whole. However, the radiation of a maximally continuous body (the Sun, a light bulb, etc.) is the sum of the radiation of a huge number of atoms. Each of them emits a wave of light for about $10^{-8}$ s, and if all atoms emit light with different polarizations, then the polarization of the entire beam will be less for the same periods of time.
