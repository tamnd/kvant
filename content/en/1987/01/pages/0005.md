ABOUT COLLISION
BALLS
AND “SERIOUS” PHYSICS

(Is the Compton effect complicated?)
Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. R. FILONOVICH

It was once said that every scientific idea goes through three stages in its history: at the moment of its appearance it seems crazy, then it becomes generally accepted and, finally, it is considered trivial. (We would like to add that in the final case the idea may turn out to be erroneous.) If you do not pay attention to the harshness of the formulation inherent in any aphorism, then you cannot agree with such a general presentation of the fate of many physical ideas and theories. One can give many examples of how provisions and formulations, over which once fiercely “broke spears,” are now found on the pages of school textbooks. Probably one of the most recent examples of this kind is the so-called Compton effect, which is included in the new school physics curriculum. But just sixty years ago, passions raged around the effect itself, and harsh, undeserved accusations were made against its first promoter. Now... However, before we talk about the effect itself, we need to discuss an issue that is already not familiar to many readers.

About one classical problem

Have you ever thought about why problems involving collisions of balls are so common in physics problems? There are many reasons for this. First of all, the impact that occurs when the balls collide with the position of mechanics is one of the simplest types of interaction, the laws of which can be relatively easily studied experimentally. That is why, during the formation of classical mechanics in the 17th century, the establishment of the laws of impact was considered the most important task. At that time, the most successful in the study of this problem was the famous Dutch physicist and mathematician H. Geugens, who wrote the essay “On the Motion of Bodies under the Influence of Impact,” in which many of the effects of the movement of balls were considered.

The significance of the impact problem is that in them, in the simplest and most distinct form, one can trace the operation of the laws of conservation of energy and momentum. We will use these laws to solve a very simple problem: a ball of mass $m_1$ hits a stationary ball of mass $m_2$ with a speed of $v_1$. Find the relative change in the kinetic energy of the first ball after the collision, if it is known that the impact is absolutely elastic and the rotation of the balls can be neglected.
