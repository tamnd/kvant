In this case, each part moves slightly, but the movement is “transferred” over significant distances. The energy of vibrations is transferred from one part to another, as if in a relay race. We call such a change in the state of the medium, propagating in it and carrying energy with it, a wave.

To understand the properties of wave motions, it is convenient to decompose them into some simpler elements. A sine wave is most often taken as such an element.

Let us imagine a “sinusoidal corrugated” surface of water, and this “corrugation” does not stand still, but moves along the $x$ axis at a constant speed (figure 1). If we cut our wavy surface

Fig. 1.

vertical plane parallel to the $x$ axis, then we get a sine wave. The distance between two adjacent peaks, two adjacent troughs, in general between two close points, being in the same phases, is the same thing - this is the wavelength $\lambda$. Since the wave travels along the $x$ axis with a constant speed, any point in space through which the wave passes, after a certain period of time $T$ appears in the same phase - the points oscillate with a period $T$. It is clear that during this time the wave should laugh along the $x$ axis to a distance $\lambda$. The speed of such a displacement $c=\lambda/T$ is called the phase speed of the wave.

There are not endless sinus waves in nature - wave motion begins somewhere and ends somewhere. True, the front wave remains almost sinusoidal for a long time, but this “almost” turns out to be very significant. To understand what's going on, consider two sine waves with different but close periods $T_1$ and $T_2$ and similar lengths $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$. If in some region of space the peaks and troughs of both waves almost coincide, then the total range - amplitude - of the oscillations of the points in space will increase noticeably. But as you move away from this region, the difference in wavelengths will lead to the fact that the phases of the waves corresponding to the same point in space will be noticeably different, and eventually the peaks of one wave begin to fall on the trough of another - the waves will cancel each other out. Then this damping is again shifted by amplification, and the whole picture will periodically repeat itself. You can verify this by drawing two sine waves with identical amplitudes with slightly different periods and adding them - superimposing one on top of the other, as in figure 2, $a$. The result of this addition is at figure 2, $b$: it is like a sine wave with a period $T$, close to $T_1$ and $T_2$, but its periodic amplitude changes. This process is called beats, and the wave is called modulated.

The change in amplitude during beats is described by a wave that “envelopes” the main - or, as they say, carrier - vibrations. Let us find the length $\lambda_a$ of this envelope wave and its period $T_a$. This is quite easy to do. The numbers of waves of the original sinusoids, lying, say, between two adjacent maxima of the envelope, must differ by one so that the peaks and troughs of these waves coincide again. For the first wave, the number of such waves is $\lambda_a/\lambda_1$, for the second - $\lambda_a/\lambda_2$. So, $\frac{\lambda_a}{\lambda_1} - \frac{\lambda_a}{\lambda_2} = 1$, from where

$$\lambda_a = \frac{\lambda_1 \lambda_2}{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1} \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta \lambda}.$$ (1)

Here we denote by $\Delta \lambda$ the difference $\lambda_2 - \lambda_1$, and replace the product $\lambda_1 \lambda_2$ with the square of the geometric mean $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ ($\lambda^2 = (\sqrt{\lambda_1 \lambda_2})^2$) - a value close to both values, as well as to the length of the carrier wave (an exact calculation for the length of the carrier wave gives $\lambda_n = 2\lambda_1 \lambda_2 / (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$). It is not difficult to obtain a similar (1) expression for the beat period: $T_a \approx T^2 / \Delta T$. Now can find the envelope speed - the speed at which the beat moves:

$$v = \frac{\lambda_a}{T_a} = \frac{\lambda^2}{T^2} \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda} = c^2 \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda},$$ (2)

where $c$ is the phase velocity of the carrier wave. If the speeds of the two sinusoids from which the carrier wave is composed are exactly the same, so that $\lambda_1 = c T_1$ and $\lambda_2 = c T_2$, then $\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\Delta T} = c$, and it turns out that $v = c$. In this case, the entire wave pattern moves at a speed of $c$ as a whole. If $c_1 \neq c_2$, then $v$ is different from $c$; it turns out that the carrier wave moves with one speed - the phase speed $c$, and the envelope - with another speed $v$, which is called the group one. It is with group velocity that energy is transferred by the wave.

The envelope wave, like the sine wave, is infinite both in time and in space. Only by adding not two, but many (there are many strict envelopes) sinusids with similar lengths and periods, can one obtain a “wave packet” - a picture of oscillations occupying a limited area

space (figure 3). And again the entire packet will move with group velocity, and the “sine wave” carrier will move with phase velocity. True, this movement cannot continue indefinitely. If the phase velocities of different sinusoids are different, then the group velocities for different pairs of sinusids, calculated using formula (2), will be different. Because of this, over large distances, the wave packet will gradually change its shape and eventually spread out, “spread out” throughout space. This phenomenon is called dispersion, from the Latin word dispersio - scattering.

So, to describe motion in a dispersive medium, we have to introduce not one, but at least two speeds!

Waves on the water

Let's return to the waves on the surface of the water. To strictly describe these waves, it would be necessary to write down the equations of motion of part of the water and try to find their solutions. However, much can be obtained without equations if you use the so-called dimensional method, which often allows you to study a phenomenon without resorting to complex mathematical calculations.

Let's see what physical quantities the speed of propagation of sine waves on the surface of water can depend on. If we neglect the influence of the atmosphere (wind), and consider the reservoir to be infinitely deep, then we have at our disposal the density of water $\rho$, the acceleration of gravity $g$ and, finally, the period of the wave itself $T$ (or its length $\lambda$) *). Let us write down the dimensions of these quantities:

$$[\rho] = \kappa \Gamma / m^3, [g] = m / c^2, [T] = c.$$

*) Another dimensional parameter that can affect the wave speed is surface tension $g$. However, as calculations show, it turns out to be significant only for very short waves (~1 cm), which we will not be interested in here.

⟦folio 20⟧

Only on them can the wave speed $c$ depend. How to combine them with the dimension of speed? Since $[c]=\text{m/c}$, and mass appears only in $[\rho]$, the density $\rho$ must be excluded. Of the remaining values ​​$g$ and $T$, the only combination is suitable: $[c]=[gT]$ (it doesn’t matter what speed – phase or group – we are talking about).

This allows us to state that $c$ and $v$ are proportional to $gT$. The proportionality coefficient cannot be determined using the dimensional method; from a more detailed theory it follows that for the phase velocity it is equal to $1/2\pi$:

$$c = \frac{gT}{2\pi}.$$

By definition $c=\lambda/T$, and from formula (3) the relationship between the length and period of the wave is obtained -

$$\lambda = \frac{gT^2}{2\pi}.$$

This correspondence is called the dispersion equation. One more expression for $c$ can be written:

$$c = \sqrt{\frac{g\lambda}{2\pi}}.$$

What is the group speed? Above we obtained the correspondence $v = c^2 \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda}$ (see (2)). Now, using the dispersion equation, we find the ratio $\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda}$:

$$\Delta \lambda = \frac{g}{\pi} T \cdot \Delta T \Rightarrow \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda} = \frac{\pi}{gT}.$$

Substituting this into the formula for $v$, taking into account (3) we obtain:

$$v = c^2 \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda} = \frac{gT}{4\pi} = \frac{1}{2} c.$$

So, for a wave on deep water, the group velocity is equal to half the phase velocity, and both of them depend on the period of the wave, i.e. the wave has dispersion.

Let us write down the calculated expressions relating the length and speed of wave propagation to its period:

$$\lambda = 1,56T^2, \quad v = \frac{1}{2} c \approx 0,78T$$

($T$ is taken in seconds, $\lambda$ is in meters, and speeds are in meters/second).

Taking an even longer period, say $T \approx 1$ h, and accordingly a wavelength of $\lambda \approx 20000$ km, we would get a fantastic speed of $v \approx 10000$ km/h. However, for large waves, it will turn out that the wave will “reach” even in the deepest place of the ocean, and our simple formulas will no longer be applicable. They are also inapplicable for very short waves, less than a few centimeters in length - here the influence of surface tension forces is felt. And all these formulas are also suitable for describing waves in a very wide range of lengths - from tens of centimeters to tens or more kilometers.

Although a wave is formed by the movement of wave particles, these particles themselves do not move with the wave at all. You can easily verify this by looking at the float: it remains in place, making only small oscillatory movements. To imagine how the particles of the wave move during the propagation of a sine wave, it is convenient to take the point of view of what is observed moving at a speed equal to the phase speed of the wave. For this, it is observed that the “corrugated” surface of the wave will stop and fall asleep, but the tanning mass of the wave will “flow” in the opposite direction with a speed of $c$. The wave particles on the surface slide along the undulating terrain in the same way as any ball fired along it with a certain initial speed would do. Returning to the top of the relief, such a ball (and wave particle) will have a speed slightly less than the average speed $c$, and if it rolls down to the bottom, it will have a speed slightly higher $c$. Particles lying under the surface also describe circles, but with smaller radii. The amplitudes of particle vibrations decrease exponentially with depth, proportional to $e^{-2\pi z/\lambda}$, where $z$ is the depth. This means that at a depth equal to the wavelength, the amplitude of wave motions is $e^{2\pi} \approx 535$ times less than at the surface. Therefore, even strong storms practically do not arise only in the ocean at depths greater than, say, hundreds of meters, and if $\lambda$ is less than $2\pi H$, where $H$ is the depth of the reservoir, then it can actually be considered infinitely deep—the waves “do not set” the day.

Of course, as a result of sea waves, the waves are usually not sinusoidal, they consist of many sinusoidal waves, and the movement of a water particle is more complex - it also seems to consist of many circles of different radii, rotation along which occurs at different speeds; but in sufficiently weak waves this movement remains limited and approximately symmetrical (up - down). Wind waves

At the end of the century, the remarkable English physicist Rayleigh remarked: “The fundamental law of sea waves is the absence of any law.” True, a descriptive classification of the state of the sea surface, necessary for sailors, has long been developed. As you know, wind strength is characterized by a 12-point Beaufort scale, and wave height is characterized by a 9-point wave scale. Excerpts from these scales are given on p. 16-17. As you can see, they are largely based on a verbal description of the situation. This classification is a bit reminiscent of Pushkin’s “The Tale of the Fish and the Fish.” Remember, at first the old man “sees that the sea has become a little rough”; then “the blue sea became clouded”, then “the blue sea turned black” and, finally, “he saw a black storm on the sea: he saw the angry waves, they walk like that, they howl and howl”... Please, here you can even indicate how many excitement points are added to each time the fish is gold!

Recently, the means of changing sea waves have been significantly improved. In addition to special wave recorders - instruments that record fluctuations in the sea surface at a given point, remote methods are measuring increasingly greater values. Simply photographing sea waves from a ship or airplane provides a lot of information. Sea radar is also effective. “Satellite oceanography” has emerged and is rapidly developing. As a result of many years of observations, extensive data on sea waves. But getting an answer to the questions about how exactly the wind excites waves on the water, how they develop further, is not at all easy, and complete clarity has not yet been achieved, although the basic mechanisms are known.
