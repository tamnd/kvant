Wonderful numbers
$\sqrt{2}$ and e

Long before Pythogoras, it was known that the area of ​​a square built on the hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle is twice the area of ​​a square built on its side. This is evidenced by figure, which occurred in the Pilmen springs of an earlier period.

The number $\sqrt{2}$ - the ratio of the diagonal of a square to its side - is the first number that has been proven to be irrational. Proof obtained in the school of Pythagoras almost 500 BC. e., caused discord in the creation in this school of a philosophical system based on the harmony of numbers.

The number $e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right) = 2,71828182845...$ appeared relatively recently. It is often called the "non-feather number". In honor of the inventor of logarithms, the Scottish mathematician John Napier (1550-1617), however, there is no firm basis that Napier had a clear concept of the number $e$.

According to legend, he was the first to divulge the fact of the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$, an attempted shipwreck. The same fate befell the one who divulged the Pythograeans’ discovery of the dodecasdrous, which initially did not appear in their system of numbers and figures. That's how people became rich in English times.

The number $e$ became important for mathematical analysis after the Byltic mathematician Gregory of Saint-Vincent discovered in 1647 the relationship between logarithms and the area under a hyperbole. In particular, he found that curved boundaries under a hyperbola with base $[1; e], [e; e^2], \dots$ have unit areas.

Rectangles with the ratio $\sqrt{2}$ are often found in architecture, for example in the proportions of the Church of the Intercession on the Nerl. They are also found in book operatini, since only such rectangles turn into similar ones when folded in half.
