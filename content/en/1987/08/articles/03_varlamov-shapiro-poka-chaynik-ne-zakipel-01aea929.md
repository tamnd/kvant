Eastern treatises and chapters in special books are associated with the ritual of tea drinking. And yet, looking at this process from a slightly different angle, you can find many interesting physical phenomena in it, the explanations of which are not even in the thickest culinary manual. Let’s approach the tea drinking process from this point of view, or more precisely, the process of preparing for it.

Let's start with an experiment. Let's put two perfect identical kettles with equal amounts of cold water on burners of the same power. We will close one of them with a lid, and leave the second open. Which kettle will boil first?

Any experienced housewife knows the answer to this question. Wanting to boil the water faster, she, without hesitation, covers the kettle with a lid, and when asked the question, she immediately answers that a kettle covered with a lid will boil sooner. Well, let’s make sure it’s right - first by experiment, by waiting for our kettles to boil, and then we’ll explain the result of the experiment from the point of view of molecular physics.

While the kettles are heating up, let’s put another exactly the same kettle on the third burner of the stove with the same amount of cold water as the first two, and try to boil it faster (with the same power of the burner). To do this, you need to somehow quickly increase the temperature of the water in it, “overtaking” its heating in the first two kettles. For example, put a boiler in the kettle as well. Well, what if there is no boiler?

Let us remember that in order to increase the temperature of water in any container, it is enough to add hotter water to it. Perhaps this technique will speed up the boiling of water in the third kettle? Not at all. Not only will it not speed up, but it will also slow down. To verify this, let’s imagine that water with mass $m_1$, which was initially in the kettle at temperature $t_1$, does not mix and does not exchange heat with added hot water with mass $m_2$ and temperature $t_2$ ($t_2 > t_1$). The heat that had to be initially transferred to water with a mass of $m_1$ to bring it to a boil (to a temperature of $t_1$) is $Q_1 = cm_1(t_1 - t_1)$. Now you will have to additionally heat up from $t_2$ to $t_1$ the finished hot water weighing $m_2$. Therefore, the total amount of heat is

$$Q = cm_1(t_1 - t_1) + cm_2(t_1 - t_2).$$

(It is clear that our “absurd” assumption that the portions of water remained unmixed did not in any way affect the law of conservation of energy in the system, but only allowed us to consider the phenomenon of the process faster.)

While we were flawlessly fiddling with the third kettle, the first one, with the lid closed, was already beginning to “sing”. Let's try to find out the reason for this strange sound and evaluate its characteristic part.

The first reason can be assumed to be vibrations of the liquid that arose when vapor bubbles separated from the bottom and walls of the vessel. These bubbles most often originate on imperfections and microcracks on the surface. The characteristic sizes of bubbles before water boils are about 1 mm (during boiling they increase significantly and can reach up to a centimeter). To estimate the frequency of the sound that arises, we need to find the time of separation of the bubbles from day t. This time characterizes the duration of the shock that the liquid receives in the process of separation of the bubble, and, consequently, the period of oscillations occurring in the liquid. The amount of sound generated is determined by the reciprocal of this time: $v \sim \tau^{-1}$.

While the bubble is at rest at the bottom, the Archimedes force $F_A = \rho_B g V_\pi$ ($V_\pi$ is the volume of the bubble) pushing it upward and the force of surface tension holding it at the bottom act on it. We preserve the known low force of gravity. As the volume of the bubble increases, the Archimedes force returns and at some point the surface tension forces become greater. The bubble begins its upward movement. It is clear that the total force acting on the bubble during its separation from the bottom is equal in order of magnitude to $F_A$. The mass of a bubble moving in a liquid is determined not by its own negligible mass (the mass of the impact and vapor contained in it), but by the so-called “added” mass, which for a spherical bubble is $m^* = \frac{2}{3} \pi r_0^3 \rho_\pi$ (in this formula $\rho_\pi$ is the density of the liquid, and $r_0$ is the radius of the bubble). The value $m^*$ actually determines the mass of liquid around the bubble that is involved in the movement when the bubble moves.

Thus, to accelerate the bubble at the initial stage of movement there is:

$$a \sim \frac{F_A}{m^*} = \frac{\rho_B g \cdot \frac{4}{3} \pi r_0^3}{\frac{2}{3} \pi r_0^3 \rho_B} = 2g.$$

The time of separation of the bubble from the bottom can now be estimated, considering its movement to be uniformly accelerated. It rises to a distance of the order of its exchange in the time:

$$\tau_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2r_0}{a}} \sim 10^{-2} \text{ с.} \quad (*)$$

The corresponding characteristic of the sound generated when bubbles come off is $v_1 \sim \tau_1^{-1} \sim 100$ Hz.

There is a second reason for the noise that occurs when heating water in a kettle. In order to get to it, we will trace the fate of the bubble of steam after it is separated from the bottom. As it floats up, the bubble falls into the upper layers of water that are not yet sufficiently warmed up. The saturated steam filling the bubble cools down, its pressure drops and can no longer compensate for the external pressure on the bubble from the water. As a result, the bubble quickly collapses and a sound pulse propagates through the liquid.

Let us evaluate the characteristic part of the sound generated when bubbles break off. Let us write down Newton's equation for the mass of water $m$ rushing inside the bubble as it collapses:

$$ma = F_\pi = S \cdot \Delta p.$$

Here $S = 4\pi r^2$ is the surface area of ​​the bubble on which the pressure force $F_\pi$ acts, and $\Delta p$ is the pressure difference at the bubble boundary, $a$ is the acceleration of the boundary movement towards the center of the bubble. It is clear that in the process of such collapse a mass of water is involved, whose order of magnitude is equal to the product of the density of water and the volume of the bubble: $m \sim \rho_B r^3$. Thus, Newton's equation can be rewritten as

What burns more - steam or boiling water? Before answering this question, it should be clarified: what burns more - a certain mass of boiling water or the same mass of steam? As we have already found out, the density of pairs at $t_{\kappa}=100^\circ\text{C}$ is 0.6 kg/m$^3$. Therefore, about 0.06 g of steam ends up on the hand. During condensation and subsequent cooling from 100 °C to body temperature $t_0$ ($t_0 \approx 37^\circ\text{C}$), heat $\Delta Q = rm_{\pi}+cm_{\pi}(t_{\kappa}-t_0) \approx 150$ J is released. It is easy to see that for the same warm air you will need almost ten times the mass of boiling water. In addition, when hunting by ferry, the affected area will be significantly larger. Thus, steam burns hotter than boiling water, primarily due to the significant heat released during condensation.

But we digress from discussing the result of our experiment with two teapots. Why did the kettle with the lid open still fall behind? Let's look at this in more detail. The answer is almost very clear: in the process of heating water in an open kettle faster, its molecules have the opportunity to continuously leave the kettle, taking energy with them and, as it were, cooling the water remaining in the kettle (this process is nothing more than evaporation). Therefore, when heated, in this case it is necessary not only to bring the water in the kettle to a boil, but also to evaporate part of it during the heating process. It is clear that this takes more energy (and therefore time) than when boiling water in a closed kettle, where the “fast” molecules are leveled out of the water and very soon form saturated steam in a confined space under the lid and, returning to the water, give off their excess energy back.

However, there are also two effective effects that counteract distance. Firstly, during the process of evaporation, the mass of water decreases somewhat, which should be brought to a boil. Secondly, boiling in an open kettle at normal atmospheric pressure occurs at a temperature of 100 °C, and in a closed one - at a higher temperature. Indeed, if the kettle is filled in such a way that vapor cannot escape through the spout, due to intense evaporation before boiling, the pressure at the surface increases, since it is now exceeded by the sum of the partial pressures of a small amount of air and water under the lid.
