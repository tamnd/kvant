**M1040**. The numbers 1, 2, ..., 3n are randomly divided into three groups of numbers in each. Prove that you can choose one number from each group so that one of them is equal to the sum of the other two.

Let's denote these three groups by the letters $A, B, C$ and assume that the numbers from 1 to $k-1$ fall into the group $A$, and the number $k$ into the group $B$. We call a triple of numbers good if they are all from different groups and the sum of two of them is equal to the third. Let us assume that there are no good tracks and show that then for every a from group C the number a-1 belongs to A. It will follow from this that there are more numbers in $A$ than in $C$, which contradicts the conditions.

Let $a$ be a number from $C$. Then $a-1$ does not belong to $B$, otherwise the triple $(1, a-1, a)$ would be good. Let's assume that $a-1$ belongs to $C$. Consider the number $a-k$. If it is in $A$, then $(a-k, k, a)$ is a good three, if in $B$, then $(k-1, a-k, a-1)$ is a good three. Therefore, $a-k$ is included in $C$. Similarly, if the number $a-k-1$ is included in $A$, then a good three will be $(a-k-1, k, a-1)$, and if it is in $B$, then $(1, a-k-1, a-k)$. Repeating this reasoning, we come to the conclusion that all natural numbers of the form $a-ik, a-ik-1$ ($i=0, 1, 2, ...$) belong to $C$. But for some numbers $a-ik$ coincides with one of the numbers 1, 2, ..., $k$ and, therefore, is included in $A$ or $B$. It is contradictory to prove that $a-1$ belongs to $A$. Therefore, if $C$ consists of the numbers $c_1, ..., c_n$, then $A$ contains the numbers $c_1-1, ..., c_n-1$, all of which are greater than 1, since 2 belongs to $A$ or $B$. But $A$ also contains 1, so $A$ has at least $n+1$ in it.

**F1048.** The projectionist mistakenly placed the film so that all the events on the screen “flowed in the opposite direction”, while automatically moving backwards. How did the speed and rooting of automation change as a result of such an error?

Let, when the tape is inserted correctly, automatically have a speed of $\vec{v}$. Then its movement in a short period of time $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$ is

$$\Delta x = \vec{x}(t_2) - \vec{x}(t_1) = \vec{v} \cdot \Delta t.$$

If the tape is inserted so that the events on the screen “flow in the opposite direction”, the movement automatically during the same time $\Delta t$ will be

$$\Delta x' = \vec{x}(t_1) - \vec{x}(t_2) = -\Delta x.$$

But $\Delta x' = \vec{v}' \cdot \Delta t$, so

$$\vec{v}' \cdot \Delta t = -\Delta x = -\vec{v} \cdot \Delta t,$$

and each speed is automatic on the screen -
$$\vec{v}' = -\vec{v}.$$

Likewise,
$$\Delta v' = v'(t_1) - v'(t_2) = -v(t_1) - (-v(t_2)) = v(t_2) - v(t_1) = \Delta v,$$
and since $\Delta v = \vec{a} \cdot \Delta t$ and $\Delta v' = \vec{a}' \cdot \Delta t$,
$$\vec{a}' = \vec{a}.$$

Therefore, when the tape is inserted backwards, the speed automatically changes direction on the screen, but the acceleration remains unchanged.

F1051. In a magnetic field, the induction of which is horizontal and equal to B, a thin metal ring rolls without breaking at a speed v, in which there is a very small gap of length l; vector B is perpendicular to the plane of the ring (Fig. 1). Find the induced emf at the moment when the AOS angle (see Fig.ok) is equal to α.

The direct way to find the EMF involves dividing the loop into small (almost rectilinear) sections and summing up all the elementary EMFs. We will do otherwise. If there is an additional loop to the whole, then the EMF in it will be equal to zero (the circuit is closed). On the other side,

$$0 = \mathcal{E}_0 + \mathcal{E}'$$

where $\mathcal{E}_0$ is the required potential difference, $\mathcal{E}'$ is the induction emf of the additional element. Hence,

$$\mathcal{E}_0 = -\mathcal{E}' = -uBl \sin \varphi$$

where $u$ is the speed of the element, $l$ is its length, $\varphi$ is the angle that the vector $\vec{u}$ makes with $\vec{B}$. As can be seen from figure 2,

$$u = 2v \sin \frac{\alpha}{2}, \varphi = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\alpha}{2}.$$

Thus,

$$\mathcal{E}_0 = -2Blv \sin \frac{\alpha}{2} \cos \frac{\alpha}{2} = -Blv \sin \alpha$$
