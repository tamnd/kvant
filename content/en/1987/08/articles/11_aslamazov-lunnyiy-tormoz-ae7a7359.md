MOON BRAKE

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. G. ASLAMAZOV

People have long believed that sea tides are associated with the Moon. The Moon attracts the water of the World Ocean, and in the part of the ocean over which the Moon is located, a water “hump” is formed. The hump “stop” under the Moon, and the Earth rotates around its axis; the rising water “creeps” onto the shore - the tide comes, the water recedes - the tide. It would seem that such an explanation looks quite natural. However, it cannot be considered satisfactory: it follows from it that hot flashes should be observed once a day, but in reality they occur every 12 hours, i.e., twice a day.

The first theory of tides to explain this phenomenon was created by Newton shortly after the discovery of the law of universal gravitation. Two humps actually form in the ocean: one at the surface of the water closest to the Moon, and the other on the opposite side of the Earth (figure 1). Let us discuss in more detail the reasons for the appearance of the second hump.

We are used to saying that the Moon revolves around the Earth. In fact, both the Moon and the Earth rotate around a common center. If this rotation did not exist, then under the influence of gravity the Moon would fall to the Earth

(more precisely, the Earth and the Moon would fall on each other - after all, the Moon also accepts the Earth). How does this rotation happen? Let's imagine that the masses of the Earth and the Moon are the same. Then they will move around a point that lies in the middle of the segment connecting their centers. Now let's mentally “increase” the mass of the Earth. Then the center of rotation will move towards the Earth. Since the mass of the Earth is much greater than the mass of the Moon (81 times), the center of rotation C turns out to lie inside the globe (but it is necessarily shifted relative to the center of the Earth).

This rotation significantly affects the tide pattern. The water moves away from the center of rotation. (Something similar happens in a glass of water when, stirring the water with a spoon, you bring

Of course, our explanation of tides is a simplified one. We did not take into account the uneven distribution of water over the Earth's surface, did not take into account the influence of gravitational forces towards the Sun and other factors that could significantly change the described picture. However, we have received the answer to the main question: the attraction to the Moon and the rotation of the Earth around the Moon of center C leads to the formation of two tidal humps in the ocean.

Now is the time to explain the principle of operation of the lunar brake. It turns out that the water humps are not located on the line connecting the centers of the Earth and the Moon (as shown for simplicity in figure 1), but are somewhat shifted to the side (figure 2). This happens for the following reason. The earth, rotating around its axis, carries with it the water in the ocean (this process is because friction exists). As a result, as the Earth rotates, more and more masses of water should be drawn into tidal humps. Deformation, however, always lags behind the force that causes it (after all, the force creates acceleration, and some time must pass for the particles to slow down and mix over noticeable distances). Therefore, the point of maximum rise of water (the top of the hump) and the point on the line of centers where the maximum force of attraction to the Moon acts on the water do not coincide. In this case, as can be seen from figure 2, the gravitational force of the Moon no longer passes through the center of the Earth’s own rotation and, as it were, tends to “turn” it in the opposite direction, in other words, it slows down its rotation. The length of the day is increasing every day!

The “moon brake” has been working flawlessly for many millions of years. In corals that lived in the ocean about 400 million years ago, scientists have discovered structures associated with daily and annual coral growth. These structures are called diurnal and seasonal rings. When the rings were counted, it turned out that each year there are 395 daily rings. The length of the year—the time it takes the Earth to complete one revolution around the Sun—has apparently remained unchanged since then. This means that then - 400 million years ago - there were only 22 hours in a day!

The lunar brake continues to work now, increasing the length of the day. What could this lead to in the future? In the end, the time of revolution of the Earth around its axis may become equal to the time of revolution of the Moon around the Earth, and then the braking will stop. The Earth will always face the Moon on the same side, just as the Moon faces us on the same side (ask yourself why this will happen). The length of the day on Earth will increase, and as a result the climate may change. The Earth will remain facing the Sun on one side for a long time, and on the other side at this time night will reign. Hot air from the heated side will rush towards the cold side with great speed, strong winds will blow on the Earth, sandstorms will arise... But such a forecast may not exist - after all, these possibilities of time are stacked, so it is simply impossible to predict all the changes that will occur in the Earth-Moon-Sun system. And if such a picture turns out to be real, then people will definitely figure out how to prevent disaster.
