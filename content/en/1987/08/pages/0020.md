In this case, each part moves slightly, but the movement is “transferred” over significant distances. The energy of vibrations is transferred from one part to another, as if in a relay race. We call such a change in the state of the medium, propagating in it and carrying energy with it, a wave.

To understand the properties of wave motions, it is convenient to decompose them into some simpler elements. A sine wave is most often taken as such an element.

Let us imagine a “sinusoidal corrugated” surface of water, and this “corrugation” does not stand still, but moves along the $x$ axis at a constant speed (figure 1). If we cut our wavy surface

vertical plane parallel to the $x$ axis, then we get a sine wave. The distance between two adjacent peaks, two adjacent troughs, in general between two close points, being in the same phases, is the same thing - this is the wavelength $\lambda$. Since the wave travels along the $x$ axis with a constant speed, any point in space through which the wave passes, after a certain period of time $T$ appears in the same phase - the points oscillate with a period $T$. It is clear that during this time the wave should laugh along the $x$ axis to a distance $\lambda$. The speed of such a displacement $c=\lambda/T$ is called the phase speed of the wave.

There are not endless sinus waves in nature - wave motion begins somewhere and ends somewhere. True, the front wave remains almost sinusoidal for a long time, but this “almost” turns out to be very significant. To understand what's going on, consider two sine waves with different but close periods $T_1$ and $T_2$ and similar lengths $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$. If in some region of space the peaks and troughs of both waves almost coincide, then the total range - amplitude - of the oscillations of the points in space will increase noticeably. But as you move away from this region, the difference in wavelengths will lead to the fact that the phases of the waves corresponding to the same point in space will be noticeably different, and eventually the peaks of one wave begin to fall on the trough of another - the waves will cancel each other out. Then this damping is again shifted by amplification, and the whole picture will periodically repeat itself. You can verify this by drawing two sine waves with identical amplitudes with slightly different periods and adding them - superimposing one on top of the other, as in figure 2, $a$. The result of this addition is at figure 2, $b$: it is like a sine wave with a period $T$, close to $T_1$ and $T_2$, but its periodic amplitude changes. This process is called beats, and the wave is called modulated.
