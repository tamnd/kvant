If these pulsations act “some into the forest, some for firewood,” in a random manner, then in the end no noticeable wave will most likely arise. The situation is different if the wind speed is close to the phase speed of the wave it excites. Then a resonance will arise: the surface vibrates in time with the air pulsations, and the wave, spreading, continuously compresses. As the amplitude increases, the wave itself begins to influence the pulsations of the air flow, calming them down and becoming even more calm under their actions, and this interconnected growth proceeds even faster than at the initial stage. Such “acceleration” can take a long time, and then the wave will reach a significant height. But eventually, even if the wind doesn't stop, the process slows down. The tops of the waves will sharpen (as in figure 5) and then collapse, forming whitecaps. Vottors, different waves do not exist on their own, but interact, that is, they transfer energy to each other, which is redistributed between them in a complex way.

As a result of all this, a picture of real wind waves is formed, consisting of waves of different lengths, amplitudes and directions. And yet this picture is dominated by waves whose phase speed is close to the wind speed $u$, and therefore the wavelength is approximately $2\pi u^2/g$: the stronger the wind, the greater the character of the length and, of course, the height of the waves. In general, the height of smooth waves does not exceed 1/7 of their length; larger ridges collapse to form caps. In light winds there are only short waves, but even then not always. As already mentioned, for very short wavelengths the influence of surface tension must be taken into account. This effect is reflected in the fact that the phase velocity does not drop to zero with a decrease in $\lambda$, but reaches a minimum for waves with a length of 1.73 cm and increases again with further wave shortening. The minimum phase velocity is 23 cm/s (for clean, surface-free water films). This is the minimum, threshold wind speed at which waves can appear, and the waves are short, “centimeter”; they are easy to notice by observing the appearance of ripples in any puddle during short-term gusts of wind.

It should be noted that fairly regular sinusoidal waves - swell - always appear on a calm sea surface. This is an echo of a distant storm: complex waves arise in the area of ​​the storm, but first of all, long waves, which have a high group speed and are weakly attenuated, escape into the distance. This is the swell, sometimes spreading over thousands of kilometers and causing unpleasant rocking of the ship.

Sea of ​​waves

In the problem of sea waves, the big questions are what is the name of the surface that touched it. “Surface” also because, as we saw above, wind waves disturb only the thin, tens of meters, upper layer of the ocean - after all, for the deepest part of it, these waves are always short. But there are other waves - tides, tsunamis - whose length is greater than the depth of the deepest places in the ocean. In such waves, the movement of water covers the entire thickness of the ocean, and they themselves propagate with the greatest speed $c=v=\sqrt{gH}$ for a given depth $H$. But that's not all. In the depths of the ocean there are “slow waves” - the so-called internal waves, traveling at speeds not exceeding one to two meters per second, but reaching enormous heights of tens, sometimes hundreds of meters. However, what about meters per second? There are also such waves - Russian waves, the period of which reaches several months, and the speed is only 1-2 centimeters per second; For such waves, the rotation of the Earth plays a decisive role. These and other waves are of interest to physicists, oceanologists, meteorologists, and they deserve a separate story.
