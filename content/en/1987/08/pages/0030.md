$$DA \cdot BC < DB \cdot CA + DC \cdot AB.$$

Let us draw an arbitrary sphere through the points $A, B$ and $C$. Let it intersect edges $DA, DB$ and $DC$ at points $A_1, B_1, C_1$. It is easy to see that triangles $ABD$ and $B_1A_1D$ are similar, and therefore $A_1B_1 = AB \cdot (DB_1/DA)$. Likewise, $B_1C_1 = BC \cdot (DB_1/DC)$. Dividing the first of these equalities term by term by the second, we obtain that

$$\frac{A_1B_1}{B_1C_1} = \frac{AB \cdot DC}{DA \cdot BC}.$$

Thus, the sides of the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ are proportional to the product of the opposite edges of the tetrahedra, and (2) follows from the triangle inequality for $A_1B_1C_1$.

The tetrahedrons discussed in this problem are called orthocentric, since their heights intersect at one point (which, generally speaking, does not occur).

**M1040**. The numbers 1, 2, ..., 3n are randomly divided into three groups of numbers in each. Prove that you can choose one number from each group so that one of them is equal to the sum of the other two.

Let's denote these three groups by the letters $A, B, C$ and assume that the numbers from 1 to $k-1$ fall into the group $A$, and the number $k$ into the group $B$. We call a triple of numbers good if they are all from different groups and the sum of two of them is equal to the third. Let us assume that there are no good tracks and show that then for every a from group C the number a-1 belongs to A. It will follow from this that there are more numbers in $A$ than in $C$, which contradicts the conditions.

Let $a$ be a number from $C$. Then $a-1$ does not belong to $B$, otherwise the triple $(1, a-1, a)$ would be good. Let's assume that $a-1$ belongs to $C$. Consider the number $a-k$. If it is in $A$, then $(a-k, k, a)$ is a good three, if in $B$, then $(k-1, a-k, a-1)$ is a good three. Therefore, $a-k$ is included in $C$. Similarly, if the number $a-k-1$ is included in $A$, then a good three will be $(a-k-1, k, a-1)$, and if it is in $B$, then $(1, a-k-1, a-k)$. Repeating this reasoning, we come to the conclusion that all natural numbers of the form $a-ik, a-ik-1$ ($i=0, 1, 2, ...$) belong to $C$. But for some numbers $a-ik$ coincides with one of the numbers 1, 2, ..., $k$ and, therefore, is included in $A$ or $B$. It is contradictory to prove that $a-1$ belongs to $A$. Therefore, if $C$ consists of the numbers $c_1, ..., c_n$, then $A$ contains the numbers $c_1-1, ..., c_n-1$, all of which are greater than 1, since 2 belongs to $A$ or $B$. But $A$ also contains 1, so $A$ has at least $n+1$ in it.
