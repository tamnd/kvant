However, Galvani still made a certain conclusion. The fact is that even in a quiet, cloudless day, he had previously observed the convulsive contractions of frog preparations suspended from the iron grate of the Balkan on wire hooks that pierced the spinal cord of the animal (probably this happened when a breath of wind brought the paw into contact with the bars of the grate). But, as often happens, when the idea arose to specifically observe this effect, it almost ceased to appear: “I began to observe the preparations at different hours for many days, but I barely noticed a few muscle contractions. Tired of waiting, I bent and pressed tightly against the lattice the hook passed through the spinal cord... Now the contractions appeared in front, but without any connection with the change in the atomosphere or electricity.”
Since it appeared as if atomspheric electricity did not play a role, at least the main one, Galvani decided, if possible, to avoid its influence and carry out the experiments indoors. Further systematic observations led him to the following conclusions: a) when a conductor connects the femoral herb (it passes through the spinal cord) from the frog leg of mice, weak contractions of the drug are observed, but not always; b) if a dense conductor made of two different metals, for example copper and zinc, is connected, strong contractions are always observed.

Galvani published the results of ten years of research in 1791 in “Tract on Electrical Forces in Muscular Movements.” This work quickly brought attention to the average Italian and foreign coppersmiths and natural scientists, who immediately began to repeat the described experiments. To explain the entire set of observed phenomena, Galvani put forward the theory of “animal” electricity, i.e. electricity, in all respects similar to “artificial” or “natural”, but expressed by the animal organism itself. The scientist believed that the contractions of the frog preparations he studied were the result of an electrical discharge passed when a metal conductor connected the nerves and muscles, which functioned as the "electric fluid"-storing linings of the Leyden jar.

Galvani's theory was met with enthusiasm by a large number of scientists, including its counterpart A. Volta (1745-1827), considered one of the leading experts on electricity. However, Volta immediately discovered a significant omission in his colleague’s reasoning. In fact, how strange it is, but Galvani not only did not try to interpret, but also did not at all give knowledge to the firmly established motor fact that powerful contractions of drugs are observed only if the connected conductor consists of two dissimilar metals! Having taken up the solution to this problem, Volta, already in 1793, came to the conclusion that from Galvani’s experiments “one cannot in any way conclude about the actual existence of animal electricity.” “I have undergone experiments,” he wrote further, “which reveal the same transition of electric fluid in the case when metallic differences were applied not to animal tissue, but to some wet objects, for example, paper, leather, sun, etc.” Another seven years passed, and Volta’s research, which began with the “Galvani riddle,” led him to a great discovery - to the image of the “Volta pillar,” the first galvanic element that opened man through the era of electric current.

Galvani was no longer alive at this time - he died in Volnier in 1798. Until his last days, Galvani remained faithful to his theory of animal electricity - a theory that most scientists have completely refuted by the work of Volta and other physicists. Meanwhile, back in 1794, Galvani described experiments in which electrical effects in frog preparations were observed without the use of any metal devices (he then carried out the preparation itself with a glass scalpel!).

Only through cavities did scientists begin to realize that Galvani's ideas about animal electricity were far from groundless. A broad medical discipline arose - electrification, and just as physicists consider Galvani to be the founder of the doctrine of electricity, electrificationists consider it the main principle of their science. It is worth adding that electrical phenomena in biological tissues turn out to be much more complex than in inanimate nature, and it has only been possible to achieve success in elucidating their “mechanisms” in our time.
