$$\frac{\rho c S}{P} \sqrt{r} e^{-\frac{\mu H_2 O}{RT}} \cdot \Delta T.$$

When heating the kettle, the temperature changes from room temperature to $T_k=373$ K. Taking into account that the main mass loss occurs at sufficiently high temperatures, let us substitute a certain average temperature $T=350$ K into the exponent. For the remaining values we use: $\Delta T \approx 80$ K, $S \approx 10^{-3}$ m$^2$, $\rho = 10^3$ kg/m$^3$, $\mu H_2 O = 0,018$ kg/mol, $c = 4,19 \cdot 10^3$ J/kg. Taking these values ​​into account, we will find what part of the water evaporates during the heating of an open kettle.

$$\frac{\Delta M}{M} \sim \frac{\rho c S}{P} \sqrt{r} e^{-\frac{\mu H_2 O}{RT}} \cdot \Delta T \sim 3 \cdot 10^{-2}.$$

Thus, in the process of heating a kettle without a lid, several percent of the total mass of water leaves it. The evaporation of this entire mass of water occurs due to the heater and, naturally, lengthens the boiling process of an open kettle. To understand how much, it is enough to note that such evaporation for the heater is equivalent to bringing 1/4 of the mass of water in a kettle to a boil (see this for yourself).

Now let's move on to considering effective alternatives to boiling an open kettle. There is nothing to say about the first of them - the constancy of mass during the heating process. It was just said that evaporation of 3% of the mass is equivalent to heating 25% of the mass, so the need to heat this 3% in a closed kettle can be neglected.

The second effect - increasing the pressure under a closed lid - also cannot compete with the evaporation of water from an open kettle. Indeed, to avoid (compared to atmospheric pressure) the pressure under the lid cannot exceed the esa of the lid relative to its area (otherwise the lid begins to bounce, releasing steam):

$$\Delta p \leq \frac{m_k g}{S} \sim \frac{3 H}{10^{-3} M^2} \sim 3 \cdot 10^3 \text{ Pa}.$$
