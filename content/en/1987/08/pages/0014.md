Clapeyron:

$$\rho_{\pi} = \frac{p_{\text{нк}} \mu_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}}{RT_{\kappa}} \approx 0,6 \text{ кг/м}^3.$$

Thus, for the rate of steam flow out of the spout we finally obtain:

$$v = \frac{PRT_{\kappa}}{rp_{\text{нк}} \mu_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} s}.$$

Substituting for estimation $P=500$ W, $s=2$ cm$^2$, $r=2,26 \cdot 10^6$ J/kg, $p_{\text{нк}}=10^5$ Pa, we find that $v \sim 1$ m/s.

And finally, the kettle boiled with the lid open. He was noticeably behind the first. By the way, you should cook it from the stove with caution - you can get seriously burned by the steam. What burns more - steam or boiling water? Before answering this question, it should be clarified: what burns more - a certain mass of boiling water or the same mass of steam? Let's look again at the ratings.

Let the volume occupied by saturated 100 degree steam under the lid of the kettle be $V_1=1$ liters and, say, one tenth of it will condense on the hand when the lid is opened. As we have already found out, the density of pairs at $t_{\kappa}=100^\circ\text{C}$ is 0.6 kg/m$^3$. Therefore, about 0.06 g of steam ends up on the hand. During condensation and subsequent cooling from 100 °C to body temperature $t_0$ ($t_0 \approx 37^\circ\text{C}$), heat $\Delta Q = rm_{\pi}+cm_{\pi}(t_{\kappa}-t_0) \approx 150$ J is released. It is easy to see that for the same warm air you will need almost ten times the mass of boiling water. In addition, when hunting by ferry, the affected area will be significantly larger. Thus, steam burns hotter than boiling water, primarily due to the significant heat released during condensation.

But we digress from discussing the result of our experiment with two teapots. Why did the kettle with the lid open still fall behind? Let's look at this in more detail. The answer is almost very clear: in the process of heating water in an open kettle faster, its molecules have the opportunity to continuously leave the kettle, taking energy with them and, as it were, cooling the water remaining in the kettle (this process is nothing more than evaporation). Therefore, when heated, in this case it is necessary not only to bring the water in the kettle to a boil, but also to evaporate part of it during the heating process. It is clear that this takes more energy (and therefore time) than when boiling water in a closed kettle, where the “fast” molecules are leveled out of the water and very soon form saturated steam in a confined space under the lid and, returning to the water, give off their excess energy back.

However, there are also two effective effects that counteract distance. Firstly, during the process of evaporation, the mass of water decreases somewhat, which should be brought to a boil. Secondly, boiling in an open kettle at normal atmospheric pressure occurs at a temperature of 100 °C, and in a closed one - at a higher temperature. Indeed, if the kettle is filled in such a way that vapor cannot escape through the spout, due to intense evaporation before boiling, the pressure at the surface increases, since it is now exceeded by the sum of the partial pressures of a small amount of air and water under the lid.
