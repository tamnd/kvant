MOON BRAKE

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
L. G. ASLAMAZOV

People have long believed that sea tides are associated with the Moon. The Moon attracts the water of the World Ocean, and in the part of the ocean over which the Moon is located, a water “hump” is formed. The hump “stop” under the Moon, and the Earth rotates around its axis; the rising water “creeps” onto the shore - the tide comes, the water recedes - the tide. It would seem that such an explanation looks quite natural. However, it cannot be considered satisfactory: it follows from it that hot flashes should be observed once a day, but in reality they occur every 12 hours, i.e., twice a day.

The first theory of tides to explain this phenomenon was created by Newton shortly after the discovery of the law of universal gravitation. Two humps actually form in the ocean: one at the surface of the water closest to the Moon, and the other on the opposite side of the Earth (figure 1). Let us discuss in more detail the reasons for the appearance of the second hump.

We are used to saying that the Moon revolves around the Earth. In fact, both the Moon and the Earth rotate around a common center. If this rotation did not exist, then under the influence of gravity the Moon would fall to the Earth

(more precisely, the Earth and the Moon would fall on each other - after all, the Moon also accepts the Earth). How does this rotation happen? Let's imagine that the masses of the Earth and the Moon are the same. Then they will move around a point that lies in the middle of the segment connecting their centers. Now let's mentally “increase” the mass of the Earth. Then the center of rotation will move towards the Earth. Since the mass of the Earth is much greater than the mass of the Moon (81 times), the center of rotation C turns out to lie inside the globe (but it is necessarily shifted relative to the center of the Earth).
