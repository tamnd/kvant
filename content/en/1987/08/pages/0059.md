How it “works”
first law of thermodynamics

Let us recall that this law is part of the case of the law of conservation of energy as applied to thermal processes. Here is its formulation: the change in internal energy ($\Delta U$) of a thermodynamic system during its transition from one state to another is equal to the sum of the amount of heat ($Q$) transferred to the system and the work ($A$) of external forces, i.e.

$$\Delta U = Q + A.$$

The first law of thermodynamics can be written slightly differently:

$$Q = \Delta U + A'$$

where $A'$ is the work of the system on external bodies.

For each state we can say that it is associated with one or another supply of internal energy. However, it cannot be said that a certain amount of heat or work is contained in the system. Both the amount of heat and work characterize the process of changing the energy of the system, and therefore for the same $\Delta U$, but for different processes both $Q$ and $A$ are different.

Problem 3. In a heat-ash cylinder with a piston there is nitrogen with a mass of $m=0,2$ kg at a temperature of $t=20^\circ C$. Nitrogen, expanding, does work $A'=4,47$ kJ. Find the change in the internal energy of nitrogen and its temperature after expansion. The specific heat capacity of nitrogen at constant volume is $c_V=745$ J/(kg K).

There is no supply of heat to the gas - after all, the cylinder is heat-ash-lined. Therefore, the first law of thermodynamics allows us to write:

$$0 = \Delta U + A'$$

i.e.

$$\Delta U = -A' = -4,47$$ kJ.

In this case, the change in the internal energy of the gas is negative—the nitrogen cools.

Knowing the heat capacity at constant volume and the change in internal energy, we can easily find the change in gas temperature. Indeed, at a constant volume the gas does not do any work ($p\Delta V=0$), and the supplied amount of heat $Q = c_V m\Delta T$ coincides with the change in internal energy $\Delta U$:

$$\Delta U = c_V m\Delta T.$$

From here we find the change in gas temperature $\Delta T = t_2 - t_1$ (here it doesn’t matter which temperature scale to use - Celsius or Kelvin) and the temperature of nitrogen after expansion:

$$t_2 = t_1 + \frac{\Delta U}{c_V m} = t_1 - \frac{A'}{c_V m} = -10^\circ C$$ (or $T_2=263$К).

Problem 4. The $p-V$-diagram (Fig. 2) depicts the process of gas expansion, in which it passes from state 1 with pressure $p$ and volume $V$ to state 2 with pressure $p/2$ and volume $2V$. Find the amount of heat imparted to this gas. Line 1-2 is a straight segment.

The main point in solving this problem is the conclusion that the initial and final temperatures of the gas are the same. Indeed, the product $pV$ and $(p/2)(2V)$ are equal, and therefore the temperatures are equal (remember the Mendleyev-Clapeyron law). But no matter how much the temperature of the gas changes, its internal energy does not change either (see the solution to the previous problem): $\Delta U=0$. Then from the first law of thermodynamics it follows that the amount of heat imparted to the gas is equal to the work done by the gas: $Q=A'$. On the $p-V$-diagram the work is represented as protected on the figure plane by the trapezoid, therefore

$$A = \frac{1}{2} \left( p + \frac{p}{2} \right) (2V-V) = \frac{3}{4} pV.$$
