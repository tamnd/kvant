Only on them can the wave speed $c$ depend. How to combine them with the dimension of speed? Since $[c]=\text{m/c}$, and mass appears only in $[\rho]$, the density $\rho$ must be excluded. Of the remaining values ​​$g$ and $T$, the only combination is suitable: $[c]=[gT]$ (it doesn’t matter what speed – phase or group – we are talking about).

This allows us to state that $c$ and $v$ are proportional to $gT$. The proportionality coefficient cannot be determined using the dimensional method; from a more detailed theory it follows that for the phase velocity it is equal to $1/2\pi$:

$$c = \frac{gT}{2\pi}.$$

By definition $c=\lambda/T$, and from formula (3) the relationship between the length and period of the wave is obtained -

$$\lambda = \frac{gT^2}{2\pi}.$$

This correspondence is called the dispersion equation. One more expression for $c$ can be written:

$$c = \sqrt{\frac{g\lambda}{2\pi}}.$$

What is the group speed? Above we obtained the correspondence $v = c^2 \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda}$ (see (2)). Now, using the dispersion equation, we find the ratio $\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda}$:

$$\Delta \lambda = \frac{g}{\pi} T \cdot \Delta T \Rightarrow \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda} = \frac{\pi}{gT}.$$

Substituting this into the formula for $v$, taking into account (3) we obtain:

$$v = c^2 \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta \lambda} = \frac{gT}{4\pi} = \frac{1}{2} c.$$

So, for a wave on deep water, the group velocity is equal to half the phase velocity, and both of them depend on the period of the wave, i.e. the wave has dispersion.

Let us write down the calculated expressions relating the length and speed of wave propagation to its period:

$$\lambda = 1,56T^2, \quad v = \frac{1}{2} c \approx 0,78T$$

($T$ is taken in seconds, $\lambda$ is in meters, and speeds are in meters/second).

To imagine what actually happens, here is a small table:

| $T, c$ | 0,5 | 1 | 5 | 15 | 20 | 50 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lambda, \text{m}$ | 0,39 | 1,56 | 39 | 156 | 620 | 3900 |
| $v, \text{m/c}$ | 0,39 | 0,78 | 3,9 | 7,8 | 15,1 | 39 |

As can be seen from the table, relatively short waves move at the speed of a pedestrian, while long waves can overtake an automatic machine.

Taking an even longer period, say $T \approx 1$ h, and accordingly a wavelength of $\lambda \approx 20000$ km, we would get a fantastic speed of $v \approx 10000$ km/h. However, for large waves, it will turn out that the wave will “reach” even in the deepest place of the ocean, and our simple formulas will no longer be applicable. They are also inapplicable for very short waves, less than a few centimeters in length - here the influence of surface tension forces is felt. And all these formulas are also suitable for describing waves in a very wide range of lengths - from tens of centimeters to tens or more kilometers.

Although a wave is formed by the movement of wave particles, these particles themselves do not move with the wave at all. You can easily verify this by looking at the float: it remains in place, making only small oscillatory movements. To imagine how the particles of the wave move during the propagation of a sine wave, it is convenient to take the point of view of what is observed moving at a speed equal to the phase speed of the wave. For this, it is observed that the “corrugated” surface of the wave will stop and fall asleep, but the tanning mass of the wave will “flow” in the opposite direction with a speed of $c$. The wave particles on the surface slide along the undulating terrain in the same way as any ball fired along it with a certain initial speed would do. Returning to the top of the relief, such a ball (and wave particle) will have a speed slightly less than the average speed $c$, and if it rolls down to the bottom, it will have a speed slightly higher $c$.
