Whirlwinds...
on the gramophone

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. M. BUBNOV

Currently, physicists have noticeably increased interest in studying the motion of liquids and gases. And this is no coincidence. It has long been known, for example, that it is the vortex movement of cyclones and anticyclones in the atmosphere that determines the weather on Earth.* (Now oceanologists have encountered a similar picture.**) It turned out that the ocean, like the atmosphere, is entirely produced by vortices (of various sizes - from centimeters to hundreds of kilometers), and if they are not taken into account, this will lead to an incorrect understanding of many important phenomena.

What characterizes the whirling motion? The main thing is to have a doctor

shaky movement relative to some center, which, in turn, can also move. In addition to the vortex movement, there may be other types of movement, and depending on this, the vortices can be either flat (for example, a cyclone can often be considered as a flat vortex, since its horizontal dimensions are much larger than the vertical ones), or those in which the vertical movements are commensurate with the horizontal ones (tornadoes, typhoons, “dust devils”, etc.).

Naturally, there are general patterns for vortex movements, regardless of their size. Studying these patterns allows us to answer many important questions: in which direction will the typhoon move? How will the weather change due to the interaction of two cyclones? Let's try to consider some features of vortex movements in simple “home” experiments.

These experiments require a jar containing water and a record player. The jar should have vertical, desired space, walls, and the player should have a spinning speed of 33 rpm. Pour warm water into a jar and place it on the player.
