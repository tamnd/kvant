⟦folio 9⟧

BYE KETTLE
DID NOT BOIL...

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. A. VARLAMOV,
A. I. SHAPIRO

Eastern treatises and chapters in special books are associated with the ritual of tea drinking. And yet, looking at this process from a slightly different angle, you can find many interesting physical phenomena in it, the explanations of which are not even in the thickest culinary manual. Let’s approach the tea drinking process from this point of view, or more precisely, the process of preparing for it.

Let's start with an experiment. Let's put two perfect identical kettles with equal amounts of cold water on burners of the same power. We will close one of them with a lid, and leave the second open. Which kettle will boil first?

Any experienced housewife knows the answer to this question. Wanting to boil the water faster, she, without hesitation, covers the kettle with a lid, and when asked the question, she immediately answers that a kettle covered with a lid will boil sooner. Well, let’s make sure it’s right - first by experiment, by waiting for our kettles to boil, and then we’ll explain the result of the experiment from the point of view of molecular physics.

While the kettles are heating up, let’s put another exactly the same kettle on the third burner of the stove with the same amount of cold water as the first two, and try to boil it faster (with the same power of the burner). To do this, you need to somehow quickly increase the temperature of the water in it, “overtaking” its heating in the first two kettles. For example, put a boiler in the kettle as well. Well, what if there is no boiler?

Let us remember that in order to increase the temperature of water in any container, it is enough to add hotter water to it. Perhaps this technique will speed up the boiling of water in the third kettle? Not at all. Not only will it not speed up, but it will also slow down. To verify this, let’s imagine that water with mass $m_1$, which was initially in the kettle at temperature $t_1$, does not mix and does not exchange heat with added hot water with mass $m_2$ and temperature $t_2$ ($t_2 > t_1$). The heat that had to be initially transferred to water with a mass of $m_1$ to bring it to a boil (to a temperature of $t_1$) is $Q_1 = cm_1(t_1 - t_1)$. Now you will have to additionally heat up from $t_2$ to $t_1$ the finished hot water weighing $m_2$. Therefore, the total amount of heat is

$$Q = cm_1(t_1 - t_1) + cm_2(t_1 - t_2).$$

(It is clear that our “absurd” assumption that the portions of water remained unmixed did not in any way affect the law of conservation of energy in the system, but only allowed us to consider the phenomenon of the process faster.)

While we were flawlessly fiddling with the third kettle, the first one, with the lid closed, was already beginning to “sing”. Let's try to find out the reason for this strange sound and evaluate its characteristic part.

The first reason can be assumed to be vibrations of the liquid that arose when vapor bubbles separated from the bottom and walls of the vessel. These bubbles most often originate on imperfections and microcracks on the surface. The characteristic sizes of bubbles before water boils are about 1 mm (during boiling they increase significantly and can reach up to a centimeter). To estimate the frequency of the sound that arises, we need to find the time of separation of the bubbles from day t. This time characterizes the duration of the shock that the liquid receives in the process of separation of the bubble, and, consequently, the period of oscillations occurring in the liquid.
