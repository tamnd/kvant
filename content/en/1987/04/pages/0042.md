The first wonderful limit

In the textbook “Algebra and the beginnings of analysis 9-10” (Moscow: Prosveshcheniye, 1986), in paragraph 20 (p. 94), the theorem on the arbitrary sine is proved: the sine function is arbitrary at any point and $(\sin x)'=\cos x$. In the proof of this formula, it is used that the ratio $\frac{\sin \frac{\Delta x}{2}}{\Delta x}$ tends to one when $\Delta x$ tends to zero:

$$\frac{\sin \frac{\Delta x}{2}}{\Delta x} \rightarrow 1$$ at $\Delta x \rightarrow 0$.

In other words, the theorem about the limit of the ratio $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ at $x \rightarrow 0$ is used:

$$\lim_{x \rightarrow 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1.$$

Mathematicians call this limit the first remarkable limit.

In this note we will prove the theorem about the limit of the ratio $(\sin x)/x$ at $x \rightarrow 0$ (in the school textbook the corresponding proof is based on geometric clarity, see p. 95). In the proof, we will need a more formal definition of the limit than that given in “Algebra and Elements of Analysis 9-10” on p. 76. Namely, we will say that the function $f(x)$ tends to the limit $L$ at $x \rightarrow a$ ($x \neq a$) if for any positive $\varepsilon$ there is a positive $\delta$ such that for all $x$ such that $|x-a|<\delta$ the inequality $|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$ holds. In other words, the function $f(x)$ tends to the limit $L$ at $x \rightarrow a$ if for any $\varepsilon>0$ one can specify a neighborhood of the point $a$ such that for all $x$ from this neighborhood, $x \neq a$, the inequality $|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$ will hold. (Think for yourself about the equivalence of all three definitions of a limit: our two and the definition given in the school textbook.)

So, let's move on to the proof of the theorem.

Let us first assume that $0<x<\frac{\pi}{2}$. Let us consider a single neighborhood with center $O$ and compare it with centers $AFC$ with the rectilinear plane $ABCD$ (see figure).

The area of ​​the segment $AFC$ is equal to the difference between the areas of the sector $OAC$ and the triangle $OAC$, i.e.

$$S_{AFC} = \frac{1}{2} x - \frac{1}{2} \sin x > 0,$$

where

$$\sin x < x.$$

Let us now estimate the area of ​​the straight line $ABCD$. Since $AD=\sin x$, $DC=1-\cos x$,

$$S_{ABCD} = \sin x (1-\cos x) = 2 \sin^2 \frac{x}{2} \cdot \sin x < 2 \cdot \frac{x^2}{4} \cdot x = \frac{x^3}{2}.$$
