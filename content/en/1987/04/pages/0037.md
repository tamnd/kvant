The peak of the substance - atoma - originated more than two thousand years ago. But, as an eastern proverb states, “no matter how much you say “halva, halva,” it won’t come to fruition.” If atoms are real, they must have sizes and weights. And the task of physics is to measure them. But how?

Put yourself in the shoes of a physicist or chemist of the late 18th century. You have at your disposal data on the composition of a number of substances, accumulated over centuries of alchemical and chemical wholesalers. For example, it is known that water consists of oxygen and hydrogen, carbon dioxide - of carbon and oxygen, etc. It is also known that there are “simple substances” that cannot be decomposed into more elementary ones; these are hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, carbon, sulfur, etc. What is your measurement capability? You can determine the weight and volume of bodies, and in the case of gas, measure pressure. True, due to the imperfection of the equipment, the entire amount of data does not differ in accuracy, errors are up to 10%. - things are in order...

Now think about how on such a basis you can measure anything related to the smallest atoms (the very existence of which was also in question). Dalton came up with an idea and, with such modest resources, began to “weigh” atoms.

He accepted that each of the undecomposed, “simple” substances consists of atoms of the same kind. Dalton understood that he was unable to determine their absolute weight (say, in grams*). It was necessary to construct it differently: take the weight of the lightest atom as one and express the weight of the rest in these units. The lightest of the “simple” substances was water

*) It is clear that in fact we are talking about mass; but we retain the terminology accepted at that time.
