M1015. Is it possible to factor the polynomial $x^4+x^3+x^2+x+12$ into factors with integer coefficients?

$$\begin{cases} a+c=1, \\ ac+b+d=1, \\ ad+bc=1, \\ bd=12. \end{cases}$$

For $f(x)=x^4+x^3+x^2+x+12$, $f(-1)=12$, $f(0)=12$, $f(1)=16$.

Divisors 12: $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 4, \pm 6, \pm 12$.

Divisors 16: $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4, \pm 16$.

One of the three $g(-1)=6$, $g(0)=3$, $g(1)=2$ corresponds to the polynomial $g(x)=x^2-2x+3$, the divisor $f(x)$.

Answer: you can. The required decomposition is obtained as follows:

$$x^4+x^3+x^2+x+12 = \frac{1}{8} (8x^4+8x^3+8x^2+8x+96) =$$

$$= \frac{1}{8} ((3x^2-x+10)^2-(x^2-7x+12)^2) =$$

$$= \frac{1}{8} (4x^2-8x+12)(2x^2-6x+8) =$$

$$= (x^2-2x+3)(x^2-3x+4).$$

A guess helped us find this decomposition. But there are more reliable ways to solve such problems. One could act “in any way”: assume that the given polynomial is decomposed into the product $(x^2+ax+b)(x^2+cx+d)$ of two square trinomials with integer coefficients, open the parentheses and compare the resulting polynomial with the given one. After equating the coefficients at the same powers $x$, the system given in the margin appears; solving it in integers is not difficult - just a little brute force is required. For polynomials of higher degree, this “method of undetermined coefficients” leads to cumbersome control systems.

There are also general rules-algorithms that allow any polynomial with integer coefficients to be expanded into a product of polynomials with integer coefficients or to verify that this is impossible, that is, to prove that the polynomial is not conductable.*)

One of the algorithms for finding the quadratic polynomial $g(x)=ax^2+bx+c$ of a given polynomial $f(x)$ was indicated by Newton (1707). Take any three integer points, say $x_0=0$, $x_1=1$, $x_2=-1$. If $f(x)=g(x)h(x)$, where $g$ and $h$ are polynomials with integer coefficients, then the numbers $g(x_0)$, $g(x_1)$, $g(x_2)$ divide the numbers $f(x_0)$, $f(x_1)$, $f(x_2)$. The last three numbers have only a finite number of divisors. For each triple, their divisor $d_0$, $d_1$, $d_2$ can be formed into a single square.*

*) I talk about irreducible polynomials with integer coefficients in the 10th and 11th issues of «Kvant» for 1986.
