The lack of visibility made the path through the ice of the night Arctic incomparably more difficult than the path to the Northern Strip in the spring of 1979. Yuri Khmelevsky, one of the most experienced participants in the expedition, recalled that due to his flat vision, he could barely see the terrain at all and was guided mainly by the one in front, all the time watching the ends of his lies; he used the same information to evaluate the decision. We finished at the drifting station "SP-27" in March, when the polar day was already quickly replacing the polar night.

Sometimes, in clear sunny weather in the central regions of the Arctic, you can observe an unusual optical effect: torsos on the horizon line suddenly grew several times in size. On average, the height of the torsos is one to three meters, and the torsos that are on the horizon line look like ten- to twenty-meter shafts. When you see this for the first time, you get a little scared: after all, the shafts of torsos are the main attractions, but here, wherever you look, there are just mountains everywhere. But all this is just an optical effect. Looking back, we unexpectedly notice that huge shafts have also grown there, and yet you have just passed this path through the common torsos.

This effect is explained by the non-uniform distribution of air density over height. As you know, pure white snow weakly absorbs the sun's rays, mainly sunlight is reflected from it. Still, the impact of the ice sheet warms up and rises slightly. We were convinced of this more than once by changing the air temperature on the snow and at head level - they differed: the surface air temperature was several degrees lower. And at an altitude of several tens and hundreds of meters, the air still remained cold (of course, if there was no wind, which could easily mix these different elephants). So it’s solid (relative to the air it turns out to be slowly cold). The density of warm air, as is known, is less, and accordingly the refractive index of light in a layer of warm air is less than in cold, more dense air. Density inhomogeneity leads to optical inhomogeneity of the air, which can cause mirages. Ice mountains that appear on the horizon in place of ordinary torsos are mirages.

During the first exploration of the Arctic - at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th centuries - there was rapid recognition of the existence of large areas of land and islands in the Arctic Ocean. Frederick Cook, a famous American polar traveler, one of the first to visit the North Pole, noted in his diaries that in clear sunny weather in April 1907, he saw in the distance, on the horizon, land covered with snow with chains of icy mountains. He saw this land several times. The first time he called his vision Bradley Land, the second - Crocker Land...

Only with the development of aviation in the thirties of our century was the myth of the undiscovered land in the North Pole region bred. The land that Arctic travelers have seen many times in the past is just an optical illusion. Perhaps these were other icebergs - they are very rare, but still found in the Arctic Ocean. And the optical effect quells the desires of travelers, helping them to make wishful thinking - everyone wants to discover an unknown land.
