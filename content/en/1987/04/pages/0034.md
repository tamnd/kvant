Wonderful numbers

The written history of the number π begins with the Egyptian panirus, dating back to approximately 2000 BC, where it is taken to be equal to (16/9)² = 3.1604... The ancient Greeks used a compass and ruler to construct a square equal in size to a given circle, i.e., to obtain an approximation of the number π.

The number τ = √5+1/2 is called the “golden section” ratio. In this relation, point C divides segment AB if AB:AC = AC:CB. (Putting AB = x and AC = 1, we get that x:1 = (x-1), whence x² - x - 1 = 0. The positive root of this equation is √5+1/2.

From the above equation it follows that τ² = τ + 1, and 1/τ = τ - 1. If from a straight line whose unequal sides are equal to τ (such an unequal straight line is called “golden”), cut off a square, we get a similar unequal straight line with a similarity coefficient of 1/τ. This process can be continued indefinitely.

To estimate the number π, Archimedes calculated the perimeters of inscribed and circumscribed polygons from the hexagon to the 96-gon. He owns the approximate π≈22/7=3.1428... Hindus in the V-VI centuries used the number √10 = 3.1611,..., the Chinese used the number 22/7, as well as the number 355/113=3.1415929...

It is curious that τ is equal to the radius of a circle circumscribed around a regular decimal square; also decimal (with good accuracy) is equal to the length of the circumscribed circle, i.e. 2π≈10 or π≈5. The correspondence 5π≈6π is even more accurate. From here π=√30 = 3.1072... More precisely π = √31=3.14138...
