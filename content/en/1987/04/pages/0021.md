Direct measurement of the distance to a quasar

One of the most difficult problems in astronomy is determining the distance to celestial bodies, especially distant ones such as quasars and galaxy clusters. To better understand how this can be done, let's first discuss a few specific issues.

The retreat of galaxies. The Universe is expanding - galaxies, quasars, clusters of galaxies are moving away from us and from each other. The removal rate v is proportional to the distance R and is related to it by a simple formula

$$v = HR,$$

where H is called the Hubbell prime.

Velocity can be determined by the effective Doppler: the change in wavelength of radiation observed as the source moves relative to the observer. Thus, as the source moves away from the observer, the radiation spectrum shifts to the red side, so that the relative increase in wavelength $\Delta \lambda / \lambda_0$ is related to the recession velocity v by the formula

$$\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda_0} = \left(1 + \frac{v}{c}\right)\gamma = \left(1 + \frac{v}{c}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.$$

The factor γ is due to the special relative theory and is due to the change in the clock rate in the moving reporting system.

Hubbell's constant. The value of H shows how many kilometers per second the acceleration speed increases with increasing distance by one merapsec (1 parekk = 3.6 light years = 3 × 10$^{13}$ kilometers). This constant is very difficult to change. To do this, again, you need to know the distance to the source.

There are various methods for estimating distance. For example, knowing the absolute and visible magnitudes of an object (you can read about this in § 23 of a school textbook on astronomy). The absolute magnitude is usually called the apparent magnitude that a luminous object would have if it were located at a standard distance of 10 parekkas from us. To calculate this value, you need to know the structure of the source, and about this you can only make assumptions that cannot be verified.

Two different groups of astrophysicists (based, very clearly, on different things about the luminous object) give two values ​​for H: 50 and 100 km/s per metalarsec. Other, intermediate, estimates are also possible.

Gravitational lens. If on the path of light rays from a distant object to us there is a body with a very large mass, so that its granite field is capable of deflecting the light like a lens, then together with one luminous object two of its images appear in the sky.

The star marked with an arrow in the photo at the beginning of the century is for us the most distant, as well as the brightest, quasar.

Such a massive body could be, for example, a galaxy. If it is located exactly on the line connecting the Earth and the light source, then you will see a luminous ring. If the galaxy is slightly pushed to the sides, then the ring turns into two luminous spots lying in the plane in which the source, the galaxy, and the observer glow.

The phenomenon of image separation by a granitization lens was discovered in 1979. Since then, several such lenses have been found. The most “powerful” among them, discovered last spring, provides two images of a distant quasar. They are located from each other at an angular distance of approximately two seconds per minute and give identical image spectra.

Distance. The fantastic discovery of the quasar doubling (and possibly tripling!) turned out to be very useful for astronomers. It was found that the magnitude of such a quasar is not constant and changes over time. The images “blink”, but not in unison - one lags behind the other by about 1.5 years. This can be understood because light traveling in outer space follows a constant from the source, and then the Hubbell constant. Calculations gave a value of H=84 km/s per metalarsec - a value lying between the current accepted limits (50 and 100).

Thus, the first problem of the so-called cosmic trigonometry was solved, and an absolute distance scale began to be built in space. If we also remember the need for an accurate clock - the pulsarch (see, for example, «Kvant», 1983, No. 12, p. 12 and «Kvant», 1985, No. 2, p. 12), then the words about the geometry of the cosmos - about space and time - acquire a clear meaning.

Everything that is said here, of course, requires verification. Time will tell whether granitium lenses will help with any accurate measurement of the H constant. For now, even the most essential things are also being questioned. It is only clear that a little more idea and observational skill will be required to solve the problem of “finding the distance from point A to point B,” if these points are separated from each other by a distance of millions of parekkas.
