⟦folio 27⟧

M1020*. On a sphere of radius 1, a) a curve is drawn whose length is less than l; b) a closed curve whose length is less than 2l. Prove that there is a plane passing through the center of the sphere that does not intersect the drawn curve. (We can assume that a curve on a sphere is a “broken line”, consisting of arcs of great circles.)

Fig. 1.

The modulus of a point A, and if there is an odd number of points of the set M on at least one grid line, take A on this line. Using the induction hypothesis, color the remaining points as required by the condition. Teler needs to prove that point A can be colored in such a way that this condition is not violated on two lines l and m passing through A.

After preliminary coloring, on each of the lines l and m there will either be an equal number of red and white points, and then our condition will not be violated for any coloring of A, or there will be 1 more points of one color than the other, and then A must be colored with the missing color. This would be impossible only if there were more white dots on one line, say on l, and more red dots on the other. But then on each of the lines l and m there is an even number of points of the set M (including A), and, therefore, by virtue of the choice of A, on all grid lines there is an even number of points from M. In this case, on all lines other than l and m, the number of white points would be equal to the number of red ones. Counting the total number of white and red points along lines parallel to lines l (including l), we would get that there are 1 more white points than red ones; when counting along lines parallel to m, it would turn out that there are more red dots. This contradiction shows that the only unpleasant option cannot occur, and point A can always be colored in accordance with the conditions.

A. P. Savin

The solution to this problem becomes shorter and clearer if it is presented in the language of spherical geometry. Therefore, with some words of this language.

The great circle, or, as is more often said, the greater circle, is the circle ω along which the sphere intersects an arbitrary plane α containing its center O; the ends of the diameter of the sphere perpedicular to the plane α are called stripes of the circle ω, the spherical distance s(A,B) between two points of the sphere A and B is defined as the length of the minor arc of the great circle connecting A and B; if the radius of the sphere is 1, then obviously

s(A,B)=∠AOB.

It is known that each of the plane angles of a trihedral angle is less than the sum of the other two (the proof can be found, for example, in the textbook: Geometry 9-10 / ed. 3. A. Skopts. - M.: Prosveshchenie, 1981, p. 224), therefore the spherical distance satisfies the “triangle inequality” *):

s(A,B)<s(A,C)+s(C,B).

In planimetry, from a similar inequality it is deduced that a segment is the shortest of the lines on a plane connecting two given points; In spherical geometry it is proved in the same way that the shortest line between two points on a sphere is an arc of a great circle.
