⟦folio 4⟧

A second through a single area perpendicular to the segment $\Delta x$ is equal to

$$q = -x \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}$$

(the minus sign indicates that the heat flow is directed towards decreasing temperature). The proportionality coefficient $x$ is called the thermal conductivity coefficient. Dimension $x$ — J m$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$ deg$^{-1}$.

The heat transfer process is determined by the heat capacity and thermal conductivity of substances, as well as their density.

Let us make an approximate estimate of the rate of temperature change during the fusion of bodies with identical properties, but differently heated. Look at figure 1. It shows the temperature distribution (along the “depth”) in the near-contact layers of the colliding bodies after a time $t$ after the start of contact. As can be seen from figure, during the time $t$ in both bodies the temperature has time to change significantly in a layer of thickness $l$ (it is clear that $l$ depends on time). The magnitude of the heat flux $q$ at this moment is approximately $\frac{x(t_2-t_1)}{l}$. This means that energy passes through the contact cross section $S$ in time $\Delta t$

$$\Delta Q = qS \cdot \Delta t \approx \frac{x(t_2-t_1)S \cdot \Delta t}{l}.$$

This energy is spent on heating the layer with mass $m \sim \rho lS$ by the temperature difference $\Delta t \sim t_2 - t_1$, i.e. $\Delta Q \sim c \rho lS \cdot \Delta t$. So,

$$\frac{xS \cdot \Delta t \cdot \Delta t}{l} \approx c \rho lS \cdot \Delta t.$$

The contact cross-section and temperature difference are reduced, and the increment of time $\Delta t$, with the accuracy of this distance, can be set equal to the time $t$ that has passed since the beginning of the contact. From the written relationship we obtain the dependence $l(\tau)$:

$$l(\tau) = \sqrt{\frac{x}{c \rho}} \tau$$

We did not put the equal sign by chance: although the estimates were approximate, the resulting dependence $l(\tau)$ is accurate (like the graphs on figure 1).

The time during which the temperature at depth $l$ becomes equal to the contact temperature depends on the values ​​of $x$, $c$ and $\rho$, determined only by the properties of the materials of the contacting bodies. The combination of these quantities $x = x/c \rho$ standing under the root in equality (1) is called the thermal diffusivity coefficient and is a characteristic of materials. For example, near water $x = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ m}^2/\text{c}$. To proceed further, we will need the warm properties of some materials - they are shown in the table on p. 6.
