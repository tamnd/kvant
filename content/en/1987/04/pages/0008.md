The values ​​of $t_0$ given in the table correspond to our estimates: still air is almost imperceptible, wooden objects are only slightly cooler than the body, glass and stone are cool to the touch, and metals are cold. Gold is the record holder for the largest value $v$; When contacting with a massive slick, the contact temperature turns out to be much closer to the temperature of gold than to body temperature.

Have you accidentally touched metal objects on a frosty winter day? In this case, the skin of the fingers sometimes sticks slightly, as if glued to the metal. This freezes a thin layer of moisture on the skin of the fingers. Therefore, the contact temperature is negative - $t_0 < 0$ °C. Can calculate how low the temperature outside must be for such freezing to be possible. From formula (2) it follows that frost with a temperature of $t < -t_\tau/v$ is dangerous. Specifically, the skin of fingers can stick to iron at temperatures below - 3.5 °C, to aluminum - at temperatures below - 12 °C, to gold - below - 2 °C.

Some people here may have a question. It is known that before the revolution there were coins in circulation. Given the rather harsh climate of Russia, it was probably quite inconvenient to have an object around that froze to your fingers even in slight frost. But for some reason I don’t remember descriptions of such episodes in Russian literature.

Right. There can be no freezing of coins to your hands. The fact is that our consideration applied only to massive objects. Thin coins warm up your hands very quickly even in severe frost. Thus, a gold coin with a thickness of $l$ about 2 mm, if you take it with two fingers, will warm up very quickly, in a time of $l^2/4\chi_{\mathrm{Au}} \approx 10^{-2}$ s. (The same, of course, applies to modern coins made from less noble metals.)

The laws of heat transfer and simple numerical estimates also explain a number of phenomena that seem paradoxical at first glance. Some blacksmiths surprise the uninitiated by being able to grab red-hot iron parts with their hands. In Bulgaria, on the islands of Polynesia and in some other countries there are people demonstrating their economic capabilities with hot coals. In both cases, there are no burns left on the skin, it does not become charred. Of course, the skin of blacksmiths’ hands is thick as a worker, and they also walk on coals as professionals, but the thickness of the skin alone cannot explain these phenomena. Another paradox comes from the region of low temperatures. It is well known to experimental physicists that getting drops of liquid nitrogen on the skin is painless. You can even drink some liquid nitrogen into your palm for a short time. The boiling point of nitrogen is 196 °C; The skin interacts with a liquid of this temperature in this experiment.

And ultra-cold contacts with a successful outcome are public for 24 hours. It consists in the fact that during the first moments of contact, a thin layer of gas with low thermal conductivity forms on the surface of the skin. In experiments with liquid nitrogen, this gas is nitrogen evaporated upon contact with the skin. In the blacksmith’s “experiment” and in “barefoot walking” on coals, this is steam
