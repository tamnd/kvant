What do we do when we want to estimate the temperature of an object that is obviously neither hot nor very cold? “We touch it with our fingers.” In a split second, the hervian endings tell whether this object is warm or colder than our skin (its overall temperature is 34-36 ° C).

However, it is not only the temperature of bodies that determines our sensations. At room temperature, wooden objects seem warmer to us than glass or stone; metals seem even colder to us, despite the fact that the temperature in the room is the same for all materials. So what exactly do your fingers feel? What happens when you come into contact with an object of a different temperature? Let us first consider the simplest case. It is known that by touch you can determine whether a person is larger or whether his temperature is elevated. We, as a rule, define even 37 °C this way; so the felt endings are approximately equal to a degree. To understand what is happening in this case from the point of view of physics is helped by the fact that the properties of the contacting bodies in this case are the same, only their temperature is different.

When bodies with different temperatures come into contact, the temperature distribution near the contact area begins to change: the adjacent layers of the cold body are sheared off, and the adjacent layers of the warm body are cooled. It is obvious that the temperature directly at the points of contact is equal to the average temperature of the bodies: $t_0 = \frac{1}{2} (t_1 + t_2)$. At the end there are several types of herbal endings (receptors) located at a depth of 0.3-0.5 mm. They transmit information about heat and cold, about pressure on the skin, about pain, etc. (The division of receptors by “specialty” is not very clear.) Since we are interested in the sensations of the body and cold, we will use a simplified model: thermoreceptors feel - “measure” - not the temperature itself, but precisely the difference between the initial body temperature and the one that is established after contact. For our sensation of the temperature of objects, the very first moments of touch are most important, when the temperature of the skin, and with it the nerve endings, just begins to change. Remember your sensations: after touching, a short but noticeable time passes, during which the color of the body changes. Let us first recall the basic concepts of the physics of heat and the definition of heat capacity and thermal conductivity. The specific heat of a substance $c$ shows how much the internal energy of units of body mass increases when the body is heated by one degree. When a body of mass $m$ is heated by $\Delta t$ degrees, its energy increases by

$$\Delta Q = cm \cdot \Delta t.$$

The dimension of heat capacity is J·kg$^{-1}$·deg$^{-1}$.

When bodies come into contact with different temperatures, thermal conductivity begins. At the same time, the energy is always forced to colder. A physical quantity that shows how intense the transfer of thermal energy is is called heat flow. The tell flux $q$ by definition is thermal energy passing per unit time through a unit second.
