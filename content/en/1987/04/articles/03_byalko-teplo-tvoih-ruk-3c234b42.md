What do we do when we want to estimate the temperature of an object that is obviously neither hot nor very cold? “We touch it with our fingers.” In a split second, the hervian endings tell whether this object is warm or colder than our skin (its overall temperature is 34-36 ° C).

However, it is not only the temperature of bodies that determines our sensations. At room temperature, wooden objects seem warmer to us than glass or stone; metals seem even colder to us, despite the fact that the temperature in the room is the same for all materials. So what exactly do your fingers feel? What happens when you come into contact with an object of a different temperature? Let us first consider the simplest case. It is known that by touch you can determine whether a person is larger or whether his temperature is elevated. We, as a rule, define even 37 °C this way; so the felt endings are approximately equal to a degree. To understand what is happening in this case from the point of view of physics is helped by the fact that the properties of the contacting bodies in this case are the same, only their temperature is different.

When bodies with different temperatures come into contact, the temperature distribution near the contact area begins to change: the adjacent layers of the cold body are sheared off, and the adjacent layers of the warm body are cooled. It is obvious that the temperature directly at the points of contact is equal to the average temperature of the bodies: $t_0 = \frac{1}{2} (t_1 + t_2)$. At the end there are several types of herbal endings (receptors) located at a depth of 0.3-0.5 mm. They transmit information about heat and cold, about pressure on the skin, about pain, etc. (The division of receptors by “specialty” is not very clear.) Since we are interested in the sensations of the body and cold, we will use a simplified model: thermoreceptors feel - “measure” - not the temperature itself, but precisely the difference between the initial body temperature and the one that is established after contact. For our sensation of the temperature of objects, the very first moments of touch are most important, when the temperature of the skin, and with it the nerve endings, just begins to change. Remember your sensations: after touching, a short but noticeable time passes, during which the color of the body changes. Let's estimate the time it takes for the temperature change to reach the thermoreceptors.

Let us first recall the basic concepts of the physics of heat and the definition of heat capacity and thermal conductivity. The specific heat of a substance $c$ shows how much the internal energy of units of body mass increases when the body is heated by one degree. When a body of mass $m$ is heated by $\Delta t$ degrees, its energy increases by

$$\Delta Q = cm \cdot \Delta t.$$

The dimension of heat capacity is J·kg$^{-1}$·deg$^{-1}$.

When bodies come into contact with different temperatures, thermal conductivity begins. At the same time, the energy is always forced to colder. A physical quantity that shows how intense the transfer of thermal energy is is called heat flow. The tell flux $q$ by definition is thermal energy passing per unit time through a unit second. If, when mixing by $\Delta x$, the temperature increases by $\Delta t$, then the heat flow is the energy passing every day.

A second through a single area perpendicular to the segment $\Delta x$ is equal to

$$q = -x \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}$$

(the minus sign indicates that the heat flow is directed towards decreasing temperature). The proportionality coefficient $x$ is called the thermal conductivity coefficient. Dimension $x$ — J m$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$ deg$^{-1}$.

The heat transfer process is determined by the heat capacity and thermal conductivity of substances, as well as their density.

Let us make an approximate estimate of the rate of temperature change during the fusion of bodies with identical properties, but differently heated. Look at figure 1. It shows the temperature distribution (along the “depth”) in the near-contact layers of the colliding bodies after a time $t$ after the start of contact. As can be seen from figure, during the time $t$ in both bodies the temperature has time to change significantly in a layer of thickness $l$ (it is clear that $l$ depends on time). The magnitude of the heat flux $q$ at this moment is approximately $\frac{x(t_2-t_1)}{l}$. This means that energy passes through the contact cross section $S$ in time $\Delta t$

$$\Delta Q = qS \cdot \Delta t \approx \frac{x(t_2-t_1)S \cdot \Delta t}{l}.$$

This energy is spent on heating the layer with mass $m \sim \rho lS$ by the temperature difference $\Delta t \sim t_2 - t_1$, i.e. $\Delta Q \sim c \rho lS \cdot \Delta t$. So,

$$\frac{xS \cdot \Delta t \cdot \Delta t}{l} \approx c \rho lS \cdot \Delta t.$$

The contact cross-section and temperature difference are reduced, and the increment of time $\Delta t$, with the accuracy of this distance, can be set equal to the time $t$ that has passed since the beginning of the contact. From the written relationship we obtain the dependence $l(\tau)$:

$$l(\tau) = \sqrt{\frac{x}{c \rho}} \tau$$

We did not put the equal sign by chance: although the estimates were approximate, the resulting dependence $l(\tau)$ is accurate (like the graphs on figure 1).

The time during which the temperature at depth $l$ becomes equal to the contact temperature depends on the values ​​of $x$, $c$ and $\rho$, determined only by the properties of the materials of the contacting bodies. The combination of these quantities $x = x/c \rho$ standing under the root in equality (1) is called the thermal diffusivity coefficient and is a characteristic of materials. For example, near water $x = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ m}^2/\text{c}$. To proceed further, we will need the warm properties of some materials - they are shown in the table on p. 6.

Have you accidentally touched metal objects on a frosty winter day? In this case, the skin of the fingers sometimes sticks slightly, as if glued to the metal. This freezes a thin layer of moisture on the skin of the fingers. Therefore, the contact temperature is negative - $t_0 < 0$ °C. Can calculate how low the temperature outside must be for such freezing to be possible. From formula (2) it follows that frost with a temperature of $t < -t_\tau/v$ is dangerous. Specifically, the skin of fingers can stick to iron at temperatures below - 3.5 °C, to aluminum - at temperatures below - 12 °C, to gold - below - 2 °C.

Some people here may have a question. It is known that before the revolution there were coins in circulation. Right. There can be no freezing of coins to your hands. The fact is that our consideration applied only to massive objects. Thin coins warm up your hands very quickly even in severe frost. Thus, a gold coin with a thickness of $l$ about 2 mm, if you take it with two fingers, will warm up very quickly, in a time of $l^2/4\chi_{\mathrm{Au}} \approx 10^{-2}$ s. (The same, of course, applies to modern coins made from less noble metals.)

The laws of heat transfer and simple numerical estimates also explain a number of phenomena that seem paradoxical at first glance. Some blacksmiths surprise the uninitiated by being able to grab red-hot iron parts with their hands. In Bulgaria, on the islands of Polynesia and in some other countries there are people demonstrating their economic capabilities with hot coals. In both cases, there are no burns left on the skin, it does not become charred. Of course, the skin of blacksmiths’ hands is thick as a worker, and they also walk on coals as professionals, but the thickness of the skin alone cannot explain these phenomena. Another paradox comes from the region of low temperatures. It is well known to experimental physicists that getting drops of liquid nitrogen on the skin is painless. You can even drink some liquid nitrogen into your palm for a short time. The boiling point of nitrogen is 196 °C; The skin interacts with a liquid of this temperature in this experiment.

And ultra-cold contacts with a successful outcome are public for 24 hours. It consists in the fact that during the first moments of contact, a thin layer of gas with low thermal conductivity forms on the surface of the skin. In experiments with liquid nitrogen, this gas is nitrogen evaporated upon contact with the skin. In the blacksmith’s “experiment” and in “barefoot walking” on coals, this is steam

and gases formed when the upper layers of the skin are heated. The thickness of this gas gap is tenths of a millimeter in order of magnitude. The gas pressure turns out to be so high that the gas layer can withstand heavy loads - even the weight of the human body. But its main advantage is high thermal insulation.

Let us estimate how long a person can withstand contact with super-hot (and super-cold) objects if the thickness of the gas layer is $l = 10^{-4}$ m. (The surface layer of the skin can withstand cooling to $0^{\circ}\text{C}$ and heating to almost $100^{\circ}\text{C}$ without heat or frostbite).

Inside the gas layer, the temperature is distributed linearly - from the temperature of the hot object (or liquid nitrogen) $t_1$ to the contact temperature $t_0$. Therefore, the heat flux is $q = \chi_{\text{raza}} \frac{t_0 - t_1}{l}$. The temperature distribution at the end depends on time in the same way as with conventional contacts. Let us equate the heat flows:

$$\chi_0 \frac{t_T - t_0}{\sqrt{\chi_0^2}} = \chi_{\text{raza}} \frac{t_0 - t_1}{l}.$$

You see, in this case, the contact temperature $t_0$ depends on time - it increases when touching hot objects and falls when touching super-cold ones.

Skin tissue is not destroyed at $0^{\circ}\text{C} < t < 100^{\circ}\text{C}$, therefore, the contact temperature can change within these limits. Let's calculate the temperature, how long it takes for the surface temperature of the skin to reach these limits:

$$\tau_{\text{makc}} = \frac{1}{\chi} \left( \frac{\chi_0}{\chi_{\text{raza}}} \frac{t_T - t_0}{t_0 - t_1} \right)^2.$$

The thermal conductivities of various gases are close to each other. Therefore, to numerically estimate $\tau_{\text{makc}}$, we take the value of the air thermal conductivity coefficient $\chi_{\text{возд}} = 0,026$ J/(m·s×deg). Calculations show that when in contact with a hot object, the temperature of which is $600^{\circ}\text{C}$, the time for heating the skin through the steam-gas gap to $100^{\circ}\text{C}$ is equal to half a second - quite enough to shift the red-hot large one or take the next step over hot coals sprinkled with ash.

When the skin comes into contact with liquid nitrogen ($t_1 = -196^{\circ}\text{C}, t_0 = 0^{\circ}\text{C}$), the skin cooling time is 1.3 seconds. This is also quite enough to surprise the uninitiated or simply drop a drop of liquid nitrogen on the floor. If there is skinned gas in the palm of the hand, the boiling drop must be moved from place to place with a movement of the hand.
