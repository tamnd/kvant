The first thing that is immediately visible is that the monatomic gas of helium in the temperature range from close to 4.2 K (the cooling temperature of helium) and up to a thousand degrees Kelvin has a constant heat capacity. But the behavior of the heat capacity of hydrogen is completely different. At low temperatures, like that of helium, it is exactly 1.5k - in this temperature range, hydrogen behaves like a monatomic gas. At $T \approx 70$ K the heat capacity begins to increase, and at $T \approx 250$ K it reaches a new, almost constant value $c_{\text{mol}} = 2,5k$. But somewhere around 1000 K a new rise begins, and at 2000 K the heat capacity of hydrogen becomes greater than 3k. With a further increase in temperature, the heat capacity continues to increase, but we will not go into this region, since too many phenomena take place in the gas when it is very heated.

Let's see how oxygen behaves in the same temperature range. For it, it is impossible to change the heat capacity in the gas phase at a temperature noticeably less than 100 K, since oxygen ceases to be a gas. However, if this were not so, then at low temperatures the heat capacity of gaseous oxygen would also be equal to 1.5k; and so the curve immediately starts at a level of 2.5k, but by 2000 K the heat capacity manages to increase to a value of 3.5k.

What conclusions can we draw from the experimental data presented?

1. There is always translational motion of gas molecules, and the heat capacity associated with it does not depend on temperature.

2. Rotations and oscillations disappear at low temperatures—they, as they say, “freeze out.” In this case, ordinary room temperature falls into a region where rotation already exists (for hydrogen molecules, for example, it “freezes out” at $T \approx 100$ K), and vibrations are still “frozen.”

3. An oxygen molecule has only one vibration, and the heat capacity when it “unfreezes” increases not by $\frac{1}{2}k$, but by 1k. This means that the heat capacity coming to each “unfrozen” vibration is equal to 1k.

This is not the number we have already encountered - this is the heat capacity that comes with each vibration in a solid! How can we understand these results?

Quantization comes to the rescue

It turns out that it's all about quantization. And an analogy with one of the first well-known quantum phenomena—the emission of electrons from a metal—will help us demonstrate this. Photoemission - the emission of electrons from a metal under the influence of light - occurs only when the energy of the light quantum $h\nu$ is greater than the work function of an electron from the metal. But there are other phenomena - thermionic emission, which is widely used in technology to create electron beams, for example in television tubes. In this case, electrons leave the metal when it is heated to high temperatures such that the energy of thermal motion $\sim kT$ becomes comparable to the work function. We can say that the emission is “frozen” at low temperatures and “unfreezes” when heated. In other words, the “freezing out” of a movement is skipped when a certain quantum of energy must be applied to excite it. And this is always true, and not just for electron emissions.

It follows: since vibrations and rotations are “frozen out,” it means that these movements will be quantized!

This conclusion seems especially unexpected when it comes to rotation - it is difficult to imagine that molecules cannot be rotated at any speed, no matter how small. But in quantum mechanics this is an immutable fact, and the molecules either do not rotate at all, or immediately so that their kinetic energy, supported by the Boltzmann constant $k$, becomes equal to approximately ten - this is a Kelvin degree (the exact numbers, of course, depend on the physical characteristics of the molecules of a particular substance).
