$$y = a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3 + \dots$$

(where the origin is chosen at the point in question). Nowadays curves are also not called analytical.

Comment. The difference in the behavior of curves of different finite smoothness was well known to Newton and is stipulated in Principia, and the expansion of all algebraic and elementary functions in rapidly expanding power series was one of his main mathematical achievements.

From Theorem 2, Newton derives a much stronger statement:

Theorem a. Any analytic oval is not algebraically squarable, even locally.

7. Proof of local algebraic non-cardability of analytic ovals. If the oval were locally, but not globally algebraically squarable, then a noticeable loop would be expressed by one algebraic function on one side of some of its points and another on the other. But for an analytical oval, the observed analytical area depends on the direction of the ray. Therefore, the indicated algebraic functions are expanded in the neighborhood of this point of the analytic oval into the same encoding power series. This means that these algebraic functions also coincide in the neighborhood of the indicated point. But then they coincide in general in court (this follows from the fact that a polynomial that is not identically zero cannot have roots larger than its degree).

So, if there were a locally algebraically squarable analytic oval, then it would be algebraically squarable in general. And since this is impossible (items 2, 3), then an analytic oval cannot be algebraically squarable even locally.

8. How do you know if you're analytical? A curve is called infinitely smooth if it is locally the graph of a function that is differentiable in how many times.

Theorem a. An infinitely smooth algebraic curve is analytic.

This fact was known to Newton, since Newton knew how to write the equation for the “branches” of an algebraic curve in the vicinity of its point in the form of a fast-coding series

$$y = a_1 x^1/n + a_2 x^2/n + a_3 x^3/n + \dots$$

(where the origin is placed at the point under study).

Newton formulated the theorem about the consistency of this series as follows: “The further the result unfolds when reaching a small $x$, the more it approaches the true value $y$, so that the difference at which it differs from the exact value $y$ finally becomes less than the given value.”

The series is constructed using the so-called Newton polygon. Newton's polygon method*), which is one of the most effective means of local analysis, has unfortunately fallen out of modern scholastic teaching.

Each term of a series with a non-integer exponent has only a limited number of arbitrary ones. If a series contains at least one such term of non-integer degree, then the curve defined by such a series can no longer be infinitely smooth in the neighborhood of the point under study.

For an infinite-smooth algebraic curve, therefore, only integer powers participate in the expansion, and this means that the curve is analytic.

Consequence c. Every infinitely smooth algebraic oval is algebraic uncardizable even locally.

So, an infinitely smooth closed curve cannot be locally algebraic to a curve satisfying the algebraic equation expressed by the equality of zero of the polynomial of their coefficients - a fact stipulated by Newton all on the same two pages of Principia, where it is explained that two algebraic curves of degree $m$ and $n$ intersect at most $mn$ points).
