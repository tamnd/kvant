Newton notes that in order to guarantee local algebraic non-squarability, it is sufficient to require that the points of a closed curve are not approached by “conjoined branches of the curve going to infinity.” Apparently, he had in mind examples like those shown in Figures 4 and 6.

In fact, the words “going to infinity” are used here by mistake; it should be meant to require the absence of any self-intersections.

The correct condition for the absence of self-intersection on a closed curve satisfying the equation $P(x, y)=0$ is that the polynomial $P$ vanishes at two points on a circle centered at any point on the curve if the radius is small enough.

Newton's method proves

Theorem. All algebraic curves that are non-self-intersecting in the indicated sense are algebraic non-squarable (hereinafter locally).

On the contrary, a self-intersecting closed curve can completely turn out to be locally algebraic squarable (for some reason Newton missed this possibility when he wrote “going to infinity”).

Thus, for the lemniscate $y^2=x^2-x^4$ (Fig. 1):

$$\int ydx = \int x\sqrt{1-x^2}dx = -(\sqrt{1-x^2})^3/3$$

is an algebraic function.

But even for self-intersecting algebraic curves, squarability is rare.

11. Newton's proof and modern mathematics. Today's ideas on which Newton's proof is based are called the ideas of analytic continuation and modification. They underlie the theory of Riemann surfaces and a number of branches of modern topology, algebraic geometry and the theory of differential equations associated primarily with the name of Poincaré - those departments where analysis is more likely to merge with geometry than with algebra.

Modern mathematics makes it possible to prove the multi-oher analysis of Newton's theory of plane curves. A sphere in three-dimensional algebraic space is squarable. This follows from Archimedes' theory, according to which the area of ​​a spherical segment is proportional to its height. Therefore, all elipides in three-dimensional (and in any odd-dimensional) algebraic space are squarable.

On the contrary, in a four-dimensional (and generally four-dimensional) space there are no algebraic squarable smooth surfaces (this generalization of Newton’s theorem applies to V. A. Vasiliev, 1987).

Newton’s forgotten proof of the algebraic non-quadratability of ovals was the first “proof of non-multiplicity” in modern mathematics - a prototype of future proofs of the unsolvability of algebraic equations in radicals (Abel) and the unsolvability of differential equations in elementary functions or in quadratures (Liouville), and Newton not without reason compared it with the proof of the irrationality of square roots in "Principles" by Euclid.

Comparing Newton’s texts today with the commentaries of his followers, I wonder how much Newton’s original presentation is more modern, understandable and rich in ideas than the translation of his geometric ideas into the formal language of Leibniz’s calculus, which is accompanied by a commentary: the two hundred years from Newton to Riemann and Poincaré seem to me a mathematical void, filled only with calculations...
