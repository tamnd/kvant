Newton set himself the goal of finding all algebraic squarable ovals. Its result is:

Theorem. Every algebraic squarable oval has singular points*: all principal ovals are not algebraically squarable.

*) A point on a curve is called singular if the magnitude of this point on the curve is not the graph of a function that is differentiable an infinite number of times. Examples: self-intersection point (Fig. 4), cusp point (Fig. 8).

Example. The algebraic ellipse is non-squarable. It follows that the Kepler equation, which determines the position of the planet on the Kepler ellipse as a function of time (in accordance with Kepler’s second law, according to which the area swept by the radius vector is proportional to time) is transcendental, i.e., cannot be solved in algebraic functions.

This example led Newton to his general theorem. An amazing theorem because at first glance, no connection is visible between algebraic squarability and singular points.
