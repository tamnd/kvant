The same thing that can be said about rotation can also be said about flasks. And now it is clear that the distinction between the contribution of vibrations to the heat costs of solids and gases at room temperature is not of a fundamental nature, but is associated with a numerical difference in the characteristics of substances. For a molecule of hydrogen or oxygen, quantum vibrations on the temperature scale are equal to thousands of degrees Kelvin, but for solids - only hundreds. And indeed, it is at such temperatures that the heat cost of solids also begins to change rapidly (figure 4). And this distinction between numbers is closely related to the fact that the melting of solids occurs at a temperature of the order of a thousand degrees Kelvin, and then the dissociation of oxygen and hydrogen molecules - at tens of thousands of degrees. But the mechanism of both processes is the same - this is the thermal buildup of vibrations to such an amplitude at which the bonds “break,” and the lower the temperature at which the vibrations occur, the lower the temperature at which the bonds “break.”

This is the beautiful picture of thermal motion we obtained from the analysis of heat. For complete harmony, one shrikh is missing: somehow it is necessary to put into one scheme $\frac{1}{2}k$ for warmth associated with translational or rotational walls of freedom, and $1k$ for oscillations. It turns out that there is a wonderful way out here too. In fact, note that $\frac{1}{2}$ appears where motion is characterized only by kinetic energy. But with oscillations there is also potential energy, and if the total energy of oscillations is equal to $kT$ (i.e., the heat value is $1k$), then this means that the system periodically transitions from a state with zero kinetic and maximum potential energy equal to $kT$ to a state with zero potential and maximum kinetic energy also equal to $kT$. And on average over time, kinetic energy is exactly equal to potential energy and, obviously, they are both equal $\frac{1}{2}kT$ - and we are back to the position.

So, the final conclusions: at a given temperature, those types of motion are excited whose quantum energy is comparable to $kT$ or less than this value. In this case, both kinetic and potential energy (if it exists!) coming to each degree of freedom is the same and equal to $\frac{T}{2}$. These findings are in excellent agreement with many experiments and are confirmed by a rigorous theory based on quantum mechanics.

Of course, the case considered here is the simplest possible. We can look deeper: when calculating the degrees of freedom, we considered the atom to be a single particle, but this is so. And each atom has its own internal degrees of freedom of movement of electrons of atomic shells relative to the nuclei. However, it takes tens and hundreds of thousands of degrees to “defrost” them. Actually, plasma physics studies gases in which the movement of electrons is “unfrozen.” You can go further - and the nuclei are composed of individual elements of particles, and these particles themselves are not at all elementary. But in order to excite such thermal movements, millions and billions of degrees are needed, or even much more, and here the trona begins, leading to the history of the birth of stars, the galaxy and the Universe itself. And the first thing on this path is ideal gases with their own laws.
