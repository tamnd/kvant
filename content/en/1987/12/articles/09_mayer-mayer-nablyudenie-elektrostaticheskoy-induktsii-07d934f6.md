Observation of electrostatic induction

V.V. MAYER,
R. W. MAYER

Let's bring a charged body to the conductor. Let, for the purpose of definition, the conductor be metallic, and the charge on the body be positive. An electric field will arise in the conductor, and free electrodes, acting against the field, will collect in the area of ​​the conductor close to the charging body. Consequently, this region will acquire a negative charge, and the region farthest from the charging body will acquire a positive charge (which will lead to the disappearance of the electric field in the conductor). The described phenomenon, as you know, is called electrostatic induction.

With electrostatic induction, a redistribution of charges occurs in the conductor, which means that a short-term electric current arises in it. Let's try to prove this experimentally using a neon light bulb.

Such a light bulb (you can, for example, buy it at a radio store) is a glass container filled with neon at low pressure. There are two electrodes on the right side of the balloon—the electrode and the cathode. If the appropriate voltage is applied to the electrodes, a glow discharge will occur in the gas, which is accompanied by a reddish-orange glow.*)

It is clear that a neon light bulb can serve as an indicator of electric current. Its main advantages are that it is compact and that even a very weak current (on the order of several microamps) causes a noticeable glow of the gas in the lamp. The idea of ​​the device for our experiment is almost very clear: the conductor in which the redistribution of charges occurs must be broken in the middle and a neon light bulb must be inserted into its gap. The specific design of the device can be very different and is determined primarily by the type

*) More details about the neon light bulb itself, its properties and practical use can be read in the article by A. A. Borovoy («Kvant», 1983, No. 6).

using a neon light bulb. In principle, any will do, but the best ones are to use bulbs like BMH-1 or BMH-2, whose electrons are equal, i.e., each of them can equally be both anode and cathode.

At figure there is a photograph of one of the possible options for the device. The conductor is formed by two metal rods. Between them, in an ebonite case with a window, there is a neon lamp of the BMH-2 type, touching the ends of the rods with its electrons. A dioral disk is attached to the end of the upper rod, which increases the color of the device. The disk is equipped with a polystylene cover that prevents spark discharge between it and the charged body.

We made such a thorough device only because we often have to demonstrate experiments and it is irrational to waste time every time preparing the equipment. If you only want to observe the phenomenon, then it is easy to figure out how to make a device from scrap materials during production, say copper wire with a diameter of 1.5-3 mm, PVC tube, solder, etc.

Now about the experiment itself. On electronic friction of an ebonite stick, a plastic counter or a strip of plexiglass. Mount the device on an insulated stand or take it in your hand by the lower rod. When the charged stick approaches the disk, you will notice a light bulb glow; Consequently, a current flows through the conductor due to the redistribution of charges. As soon as the rod becomes motionless relative to the disk, the glow will stop: the charges in the conductor have been redistributed and are again in equilibrium. When you remove the stick from the disk, the light comes on again. The higher the speed of movement of the stick, the brighter the glow of the light bulb, since the greater the current passing through the conductor.

Note that the experiment is best carried out in twilight, since the glow of a neon light bulb is not great.
