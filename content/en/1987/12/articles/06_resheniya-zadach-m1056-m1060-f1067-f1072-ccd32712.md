The square is divided into 994 areas, as shown in figure: the first area is the corner cell, the second is the $3 \times 3$ square without the corner cell, the third is the $5 \times 5$ square without the first two areas, and so on. In each of the 993 regions, starting from the second, the sum of the numbers does not exceed 2. Indeed, let us place squares $2 \times 2$ (red squares on figure) in such a region. Cell $A$ will be covered by immi twice, and cell $B$ will not be covered by any squares. Let the number $a$ be in the cell $A$, and the number $b$ be in the cell $B$. By convention, the sum of the numbers in each square $2 \times 2$ is 0, so the sum of the numbers in this area is $b - a$, and therefore does not exceed 2. Thus, the sum of the numbers does not exceed $1 + 2 \cdot 993 = 1987$.

M1060. There are two closed broken lines on the plane, each with an odd number of zenes. All straight lines containing the zenev of these broken lines are different, and no three of them intersect at one point. Prove that one zen can be selected from each broken line so that they are opposite sides of a certain convex quadrilateral.

If one of the links of the first broken line is parallel to a certain zen of the second, then the statement of the problem is obvious (these two zenes are the bases of the trapezoid). Therefore, we will further assume that no two zenins of different broken lines are parallel.

The line containing the zeno will be called the carrier line of this zeno. Since both broken lines consist of an odd number of zenevs, the number $N$ of intersection points of the carrier lines of one broken line with the non-support lines of the other is odd.

Let us assume that the statement of the problem is false. Then, no matter how you choose a zen on each broken line, the point of intersection of their non-existing straight lines will lie on one of these two zens. Therefore, the number $N$ can be represented as (the number of intersections of the first broken line with the non-existent lines of the second) + (the number of intersections of the second broken line with the non-existent lines of the first) - (the number of intersections of the broken lines with each other),
since the last number is counted twice in the sum of the first ones. Let us show that each of these numbers, and therefore the number $N$, is even, in contradiction with what was said above. Thus, the problem will be solved.

We will bend one broken line in a certain direction until it stops intersecting with the other, and watch how the number of intersection points changes. It is clear that it can change only at the moment when some vertex $A$ of one of the broken lines falls on a certain zeno $l$ of the other. For example, when you can choose so that the vertex $A$ cannot coincide with the vertex or self-intersection point of another broken line. Then it is enough to consider two cases: when two zenens emerging from the vertex $A$ lie on opposite sides of $l$ (Fig. 1) and on the same side (Fig. 2). In the first case, the number of intersections does not change, in the second it increases or decreases by 2. How long will it take to deflate an input bubble of a larger radius in the same way? Assume that the air moves through the coarse rather slowly.

The air flows through the coarse slowly, so the force of friction can be considered proportional to the speed of air flow. The excess pressure in the bubble is small compared to atmospheric pressure, so we neglect the change in air density in the bubble.

F1071. In the diagram shown in figure 1, the light bulb burns equally brightly both with the key K closed and with the key K open; $R_1 = R_3 = 90$ Ohm, $R_2 = 180$ Ohm, $U = 54$ V. Find the direction on the light bulb.

Note that the resistance of the light bulb depends on the direction on it: with increasing voltage, the heating of the filament increases, and the resistance of the light bulb increases. According to the conditions of the problem, the heating of the threads with the key closed and open is the same; therefore, the direction on the light bulb $U_\pi$ and its resistance $R_\pi$ are the same in both cases.

Figures 2 and 3 show equivalent circuits with the switch closed and open. Resistance of sections $BC$ and $AC$ —

$$R_{BC} = \frac{RR_3}{R + R_3}, \quad R_{AC} = R_2 + R_{BC}. \tag{1}$$

Substituting into (1) the values ​​of resistances $R_2$ and $R_3$ (in ohms), we obtain

$$R_{BC} = \frac{90R}{R + 90}, \quad R_{AC} = \frac{270(R + 60)}{R + 90}. \tag{2}$$

Voltage on the light bulb when the key is closed

$$U_\pi = \frac{U}{R_{AC}} R_{BC}.$$

Taking into account (2) and the fact that $U = 54$ B, we obtain

$$U_\pi = \frac{18R}{R + 60}. \tag{3}$$

A similar calculation with the key open gives

$$U_\pi = \frac{36R}{R + 150}. \tag{4}$$

From equations (3) and (4) the voltage across the light bulb is found:

$$U_\pi = 6 \text{ В.}$$

V. I. Chivilev

F1072. Why does the presence of an ultrafillet component in the spectrum worsen the sharpness of the image obtained on photographic film?

The index of glass prefill and, therefore, the focal length of the object depends on the wavelength of the radiation. By choosing the shape and materials of the object and the optimal distance to the film, the virtual elimination of blurring of the image when photographing will be added. The corresponding tricks do not concern wavelengths outside the visible part of the spectrum. Therefore, the contact of ultraphyllet rays with photographic film will lead to a general blurring of the image. To combat this disadvantage, filters are used that do not transmit ultraviolet images. For the same reason, on some objects a separate distance cabinet is applied for shooting in the infrared range.
