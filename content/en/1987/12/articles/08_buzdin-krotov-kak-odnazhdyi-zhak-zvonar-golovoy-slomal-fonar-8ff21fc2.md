How many different colors are there in the world? Where do all the different flowers come from? These and many other questions have occupied beloved people for a long time. The great Newton made a great contribution to the creation of the science of color. He observed the decomposition of sunlight (white) light as it passed through a glass prism into the so-called spectrum (from the Latin spectrum). The resulting colors exactly match the colors of the rainbow. The prism allowed Newoton to obtain rainbows in “laboratory” conditions. Here they are, the colors of the rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. Look again at the title of the article. We used a humorous phrase to help fill in the sequence of colors in the solar spectrum (as you can see, there is some truth in our joke). Surely some of you will remember another mnemonic rule - the phrase “every hunter wants to know where the pheasant is sitting.”

It was thanks to Newton's experiments at the end of the 17th century that it was established that white light is a mixture of different colors. (In particular, Newton showed that if white light decomposed into a spectrum is mixed, then white light will again be obtained.) It took a long time until scientists realized that light is an oscillation of electric and magnetic fields related to each other propagating in space, in other words, electromagnetic waves. The most common example of such a wave is radio waves. It was proven that the nature of light and radio waves are the same, they differ only in the vibration frequency. The frequency of radio waves is thousands of times lower than that of light waves. Each color corresponds to a certain vibration frequency. If we use an analogy with a musical instrument, then the red color corresponds to “bass”, and violet corresponds to high sweat.

The speed of propagation of electromagnetic waves is $c = 300\,000$ km/s. It is many thousands of times faster than the speed of sound, so a radio listener sitting at home in Vladivostok near the radio hears the singer’s voice at a concert, transported from Moscow, earlier than a listener sitting in the back row of the concert hall.

The given value for the speed of light ($c = 3 \cdot 10^5$ km/s) refers to taste. When light propagates in a prophetic substance, the speed turns out to be lower. Of particular interest is the fact that when light of different colors passes through matter, it travels at different speeds. This phenomenon is called dispersion, and it is thanks to dispersion that it decomposes white light into a color spectrum.

So, from white light you can get all the colors of the rainbow. What do the different classic colors on this page mean? The blueness of the sky is usually explained by the scattering of light in the atmosphere. However, why then is the night sky not blue even with a full moon? Or why are different parts of the sky colored blue differently - some more brightly, others less so? And if we turn to the sunset? An attentive physicist observing the sunset noted that first the western part of the sky takes on a yellow or orange tint; then, when the sun turns fiery red, the glow of the sky changes from yellow-orange to bright green; finally, up to approximately 25° above the horizon, the sky turns pink.

Since the cause of all the listed color collisions is the scattering of sunlight in the atmosphere, let us dwell on this process in more detail.

The explanation of the peculiarities of the color of the sky belongs to the English physicist W. Rayleigh. In general terms, the body's day is as follows. The color of the sky is determined by the dependence of light scattering on the frequency of the latter. An electromagnetic wave coming from the sun “swings” the electrons that make up the air molecules. The rays of the blue part of the spectrum have the strongest effect on electrons. Thus, from the reduced light wave, the electrons of air molecules are “taken away” by vibrations of the blue part of the spectrum. But, having entered into a forced oscillatory motion, the electrons give back the energy taken from the wave. However, they give off this energy in all directions, and not just in the direction of the fall of the initial wave from the sun. This process is the scattering of light.

The existence of an explanation for the scattering of light in the atmosphere is the uneven distribution of air molecules in the atmosphere - constantly occurring fluctuations in air density. If the molecules were evenly distributed in space, the scattering would be perfect—the sky would simply be black.

So, air molecules scatter the blue part of the spectrum most strongly, and therefore the sky that we see in diffused light appears blue to us.*) The farther away the corresponding parts of the sky are from the sun, the less bright they will seem to us. The reason is that the further the sun's rays travel through the atmosphere, the less their share is of the blue component. Now, you probably understand why they say “the sun is red”? The received solar rays on the way to us “lose” the blue component, and an additional share of the radiation coming to the sky is observed, which makes up the red-yellow part of the spectrum.

It is clear that smoke, dust and other small particles contained in the air exist in ways that influence the scattering of light in the atmosphere. It is known that after large volcanic eruptions, sunrises and sunsets sometimes display amazing colors - the sun and moon can even turn blue. Let us give a description of the consequences of the catastrophic eruption of the Krakatay volcano in 1883, which is given in his book “Entertaining Geology” by the substitute Russian scientist V.A. Obruchev: “Fine ash darkened the Sun in Japan and in other places at a distance of over 3000 kilometers. This ash, floating for a long time in the atmosphere, caused the blue color of the sun and moon in Africa and the Pacific Islands and the replacement red dawns at the end of 1883 and the beginning of 1884, observed throughout the Earth.

The blue color of the sun and moon in this case is due to the scattering of light on atmospheric azrosols with a size of 0.4-0.9 microns, i.e., a size comparable to the wavelength of visible light. Atmospheric azrosols of the indicated sizes are formed by deposition on solid particles of smoke, ash, etc. during volcanic eruptions and large forest fires. “Blue mountains, blue distances, blue cities” - all these positive combinations of words are conditionally used as symbols of pristineness, extraordinary purity, and mystery. The most surprising thing is that physics was involved here too.

So, sometimes over an open area covered with greenery, not too polluted due to human activity, a very red, tinny blue haze appears. The blue mountains of the Caucasus are often mentioned in literature. The Blue Mountains in Australia are famous for their blue haze. The blue color of the “haze” is due to the scattering of light on the smallest parts, the size of which is much smaller than the wavelength of visible light. Such parts can be organic macromolecules emitted by the plant, as well as tiny particles swept off the sharp parts of plants by the earth's electric field. So, in places where tiny particles accumulate, an additional effect of scattering of the blue part of the spectrum occurs.
