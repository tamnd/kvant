KVANT 2008/№4

“Let’s try,” the robbers answered somewhat puzzled. They had never drunk so strangely before. The mathematician began to fill the goblet. He became more and more complete. Finally, one of the robbers could not stand it and shouted: “Stop!” Otherwise it will be too much. Now it’s just 1/10 of the bottle! – But others don’t think so? – asked the mathematician. “Of course not,” confirmed the robber, “they were silent, which means they believe that there is less than 1/10.” - Well, let it be! Then this is your share! “And we will continue,” the mathematician said and began to fill the next cup. – What if several people shout together? – one of the remaining suddenly became worried. “We’ll give a share to any of them and continue dividing.” All the same, in the end, everyone will get at least 1/10 from their point of view,” the mathematician reassured everyone. The robbers thought for a while. - Brilliant! And it’s so simple,” the smartest of them finally said. - Why couldn’t we divide the spoils like that? – he asked. What do you think, reader?

Comments The fairy tale is a lie, but there is a hint in it. We hope the reader understood the problem and the beautiful solution presented in this tale. The sharing algorithm described in it is, of course, not the only one. Perhaps the reader managed to come up with his own. But if not, let the following problems help him see other ways of dividing. Problem 1. The main problem in dividing into three was this: when one of the robbers divided the loot into three parts, the remaining two indicated the largest part in the same way, and the smallest – differently. You can get out of this situation like this: the two remaining robbers divide the largest part among themselves, and each of them also divides the middle (from their point of view) part in half with the first robber. Show that with such a division everyone should be happy. Problem 2: Verify that the following algorithm also solves the problem for three people. The two share all the spoils between themselves. The third then divides the share of each of the first two robbers into three parts. The first two choose a smaller portion of their three parts and give it to the third. Problem 3. Does this algorithm work for four people: first, three divide everything among themselves, then each of them divides his portion into four parts, after which the last robber takes the best piece from each? Note that the algorithm is similar to the previous one, but still slightly different. Problem 4. Formulate and test an algorithm for N robbers based on ideas from previous problems. Problem $5^{*}$. Suppose that in the gang there is an ataman who has set an additional condition - he must

get no less than any other robber (from your point of view). Suggest an appropriate method of division: a) for the case of three robbers; b) for an arbitrary number of them. Problem $6^{**}$. Will three envious people manage to divide the spoils so that everyone is sure that no one got more than himself? Is it possible to come up with a way to share for any number of envious people? Note that this problem is more difficult than the original Three Thieves problem or the Ataman problem. For those who would like to delve deeper into the mathematical intricacies, we recommend looking on the Internet at page http://www.turgor.ru/lktg/1999/razbr.php (which contains a whole research series of problems about robbers, offered in 1999 to participants in the XI Summer Conference of the Tournament of Cities). There are a few more sensitive issues. What if the robbers start cheating, for example, cooperating with each other and dividing unequally? Could this be beneficial? But this is a topic for another discussion. The previous problems implicitly assumed that the loot was infinitely divisible (i.e., each set of treasure could be divided into any number of parts equal from the point of view of a given robber). But what if the production is not very divisible? For example, how to divide a TV or a diamond? If the robbers have money, we can propose such an elegant algorithm. Each robber values ​​each item at the amount of money he believes the item is worth. The one who gives the largest amount pays it and takes the item for himself. After that, all money paid is divided equally. Here is another modification of this idea, which the author successfully used in practice. There is a buyer and there is a seller. The seller wants to sell at a higher price, but is not obliged to sell. The buyer wants to buy cheaper, but is not obliged to buy. Instead of haggling, you can offer this method. The seller writes on a piece of paper the minimum amount A for which he is willing to sell the goods, and the buyer writes the maximum amount B that he is willing to pay. Then they look at the pieces of paper. If A>B, then the transaction will not take place. Otherwise, the price will be the arithmetic average, and both will be happy. Of course, in both this and the previous algorithm, the question of cheating is especially interesting. And one last thing. But in practice, how do you divide a full glass of water really equally (considering the glass to be a cylinder)? It’s very simple: slowly (so that the surface is flat) pour out the water until you see the beginning of the bottom. For reasons of symmetry, only half will remain in the glass.
