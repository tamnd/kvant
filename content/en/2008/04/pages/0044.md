So, the problem posed at the beginning of the article is completely solved. Let's sum it up. The following can be cut into n triangles similar to the original one: g for n = 2, 3 – any right triangle and no other; g for n = 4 and n ³ 6 – any triangle; g for n = 5 – any right triangle, as well as a triangle with angles $120^\circ, 30^\circ, 30^\circ$. Exercise 1. For right triangles, the result follows from problem 3. For isosceles and equilateral triangles, it is obvious: let’s find the height. Conversely, let the triangle be cut into two similar ones. They are rectangular (similar to the solution to problem 3). Two uncut angles are either equal to each other (i.e., an isosceles or equilateral triangle) or equal to two different acute angles of a right triangle. Then their sum is equal to $90^\circ$, and the original triangle is right-angled. Exercise 2. a) Divide each side of the triangle into n equal parts. Through the division points we draw straight lines parallel to the sides of the triangle. b) Similar to exercise 1, we find that small (obtained by cutting) triangles are right-angled and have a common leg. Since the triangles are equal, the second legs are also equal. This means that in the original triangle one of the heights is the median, i.e. adjacent sides are equal. c) From the solution to item b), taking into account problem 3, it is clear that the triangle is isosceles and rectangular. Such a triangle obviously admits the required cutting.

Exercise 3. The answer is the same as in exercise 1: all right, isosceles and equilateral triangles and only them. For such triangles, cutting into three similar ones is clear from the previous one. Conversely, let the triangle be cut into three similar ones. If all three of its corners are cut, then we obtain a configuration with figure 8; Arguing as in step b) of the solution to Problem 9, we obtain that the triangle is equilateral. If one of the corners is not cut, then, reasoning as in solving problems 4 and 3, we find that the small triangles are right-angled and one more corner of the original triangle is not cut. Then either one of the uncut angles is a right angle, or two such angles are equal to each other (and the triangle is isosceles or equilateral), or they are equal to two different acute angles of a small triangle. In the latter case, their sum is $90^\circ$, and the original triangle is right-angled. The only value left unexplored is: n = 5. Not every triangle can be cut into 5 pairwise similar ones $треугольников.^{3}$ From Exercise 1 and Problem 3 it is clear that all rectangular, isosceles and equilateral triangles are suitable: we cut them into two similar rectangular ones, and then cut one of them into four similar ones. Note that the triangle with figure 9 is also isosceles. However, the article by C.A.Jones, P.Jones and A.B.Bolt mentioned above shows six non-isosceles and non-right triangles that can be cut into 5 pairwise similar ones. It is unknown whether this list can be expanded. Perhaps one of the interested readers will be able to prove (or refute) this. The author is grateful to S.V. Markelov for information about this article and some other publications mentioned above.

Will our boat survive?

S. BOGDANOV, O. POPOV, D. TARASOV

MANY OF US MADE BOATS AS BOYS - from boards, bars, boxes, and sometimes from walnut shells or old galoshes. They turned out to be different: sometimes light and nimble, like Indian canoes, sometimes heavy and clumsy, like ancient armadillos. Some were completely unsightly, floating only on their sides, with a list, or even capsizing. There were also various books with drawings of fabulously beautiful ships with intricate rigging, proud stems and full sailing equipment. Finally, there was a mechanics course at the university - with refined simplicity of formulas and succinct formulations, strictly outlining the realities and boundaries of the possible: “the ship is stable if the metacenter is located above the center of gravity.” Everything is evidence-based, clear and understandable, and the calculation algorithms and principles for improving ship hulls, their contour lines, keels and frames are clear. And yet, what attracts is the distant, when everything was ahead and, although vague and foggy, everything was possible. When the feeling of pride as a master and professional has not yet replaced the unaccountable joy from unexpected discoveries in simple matters, with ordinary things... And simple things still give rise to surprise and small discoveries. And sometimes they allow you to look at the complex in a new way. Our boat. Let's consider, for example, a long block of rectangular cross-section - why not a model of a ship, because this is the basis of the hull, and the rest can be imagined and then completed.
