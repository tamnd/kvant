Triumph of fundamental science

The discovery that was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for 2007, in the “Science News” section, strictly speaking, could have appeared twenty years ago. In 1988, French physicist Albert Fehr and German physicist Peter Grünberg independently discovered an effect called giant magnetoresistance (in international scientific language - Giant Magnetoresistance, GMR). The work of these scientists was experimental in nature and was carried out by groups of researchers. But future Nobel laureates not only led these groups, but also gave theoretical justification for the phenomenon. There are several effects in the physics of magnetic phenomena that are of great practical importance. These are rotation of the plane of polarization of light when interacting with a magnetic substance, giant magnetostriction (a significant change in the volume of a body when it is magnetized), magnetocaloric effect (temperature change during adiabatic magnetization or demagnetization of a material in an external magnetic field) and, finally, the phenomenon of giant magnetoresistance. The word "giant" should not be misleading. In this case, it means a significant – up to 20 times – increase or decrease in the electrical resistance of a substance when the direction of the magnetic field in it changes. The effect manifests itself in thin (on the order of nanometers) layers of matter adjacent to ferromagnetic layers that create a magnetic field. The essence of the phenomenon turned out to be related to the presence of spin in electrons, i.e. own rotation, characterized by mechanical and magnetic moments. If you choose a certain direction, then the electron spin has two possible orientations - along this direction and against it. The spin of an elementary particle is a quantum phenomenon. This means that it is in principle impossible to imagine any geometric visual model of a rotating electron. And only a very rough analogy can be drawn between spin and, for example, a coil with current. The only thing that a spin has in common with a turn is that they both tend to orient themselves in a magnetic field so that the energy of magnetic interaction becomes minimal. Such “oriented” electrons pass through a substance more easily under the influence of an electric field - the electrical resistance of the substance is low. But if the direction of the magnetic field suddenly changes to the opposite, then the spins of the electrons will be anti-oriented with respect to it and will tend to reorient. According to the classical theory of electrical conductivity, the speed of chaotic (thermal) movement of electrons will increase, which will increase the electrical resistance of the substance. The studies of Fehr and Grünberg became pioneers in the question of the influence of electron spin on the electrical conductivity of a substance. It should be noted that these studies became possible only after the development of technology for manufacturing the thinnest layers of metal - what is today called nanotechnology. Why did the discovery of the GMR effect, related to fundamental science, receive such great importance and fame that it was recognized by the Nobel Committee as worthy of this high award? The fact is that on its basis a new scientific and technical direction was born that uses spin effects in electronics - spintronics. Spintronic devices developed over the past years have made it possible, for example, to increase the effective density of information recording on magnetic media, in particular on hard drives, by hundreds and even thousands of times. Simply, an increase in the density of information recording was achieved without spintronics - based on miniature inductors. But the effective recording density, i.e. the ability to read information using the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction fundamentally did not allow further miniaturization. And based on the GMR effect and modern nanotechnology, such miniature reading devices were designed that the capacity of hard drives increased to terabytes (thousands of gigabytes). Without these devices, modern supercomputers, laptops and PDAs would be impossible. The discovery of the effect of giant magnetoresistance has become a symbol of the main feature of modern science - its inextricable and rapid connection with technical implementations. And we join the words of our colleagues from the journal “Science and Life”: the Nobel Prize in Physics for 2007 is, first of all, a triumph of fundamental science! L. Belopukhov
