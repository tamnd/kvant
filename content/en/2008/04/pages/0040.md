In the summer of 2002, a message appeared from physicists from Australia: they were able to teleport a laser beam over a distance of 1 meter. It looked like the beam disappearing at one point and appearing at another. Japan has announced a breakthrough in the field of instantaneous data transmission over unlimited distances, paving the way for the creation of ultra-high-speed quantum computers and hack-proof information encryption systems. A group of physicists led by Professor of the University of Tokyo A. Furusawa was the first in the world to carry out quantum teleportation between three particles. Physicists from the University of Vienna managed to teleport particles of light over a distance of 600 meters - from one bank of the Danube to the other. For this experiment, an under-river sewer system was used, along which a fiber optic cable was laid. A line laid under the Danube connected two laboratories, one of which was called “Alice”, and the other “Bob”. Alice transmitted three different quantum states of photons, and Bob was able to reproduce them. At the same time, the teleportation process did not occur instantly, since it is limited by the speed of light. Scientists say that the value of the current experiment lies in the fact that it was carried out under conditions as close as possible to real ones. Two interesting articles - theoretical and experimental - were recently published in the journal Nature. They lead in different ways, but to one cherished goal - the creation of a quantum computer capable of solving problems that are fundamentally beyond the capabilities of modern computing machines. In a theoretical article, US National Institute of Standards and Technology professor E. Neill proposed a new hierarchical quantum computer architecture. According to the author, this architecture makes it possible to build a quantum computer based on components already implemented in the experiment. The main obstacle to the creation of quantum computers is the rapid destruction of delicate quantum information by external noise. A unit of quantum information, a qubit, capable of representing either a logical zero or one, or both, is realized as the quantum state of a photon, atom, ion or other microparticle. This is the basic block of a quantum computer. So far, scientific laboratories have been able to carry out quantum calculations for only a few qubits, which, of course, is not enough for practical calculations. And the more qubits in a quantum computer, the faster the information is destroyed. To get around this difficulty, Neill proposed organizing all the qubits of a quantum computer into a simple pyramidal structure of small blocks of qubits. Quantum data will be teleported from level to level and constantly checked for integrity. With such a hierarchical structure of the computer, calculations can be carried out even if the probability of an error of one qubit during the calculations is three percent. And this level has already been achieved in experiments on ion quantum computers. There are no problems with teleportation of quantum information. In the work of a large group of European scientists, coordinated from the University of Vienna, it was possible for the first time to experimentally implement the “unidirectional” quantum computing proposed by theorists. A unidirectional quantum computer is fundamentally different from a conventional quantum computer and is even forcing scientists to rethink the very concept of quantum computing. In a conventional quantum computer, the initial quantum states of the qubits are first prepared, then the computational algorithm is implemented through a sequence of time-reversible manipulations with the states of the qubits, and the result of the calculations is measured. It is the implementation of reversible manipulations, i.e. “entanglement” of the states of qubits, which are easily destroyed by any noise, causes the main difficulties. In a unidirectional quantum computer, a cluster of quantum particles is initially prepared in a highly “entangled” state, and the quantum computing algorithm is reduced to a sequence of irreversible measurements of the states of the qubits. The irreversibility of calculations, which are much less spoiled by noise, makes it necessary to call such a computer unidirectional. Theorists have shown that in a unidirectional way it is possible to implement a search algorithm, to which it is possible to reduce all other “overwhelming” algorithms, which can be solved on a regular computer only by directly enumerating all answer options. In the experiment, a unidirectional computer was built on four qubits, physically realized by the polarization states of four photons. The experimental setup is a complex combination of pulsed lasers, polarization filters, nonlinear optical crystals and photodetectors. Four photons were prepared in an “entangled” quantum state, and then a series of measurements of their polarization allowed the algorithm to be successfully executed. Of course, four qubits is not a lot, but at this stage the most important thing is experimental proof of concept. So far, the relatively young direction of unidirectional quantum computing is less developed than the traditional one. However, it is with it that a number of experts pin high hopes for the creation of quantum computers for practical calculations in the foreseeable future. It is difficult to say whether scientists will succeed in this, but judging by the fact that new solutions to old problems are constantly being proposed, their hopes are by no means groundless. Recently, there has been a merger of quantum computers with quantum communication (teleportation), naturally imaginary, since neither one nor the other exists yet. There is already talk about the quantum Internet. The fact is that in a quantum computer it is very difficult to arrange two-qubit operations, which precisely lead to entangled states - the basis of quantum computing. It may be a simpler (cheaper) method to supply the consumer with ready-made entangled qubits from some kind of supercomputer connected to a quantum communication network.
