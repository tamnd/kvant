THEY WRITE TO US

Physics in Animal Tales

Recently, the next edition of the book by the famous writer and nature connoisseur of Canada and North America E. Seton-Thompson “Stories about Animals” was published. In the book, the world of this part of the world, far from us, appears before the reader as if alive. The heroes of his stories - sometimes touching, sometimes tragic - are animals whose habits are well known. But let's look at the book through the prism of physics - after all, every natural phenomenon, including living ones, also has a physical side, without, of course, being completely reduced to it. Those who have read this book (which I sincerely advise everyone to do), of course, remember the story “Jack the War Horse. The story of a rabbit." It humorously describes a "running race" between a rabbit and dogs. When the dogs begin to overtake the rabbit while running in a straight line, he begins to maneuver, abruptly changes the direction of his run, dodging them. A well-known property of bodies – inertia – stands on his side. A dog is more massive than a rabbit, so it is more difficult for it to quickly change the magnitude and direction of its body speed in order to pursue the rabbit, which it instinctively uses. Other factors favoring the rabbit with such tactics include jumping, as a common method of movement, and the difficulty of predicting its next maneuver. In the story about the carrier pigeon Arno, the hero becomes a victim of dove falcons. This predator dives at its prey and, having accelerated, hits it in the back with its paws from above. Moreover, it is not the claws that kill the bird, which are resisted by elastic and durable plumage, but fractures of the cervical vertebrae, the cause of which is the inertia of its head. The push of the falcon's paws imparts such a large acceleration to the bird's body that its neck cannot transmit it to its head, since for this to happen a force must be applied to the neck that exceeds its strength in this direction. Describing what is happening, they often say: “While the body of the bird received a push (acceleration), the head continues to move by inertia.” This is true only for the very first moment of time after the impact, when the force acting on the bird’s head from the side of the body is small and has not had time to impart noticeable acceleration to it. A person may find himself in a similar situation, but it is no longer predators that threaten him, but the means of transportation he himself has invented. This often happens to passengers in a car when it is rear-ended by another vehicle (called whiplash). In this case, head restraints installed on the backs of the seats are designed to save the cervical vertebrae from damage. When describing the behavior of a fox pursued by dogs, Seton-Thompson ignored one fact that is of some interest to us - and both foxes and some other animals resort to this technique. It lies in the fact that the fox, escaping from the chase and sharply changing the direction of movement to do this, skillfully uses its tail. Turning in one direction, she forcefully moves her tail in the opposite direction. However, there is always a reaction to every action, so the tail, in turn, acts on the back of the animal’s body with a force that is the same in magnitude, but opposite in direction. Thanks to this, the animal manages to make a turn without allowing the rear part of the body to “skid”. You will say that a tail set in motion is not left to the mercy of fate. But the fact is that the technique in question allows, by making a quick turn, to stretch out the expenditure of forces on it over time, without overloading the muscles and without exceeding the limit of the adhesion force of the paws to the ground. To do this, first they “take” a certain amount of movement from the tail, and a little later they “return” it to it. In addition, if the pursuer focused his attention on the part of the victim being caught up with him, closest to him - on its tail, then the movement of the tail in the direction opposite to the turn confuses the pursuer. It is no coincidence that the tip of the tail has a distinctive white color. This technique, which makes turning easier, is used by many animals with a relatively massive tail (relative to the weight of the entire body), including predators, for example, the best runner, the cheetah. And the larger the proportion of tail mass in relation to the entire body mass, the better results this technique gives. Naturalists note that, using its relatively massive tail as a rudder, or, more precisely, a counterweight, the jerboa can change the direction of movement in the most unexpected way, jumping on loose sand. In one of the stories, Seton-Thompson describes how his companion, an experienced hunter, determines the direction of the wind, which he needs to know in order to approach the animal unnoticed. To do this, he wets one side of his index finger and, holding it vertically, slowly turns the wet surface in all directions. And then he concludes that the wind blows from the direction in which the finger feels the greatest cold. The author further concludes that animals apparently do the same thing, only they always have a wet nose for this purpose. Let's discuss this phenomenon from the point of view of physics. Liquid evaporates from a wet surface, and at the same time it cools. The blowing air carries evaporated molecules away from the surface and prevents their return and the energy they carried away. This increases evaporation and the accompanying cooling. This process occurs most intensively on the windward side, i.e. facing where the wind is blowing.
