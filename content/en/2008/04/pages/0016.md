Proof of Feuerbach's theorem

The key moment has arrived: the transition from Thébault to Feuerbach. Using the same technique with which we proved Thebaud's theorem, we will prove Feuerbach's theorem. Let ABC be the given triangle, $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}$ the midpoints of its sides, $A_{2}, B_{2}, C_{2}$ the bases of the altitudes. The last six points, as we know, lie on one circle - the circle of nine points. It is necessary to prove that it touches the inscribed and all excircles of triangle ABC. The idea is this: let's select a pair of circles - the inscribed circle of triangle ABC and one of its excircles, say the one that touches side AC. Let's try to imagine this pair as a pair of Thebaut circles for some triangle inscribed in a circle of nine points. If this can be done, then Feuerbach's theorem will be established. In fact, Thebaud circles (by definition!) are tangent to the circumcircle of the triangle, i.e. our circle of nine points. This means that the circle of nine points touches the incircle and one of the excircles. Having done the same with other excircles, we obtain the required result. The main question is which of the triangles inscribed in a circle of nine points should be taken? Neither the “middle” triangle $A_{1} B_{1} C_{1}$ (with vertices in the middle of the sides) nor the “orthotriangle” $A_{2} B_{2} C_{2}$ (with vertices at the bases of the heights) are suitable for this purpose. Even if we limit ourselves to only six points $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}, A_{2}, B_{2}, C_{2}$, then there are 20 triangles with vertices at these points (why exactly so many?). All of them will be inscribed in a circle of nine points. And only one of them will do! Let’s not bore the reader, let’s say the answer right away: this is a triangle $B_{1} B_{2} A_{2}$ (Fig.9). Its vertices are the bases of two heights - $A_{2}$ and $B_{2}$ and the middle of the side - $B_{1}$.

The inscribed and excircles of triangle ABC, tangent to side AC, are Tebaut circles for triangle $B_{1} B_{2} A_{2}$ and for point C on the extension of its side $B_{1} B_{2}$. In this case, the case of Thebaud's theorem arises, depicted in figure 8, b (when the point M is taken on the extension of the side of the triangle). So, if in Tebo’s theorem we take the point M not on the side, but on the extension of the side of the triangle, then we obtain Feuerbach’s theorem. Thus, both theorems are two facets of the same statement. And both are proven using the main theorem. Now we need to prove that this is indeed the case: the inscribed and excircle are Thebaut circles for the triangle $B_{1} B_{2} A_{2}$ and the point C on the extension of its side $B_{1} B_{2}$. To do this, we will use the main theorem. If we show that the segment MN between the points of tangency of the inscribed circle of triangle ABC with sides CA and CB, respectively, contains the center of the inscribed circle $I_{1}$ of triangle $B_{1} B_{2} A_{2}$, then everything will be done. In fact, it turns out that through the center of the incircle $I_{1}$ we drew a line MN, forming equal angles with the lines $CA_{2}$ and $CB_{2}$. Then from the theorem (more precisely, from its case presented in figure 6,a) it follows that the circle tangent to these lines at points M and N (this is the incircle of triangle ABC) is inscribed in the curvilinear triangle $A_{2} CB_{2}$, bounded by the segments $CA_{2}, CB_{2}$ and the arc $B_{2} A_{2}$ of the circle of nine points. This means that it touches the circle of nine points. As we can see, the reasoning is the same as in the construction in Apollonius’ problem. For an excircle the proof is completely similar. Thus, it remains to prove that the line MN contains the center of the incircle $I_{1}$ of the triangle $B_{1} B_{2} A_{2}$.
