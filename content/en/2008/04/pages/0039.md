In other words, Bennett showed that, in principle, coherent particles can serve as a kind of “transport.” By attaching a third – “information” – particle to one of the linked particles, you can transfer its properties to another, even without measuring these properties. Pioneers in the experimental implementation of such a communication channel were the work of two research groups - a group of Austrian researchers from the Institute of Experimental Physics of the University of Innsbruck, headed by A. Zeilinger, and a group of Italian researchers from the University of Rome, led by F. De Martini. The experiments of these groups have proven the feasibility of EPR principles in practice for transmitting polarization states between two photons through optical fibers via a third one at distances of up to 10 kilometers, i.e. teleportation of a quantum state was observed over gigantic (on a microworld scale) distances. Instead of using a separate “messenger photon,” the Italian researchers proposed considering two characteristics of each bound particle simultaneously: polarization and direction of motion. This makes it possible to theoretically describe them as separate particles and at the same time, carrying out measurements only with the first particle, obtain the characteristics of the second without touching it, i.e. carry out teleportation. Similar experimental results were obtained in other laboratories in Europe, the USA and Asia, so photon teleportation has now become quite commonplace for physicists. However, so far no one has been able to teleport objects as heavy (by the standards of the microworld) as atoms with multinucleon nuclei. Physicists from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology achieved success with beryllium-9 ions, and their colleagues at the University of Innsbruck worked with even more massive calcium-40 ions. The behavior of each quantum system, be it a photon, an elementary particle, an atom or a molecule, is described by a certain set of parameters - quantum numbers. Such parameters in their totality provide all the completeness of information about the object, achievable within the framework of quantum mechanics. For example, a single electron flying freely in empty space is described by only two parameters - momentum and spin. To fully describe the electron that is part of the hydrogen atom, four parameters are needed: the principal quantum number, which determines its energy, the orbital quantum number, which specifies the angular momentum of the electron, the magnetic quantum number, which characterizes the orientation of this moment, and, finally, again the spin. Other quantum objects may require different quantum numbers. The complete set of quantum numbers of any quantum object, for simplicity let’s call it a particle, is precisely what is called its state. Both of the mentioned research teams, American and Austrian, worked with ions captured in traps, which they manipulated using laser pulses. Under these conditions, the dynamics of each particle could be described using only one quantum parameter - spin. The spin value of each particle was the same, but its direction could change. Interestingly, each act of teleportation took only 4 milliseconds, and it was carried out according to computer commands. For the Austrians, the accuracy of information transfer was 75%, and for the Americans - 78%.

Teleportation of not only photons, but also other particles - electrons, atoms and even ions - will allow the transfer of a quantum state from a short-lived particle to a more stable one. In this way, it will be possible to create storage devices where information brought by photons will be stored on ions isolated from the environment. After reliable methods of quantum teleportation are created, real prerequisites will arise for the creation of quantum computing systems. Teleportation will provide reliable transmission and storage of information against the background of powerful interference, when all other methods are ineffective, and can be used for communication between several quantum computers. In addition, the methods developed by the researchers are of great importance for future experiments in quantum mechanics, for testing and refining a number of modern physical theories. In various countries, programs are being discussed to use the effect of quantum teleportation to create quantum optical computers, where photons will be information carriers. The operating speed and volume of information of quantum computers will be tens of orders of magnitude greater than those of existing computers. Quantum teleportation could play a significant role in future information transfer systems. If a quantum mechanical computer is ever created, the theoretical foundations of which were laid by R. Feynman in the late 70s - early 80s of the last century, then the information in it will be stored in the form of a set of quantum states. The effects of EPR and quantum teleportation will make it possible to copy and exchange information inside a quantum computer. And not only computers - it will be possible to teleport data in any digital systems. The advantage of the teleportation method of transmitting information is that an exact copy of the state, which is an elementary memory cell, is transferred from one point in space to another. Thus, it becomes possible to transmit information without loss, with 100% efficiency. It is the prospect of creating fundamentally new communication systems with the absolute impossibility of unauthorized access to data that determines the current scientific excitement around quantum teleportation. However, as for engineering solutions to this problem, one should not think that they will be found tomorrow: technological developments have only just begun. In fact, until very recently, physicists denied the possibility of teleportation primarily because any particles are both particles and waves. This is where the difficulty lies: in order to create an exact duplicate of a particle, it is necessary to first determine its speed and its wave properties, in particular its momentum. But this is prohibited by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. According to this principle, it is impossible to simultaneously determine both the wave and corpuscular properties of a particle. And now (as already mentioned) this “quantum paradox” is being experimentally resolved in many research laboratories around the world. Experiments conducted by Danish scientists at the end of September 2001 proved the practical possibility of such teleportation. Experimenters have achieved a quantum connection between two clouds of cesium vapor cooled to almost absolute zero, located at a considerable distance from each other. The Danes managed to bind about a million cesium atoms, albeit for a very short time - for a fraction of a millisecond. The connection should lead to mutual influence of the vapor clouds.
