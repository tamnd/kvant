Physics in Animal Tales

Recently, the next edition of the book by the famous writer and nature connoisseur of Canada and North America E. Seton-Thompson “Stories about Animals” was published. In the book, the world of this part of the world, far from us, appears before the reader as if alive. The heroes of his stories - sometimes touching, sometimes tragic - are animals whose habits are well known. But let's look at the book through the prism of physics - after all, every natural phenomenon, including living ones, also has a physical side, without, of course, being completely reduced to it. Those who have read this book (which I sincerely advise everyone to do), of course, remember the story “Jack the War Horse. The story of a rabbit." It humorously describes a "running race" between a rabbit and dogs. When the dogs begin to overtake the rabbit while running in a straight line, he begins to maneuver, abruptly changes the direction of his run, dodging them. A well-known property of bodies – inertia – stands on his side. A dog is more massive than a rabbit, so it is more difficult for it to quickly change the magnitude and direction of its body speed in order to pursue the rabbit, which it instinctively uses. Other factors favoring the rabbit with such tactics include jumping, as a common method of movement, and the difficulty of predicting its next maneuver. In the story about the carrier pigeon Arno, the hero becomes a victim of dove falcons. This predator dives at its prey and, having accelerated, hits it in the back with its paws from above. Moreover, it is not the claws that kill the bird, which are resisted by elastic and durable plumage, but fractures of the cervical vertebrae, the cause of which is the inertia of its head. The push of the falcon's paws imparts such a large acceleration to the bird's body that its neck cannot transmit it to its head, since for this to happen a force must be applied to the neck that exceeds its strength in this direction. Describing what is happening, they often say: “While the body of the bird received a push (acceleration), the head continues to move by inertia.” This is true only for the very first moment of time after the impact, when the force acting on the bird’s head from the side of the body is small and has not had time to impart noticeable acceleration to it. A person may find himself in a similar situation, but it is no longer predators that threaten him, but the means of transportation he himself has invented. This often happens to passengers in a car when it is rear-ended by another vehicle (called whiplash). In this case, head restraints installed on the backs of the seats are designed to save the cervical vertebrae from damage. When describing the behavior of a fox pursued by dogs, Seton-Thompson ignored one fact that is of some interest to us - and both foxes and some other animals resort to this technique. It lies in the fact that the fox, escaping from the chase and sharply changing the direction of movement to do this, skillfully uses its tail. Turning in one direction, she forcefully moves her tail in the opposite direction. However, there is always a reaction to every action, so the tail, in turn, acts on the back of the animal’s body with a force that is the same in magnitude, but opposite in direction. Thanks to this, the animal manages to make a turn without allowing the rear part of the body to “skid”. You will say that a tail set in motion is not left to the mercy of fate. In one of the stories, Seton-Thompson describes how his companion, an experienced hunter, determines the direction of the wind, which he needs to know in order to approach the animal unnoticed. To do this, he wets one side of his index finger and, holding it vertically, slowly turns the wet surface in all directions. And then he concludes that the wind blows from the direction in which the finger feels the greatest cold. The author further concludes that animals apparently do the same thing, only they always have a wet nose for this purpose. Let's discuss this phenomenon from the point of view of physics. Liquid evaporates from a wet surface, and at the same time it cools. The blowing air carries evaporated molecules away from the surface and prevents their return and the energy they carried away. This increases evaporation and the accompanying cooling. This process occurs most intensively on the windward side, i.e. facing where the wind is blowing.

Pioneers in the experimental implementation of such a communication channel were the work of two research groups - a group of Austrian researchers from the Institute of Experimental Physics of the University of Innsbruck, headed by A. Zeilinger, and a group of Italian researchers from the University of Rome, led by F. De Martini. The experiments of these groups have proven the feasibility of EPR principles in practice for transmitting polarization states between two photons through optical fibers via a third one at distances of up to 10 kilometers, i.e. teleportation of a quantum state was observed over gigantic (on a microworld scale) distances. Instead of using a separate “messenger photon,” the Italian researchers proposed considering two characteristics of each bound particle simultaneously: polarization and direction of motion. This makes it possible to theoretically describe them as separate particles and at the same time, carrying out measurements only with the first particle, obtain the characteristics of the second without touching it, i.e. carry out teleportation. Similar experimental results were obtained in other laboratories in Europe, the USA and Asia, so photon teleportation has now become quite commonplace for physicists. However, so far no one has been able to teleport objects as heavy (by the standards of the microworld) as atoms with multinucleon nuclei. Physicists from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology achieved success with beryllium-9 ions, and their colleagues at the University of Innsbruck worked with even more massive calcium-40 ions. The behavior of each quantum system, be it a photon, an elementary particle, an atom or a molecule, is described by a certain set of parameters - quantum numbers. Such parameters in their totality provide all the completeness of information about the object, achievable within the framework of quantum mechanics. For example, a single electron flying freely in empty space is described by only two parameters - momentum and spin. To fully describe the electron that is part of the hydrogen atom, four parameters are needed: the principal quantum number, which determines its energy, the orbital quantum number, which specifies the angular momentum of the electron, the magnetic quantum number, which characterizes the orientation of this moment, and, finally, again the spin. Other quantum objects may require different quantum numbers. The complete set of quantum numbers of any quantum object, for simplicity let’s call it a particle, is precisely what is called its state. Both of the mentioned research teams, American and Austrian, worked with ions captured in traps, which they manipulated using laser pulses. Under these conditions, the dynamics of each particle could be described using only one quantum parameter - spin. The spin value of each particle was the same, but its direction could change. Interestingly, each act of teleportation took only 4 milliseconds, and it was carried out according to computer commands. For the Austrians, the accuracy of information transfer was 75%, and for the Americans - 78%.

Teleportation of not only photons, but also other particles - electrons, atoms and even ions - will allow the transfer of a quantum state from a short-lived particle to a more stable one. In this way, it will be possible to create storage devices where information brought by photons will be stored on ions isolated from the environment. After reliable methods of quantum teleportation are created, real prerequisites will arise for the creation of quantum computing systems. Teleportation will provide reliable transmission and storage of information against the background of powerful interference, when all other methods are ineffective, and can be used for communication between several quantum computers. In addition, the methods developed by the researchers are of great importance for future experiments in quantum mechanics, for testing and refining a number of modern physical theories. In various countries, programs are being discussed to use the effect of quantum teleportation to create quantum optical computers, where photons will be information carriers. The operating speed and volume of information of quantum computers will be tens of orders of magnitude greater than those of existing computers. Quantum teleportation could play a significant role in future information transfer systems. If a quantum mechanical computer is ever created, the theoretical foundations of which were laid by R. Feynman in the late 70s - early 80s of the last century, then the information in it will be stored in the form of a set of quantum states. The effects of EPR and quantum teleportation will make it possible to copy and exchange information inside a quantum computer. And not only computers - it will be possible to teleport data in any digital systems. The advantage of the teleportation method of transmitting information is that an exact copy of the state, which is an elementary memory cell, is transferred from one point in space to another. Thus, it becomes possible to transmit information without loss, with 100% efficiency. It is the prospect of creating fundamentally new communication systems with the absolute impossibility of unauthorized access to data that determines the current scientific excitement around quantum teleportation. However, as for engineering solutions to this problem, one should not think that they will be found tomorrow: technological developments have only just begun. In fact, until very recently, physicists denied the possibility of teleportation primarily because any particles are both particles and waves. This is where the difficulty lies: in order to create an exact duplicate of a particle, it is necessary to first determine its speed and its wave properties, in particular its momentum. But this is prohibited by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. According to this principle, it is impossible to simultaneously determine both the wave and corpuscular properties of a particle. And now (as already mentioned) this “quantum paradox” is being experimentally resolved in many research laboratories around the world. Experiments conducted by Danish scientists at the end of September 2001 proved the practical possibility of such teleportation. Experimenters have achieved a quantum connection between two clouds of cesium vapor cooled to almost absolute zero, located at a considerable distance from each other. The Danes managed to bind about a million cesium atoms, albeit for a very short time - for a fraction of a millisecond. The connection should lead to mutual influence of the vapor clouds. Two interesting articles - theoretical and experimental - were recently published in the journal Nature. They lead in different ways, but to one cherished goal - the creation of a quantum computer capable of solving problems that are fundamentally beyond the capabilities of modern computing machines. In a theoretical article, US National Institute of Standards and Technology professor E. Neill proposed a new hierarchical quantum computer architecture. According to the author, this architecture makes it possible to build a quantum computer based on components already implemented in the experiment. The main obstacle to the creation of quantum computers is the rapid destruction of delicate quantum information by external noise. A unit of quantum information, a qubit, capable of representing either a logical zero or one, or both, is realized as the quantum state of a photon, atom, ion or other microparticle. This is the basic block of a quantum computer. So far, scientific laboratories have been able to carry out quantum calculations for only a few qubits, which, of course, is not enough for practical calculations. And the more qubits in a quantum computer, the faster the information is destroyed. To get around this difficulty, Neill proposed organizing all the qubits of a quantum computer into a simple pyramidal structure of small blocks of qubits. Quantum data will be teleported from level to level and constantly checked for integrity. With such a hierarchical structure of the computer, calculations can be carried out even if the probability of an error of one qubit during the calculations is three percent. And this level has already been achieved in experiments on ion quantum computers. There are no problems with teleportation of quantum information. In the work of a large group of European scientists, coordinated from the University of Vienna, it was possible for the first time to experimentally implement the “unidirectional” quantum computing proposed by theorists. A unidirectional quantum computer is fundamentally different from a conventional quantum computer and is even forcing scientists to rethink the very concept of quantum computing. In a conventional quantum computer, the initial quantum states of the qubits are first prepared, then the computational algorithm is implemented through a sequence of time-reversible manipulations with the states of the qubits, and the result of the calculations is measured. It is the implementation of reversible manipulations, i.e. “entanglement” of the states of qubits, which are easily destroyed by any noise, causes the main difficulties. In a unidirectional quantum computer, a cluster of quantum particles is initially prepared in a highly “entangled” state, and the quantum computing algorithm is reduced to a sequence of irreversible measurements of the states of the qubits. The irreversibility of calculations, which are much less spoiled by noise, makes it necessary to call such a computer unidirectional. Theorists have shown that in a unidirectional way it is possible to implement a search algorithm, to which it is possible to reduce all other “overwhelming” algorithms, which can be solved on a regular computer only by directly enumerating all answer options. In the experiment, a unidirectional computer was built on four qubits, physically realized by the polarization states of four photons. The experimental setup is a complex combination of pulsed lasers, polarization filters, nonlinear optical crystals and photodetectors. Four photons were prepared in an “entangled” quantum state, and then a series of measurements of their polarization allowed the algorithm to be successfully executed. Of course, four qubits is not a lot, but at this stage the most important thing is experimental proof of concept. So far, the relatively young direction of unidirectional quantum computing is less developed than the traditional one. However, it is with it that a number of experts pin high hopes for the creation of quantum computers for practical calculations in the foreseeable future. It is difficult to say whether scientists will succeed in this, but judging by the fact that new solutions to old problems are constantly being proposed, their hopes are by no means groundless. Recently, there has been a merger of quantum computers with quantum communication (teleportation), naturally imaginary, since neither one nor the other exists yet. There is already talk about the quantum Internet.
