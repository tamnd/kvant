It's interesting that...

...Plato considered heat to be what remains of fire in heated bodies when the flame is extinguished; Bacon believed heat to be an "expanding motion"; according to Gassendi, heat and cold are different matter, and cold consists of “sharp” atoms in the shape of a tetrahedron; Galileo taught that cold is not a “positive quality”, but is simply the absence of heat. ...the experiment described in “Microexperiment” was conducted back in the 17th century by the English philosopher John Locke to prove the subjectivity of human sensations. But, in addition to its philosophical significance, experience forever closed the possibility of using our body as a thermometric instrument and using it to determine temperature. ...Richmann’s work “Reflections on the amount of heat that should be obtained by mixing liquids having certain degrees of heat,” which he reported in 1744 at the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, laid the foundation for accurate quantitative calculations in the field of heat engineering. Although Richman himself did not distinguish between the concepts of “temperature” and “heat,” he managed to derive a formula for determining the temperature of a mixture of homogeneous liquids and experimentally study the influence of temperature, shape and surface of bodies, as well as the speed of movement of the cooling medium on heat transfer. ...Richmann's experiments were repeated in 1772 by the Swedish physicist Johann Wilcke, who then introduced a unit of measurement for the amount of heat. It formed the basis of the modern definition of calorie, although this name arose only in 1852 in France. With the advent of the joule, the calorie began to be forced out of scientific use, but it is still in use, for example, when assessing the energy value of food. ...despite the long confusion in the definition of thermal concepts and the use of a mythical material entity - caloric, by the 19th century the foundation was laid for thermometry - a branch of physics that studies methods of measuring temperature, and calorimetry - the sum of methods for measuring various thermal effects. ... long-known thermal phenomena for a long time seemed to be an area completely separate from mechanical phenomena. It is therefore not surprising that scientists are trying to find a connection between heat and mechanics, treating, say, temperature as an analogue of pressure in a continuous medium. Just as mechanical equilibrium in such an environment is formed by equalizing pressures, thermal equilibrium requires equality of temperatures. ...the concept of thermal equilibrium, through which in physics they come to the concept of temperature, can be characterized as a dynamic equilibrium, when molecular-scale processes occur very intensively, but all macroscopic processes stop. ...the first example of the process of establishing thermal equilibrium, when heat is transferred from a more chaotic system to a more ordered one, was Brownian motion. Small impurity particles in a liquid form a system similar to an ideal gas of particles, although they do not interact with each other, but are affected by the molecules of the liquid in which they float. ...in a heated plasma there can be two temperatures in one place. Each of the systems that make up the plasma - electrons and ions - is itself in thermal equilibrium. The heat flow between the ions and electrons nevertheless exists, but it is very weak, and the temperatures equalize relatively slowly. ...an experimental test of the first law of thermodynamics was carried out more than once in special calorimeters, where the heat released in the life processes of various creatures - from mice to humans - was measured. As it turned out, it fully corresponded to the energy absorbed along with nutrients. This rejected the idea that organisms could be independent sources of any new form of energy, and ultimately led to the idea of ​​living organisms as open thermodynamic systems far from equilibrium.
