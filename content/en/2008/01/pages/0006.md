Shock waves and detonation

L. BELOPUKHOV

THE ARTICLE “EXPLOSION” SAID THAT any explosion is necessarily accompanied by the formation of shock waves in space and that a shock wave is an indispensable element of the detonation explosive process in an exploding gas (or dust-air) environment and in a condensed explosive.

A little about gas dynamics

Shock waves are a very interesting natural phenomenon. Their existence was predicted by many scientists, in particular the famous mathematician Riemann. His name is associated primarily with the development of the ideas of his teacher Gauss in the field of geometry. In 1854, the 28-year-old Riemann gave the general idea of ​​mathematical space, and then developed in detail one of the non-Euclidean geometries that has since bore his name. But Riemann made contributions to many areas of mathematics. Thus, in his work on the theory of differential equations, he discovered (on paper) the possibility of shock waves. It was on this occasion that the popular saying arose: “a phenomenon arose at the tip of a theorist’s pen.” The differential equations that Riemann studied represent the mathematical apparatus of a science called gas dynamics. It studies, as the name suggests, the movement of air masses, in particular storms and hurricanes, the flow of gas through pipes, the movement of gas in turbines and rocket engine nozzles, and much more. The basic gas-dynamic equation is Newton's law applied to an element of a gaseous medium (in this case it is called Euler's equation). But since a gas element changes its density and often its temperature when moving, it is necessary to use energy (thermodynamic) equations in addition to power ones. One of the simple consequences of solving the system of gas dynamics equations is the wave equation, in which the wave speed for an ideal gas depends only on temperature. These are sound waves, i.e. longitudinal vibrations of the gas element. The speed of sound is v =

where T is the absolute temperature, M is the molar mass, R is the universal gas constant, $\gamma$ is the adiabatic index, equal to the ratio of heat capacities at constant pressure and constant temperature. For air at temperatures less than 1000 K, the adiabatic index is 1.4. The speed of sound is v = 20 T (m s), which gives 342 m/s for standard conditions. If we take into account the non-ideal nature of the gas, the possibility of high temperatures at which the adiabatic exponent is not constant and is expressed by a complex function of state parameters, then the system of gas-thermodynamic equations becomes quite complex. Since gas dynamics is not interested in individual atoms or molecules, but considers the gas element, which in theory can be an infinitesimal quantity, then in the differential equations of gas dynamics and in their solutions all state parameters are continuous functions of coordinates and time. But in the 1860s, Riemann showed that there was a special solution to the system of gas-thermodynamic equations. Not a general solution, the methods for obtaining which he developed, and not particular solutions that follow from the general solution using initial and boundary conditions, but a special solution that cannot be obtained by standard mathematical methods. This special solution represents a discontinuity in the functions of pressure, density and velocity of the medium, i.e. an instantaneous, widthless jump in parameters. This jump is called a shock wave. The word “wave” does not accurately convey the nature of the phenomenon, since in the usual understanding a wave is characterized by periodicity, frequency, and a jump is a one-time sharp change. It would be more correct to call the phenomenon a shock pulse.

Emergence of a shock wave

The formation of a shock wave is easiest to imagine using the example of the plane movement of a gas in a pipe into which an accelerating piston is moving. When the piston begins to move and begins to move and compress the adjacent layer of gas, the news of this event (an increase in pressure and density) spreads through the gas at the speed of sound.
