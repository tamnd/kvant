In the design of a nuclear bomb, the tasks are opposite. It is necessary to ensure the fastest and most complete conversion of uranium or plutonium into fission products. As is known, in a small volume of an isotope, the fission reaction does not become an avalanche, because most of the neutrons manage to fly out of this volume, failing to produce the next fission. Only starting from a certain “critical” size can a chain reaction take place (they often talk not about the critical size, but about the critical mass, which, however, is the same thing from a physical point of view). Therefore, the bomb must contain several separate, subcritical blocks of nuclear “fuel”, which at some point must be combined. The speed and synchronicity of their connection determines the complete use of nuclear matter and, consequently, the energy of the explosion. The question may arise: why in nature, where the sizes of rock blocks containing uranium reach large values, does the fission reaction not develop? The fact is that there is very little uranium-235 isotope in the rock, and the spontaneous fission reaction of uranium (there is such a process) occurs very rarely. And all the resulting neutrons are absorbed by the surrounding nuclei of the uranium-238 isotope, giving birth to various radioactive isotopes and creating a general increased background radiation in the vicinity of uranium deposits. In the design of nuclear bombs, in addition to devices for synchronously connecting fuel blocks, many other things are used, in particular, neutron reflectors and special sources of neutron radiation. Creating a special test site also turned out to be a difficult task. Suffice it to say that the test site consists of many kilometers of lines with instrument systems for a wide variety of purposes (measurement of shock wave parameters, fluxes of radioactive particles and radiation, thermal and light radiation), engineering structures for military and civil purposes, military equipment, experimental animals, etc. Test sites are institutes where measurement results are processed and emerging scientific problems are analyzed. This is, finally, a huge military economy, served by several divisions. The Semipalatinsk test site was created in record time in 1948–49 according to the project of G.L. Shnirman under the leadership of M.A. Sadovsky, future academician, director of the Institute of Earth Physics of the USSR Academy of Sciences.

Thermonuclear weapons

The thermonuclear reaction is the opposite of the fission reaction of heavy nuclei in terms of the role of interaction between particles. This is a synthesis reaction, i.e. combining isotopes of hydrogen into a heavier helium nucleus. In this case, the electrical repulsive forces are positive

charged nuclei interfere with the fusion process, and the kinetic energy of the reaction products is obtained as a result of the “triggering” of nuclear attractive forces. But these forces turn on only when the particles approach a distance of the order of $10^{-} 15$ m. And in order to overcome electrical repulsion, the connecting nuclei need to have a speed of the order of tens of thousands of kilometers per second, which corresponds to a temperature of tens and hundreds of millions of degrees. This is why the fusion reaction is called a thermonuclear reaction. You can impart such speed to particles in different ways. For example, you can focus hundreds of streams of fast electrons in one place so that they transfer their kinetic energy to the nuclei. You can use powerful laser radiation. One of the promising ways is to accelerate nuclei with a powerful electric field (spark discharge, lightning). But all these ways are not suitable for nuclear weapons, which must be quite portable. The only way is to place a nuclear bomb inside a container containing light elements, say the hydrogen isotopes deuterium and tritium (or lithium deuteride). The kinetic energies (or temperatures) necessary to begin the thermonuclear fusion process are provided by the fireball of a nuclear explosion. The fusion process that has begun will support itself, since the products of the fusion reaction will have greater kinetic energy than the original nuclei. In reality, however, everything turned out to be not so simple. For powerful thermonuclear bombs, it was necessary to come up with schemes for the additional “production” of neutrons from fission reactions of uranium isotopes (except for the primary “ignition” bomb) and the compression of hydrogen “raw materials” by radiation from a secondary nuclear uranium explosion. This principle is called radiation implosion. At the same time, the nuclear fuel supply was fully used. Successful tests of the new design in 1955 at the Semipalatinsk test site and in 1961 at the Novaya Zemlya test site opened the era of the arms race and at the same time raised the question of ending this race. The resolution of this contradiction may have been one of the reasons for the breakdown of our state that occurred in 1991. In 1996, an international agreement was finally reached to stop all nuclear weapons testing, as well as the use of nuclear explosions for peaceful purposes. The format of this article does not allow us to consider in detail the issue of a stationary, controlled thermonuclear fusion process of light nuclei, or thermonuclear power plants. Fifty years ago, optimists thought that this problem would be solved in 10–20 years. Modern optimists believe that it will be solved in the 21st century. And modern pessimists believe that the laws of nature will never allow this to be done. But what will humanity have left to hope for when coal, oil and gas run out and all uranium deposits are exhausted? And pessimists expect this to happen already in the current millennium.
