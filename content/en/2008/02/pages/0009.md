Physics of a nuclear explosion

L. BELOPUKHOV emphasized the fact that the manifestations of an explosion are very similar for a variety of sources of the explosive process. So is it worth specially considering such a specific source of explosion as nuclear transformations? I think this is worth doing for the following reasons. Firstly, the black days of August 1945, when more than 100,000 Japanese died from nuclear weapons used for the first time by the Americans, will forever remain inscribed in the history of mankind. Secondly, on October 30, 1961, at 11:33 am Moscow time, an analogue of the biblical apocalyptic “Armageddon” broke out on the Novaya Zemlya archipelago - the most terrible illustration of the power of modern science and technology. In a split second, energy was released equal to that which the Earth received from the Sun during this time ($( 10^{18}$ J). It became clear to politicians around the world that the third world war would mean the death of all humanity. However, the likelihood of using nuclear weapons is, alas, not zero. The words “weapon-grade plutonium” do not disappear from political news. Thirdly, many outstanding scientists participated in the Soviet atomic project, including Academician Isaac Konstantinovich Kikoin, whose centenary is celebrated this year. Their participation was not forced by the Soviet system. It was determined by the understanding of the need for this work - after all, there, overseas, it began before us. And the hearts of many scientists were torn between duty and a feeling of horror from “let the genie out of the bottle” (V. Vysotsky, “March of Physicists”). And fourth, the deep physics of nuclear energy release is the same for explosive and peaceful uses. And in the third millennium, humanity cannot avoid an almost complete transition to nuclear energy.
