But even then, communicating with Isaac Konstantinovich’s employees, I eventually learned that his working day exceeded 12 hours. In the morning at 8.30 he came from home and went to his office to familiarize himself with urgent matters and sign urgent papers. At 9–10 o’clock I took the chief engineer with me and toured the experimental laboratories and production departments. Here, directly on the spot, issues that arose were discussed and resolved, and instructions were given. By 11 o’clock he came to the isotope separation building to the “turntables” (centrifuges) and sat down there in the hall. Any employee could approach him with their questions. Then I.K. he returned to his office and continued to do business, communicate with employees who had been called or came to him on their own initiative. Sometimes I went to different authorities. The working day ended at 9–10 pm. Our evening conversations, despite the fact that Secretary I.K. postponed not particularly urgent telephone calls to the next day; they were constantly interrupted by urgent calls, often long-distance. Sometimes there was a persistent signal from a white telephone, which did not have a dial, but only a golden coat of arms - “Kremlin”. I left the office and waited for the conversation to end. When we stayed up until very late, I.K., leaving the entrance, often pointed to me at the windows that were lit in the building and said: “There are guys more diligent than you and me.” Rare days off were allocated for his work on textbooks. Before every New Year, I.K. he made a round of literally all the employees of his department (hundreds of people!), congratulated them, and inquired about their health and life. * * * Isaac Konstantinovich himself was a witty person and knew how to appreciate the jokes of others. Perhaps one of the most famous funny stories concerned the gift that Anatoly Petrovich Alexandrov gave him. The director of the Kurchatov Institute presented his deputy with a wonderful set - a large glass samovar and a glass teapot standing on it. A clear liquid was poured into the samovar, which soon turned out to be vodka, and in the kettle there was a brown drink - cognac. The gift caused laughter and some amazement, since it was known that I.K. absolute teetotaler. But soon everything was explained. After the samovar and kettle were placed in Kikoin’s rest room, Anatoly Petrovich began to occasionally complain to his employees that he was tired and he needed to go to I.K. to drink his wonderful tea. The academicians retired, and one drank real tea, and the second sipped his own, a gift. Aleksandrov sometimes brought the most important (or especially pleasant to him) guests, including members of the Politburo, to “tea” with Kikoin. I remember I.K.’s joke addressed to me personally. I, using my observation post on the balcony, saw I.K.’s car approaching. and ran to the first floor to meet him back in the lobby. The academician greeted me and said to me very menacingly: “What are you doing, sir, running around here like a clerk? If you please, walk like a merchant of the first guild! The position obliges." * * * The physics and mathematics school at the Kurchatov Institute worked successfully, became known as SHEN - School of Natural Sciences - and in the fall of 1984 it was awarded Certificates of Honor from the Ministry of Education and the Komsomol Central Committee. The diplomas were presented in a solemn ceremony in the large hall of the House of Culture of the Kurchatov Institute. Representatives of the Ministry and Komsomol leaders spoke, and two academicians, Spartak Timofeevich Belyaev and Isaac Konstantinovich Kikoin, accepted awards from the Directorate of the Institute. S.T. Belyaev spoke first, and his speech strongly contrasted with the previous official words of officials. He said that in the learning process both sides learn - both the student and the teacher. They acquire not only technical knowledge, but also human knowledge. And then I.K. came out to the ramp. I haven’t seen him for a long time; I was on a business trip to the Rivne NPP for almost a year. The academician was very old and looked unhealthy. The traditional black suit seemed too big for him. “I want to tell you what I was thinking when I accepted the award,” Kikoin said. “I thought that here I am, an academician, the leader of a large team, deputy director of the Kurchatov Institute, I have many awards, including two hero stars and many other high orders, I am a laureate of the Lenin and State Prizes...” “Why is he saying this,” I thought. “Somehow it’s not modest.” But the very next few words made me forget everything, grab the book lying next to me and, in its margins, start to write down at random what I heard. “And yet, without any doubt, I would exchange all this - position, degrees, titles - for your youth, for your 15, even 17 years. And I need to change because during my long life I didn’t have time to enjoy my beloved physics, I didn’t have enough time, I see clearly now - I didn’t have enough. Although there was not a single day in my life, not a day off, not a holiday, not a vacation, when I didn’t do it. I often have dreams about physics. And still there was not enough time. You will find out for yourself how it happens when you live life. So don't waste time now. It still won’t be enough, but at least it won’t be so offensive. You know, a scientist is not a job title or a place of work. So he entered the laboratory - and began to think about science, and became a scientist. This is wrong. Being a scientist is a constant and often painful, and sometimes wonderful state. That’s what I was thinking about when they handed me this award.” More I.K. I haven't seen it.
