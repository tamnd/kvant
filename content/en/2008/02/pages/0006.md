On the other hand, we had no intention of stopping teaching altogether. Moreover, the idea was already being formed to create an evening physics and mathematics school for gifted children at the Kurchatov Institute and teach them there using our own methods. Therefore, the second conclusion is to invite Isaac Konstantinovich to use our school audience to test his ideas, which will be embedded in the new physics course. I communicated these two conclusions to the academician by phone and received his approval for further cooperation. Since then, visits to the office of I.K. have become quite frequent. At first we all appeared there together. However, various everyday reasons led to the fact that I increasingly began to come alone, especially after the magazine «Kvant» was founded and I became responsible for the “Laboratory «Kvant»” section. * * * Unfortunately, over the past decades, my memories have begun to resemble an old movie. Sometimes the film breaks, erased frames often flash, and the sound disappears. But suddenly color appears at some point, scenes are filled with sound and meaning. I want to talk about them. The scene of action is almost always the same - the office of Isaac Konstantinovich. Tall, very thin, in his usual black suit, he sits in a chair at a large desk and holds an extinguished pipe in his hands. There are bookcases behind the chair and along the other walls of the office. Books, books, books. In Russian, German, English. Books on physics, engineering, reference books, dictionaries... It’s hot in the huge office, I’m constantly wiping myself with a handkerchief, and I.K. is apparently freezing. There are rumors that he still hasn't completely overcome tuberculosis and that he has a bad stomach. Sometimes I ask questions that are not directly related to the topic of our meeting. The academician answers and begins to talk about something. Despite his quiet voice, he is a wonderful storyteller. * * * “Where did I study? When we moved to Pskov, and this was in 21, I moved to a school that was located a few houses away from ours. Labor school No. 1, the former First Gymnasium, famous in Pskov. What is it famous for? Firstly, by age - it opened under Alexander I. Secondly, by an excellent teaching staff. Thirdly, of course, by our graduates.” The academician lists several truly famous names. I remembered the writers Yu. Tynyanov and V. Kaverin, the microbiologist L. Zilbert and also the violinist G. Barinova. “During these years, the school, like the whole country, was very poor. There were not enough teacher salaries. There was no head of the physics department, there was no librarian. And after a while I was offered, on a voluntary basis, to put the library in order (and there were tens of thousands of volumes) and try to set up at least some demonstrations in physics. As they say, “there would be no happiness, but misfortune would help.” I spent all my free time either sorting and

I voraciously read books in the library, mainly on physics and mathematics, or came up with demonstrations on physics and often assembled the necessary instruments myself. It was then that I firmly decided that I would become a physicist.” I note that these hobbies obviously did not affect I.K.’s overall academic performance, since he graduated from school at the age of 15, having “skipped” the grade twice. Later I learned that Isaac Konstantinovich went to celebrate the 180th anniversary of the first gymnasium and spoke there to students and teachers. * * * As I communicated with I.K. I became more and more convinced of his excellent memory. Usually at the end of our meetings he was interested in my work affairs and news in the field of neutrino physics. And so, while talking about the famous experiments of the group of American professor F. Reines, I gave the values ​​of the cross section for the interaction of reactor antineutrinos with protons and deuterons. A few months later, when I.K. asked how things were going with my dissertation, I began to talk about the expected counting rate of the designed detector, and he quickly checked my calculations with a pencil on some piece of paper lying on the table (thank God, the results coincided). Only a few minutes later it dawned on me that I.K. knows the neutrino cross sections from somewhere. I asked him about this and received a discouraging answer: “But you yourself named them to me then and then.” This is what I.K. himself said: “I can’t complain about my memory. For example, when I was tutoring, I solved all the problems from the math problem book. And during the entrance exam to the Leningrad Polytechnic Institute, I didn’t even read the statement of the problem, but looked at the number that the teacher indicated and immediately said the answer. He checked and was very surprised every time. And I explained that the problem was easy and could be solved orally. And I never wrote down lectures, I immediately memorized them. Many times in my life, a good memory literally saved me. And sometimes she was a great help to other people.” And then came one of I.K.’s interesting stories, which I will try to reproduce. “This was in the early fifties. Then the fight against sycophancy towards foreign science was in full swing. Perhaps there were rational grains in this campaign, but they very soon found themselves literally buried under a pile of ignorant articles, speeches and simply denunciations. With their help, people who could not or did not want to engage in real science tried to clear their way to the top. Of course, you know for yourself how this affected our biology. It was literally thrown back decades. They also attacked physics, the theory of relativity, and quantum mechanics. They declared that these were completely false, bourgeois sciences. It’s good that they haven’t been called the same name as cybernetics. But here the fighters against sycophancy had to retreat quite quickly.
