propagation of light

First, as promised in the previous issue of Kaleidoscope, we will continue our discussion of wave phenomena. Secondly, following Bragg, we will extend the concept of “light” to all kinds of electromagnetic waves, although, of course, we will leave the leading role for visible light. Thirdly, similar to last time, we will focus primarily on the processes of scattering and absorption. It turns out that even with such a limitation, the scope of our section is not able to accommodate even a small fraction of the whole variety of issues related to all-wave optics. Indeed, we come into contact with them in everyday life and on vacation, in medicine and art, in science and technology. Scattering and being absorbed, light waves penetrate everything around us - whether we are selecting paints for repairs or paintings, whether we are trying to explain atmospheric or astronomical phenomena, whether we are choosing dishes for cooking, whether we are looking for ways to study matter, or solving problems of transmitting information. Nevertheless, the general patterns of wave phenomena discovered by scientists make it possible to successfully cope with both everyday everyday difficulties and fundamental scientific obstacles. And as a reward for overcoming them, optics sometimes gives such aesthetic pleasure and such emotional uplift that even outstanding rational minds are unable to hide them. Well, let's try to turn the absent-minded into those who will be absorbed in this topic.

It is curious that... ...to explain the blueness of the sky, many hypotheses were put forward hundreds of years ago. For example, it was interpreted as a mixture of “light and darkness” in certain proportions; it was assumed that air particles were colored blue or that they luminesced under the influence of sunlight. The great poet Goethe, who showed interest in the theory of the origin of colors, criticized Newton for not giving an answer to this question. ... Tyndall, having discovered the absorption of infrared rays by carbon dioxide, conducted an experiment in which he passed these rays through a tube filled with exhaled air. The absorption observed in this case also became convincing optical evidence of the now well-known fact that a person exhales carbon dioxide. ...with the light hand of Rayleigh, it was long believed that the scattering of solar rays occurs on gas molecules, and only in 1940 academician L.I. Mandelstam overcame this misconception, showing that in reality the scatterers are inhomogeneities in air density. ...even soot and platinum black - the blackest known paints - do not completely absorb all the radiation incident on them, scattering about 1-2% of it. The best white paint scatters 91% of the light falling on it, while freshly fallen snow scatters only 80%. ...during volcanic eruptions and large forest fires, the atmosphere is saturated with aerosols consisting of particles of such sizes that light from the long-wave part of the spectrum is scattered more strongly than short-wave light. When observed through such an aerosol, the Moon and Sun become blue, which was recorded by Russian geologist V.A. Obruchev after the catastrophic eruption of the Krakatoa volcano. ...one of the most important advantages of fiber-optic communications is that the signal attenuation is so insignificant that the distance between repeaters can reach 100-200 kilometers - many times greater than in conventional wire lines. Advances in fiber manufacturing technology have made it possible to come close to the theoretical limit of losses caused not by defects in the substance, but by Rayleigh scattering and absorption of light by the atoms that make up the fiber. ...several Nobel Prizes in physics at the beginning of the 20th century were awarded for the development of a method of X-ray structural analysis, thanks to which, for example, it was possible to decipher the structure of complex crystals. Today, by studying the X-ray radiation scattered and absorbed by samples, it is possible to establish in detail the chemical composition of a substance or observe dynamic changes in the structure of proteins. ...attempts to use the connection between optical and acoustic phenomena were made back in the 19th century and led to the fact that in 1880, independently in three different laboratories - White, Tyndall and Roentgen - the photoacoustic effect was discovered, which consisted in converting a modulated light flux into sound. ...thanks to frequency-tunable lasers, it has become possible to convert weak infrared signals into the visible range. This has found application in observing astronomical objects, since the sensitivity of the photodetectors used there in the optical range is several orders of magnitude higher than in the infrared range. ...fine cosmic dust not only weakens the light of the stars reaching us, but also changes their color. Blue rays are absorbed most strongly by dust, resulting in reddening of light passing through the interstellar medium.
