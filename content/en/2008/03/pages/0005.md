In 1947, an experimental cascade of diffusion machines was launched in Laboratory No. 2 (as the Institute of Atomic Energy was originally called) in the Kikoinsky department. It was shown that, in principle, the problem of enriching uranium to the required content $изотопа^{235} U$ can be solved. In 1949, the first gas diffusion plant (Combine No. 813) started operating in the Urals. After a series of failures, the plant began to produce weapons-grade uranium, and the second test of atomic weapons in the USSR in 1951 was carried out with a product filled with this product. After gaseous diffusion technology was fully mastered and enterprises were built that provided the required amount of weapons-grade uranium, I.K. Kikoin returned to research on centrifuge technology. Converting the separation industry to centrifuges could greatly reduce the colossal energy consumption required for gaseous diffusion. Probably, there was also a natural dissatisfaction of the real scientist, who, in essence, followed the path already taken by the Americans. And the theory indicated that centrifuge technology was more suitable for separating uranium isotopes. And as for the enormous technical difficulties of its implementation, gaseous diffusion was not easy. Work on the creation of gas centrifuges began at the end of 1946 by a group of employees of Institute “A” in Sukhumi. The group was led by German physicist Max Steenbeck. He was found in the summer of 1945 in a prisoner of war camp in Poland and brought to the USSR. At first, Steenbeck proposed making a centrifuge with a 10-meter rotor, the linear rotation speed at the periphery should have been 250 m/s. Six years later, in 1952, Steenbeck worked with a centrifuge with a 6-meter rotor with a diameter of 58 mm. The rotor was flexible, consisting of ten short pipes with nine bellows connections. The rotation speed was 1200–1400 rpm, which corresponds to a linear speed at the periphery of the rotor of 220–240 m/s. It was supposed to transfer matter from centrifuge to centrifuge using condensation/evaporation (the so-called solid-phase transfer method), which created almost insurmountable difficulties when connecting centrifuges in a cascade, and, as is known, Steenbeck never created a cascade. True, in experiments with uranium hexafluoride in a Steenbeck centrifuge, the separation effect was 50% of the theoretical one. But the main thing that Steenbeck came up with was the design of the centrifuge rotor support unit. The rotor rotated, supported by a thin steel needle, and the upper end of the rotor was held by a permanent magnet. In 1951, an employee of I.K. Kikoin, a talented engineer E.M. Kamenev, put forward the idea of ​​a centrifuge with a short, rigid, thin-walled rotor and a lower needle support. This made it possible to significantly increase the rotor speed. The decisive role was played by I.K. Kikoin’s proposal to introduce stationary sampling tubes at the ends of the rotor into the peripheral compacted gas layer. High gas dynamic

the pressure in this layer made it possible to create the necessary flows of light and heavy fractions with their transfer in a cascade from centrifuge to centrifuge through the gas phase. At the same time, the interaction of the rotating gas with the stationary sampling tubes ensured the occurrence of countercurrent circulation inside the rotor at the required speeds. In April 1953, a patent was issued for such a centrifuge, and in July of the same year, another patent was received - for a hydrodynamic method (using fixed tubes) of transferring gas from centrifuge to centrifuge. It is clear that both patents were closed patents. Then things went like this. In 1953, the Ministry of Medium Machinery made a decision to develop centrifuge technologies on an industrial scale. In 1954, the first samples of machines were created, and on October 10, 1955, the Council of Ministers decided to build an experimental centrifuge plant in the Urals (at Plant No. 183). This plant was built and installed in 1956–57, with a total of 2,432 centrifuges installed. The plant was launched on November 2–4, 1957. It should be noted that this was the first successful experience in the industrial use of centrifuge technology in world practice. In general, the experience of operating the pilot centrifuge plant turned out to be very successful. On May 5, 1958, at the Scientific and Technical Council of the Ministry of Medium Machine Building, under the chairmanship of I.V. Kurchatov, a decision was made to transition to the industrial use of the centrifugal method of separating uranium isotopes, and on August 22, 1960, by order of the Ministry of Medium Machine Building, a design assignment was approved for the construction of the world's first industrial gas centrifuge plant. And on November 4, 1962, the first stage of the plant was launched (the plant as a whole was launched in several stages in 1962–64). The production itself is amazing. The plant's workshop is almost a kilometer long and contains 700,000 centrifuges connected in cascades. They work almost silently. There are no people in the workshop.
