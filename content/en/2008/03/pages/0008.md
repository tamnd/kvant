Nanotechnology: when size matters

K. BOGDANOV

You can say the word "bird" in many different languages, but by doing so you still won't learn anything new about birds. Isn't it better to first just look at any bird and wonder what it is!.. There is a big difference between knowing the name of something and knowing how it works. R. Feynman

FICTION IS BECOMING REAL BEFORE OUR EYES - people have learned to move individual atoms and put them together, like cubes, into devices and mechanisms of unusually small sizes and therefore invisible to the ordinary eye. A whole branch of science has emerged - NANOTECHNOLOGY, which has absorbed the latest achievements of physics, chemistry and biology. Nanotechnologists work with negligibly small objects whose dimensions are measured in nanometers. Nanotechnology is not just a quantitative, but a qualitative leap from working with matter to manipulating individual atoms. Translated from Greek, the word “nano” means dwarf. One nanometer (nm) is one billionth of a meter $( 10^{-} 9$ m). A nanometer is the same number of times less than one meter as the thickness of a finger is less than the diameter of the Earth. The sizes of objects that nanotechnologists deal with range from 0.1 to 100 nm. Most atoms have a diameter of 0.1 to 0.2 nm, the thickness of DNA strands is about 2 nm, the diameter of red blood cells is 7000 nm, and the thickness of a human hair is 80,000 nm. Scientists argue that in the coming 21st century, the core of the new technical revolution will be materials, medicines, devices, communications and delivery devices made using nanotechnology.

Where it all began

The idea that it might be possible to assemble devices and work with objects that are nanoscale was first proposed by Nobel laureate Richard Feynman in a 1959 speech at the California Institute of Technology. The lecture was called “There's Plenty of Space Down There!” The word “below” in the title of the lecture meant in “a world of very small dimensions.” Feynman then said that someday, perhaps in the year 2000, people will be surprised that before 1960 no one took research into the nanoworld seriously. According to Feynman, a person lived for a very long time, not noticing that a whole world of objects lived next to him, which he was not able to discern. Well, if we don’t see these objects, then we can’t work with them. However, our cells - the building blocks of which our body is composed - have perfectly learned to work with nano-objects. Throughout its life, a cell assembles molecules of complex substances from various atoms. Having collected these molecules, the cell places them in different parts - some end up in the nucleus, others in the cytoplasm, and others in the membrane. Imagine the possibilities that will open up for humanity if it masters the same nanotechnology that every human cell already possesses. Feynman describes the implications of the nanotechnology revolution for computers: “If, for example, the diameter of the connecting wires were from 10 to 100 atoms, then the size of any circuit would not exceed several thousand angstroms. Everyone who is associated with computer technology knows about the opportunities that its development and complication promises. If the number of elements used increases by millions of times, the capabilities of computers will expand significantly. They will learn to reason, analyze experience and calculate their own actions, find new computational methods, etc. An increase in the number of elements will lead to important qualitative changes in the characteristics of the computer.” Having called scientists to the nanoworld, Feynman immediately warns about the obstacles that await them there, using the example of manufacturing a microcar only 1 mm long. Since the parts of an ordinary car are made with an accuracy of $10^{-} 5$ m, the parts of a microcar should be made with an accuracy of 4000 times higher, i.e. $2,5_{Ч} 10^{-} 9$ m. Thus, the dimensions of the microcar parts must correspond to the calculated ones with an accuracy of $\pm 10$ layers of atoms. The nanoworld is not only full of obstacles and problems. Good news awaits us in the nanoworld - all the parts of the nanoworld turn out to be very durable. This happens due to the fact that the mass of nanoobjects decreases in proportion to the third power of their size, and their cross-sectional area - in proportion to the second power.
