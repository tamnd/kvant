Nanowires do not exist in nature. In laboratories, nanowires are most often produced by epitaxy, i.e. oriented growth of one single crystal on the surface of another, when crystallization of the substance occurs in only one direction. For example, a silicon nanowire can be grown as shown in figure 2 on the left. Nanocha-

A gold particle is placed in an atmosphere of silane gas $( SiH_{4} ),$ and this nanoparticle becomes a catalyst for the reaction of silane decomposition into hydrogen and liquid silicon. Liquid silicon rolls off the gold nanoparticle and crystallizes underneath it. If the silane concentration around the nanoparticle is maintained unchanged, then the epitaxy process continues, and all new layers of liquid silicon crystallize on its already solidified layers. As a result, the silicon nanowire grows, lifting the gold nanoparticle higher and higher. In this case, obviously, the size of the nanoparticle determines the diameter of the nanowire. Moreover, the more of these dislocations there are and the faster they can move along the sample, the more microcracks there will be. When the tensile stress reaches the tensile strength, adjacent microcracks, connecting with each other, reach a critical size, and the sample fails. A nanowire is a single crystal whose crystal lattice has virtually no defects (dislocations). Moreover, the surface area to volume ratio for nanowires is much larger than for samples of conventional sizes. Therefore, the surface of nanowires easily “absorbs” outward dislocations without the formation of microcracks. In addition, the surface of the nanowire, which has an extremely small radius of curvature (about 10 nm), is highly compressed and therefore prevents the dislocation from moving outward, i.e. microcrack formation. Why do nanoparticles melt at low temperatures?

As particle sizes decrease, not only mechanical properties change, but also thermodynamic characteristics. Thus, the melting temperature becomes much lower than that of regular-sized samples. figure 5 shows the experimental curve of the decrease in the melting temperature of aluminum nanoparticles with decreasing particle size. In this case, the melting temperature of a 4 nm particle decreases by $140 ^\circ$ C compared to the melting temperature of a standard-sized aluminum sample.
