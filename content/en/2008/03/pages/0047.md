About Singing Wires, or Mysteries of the Spring

A. SERGEEV, N. ZHDANOVA, I. SERGACHEV, R. STRUNGIS, A. PYATAKOV

THE SPACE SURROUNDING US IS FILLED WITH A VERY DIFFERENT SOUND. We listen to some of them with interest and attention, for example, the voices of our interlocutors, news reports, and favorite music. We try to ignore other sounds, while many of them deserve attention and, moreover, can even become the subject of scientific research. All of you have probably stood next to the railway tracks and heard the hum of a locomotive, the rumble of a freight train, or the grinding of the wheels of a slowing train. These rough, loud sounds make up the familiar cacophony of the railway and arouse discomfort rather than the curiosity of the researcher. At the same time, near the tracks you can hear sounds that can hardly be described as anything other than amazing. In the tense silence preceding the arrival of the next train, listen to the sounds from the wires along which the current collectors of the electric train slide. These sounds will have nothing in common with clicks or other familiar knocking sounds. You will hear a ringing “chirp”, consisting of many sounds of single blows, each of which, if you set out to depict it in writing in letters, is best conveyed by the combination “tiu”. It is important to note that sounds of this kind occur not only when wires collide. They can be heard if someone hits the rail a few hundred meters away from you. Similar sounds are generated by the impact of light pebbles on thin ice in the fall, when ponds are just beginning to be covered with an ice film. Let's try to understand all these phenomena: when do they arise, what general patterns do they have? In all cases, the cause is a blow, the sound of which in the immediate vicinity bears little resemblance to the melodic sound of “tiu”. But as we move away from the place of impact, the sound becomes more and more musically “colored”: the further we go, the brighter and more distinct. Acoustics experts will tell us that the impact creates a bending wave (Fig.1) in the wire, rail, layer of ice, and the characteristic sound of “tiu” is explained by a wave phenomenon called dispersion.

The medium of wave propagation is said to have dispersion if the speed of the wave in this medium depends on its frequency. A well-known example of the dispersion of electromagnetic waves is the decomposition of white light into a spectrum by a prism. White light contains waves of different frequencies, i.e. light of different colors. Due to dispersion, waves corresponding to light of different colors have different speeds and are therefore refracted differently by the glass of the prism. This is how a rainbow appears on the screen - a spectrum of white light. From a physics point of view, the rainbow stripe on the screen represents a smooth change in the frequency of light with a change in coordinate. If people could “see” sounds, our sound “tiu” would look exactly the same: it also has a gradual decrease in the frequency of the sound over time. But is dispersion of sound waves possible in principle? When sound propagates in the air, depending on the distance to the source, only its volume can change, but not the nature of the sound. It’s easy to imagine what, for example, a piano concert would turn into if air had dispersion properties: high notes would have a higher propagation speed than low ones, and for listeners sitting in the middle of the stalls, the music would turn into an unrecognizable jumble of sounds. A contradiction arises: on the one hand, the sound “tiu” is very similar to the manifestation of dispersion, and on the other, everyday experience tells us that there is no dispersion of sound waves. The key to the solution lies in the fact that most of the objects around us are not unlimited homogeneous media, like air, but have a specific shape. All of these objects have at least one size that is much larger than the others and at least one that is much smaller than the bending wavelength. Such extended objects are waveguides, i.e. channels through which waves can travel. Acoustic waveguides differ from unconfined media by the presence of unusual phenomena and interesting patterns of elastic wave propagation. Dispersion is one such phenomenon. The impact excites many oscillations of different frequencies, and a group of waves begins to propagate along the waveguide. Dispersion occurs when high-frequency waves travel faster than low-frequency waves. This leads to the fact that at a great distance, low-frequency waves lag significantly behind high-frequency waves, and the sound of the impact is “stretched”, turning into the familiar “tiu” sound. This is a qualitative explanation of the phenomenon, but we wanted to understand it in more detail, obtaining quantitative relationships. It is easy to understand that conducting full-scale experiments (i.e., in natural conditions, not in a laboratory) is very difficult: trains constantly follow the rails, the wires (as a rule) are under high voltage, and the choice of thin ice means being tied to rare weather conditions.
