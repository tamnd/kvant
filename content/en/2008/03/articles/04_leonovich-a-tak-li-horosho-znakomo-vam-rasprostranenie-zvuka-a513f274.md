Many physicists involved in acoustics have paid attention to the similarity of some sound phenomena with optical ones. The opposite is also true: the study of light prompted us to look for analogies with the propagation of sound vibrations. We also encountered the same thing when we decided to dedicate the new issue of Kaleidoscope to wave processes - we discovered many points of intersection between acoustics and optics. We had to divide the topic in half, and if today we focus on sound, then next time we will definitely turn to electromagnetic waves, primarily light. Let us note right away that here and there, in all the diversity of wave phenomena, we will be primarily interested in situations associated with scattering and absorption. It cannot be said that they are given enough attention in the course of elementary physics, while in life we ​​encounter them every day. If we talk about sound waves, they are one of the most important sources of information about the world around us. Spoken speech, music, sounds of wildlife, telephone messaging, audio technology - we live in an acoustically rich atmosphere. The density of harmful sounds has also increased immeasurably—noises that are annoying and sometimes downright dangerous to health. How can we convey some of them to us with as little loss and distortion as possible, and others (if we cannot get rid of them) at least weaken them? How can we put inaudible sounds at our service? In our search for answers, let's follow Feynman's advice and start with relatively simple acoustic examples.

It is curious that... ...diagnostic methods that have long been known in medicine - tapping and listening - have found application in acoustic flaw detection, which makes it possible to determine the presence of inhomogeneities in it by the scattering and absorption of the sound signal sent into the medium under study. ...the solution to the “gallery of whispers” effect described in problem 16 was found in 1904 by the famous Lord Rayleigh during his observations and experiments in St. Paul's Cathedral in London. Almost a hundred years later, this type of wave became the subject of research and application in optics, for example, for frequency stabilization of lasers or frequency conversion of a light beam. ...infrasound waves are very weakly attenuated in the atmosphere, ocean and earth's crust. Thus, a powerful low-frequency disturbance caused by the eruption of the Indonesian volcano Krakatoa in 1883 circled the globe twice. ...with distance from the epicenter of a nuclear explosion, the shock wave turns into an acoustic wave, and short waves decay faster than long waves, and at large distances only low-frequency oscillations persist. The recording of such infrasonic waves was proposed in the mid-50s of the last century by academician I.K. Kikoin as a method for detecting nuclear explosions, which was subsequently successfully used to record tests conducted by the United States in the Pacific Ocean. ... Bell's invention of the telephone was preceded by his thorough study of acoustics and many years of work at the Boston school for the deaf and dumb, who were also intended to use sound amplifiers and devices designed by him for teaching speech understanding. ...the peculiarity of freshly fallen snow to absorb mainly high frequencies was noticed by the English physicist Tyndall, who combined acoustic and optical studies. And Rayleigh, who was looking for something common in all oscillatory processes, was able to explain the increase in the tone of the echo in a pine forest by better scattering and reflection of short sound waves by thin needles than long ones, as in the scattering of light in the atmosphere. ...in one of the rooms of the conservatory in the Australian city of Adelaide, it was impossible to listen to the piano being played - the hall resonated so piercingly and sharply. They found a way out of this situation by hanging from the ceiling several half-meter-wide strips of twill - cotton fabric with a special surface finish that allows it to absorb sound well. ...sound vibrations with a frequency of 200–400 hertz, at sufficiently high levels of intensity, can very strongly mask almost all higher frequencies. For example, the melodies of the organ and double bass are clearly audible in the orchestra, although their relative loudness does not exceed such high-pitched instruments as the violin and cello. ...if you “sound” with sirens pipelines for transporting bulk cargo - flour, coal dust, crushed ore, then their throughput increases. Such devices are used in ports to unload powdered materials from the holds of cargo ships. Their only drawback is their piercing howl. ...sound frequency vibrations can be used to dry a variety of materials at relatively low temperatures, including due to their local heating when absorbing acoustic waves. ...ultrasound is capable of “mixing” mercury or oil with water, grinding solid substances into powder in the manufacture of medicines, drilling a square hole in metal, cutting and drilling glass and quartz, joining “unsolderable” materials and much more amazing things, but, alas, it is impossible to create an ultrasonic weapon. The peculiarities of the propagation and absorption of ultrasound lead to such strong attenuation that even over a distance of only a few tens of meters it transmits energy sufficient to operate only ... a light bulb from a flashlight.
