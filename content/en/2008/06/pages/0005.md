About how we see and about light dimensions

Light is perceived by the retina, which covers the fundus of the eye, and it is on it that the lens-lens focuses the image of surrounding objects (Fig.1). The retina contains about 125 million nerve photoreceptors, which because of their elongation are called

rods, and approximately 6–7 million cones, named for their conical shape. The rods are concentrated mainly at the edges of the retina, and the cones are located in a small central zone. Cones are hundreds of times less sensitive than rods, but they “distinguish” colors, while rods only respond to changes in the brightness of light. Quotes have to be used because the visual image is formed in the occipital regions of the brain, and the eye is just a photodetector that receives external information, transforms it and transmits it through nerve fibers to the brain. In other words, we “look” with our eyes, but “see” with the back of our heads. Eye automation is fine-tuned by Nature so that during the day only cones are active, and at dusk only rods are active. Therefore, the statement “all cats are gray at night...” has a completely scientific basis. Color perception is a unique human ability; apparently, it is accessible exclusively to a highly organized brain. All animals see, many more sharply than us, some in almost complete darkness, but almost all of them are in an eternal gray twilight (whether a dog distinguishes cheese from meat by color is still a subject of debate among scientists). And for us, perhaps, it is simply impossible to imagine life without colors; at the subconscious level, the psyche correlates with the color environment: the lyrical mood is “colored” in blue and, on the contrary, blue immerses us in the lyrics; drama – “purple”, orange causes major

An ordinary person distinguishes up to two hundred color shades, artists - up to several thousand (so they claim, but perhaps something else is mixed here), and in special technical color atlases (for example, in the textile industry) millions of colors are numbered. Where else can you find such diversity - in sounds, smells, tastes? It's funny to even try to compare. Physiologically, our color perception is based on the presence of three types of cones, selectively sensitive to red, green and blue colors (trichromatic vision). Using technical analogies, we can say that each light-sensitive “pixel” of the eye is formed by an R-G-B triad, as in a color video camera (R, G, B are the first letters of the corresponding English words red, green, blue). The creators of LEDs strive to satisfy the “requirements” of the eye to the maximum extent possible. For lighting, you need white light with a continuous spectrum, like the sun, - this ensures ideal color rendition and natural coloring of surrounding objects. To display information, it is more reliable to use spectrally homogeneous colors. In other words, in the first case the eye reacts to the appearance and color of illuminated objects, in the second - to the light source itself. Light measurements are also adjusted to the physiology of the eye, its sharply changing spectral sensitivity (Fig.2). Impacts that cause the same visual effects are considered identical.
