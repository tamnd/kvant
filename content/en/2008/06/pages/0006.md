KVANT 2008/№6

Lamps that illuminate a certain area (for example, a table lamp) are characterized by luminous intensity (spatial luminous flux density), measured in candelas (1 cd = 1 lm/stere), and the angle of radiation. For example, an LED traffic light provides a luminous intensity of 300–500 cd at an angle of 20 degrees. A luminous flux of 1 lm creates a site illumination of $1 м^{2}$ equal to 1 lux (1 lux); for reading, 500 lux is quite comfortable; in the operating room they sometimes want to have 50,000 lux. Brightness is the ratio of luminous intensity to the area of ​​radiation; for a traffic light it is 10 thousand cd $м^{2}$, and for a home TV screen it is 500 cd $м^{2}$.

"Losev's Glow"

Let's return to our story. Losev, 1922... At first he was not even a laboratory assistant, he was hired as a messenger, i.e. no one and with no salary, but most importantly, they were allowed to study radio engineering. Then it was a “disease” of inquisitive youth, just like aviation in the 30s, atomic physics in the 50s, and now business. Oleg is from Tver, in Nizhny Novgorod he had “neither a stake nor a yard,” he spent the night in the laboratory building on the attic landing, went hungry (like everyone else) and worked, worked, worked to the point of delight and exhaustion. Unexpectedly and against all logic, he discovered that some crystal detectors not only detected, but also amplified radio signals - an explanation for this effect was given only three decades later, after the invention of the transistor. The radio receiver constructed on such a detector, the kristadin, brought Losev world fame and a place in the history of technology. But here we are talking about his other, even more significant discovery. Oleg noticed that silicon carbide detectors, when current is passed through them, emit a weak greenish light, and since 1927 he took up this phenomenon specifically, devoting himself entirely to it. (Alas, his life turned out to be disappointingly short - Oleg Vladimirovich Losev died of exhaustion and cold in besieged Leningrad in January 1942.) His experiments showed that the glow was not associated with either surface electrical discharges or heating; the “cold light” came from the crystal itself. Already in 1930, Losev gave a satisfactory theoretical interpretation of his experiments based on existing quantum concepts. In today's language, he discovered injection luminescence of semiconductors, then called “Losev Light” in the West. The door to his worldwide recognition was opened by the fact that he promptly published in German scientific journals. Apparently, it is also significant that during his life such wonderful scientists as V.L. Levshin, V.K. Lebedinsky, M.A. Bonch-Bruevich, A.F. Ioffe were close to him. They were not his scientific supervisors in the generally accepted sense, but they undoubtedly had a huge influence on his personality and his recognition. It is characteristic that none of the masters was attributed to Losev as a co-author - the ethics of the scientific community at that time were scrupulous. For practical purposes, the “Losev glow” was too weak, then science did not yet know how to strengthen it, and in the meantime the Second World War broke out, and interest in the interesting phenomenon virtually disappeared. Only almost two decades later, after the invention of the transistor (1948) and the development of the p–n junction theory by the American W. Shockley (1949), they again turned to explaining the luminescence of semiconductors. When current flows through the diode, some of the electrons of the semiconductor are “thrown” from the equilibrium lower energy level to a higher excitation level, the decisive role in this is played by the presence of a p–n junction, the potential barrier of which is overcome by the electrons. And reverse transitions of electrons from a high quantum level to lower ones are accompanied by the release of energy in the form of photons, the flow of which forms light radiation (Fig.3). The maximum energy gap $E_{з}$ is determined by the very nature of the semiconductor $( E_{з} = = E_{п} - E_{в}$, where $E_{п}$ and $E_{в}$ are the boundaries of the conduction band and valence band, respectively) and is called the band gap, the position of other levels $E_{1}, E_{2}, K$ is determined by the type of introduced doping impurities and Fig. 3. Scheme of light generation in an uncontrolled semiconductor defects. The more significant the energy “jump” of the electron $\Delta E$, the shorter the radiation wavelength $\lambda ,$; the limiting capabilities are realized at $\Delta E = E_{з}$. It is easy to see that only gallium nitride GaN is suitable for generating “blue” photons ($(\lambda = 465$ nm), while the red-yellow part of the spectrum can be successfully “closed” by ternary compounds GaAsP and GaAlAs. Note that often transitions of electrons from the upper level to the lower level do not occur directly, but through many intermediate levels with the emission of the same many quanta corresponding to infrared (IR) radiation. But these are non-radiative transitions, which from the point of view of light generation represent a waste of energy. The developed theory (1951) explained, in particular, the low intensity of the “Losev glow” - the “fault” lay entirely on silicon carbide, where non-radiative transitions predominate. We figured out that transistor germanium and silicon were not suitable, “calculated” what exactly was needed for effective luminescence, but... such semiconductors did not exist in nature.
