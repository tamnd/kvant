Addition of mutually perpendicular vibrations

This article discusses fairly simple experiments in which you can get acquainted with the addition of two mutually perpendicular oscillations. Try to carry out the suggested experiments yourself and explain the results obtained.

I. Drive a nail into the top of the door frame. Lay down according to him, giving him the shape of a hook. Tie a thin, strong, non-twisting thread (string) 1.4-1.6 m long to the hook. Tie a weight weighing 0.5-0.6 kg to the free end of the thread.

You will receive a so-called mathematical matnik. For small deviations from the equilibrium position (as well as for the specified values ​​of the length of the thread and the mass of the weight), the oscillations of the matrix can be considered harmonic.

Using a stopwatch or the second hand of a wristwatch, determine the period of oscillation of the matrix.

nick in the plane of the door frame (we will call it the main plane). Repeat the experiment, causing the matrix to oscillate with the same amplitude in a plane perpendicular to the main one (let's call it the perendicular plane). Make sure that the found values ​​of the oscillation periods in both services practically coincide.

Now perform experiments on the addition of the two indicated vibrations. First, cause the matrix to oscillate with an amplitude of 20-25 cm in the main plane along the X axis. Then push the weight (for example, using a hammer) with the expectation of causing it to oscillate in a perpendicular plane along the Y axis.

You will see that the movement of the load will change. First of all, the load will move along a different trajectory, and this trajectory is determined by the correspondence of the amplitude of the components of the oscillations and the initial phase shift between them.

What types of trajectories emerge? Let us first consider several special cases. Suppose you hit the load at the moment when it passed the equilibrium position, the load will oscillate in a straight line making a certain angle with the X axis (Fig. 1).

Now hit the load at the moment of its greatest displacement from the equilibrium position - the load begins to move along an ellipse (Fig. 2). If you strike in the other extreme position of the load (on the opposite side from the equilibrium position)

Novesiya), the trajectories of the load will still be an ellipse, but the direction of movement will change to the opposite.

It turns out that in all other cases the load will also move along an ellipse, and the direction of movement will be determined by which extreme position the load is close to at the moment of impact. Check it out. In addition, make sure that movement along any trajectory occurs with a period equal to the period of the extreme position close to the load at the moment of impact.

Note that to successfully carry out the experiments described, some training is necessary. It is especially difficult to strike a load in the equilibrium position, when the speed of movement of the load is most recorded:
$$x = x_m \cos \omega t,$$
and along the $Y$ axis - according to the law
$$y = y_m \cos (\omega t - \varphi_0).$$

Here $x$ and $y$ are the mixed matrix of the equilibrium position, $x_m$ and $y_m$ are the extreme amplitudes, $\omega$ is the cyclic extreme frequency and $\varphi_0$ is the initial phase shift.

In the first particular case, when the blow is delivered in the equilibrium position, the phase shift $\varphi_0$ can be equal to either 0 or $\pi$.

If $\varphi_0 = 0$, the extremes of the matrix are described by the equations
$$x = x_m \cos \omega t$$
and $$y = y_m \cos \omega t.$$
Let's divide the second equation by the first:
$$\frac{y}{x} = \frac{y_m}{x_m},$$
or $$y = \frac{y_m}{x_m} x.$$

This is the equation of a straight line inclined to the $X$ axis at an angle $\alpha$ such that $\tg \alpha = \frac{y_m}{x_m}$. In particular, when the amplitude of extremes ($y_m = x_m$) $\tg \alpha = 1$ and $\alpha = 45^\circ$ are equal.

If $\varphi_0 = \pi$, the equation of the extreme matrix is:
$$x = x_m \cos \omega t,$$
$$y = y_m \cos (\omega t - \pi) = -y_m \cos \omega t.$$

From here
$$\frac{y}{x} = -\frac{y_m}{x_m},$$
or $$y = -\frac{y_m}{x_m} x.$$

At $y_m = x_m \tg \alpha = -1$ and $\alpha = 135^\circ$.

The second partial case, when the blow is applied in the extreme positions of the load, corresponds to phase shifts $\pi/2$ or $3\pi/2$. For $\varphi_0 = \pi/2$ we can write:
$$x = x_m \cos \omega t,$$
$$y = y_m \cos (\omega t - \pi/2) = y_m \sin \omega t.$$

After simple algebraic transformations we get:
$$\frac{x^2}{x_m^2} + \frac{y^2}{y_m^2} = 1.$$

This is a real equation of an ellipse.
At $\varphi_0 = 3\pi/2$ the result was the same.
If $y_m = x_m$, the ellipse turns into a circle, the equation of which is
$$x^2 + y^2 = x_m^2.$$

Note that with phase shift $\varphi_0 = \pi/2$ the movement of the load along the ellipse (or, in particular, along the circle) occurs counterclockwise, and with $\varphi_0 = 3\pi/2$ - clockwise.

P. Let's move on to more complex experiments. Drive another nail into the doorway at a distance of 30-40 cm from the first and bend it slightly again. Hang the weight on two threads attached to two hooks. You will receive a matrix with double (or bifilar) suspension.

Select the lengths of the threads such that the distance from the load to the doorway is longer than the threads of the simple matrix with which you carried out the first series of experiments.

Call the extremity of the matrix in the perpendicular plane and experimentally determine its extremity period. Make sure that the period found practically coincides with the extreme period of the mathematical matrix.

How will a matrix with an unlinked suspension behave if it is forced to oscillate simultaneously in two mutually perpendicular directions?

Cause the matrix to oscillate in the main plane. When the load is in one of the extreme positions, remove it in the perpendicular direction - the matrix will begin to oscillate in the perpendicular plane too. Try to make sure that the amplitude of both vibrations coincides.

First, the load moves in a circle around the vertical axis (Fig. 4). After some time, the circle turns into an ellipse. The major axis of the ellipse gradually rotates, and the minor axis decreases, so that at some point the ellipse contracts into a segment. Then the load moves along the ellipse again, but in the opposite direction; after a while the ellipse turns into a circle. Then everything is repeated periodically. It is characteristic that with each transition of the ellipse to a straight line, the direction of rotation of the load changes to the opposite.

Let's try to explain this behavior of the matrix. Let us recall that the frequencies of matrix oscillations in two mutually perpendicular directions are not the same - in the main plane along the $X$ axis, the frequency is slightly higher than in the perpendicular one along the $Y$ axis. This leads to the fact that the phase difference of oscillations gradually changes over time (and does not remain constant, as in perpendicular).

Suppose that at the initial moment the phase difference of oscillations is equal to $\pi/2$ - the load moves around the circle counterclockwise (see Fig. 4, a). Over time, the oscillations along the $Y$ axis remain from the oscillations along the $X$ axis more and more - the circle turns into an ellipse. When the lag reaches $\pi$, the load will oscillate in a straight line, making an angle $135^\circ$ with the axis $X$. With a phase difference $3\pi/2$, the load will again move in a circle, but in a clockwise direction (see Fig. 4, b). After some time, the phase difference between the oscillations becomes equal to $2\pi$, that is, in other words, the oscillations will coincide in phase - the load will move in a straight line inclined to the axis $X$ at an angle $45^\circ$. Finally, the phase difference will increase again to $\pi/2$ - the load, as at the initial moment, the load moves around the circle counterclockwise. And so on.
