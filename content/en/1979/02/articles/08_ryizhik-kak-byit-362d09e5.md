$$m_1' = \frac{m_1}{\sqrt{1 - v_1^2/c^2}}, \quad m_2' = \frac{m_2}{\sqrt{1 - v_2^2/c^2}}.$$

They show that the mass of a body is not a constant value at all - it depends on the speed of the body and increases with increasing speed. And just as speed is a relative quantity and changes when moving from one coordinate system to another, so mass is a relative quantity.

However, when the speed of the body is very small compared to the speed of light $c$, then, as can be seen from formulas (5), we can assume that $m_1' = m_1$ and $m_2' = m_2$. This means that the Newtonian masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ are inertial masses of slow motion, in the limit of bodies at rest. They are called rest masses. In Newtonian mechanics, the inertia of a body is expressed by the rest mass, which is indeed an unchanging characteristic of the body.

Those speeds that are encountered in ordinary technical practice are always much less than the speed of light. What should I do?

In some schools, in some I was looking for a persona for the function $f(x) = \sin x \cdot \cos x$.

And two answers were received:

$$F_1(x) = \frac{1}{2} \sin^2 x$$

$$F_2(x) = -\frac{1}{2} \cos^2 x.$$

Since everyone believed that they had found the same antiderivative, the equality appeared on the board

$$\frac{1}{2} \sin^2 x = -\frac{1}{2} \cos^2 x,$$

where did it turn out to be faster?

$$\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 0,$$

or, simply put;

$$1 = 0.$$

It was necessary to get rid of such nonsense layer by layer and get answers that were written differently:

$$F_1(x) = \frac{1}{2} \sin^2 x + C$$

$$F_2(x) = -\frac{1}{2} \cos^2 x + C.$$

However, equality appeared on the board again

$$\frac{1}{2} \sin^2 x + C = -\frac{1}{2} \cos^2 x + C,$$

from which, after subtracting $C$, we again got $1 = 0$.

And then the answers were written down very carefully:

$$F_1(x) = \frac{1}{2} \sin^2 x + C_1$$

$$F_2(x) = -\frac{1}{2} \cos^2 x + C_2.$$

But these constants can be any numbers, including the same ones. And again equality appeared on the board

$$\frac{1}{2} \sin^2 x + 1979 = -\frac{1}{2} \cos^2 x + 1979,$$

from which, after subtracting 1979, we again got $1 = 0$.

In that school, in that class, they eventually learned what you can and cannot write when dealing with antiderivatives. What about you?
