⟦folio 8⟧

Impossible objects

On page 1 of the cover, “impossible objects” fly across the starry sky: the red staircase always descends in a rather circular manner, but after going around a full turn along it, you find yourself in the original place; the three parallel cylindrical legs of the purple piece manage to adjoin the connected crossbar... in two places; Something is also wrong with the blue and orange objects - looking at them for a long time causes an unpleasant sensation of internal contradiction.

Try to understand what is the secret of the “impossibility effect” in each case. However, are all these objects impossible? Is it possible to “see” some of them in such a way that there is no contradiction?

It turns out that using the orange triangle (this is the so-called L. and P. Penrose triangle) you can construct a real object, which from some angles will look exactly like the Penrose triangle. This has already been written about in «Kvant» (1971, No. 5, pp. 26-29). Here we place two new design solutions for this triangle, sent to the editor along with photographs by the architect V. Koleichko.

Follow the first two photos (Fig. 1, 2). They show the same object, consisting of “twisted” wooden blocks, glued together with a “tray” square”, removed from different points. The photographer found a point (Fig. 1) from which all three bars appear straight.

Even more interesting are the following photographs (Fig. 3, 4). Trung to believe that the same item is formatted on them.

The principle of constructing this item is quite simple. Let's draw π on the plane.

image of the Penrose triangle (Fig. 5) and choose points A' and B' above so that (AA') || (BB'). Then any curve γ connecting A' to B' and lying in the plane ABB'A' will look like a segment if one looks at it by placing the eye in the plane ABB'A' or at a point located “very far” above the plane π in the direction of the ray [AA']. By doing the same with other segments, you can construct the spatial object shown in figure 3.

It is clear that the eye (or camera), placed far enough in the direction of the ray [AA'], will see this object as it is depicted on the π plane.
