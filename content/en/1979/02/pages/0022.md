$$\frac{m}{m_{\text{эт}}} = \frac{\overrightarrow{a}_{\text{эт}}}{\overrightarrow{a}}$$

where $m_{\text{эт}}$ is the mass of the standard, $\overrightarrow{a}_{\text{эт}}$ and $\overrightarrow{a}$ are, respectively, the accelerations of the standard and the body, acquired in the process of interaction. From (3) there is

$$m = \frac{\overrightarrow{a}_{\text{эт}}}{\overrightarrow{a}} m_{\text{эт}} = \frac{\overrightarrow{a}_{\text{эт}}}{\overrightarrow{a}} 1 (\text{кг}).$$

But let’s return to correspondence (2), from which formulas (3) and (3a) follow. Let us think about the extent to which it is justified by experience. Experience has only shown us that the ratio of the accelerations of two interacting bodies is a completely definite value (formula (1)).

Next, we stipulated (precisely stipulated!) to attribute a larger mass to a body whose acceleration is less, that is, to a more inert body.

We expressed this mathematically in equation (2), from which it follows that greater mass corresponds to less acceleration. But together with equation (2) we can write, for example, the following formula:

$$\frac{\overrightarrow{a}_2}{\overrightarrow{a}_1} = \frac{m_1^2}{m_2^2}$$

or

$$\frac{\overrightarrow{a}_2}{\overrightarrow{a}_1} = \frac{m_1^n}{m_2^n}.$$

From these formulas it also follows that of two interacting bodies, only the larger mass receives less acceleration! Why did we choose the simplest formula (2), from which the above method of changing mass follows?

Because with this method of changing mass, it turns out that the mass of several bodies, complex together, is equal to the sum of the masses of the added bodies (the law of conservation of mass).

Let's imagine that during the interaction of two bodies $A$ and $B$ we changed their accelerations and, using formula (2), found that the mass of the body $B$ is the second larger mass of the body $A$ (Fig. 3, a):

$$m_B = 3m_A.$$

Let's take another third $D$, bring it into interaction with the body $A$, change the accelerations of these bodies and find the ratio of their masses. Suppose it turns out that the mass of the body $D$ is twice the mass of the body $A$ (Fig. 3, b):

$$m_D = 2m_A.$$

Let us now connect the bodies $B$ and $D$ together. Let us bring the “complex” body $B + D$ into interaction with the body $A$ and from their changed accelerations we will find the ratio of the mass of the body $B + D$ to the mass of the body $A$. Experience will show that (Fig. 3, c):

$$m_{B+D} = 5m_A.$$

It turns out that

$$m_{B+D} = m_B + m_D$$

— the masses add up.

If we use the correspondence (*) to change the masses of a body, it turns out that the masses of the bodies do not add up. That is why correspondence (2) is accepted in physics.

In everyday life and in technology, in scientific research, mass is often (but not always) changed in another way - weighing. This method is not related to the law of motion; it is connected with the law of universal gravitation, and it changes not the inertial mass, but the so-called gravitational mass. But surprisingly, these two coincidences coincide in their role in the mechanics of mass. This article is about inertial mass, which is a measure of the inertia of a body. It is precisely because of the essence of inertial mass, because of the essence of the property of inertia, that the interaction of bodies causes the appearance of acceleration (and not speed) in interacting bodies.

In everyday life, and sometimes in books on physics, the word “mass” refers to the amount of substances in a body. But the amount of substances in a body is determined by the number of particles (atoms or molecules) of a given substance in it. And mass determines the inertia of a body and nothing else. But the mass of a body of a given substance is proportional to the number of particles of this substance in the body. Measuring mass is easy, but counting the number of particles is difficult. For this reason, mass is used as a measure of the amount of a substance.
