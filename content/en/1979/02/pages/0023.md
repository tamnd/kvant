Mass in relative theory (Addendum)

At the beginning of the century, Einstein created the so-called special theory of the relative, making a revolution in physics no less significant than Newton's mechanics in its time. It influenced all of physics, and mechanics was no exception. The relative theory showed that Newton's laws of mechanics are only approximately correct. In particular, formula (1) given by us is not entirely accurate. If two bodies interact as shown in figure 2, b, and at the same time acquire accelerations $a_1$ and $a_2$, then in the action of solidity it is not the relation $|\vec{a}_2|/|\vec{a}_1|$ that remains constant, but the relation

$$\frac{|\vec{a}_1|}{\sqrt{1-v_2^2/c^2}} = \frac{m'_2}{m'_1}.$$

where $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ are the corresponding velocities of the first and second bodies, $c$ is a constant quantity that has the dimension of speed and is equal to $3.10^8$ m/s (the same is the value of the speed of light!).

The consequence of this relationship is also associated with the property of inertia of bodies, expressed by their masses. So we can write again, now denoting the masses of the bodies by $m'_1$ and $m'_2$:

$$\frac{|\vec{a}_1|}{\sqrt{1-v_2^2/c^2}} = \frac{m'_2}{m'_1}.$$

Rewrite the formula as follows:

$$\frac{|\vec{a}_1|}{\sqrt{1-v_2^2/c^2}} = \frac{m'_2}{m'_1}.$$
