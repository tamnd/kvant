2. Let's find out what happens to a conductor with current in a magnetic field.

To determine the distance of a metal conductor. As is known, in a metal conductor the current represents the ordered movement of free electrics. In a magnetic field, they will be affected by the Lorentz force, the direction of which, as usual, can be determined by the left-hand rules. Let the current through the conductor flow from right to left (electrons move from left to right), and the magnetic field is directed away from us perpendicular to the plane of the drawing, then the Lorentz force acts on each electron, in a downward direction (Fig. 3).

Since electrons are free, they will learn to move under the influence of these forces. As a result, an excess of negative charges is formed on the lower side of the conductor, and a deficiency of them on the upper side. In other words, a transverse electric field $\vec{E}$ is created in the conductor (the direction in our case is from top to bottom) and a transverse potential difference $U$ arises.

(This effect is called the Hall effect. It was discovered by Hall in 1879 and was explained by Lorentz the following year, 1880.)

The movement of electrons across the conductor will continue until the resulting electric field compensates for the action of the Lorentz force (Fig. 4):

$$\vec{F}_{\text{эл}} = -\vec{F}_{\text{л}},$$

or

$$e|\vec{E}| = e|\vec{v}||\vec{B}|$$

(here $e$ is the electron charge, $\vec{v}$ is the average speed of the ordered movement of electrons, $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field induction).

From here it is not difficult to find the direction of the electric field and the transverse potential difference:

$$|\vec{E}| = |\vec{v}||\vec{B}|$$ and $U = |\vec{E}|d = |\vec{v}||\vec{B}|d,$

where $d$ is the width of the conductor.

On the other hand, the average speed $\vec{v}$ is related to the current $I$ in the conductor:

$$I = en|\vec{v}|S,$$

where $n$ is the number of free electrons in a unit volume of the conductor, $S$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the conductor.

Then finally for the potential difference $U$ we obtain:

$$U = |\vec{v}||\vec{B}|d = \frac{d}{enS}I|\vec{B}|.$$

Let us continue our discussions about the behavior of a conductor carrying current in a magnetic field. As was shown back in 1820 by Ampere, on a section of a conductor of length $\Delta l$ through which current $I$ flows (the so-called current element $I\Delta l$), a force $\vec{F} = |I\Delta l||\vec{B}|sin\alpha$ acts from the magnetic field $\vec{B}$, making an angle $\alpha$ with the current element.
