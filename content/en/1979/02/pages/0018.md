Based on such observations, Aristotle divides all movements into two classes: violent and natural. The cause of violent movements is the influence of other bodies, that is, forces. As for natural movements, the reason is completely different. Aristotle defines it this way: every body is destined by nature to a certain place. Heavy bodies are assigned a place at the bottom, light bodies at the top. Planets and stars are predetermined to rotate eternally around the Earth. So the cause of natural movements is desire. bodies to take the place that is determined for them by nature.

But heavy bodies can also move upward. Like throwing a stone up? According to Aristotle, this is already a violent movement. And the reason for it lies in the fact that nature does not allow the existence of emptiness: the air strives to immediately fill the space vacated by the body during movement, and constantly pushes this body. (This idea of ​​the “fear of the void” persisted in physics until Galileo and Torricelli, who finally put an end to it.)

We see that Aristotelian mechanics was not a unified science of the laws of motion. Different causes were attributed to different movements, and the causes themselves were formulated very vaguely. But the authority of Aristotle was strong that his mechanics existed for almost two millennia, until the time of Galileo and Newton.
