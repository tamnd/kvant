Let's assume that the captain dozes off briefly, but continues to hold the steering wheel in the same position. While he sleeps, let's look at the ice floe $C\Pi$ floating with the flow. This ice floe floats rotating: it is driven into rotation by water, which at one edge of the ice floe ($C$) lags behind it, and at the other edge ($\Pi$) it overtakes it. The same thing happens with the boat. It will turn, and the direction of speed $\vec{c}$ will change (despite the steering wheel being held motionless). Upon waking up, the captain will see that the boat, which described the arc $JE$, is moving away from the shore.

Now it is not difficult for us to depict the picture of the propagation of sound signals. Their trajectories (“rays”) are curved, like the trajectory of a boat with a sleeping captain (Fig. 2; the wind blows from right to left). In this case, region $MLN$ turns out to be a “dead zone”: sound “rays” do not enter it. A person in this area hears almost no sound from source $S$. (Some of the audibility in the "dead zone" is due to the scattering of sound by reflection from the ground, as well as the diffraction of sound.)

Let us remember, however, that we discovered the inconsistency of the first explanation of the phenomenon when attempting to quantify it. Let's try to check the quantitative assessment for the second explanation. Let's estimate the distance to the “dead zone”. The distance “rays” $SLM$ (see Fig. 2), we will consider its direction to be close to horizontal.
