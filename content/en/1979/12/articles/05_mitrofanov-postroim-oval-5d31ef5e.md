⟦folio 5⟧

If the focus is larger than the infinite one, then the curve is an ellipse; if the degree of the focus removed to infinity is large, then the curve is a hyperbola. Obviously, the first case corresponds to the reflection of parallel rays into the focus, when the speed of light does not change when reflected from the surface; the second case is the refraction of parallel rays into the focus of a body that is optically denser than the surrounding space; the third is the refraction of a parallel beam into an optically less dense medium.

Descartes described his ovals in Geometry, also suggesting a method for constructing them, but only in a partial case, and his method turned out to be less simple than Mr. Maxwell's. An illustration of the optical properties of ovals was given by Newton using sines and by Huygens (in his Treatise on Light). But hardly anyone suspected that there was such an easy and elegant way of constructing ovals using pins and thread in any case, when the orders of the tricks were measured. For example, on figure 2

A curve with second- and third-order foci defines a cross-sectional shape of the surface of a stacked body with a refractive index of 1.5, suitable for rays emitted from point f to converge at point F.

As for complex curves with three or more focuses, we are not able to give them a clear physical interpretation today, but this method of constructing curves that satisfy the equation

$$mr + nr' + pr'' + \dots = const,$$

due to its simplicity, it is of independent interest. And if, following Mr. Maxwell, we consider Descartes' ovals to be the limiting case of oval curves with many foci, then we have a further generalization of the same kind as that of Montiocles *, who proposed to consider a finite section as a special case of Descartes' ovals.

*) Jean-Etienne Montucla is an 18th-century French scientist, mechanic, and author of the multi-volume “History of the Exact Sciences.”

Let's build an oval

For those who are interested in the work of J. Maxwell (p. 3), we present here several geometric problems for constructing ovals using various technical means. To make drawings, in addition to a pencil, a compass and a ruler, you will need a flat board, pins or thin nails, a thick thread and a thin long metal rod (you can use a straight piece of hard wire) with a ring that slides freely along it. And to solve problems - ingenuity! (However, impatient drawing enthusiasts can look at the solutions on p. 63.)

To warm up, start by repeating Maxwell's constructions: attach a sheet of drawing paper to the board with heels, insert two pins into it, wrap the thread as shown in figure 2 on p. 4, and draw a Descartes oval of the first kind of type m:n = 2:3. (If your thread slips off a pencil, we recommend making a circular hole at its tip so that the thread does not slip out.)

Problem 1. Indicate a method for constructing points lying on a Descartes oval of the first kind, using only a compass and a ruler. Test it experimentally by constructing several points on the Maxwell direction of a Descartes oval of type 2:3.

Problem 2. Indicate a method for drawing the profile of a plano-convex lens (see Fig.) of thickness d (along the axis), focusing a point source A (located along the axis at a distance l₁ from the convex surface) to point B (at a distance l₂ from the flat surface), if the refractive index of the material from which the lens is made is 1.5 or 2. (Here, except for the thread and pins, you will have a rod with a ring ready!)
