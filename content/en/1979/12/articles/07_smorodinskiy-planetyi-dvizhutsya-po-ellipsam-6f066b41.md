“Each planet revolves around the Sun in an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.”
This law of physics was discovered by Kepler in the 16th century. As “Kepler’s first law,” it was included in all textbooks, became familiar and “self-evident.” And for us there is nothing strange in the behavior of the planet.

But in fact, this fact is surprising. Why ellipse? Of course, the planet must move along some curve. But why does it move along a closed curve? Why does a planet, after making a full revolution around the Sun, return to the same point? How does she remember this point? Why does the planet's motion in the Sun's gravitational field appear to be periodic? This is the property of Vesyna

unusual. If the force of attraction of the planet to the Sun were not inversely proportional to the square of the distance, but would change, for example, according to the law $1/r^3$ or even $1/r^2,^{01}$, then the planet’s orbit would not be closed.

From here, by the way, it follows that in the solar system, planets, generally speaking, cannot move along an ellipse. The attraction of other planets, and especially of such a giant as Jupiter, should somehow affect the movement of the planet. And it has an effect: having made a full revolution around the Sun, the planet does not return exactly to its old place, but “slips through” it. This explains the change in the perihelion of Mercury in the direction of its movement by approximately 1.2″ per revolution (by 5″ per Earth year). (Fortunately, Kepler did not know about this - his observations of Mars, for which he built his theory, were not accurate enough to notice the column's small "precession".)

It was not easy for Kepler in the 16th century to free himself from the old conviction that celestial bodies - the most “ideal” bodies in our world - should also move along the most ideal curves, that is, along circles. Yes-

Law of Conservation of Energy

When a planet moves, its total energy $E$ remains constant:
$$E = \text{const.}$$

This energy is equal to the sum of the kinetic energy $K = \frac{mv^2}{2}$ and the potential energy of the planet in the field of the Sun's gravitational force
$$\Pi = -\gamma \frac{mM}{r} = -\frac{\alpha m}{r} \quad (\alpha = \gamma M)^*$$

$(m - \text{masca планеты}, M - \text{masca Солнца}, r - \text{расстояние между ними и } \gamma - \text{гравитационная постоянная}).$ So,
$$E = \frac{mv^2}{2} - \frac{\alpha m}{r}. \quad (1)$$

For convenience, together with the constant $\Sigma$ we introduce another constant $v_0$ such that
$$E = -\frac{mv_0^2}{2}.$$

We put a minus sign in this formula because the total energy of the planet is negative, and $v_0^2 > 0$.

We can now write formula (1) so that the mass “falls out” of the equation:
$$-\frac{v_0^2}{2} = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\alpha}{r},$$
or
$$\frac{v^2}{2} + \frac{v_0^2}{2} = \frac{\alpha}{r}. \quad (1')$$

Dividing both sides of this equality by $v_0^2/2r$, we get
$$\left(\frac{v}{v_0}\right)^2 r + r = \frac{2\alpha}{v_0^2}.$$

 We denote the constant $2\alpha/v_0^2$ by $2\alpha$, and the ratio by $v/v_0 - \text{буквой } u$.

Moment of impulse

As the planet moves, another quantity remains constant—angular momentum:
$$L = mvr \sin \alpha,$$
where $\alpha$ is the angle between the radius vector $r$ of the planet and the vector $v$ of its velocity (angular momentum is described in the Appendix).

It will be more convenient for us to introduce together $L$ a quantity that also remains constant—
$$L_0 = L/m = rv \sin \alpha.$$

From figure 2 it is clear that $v \sin \alpha$ is the projection of the vector $v$ onto the direction perpendicular to the radius vector $r$. This means that the magnitude $(v \sin \alpha)/r$ is equal to the angular velocity $\omega$ of the planet at the moment when its position is determined by the radius vector $r$. Thus,

Equation of motion

According to Newton's second law, under the influence of the force of attraction towards the Sun $F = -\gamma \frac{mM}{r^2}$ the planet moves, accelerating
$$w = -\gamma \frac{M}{r^2} = -\frac{a}{r^2}.$$

Laplace vector

Now we can proceed directly to our task. Let's try to find a vector associated with the orbit of the planet, which remains constant when the planet moves and which we can associate with the vector $2c$.

When moving in a circular orbit, the angular velocity is constant and the vectors $v$ and $n$ remain mutually redistributed all the time. When moving along a non-circular orbit (for example, along an ellipse), the angular velocity changes with time and the angle between the vectors $v$ and $n$ does not remain constant.

Let us find by what law the vector $n$ changes. To determine this, we will assume that the orbital motion will occur counterclockwise.

Vector $n$ as a result of rotation will turn into a new vector $n + \Delta n$, but the length of this new vector, of course, is equal to one. Ellipse equation

If the reader is tired of transformations, then he will be pleased to learn that the problem has already been solved. The trajectory of the planet is contained in two formulas within the framework: in formula (*), which we obtained from the law of conservation of energy,

$$u^2 r + r = 2a$$

and in formula (***).

To verify this, let’s calculate the square of the length of the vector $\vec{r}_1$:

$$\vec{r}_1^2 = u^4 r^2 - 4 u^2(r \cdot u)^2 + 4(r \cdot u)^2 u^2 = u^4 r^2.$$

It follows that the length of the vector $\vec{r}_1$ is equal to $u^2 r$, that is, just the first term in equality (*).

Now this equality (*) looks like the equation of an ellipse

$$r_1 + r = 2a.$$

So, we have proven that the three vectors $\vec{r}, \vec{r}_1$ and $2c$ form a triangle, the sum of the two sides of which is constant and equal to $2a$. But this also means that the geometric locus of the ends of all possible radius vectors $r$ (with a constant vector $c$) is an ellipse.

Application

The law we are talking about was first established by I. Kepler. While studying the motion of planets around the Sun, Kepler discovered that the area “swept” by the planet’s radius vector per unit time is constant. It does not depend on the position of the planet in orbit. Let us denote by $r$ the radius vector of the planet and by $v$ its speed at time $t$ (Fig. 6). In a small period of time $\Delta t$ the planet will move $v \Delta t$. For small $\Delta t$, the area of ​​the ellipse sector coincides with the area of ​​the triangle constructed on the vectors $r$ and $v \Delta t$, that is

$$\Delta S = \frac{1}{2} rv \Delta t \sin \alpha,$$

side $v_0$ is the value of the planet’s speed at the moment when the planet is at the end of the semi-minor axis. Let's show it. At the end of the semi-minor axis, by definition, the lengths of the radius vectors $r$ and $r'$ are equal. Therefore, $u^2 = 1$ (since $r_1 = u^2 r$). This means that $v = v_0$. From here, in particular, it follows that the semi-minor axis of the ellipse $b$ is equal to

$$b = L_0/v_0$$

(this equality is explained in figure 5).

So, let's summarize. We have shown that the planet moves in an ellipse, at one of the foci of which the Sun is located. The parameters of this ellipse are:

semi-major axis: $a = \alpha/v_0^2$,
minor axis: $b = L_0/v_0$,
where $\alpha = \gamma M$ (M is the mass of the Sun), $L_0$, $v_0$ are constants for a given planet.

$$vr \sin \alpha \approx v' r' \sin \beta = \text{const.}$$

It only remains to note that the area of ​​a triangle constructed on the same vectors is half the area of ​​a parallelogram, and we arrive at the second law:

Kepler: $$\frac{1}{2} vr \sin \alpha = \text{const.}$$

In the proof, we use only the fact that the force acting on the planet is directed along the radius vector. In such cases, they say that the body moves under the influence of a central force, that is, a force always directed towards one point (or away from it). Therefore, the statement we have proven can be formulated as follows:

For a material lobe, which moves under the influence of a central force, the values ​​of $rv \sin \alpha$ remain constant. Remains constructed in quantities $mvr \sin \alpha$, where $m$ is the mass of the material POINT. But $mv = p$ is the momentum of the particle. The quantity $L = pr \sin \alpha$ is called angular momentum. This is how we arrived at the law of conservation of angular momentum:

When the material LOBE moves in the field of the central force, the angular momentum remains constant.

This law plays a role for motion in a field of central force similar to the role of the law of conservation of momentum for motion in the absence of forces.
