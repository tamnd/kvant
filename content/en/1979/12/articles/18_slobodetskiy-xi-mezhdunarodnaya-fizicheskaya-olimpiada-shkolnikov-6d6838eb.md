XI International Physics Olympiad for SCHOOLCHILDREN

At the beginning of July, Moscow hosted participants and guests of the XI International Physics Olympiad for schoolchildren. In our country, such an Olympiad was held for the second time; for the first time, the Soviet Union organized the International Physics Olympiad in 1971.

Schoolchildren, or rather school graduates, from ten European countries came to the Olympiad in Moscow: Bulgaria, Hungary, East Germany, Poland, Romania, USSR, Finland, Germany, Czechoslovakia and Sweden. Each country's team consisted of five participants and two leaders.

The USSR team included the winners of the XIII All-Union Schoolchildren Olympiad in Physics:

Serdey Gordienko - graduate of school No. 2 in Smolevchi,
Maxim Tsypin - graduate of school No. 2 in Moscow,
Serdey Shpilkin is a graduate of boarding school No. 18 at Moscow State University,
Olee Yushchuk - graduate of school No. 145 in Kyiv,
Neor Yasonov is a graduate of boarding school No. 18 at Moscow State University.

The team leaders were V. A. Orlov, a senior researcher at the Research Institute of Content and Teaching Methods of the USSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences, and S. M. Kabardina, a junior researcher at the same institute.

First of all, it was necessary to determine the contents of the “black box”. It was natural to call it a change in resistance $R$ between all parameters of the terminals at constant current. This allowed us to draw up the circuit shown in figure 2, and assume that elements $b$ and $d$ are capacitors, and elements $a$ and $c$ are resistors or some combination of resistors and an inductor (since only resistances $R_{12}$ and $R_{34}$ turned out to be non-zero).

The resistances could be changed with a meter, but it was better to collect the target shown at figure 3, and by changing the input voltage using a variable resistor, plot a graph of the current $I$ versus voltage $U$. Using this graph, it was possible to find the resistance between the corresponding terminals, and also make sure that there were no current sources inside the “black box” (the graph of the dependence of $I$ on $U$ passes through the origin of coordinates) or diodes (for this it was necessary to change the polarity of the source connection).

Otuchtivkov Olympida also needed to estimate the error in changes in resistance $R_{12}$ and $R_{34}$ and estimate the minimum possible resistance value between other terminal parameters.

The second stage of the work was changing the imperases $Z$, that is, resistance at alternating current. To do this, follow the following rules 3, and draw graphs of current versus voltage. Measurements showed that in this case, only in sections 1–2 and 3–4 the impedances are not equal to zero, and $Z_{12} > R_{12}$ and $Z_{34} > R_{34}$. This allows us to draw a new conclusion about the contents of the “black box” (Fig. 4) - inside it there is a transformer, the windings of which are in series (with a parallel connection of the inductor and resistor $Z < R$).

The various parameters of the black box then had to be determined. Since the resistances $R_{12}$ and $R_{34}$ were already determined, and the frequency of the alternating current was given, it was not difficult to find the inductances of the coil from the impedance $Z_{12}$ and $Z_{34}$ and detect their dependence on voltage (the latter is associated with a change in the magnetic permeability of the transformer core). Then it was necessary to check whether there was an inductive coupling between the coils, find the transformation coefficient ($k = U_{12}/U_{34}$) and indicate the maximum operating voltage of the transformer (at which saturation of the transformer core does not yet exist).

It was also necessary to estimate the maximum capacitance value between terminals 2 and 4, 1 and 3.

It is interesting to note that according to the wholesale data, it was possible to determine how the windings of the transformer were wound - in one direction or not. To do this, it was enough to compare the drop voltage of the voltmeter needles connected to the transformer windings when connected to one of the windings of a direct current source. Unfortunately, not a single section of the Olympics did this.

As usual, the solution to each theoretical problem was worth 10 points, and the experimental one - 20 points. The test showed that the Olympiad sections coped well with the first and second theoretical problems: the first problem was solved by half of the participants, the second by 44 out of 50. The third problem turned out to be much more difficult: only 9 people solved it. Half of the participants successfully completed the experimental task. Maxim Tsypin (USSR team) received the maximum number of points (43 out of 50).

The conclusion of the Olympiad was solemn, at which the Chairman of the International Commission S.P. Kapitsa presented awards to the Olympiad winners. From the Academy of Sciences

The USSR was now congratulated by academician I.K. Kikoin.

Eight Olympiad participants were awarded I degree diplomas: Ivan Gantsev (People's Republic of Belarus), Kalinoy Pavol (Czechoslovakia), Andrzej Pracimo (Poland), Zolman Kaufman (Hungary), Maxim Tsypin (USCP), Sergey Shpilkin (USCP), Olee Yushchuk (USSR), Igor Yasonov (USCP). They all also received special prizes. Four participants of the Olympiad were awarded II degree diplomas and prizes, fourteen participants were awarded III degree diplomas and prizes. Among those awarded with a third degree diploma is Sergei Gordienko. Fifteen Olympiad participants were awarded certificates of honor.

Olee Yushchuk received a special prize - for the youngest participant in the Olympiad - from the magazine “Soviet Woman”. For the best solution to the second theoretical problem, a special prize from the journal “Technology for Youth” was awarded to Lars Uhlander (Sweden). Timo Tuulitsemi (Finland) and Mix Khartoum (GDR) received special prizes from the magazine “Technology for Youth” for the best performance of the experimental task.

The European Physical Society showed great interest in the Olympiad. The best from each team received a book on the future of physics, published by the ETF, and an invitation to attend a youth science seminar held by the society in Italy.

The Olympiad participants toured Moscow and went on excursions to the Kremlin, Leninsky Gorki, and VDNKh.

In conclusion, we note that international school Olympiads, without a doubt, bring enormous benefits to both participants and organizers. They give future physicists the opportunity to meet their peers from other countries. They help Olympiad builders from different countries exchange experience with their colleagues, see their successes and shortcomings, identify the level of education they need to strive for, and establish creative contacts. And most importantly, international school Olympiads, like all international forums, help strengthen mutual understanding and friendship.
