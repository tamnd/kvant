Ya. Smorodinsky

Planets
moving
by ellipses

“Each planet revolves around the Sun in an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.”
This law of physics was discovered by Kepler in the 16th century. As “Kepler’s first law,” it was included in all textbooks, became familiar and “self-evident.” And for us there is nothing strange in the behavior of the planet.

But in fact, this fact is surprising. Why ellipse? Of course, the planet must move along some curve. But why does it move along a closed curve? Why does a planet, after making a full revolution around the Sun, return to the same point? How does she remember this point? Why does the planet's motion in the Sun's gravitational field appear to be periodic? This is the property of Vesyna

unusual. If the force of attraction of the planet to the Sun were not inversely proportional to the square of the distance, but would change, for example, according to the law $1/r^3$ or even $1/r^2,^{01}$, then the planet’s orbit would not be closed.

From here, by the way, it follows that in the solar system, planets, generally speaking, cannot move along an ellipse. The attraction of other planets, and especially of such a giant as Jupiter, should somehow affect the movement of the planet. And it has an effect: having made a full revolution around the Sun, the planet does not return exactly to its old place, but “slips through” it. This explains the change in the perihelion of Mercury in the direction of its movement by approximately 1.2″ per revolution (by 5″ per Earth year). (Fortunately, Kepler did not know about this - his observations of Mars, for which he built his theory, were not accurate enough to notice the column's small "precession".)
