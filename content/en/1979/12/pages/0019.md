$$\Delta(-L_0 v' - \alpha n) = 0.$$

But this means that the vector

$$\vec{A} = -L_0 v' - \alpha n$$

remains constant as the planet moves. As we will see later, this is the vector we are looking for.

Let's transform formula (7). First of all, let's get rid of the vector $v'$. We will do this using figure 4. As can be seen from figure,

$$v' = \frac{\cos \alpha}{\sin \alpha} \vec{v} - \frac{v}{\sin \alpha} \vec{n}.$$

Considering that $\sin \alpha = L_0/rv$ and $\cos \alpha = \frac{r \cdot v}{rv}$, we can write the expression for $v'$ as follows:

$$v' = \frac{1}{L_0} [(r \cdot v) \vec{v} - v^2 r]$$

(since $rn = r$). Substituting this $v'$ into formula (7), we get:

$$\vec{A} = v^2 r - (r \cdot v) v - \alpha r/r.$$

(8) This vector in mechanics is called the Laplace vector.

We will also transform expression (8). From formula (1') for the law of conservation of energy we find:

$$\frac{\alpha}{r} = \frac{v^2 + v_0^2}{2}.$$

Let us substitute this expression into (8), divide both sides by $v_0^2/2$, move on to the new variable $u = v/v_0$ and denote

$$\vec{2c} = u^2 r - 2(r \cdot u) u - \vec{r}$$

$$\vec{r}_1 = u^2 r - 2(r \cdot u) \vec{u},$$

Ellipse equation

If the reader is tired of transformations, then he will be pleased to learn that the problem has already been solved. The trajectory of the planet is contained in two formulas within the framework: in formula (*), which we obtained from the law of conservation of energy,

$$u^2 r + r = 2a$$

and in formula (***).

To verify this, let’s calculate the square of the length of the vector $\vec{r}_1$:

$$\vec{r}_1^2 = u^4 r^2 - 4 u^2(r \cdot u)^2 + 4(r \cdot u)^2 u^2 = u^4 r^2.$$

It follows that the length of the vector $\vec{r}_1$ is equal to $u^2 r$, that is, just the first term in equality (*).

Now this equality (*) looks like the equation of an ellipse

$$r_1 + r = 2a.$$

So, we have proven that the three vectors $\vec{r}, \vec{r}_1$ and $2c$ form a triangle, the sum of the two sides of which is constant and equal to $2a$. But this also means that the geometric locus of the ends of all possible radius vectors $r$ (with a constant vector $c$) is an ellipse.

Now the geometric meaning of the introduced constants is clarified; $a = \alpha/v_0^2$ is the large resulting ellipse. It depends only on the total energy of the planet and does not depend on the angular momentum. The vector $2c$ connects the two foci of the ellipse.
