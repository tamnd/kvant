Thus, we will assume that
$$\Delta r = v \Delta t,$$
$$\Delta v = w \Delta t^*).$$

Naturally, when calculating the change in the area of a parallelogram, we will also be interested only in the rate of change in area, and we will discard accelerations (values proportional to $(\Delta t)^2)$. The acceleration of the planet is proportional to the force $\vec{F}$ acting on the planet. The force is directed towards the Sun, i.e., opposite in direction to the radius vector. Consequently, the vector $\Delta v$ is parallel to the vector $\vec{r}$. It is directed opposite to the vector $\vec{r}$. It is not difficult to draw the vectors $\Delta r$ and $\Delta v$, then the vectors $r' = r + \Delta r$ and $v' = v + \Delta v$ and construct parallelograms on them (Fig. 7) The areas of these parallelograms are equal to the correspondence $S_2 = v' r' \sin \beta$, where. $\beta$ is the angle between the vectors $v'$ and $r'$, and $S_3 = \Delta r \Delta v \sin \alpha$. It is easy to show that.
$$S_2 = S_1 + S_3.$$
Let us note that the area of ​​$S_3$ is proportional to $\Delta t^2$, since both $\Delta r$ and $\Delta v$ are proportional to $\Delta t$. Since we are neglecting the magnitude conditions proportional to $(\Delta t)^2$, we can also throw out the area $S_3$. We get the approval we need
$$S_2 = S_1,$$
that is, the area of ​​the parallelogram does not change with time.

Our reasoning, of course, is not very rigorous, but it can be made accurate, especially if you know how to differentiate.

Let's return now to physics. If $\Delta t$ is small, then the area of ​​a small parallelogram can be considered very small. So, when the planet moves, the area of ​​the parallelogram constructed on the vectors $r$ and $v$ remains constant:
$$vr \sin \alpha \approx v' r' \sin \beta = \text{const.}$$

It only remains to note that the area of ​​a triangle constructed on the same vectors is half the area of ​​a parallelogram, and we arrive at the second law:

Kepler: $$\frac{1}{2} vr \sin \alpha = \text{const.}$$

In the proof, we use only the fact that the force acting on the planet is directed along the radius vector. In such cases, they say that the body moves under the influence of a central force, that is, a force always directed towards one point (or away from it). Therefore, the statement we have proven can be formulated as follows:

For a material lobe, which moves under the influence of a central force, the values ​​of $rv \sin \alpha$ remain constant. Remains constructed in quantities $mvr \sin \alpha$, where $m$ is the mass of the material POINT. But $mv = p$ is the momentum of the particle. The quantity $L = pr \sin \alpha$ is called angular momentum. This is how we arrived at the law of conservation of angular momentum:

When the material LOBE moves in the field of the central force, the angular momentum remains constant.

This law plays a role for motion in a field of central force similar to the role of the law of conservation of momentum for motion in the absence of forces.
