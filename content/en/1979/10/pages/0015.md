When I presented these messages to my workmates, they laughed, but agreed with me, and the most decisive of them, declaring that there was no point in dragging their feet, went to the experimenters and brought them a rubber shock-absorbing rope. This rope consists of many thin elastic bands. Having cut these thin elastic bands into pieces 75 cm long, we made from them several tow-wheels about one centimeter thick. The manufacturing technique was simple: from each piece, by tying its ends, they made a ring, and from seven to eighty such rings they made one thick stake-cord. To prevent the tourniquet from falling apart, it had to be re-cut in several places (just as the rods were re-cut when making a broom).

If such a hoop is put on both legs, then in a position where the legs are put together, the hoop turns out to be non-growing, but will tightly wrap around the legs without sagging. When walking or running, it will periodically stretch. The elastic forces arising in this case will tend to return the legs to the equilibrium position, which will lead to an increase in the resonant frequency of vibrations of the legs. However, such a ring, clasping the legs at the level of the calves, increases the resonant frequency by one and a half times.

To ensure that the ring did not bend the blood vessels, we put it on top of rigid bandages made of cardboard or other suitable material. Finally, to prevent the hoop from moving off the bandages (up the leg), we attached it to the bandages at two points, and connected the other two points of the hoop with twine to the shoes. The twine turns to move the points of the ring to which they were attached up the leg, but did not hinder the movement of the ring in the horizontal plane, since the angle between the twine and the leg is always small and the twine remains practically parallel to the leg.
