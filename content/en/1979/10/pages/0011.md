When walking, the legs undergo forced vibrations under the action of the mouse. It is known that during forced oscillations their scope depends not only on the magnitude of the force, but also on the frequency of its action. The frequency at which the maximum swing is obtained is called the resonant frequency.

The resonant frequency $v_p$ almost coincides with the frequency $v_0$ of free oscillations of the pendulum:

$$v_p = v_0 \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2},$$

where $\alpha = \frac{1}{2\pi} \ln n \approx 0,37 \lg n$, $n$ is a number showing how many times the amplitude of free oscillations decreases in one period. The amplitude of free oscillations of a human leg decreases in one period by approximately two times, so that in our case $n \approx 2$, $\alpha \approx 0,11$ and the resonant frequency from the natural frequency is only 0.6%.

It is clear that from the point of view of saving energy, it is most beneficial to walk by moving your legs with a resonant frequency. At this pace of walking, the potential energy stored when lifting the leg most fully transforms into kinetic energy at the moment the leg passes through the equilibrium position, and after a quarter of the period (half a step), the kinetic energy of the oscillatory movement most fully transforms into potential. The maximum values ​​of potential and kinetic energy in this case are almost the same, and the additional load on the legs associated with the need to “hang” the norm while walking is minimal.

People actually walk with their feet moving at a resonant frequency. The deviations of the frequency “works” from the resonant balance are not large. It is clear why: the greater this deviation, the greater the additional load on the muscles associated with overcoming the inertia of the leg at a strong fast pace of walking or with overcoming the force of gravity acting on the legs at a strong slow pace.

The perfect situation is similar to the familiar practice of swinging on a swing: it is relatively easy to swing the swing at its resonant frequency, but if you try to swing it with a frequency, say, twice as high, you will quickly get tired, and the swing will practically not budge.

Now it is clear why a person, if he needs to walk quickly, first of all increases the width of his step, and not the pace of walking. In this case, the speed of movement increases, and the resonant frequency of the leg oscillations changes very little: it is known from physics that the resonant frequency is almost independent of the size of the oscillations.

In addition to increasing the width of the step, there is another reserve for increasing the speed of walking: bending the leg at the knee while it is lifted off the ground. This, of course, does not affect the width of the step, but the resonant frequency increases, and a person, having thus “adjusted” the resonant frequency of his legs to a higher walking pace, can walk quickly.
