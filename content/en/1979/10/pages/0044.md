3. Surface tension is capable of lifting liquid to a relatively large height.

Take a glass tube with a very small internal diameter (d≈1 mm), the so-called capillary. Lower one end of the capillary into a vessel with water - the water rises above the water level in the vessel. The thinner the capillary, the higher the water rises along its walls. If you've ever had blood taken from your finger, you've already seen it - the nurse collects a drop of blood into a capillary.

Capillary phenomena can be observed everywhere. This is the raising of water through the smallest holes in a lump of sugar, the raising of kerosene along the wick in a kerosene lamp, the absorption of moisture from the soil by the roots of various plants, etc.

A more modest experiment can be done with a not very thin tube. Fill it with water and close the lower end of the tube with your finger (Fig. 7). You will see that the water level in the tube is bent. This is the result of the fact that water molecules are more closely attached to the molecules of the walls of the vessel than to each other.
