This comparison is pretty good. The surface layer of a liquid is, indeed, somewhat similar to a stretched film (although the origin of the special properties of the surface layer is completely different from that of a stretched film). Let's try to figure this out.

In the depths of the liquid, each molecule is surrounded on all sides by neighbors who pull it in all directions equally. the molecules of the surface layer on top do not have neighbors, so they experience attraction from the underlying molecules. The liquid seems to strive to have a minimum number of molecules on its surface. As a result, the surface layer of the liquid is, as it were, in a tense state, like a flexible film. Otherwise, we will call the surface of the liquid a film, but we will conclude this word as a moment.

So, not only the water meter, but also denser bodies, in our experience - a metal rod, are held on the surface of the liquid, and they do not “float” in the usual sense of the word, but are held by the surface by the tension of the liquid.

2. It turns out that the tension of the surface “film” depends on the chosen liquid.

Place the spoon on the surface of the water. Take a match, cut off the head of it (mainly to avoid getting dirty), rub the tip of the match with soap and touch it to the water on the side of the match, at a distance of about 1 cm from it. The stick will immediately bounce off the match. This happens because the molecules of the soap disorder are weaker than the molecules of pure water, attracting the stick to themselves. Different forces act on the stick from different sides; it moves in the direction of the greater force. It is nice to say that the surface tension of clean water is greater than that of soapy water.

You can drive the stick this way all over the plate. Be sure to bend it to the edge of the plate and repeat the experiment. How is the tree behaving now?

Keep in mind that soap spreads very quickly over the surface of the water, so change the water in the bowl often.
