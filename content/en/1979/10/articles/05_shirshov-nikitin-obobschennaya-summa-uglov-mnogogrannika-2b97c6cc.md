Generalized sum of angles of a polyhedron

In Euclidean geometry, the theorem on the sum of the angles of a plane polygon occupies an important place, so the question naturally arises about the spatial analysis of this theorem. This article describes such analysis and related concepts.

Measure of a $n$-faceted angle

We will study plane and polyhedral angles as follows. Let $AOB$ be an arbitrary angle on the plane. Let us construct a circle of radius $r > 0$ with a center at point $O$ and take the meter of angle $AOB$ as the ratio of the length of the arc $A'mB'$ located inside the angle $AOB$ to the length of the entire circle (Fig. 1).

With this study of angles, for example, a meter of an angle of one degree is equal to $\frac{1}{360}$, a meter of an angle of l radians is equal to $\frac{1}{2}$, and the well-known theorems on the sum of the angles of a triangle and the sum of the angles of a convex $n$-gon are formulated with correspondence as follows: the magnitude of the sum of the angles of a triangle is equal to $\frac{1}{2}$ and the magnitude of the sum of angles of a convex $n$-gon is equal $\frac{n}{2} - 1$.

Consider an arbitrary $n$-faceted angle. Around its vertex (and in the case of a dihedral angle, around an arbitrary point on the edge of this angle) we construct a sphere and a surface using the $n$-hedral angle, the ratio of the area of ​​the part of the sphere located inside the angle to the area of ​​the entire sphere (Fig. 2).

If $n > 2$ and $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ are the points of intersection of a $n$-faceted angle with a sphere, then by $|A_1, A_2 \dots A_n|$ we denote the measure of our $n$-faceted angle, and by $|\hat{A}_1|, |\hat{A}_2|, \dots, |\hat{A}_n|$ we denote the correspondence of the measure inside

The measure of a dihedral angle, as can be seen from figure 3, is equal to the measure of its linear angle. From the well-known formula for the area of ​​a spherical triangle, which gives us an expression for the area in terms of angles measured in radian measure (see «Kvant», 1977, No. 1, p. 9), moving on to our notation, we obtain

$$2|ABC| = |\hat{A}| + |\hat{B}| + |\hat{C}| - \frac{1}{2},$$

where $\hat{A}$, $\hat{B}$, $\hat{C}$ are the internal dihedral angles at the edges $OA$, $OB$, $OC$ of our triangular angle.

Indeed, the area of ​​the figure $\Phi_1$, composed of two spherical triangles $ADC$ and $A'D'C$, coincides with the area of ​​the 2-gon $C^*$), the area of ​​the figure $\Phi_2$, composed of triangles $AD'B$ and $A'DB$, coincides with the area of ​​the 2-gon $\hat{B}$; the figure $\Phi_3$, limited to receiving $ABA'$ and $A'CA$, is the 2-gon $\hat{A}$. The intersection of these three figures $\Phi_1 \cap \Phi_2 \cap \Phi_3 = \Phi_1 \cap \Phi_2 = \Phi_2 \cap \Phi_3 = \Phi_3 \cap \Phi_1$ is the spherical triangle $ABC$ of interest to us. Therefore, calculating the sum of the areas of these three figures, we will calculate the area of ​​the triangle $ABC$ three times, that is, two extra times. Subtracting from this sum the doubled area of ​​the triangle $ABC$, we obtain the area of ​​the visible (at figure 4) surface. So, $1/2 = |\hat{A}| + |\hat{B}| + |\hat{C}| - 2|ABC|$, i.e. formula (1) is valid.

From equality (1) it follows that the sum of the measures of the internal dihedral angles of an arbitrary triangular angle exceeds its double measure by $\frac{1}{2}$.

Let us now find the measures of a bevel $n$-faceted angle. Note that every convex $n$-gon can be divided into $n - 2$ triangles. Similarly, any convex $n$-angle ($n > 2$) can be partitioned into $n - 2$ triangular angles, and the corresponding spherical $n$-angle can be partitioned into $n - 2$ spherical triangles (Fig. 5). Applying formula (1) to all these triangles and summing up, we see that for the spherical $n$-gon $A_1A_2 \ldots A_n$ corresponding to a certain convex $n$-faceted angle, the formula holds

$$|\hat{A}_1| + |\hat{A}_2| + \ldots + |\hat{A}_n| - 2|A_1A_2| \ldots A_n|$$

(that is, the sum of the measures of the internal two-dimensional angles of a $n$-facet angle is $\frac{n}{2} - 1$ greater than twice the measure of a $n$-facet angle (at $n > 2$).

The quantity $|\hat{A}_1| + |\hat{A}_2| + \ldots + |\hat{A}_n| - 2|A_1A_2 \ldots A_n|$ will henceforth be called the excess of the polyhedral angle $OA_1A_2 \ldots A_n$.

Sum of angles of a polyhedron

Let us now consider an arbitrary tetrahedron. Let us denote by $\alpha_i$ ($i = 1, 2, 3, 4$) the values ​​of its triangular angles, and by $\beta_j$ ($j = 1, 2, \ldots, 6$) the values ​​of its two-dimensional angles. The excess of any triangular angle is $\frac{1}{2}$, so the sum of the excesses of all triangular angles is $\frac{6}{2} \beta_j - 2 \sum_{i=1}^{4} \alpha_i = 2$. Hence,

$$\sum_{j=1}^{6} \beta_j - \sum_{i=1}^{4} \alpha_i = 1.$$

Consider an arbitrary convex $k$-gonal paramid. Let us denote by $\alpha$ the magnitude of the $k$-angle (at the top of the paramid), by $\bar{\alpha}$ the sum of the magnitude of all two-dimensional angles at the base of the paramid, and by $\bar{\beta}$ the sum of the magnitude of all two-dimensional angles formed by its lateral faces. As in the case of the tegrahedron, we calculate the sum of the excesses over all vertices of the paramid.

From formula (2) it follows that $\bar{\beta} - 2\alpha = \frac{k}{2} - 1$, and from formula (1) we obtain that $\bar{\beta} + 2\beta - 2\bar{\alpha} = \frac{1}{2} k$, therefore,

$$\beta - \bar{\alpha} + \alpha = \frac{1}{2},$$

$$\beta + \bar{\beta} - (\alpha + \bar{\alpha}) = \frac{k + 1}{2} - 1.$$

Thus, from formulas (3) and (5) it follows that both for a tegrahedron and for any convex $k$-gonal paramid, the difference between the sum of the magnitudes of all two-dimensional angles and the sum of the magnitudes of all polyhedral angles at the vertices is a quantity that depends very much on the number of faces of the paramid.

Let us now consider an arbitrary convex polyhedron with $n$ faces (Fig. 6). Let us choose an arbitrary internal point $A$ and connect it to all points of the edges of the polyhedron. As a result, we obtain a partition of the polyhedron into $n$ paramides.
