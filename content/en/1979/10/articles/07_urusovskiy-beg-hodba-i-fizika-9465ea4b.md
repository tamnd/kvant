When walking, the legs undergo forced vibrations under the action of the mouse. It is known that during forced oscillations their scope depends not only on the magnitude of the force, but also on the frequency of its action. The frequency at which the maximum swing is obtained is called the resonant frequency.

The resonant frequency $v_p$ almost coincides with the frequency $v_0$ of free oscillations of the pendulum:

$$v_p = v_0 \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2},$$

where $\alpha = \frac{1}{2\pi} \ln n \approx 0,37 \lg n$, $n$ is a number showing how many times the amplitude of free oscillations decreases in one period. The amplitude of free oscillations of a human leg decreases in one period by approximately two times, so that in our case $n \approx 2$, $\alpha \approx 0,11$ and the resonant frequency from the natural frequency is only 0.6%.

It is clear that from the point of view of saving energy, it is most beneficial to walk by moving your legs with a resonant frequency. At this pace of walking, the potential energy stored when lifting the leg most fully transforms into kinetic energy at the moment the leg passes through the equilibrium position, and after a quarter of the period (half a step), the kinetic energy of the oscillatory movement most fully transforms into potential. The maximum values ​​of potential and kinetic energy in this case are almost the same, and the additional load on the legs associated with the need to “hang” the norm while walking is minimal.

People actually walk with their feet moving at a resonant frequency. The deviations of the frequency “works” from the resonant balance are not large. It is clear why: the greater this deviation, the greater the additional load on the muscles associated with overcoming the inertia of the leg at a strong fast pace of walking or with overcoming the force of gravity acting on the legs at a strong slow pace.

The perfect situation is similar to the familiar practice of swinging on a swing: it is relatively easy to swing the swing at its resonant frequency, but if you try to swing it with a frequency, say, twice as high, you will quickly get tired, and the swing will practically not budge.

Now it is clear why a person, if he needs to walk quickly, first of all increases the width of his step, and not the pace of walking. In this case, the speed of movement increases, and the resonant frequency of the leg oscillations changes very little: it is known from physics that the resonant frequency is almost independent of the size of the oscillations.

In addition to increasing the width of the step, there is another reserve for increasing the speed of walking: bending the leg at the knee while it is lifted off the ground. This, of course, does not affect the width of the step, but the resonant frequency increases, and a person, having thus “adjusted” the resonant frequency of his legs to a higher walking pace, can walk quickly.

Human hands are also adapted for walking. Since the legs move in two different parallel planes, located at some distance from each other, the movement of the leg causes a moment of force that tends to rotate the person’s body around a vertical axis. To maintain a straight gait, people swing their arms “toward” the movement of the leg. With such a movement of the arms, a moment of force arises that is opposite to the moment of force caused by the movement of the leg and compensates for it. Backwards decrease

This means that increasing beta speed by just 10% requires a one-third increase in energy expenditure, and doubling the speed would require a multiplied increase in energy expenditure. As for the required forces with which mice must move their legs at a fast pace, they are proportional to the square of the speed.

This is why it is so difficult to “squeeze out” those extra fractions of seconds in the 100-meter dash. The second method of solving problems is incomparably superior. If the potential energy stored during each lifting of the leg is not enough to impart the required speed of oscillatory movement to norte, then by tying the legs with an elastic rubber cord or attaching elastic elements to the legs in some other way, we strive to return the leg to a position of equilibrium when the deviation from this position is made, we will thereby increase the potential energy accumulated (due to kinetic) when lifting the leg. As a result, the useless additional load on the muscles associated with the need to overcome the inertia of the legs when walking and running will be reduced. The elastic elements attached to the legs will act as reservoirs of potential energy, replenished and emptied by the movement during each period of oscillation, and, as in all oscillatory systems, they will increase the resonant part of the oscillations. The greater the elasticity of these elements (for example, than just the rubber cord connecting the legs), the higher the resonant part. This means that in order to walk and run faster, you need... to tie your legs (of course, with an elastic connection).

One side of the German fairy tale tells of a man who could run extremely fast. To walk at a normal speed, he tied weights to his legs. Skorod knew what he was doing. After all, by doing so, he increased the reduced length of the leg, and, consequently, decreased their resonant part of the oscillations, and with it the speed of movement. But if he attached elastic elements to his legs together with weights, he would run even faster. However, one might suspect that the secret of the fast beta walker lies precisely in the fact that his legs had a higher resonant part of the oscillations than those of other people.

Any healthy person can become a walker by increasing the resonant part of the oscillations with the help of elastic elements.

Let's imagine such a person taking part in athletics. Before the start, he ties his legs and, in order to somehow explain his strange behavior, he modestly tells the judges that he runs very fast and, in order not to run too far from his rivals, has a common practice of slightly tying his legs. Having taken his place at the start, our athlete takes one leg back with some force, thereby “charging” it with potential energy. This can be done, for example, by tying the arms (higher hands) to the waist with elastic bands. The general choice is this: if a person is “tied” hand and foot, he will run faster.
