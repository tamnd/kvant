This comparison is pretty good. The surface layer of a liquid is, indeed, somewhat similar to a stretched film (although the origin of the special properties of the surface layer is completely different from that of a stretched film). Let's try to figure this out.

In the depths of the liquid, each molecule is surrounded on all sides by neighbors who pull it in all directions equally. the molecules of the surface layer on top do not have neighbors, so they experience attraction from the underlying molecules. The liquid seems to strive to have a minimum number of molecules on its surface. As a result, the surface layer of the liquid is, as it were, in a tense state, like a flexible film. Otherwise, we will call the surface of the liquid a film, but we will conclude this word as a moment.

So, not only the water meter, but also denser bodies, in our experience - a metal rod, are held on the surface of the liquid, and they do not “float” in the usual sense of the word, but are held by the surface by the tension of the liquid.

2. It turns out that the tension of the surface “film” depends on the chosen liquid.

Place the spoon on the surface of the water. Take a match, cut off the head of it (mainly to avoid getting dirty), rub the tip of the match with soap and touch it to the water on the side of the match, at a distance of about 1 cm from it. The stick will immediately bounce off the match. This happens because the molecules of the soap disorder are weaker than the molecules of pure water, attracting the stick to themselves. Different forces act on the stick from different sides; it moves in the direction of the greater force. It is nice to say that the surface tension of clean water is greater than that of soapy water.

You can drive the stick this way all over the plate. Be sure to bend it to the edge of the plate and repeat the experiment. How is the tree behaving now?

Keep in mind that soap spreads very quickly over the surface of the water, so change the water in the bowl often.

3. Surface tension is capable of lifting liquid to a relatively large height.

Take a glass tube with a very small internal diameter (d≈1 mm), the so-called capillary. Lower one end of the capillary into a vessel with water - the water rises above the water level in the vessel. The thinner the capillary, the higher the water rises along its walls. If you've ever had blood taken from your finger, you've already seen it - the nurse collects a drop of blood into a capillary.

Capillary phenomena can be observed everywhere. This is the raising of water through the smallest holes in a lump of sugar, the raising of kerosene along the wick in a kerosene lamp, the absorption of moisture from the soil by the roots of various plants, etc.

A more modest experiment can be done with a not very thin tube. Fill it with water and close the lower end of the tube with your finger (Fig. 7). You will see that the water level in the tube is bent. This is the result of the fact that water molecules are more closely attached to the molecules of the walls of the vessel than to each other. In this case, they say that the liquid wets the solid.

4. Not all liquids and not in all tubes “cling” to the walls. It also happens that the liquid in the capillary drops below the level in a wide vessel, while its surface is convex. Such a liquid is said to not wet the surface of a solid. The attachment of liquid molecules to each other is stronger than to the molecules of the walls of the vessel. This is how, for example, root behaves in a glass capillary (Fig. 8).

Pipette some water. Carefully drop one drop onto a clean glass and the second onto the buttered bread. The first drop will spread on the glass, but the second (on butter) will not. Conclusion - water wets glass, but does not wet oil.

How do you answer the well-known question: is it possible to carry water in a sieve? Take a sieve, grease it with oil or, better yet, rub it with a candle. Pour some water into the sieve - it won't flow out! It turns out that the water is retained by the surface “film” formed due to the sieve cells not being wetted with water. If you don’t have a sieve, you can use it instead, for example, a grater or a tin can, in which small holes are punched with a thin nail.
