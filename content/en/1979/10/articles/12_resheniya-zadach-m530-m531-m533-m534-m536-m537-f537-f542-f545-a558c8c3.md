Let's replace our sheet of paper with a table of zeros and ones: if the cell is black, we will write one in its place, and if it is white, we will write zero (Fig. 2). Let us determine for each cell the sum of its neighbors “modulo 2”, that is, according to the following rule:

$$0+0=1+1=0;$$
$$1+0=0+1=1.$$

We call the sum of the neighbors of a given cell its halo, and together with the words “black cell” we will write “1-cell”.

From the conditions of the problem it follows that the state of the cell (i.e., the number written in it) at some point in time is equal to its halo at the previous moment.

a) If the set $B$ of “1-cells” does not change when repainting, then the halo of any “1-cell” set $B$ is equal to 1. Let’s add up the haloes of all “1-cells”. Each side common to two “1-cells” contributes two units to this sum (of all halos); therefore, the sum of all halos is equal to the sum of an even number of ones, that is, equal to zero. But since the halo of each “1-cell” is equal to 1, we find that there are an even number of cells in $B$.

b) We will write $A \rightarrow B$ if, when repainting, table $A$ goes into table $B$. Let us use the sum of the table $A$ and $B$ to form the table $A+B$, which is obtained by blockwise addition of the numbers in the table $A$ and $B$ (according to the above rules; see Fig. 3).

Lemma a. If $A \rightarrow A_1$, $B \rightarrow B_1$, then $A+B \rightarrow A_1+B_1$.

This follows from the fact that when folded, the cell halos also fold.

◆ This problem is best solved using motion graphs. The constancy of the speed of motion of a point means that its motion graph forms the same angle with the time axis t (Fig. 4). Let the moments of meetings be t₁, t₂, t₃, ... It is clear that both motion graphs in the sections t₁ < t ≤ t₂ and t₂ ≤ t < t₃ are symmetrical with respect to the straight line t = t₂, so the third meeting will occur at the same point C as the first. In the same way, the straight line t = t₃ is the axis of symmetry of motion graphs for t ≤ t₃ and t ≥ t₃; therefore, the fourth meeting will occur at the same point D as the second, and so on: every odd meeting will occur at point C, every even one, including the 1978th, at point D.

From our solution it is also clear that the numbers t₁, t₂, t₃, ... form an arithmetic progression: meetings occur at equal intervals d = tₙ₁ + tₙ₂, and the whole picture is completely repeated with a period T = 2d.

This is observed - a special case of a useful fact: if the graph of the function y = f(x) is symmetrical with respect to the lines x = a and x = b (that is, f(a + x) = f(a - x) and f(b + x) = f(b - x)), then the function f is periodic with a period of 2|b - a| (in our problem the role of x is played by t).

F537. By polishing a metal plate it can be made mirror-like. Estimate what is the power of the everyday maximum height of the cervical surface remaining after polishing so that the plate specularly reflects a beam of light with a wavelength of $\lambda = 5 \cdot 10^{-8}$ m, incident on it at an angle of $\alpha = 45^\circ$.

Fig. 13.

$\widehat{MBL} = \alpha, \widehat{MKB} = \beta.$
$\Delta = |AD| - |CB|$
$|AD| = |AK| \sin \beta = (a + h \text{ ctg } \beta) \sin \beta = a \sin \beta + h \cos \beta.$
$|CB| = |CL| - |BL| = |AL| \sin \alpha - \frac{h}{\cos \alpha} = (a + h \text{ tg } \alpha) \sin \alpha - \frac{h}{\cos \alpha} = a \sin \alpha + h \frac{\sin^2 \alpha - 1}{\cos \alpha} = a \sin \alpha - h \cos \alpha.$
$\Delta = a (\sin \beta - \sin \alpha) + h (\cos \beta + \cos \alpha).$

In order for a rough surface to reflect light specularly, it is necessary that the reduced intensity of the reflected light does not occur due to the interference of waves reflected from different points on the surface. For this, the path difference $\Delta$ of rays reflected at the same angle from different points on the surface must be much less than the wavelength.

Obviously, the beam reflected from the surface and from the top of the roughness has the greatest difference in path (points $A$ and $B$ on figure 13). Let us denote $\alpha$ the angle of incidence and $\beta$ the angle at which reflection from the surface is observed. Drawing a perpendicular $AC$ from point $A$ to the incident rays, we find point $C$ on ray 2, at which the phase of the wave coincides with the phase at point $A$ ($AC$ is the front of the incident wave). Drawing a perpendicular from point $B$ to the reflected rays, we find point $D$, at which the phase of the reflected wave coincides with the phase at point $B$ ($BD$ is the front of the reflected wave). The path difference $\Delta$ of rays 1 and 2 is $|AD| - |CB|$. Carrying out calculations, we find (see Fig. 13):

$$\Delta = a (\sin \beta - \sin \alpha) + h (\cos \beta + \cos \alpha).$$

The reflection will be specular if $\Delta \ll \lambda$. Since the distances $a$ between the depressions and protrusions on the reflecting surface can be completely arbitrary (both small and large), then to satisfy condition $\Delta \ll \lambda$ the first term in (*) must be equal to zero. This means that specular reflection is possible only if $\alpha = \beta$, that is, the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.

In this case, $\Delta = 2h \cos \alpha$, and the reflection will be specular if $2h \cos \alpha \ll \lambda$, that is, $h \ll \frac{\lambda}{2 \cos \alpha}$. (Note. Since at $\alpha \rightarrow \frac{\pi}{2} \cos \alpha \rightarrow 0$, at large angles of incidence ($\frac{\pi}{2}$), a rough surface with large roughnesses also becomes mirror-like.)

At $\lambda = 5 \cdot 10^{-8}$ m and $\alpha = 45^\circ h \ll 3,6 \cdot 10^{-8}$ m.

The change in internal energy of the system is $\Delta U = \sigma \Delta s + \Delta u$. Here $\sigma \Delta s$ is the change in surface energy due to a change in the surface area of ​​the bubbles by $\Delta s$ (σ is the surface tension of water); $\Delta u$ - change in internal energy of air.

The internal energy of air as a result of the merging of bubbles changes by the following values:

$$\Delta u = \frac{5}{2} \frac{m}{\mu} R \Delta T,$$

where $m$ is the mass of air in the bubbles; $\Delta T$—change in air temperature. (Air consists mainly of diatomic molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, so it can be considered a diatomic gas. The internal energy of one mole of a diatomic gas is $5/2 RT$.)

The pressure $p$ of air in the bubbles in our case remains practically constant. Indeed, the pressure $p$ differs from the pressure in water at the bubble level by values ​​$\frac{2\sigma}{r} (r = r_1 = r_2)$ - in the original bubble and by values ​​$\frac{2\sigma}{\rho}$ - in a new bubble of radius $\rho$ ($\rho > r$). Since fusion occurs at the surface of the water, we can assume that the pressure in the water is equal to atmospheric pressure, that is, equal to $p_0 \approx 10^5$ H/$m^2$. Since $\frac{2\sigma}{r} = \frac{2 \cdot 7,3 \cdot 10^{-2}}{3 \cdot 10^{-3}} \approx 49$ (H/$m^2$) «$p_0$, we can assume that the pressure in the bubbles remains unchanged and equal to $p_0$. Therefore, according to the equation of gas state, $p_0 \Delta V = \frac{m}{\mu} R \Delta T$, hence $\Delta T = p_0 \Delta V \frac{\mu}{mR}$. Thus,

$$A = p_0 \Delta V = c \Delta s + \frac{5}{2} p_0 \Delta V,$$

or $\sigma \Delta s = -\frac{3}{2} p_0 \Delta V$.

$$10^5 \rho^3 + 14,6 \cdot 10^{-2} \rho^2 = 5,4 \cdot 10^{-3}.$$
