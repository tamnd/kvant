⟦folio 10⟧

New developments in the Rutherford model. In 1902, Lenard staged an interesting experiment. The main part of its installation was a glass tube with three sculpted electrodes - the prototype of the modern triode (Fig. 1). The tube was filled with gas, the energy of injection of which was changed. The gas pressure in the tube was about 0.01 mmHg. Art. The entire circuit is set to figure 2. Between cathode K and grid C, a voltage $U_1$ is supplied from battery $^1$, which can be varied using potentiometer $R_1$. Between the grid and anomer A, voltage $U_2$ is supplied from battery $^2$. This voltage can be adjusted using potentiometer $R_2$.

The idea of ​​the experiment is as follows. The electrodes flying out of the cathode enter the electric field in the cathode-grid space and, accelerated by this field, move towards the grid. (Voltage $U_1$ is called accelerating.) After passing through the grid, the electrodes enter the grid-anode space. Here the electronic voltage $U_1$ is called accelerating. When controlling the accelerating field, these motions do not change (only the direction of the electrode velocity changes, and the abscylton value of the velocity remains unchanged, since the mass of the atom is thousands of times greater than the mass of the electrode). So the electrodes, accelerated by the electric field, move in an arc towards the grid. Near the grid, the electrode energy reaches a maximum value determined from the condition

$$K = \frac{mv^2}{2} = eU_1$$

(m is the mass of the electrode, e is the charge of the electrode).

As the accelerating voltage $U_1$ increases, the energy of the electrode increases. Lenard believed that when the maximum energy of the electrode licking the grid is equal to or slightly exceeds the energy of insertion of a gas atom, the collision of the electrode with the atom becomes inelastic. In this case, the electron separates almost all of its energy from the atom, and this energy goes into internal atomic processes—it is atomized.
