We, as a rule, are from significantly greater distances (usually from a visual distance of 25 cm), so a very narrow beam of rays, having approximately the same angles of inclination, immediately hits us. (Check for yourself that for rays hitting the center of the pupil and its edges, a system of equations similar to equations (1) - (3) will be consistent, that is, the extensions of the refracted rays will intersect at one point.)

Thus, we can see objects under water due to the fact that our eyes immediately limit the width of the light beams created by the image.

Problem 2. There is a flat mirror at the bottom of a vessel filled with water. A person, bending over a vessel, sees the image of his eye in the mirror at a distance of $d = 25$ cm (Fig. 2). The distance from the eye to the surface of the water is $h = 5$ cm. The refractive index of water is $n = 4/3$. Determine the depth $H$ of the vessel.

The most important thing when solving a problem of this kind is to carefully construct the path of the rays. Let point $A$ be some point of the human eye. To construct an image of a point, you need to use at least two rays.

Fig. 2.

One ray is usually taken to be a ray perpendicular to the interface between different media—at figure 2 this is ray $AB$. It passes the interface without refraction and is reflected from the mirror without changing the angle of inclination.

The second ray of the empty space will be an arbitrary ray $AC$. At the interface between air and water (at point $C$), it will experience refraction, it will reach the mirror, at point $D$ it will be reflected from it, will be refracted again at the interface (at point $E$) and will be released into the air in the direction $EF$.

At the intersection of the continuations of the rays $BA$ and $EF$ there will be an image $A'$ of the selected point $A$.

Now let's do the calculations. Since the size of the pupil of the eye (they are significantly smaller than the distances $d$, $h$ and $H$), all the angles of the eye that interest us, and their tangents, can be replaced by snuses or the angles themselves. (Note that at figure 2, for clarity, the horizontal scale increases greatly.) As follows from figure 2,

$$|BC| = h \mathrm{tg} \alpha_1 = h \sin \alpha_1,$$
$$|CE| = 2H \mathrm{tg} \alpha_2 = 2H \mathrm{tg} \beta_1 = 2H \sin \beta_1/n,$$
$$d = h + |BA'| = h + |BE|/\mathrm{tg} \beta_3 = h + |BE|/\mathrm{tg} \alpha_1 = h + (|BC| + |CE|)/\sin \alpha_1 = 2h + 2H/n.$$

From here

$$H = n (d/2 - h) = 10\text{ см.}$$

You have seen that in this problem the eye is both a source of light and a receiver. Similar situations are possible when considering optical systems containing flat or spherical mirrors.
