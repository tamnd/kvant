Experiments
Franka and Germa

The beginning of the 20th century occupies an exceptional position in the history of physics. This was a period of shifting old, established ideas about the structure of the materials of the world. In a number of cases, this shift required the abandonment of the laws of classical mechanics and classical electrodynamics.

In 1900, Planck created the quantum theory of radiation. In 1905, Einstein formulated the principle of the special theory of relativity. During the same period, science came close to the question of the physical nature and internal structure of atoms. In 1911, the results of Rezerforad's experiments were published, on the basis of which a nuclear model of atoms was created, which contradicts classical physics. In 1913, Bohr formulated quantum principles that explain the Rutherford model of atoms and the nature of atomic spectra, but contradict both classical mechanics and classical electrodynamics.

Each of these discoveries pointed to new paths to understanding the material world. But the development of new ideas always rests on a solid experimental foundation. That is why experimental testing of new hypotheses plays an extremely important role in science.

The experiments of German physicists James Frank and Gustav Herm were essentially the first experimental confirmation of Bohr's findings.

Historical experiments were a continuation of the work of the German physicist Lenard, who tried to experimentally determine the energy of ionization of atoms long before the emergence of

New developments in the Rutherford model. In 1902, Lenard staged an interesting experiment. The main part of its installation was a glass tube with three sculpted electrodes - the prototype of the modern triode (Fig. 1). The tube was filled with gas, the energy of injection of which was changed. The gas pressure in the tube was about 0.01 mmHg. Art. The entire circuit is set to figure 2. Between cathode K and grid C, a voltage $U_1$ is supplied from battery $^1$, which can be varied using potentiometer $R_1$. Between the grid and anomer A, voltage $U_2$ is supplied from battery $^2$. This voltage can be adjusted using potentiometer $R_2$.

The idea of ​​the experiment is as follows. The electrodes flying out of the cathode enter the electric field in the cathode-grid space and, accelerated by this field, move towards the grid. (Voltage $U_1$ is called accelerating.) After passing through the grid, the electrodes enter the grid-anode space. Here the electronic voltage $U_1$ is called accelerating. When controlling the accelerating field, these motions do not change (only the direction of the electrode velocity changes, and the abscylton value of the velocity remains unchanged, since the mass of the atom is thousands of times greater than the mass of the electrode). So the electrodes, accelerated by the electric field, move in an arc towards the grid. Near the grid, the electrode energy reaches a maximum value determined from the condition

$$K = \frac{mv^2}{2} = eU_1$$

(m is the mass of the electrode, e is the charge of the electrode).

As the accelerating voltage $U_1$ increases, the energy of the electrode increases. Lenard believed that when the maximum energy of the electrode licking the grid is equal to or slightly exceeds the energy of insertion of a gas atom, the collision of the electrode with the atom becomes inelastic. In this case, the electron separates almost all of its energy from the atom, and this energy goes into internal atomic processes—it is atomized. Secondary charged particles appear - electrodes and positive ions. All electric

Although the experiment actually observed the emergence of an aode current at certain values ​​of the accelerating potential, Lenard was still unable to obtain reliable experimental results due to the technical imperfections of his setup. It is important to note that Lenard proceeded from an incorrect understanding of the interaction of electrons with atoms, since he could not yet know about the existence of discrete energy levels in an atom. For this reason, Lenard's experiment did not leave a noticeable mark on physics, although it is undoubtedly interesting from a historical perspective. Lenarda's improved technique was subsequently used in the classical experiments of Frank and Hermtz.

Joint work between Frank and Germany in 1911 at the University of Berlin. During this period, they studied the laws of electric current in gases. Naturally, the external energy of induction of atoms was of interest to them. To determine it, they first completed the Lenarda method, and later made changes to it.

First of all, the design of the lamp was changed. The cathode-grid distance was made significantly larger than the grid-aode distance (Fig. 3).

The pressure of the gas (or steam) guiding the tube was increased to 1 mmHg. Art. Accordingly, electrons escaping from the catath experienced repeated collisions with gas (or vapor) atoms in the cathode-mesh space. Frank and Hermtsy reduced the braking voltage and made it constant (it was selected in the range of 0.5-2 V). Positive current appears in the aode circuit as soon as the accelerating voltage $U_1$ becomes greater than the braking voltage $U_2$.

When starting the experiment, Frank and Hermtz, like Lenard, intended the energy of induction. The results of one of the experiments with a lamp filled with the mercury parameter are shown in figure 4. The red curve on this graph shows the nature of the change in current in the aode circuit when the accelerating voltage changes (current values ​​​​are indicated in units). First of all, attention is drawn to the fact that at equal intervals of voltage $U_1$ the current drops sharply. The maximum current values ​​correspond to voltages of 4.9 V, 2·4.9 V and 3·4.9 V.

Frank and Hertz interpreted the results obtained as follows. As the accelerating voltage increases, the current in the aode circuit increases (from the moment when $U_1$ becomes greater than $U_2$). When $U_1$ becomes equal to (or slightly greater than) 4.9 V and the electron “gains” an energy of 4.9 EV on its way to the grid, the collision of an electron with an atom at the grid will be inelastic - the atom is nonized, and the electron that loses energy is captured by the grid. As a result, the current in the aode circuit drops sharply.

With a further increase in the accelerating voltage, the following occurs: the electron manages to gain an energy of 4.9 EV without reaching the grid, so that the region of its inelastic collision with the atom “comes close” to the cathode. In this case, the electron, which has lost energy during the collision, continues to move accelerated towards the grid under the influence of the electric field. If along this path the electron is accelerated by the electric field to an energy exceeding the value $eU_2$, then it overcomes the braking effect of voltage $U_2$ and enters the AOD. In the graph, this process corresponds to the region of the aode current after the first decline. When the value $U_1$ becomes such that the electron, which lost energy (4.9 EV) in the first inelastic collision, manages to reach the grid with the value $eU_2$, its collision with the atom will again collide inelastically, and it no longer ends up on the atom, but is captured by the grid. This is the decline of the curve by figure 4 after the second maximum. The third maximum indicates that on the path between the cathode and the grid, electrons undergo inelastic collisions with atoms three times.

Based on the data obtained, Frank and Hertz concluded that the ionization energy of mercury is equal to 4.9 EV - the energy that an electron gains between two successive inelastic collisions with atoms. They obtained a similar curve in experiments with a lamp filled with gel, and found that the ionization energy of mercury was 19.8 EV.

Having been increased in that they did change the ionization energy, Frank and Hertz decided, however, to perform another experiment. The following circumstance prompted them to make this decision. It was known that mercury vapor strongly absorbs radiation with a wavelength of $\lambda = 2536$ Å. According to Planck's theory, this radiation corresponds to the energy

$$E = hv = \frac{hc}{\lambda} (\text{Дж}) =$$

$$= 1,6 \cdot 10^{19} \frac{hc}{\lambda} (\text{ЭВ}) \approx 4,84 \text{ ЭВ}.$$

This value coincides very well with the value of the ionization energy of mercury obtained by Frank and Hertz - 4.9 EV. Is this a coincidence? This is exactly what the decision was to check: to see if at a voltage $U_1 = 4,9$ V radiation with a wavelength of 2536 Å appears.

This wavelength lies in the ultraphyllolot region. Therefore, Frank and Hertz made a tube from quartz, transparent to ultrafilt light (the tube with a glass balloon that they had previously used was not suitable - glass does not transmit ultraviolet light). The design of this tube was much simpler than the previous one. It only contained a nakada filament (cathode) and a grid. They filled the tube with mercury vapor. An ultrafiltration spectral apparatus, sensitive in the ultrafiltration region of the spectrum), aimed at the tube, was supposed to register the radiation of mercury vapor if this radiation occurred.

The results obtained by Frank and Hertz were as follows. As long as the direction $U$ between the catalog and the grid was less than 4.9 V, the current in the grid circuit increased with increasing $U$ and there was no radiation. When this direction became equal at approximately $4,9$ V, the current in the circuit dropped sharply and spectral detection with a wavelength of ... 2536 Å!
