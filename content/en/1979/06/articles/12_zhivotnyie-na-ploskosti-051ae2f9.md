We, as a rule, are from significantly greater distances (usually from a visual distance of 25 cm), so a very narrow beam of rays, having approximately the same angles of inclination, immediately hits us. (Check for yourself that for rays hitting the center of the pupil and its edges, a system of equations similar to equations (1) - (3) will be consistent, that is, the extensions of the refracted rays will intersect at one point.)

Thus, we can see objects under water due to the fact that our eyes immediately limit the width of the light beams created by the image.

Problem 2. There is a flat mirror at the bottom of a vessel filled with water. A person, bending over a vessel, sees the image of his eye in the mirror at a distance of $d = 25$ cm (Fig. 2). The distance from the eye to the surface of the water is $h = 5$ cm. The refractive index of water is $n = 4/3$. Determine the depth $H$ of the vessel.

The most important thing when solving a problem of this kind is to carefully construct the path of the rays. Let point $A$ be some point of the human eye. To construct an image of a point, you need to use at least two rays.

Fig. 2.

One ray is usually taken to be a ray perpendicular to the interface between different media—at figure 2 this is ray $AB$. It passes the interface without refraction and is reflected from the mirror without changing the angle of inclination.

The second ray of the empty space will be an arbitrary ray $AC$. At the interface between air and water (at point $C$), it will experience refraction, it will reach the mirror, at point $D$ it will be reflected from it, will be refracted again at the interface (at point $E$) and will be released into the air in the direction $EF$.

At the intersection of the continuations of the rays $BA$ and $EF$ there will be an image $A'$ of the selected point $A$.

Now let's do the calculations. Since the size of the pupil of the eye (they are significantly smaller than the distances $d$, $h$ and $H$), all the angles of the eye that interest us, and their tangents, can be replaced by snuses or the angles themselves. (Note that at figure 2, for clarity, the horizontal scale increases greatly.) As follows from figure 2,

$$|BC| = h \mathrm{tg} \alpha_1 = h \sin \alpha_1,$$
$$|CE| = 2H \mathrm{tg} \alpha_2 = 2H \mathrm{tg} \beta_1 = 2H \sin \beta_1/n,$$
$$d = h + |BA'| = h + |BE|/\mathrm{tg} \beta_3 = h + |BE|/\mathrm{tg} \alpha_1 = h + (|BC| + |CE|)/\sin \alpha_1 = 2h + 2H/n.$$

From here

$$H = n (d/2 - h) = 10\text{ см.}$$

You have seen that in this problem the eye is both a source of light and a receiver. Similar situations are possible when considering optical systems containing flat or spherical mirrors.

A special and very general class consists of tasks related to the correction of eye defects with the help of glasses. As is known, the human eye is capable of accommodation, that is, “adapting” to a sharp vision of objects located at different distances from it. Due to certain principles, determining acco-

the modulations are not the same for every eye.

If a person clearly sees objects that are no further than a certain distance, such a vision defect is called myopia. If a person clearly sees objects located no closer than a certain distance, this is far-sightedness. Defects such as myopia and far-sightedness can be more or less successfully compensated with the help of glasses (it is the commen that is reversed, not cured).

What is the role of glasses? Obviously, their lenses should create images of objects being screened at distances convenient for a given eye. This means that one of the requirements for spectacle lenses is that the images of objects are within the clear vision of the eyes. The second requirement is that the resulting images must, of course, be straight. And the direct images created by thin lenses are always minimal. Thus, the lenses of the glasses must create direct minimal images at distances within the limits of accommodation of the eyes.

Let's look at two specific problems.

Task 3. A nearsighted person sees objects located no further than 0.5 m from him. What kind of glasses should this person be prescribed so that he can be loved by the stars?

As we have already said, the spectacle lens must create a minimum image located at the distance from which the eye sees this image, in our case - at a distance of 0.5 m from the eyes.

Since the image of a distant object (and the stars are actually distant from the observer) is in the focal plane of the lens, this person should be prescribed glasses with lenses having a focal length of 0.5 m. In this case, the lenses should be divergent so that the images are minimal.

So, a nearsighted person should be prescribed glasses with diverging lenses of optical power $D = -2$ dioptres.

Problem 4. A farsighted person sharply sees objects located no closer than $d = 1$ m from him. What glasses does he need to recite the gazema while holding it $d_0 = 25$ cm from his eyes?

Let us clearly formulate what is required from a spectacle lens in this case. This lens should create a minimal image of an object located at a distance of $d_0 = 25$ cm (a small gap between the eye and the lens can be claimed), at a distance of $f = d = 1$ m. There is enough data to use the lens formula:

$$D = \frac{1}{d_0} - \frac{1}{f} = 3 \text{ дитр}$$

(the minus sign is in the formula because the image should be minimal).

Thus, a far-sighted person needs glasses with a converging lens with an optical power of $D = +3$ dioptres.
