M513. Prove that there is a number $A$ such that the graph of the function $y = A \sin x$ can describe at least 1978 pairwise non-constructive squares. (A square is called inscribed if all its vertices belong to the graph.)

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

In order for the point $(x; y)$ to be the vertex of a square inscribed in the graph $\Gamma$ with center $O = (0; 0)$, the conditions $(x; y) \in \Gamma$ and $(-y; x) \in \Gamma$ are sufficient, that is, the conditions

$$y = A \sin x, -x = A \sin y;$$

in this case, $(-x; -y) \in \Gamma$ and $(y; -x) \in \Gamma$, since $O$ is the center of symmetry of the graph $\Gamma$ (Fig. 1; the function $y = A \sin x$ is odd). Thus, each root of the equation

$$\sin (A \sin x) = -\frac{x}{A}, x > 0, y = A \sin x > 0$$ (1)

corresponds to an inscribed square with center $O$. Let us show that for large $A$ this equation has many roots.

On each of the segments $x \in [2\pi k; 2\pi k + \pi/2]$ the values ​​$y = A \sin x$ increase from 0 to $A$; on each of the segments $x \in [2\pi k + \pi/2; 2\pi k + \pi]$ decreases from $A$ to 0 (Fig. 2; $k = 1, 2, \dots$). Let's take $A = 2\pi n$. Then the values $z = \sin y$ at $y$, change from 0 to $A$, runs through the segment $[-1; 1]$ $2n$ times (this “run” starts and ends at $z = 0$, Let $k < n$. Then on each segment $[2\pi k; 2\pi k + \pi/2]$ and $[2\pi k + \pi/2; 2\pi k + \pi]$ equation (1) will have $2n$ roots - for each transition $z$ from 0 to $(-1)$ and back, the graph $z = \sin (A \sin x)$ intersects the straight line $z = -\frac{x}{A}$ $2n$ times, since $|x/A| < 1$ at $0 \leq x \leq 2\pi k + \pi < A$, and in total equation (1) will have $4n^2$ roots ($k = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1$).

To make sure that the square is pairwise non-constructive, we take only $x \in [0; \pi/2]$. For $A > 1978\pi$, equation (1) will have this segment of at least 1978 roots, and the more $x$, the more $y = A \sin x$ and $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$; This means that all the corresponding 1978 squares are non-constructive.

Another, more geometric solution to problem M513 can be obtained based on the following consideration: let us rotate the graph $\Gamma$ of the function $y = A \sin x$ by $90^\circ$ around the point $O$. Then any point of intersection of graph $\Gamma$ and rotated graph $\Gamma'$ (except $O$) serves as the vertex of a square with center $O$ inscribed in $\Gamma$.

Note that all the squares discussed in our solutions have centers of symmetry at the point $O$. Apparently, any square inscribed in $\Gamma$ has a center of symmetry at one of the points $(2\pi n; 0)$ (i.e., in one of the centers of symmetry of the graph $\Gamma$); try to prove it.

N. Vasiliev

M514. Prove that there exists a maximal operational sequence $(x_n)$ such that for any distinct $m$ and $k$ the inequality holds

$$|x_m - x_k| \geq |m - k|^{-1}.$$

We will present two solutions to the problem, leaving the reader to judge the advantages and disadvantages of each of them.

Note that it is sufficient to construct a bounded sequence $(x_n)$ satisfying (1) for $\varepsilon = 1/10$. In this case, it is necessary that the members of the sequence, the numbers of which differ by 1, differ by at least $1/10$, the members by -

1) the cipher of the digit 0 is the digit 0;
2) any digit can be uniquely reconstructed (“deciphered”) by its digit;
3) if the digits of two digits are adjacent, m. e. differ by 1 or 9, then the numbers themselves are not adjacent.

Let us denote the encrypted sequence by $(y_n)$. Let us prove that the sequence $(y_n)$ is such that for any distinct $m$ and $k$ the inequality

$$|y_m - y_k| \cdot |m - k| \geq 1/100.$$

Let $m > k$, $m = \overline{a_l \dots a_1}$, $k = \overline{b_l \dots b_1}$ (we can always assume that $k$ has the same number of digits as $m$, adding, if necessary, the number $k$ on the left with a certain number of zeros; in this case, according to 1) the number $y_k$ will be filled with zeros on the right).

Let $y_m = 0$, $\overline{\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_{s-1} \alpha_s \dots \alpha_l}$, $y_k = 0$, $\beta_1 \dots \beta_{s-1} \beta_s \dots \beta_l$,

and $y_m - y_k = 0$, $0 \dots 0$? ...?

(We believe that $y_m > y_k$; the asterisk * indicates the first significant digit, signs? - whatever numbers you like.) Then $|y_m - y_k| \geq 10^{-s}$. Let us now compare the numbers $m$ and $s$. To do this, compare the numbers $\overline{\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_{s-1}}$ and $\overline{\beta_1 \dots \beta_{s-1}}$. Since the expansion of the number $y_m - y_k$ has a zero after the decimal point $s-1$, these two numbers are either the same or differ by 1.

With ray 1. If $\overline{\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_{s-1}} = \overline{\beta_1 \dots \beta_{s-1}}$, then $\alpha_s \neq \beta_s$; This means $a_s \neq \beta_s$, whence $|m - k| \geq 10^{s-1}$, since when we subtract $k$ from $m$ we get a non-zero number ending in $s-1$ zero.

With ray 2. If $\overline{\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_{s-1}} = \overline{\beta_1 \dots \beta_{s-1}} + 1$, then the numbers $\alpha_{s-1}$ and $\beta_{s-1}$ are adjacent. Then, by property 3), the digits $a_{s-1}$ and $b_{s-1}$ are not adjacent, which means that in the decimal expansion of the number $m-k$ the digit in the digit $10^{s-2}$ is not equal to zero, hence $|m - k| \geq 10^{s-2}$.

In both cases $|y_m - y_k| \cdot |m - k| \geq 10^{-2}$, which is what needed to be proven.

Second solution. Let us put $x_n = n\sqrt{2} - a_n$, where $a_n$ is the largest integer, does not exceed $n\sqrt{2}$. It is clear that $0 < x_n < 1$. Let us show that for any $m > k > 1$

$$|x_m - x_k| \cdot (m - k) > 1/4,$$

that is, that the sequence $(4x_n)$ is excluding.

Let us evaluate the strength of the quantity

$$|x_m - x_k| = |(m - k)\sqrt{2} - a_m - a_k|$$

$$= \frac{|2(m - k)^2 - (a_m - a_k)|}{|(m - k)\sqrt{2} + (a_m - a_k)|}.$$

The numerator is an integer other than zero (the number $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational), so it is not less than 1. The denominator is easy to estimate, using the fact that $a_m < m\sqrt{2} + a_k > k\sqrt{2} - 1$:

$$(m - k)\sqrt{2} + (a_m - a_k) < 2(m - k)\sqrt{2} + 1 < (m - k)(2\sqrt{2} + 1) < 4(m - k).$$

From here we get $|x_m - x_k| > \frac{1}{4(m - k)}$.

F521. figure 11 shows part of the trajectory of good chalk, thrown under the lot towards the horizon. In lobe A the chalk had a speed equal to the abscise value of 20 m/s. How long did it take the lemelo to move from chamber A to chamber B?

A body thrown at an angle to the horizon moves uniformly accelerated by the free fall acceleration $g$; the projection of its velocity onto the $OX$ axis remains unchanged, and the velocity projection onto the vertical $OY$ axis changes with time according to the law

$$v_y = v_0 + gt,$$

where $g = -9,8 \text{ m/c}^2$ is the projection of the vector $g$ onto the axis $OY$. If $t_0$ is the time of movement of the body from point A to point B, then $v_{By} = v_{Ay} + gt_0$, whence

$$t_0 = \frac{v_{By} - v_{Ay}}{g} = \frac{\Delta v_y}{g}.$$

Therefore, to find $t_0$, you need to find the change in the projection of the body’s velocity onto the axis $OY$.

Vectors $v_A$ and $v_B$ of the body’s velocity at points A and B are directed tangent to the body’s trajectory at these points. Let's draw the vector $v_A$ on an arbitrarily chosen scale and draw a tangent to the trajectory at point B. Find the projection $v_x$ of the body's velocity on the axis $OX$ at point A and plot a segment of the same length on a horizontal line drawn through point B. Now draw a vertical line through the end of the postponed segment, find the vector $v_B$ and its projection $v_{By}$ to axis $OY$. Having measured $v_{Ay}$ and $v_{By}$ using a ruler, we find the value $\Delta v_y$ (it does not depend on the selected scale) and then the time $t_0$ of the body’s movement from point A to point B.

In our case $\Delta v_y = |v_{By}| + |v_{Ay}| = 33 \text{ (м/c)}$, $t_0 \approx 3,3 \text{ c}$.

F524. The helicopter model, depicted at 1/10 life-size, is kept in the air by a momora power of 30 W. Each bit is the minimum power of the helicopter depicted from the same materials as the model?

The center of mass of the system of motion is the same as a particle with mass $M=m_1+m_2$ moves when it is acted upon by forces acting on the system. In our case, only gravity acts on the system. Therefore, the center of mass of the system continues to move along the same parabola along which it moved before the shell exploded.

It is not difficult to find successive positions of the center of mass. From figure it is clear that point a is the vertex of the parabola. This follows from the fact that the previous and subsequent points at which the outside is located ($b$ and $b'$) lie on the same horizontal line. Consequently, after the projectile ruptures, the center of mass of the system, after successive equal intervals of time, appears on the same horizontal lines as points c, d, e, f, etc. Points $c', d', e', f'$, etc., at which the center of mass is located, symmetrical to points c, d, e, f, etc. relative to the vertical line passing through point a.

At the moment when the fragments are at points A and B, their center of mass is at point $e'$. By figure it is found that the point $e'$ divides the segment AB into parts whose lengths are related as $r_A:r_B=2:1$ (hence $m_A:m_B=1:2$).

To find the position of the second fragment after successive equal intervals of time, we draw straight lines through the points $A_1$ and $c'$, $A_2$ and $d'$, $A_3$ and $f'$, etc. On these straight lines from the point $c', d', e', f'$, etc. we plot segments whose lengths are equal to the corresponding $1/2|A_1c'|$, $1/2|A_2d'|$, $1/2|A_3f'|$, etc. The ends of these segments are points $B_1$, $B_2$, $B_3$, etc. - and indicate the positions of the second fragment in accordance with the moments of time.

A helicopter (or model) stays in the air thanks to the reactive force that occurs when the propeller is “thrown” against the air stream. According to Newton's III law, the reactive force acting on the helicopter (on the propeller) is equal in absolute value to the force acting on the jet.

Let us denote $\rho$ the air density, $S$ the area of ​​the jet and $v$ the absolute value of the jet speed. Then, during the time $\Delta t$, the propeller is thrown out of the air $Sv\Delta t$ with a mass $m=\rho Sv\Delta t$, and the air momentum changes by the amounts

$$\Delta p=mv=\rho Sv^2\Delta t.$$

According to Newton’s II law, a force $F$ acts on the vises such that its absolute value is equal to

$$F=\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta t}=\rho Sv^2.$$

The same absolute force acts on the helicopter. In order for the helicopter to remain in the air, $F$ must be equal to the absolute value $Mg$ of the force of gravity acting on the helicopter:

$$\rho Sv^2=Mg.$$

The power $N$ of the propulsion is equal to the energy imparted to the air in 1 second:

$$N=\frac{mv^2}{2\Delta t}=\frac{\rho Sv^3}{2}.$$

Substituting from (*) $v=\sqrt{\frac{Mg}{\rho S}}$, we get

$$N=\frac{1}{2}Mg\sqrt{\frac{Mg}{\rho S}}.$$

Since the mass $M$ of a helicopter is proportional to its volume, that is, the cube of linear dimensions $M \sim L^3$, and $S \sim L^2$, then

from (**) we have:

$$N \sim L^{7/2}.$$

This means that the ratio of the engine powers of the helicopter and the model must be equal to the ratio of their linear dimensions to the power of 7/2, that is

$$\frac{N}{N_{\text{мод}}} = \left( \frac{L}{L_{\text{мод}}} \right)^{7/2}.$$

From here

$$N = N_{\text{мод}} \cdot 10^{7/2} \approx 95 \text{ кВт}.$$
