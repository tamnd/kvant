Mysterious study

In 1919, Rutherford was the first to artificially split the nitrogen nucleus with $\alpha$ particles. This nuclear reaction in modern notation looks like this:
$$\frac{14}{7}N + \frac{4}{2}He \rightarrow \frac{17}{8}O + p$$
($\frac{14}{7}N$ - nitrogen nucleus, consisting of 7 protons and 7 neutrons; $\frac{4}{2}He$ - helium nucleus, or $\alpha$-particle (2 protons, 2 neutrons); $\frac{17}{8}O$ - nucleus of one of the oxygen compounds (8 protons, 9 neutrons); $p$ - proton).
In subsequent years, the integration of the study of nuclear reactions occurring under the influence of $\alpha$ particles began. It was found that multi-light elements, when bombarded with $\alpha$-particles, emit protons. Many, but not all!
In May 1930, German physicists Bothe and Becker discovered that beryllium, when bombarded with $\alpha$ particles, does not emit protons, but some other emission, hitherto unknown. This radiation did not leave any traces in the cloud chamber; This emission passed through quite a few svizz slabs (10 cm thick) almost without weakening. Therefore, it was suggested that this very hard $\gamma$-radiation is electromagnetic radiation, the quanta of which ($\gamma$-quanta) carry enormous energy.
French physicists Irene and Frederic Jollu-Curie became interested in the mysterious Bothe-Becker radiation. They placed a barrier made of a hydrogen-containing substance - paraffin - in the path of the radiation and immediately obtained an interesting result: the paraffin barrier, unlike the lead barrier, reliably blocked the Bothe-Becker radiation; At the same time, a large number of fast protons flew from the paraffin in the direction of the scale’s radiation.
Jollu-Curie suggested that protons are recoil nuclei that appear as a result of the collision of photons with very high energy with hydrogen atoms present in paraffin. Protons are “knocked out” from hydrogen atoms. Knowing the proton energy,

Discovery of the neutron by Chadwick

Having become acquainted with the work of Jollus-Curie, the English physicist D. Chadwick from the Rutherford laboratory in Cambridge agreed that the French physicists (as well as the German physicists Bothe and Becker) were dealing not with hard electromagnetic radiation, but with neutrons. Chadwick was a student of Rutherford; under the influence of his teacher, he had long been thoroughly imbued with the idea of ​​the “neutron”, looked for neutrons, and expected to meet them in any new radiation. He later admitted: “My thoughts, naturally, revolved around neutrons.”

Chadwick repeated the Jollu-Curie experiments, introducing additional existence into them in order to test the neutron hypothesis: he used different shutters containing light elements (hydrogen, lithium, carbon, nitrogen and others). Chadwick discovered that the mysterious radiation produced by the bombardment of beryllium with alpha particles is capable of creating not only protons, but also other recoil nuclei - nuclei of lithium, carbon, nitrogen, etc.

To measure the energy of the recoil nuclei, Chadwick used a pulsed nomization chamber and an oscilloscope. The ionization chamber has two electrodes, between which a potential difference (10²—10³ V) is applied. The recoil core, singing near these electrodes, produces nomization of the atoms of the gas guiding the chamber. The recoil ions and electrons that appear along the way of the nucleus move under the influence of the electric field to the corresponding connecting electrodes; As a result, a current pulse appears in the circuit of the nomization chamber, which can be successfully applied to the oscillograph. By measuring the magnitude of the pulse observed on the oscilloscope, Chadwick determined the energy of various recoil nuclei (protons, nitrogen nuclei, etc.). Having then compared the energies, he came to the conclusion: the radiation that arose during the bombardment of beryllium by α-particles is nothing more than a flow of neutral particles with a mass almost equal to the mass of a proton.

And so on February 27, 1932 (that is, just a month after the publication of Jollus-Curie’s work), Chadwick’s short message “The Possibility of the Existence of the Neutron” appears. The message said, in part:

“... The results obtained a lot are very difficult to explain based on the assumption of electron radiation, if energy and momentum must be conserved during a collision. However, the difficulties disappear if we assume that the radiation consists of particles with mass 1 and contamination 0, that is, neutrons. It can be assumed that as a result of the capture of an α particle by a Be nucleus, a $^{12}$C nucleus is formed and a neutron is emitted... The collisions of these neutrons with the atoms through which they pass create recoil atoms; The observed energy of recoil atoms is in full agreement with this point of view.”

Chadwick's reasoning and rather simple calculations, based on the laws of conservation of energy and momentum, scales are respectable. Let's try to repeat them.

Let us assume that a nucleus of mass $m$ (putative neutron), having a speed of $v$, experiences any elastic collision with a nucleus at rest of mass $m'$. The laws of conservation of energy and momentum for a given collision have the form:

$$mv^2/2 = mv_1^2/2 + m'v_2^2/2,$$

$$mv = mv_1 + m'v_2,$$

where $v_1$ and $v_2$ are the velocities of nuclei $m$ and $m'$ after collision. Note that the vectors $v, v_1$ and $v_2$ are directed along the same straight line. From these equations we find the speed of the recoil core (core $m'$):

$$v_2 = 2vm/(m + m')$$

($v_2, v$ are absolute values ​​of velocities). Let us further assume that in one case the minshen core has mass $m'$ (we denote its speed after the collision as $v_2'$), and in the other case it has mass $m''$ (velocity $v_2''$). From (5) it follows that

$$v_2'/v_2'' = (m + m'')/(m + m'').$$

In the case of hydrogen-containing and nitrogen-containing minienes, the values ​​of the masses of recoil nuclei of the corresponding $m'=1$ and $m''=14$. Having measured the energies of recoil nuclei for these minienes, Chadwick found that $v_2'/v_2'' \approx 7$. In this case, correspondence (6) takes the form

$$(m + 14)/(m + 1) \approx 7,$$

whence it immediately follows that $m \approx 1$.

The development of nuclear reactions is easy to explain. When a charged particle collides with a nucleus, the probability of its penetration into the nucleus is limited by the Coulomb repulsion force. In the case of a collision of a neutron with a nucleus, restrictions of this type do not exist. The force of interaction between a neutron and a nucleus is initially very small; Only at very short distances does it begin to grow rapidly and has an attractive character... Therefore, even neutrons of very low energy can penetrate the nucleus.”
In 1934, the Italian physicist E. Fermi discovered the artificial radioactivity of atomic nuclei under the influence of neutron irradiation.
Also in 1934, Fermi and his colleagues observed the fission of nuclei of heavy elements under the influence of slow neutrons. (True, Fermi did not give a correct interpretation of the observed phenomenon. Only in 1939 did the German physicists O. Hahn and F. Strassmann make the correct theoretical conclusion: under the influence of slow neutrons, the fission of heavy nuclei with the formation of radioactive waste occurs.)
The discovery of the neutron began the era of nuclear physics, which was marked in the mid-20th century by the development of elementary particle physics and the development of nuclear technology.
In the history of physics, 1932 is usually called the “year of miracles,” in recognition of the abundance of remarkable discoveries that occurred that year. One can agree with this name even if we take into account only the discovery of the neutron.
