Discovery of the neutron by Chadwick

Having become acquainted with the work of Jollus-Curie, the English physicist D. Chadwick from the Rutherford laboratory in Cambridge agreed that the French physicists (as well as the German physicists Bothe and Becker) were dealing not with hard electromagnetic radiation, but with neutrons. Chadwick was a student of Rutherford; under the influence of his teacher, he had long been thoroughly imbued with the idea of ​​the “neutron”, looked for neutrons, and expected to meet them in any new radiation. He later admitted: “My thoughts, naturally, revolved around neutrons.”

Chadwick repeated the Jollu-Curie experiments, introducing additional existence into them in order to test the neutron hypothesis: he used different shutters containing light elements (hydrogen, lithium, carbon, nitrogen and others). Chadwick discovered that the mysterious radiation produced by the bombardment of beryllium with alpha particles is capable of creating not only protons, but also other recoil nuclei - nuclei of lithium, carbon, nitrogen, etc.

To measure the energy of the recoil nuclei, Chadwick used a pulsed nomization chamber and an oscilloscope. The ionization chamber has two electrodes, between which a potential difference (10²—10³ V) is applied. The recoil core, singing near these electrodes, produces nomization of the atoms of the gas guiding the chamber. The recoil ions and electrons that appear along the way of the nucleus move under the influence of the electric field to the corresponding connecting electrodes; As a result, a current pulse appears in the circuit of the nomization chamber, which can be successfully applied to the oscillograph. By measuring the magnitude of the pulse observed on the oscilloscope, Chadwick determined the energy of various recoil nuclei (protons, nitrogen nuclei, etc.). Having then compared the energies, he came to the conclusion: the radiation that arose during the bombardment of beryllium by α-particles is nothing more than a flow of neutral particles with a mass almost equal to the mass of a proton.
