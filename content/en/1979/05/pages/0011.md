Rutherford's neutron hypothesis

Rutherford came to the neutron hypothesis by thinking about the problem of the structure of atomic nuclei. For example, it was known that an atom of a gel has 2 electrons and, therefore, the nucleus of this atom must contain 2 protons (after all, an atom is neutral). However, the mass of the gel atom is not two, but four times greater than the mass of the hydrogen atom. In this regard, the idea arose that the gel core consists of 2 electrons and, accordingly, 4 protons. From Rutherford's point of view, the gel core consists of 2 protons and 2 neutral doublets. An oxygen atom, for example, has 8 electrons, and its mass is 16 times greater than the mass of a hydrogen atom; therefore, it could be assumed that the oxygen nucleus contains 16 protons and 8 electrons (according to Rutherford - 8 protons and 8 neutral doublets).

Why does Rutherford present the neutron as a “doublet” of a proton and an electron, and not as an independent neutral particle? This can be understood if we remember that in the 20s of our century only three elementary particles were known - electron, proton, photon; at that time it was generally accepted that these particles completely exhausted the entire set of “elementary bricks” from which matter was built. By viewing the hypothetical neutron as a “union” of a proton and an electron, Rutherford thereby followed the generally accepted concept. In addition, the β-radioactivity of nuclei was discovered back in 1898, as it seemed, quite definitely indicating the presence of electrons inside atomic nuclei (β-radiation of nuclei - electron flow).

The limitations of Rutherford's neutron hypothesis do not detract from its historical value. Rutherford managed to infect many of his students with the idea of ​​the “neutron” (even if represented in the form of a miniature hydrogen atom). And it is completely natural that it was one of them, D. Chadwin, who actually managed to discover the neutron - not a neutral “neutral doublet” of a proton and an electron, but a new elementary particle.

This happened in 1932. The discovery was preceded by a number of interesting events in the world of physics.

"Expulsion" of an electron from the atomic nucleus

One of these events occurred in 1924, when the French physicist Louis de Broglie proposed to compare the neculovolin with the electron. The length of this wave λ was expressed in terms of the electron momentum p as follows:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p},$$

where $h=6.6 \cdot 10^{-34}$ J·s is the universal physical constant - Planck’s constant. In 1927, de Broglie's hypothesis (and, in part, formula (1)) received experimental confirmation in experiments that discovered the diffraction of electrons when passing through thin metal plates.

The discovery of electrons' wave properties significantly changed physicists' ideas about the structure of the atomic nucleus. Now it has become clear: electrons are not part of the atomic nucleus - for the reason that the electron simply cannot “fit” inside the nucleus.

Conventional diagrams illustrating the structure of the gel core: a) - proton-electron model; b) - model with neutral Rutherford doublets; c) - proton-neutron model. Designations: 1 - proton, 2 - electron, 3 - neutral doublet, 4 - neutron.

In fact, in order to fit into the nucleus, an electron must have a wavelength λ that does not exceed the diameter d of the nucleus: λ<d. Using (1), we obtain from here the inequality

$$p > h/d.$$
