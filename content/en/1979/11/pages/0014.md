⟦folio 12⟧

Energy is released when a hydrogen molecule is formed from atoms. The difference between the binding energies of the products and the starting substances is called the thermal effect of the reaction (most often, the internal energy of the molecules turns into heat as a result of the reaction). The thermal effect, calculated per unit mass or volume of a substance, is a heattropy.

If a reaction is accompanied by the release of heat, this does not mean at all that it should proceed quickly. This illustrates figure 1. During the reaction A+B→C+D, the bonds in molecules A and B must be broken or at least significantly weakened. For this, a certain amount of energy E is required (as defined for each reaction). It is called the activation energy of the reaction. It is clear that the order of magnitude of the activation energy should be close to the sum $U_{AB}$ of the binding energies of molecules A and B. Thus, the course of the chemical process in the initial energetic stage is hindered. The molecules entering into the reaction must have sufficient thermal energy to overcome the barrier of value E.

The destruction or weakening of chemical bonds can occur under the influence of thermal (translational or vibrational) movement of atoms and molecules. Nature is designed in such a way that the energy of thermal motion at moderate temperatures is usually much less than the activation energy. The average energy of thermal motion is of the order of $kT$, that is

$$\sim 4 \cdot 10^{-21} \text{ Дж},$$

while the activation energy is hundreds of times greater. This is the reason for the columnar number of effective collisions. A chemical reaction occurs only between those molecules whose thermal energy is much higher than the average. And there are very few of them.

The number of molecules possessing thermal motion energy E at temperature T is expressed by a simple formula:

$$y = e^{-E/kT}.$$
