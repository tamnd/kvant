Of course, the reader has already drawn attention to the punctuated curve $P_-^{(k)}$ on figure 3, corresponding to the temperature $T_0^{(k)}$. $T_0^{(k)}$ is the critical initial temperature. Functions $P_+$ and $P_-$ also contain other parameters, the change of which transfers the system from a subcritical to a supercritical state (task 3 is devoted to this issue).

Let's now move on to mathematics, that is, we will get the number of critical explosions. It should have the character of some inequality of type $T_0 > T_0^{(k)}$, which would include all the parameters of the problem. We want to find the tangency condition for two curves corresponding to the functions $P_+(T)$ and $P_-(T)$. For high school students familiar with differential calculus, this is a good opportunity to test their knowledge. Those who do not have differentiation must believe in the final result and understand its physical meaning.

In order for the graphs of functions to touch at a given point, two conditions must be met: firstly, the values ​​of the functions at this point must be equal, and secondly, the values ​​of arbitrary functions at this point must coincide.

These two conditions determine the critical temperature, that is, the temperature of the substance that separates the tangency point of the graphs $P_+(T)$ and $P_-(T)$:

$$T_k = T_0 + \frac{kT_0^2}{E}$$

(the value of $T_0$ in this formula is $T_0^{(k)}$).

The value $T_k$ determines the critical thermal explosion:

$$\frac{zQV}{\alpha S} \frac{E}{kT_0^2} e^{-E/kT_0} \geq \frac{1}{e}.$$

(Substituting the value $T_k$ into equality (**) gives the conditions for the critical regime; the “greater than” sign determines explosion.)

Thus, knowing the shape and dimensions of a volume containing a substance with known physicochemical properties, we can predict whether an explosion will occur or not.

Now we can move on to the investigation of the explosions of ships loaded with ammonium nitrate. It has already been said that during its decomposition heat is released, and since the rate of the chemical reaction increases with temperature, then in principle the explosive release of ammonium is possible. However, opportunity is not necessity. Only by turning to numerical estimates can it be established whether a thermal explosion will occur or whether the causes of the disaster should be sought in something else.

Let's rewrite formula (**) in a slightly different form. It is obvious that the ratio $V/\alpha S$ is proportional to the square of the linear size of the body $r^2$. Therefore, the thermal explosion criterion can be written in the form of a condition that determines the critical size of the body $r_k$:

$$r_k = \left( \frac{C\lambda kT_0^2}{zQE} e^{E/kT_0} \right)^{1/2},$$

at $r > r_k$ — explosion.

(The constant $C$ characterizes the influence of body shape.)
