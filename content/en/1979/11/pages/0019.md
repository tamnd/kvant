About vortex stakes

How can you obtain water and air vortex wheels in laboratory conditions? What properties do they have? Is it possible for rings to interact with prerads and with each other? You will receive answers to these and many other similar questions by going through the proposed article.

The authors of the article, while still schoolchildren, discovered many interesting effects and behavioral features of vortex rings. They managed to explain almost everything qualitatively.

Formation of vortex rings

To obtain vortices in the air under LAB-rotor conditions, we used the Theta apparatus (Fig. 1). It is a cylinder, one end of which (membrane) is covered with some elastic material (for example, leather), and the other has a round hole (diaphragm).

Inside the cylinder there are two vessels: one with hydrochloric acid (HCl), the other with ammonia (NH₄OH). As a result in the cylinder

a thick fog (smoke) is formed from particles of ammonium chloride (ammonia NH₄Cl).

By hitting the_membrane, we impart speed to the neoctor applying the word of smoke to the_membrane. Once in motion, this layer will cause compaction of the neighboring layer, the next one, and so on. When the seal reaches the diaphragm, smoke will escape from the hole, set in motion the previously exhausted air and, thanks to frictional friction, itself will spin into a smoke ring.

Perhaps strata solidification plays a major role in the formation of vortex rings? Let's check it out. Instead of the usual diaphragm, we’ll put it in the Teta apparatus, it’s decided. If our hypothesis is correct, there should be many small rings. However, experience shows that this is not so—one large vortex ring (Fig. 2) is formed.

It is very important that the smoke comes out of the device in separate portions and not in a continuous stream. If the membrane is replaced with a piston and stirred, a continuous stream of smoke will appear from the hole together with the rings.

Vortexes in water can be obtained using an ordinary pipette and ink. Having collected ink into a pipette, you need to drop it from a height of 2-3 cm into an aquarium with well-settled water (in which there are no convection currents). In arbitrary water there are well-established ink rings (Fig. 3).

You can do it a little differently: release a stream of ink from the pipette directly into the water (Fig. 4). In this case, the vortex rings are somewhat larger in size.
