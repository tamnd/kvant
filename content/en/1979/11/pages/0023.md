How to break a square

Is it possible to cut a square into some number less than pairwise non-congruent squares? For a long time, mathematicians (among them and Hugo Steinhaus) assumed that it was impossible. The possibility of such a division was first established by the German geometer R. Spagh in 1939: he managed to divide the square into 55 pairwise non-congestive squares. The number 55 was very soon reduced: in 1940, the English mathematicians A. Ston and W. Tatti established that a square can be cut into 28 pairs of non-congestive squares, and this can be done in two different ways. Then they, together with K. Smith and R. Brook, reduced the number 28 to 26.

The 26-square partition should be considered the "most economical" of all possible partitions. However, in 1948, the Englishman F. Wilkosk constructed a division of a square into 24 non-repeating squares. This “record” lasted for thirty years. In 1978, the Dutch mathematician Duwesin pointed out the division of a square into 21 different squares (see Journal of Combinatorial Theory, 1978, V25, p. 240-243). Duvesin's partition is depicted on our cover (the numbers indicate the lengths of the sides of the squares of the partition, if the length of the side of the original square is 112). Duvesin proved that the number 21 can no longer be reduced (previously it was only proven that the number of squares of the partition is not less than 14; see, for example, p. 29 of I. M. Yaglom’s book “How to cut a square?”, M., “Nauka”, FM, 1969).
