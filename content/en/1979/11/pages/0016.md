The equality of $P_+(T)$ and $P_-(T)$ corresponds to the condition of thermal equilibrium (stationarity) - all the released energy is removed from the system to the environment. By simple reasoning (problem 2) we can show that intersection points of type $a$ (the heat flow curve is steeper than the heat release curve) are stable, and points of type $b$ are unstable.

Let us now consider the behavior of the reacting system over time. Let at the initial moment $t=0$ the substance have a temperature $T_0^{(1)}$ equal to the ambient temperature (point $A$ at figure 3). Since at this point $P_+ > P_-$, the body begins to heat up. At figure 4 this process will correspond to the movement of the system along curve 1. It can be seen that the temperature increase slows down over time. This is due to the decrease in the difference $P_+ - P_-$ during heating, which determines the increase in temperature. The heating process will end at point $B$ (see figure 3), which is stable. A similar dependence (curve 2 on figure 4) of temperature on time will be for a higher initial temperature $T_0^{(2)}$. The system is heated to a slightly higher final temperature than in the first case and will reach a stable state.

Fig. 4. Temperature change over time for subcritical (curves 1 and 2) and supercritical (curve 3) modes.

By increasing the ambient temperature (and, accordingly, the initial temperature of the substance), we will come to a qualitatively different heating regime. Let's see how the heating process proceeds at the initial temperature $T_0^{(3)}$ (curve 3 at figure 4). At the beginning of the process, the dependence $T(t)$ is similar to the two previous cases: the difference $P_+ - P_-$ decreases over time, so the temperature rise is replaced. The system passes through a narrow gap between the heat release and heat flow curves for a very long time - on the temperature dependence $T(t)$ this corresponds to an almost horizontal section. After the difference $P_+ - P_-$ passes through the minimum, a sharp (faster than exponential) increase in temperature begins. A self-accelerating process of energy release will arise. This thermal self-acceleration of an exthermic chemical reaction is called a thermal explosion.

One of the most remarkable aspects of this phenomenon is the existence of a critical condition. We see that a smooth change in $T_0$ leads to a sharp qualitative change in the behavior of the system. Temperature dependence $T(t)$ can be of only two types - either weak heating, or a sharp (after some smooth period) increase in temperature - explosion.
