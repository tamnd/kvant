Thermal explosion

In January 1953, the screw steamer Tyrrhenya with a displacement of four thousand tons exploded and sank in the Red Sea. In the history of maritime shipping, larger disasters are also known - explosions and fires on tankers and ships transporting ammunition. But the Tyrrhenya was, at first glance, carrying a cargo that was harmless from an explosive point of view - ammonium nitrate. If this were the only fact, then the cause of the explosion could be sought among random circumstances. However, the Lloyd's Register of Shipping, which records all major maritime disasters, lists several ten fires and explosions associated with ammonium nitrate. They became more common in the forties and fifties, when the rate of industrial production of this substance increased sharply. The year 1947 turned out to be a record year - during the loading of ships in various ports there were three powerful explosions, which led to a large number of casualties.

Meanwhile, ammonium nitrate (ammonium nitrate, or ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃) has long been used in large quantities in technology and agriculture. Its annual production is ten million tons. It is enough to look at the chemical formula of this compound to tell where it can be used. Ammonium nitrate contains a lot of nitrogen, and it is bound into the two best ions absorbed by plants—ammonium and nitrate. Therefore, ammonium nitrate is one of the most valuable mineral fertilizers. And the presence of a large amount of oxygen provides a second, not always peaceful, use of ammonium nitrate as an oxidizing agent in the production of gunpowder and explosives. Without organic impurities and metals, ammonium nitrate does not explode. It is often used in chemical demonstration experiments. A mixture of NH₄NO₃ with tropyl or aluminum is a strong explosive.

Let's try to figure out why a completely safe mineral fertilizer - ammonium nitrate, which can be stored in large quantities for years in warehouses or in the open air, subsequently became the cause of tragedies at sea.

The explosive process is characterized by the very rapid release of a large amount of energy, which is accompanied by a sharp change in the state of the substance. The gases formed during the explosion can perform significant mechanical work during their distribution.

Terms like “fast” and “big” are, of course, relative. With a kilogram of coal, about thirty million joules are released into the air. This heat is enough to heat combustion products to temperatures of many thousands of degrees. This temperature at normal gas density corresponds to a pressure of hundreds of atmospheres. Thus, from an energy point of view, the coal-oxygen system has enough energy to cause an explosion. However, coal does not explode, but burns easily.

The fact is that in this case the second condition of the explosion is not met - the speed of energy release. Indeed, the combustion of coal begins from the surface; the deeper layers begin to burn only after they have warmed up due to the heat released during the reaction in the surface layer. Warming up, as well as the supply of oxygen, occurs so slowly that the combustion products have time to heat up, and there is no significant increase in pressure.

However, there are also substances whose chemical transformations occurred without the supply of an extraction oxidizer. Examples include solid rocket propellants, dynamite, etc.

One of these substances is trinitropoluene - С₇H₅(NO₂)₃. Experts usually call it trope or top. If you burn the fire from the surface, it easily ignites and burns. At the same time, the inside of the substance slowly erupts. And despite the fact that the heat output of this substance is much less than that of conventional fuels (only 4 MJ/kg), tropyl can explode. To do this, you need to increase the temperature throughout the entire volume of the substance. This can be done in different ways, for example, by compression. In this case, the reaction proceeds throughout the entire volume and so quickly that the resulting gaseous products do not have time to expand significantly during the reaction. Occupying a volume approximately equal to the volume of the original charge, and being heated to a high temperature, these gaseous products create a pressure of tens or hundreds of thousands of atmospheres. The subsequent rapid distribution of gases leads to destruction of the environment - an explosion will occur.

In this article we want to introduce the reader to a thermal explosion - a spontaneous explosive process associated with the release of chemical energy.

First of all, we need to recall the basic facts from the study of the rate of chemical reactions.

The first thing that catches your eye when studying a chemical transformation is its low speed. Indeed, in a gas, for example, under normal conditions, each molecule experiences an average of 10¹⁰ centigrade per second. If every collision resulted in a reaction, it would happen instantly. Experience does not confirm this. For example, a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen (explosive gas) at room temperature can be kept perfectly effective, that is, it leads to a reaction.

A chemical act is the transformation of some molecules into others. In this case, one bond between atoms is destroyed and new ones arise. If the new bonds are different from the old ones, then the reaction occurs with the release of heat (an exothermic process). This, however, does not mean that the reaction should be faster. After all, before forming new connections (even empty ones that are stronger), you need to destroy the old ones, that is, expend energy. The strength of any component system (atom, molecule, nucleus) is characterized by bond energy. It is equal to the work that needs to be done to decompose the system into its constituent parts. For example, the binding energy of a hydrogen molecule is 7.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. This energy must be expended to obtain two hydrogen atoms, and, conversely, exactly this much

Energy is released when a hydrogen molecule is formed from atoms. The difference between the binding energies of the products and the starting substances is called the thermal effect of the reaction (most often, the internal energy of the molecules turns into heat as a result of the reaction). The thermal effect, calculated per unit mass or volume of a substance, is a heattropy.

If a reaction is accompanied by the release of heat, this does not mean at all that it should proceed quickly. This illustrates figure 1. During the reaction A+B→C+D, the bonds in molecules A and B must be broken or at least significantly weakened. For this, a certain amount of energy E is required (as defined for each reaction). It is called the activation energy of the reaction. It is clear that the order of magnitude of the activation energy should be close to the sum $U_{AB}$ of the binding energies of molecules A and B. Thus, the course of the chemical process in the initial energetic stage is hindered. The molecules entering into the reaction must have sufficient thermal energy to overcome the barrier of value E.

The destruction or weakening of chemical bonds can occur under the influence of thermal (translational or vibrational) movement of atoms and molecules. Nature is designed in such a way that the energy of thermal motion at moderate temperatures is usually much less than the activation energy. The average energy of thermal motion is of the order of $kT$, that is

$$\sim 4 \cdot 10^{-21} \text{ Дж},$$

while the activation energy is hundreds of times greater. This is the reason for the columnar number of effective collisions. A chemical reaction occurs only between those molecules whose thermal energy is much higher than the average. And there are very few of them.

The number of molecules possessing thermal motion energy E at temperature T is expressed by a simple formula:

$$y = e^{-E/kT}.$$

Thus, the rate of a chemical reaction, that is, the amount of substances (in kilograms, moles, number of particles) reacted per unit time per unit volume, can be written in the following form:

$$w = z e^{-E/kT},$$

where the values ​​of $z$ are determined either by the number of collisions (in gases) or by the number of molecular bonds, the rupture of which leads to a chemical transformation (in condensed bodies). (The dimensions of $w$ and $z$ are the same: $k r/(m^3 \cdot c)$, mol/(m$^3 \cdot c$) or $1/(m^3 \cdot c)$.) We are now prepared to explain the phenomenon of thermal explosion. The reason leading to thermal self-ignition is very simple. If a reaction produces heat, the substance heats up. With heating, the rate of chemical reaction increases. This, in turn, should lead to a more intense release of energy. The possibility of such a progressively self-accelerating chemical reaction was pointed out at the end of the last century by the famous Dutch chemist Van't Hoff. The quantity of thermal explosion was given in 1928 by academician N. N. Semenov. It can rightfully be classified as a classic. The theory is simple enough that it can be explained “on fingers”. On the other hand, even now, half a century after the creation of the theory, it remains the basis for the study and solution of more and more new problems in chemical physics - the scientific study of the physical foundations of the chemical process.

Of course, the quick conclusion that any mixture that undergoes an exothermic reaction will eventually explode is incorrect. After all, the vessel in which the reaction occurs is limited and the heat released can be dissipated into the environment. Competitive heat release and heat removal leads to very free behavior of the chemical self-accelerating system.

Let's move on to the quantitative side of the phenomenon. Let there be a reaction in a certain volume $V$ with the heat of removal $Q$ J/kg. The amount of heat released every second in the entire volume is equal to

$$P_+ = z Q V e^{-E/kT} (\text{Дж/c}),$$

where $T$ is the temperature of the reactant in volume $V$.

Let the ambient temperature be constant and equal to $T_0$. Heat losses from the reaction volume are proportional to the difference $T - T_0$ and the area $S$ of the surface limited by the volume $V$. Consequently, every second the reacting substance gives off quantitative heat to the environment

$$P_- = \alpha S (T - T_0),$$

where $\alpha$ is the so-called thermal conductivity coefficient. Naturally, this quantity must contain a characteristic of the ability of a substance to conduct heat - thermal conductivity coefficient $\lambda$ - and a characteristic size of heat - $r$ (for a ball, for example, its radius). Thus,

$$\alpha = K \lambda / r.$$

Coefficient $K$ is a certain constant for a given body, which characterizes the influence of the body’s shape on the thermal conductivity values. It is clear why the size $r$ is included in the denominator: for the same temperature difference between two points of the body, the heat flux (the amount of heat passing per unit time through a unit area) is smaller, the greater the distance between these points.

By increasing the ambient temperature (and, accordingly, the initial temperature of the substance), we will come to a qualitatively different heating regime. Let's see how the heating process proceeds at the initial temperature $T_0^{(3)}$ (curve 3 at figure 4). At the beginning of the process, the dependence $T(t)$ is similar to the two previous cases: the difference $P_+ - P_-$ decreases over time, so the temperature rise is replaced. The system passes through a narrow gap between the heat release and heat flow curves for a very long time - on the temperature dependence $T(t)$ this corresponds to an almost horizontal section. After the difference $P_+ - P_-$ passes through the minimum, a sharp (faster than exponential) increase in temperature begins. A self-accelerating process of energy release will arise. This thermal self-acceleration of an exthermic chemical reaction is called a thermal explosion.

One of the most remarkable aspects of this phenomenon is the existence of a critical condition. We see that a smooth change in $T_0$ leads to a sharp qualitative change in the behavior of the system. Temperature dependence $T(t)$ can be of only two types - either weak heating, or a sharp (after some smooth period) increase in temperature - explosion.

It is obvious that there must be some critical temperature $T_0^{(k)}$ that separates these two types of regimes. Of course, the reader has already drawn attention to the punctuated curve $P_-^{(k)}$ on figure 3, corresponding to the temperature $T_0^{(k)}$. $T_0^{(k)}$ is the critical initial temperature. Curves $P_+$ and $P_-$ have a tangent point (point $C$). If the initial temperature is lower only due to a change in the initial temperature. Functions $P_+$ and $P_-$ also contain other parameters, the change of which transfers the system from a subcritical to a supercritical state (task 3 is devoted to this issue).

Let's now move on to mathematics, that is, we will get the number of critical explosions. It should have the character of some inequality of type $T_0 > T_0^{(k)}$, which would include all the parameters of the problem. We want to find the tangency condition for two curves corresponding to the functions $P_+(T)$ and $P_-(T)$. For high school students familiar with differential calculus, this is a good opportunity to test their knowledge. Those who do not have differentiation must believe in the final result and understand its physical meaning.

In order for the graphs of functions to touch at a given point, two conditions must be met: firstly, the values ​​of the functions at this point must be equal, and secondly, the values ​​of arbitrary functions at this point must coincide.

These two conditions determine the critical temperature, that is, the temperature of the substance that separates the tangency point of the graphs $P_+(T)$ and $P_-(T)$:

$$T_k = T_0 + \frac{kT_0^2}{E}$$

(the value of $T_0$ in this formula is $T_0^{(k)}$).

The value $T_k$ determines the critical thermal explosion:

$$\frac{zQV}{\alpha S} \frac{E}{kT_0^2} e^{-E/kT_0} \geq \frac{1}{e}.$$

(Substituting the value $T_k$ into equality (**) gives the conditions for the critical regime; the “greater than” sign determines explosion.)

Thus, knowing the shape and dimensions of a volume containing a substance with known physicochemical properties, we can predict whether an explosion will occur or not.

Now we can move on to the investigation of the explosions of ships loaded with ammonium nitrate. It has already been said that during its decomposition heat is released, and since the rate of the chemical reaction increases with temperature, then in principle the explosive release of ammonium is possible. However, opportunity is not necessity. Only by turning to numerical estimates can it be established whether a thermal explosion will occur or whether the causes of the disaster should be sought in something else.

Let's rewrite formula (**) in a slightly different form. It is obvious that the ratio $V/\alpha S$ is proportional to the square of the linear size of the body $r^2$. Therefore, the thermal explosion criterion can be written in the form of a condition that determines the critical size of the body $r_k$:

$$r_k = \left( \frac{C\lambda kT_0^2}{zQE} e^{E/kT_0} \right)^{1/2},$$

at $r > r_k$ — explosion.

(The constant $C$ characterizes the influence of body shape.)
