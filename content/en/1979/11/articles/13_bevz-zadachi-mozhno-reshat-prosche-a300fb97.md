Problems can be solved more easily

Those preparing to build universities are asked beforehand:

— Can entrance exams in mathematics offer tasks for which you need to know theorems that are not covered by the school curriculum?
- No, they can’t!

Hearing such an answer, one of my academic classics objected, showing I. Gabovich’s article “Vectors help on the exam” («Kvant», 1979, No. 1):

- Not true. After all, formulas

$$\overrightarrow{BD} = \frac{n}{m+n} \overrightarrow{BA} + \frac{m}{m+n} \overrightarrow{BC},$$

$$\overrightarrow{MN} = \frac{n}{m+n} \overrightarrow{AC} + \frac{m}{m+n} \overrightarrow{BD}$$

They don’t study them at school, but tasks on the application of these formulas are offered at Moscow State University, and at MIPT, and at the Kiev Polytechnic...

Unfortunately, many readers «Kvant» think so and are mistaken.

From the fact that some problems can be solved using some formulas, it does not follow that they cannot be solved without these formulas. For example, consider the tasks from the mentioned article.

Problem 1 (MSU, Faculty of Economics, 1978). In triangle KLM, point A is taken on side KL so that |KA|:|AL|=1:3; on the LM side point B is taken so that |LB|:|BM|=4:1. Let C be the intersection point of lines KB and MA. It is known that

$$S_{KLB} = \frac{4}{5} s, \quad S_{ALM} = \frac{3}{4} s,$$

$$S_{KLC} = 4p, \quad S_{LCB} = 4q,$$

$$S_{KLC} - S_{LCB} = 4p - 4q = 4(p - q) = 4(S_{KLB} - S_{ALM}) = 4 \cdot \frac{1}{20} s = \frac{1}{5} s,$$

$$S_{KLC} + S_{LCB} = S_{KLB} = \frac{4}{5} s.$$

From the last two equalities s=2S_{KLC}=4.

Problem 2 (MIPT, 1977). The base side ABCD of the correct prism ABCD $A_1B_1C_1D_1$ has length 2a, the side edge has length a. We consider segments with ends on the diagonal AD$_1$ of the face and the diagonal DB$_1$ of the prism, parallel to the plane AA$_1B_1B$.

1) One of these segments is drawn through the point M of the diagonal AD$_1$ such that |AM|:|AD$_1$|=2:3. Find its length.

2) Find the shortest length of all segments.

This problem can be solved using the coordinate method.

Solved and e. We will place this prism in a rectangular coordinate system, as shown in figure 2. Through point X of the edge AD, distant from D at a distance x, we draw a plane XTT$_1$X$_1$, parallel to the face ABB$_1$A$_1$. Let R and S be the intersection points of this plane with [AD$_1$] and [DB$_1$]. The abscissas and ordinates of the points R and S can be determined verbally, and the applicates can be determined using the properties of similar triangles ADD$_1$ and AXR, DBB$_1$ and DQS. We have: $R(x; 0; \frac{2a-x}{2})$, $S(x; x; \frac{x}{2})$. So |RS| = $\sqrt{x^2 + (a-x)^2}$.

1) If $R=M$, that is, $|AR|: |AD_1|=2:3$, then $x=\frac{2}{3}a$ and $|RS|= \frac{a}{3}\sqrt{5}$.

2) $|RS|$ will have the smallest value at the smallest value of the function $f(x)=x^2+(a-x)^2$ ($x\in[0;2a]$), that is (derivative!) at $x=\frac{a}{2}$.

Answer. $\min |RS|=\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{2}$.

Task 3 (MIPT, 1978). The length of the edge of a regular metrahedron $ABCD$ is $a$. Point $E$ is the midpoint of edge $CD$, point $F$ is the midpoint of height $BL$ of face $ABD$. The segment $MN$ with ends on the lines $AD$ and $BC$ intersects the line $EF$ and is perpedicular to it. The smallest emodezka.

Solution *). Let's connect the midpoints of the edges $AB, AC, BD$ and $CD$ - we get a square (Indication e. $AD\perp BC$), in which $E$ is one of the vertices, $F$ is the middle of the side (Fig. 3). Straight lines $AD$ and $BC$ are parallel to the plane of this square and “hang” above its middle lines (Fig. 4). Let's project $MN$ onto the square plane. From $MN\perp EF$ it follows that the projection is $M'N' \perp EF$ (Fig. 5). Since the lines $AD$ and $BC$ are at the same distance from the plane of the square, the point $[M'N']\cap[EF]$ divides the segment $M'N'$ in half m. Hence (Fig. 5) $OM'|=\frac{a}{3}$ and $ON'|= \frac{a}{6}$. When projected onto the plane of a square, the midpoints $L$, $P$ of the segments $AD$ and $BC$ fall at the point $O$. Therefore $|LM|=|OM'|=\frac{a}{3},|PN|=|ON'|=\frac{a}{6}$. From the rectal $\triangle APL$ there is $|PL|=\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{2}$. Segments $ML, LP$ and $PN$ are mutually peredicular. Therefore (§ 49 “Geometry 10”) $|MN|= \sqrt{|ML|^2+|LP|^2+|PN|^2}=\frac{a}{6}\sqrt{23}$.

Comment. Many facts concerning a regular metrahedron become obvious if such a metrahedron is “embedded” in a cube (Fig. 6).

Task 4 (Kyiv Polytechnic Institute, 1978). A circle is circumscribed around an equilateral triangle whose side is $a$. Proven *) Proposed by L. G. Makar-Limanov.

Note that the sum of the squares of the distances from an arbitrary point on the circle to the vertices of this triangle is equal to $2a^2$.

Solution (Fig. 7). Let $M$ be an arbitrary point on the circle. Then $\overrightarrow{MA} = \overrightarrow{MO} + \overrightarrow{OA}$, $\overrightarrow{MB} = \overrightarrow{MO} + \overrightarrow{OB}$, $\overrightarrow{MC} = \overrightarrow{MO} + \overrightarrow{OC}$. Considering that $|MO| = |OA| = |OB| = |OC| = R$, we get
$|MA|^2 + |MB|^2 + |MC|^2 = 6R^2 + 2\overrightarrow{MO} \cdot (\overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{OB} + \overrightarrow{OC})$.
Since the triangle $ABC$ is regular, $\overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{OB} + \overrightarrow{OC} = 0$ is, and $R = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}$ is.
Therefore, $|MA|^2 + |MB|^2 + |MC|^2 = 6 \cdot \frac{a^2}{3} = 2a^2$.

This solution is not only simpler than that found in I. Gabovich’s statistic, but also more general. It is easy to generalize to the case of a regular $n$-gon, and not necessarily inscribed in a circle; it can also be described, and not only for planimetric problems. For example, the assumption above is valid for the following stereometric problem: around an equilateral triangle, the side of which is equal to $a$, a sphere is described, the center of which coincides with the center of the given triangle; prove that the sum of the squared distances from an arbitrary point on the sphere to the vertices of this triangle is equal to $2a^2$.
