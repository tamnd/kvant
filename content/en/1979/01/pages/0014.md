When we move the pistons in a cylinder - once at the bottom, and once at the top - their movement is the same. In this case, the work is indeed proportional to the forces. Here Tanya was right. When she decided about bonfires, she was mistaken. I'll try to explain.

The combustion products of the bundles at the bottom and at the top are equal in mass, but not at all the same in volume. At the top, the pressure is less, there the same mass of gas will occupy a significantly larger volume, carrying the surrounding air of the city further. This means that the path along which work is burned against the forces of atmospheric pressure will also be larger. As is known, work during expansion is determined by the product of $p\Delta V$ - the magnitude of the pressure being overcome and the change in volume. The initial volume - the volume of firewood - is disproportionately small compared to the final volume of combustion products, so that the change in volume $\Delta V$ and the final volume $V$ are practically equal: $p\Delta V \approx pV$. But according to the Boyle-Mariotte law, for equal masses of a certain gas, the product $pV$ does not depend on pressure, it is the same both at the top and at the bottom of the hill. Consequently, the work of removal of ambient air is no different.
