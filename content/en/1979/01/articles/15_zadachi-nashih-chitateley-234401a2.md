⟦folio 44⟧

I think that the presence of supporting lines in flat convex figures (similarly, supporting planes in convex bodies), although it is a very important property, will it surprise anyone: it is quite very. The next fact about the city is more surprising.

Theorem. If several convex figures are given on a plane, each three of which have a common point, then there is a point that simultaneously belongs to all these points.

The requirement for figures to be convex exists. Indeed, look at figure 5: of the four figures depicted on it, only one is non-convex; however, although any three figures have a common point, there is no point that belongs simultaneously to all four REM figures.

What if there are several convex figures on the plane such that any two of them have a common point? Is there necessarily a point common to all these figures? Show yourself that such a point may not exist.

Did you notice that we always emphasized that the figures in question are flat? And this is not without reason, because there are four convex bodies in space, each three of which have a common point, but there is no point common to all four REM bodies, for example, the four faces of the triangular paramid shown in figure 6. Does this mean that a similar theorem no longer holds in space? It turns out that in order for it to remain true in space, it only needs to be “tweaked” a little. Namely:

Theorem'. If several convex bodies are given in space, each four of which have a common point, then there is a point that simultaneously belongs to all these points.

The theorem that we have just talked about was discovered not so long ago - in the early 20s of our century by the original mathematician E. Halley and bears his name (Helly formulated it for convex bodies located in n-dimensional space). Helly's theorem can also be considered for convex sets lying on a line (convex sets on a line are individual segments, rays, as well as the entire line and the empty set). On a straight line, Helly's theorem sounds like this:

Theorem'. If several convex sets are given on a line, each two of which have a common point, then there is a point that simultaneously belongs to all these points.

Try to prove this theorem yourself.

If you are interested in convex figures, read the book by I. M. Yaglom and V. G. Boltyansky, which is called: “Convex figures.”
