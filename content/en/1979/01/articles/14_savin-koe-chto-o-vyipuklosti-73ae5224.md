Something
about convexity

While studying geometry at school, you will encounter different planes and spatial figures. So, you already know what a triangle, square, circle, cube, pyramid, ball, cone, cylinder are. Of course, this list is far from exhausting the entire variety of forms that exist in nature. The human imagination can create a huge number of intricate and whimsical forms. Naturally, only a few of these forms have names. And is it necessary to name them all?

When the gardener laments: “Curse the birds, they pecked all my lice!” - he doesn’t care what kind of birds did it - sparrows, druses or a parrot. He talks about creatures that fly, sit on tree branches and are not averse to eating juicy cherries. Did you know that there are 8,616 different species of birds? Is it possible to remember all their names? Of course not. Zoologically, all birds are divided into 40 orders: pigeons, gulls, passerines, etc., combining birds with similar properties into one order. And if you know these forty orders, as well as the two or ten named birds that live in your area, then your acquaintance with birds can already be considered completed.

It’s the same in geometry: you introduce the most frequently occurring figures. Is it possible to identify any interesting aggregates—“orders” of figures? Yes, and this is one of the purposes of geometry. The property that unites the figures into the “order” that we want to talk about is called “convexity”.

The word convex is not new to you. However, try to give this concept a clear definition and you will see that it is not so easy to do. Let's see how it goes

We read: “Arc-shaped - having the shape of an arc.” What is an arc? "An arc is part of a circle, a circle, or a circular curved line." This is where our patience breaks: firstly, curved lines can be very diverse, and secondly, a circle is not a line. Based on this definition of convexity, can anything be said about the convexity (or slightness) of, for example, a cube? In my opinion, it’s impossible. So such a “definition” cannot be arranged by mathematics.

In mathematics, the concept convex has a clearly defined meaning. A set of lobes is called convex if, together with any two of its points A and B, the entire segment [AB] also belongs to this set.

Now it is quite clear that a cube is a convex body. But the figure at figure 1 is not convex. It is not difficult to understand that any triangle is a convex figure. And quadrilaterals can be either convex or small (Fig. 2).

Well, is it possible to say something about convex sets? Can. For example, prove (this is quite simple) that the intersection of two or more convex sets is again a convex set (we will also consider the empty set to be convex). It is very difficult to prove such a statement: through any boundary of a convex figure on a plane one can check a straight line so that the entire figure lies on one side of this straight line. Such a straight line is shown in figure 3; it is called the reference line. If the boundary point is a "corner" point, as in figure 4, several reference lines can be checked through it. Prove on your own that if some plane figure has a supporting line at each boundary point, then this figure is convex. Thus, the formed statement can be taken as the definition of a convex figure.
