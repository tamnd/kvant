The famous story that Newton's discovery of the law of universal gravitation was led by an apple falling from a tree has an interesting history.

S.I. Vavilov, in his excellent biography of Newton, writes that this story is apparently reliable and is not a legend. He refers to the testimony of Stuckley, a close acquaintance of Newton:

“The afternoon (at Newton's in London) the weather was hot; we went into the garden and drank tea under the shade of several apples; it was just the two of us. By the way, Sir Isaac told me that he was in exactly the same region when the idea of ​​gravity first occurred to him. It was caused by an apple falling while he was sitting deep in thought. Why does the apple always fall back, he thought to himself, why not to the side, but always towards the center of the Earth. There must be a pleasant force in the materials, concentrated at the center of the Earth. If materia so secretes other matter, then there must be proportionality to its quantity. Therefore, the apple welcomes the Earth just as the Earth welcomes the apple. There must follow a force similar to that which we call gravity, extending throughout the entire universe."

Obviously, these reflections on gravity date back to 1665 or 1666, when, due to an outbreak of plague in London, Newton was forced to live in the countryside. The following entry was found in Newton’s papers regarding the “plague years**: “... at that time I was at the mercy of my inventive powers and thought about mathematics and philosophy more than ever since.”

Stuckley's testimony was unknown to many (Stackley's memoirs were published only in 1936), but the important French writer Walter, in a book published in 1738 and dedicated to the first popular presentation of Newton's idea, cited the same story with reference to Newton's niece. The story quickly became popular, but caused doubts among many. It was believed that this was another invention of Walter, who was one of the wittiest people of his time. There were people who were even outraged by this story. Among the latter was the great mathematician Gauss. He said:

The great Russian teacher K.D. Ushinsky, on the contrary, saw a deep meaning in the story of the apple. Contrasting Newton with so-called secular people, he wrote:

“It took the genius of Newton to suddenly be surprised that the apple fell to the ground. All-knowing people of the world are not surprised by such “vulgarities”. They even consider surprise at such combined events a sign of a petty, childish, not yet formed practical mind, although at the same time they themselves are often surprised at actual vulgarities.”

The same idea was expressed in a positive form by the Soviet poet Kaisyn Kuliev in his wise poem “Living in Wonder”:

"Great creations are born
Isn't it because there's something wrong somewhere?
Obvious phenomena are surprising
Scientists, artists, poses.”

Here are a few more examples of how the story of the apple was reflected in fiction.

Newton’s correspondent, the great English poet Byron, begins the tenth song of “Don Juan” with the following two lines *):

“The apple, having fallen, listened to Newton’s deep thoughts, And they say (I will not answer for the sages of the treaty and scientists), He found in this a way to prove very clearly the force of gravity. With the fall, therefore, and the apple, only he Was able to cope with Adam's times.

Finally, Mark Twain gave the whole episode a humorous twist. In the story “When I Served as a Secretary” he writes:

“What are words? A creation of chance! Sir Isaac Newton discovered that apples fall to the ground - pure word, such trifling discoveries were made by millions of people before him. But Newton had influential parents, and they turned this trivial incident into an extraordinary event, and the simpletons took up their cry. And then in an instant Newton became famous.”
