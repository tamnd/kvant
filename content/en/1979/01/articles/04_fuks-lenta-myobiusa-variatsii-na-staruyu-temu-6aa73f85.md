Mobinus strip.

Variation on an old theme

This article again brings us back to a remarkable representative of one-sided surfaces - the Möbinus strip (see also «Kvant»: 1974, no. 3; 1975, no. 7; 1978, no. 5 and no. 6). It turns out from a narrow strip of paper twisted 180° and glued into a ring. Here we discuss the question of which strip is suitable for making a model of a Möbinus strip.

I will not suggest that you run your finger along the Mobinus strip or cut it along the midline. We are talking about prophetic, less known things. I'll start with a question:

1. What shape of paper strip should you take to glue a Möbinus strip?

Expected answer: the strip should be narrow and long, with the possibility of a length-to-width ratio. Let's fold it, we won't make a Möbinus strip from a square sheet.

This is true, but with one caveat that is easy to underestimate: size restrictions only matter if the paper is prohibited from being wrinkled. If crumpling the paper is not prohibited, then a Möbinus strip can be glued not only from a square, but from a rectangle of any size—the glued sides can even be angularly times the length of the non-glued ones.

You can do it like this (Fig. 1). Fold the straight sheet into an accordion and bend it an even number of times. Then from this accordion, like from a thick paper strip, we glue a Möbinus strip, placing the corresponding parts of the accordion into each other. From figure 1 it is clear that the sheet of paper from which the Möbinus strip was glued turned out to be crumpled.

Let us now assume that the paper strip can be bent, but not wrinkled. Moreover, the width of the strip is per unit. It is clear that the longer the strip, the easier it is to glue a Möbinus strip from it. Thus, there is any number λ such that a Möbinus strip can be glued from a strip of length greater than λ, but not from a strip of length less than λ (we are not interested in what will happen for a strip whose length is exactly equal to λ). We really wanted to find this λ.

Surprisingly, the solution to this problem is still unknown.

2. An uncrumpled sheet of paper is a “developable surface”

Since the requirement of not crumpling the paper is so important, then what is its mathematical meaning.

It is easy to understand that prohibiting paper creasing means limiting the ability to manipulate a sheet of paper. For example, a sheet of paper, without being wrinkled, can be rolled into a tube or folded in half without a fold, but cannot be folded into four (Fig. 2). You can make a cone (“funtik”) from a sheet of paper without crumpling it, but you cannot make a sphere or even a piece of it (Fig. 3): press the sheet of paper to the globe, and folds will certainly appear. As you can see, a sheet of paper cannot be given any shape.

Mathematicians call surfaces that can be made from a sheet of paper by bending it without crushing it, developing it. (Examples of developable surfaces are shown in figure 4.) Of course, in mathematics, developable surfaces are defined differently: in mathematical language there are no words “paper”, “crumple”, “make”. There is a whole theory of developing surfaces, among the achievements of which is a satisfactory answer to the question of what they are

1. Through each point A of the developable surface that does not lie on its boundary, we pass a segment lying on the surface that does not end at A. In other words, at each point, a knitting needle can be attached to the developable surface (a curved but not crumpled sheet of paper) so that it is adjacent to the surface for some extent on both sides of the taken point. Such a segment is called a generator of the surface (let us agree that this name applies only to segments of maximum length that lie entirely on the surface, that is, to segments that are not contained in larger segments with this property).

2. If two different generators pass through a point A that does not lie on the boundary of a surface, and A is not the end of either of them, then a sufficiently small piece of the surface surrounding A is flat. In this case, we will call point A flat.

3. If a point A, which does not lie on the boundary of the surface, is the end of some generator, say a, then the flat points make up the points A is arranged like this. On my side of the generatrix b, with which the generatrix a is located, a flat piece is adjacent to the generatrix b, on the other side of b, as close to point A, there are non-flat points. In this situation we will call point A semi-flat.

By emphasizing that if a point on a surface is neither boundary nor flat, then a single generatrix that does not end at it passes through it, and the ends of this generatrix lie on the boundary of the surface.

Examples of measures. A sheet of paper, rolled into a tube or into a pound, has no flat (or semi-flat) points. 3. Theorem 1: $\lambda \geqslant \frac{\pi}{2}$

Proof. Let a Möbius strip be made of a paper strip of length $l$. We wrap a length of paper tape around it. This tape (we neglect the thickness of the paper) will be composed of rectangles of the same length, each of which takes the shape of our Möbius strip. Let us mark rectilinear generators and flat points on the long tape (as in figure 66). The result will be something like figure 7. The picture is periodic: everything repeats with a period equal to 2l. You can say more: when you shift left or right by $l$, the picture changes, but in a strictly defined way; namely, it turns over (that is, it is mirrored in the middle line of the strip). The areas of flat points are quadrilaterals (which can degenerate into triangles) bounded by two segments of opposite edges of the tape and two segments passing along the tape. The parts of the tape that do not fall into these areas are kneaded with generatrices, the ends of which lie on the edges of the tape. (All this follows from properties 1-3 of developable surfaces given in paragraph 2.) Flat areas can also be replaced by generators, so that the entire tape will be covered by a continuous family of generators (Fig. 8). Generators in identical quadrangles can be chosen in the same way, so that the periodicity described above is preserved.

Let's take any generator from our family, say, $[AB]$. If we cut it symmetrically in the middle line of the strip and then move it in any direction (say, to the right) to $l$, we get the segment $CD$, which is also a generator from our family (Fig. 9). Note (this is important) that $|AC| + |BD| = 2l$. When winding our length of tape onto a Möbius strip, the generators $[AB]$ and $[CD]$ will take the same position, with the point $A$ aligned with $D$, and the point $B$ with $C$; in other words, the segments $AB$ and $CD$ will make an angle of $180^\circ$ in space. Between $[AB]$ and $[CD]$ there is a continuous family of generators. When moving from $[AB]$ to $[CD]$, the magnitude of the angle that these generators make in the space $[AB]$ continuously changes from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$. 4. Theorem 2: $\lambda \leq \sqrt{3}$

This theorem is simpler than the previous one: to prove it, it is enough to explain how to glue a Möbinus strip from a strip whose length is greater than $\sqrt{3}$. Let us first assume a triangle (Fig. 12). Let's fold the strip along the sides of these triangles, through the fold directions (Fig. 13; take scissors and paper and do this). The edges $AB$ and $CD$ of the strips will align, with point $A$ aligned with point $D$ and point $B$ with point $C$. The result is a Möbinus strip.

In this construction, the main rule was violated - do not wrinkle the paper. But it is easy to understand that if the length of the strip is even slightly greater than $\sqrt{3}$, then the break along the generatrix can be replaced by bending,

5. Why can't we find λ more accurately?

Until the problem is solved, it is difficult to say why it is not solved. Nevertheless, sometimes in various unsolved problems it is possible to trace common difficulties, to mark, so to speak, difficult places on a mathematical map, which sometimes makes it possible to imagine success or failure in solving a particular problem.

In the previous section we proved that λ is one of the points of the segment [π/2; √3] . Which one? Perhaps it is possible to formulate at least a plausible hypothesis on this score? I think that λ = √3, and I am not surprised that this could not be proven.

The point is this. The proof of Theorem 1 leaves unused one important property of our Möbius strip - its lack of self-intersections. The self-intersection of a tape cannot be made of paper, but it can be imagined: like the self-intersection of a line on a plane, it “passes through itself,” and it can be divided into parts, each of which does not have self-intersections.

Let's assume that, speaking of paper Möbius strips, we allowed them to have self-intersections from the very beginning. Then λ takes on a new meaning - the new value of λ will be less than or equal to the previous one. In this case, Theorem 1 will remain true, and not a single word will have to be changed in its proof: the absence of self-intersection is not used in this proof. As for Theorem 2, if self-intersections are allowed, it can be valid. Exactly:

Theorem 3. A Möbius strip with self-intersections can be glued together from a strip of any length greater than λ/2.

It's done like this. Take a sufficiently large odd n and construct

Thus, if we wanted (for tapes without self-intersections) to prove that $\lambda \geq \mu$, where $\mu > \sqrt{3}$, we would have to take into account the absence of self-intersections in the proof. The beginning or absence of self-intersections of a particular figure in three-dimensional space is a problem of “three-dimensional geometry of arrangement.” All the experience of mathematics shows that problems about location in three-dimensional space are very difficult. After all, this geometry includes, say, the theory of knots and gears, known for its inaccessibility («Kvant», 1975, No. 7, p. 6); in it such phenomena as “Alexader’s horned sphere” arose («Kvant», 1977, No. 7, p. 22); Its simplest questions, for example, whether it is possible to turn a torus inside out through a small hole, baffle our intuition. 6. Conclusion: about one similar problem

Let's glue together from a paper strip not a Möbius strip, but a cylinder (the strip can be any size). Is it possible to turn the cylinder inside out without creasing the paper?

inside out? If the cylinder is wide and low, then, of course, it is possible, but if it is narrow and high, then it is impossible. Let us denote by σ such a number that if the ratio of the length of the strip to the width is greater than σ, then the cylinder can be turned out, but if it is less, then it is impossible. I leave the proofs of the following three theorems (more difficult than Theorems 1, 2, 3) to you as a task:

Theorem 1'. $\sigma \geq \pi$.

Theorem 2'. $\sigma \leq \pi + 2$.

Theorem 3'. Let us assume that self-intersections are allowed during the eversion process. Then any cylinder made from a strip whose length to width ratio is greater than π can be turned inside out (without creasing the paper).
