Let's burn energy!

Conversation among physics lovers

Yu ra. Let's discuss this problem:
Alpinsit and the tourist stocked up on the same bundles of brushwood. The tourist laid out his cosmere at the foot of the hill, and his alpinsit at the top. The latter, I must admit, had a great time while he was camouflaging the fagot up the hill. Speaking in the language of physics, alpinsite performed mechanical work, imparting potential energy with its shot. So where did this energy go when the brushwood burned?

Kolya. The energy could not burn out. One piece of wood burned, and its potential energy was necessarily transformed into something else.

Yu ra. Which one exactly?

Kolya. Obviously, the energy of combustion products - ash and smoke. After all, being at the top, they also have potential energy relative to the foot of the hill.

Tanya. The ash can indeed then be thrown down and forced to do mechanical work. And here's the smoke...

Kolya. Well, the smoke rushes high and is capable of lifting something. Let's fill a big ball with this smoke, attach a basket...

Tanya. All this could just as easily have been accomplished by the smoke from a tourist’s fire - it would have been no worse. What then is the additional energy of that fagot that burns on the top of the hill? There is no point in talking about ash: its mass is insignificant compared to the mass of burnt firewood. And if you replace a bundle of brushwood with a canister of kerosene, then there will be no ash at all.

Kolya. Apparently I was in a hurry. Why does hot smoke rush upward? Because it is lighter than air. In its place, heavier cold air descends. Because of this, various complications can arise. In order not to get confused in them, let's imagine that everything happens not on Earth, but on the Moon...

Tanya. How can fires burn on the Moon, where there is no air?

Kolya. Let them burn not fires, but special burners: acetylene in one cylinder, oxygen in the other, both liquefied. Where both jets meet, the fire burns. Combustion products, even

Tanya. Let's try to figure it out. Remember, floating a ball in water does not violate the law of conservation of energy. True, the potential energy of the ball increases as it floats up. But it grows due to a decrease in the potential energy of the water that falls to the place of the lifting ball. Since the mass of the sinking water is greater, its potential energy is also sufficient to increase the kinetic energy of the ball and to do work against the forces of liquid friction (with corresponding heating of the ball and water). In a word, due to the potential energy of the lowering water, the potential, kinetic, and internal energy of the ball, as well as the internal energy of the water surrounding the ball, are replenished. Everything here is completely clear.

Kolya. Our case is not so simple: before sorting there was a log that “sank” in the air, but the products of sorting “float” in it! How to understand the energy transformation of “sinking” into “floating”?

Tanya. Wouldn't a mechanical model in the form of a cylinder (pipe) immersed in water with two pistons (Fig. 1) help us? When the pistons are spread out, the cylinder floats, when they are pushed down, it sinks. And in both cases, he is capable of voluntary work - for example, lifting loads (Fig. 2). When the cylinder is near the bottom, we expand the pistons, and when it floats to the surface, we push it down. So the cylinder moves periodically up and down...

Kolya. Is the “perpetual motion machine” going to pop up!?

Tanya. Not at all: in order to move the pistons deep under water, work must be done against the forces of hydrostatic pressure. And for this you need a source of energy.

Kolya. I think I caught a similarity with our fagots. Firewood “sinks” in the air, and smoke “floats” because the volume of smoke in the city is greater. This means that the smoke generated during combustion will cut off the surrounding air in all directions, while doing work against the forces of atmospheric pressure. This work can only be accomplished using part of the chemical energy of the burning fuel. And only the rest of this energy goes to increase the temperature.

Tanya. Once the analogy has been placed, let’s take it to its logical conclusion. We burn one bundle at the bottom, and two rise to the top on their own - just like a cylinder floats up when the pistons are spread out.

Yu ra. Is it not in vain that we believed in this difference in the work carried out by smoke in the surrounding air below and at altitude? Work depends not only on strength, but also on the path. When we move the pistons in a cylinder - once at the bottom, and once at the top - their movement is the same. In this case, the work is indeed proportional to the forces. Here Tanya was right. When she decided about bonfires, she was mistaken. I'll try to explain.

The combustion products of the bundles at the bottom and at the top are equal in mass, but not at all the same in volume. At the top, the pressure is less, there the same mass of gas will occupy a significantly larger volume, carrying the surrounding air of the city further. This means that the path along which work is burned against the forces of atmospheric pressure will also be larger. As is known, work during expansion is determined by the product of $p\Delta V$ - the magnitude of the pressure being overcome and the change in volume. The initial volume - the volume of firewood - is disproportionately small compared to the final volume of combustion products, so that the change in volume $\Delta V$ and the final volume $V$ are practically equal: $p\Delta V \approx pV$. But according to the Boyle-Mariotte law, for equal masses of a certain gas, the product $pV$ does not depend on pressure, it is the same both at the top and at the bottom of the hill. Consequently, the work of removal of ambient air is no different.

Yura. To be fair, it must be said that the fictitious analogy with cylinders also correctly reflects our “problem of fires,” but in a different version. Tanya. In fact, we have figured out three different combustion processes: in the absence of air (on the Moon), inside closed shells and in the earth’s atmosphere. It turned out that the energy imparted to the fuel during its rise to the top has a specific application every time. In the first case, it replenishes the potential energy of combustion products, and in the second, it provides additional heat release during combustion, and in the third, it goes to increase the internal energy of the rising exhaust.
