A door loaded with a spring moves with its roots because it is influenced by the spring. A piece of iron moves with its roots under the influence of a magnet. But do these influences influence one another? It is clear that they are completely different in nature. At the time of Newton, nothing was known about the nature of all these influences. But in mechanics, it turns out, you may not even know this. The only important thing is that the effect on the body is that rooting is imparted to the body. This means that influences (forces) that are completely different in nature can be compared with each other if we are able to express them in numbers. And for this it is necessary to establish how the forces are connected with the roots communicated by them.

Newton's second law
This connection was established by Newton from the analysis of experimental data. He formulated it in the form of the second law of motion (Newton's second law).

As Newton himself stated, this law states: “The change in motion is proportional to the applied driving force and has the direction in which this force acts.”

This formulation contains the basic idea of ​​mechanics: force causes a change in motion, and not the motion itself, as they thought before Newton (and as people who have not studied mechanics still think). However, the words “change in movement” are somewhat mysterious: after all, movement is a process, not a quantity; Only quantities can be proportional to each other. It is also unclear how Newton knew that there was just such a law - the law of proportionality between force and change in motion. Of course, this law was established on the basis of experience or experiences. But which ones? Newton does not report this. He simply “tells” bodies to behave one way and not another.

We can now assume that the words “change in motion” are familiar, since rooting represents a rapidly changing speed of movement of a body if its mass is constant *). As for the experience from which the connection between rooting and force can be established, it must consist in the fact that different bodies (different in mass) are supported by the action of the same force; At the same time, it is necessary to change the roots that these bodies receive. The quantity that turns out to be the same for all bodies in these changes will serve as a measure of the force applied to these bodies.

In nature there is one and only force, which does not depend on the body to which it is applied. This is the elastic force of a deformed body, such as a spring. This force is called the elastic force. The peculiarity of the elastic force is that it depends only on how deformed the body is. Thus, the elastic force of a spring depends only on its stretching (or rolling) compared to its original length (this fact was studied in Newton’s time). Therefore, if you keep the tension of the spring constant (fixed), then it acts on any body attached to it with the same force, regardless of what the mass of this body is (of course, it is necessary that the mass of the force of the spring be inconvertibly small compared to the mass of the body attached to it).

So, the experiment that allows the connection between rooting and force can only be carried out using a spring. This experience is described in the textbook “Physics 8” (p. 85). It turns out that a spring, rolled (or stretched) to a fixed length, imparts roots to the bodies attached to it, the directions of which always coincide with the direction of action of the elastic force on the bodies, and the absolute values ​​are different for a body with different masses.

*) In modern books they usually write, as if “correcting” Newton, together “change in motion” - “change in momentum.” In this case, the quantity of motion is understood as the product of a body’s mass and its speed (this quantity is more often called impulse). It is not difficult to understand that this interpretation of Newton’s words does not contradict the one we accept.
