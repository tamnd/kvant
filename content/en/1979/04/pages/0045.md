Movement of charged particles in electric and magnetic fields

The action of electric and magnetic fields on charged particles leads to many interesting phenomena and is widely used in modern technology. This, for example, is the control of the movement of electrons in various electrovacuum devices and the acceleration of charged particles to high energies, the focusing of particles using electrostatic and magnetic lines, and the separation of particles according to their distant charges in mass spectrographs.

Let us limit ourselves to considering the simplest cases - the movement of charged particles in uniform and stationary (independent of time) electric and magnetic fields.

In an electric field with direction $\vec{E}$, particles with charge $q$ and mass $m$ are acted upon by an electric force

$$\vec{F}_{\text{эл}} = q\vec{E},$$

which imparts acceleration to the particle

$$\vec{a} = \frac{\vec{F}_{\text{эл}}}{m} = \frac{q}{m}\vec{E}.$$

This acceleration is directed along the field if $q > 0$, and against the field if $q < 0$. Consequently, the positive part of the particle begins to move equally along the field, and the negative part begins to move against the field.

Let a particle move from a point with potential $\varphi_1$ to a point with potential $\varphi_2$. At the same time, its kinetic energy changed by the amount

$$\Delta W_{\kappa} = -q(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1).$$

This is why it is convenient to change the energy of charged particles in electron volts (1 eV is the energy that a particle with a charge equal to the charge of an electron acquires when passing through a potential difference of one volt).

A moving charged particle in a magnetic field is acted upon by a magnetic force called the Lorentz force $\vec{F}_{\pi}$. Her model

$$|\vec{F}_{\pi}| = q|\vec{v}||\vec{B}| \sin \alpha,$$

where $q$ is the charge of the particle, $\vec{v}$ is its speed, $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field induction and $\alpha$ is the angle between the vectors $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$. A characteristic feature of this force is that it is always perpendicular to the directional movement of the particle and the induction of the magnetic field.

Since the Lorentz force is perpendicular to the velocity of the particle, the work done by this force is zero. Consequently, if no other forces act on the particles, when moving in a magnetic field, the energy of the particle will remain constant *).
