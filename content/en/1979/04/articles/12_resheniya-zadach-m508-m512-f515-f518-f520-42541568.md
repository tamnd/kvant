M508. A circle tangent to three semicircles with diameters [AB], [BC] and [AC] (С∈[AB], Fig. 1). Let us prove that the radius of the input circle is less than the distance from its center to the spicy AB.

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

Fig. 3.

Let $O_1$ be the center of semicircle $\gamma_1$ with diameter [AC], $O_2$ be the center of semicircle $\gamma_2$ with diameter [BC], $O_3$ be the center of semicircle $\gamma_3$ with diameter [AB], and finally $O$ be the center of circle $\gamma$ tangent to the three data semicircles (Fig. 1). Let us denote the lengths of the radii of the circles $\gamma_1, \gamma_2$ and $\gamma$, corresponding by $r$, $R$ and $x$. Then the length of the radius of the semicircle $\gamma_3$ is equal to $R + r$.

Consider the triangle $O_1OO_2$. We have: $|O_1O| = r + x$, $|OO_2| = R + x$, $|O_1O_2| = R + r$. Let us denote the distance from the center $O$ to the spicy AB by $h$. Having written the area of ​​the triangle $O_1OO_2$ using Heron’s formula and through the length of the base $[O_1O_2]$ and the height dropped from the vertex $O$, we obtain the equation

$$\sqrt{Rrx(R + r + x)} = \frac{1}{2}(R + r)h.$$

A similar equation for triangle $O_1OO_3$ has the form

$$\sqrt{rx(R + r)(R - r)} = \frac{1}{2}Rh.$$

By squaring the left and right sides of the general equations and then subtracting from one equation the other, we get

$$rx[R(R + r + x) - (R + r)|(R - r)] = \frac{1}{4}h^2[(R + r)^2 - R^2],$$

whence $x^2 = h^2/4$, that is, $x = h/2$, which is what was required.

This problem can be solved more thoroughly if we apply inversion (about inversion and its properties, see “Quant” - 1974, No. 1, p. 16, 1976, No. 2, p. 34, 1977, No. 6, p. 38).

Let us invert $I$ with respect to an arbitrary circle with center at point $C$. With this inversion, circles $\gamma_1, \gamma_2, \gamma_3$ and $\gamma$ are transformed as shown in figure 2: circles $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ will transform, correspondingly, into straight lines $l_1$ and $l_2$, perpendicular to [AB], and circles $\gamma_3$ and $\gamma'$, the corresponding lines $l_1$ and $l_2$, and the center of the circle $\gamma'$ belongs to spicy AB (this line is highlighted in red in Figures 1 and 2).

It is easy to report that circles $\gamma$ and $\gamma' = I(\gamma)$ are prepared with the center of preparation $C$. This can be seen from figure 3: the tangents to the circle $\gamma$ passing through the point $C$, when inverted, $I$ turns into itself and is tangent to the circle $\gamma' = I(\gamma)$. Let us denote the radius of the circle $\gamma'$ by $x'$, and by $d$ the distance from the center of the circle $\gamma'$ to the spicy AB. Since $\gamma$ and $\gamma'$ are cooked, we have $h/d = x/x'$ (h is the distance from the center of circle $\gamma$ to the spicy AB, x is the radius of $\gamma$). But, obviously, $x' = d/2$ (circles $\gamma'$ and $\gamma'_3$ are the same radius, see Fig. 2), so $x = h/2$, which is what was required.

M509. Solution in natural numbers to the equation:
a) $2^x + 1 = 3^y$;
b) $z^x + 1 = (z + 1)^2$;
c) $z^x + 1 = (z + 1)^y$.

It is easy to see that equations a) and b) are special cases of equation b) for $z = 2$ and $y = 2$, respectively. We will consider cases a) separately and then solve equation b).
a) If $x = 1$, then also $y = 1$.
Let $x > 1$. Then $2^x + 1 \equiv 1$ (mod 4), which means that $y$ is an even number (if $y$ is odd, then $3^y \equiv 3$ (mod 4), and

equality $2^x + 1 = 3^y$ is impossible). So, $y = 2k, 2^x = 3^{2k} - 1$, that is $2^x = (3^k - 1)(3^k + 1)$. Since $x > 1$, both the factors $3^k - 1$ and $3^k + 1$ must be powers of two (differing from each other by 2). This is only possible with $k = 1$. Therefore $y = 2$, so $2^x + 1 = 9$, and $x = 3$.

So, control a) has two solutions: (1; 1) and (3; 2).
b) The control solution b) will be carried out in several stages.
Let $z$ have a prime divisor $p$: $z = p^m z_1, m \geq 1, z_1$ and $p$. Let $y = p^n y_1$, where $n \geq 0$ and GCD $(y_1, p) = 1$.

Consider the expression $(1 + z)^{y_1}$. We have:

$$(1 + z)^{y_1} = 1 + y_1 z + C_{y_1}^2 z^2 + \ldots + z^{y_1} =$$

$$= 1 + z \left( y_1 + C_{y_1}^2 z + \ldots + z^{y_1} - 1 \right).$$

Since $y_1$ is not divisible by $p$ (we will write it like this: $y_1 \neq 0$ (mod $p$)), we get

$$(1 + z)^{y_1} = 1 + p^m A,$$ where $A \equiv y_1 z_1$ (mod $p$).

Let us now consider $(1 + z)^y$. Let us prove the following auxiliary statement (true, however, under some restrictions): for any natural number $B$

$$(1 + p^i B)^p = 1 + p^{i+1} D(i > 0),$$

with $B \equiv D$ (mod $p$). In fact, let's apply Newton's biome formula:

$$(1 + p^i B)^p = 1 + C_p^1 \cdot p^i B + C_p^2 \cdot p^{2i} B^2 + \ldots$$

$$\ldots + p^{i_p} B^p = 1 + p^{i+1} B + C_p^2 \cdot p^{2i} B^2 + \ldots + p^{i_p} B^p.$$

It is easy to see that if $2i > i + 1$, that is, $i > 1$, then the statement is true. If $i = 1$, then the coefficient of $p^{i+1}$ will be equal to $B + C_p^2 B^2$. If $p$ is odd, then $C_p^2$ is divisible by $p$, so $B + C_p^2 B^2 \equiv B$ (mod $p$), otherwise the statement for odd $p$ is true in this case as well.

Let $z$ have an odd prime divisor $p$. Let us apply the proven statement to the expression $(1 + z)^y$. We have:

$$(1 + z)^y = ((1 + z)^{y_1})^{p^n} = (1 + p^m A)^{p^n} =$$

$$= (1 + p^{m+1} A_1)^{p^{n-1}} = \ldots = 1 + p^{m+n} A_n,$$

and $A \equiv A_1 \equiv \ldots \equiv A_n$ (mod $p$), that is, $A_n$ is not divisible by $p$. We are looking for control solution b): $(1 + z)^y = 1 + z^x$, or $p^{m+n} A_n = p^{mx} z_1^x$. Since $z_1 \neq 0$ (mod $p$), the comparison $m + n = mx$ must perform.

On the other hand, it is very indo that $x \geq y$. If $x > y$, then $x > p^n$, hence $m + n > mp^n \geq m (1 + n (p - 1))$, and $n > mn (p - 1)$, which is impossible.

The case when $z$ has no odd divisors, but is divisible by 4, is broken down similarly. Thus, if $x > 0$, then equation b) cannot have solutions $(x; y; z)$ in natural numbers such that $z$ is divisible by an odd number or by 4. The case $z = 2$ is analyzed in part a). It remains to consider the case when $z = 1$ and the case $x = y$.

If $z = 1$, we obtain the equation $1^x + 1 = 2^y, 2^y = 2$, from which $y = 1$, $x$ are any.

In the case of $x = y$ we have:

$$z^x + 1 = (z + 1)^x,$$

that is

$$1 = (z + 1)^x - z^x = [(z + 1) - z] \times$$

$$\times [(z + 1)^{x-1} + (z + 1)^{x-2} z + \ldots + z^{x-1}] =$$

$$= (z + 1)^{x-1} + (z + 1)^{x-2} z + \ldots + z^{x-1} \geq x.$$

Thus, $x = y = 1$, $z$ are any.

Fig. 4.

Let's carry out $(NC) \parallel (BM)$ and $(BN) \parallel MC$ (Fig. 4). Then the quadrilaterals $BNCM$ and $ANCD$ are parallelograms, which means that $\overrightarrow{NAB} = \overrightarrow{NCB}$ and the points $A, B, N, C$ lie on the same circle. Therefore $\overrightarrow{BCM} = \overrightarrow{ACD}$, which is what needed to be proven.

Let us present another solution to this problem, based on the fact that the quadrilaterals $ABCD$ and $MB'CD'$ are similar; here $B' = (BM) \cap (DC), D' = (DM) \cap (BC)$. Indeed, these quadrilaterals have a promising angle $C$, and the remaining angles are pairwise contiguous: $\overrightarrow{A} = \overrightarrow{M}, \overrightarrow{B} = \overrightarrow{B'}, \overrightarrow{D} = \overrightarrow{D'}$. However, concussive angles are not enough to resemble quadrilaterals. Note now that the triangles $BD'M$ and $DMB'$ are similar. From this similarity and the fact that the quadrilateral $ABMD$ is a parallelogram, the equality follows

$$\frac{|MB'|}{|AB|} = \frac{|MD'|}{|AD|},$$

from which the similarity of the quadrilaterals $ABCD$ and $MB'CD'$ already follows. From this similarity it follows in turn that $\overrightarrow{ACD} = \overrightarrow{MCD'}(= \overrightarrow{MCB})$.

**M512. Let $f(x) = x^3 - x + 1$. Prove that any natural numbers $m > 1$ $m, f(m), f(f(m))$, $f(f(f(m)))$, ... are pairwise relatively prime.

Let us put $f_1(x) = x^3 - x + 1$, and for each $n = 2, 3, \dots, f_n(x) = f(f_{n-1}(x))$.

Then $f_1(0) = f_1(1) = 1$ and (by induction) $f_n(0) = f_n(1) = 1$ for each $n$. Thus, the free term $f_n(0)$ of the polynomial $f_n(x)$ with integer coefficients is equal to 1. Therefore, for any natural number $n$ and integer $a$, the number $f_n(a)$ gives remainder 1 when divided by $a$. It follows that for any numbers $k > l > 0$ and $m$ the number $f_k(m) = f_{k-1}(f_l(m))$ when divided by $f_l(m)$ leaves a remainder of 1, so $f_k(m)$ and $f_l(m)$ are coprime.

F515. A lectic rod of length l is fixed in a vertical plane on an axis passing through point O; we divide the rod in a relative ratio of 1:3. A soft ball of mass m is attached to one end of the rod, the other end of the rod is attached to a horizontal spring of stiffness k (Fig. 5). The pressure does not stretch when the rod is vertical. Let us define nonPIO of small vibrations of the rod.

Fig. 5.

The displacement of the upper and lower ends of the rod from the vertical during vibrations will be denoted by the correspondence of x₁ and x₂. Then
x₁ = l/4 sin φ, x₂ = 3l/4 sin φ.

The projections of the forces acting on the ball onto the X axis, tangent to the trajectory of the ball (Fig. 5), are equal to - m|g| sin φ and - |F'|, where F' is the force of the normal reaction of the rod. Since the rod is non-isom, the moments of forces F and F' are equal. Hence,
|F'|/3 l = |F|/4 l,
where
|F'| = kx₁/3 = 1/12 kl sin φ.

Now let's write down the equation of motion of the matrix:
- m|g| sin φ - |F'| = ma_T,
or
1/m (kl/12 sin φ + m|g| sin φ) = ω₀² 3l/4 sin φ.

Expressing the cyclic frequency ω₀ through the period T and reducing by sin φ, we find T:
T = 6π √(kl + 12m|g|).

Rays passing without refraction from point A to point A' and from point B to point B' pass through the optical center of the line. Therefore, point O is the optical center of the line (see Fig. 6).
