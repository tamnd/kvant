Movement of charged particles in electric and magnetic fields

The action of electric and magnetic fields on charged particles leads to many interesting phenomena and is widely used in modern technology. This, for example, is the control of the movement of electrons in various electrovacuum devices and the acceleration of charged particles to high energies, the focusing of particles using electrostatic and magnetic lines, and the separation of particles according to their distant charges in mass spectrographs.

Let us limit ourselves to considering the simplest cases - the movement of charged particles in uniform and stationary (independent of time) electric and magnetic fields.

In an electric field with direction $\vec{E}$, particles with charge $q$ and mass $m$ are acted upon by an electric force

$$\vec{F}_{\text{эл}} = q\vec{E},$$

which imparts acceleration to the particle

$$\vec{a} = \frac{\vec{F}_{\text{эл}}}{m} = \frac{q}{m}\vec{E}.$$

This acceleration is directed along the field if $q > 0$, and against the field if $q < 0$. Consequently, the positive part of the particle begins to move equally along the field, and the negative part begins to move against the field.

Let a particle move from a point with potential $\varphi_1$ to a point with potential $\varphi_2$. At the same time, its kinetic energy changed by the amount

$$\Delta W_{\kappa} = -q(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1).$$

This is why it is convenient to change the energy of charged particles in electron volts (1 eV is the energy that a particle with a charge equal to the charge of an electron acquires when passing through a potential difference of one volt).

A moving charged particle in a magnetic field is acted upon by a magnetic force called the Lorentz force $\vec{F}_{\pi}$. Her model

$$|\vec{F}_{\pi}| = q|\vec{v}||\vec{B}| \sin \alpha,$$

where $q$ is the charge of the particle, $\vec{v}$ is its speed, $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field induction and $\alpha$ is the angle between the vectors $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$. A characteristic feature of this force is that it is always perpendicular to the directional movement of the particle and the induction of the magnetic field.

Since the Lorentz force is perpendicular to the velocity of the particle, the work done by this force is zero. Consequently, if no other forces act on the particles, when moving in a magnetic field, the energy of the particle will remain constant *).

Problem 3. A bunch of singly charged positive ions Li$^+$(A=6) is emitted by emmeter “E”.
The nonons are accelerated by the electric field $u$, passing through a potential difference $U=3000$ V, and enter a chamber with a transverse magnetic field $|\vec{B}| = 3 \cdot 10^{-2}$ T (Fig. 4). Find the beam deflection values ​​$h$. Chamber length $L=15$ cm, electron charge $e=1,6 \cdot 10^{-19}$ C, promon mass $m_p=1,67 \cdot 10^{-24}$ e.

Having passed through the accelerating potential difference $U$, each one acquires kinetic energy

$$\frac{m|\vec{v}|^2}{2} = eU,$$

where $m$ is the mass of the ion, $\vec{v}$ is its speed. From here

$$|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{\frac{2eU}{m}}.$$

At this speed he enters a chamber where only a magnetic field exists. The Lorentz force begins to act on it, which will impart centripetal acceleration to it. Since the Lorentz force at each moment is perpendicular to the speed of the ion, it will move in a circle. The radius of this circle can be found from the equation of motion of the ion

$$\frac{m|\vec{v}|^2}{R} = e|\vec{v}||\vec{B}|.$$

Taking into account the fact that $|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{\frac{2eU}{m}}$, we get

$$R = \frac{m|\vec{v}|}{e|\vec{B}|} = \frac{1}{|\vec{B}|} \sqrt{\frac{2mU}{e}}.$$

The amount of beam deflection $h$ is found from simple geometric calculations (see Fig. 4):

$$h = R - \sqrt{R^2 - L^2} \approx 0,02 \text{ м}.$$

Problem 4. A weakly diverging electron beam leaves the point $O$ with a speed $v$ and moves in a longitudinal magnetic field with induction $\vec{B}$ (Fig. 5). At what values ​​of the magnetic induction model will the beam be focused onto the screen “E” located at a distance $L$ from point $O$?

Let us consider the motion of an individual electron whose velocity vector lies in the figure 5 plane and makes an angle $\alpha$ with the vector $\vec{B}$.

Since the electron is only affected by the Lorentz force, the projection of which on the direction of the field is zero, the projection of the electron velocity on this direction will remain constant and equal to $|\vec{v}| \cos \alpha$.

In a plane perpendicular to the vector $\vec{B}$, the electron will move in a circle with a linear speed equal to $|\vec{v}| \sin \alpha$. Let us write down the equation of motion of an electron in a circle:

$$\frac{m(|\vec{v}| \sin \alpha)^2}{R} = e |\vec{v}| |\vec{B}| \sin \alpha.$$

From here we find the radius of the circle $R$ and the period of revolution $T$:

$$R = \frac{m |\vec{v}| \sin \alpha}{e |\vec{B}|},$$

$$T = \frac{2\pi R}{|\vec{v}| \sin \alpha} = \frac{2\pi m}{e |\vec{B}|}.$$

At the same time, the electron moves uniformly across the field with a speed $|\vec{v}| \cos \alpha$.

If after time $\tau = \frac{L}{|\vec{v}| \cos \alpha}$ the electron ends up at point $O'$ (see Fig. 5), this will mean that it has made an integer $n$ full revolutions:

$$\tau = nT,$$

or

$$\frac{L}{|\vec{v}| \cos \alpha} = n \frac{2\pi m}{e |\vec{B}_n|},$$

where $n=1, 2, 3, \ldots$. As a result, we obtain a discrete set of magnetic induction vector model values ​​at which the electron will hit point $O'$:

$$|\vec{B}_n| = \frac{2\pi m |\vec{v}| \cos \alpha}{e L} n.$$

Strictly speaking, the values ​​of the focusing fields depend on the angle $\alpha$. However, at small angles $\cos \alpha \approx 1$ and

$$|\vec{B}_n| \approx \frac{2\pi m |\vec{v}|}{e L} n.$$
