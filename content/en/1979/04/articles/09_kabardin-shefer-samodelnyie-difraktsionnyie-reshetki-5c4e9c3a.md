Homemade diffraction gratings

Diffraction gratings were first used to study spectra in 1821 by the German physicist Fraunhofer. His gratings, made by winding the finest wires onto two parallel wires, contained only a few tables of wires per millimeter of grating length. (Note that good modern gratings, which are the product of a plate with opaque lines applied to them, have up to several thousand lines per 1 mm.)

Try to make a simple diffraction grating. At home, this can be done, for example, photographically.

To do this, you need to draw a system of parallel stripes of the same width on the sheet of paper with a poster pen. The distance between the stripes can be chosen equal to their width. After photographing and developing on the negative, you will get a system of light and dark stripes, that is, an alternation of spaces that are clear and opaque to light. Having strengthened the resulting negative in the frames for diapasites, the work on manufacturing the diffraction grating can be considered completed.

Photography is best done in good sunlight or backlit with a fluorescent lamp.

From one template you can obtain diffraction gratings with different periods if you photograph it from different distances. Let, for example, the width $A$ of light stripes and the width $B$ of dark stripes on the template be the same and equal to 3 mm; we want to obtain a grating with a constant of $d = a + b = 0,05$ mm ($a = b = 0,025$ mm), the shape distance of the camera object is $F = 50$ mm. Then the distance $s$ to the template can be found from the expression

$$\frac{s}{F} = \frac{A}{a},$$

where

$$s = F \frac{A}{a} = 6 \text{ м.}$$

The grating obtained after development will contain one millimeter

$$n = \frac{1}{d} = \frac{1}{0,05} = 20 \text{ штрихов},$$

that is, the example is the same as in the first Fraunhofer lattices.

The main purpose of a diffraction grating as a spectral device is to determine the wavelength of light. From the condition

$$d \sin \varphi = k\lambda$$

the main maxima of the diffraction pattern obtained using a grating, it follows that the lower the grating constant $d$, the greater the angle at which the maximum corresponding to the wavelength $\lambda$ will be observed in the spectrum of the $k$ order.

This means that with a decrease in the grating constant (with an increase in the number of pins per unit length), the ability to separate closely spaced spectral lines increases (as they say, the grating dispersion increases)*). In addition, as the total number of stiriki increases, more light enters the main maxima, and they become increasingly bright.

In this regard, it is desirable to produce a lattice with the possibility of greater

the total number of strokes $N$ and a possibly smaller lattice constant $d$.

Fulfillment of the first requirement is determined mainly by patience and accuracy. You need to draw templates on two or three sheets of whatman and then glue them into one large template for photographing (or take one sheet of whatman and reduce the width of the light and dark stripes to one millimeter).

The second requirement is limited by the resolution of the photographic emulsion due to its granular structure. Photographic films with a color value of 130-250 GOST units have a resolution of no more than 75-70 lines per millimeter; with a decrease in color value to 32 GOST units, the resolution increases to 115 lines per millimeter.

The period $d$ of the resulting diffraction grating can be determined by changing the size $C$ of the listavatman image on the negative and dividing it by the number $N$ of black lines drawn on the drawing before photographing:

$$d = \frac{C}{N}.$$

After making the grating, you can start experimenting. At one end of a wooden ruler 40-50 cm long, attach a diffraction grating, and at the other, a homemade scale of millimeter paper glued to a strip of cardboard with a slot. Using a homemade diffraction grating, you can estimate the boundaries of the spectral color intensity of the eye. To do this, you need to determine the wavelength of red light at one end of the observed spectrum and the wavelength of violet light at the other end of the spectrum. If the grating period $d$ and the order of the spectrum $k$ are known, determining the wavelength $\lambda$ indicates a change in the angle $\varphi$ between the normal to the grating plane and the direction to the position of the maximum corresponding to the image of the screen image and

$$d \sin \varphi = k \lambda,$$

$$\lambda = \frac{d}{k} \sin \varphi.$$
