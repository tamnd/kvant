⟦folio 16⟧

One can imagine that an oscillator is a kind of “particle” with mass β, oscillating external rigidity γ. In order to formulate the basic idea of ​​quantum mechanics for this object, we take the wavelength λ of the wave process associated with our “particle”:

$$\lambda = 2\pi \hbar / \beta q.$$

The denominator is the product of “mass” and its speed. Since the speed changes as the particle moves, the wavelength also changes - it is the minimum value of the equilibrium position and grows in those areas where the speed of the “particle” is low.

Let the “particle” move in the region from - $q_0$ to $q_0$. (We emphasize that $2q_0$ is not the swing of the oscillating oscillator, but the region in which its coordinate “trembles.”) In order to form a standing wave, an integral number of half-waves must be placed on the “length” $2q_0$: $2q_0 = (n+1)\lambda/2$ ($n=0, 1, 2, \dots$). As an estimate of the speed, we take the value of the speed at $q=q_0$, that is, $(q)_0 = 2\pi \hbar / \beta \lambda = \pi \hbar (n+1)/2\beta q_0$. Substituting this value into the expression for kinetic energy, we find:

$$W_n = \frac{\pi^2 \hbar^2}{8\beta} \frac{(n+1)^2}{q_0^2}.$$

For the total energy we get:

$$E_n(q_0) = \frac{(\pi \hbar)^2}{8\beta} \frac{(n+1)^2}{q_0^2} + \frac{\gamma q_0^2}{2}.$$

For small values ​​of $q_0$ the energy is high due to the first term, and for large values ​​of $q_0$ - due to the second. The approximate value of $q_0$, and even the least energy, will be obtained if we equate both terms. From this condition we find:

$$q_0^2 = \frac{\pi \hbar (n+1)}{2\sqrt{\beta \gamma}}.$$

Substituting into the expression for energy, we get:

$$E_n = \frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \sqrt{\gamma/\beta} (n+1) = \frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \omega (n+1) \quad (n=0, 1, 2, \dots).$$

The value $\omega = \sqrt{\gamma/\beta}$ represents the frequency of the oscillating classical oscillator.

An accurate calculation for energy results in:

$$E_n = \hbar \omega \left( n + \frac{1}{2} \right).$$

Thus, we made a mistake in the numerical factor ($\pi/2$ instead of 1), as well as in the numerical value of the energy in the lowest state, that is, at $n=0$ ($\frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \omega$ instead of $\frac{1}{2} \hbar \omega$). But everything else worked out exactly! Now that the result has been obtained, we should think about what we used to obtain it and what follows from the expressions we obtained for the oscillator energy and for the value $q_0^2$.

First of all, we applied to our oscillator, without being interested in how it is structured, the principles of quantum mechanics, first established in relation to electrons. Of course, it is natural to expect that the general principles should be the same for other particles whose masses differ from the mass of the electron. Indeed, such a generalization was confirmed with great accuracy by experiment. But why can these principles be applied to such a system as a resonant circuit, where the role of the “coordinate” is played by the charge on the capacitor plates? Here we come to an important assumption, which is widely used and used in theoretical physics of the 20th century. If two systems have energy that equally depends on coordinates and velocities, then such systems have the same properties, despite the fact that “coordinates” and “velocities” may have completely different meanings in these systems.

There was not a single example where this assumption contradicted experience. Therefore, we have the right to consider that we have solved the problem of applying quantum mechanics for all possible oscillators at once.
