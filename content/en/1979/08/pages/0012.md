The reason for the separation of these two processes is not only that each of them requires its own special knowledge: knowledge of measurement methods - in one case, and mastery of the mathematical apparatus of thinking and various forms of intuition. Intuition, that is, the ability to recognize the right path, plays a crucial role, especially in the first stages of work. Unfortunately, the gift of intuition does not arise immediately, but as a result of hard work, as a reward for solving many scientific problems.

Since theoretical physics deals with more abstract concepts than experimental physics, theoretical physics requires a more abstract form of intuition, sometimes close to the intuition of mathematics. In the past century, when physics was not yet so specialized, many physicists combined both processes. Thus, Maxwell, who theoretically obtained equations that describe all electromagnetic phenomena, was also involved in experiments. Hertz, who discovered electromagnetic waves experimentally, was also a good theoretical scientist. And yet, in each case, it is possible to indicate which of the procession is the main one: for Maxwell it is theoretical physics, and for Hertz it is experimental physics.

In the 20th century, one of the most remarkable examples of a “universal” physicist - both theorist and experimentalist - was Enrico Fermi. Along with many other works, Fermi created the theory of radioactive decay and, together with the physicists of his group, discovered experimental artificial radioactive elements that arise when bombarded by neutrons.

Another example of an outstanding theoretician closely associated with experiment was Academician G.I. Bud-

ker, who combined theoretical physics with substitute engineering ideas. He led the theoretical development and practical implementation of accelerators using colliding beams of charged particles at the Novosibirsk Academy Town.

However, these are rare exceptions, and a young person interested in physics must decide for himself which of the two processions he chooses.

In what follows we will talk about the work of theoretical physicists.

Physics and mathematics

So, the task of a theoretical physicist is to obtain relationships between observable quantities using mathematical calculations. Doesn't this mean that theoretical physics is something of applied mathematics? This point of view is completely wrong. Both in the nature of the problems and in the methods of approaching problems, mathematics and theoretical physics are categorically different.

In mathematics, the most important role is played by the mathematical aspects, that is, the logical impeccability of all conclusions arising from the accepted axioms, and the study of all logical possible relationships. The task of physics is to construct, as far as possible, an accurate picture of the world without strict “rules of the game”, using all known experimental and theoretical facts, using the basic intuition of the contract, which will later be tested experimentally. Thus, a mathematician explores all logical possible types of geometry, while a physicist finds out what geometric relationships are realized in our world.

A mathematician, even if he deals with applied problems, that is, problems that do not come from mathematics, takes on solving only those problems that do not require additional unproven propositions. A physicist, as a rule, deals with problems in which there are insufficient initial data.
