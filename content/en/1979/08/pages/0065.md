8. Let the fourth-order curve $P(x,y)=0$ contain, in addition to two ovals (one of which is embedded in the other), at least one more point. Let us draw a straight line through this point intersecting both ovals; the resulting straight line has 5 or more points in common with our curve. Let the equation of this line be $Ax+By+C=0$. Let's write the system:

$$\begin{cases} P(x,y)=0 \\ Ax+By+C=0 \end{cases}$$

Using equation (2), we express one of the variables in terms of the other and substitute it into equation (1). We get an equation of the fourth degree. As is known, it can have more than four solutions, which contradicts the presence of fifth points in a straight line and a curve.

9. Let the curve $P(x,y)$ consist of 5 or more ovals. Let’s take a point on each of the ovals and draw a second-order curve through the resulting 5 points (its equation is $Q(x,y)=0$). It is clear that the points can be chosen so that this curve intersects all ovals and does not touch them. Then the curves $P(x,y)=0$ and $Q(x,y)=0$ will have 10 (or more) intersection points. We will get a contradiction if we prove that the system

$$\begin{cases} P(x,y)=0 \\ Q(x,y)=0 \end{cases}$$

has no more than 8 solutions (recall that $P(x,y)$ and $Q(x,y)$ are polynomials of fourth and second degree, respectively); A more general fact is also true: if $P(x,y)$ has degree $m$, and $Q(x,y)$ has degree $n$, then system (3) has at most $m \cdot n$ solutions *). In order to prove this,

*) One caveat should be made: the number of solutions to system (3) can be infinite. (For example, the curve $P(x,y)$ consists of two circles, and $Q(x,y)=0$ is one of these circles.) This case, however, is not interesting to us (why?). If the number of solutions is finite, then it does not exceed $m \cdot n$.
