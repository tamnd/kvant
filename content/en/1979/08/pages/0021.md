The sounding properties of empty vessels have been known for a long time. Thus, even in the ancient theater, large clay or bronze vessels were used to enhance the actor’s voice. The so-called Helmoltz resonators - spherical or bottle-shaped cavities with a narrow, long neck - have received wide practical application in actuators.

Using a set of Helmoltz resonators (named after the German physicist Helmoltz, the creator of the physical theory of hearing), complex sounds can be analyzed. The action of the resonator is based on the resonance of the oscillations of the influence contained inside the resonator. Of all the vibrations that make up a complex sound wave, each resonator responds most strongly to a frequency equal to the natural frequency of the vibrations of the influence in it.

What and how does the natural frequency of oscillations of a Helmoltz resonator depend? If the dimensions of the resonator are small compared to the sound wavelength, it can be considered as an oscillatory system, similar to a spring pendulum. Since the throat is narrow, during oscillations the speed of movement of the action in the throat is much higher than the speed of action inside the cavity, so that the role of the oscillating mass is played mainly by the action in the throat. In other words, the kinetic energy of the oscillatory system is concentrated mainly in the throat. The impact in the throat has much less elastic energy than in the cavity; the change in the volume of impact in the throat during vibrations can be neglected and we can assume that this entire impact as a single whole is a kind of “air plug”, and the volume of impact in the cavity changes noticeably. Thus, the impact is the role of the spring in our oscillatory system.

As is known, the natural frequency of oscillation of a spring pendulum is equal to

$$v = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$
