Thus, a drop of fat will float on the surface of the broth without spreading if the surface tension coefficient of the broth is less than the sum of the surface tension coefficients of the fat and the broth-fat interface:

$$\sigma_1 < \sigma_2 + \sigma_{12}.$$

In the particular case, when the drop is very thin (almost flat), the angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are small ($\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$) and the equilibrium condition of the drop has the form:

$$\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 + \sigma_{12}.$$

At

$$\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 + \sigma_{12}$$

there are no values ​​of angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ for which equality (*) would hold. This means that in this case, liquid 2 does not collect in a drop on the surface of liquid 1, but spreads over it in a thin layer.

Now we will try to explain the results of experiments with a spoon of broth. Drops of fat float on the surface of the broth, which means that the correspondence (**) is fulfilled. Why, when the broth in a spoon decreases, do the drops spread over its surface, increasing in size? What does this change?

We offer this explanation. The coefficients of surface tension of fat $\sigma_2$ and the broth-fat interface $\sigma_{12}$ remain unchanged. It remains to assume that when pouring part of the broth from a spoon, the coefficient of surface tension of the broth $\sigma_1$ changes. But broth (to a first approximation) is water. Can the coefficient of surface tension of water change if the amount of water in the vessel is reduced? Apparently not. True, broth is not just water, but water covered with a thin layer of fat. When we pour broth from a spoon, we also pour out some of the fat, so that the thickness of the layer of fat covering the broth decreases.
