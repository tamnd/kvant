$$\frac{M}{R} = \frac{M}{R - \delta} + \frac{\mu}{r + h - \delta}.$$

Let's transform this expression:

$$-M \frac{\delta}{R(R - \delta)} = \frac{\mu}{(r + h) - \delta} \Rightarrow$$

$$\Rightarrow -\frac{M}{\mu}[(r + h)\delta - \delta^2] = R(R - \delta).$$

Neglecting $\delta^2$ on the left side and $\delta$ on the right, we get

$$-\frac{M}{\mu}(r + h)\delta \approx R^2,$$

or, substituting $-\frac{M}{\mu} = -\frac{R^3}{r^3}\frac{\rho_\pi}{(\rho_B - \rho_\pi)},$

$$\frac{(r + h)}{r^3}\frac{R\delta\rho_\pi}{(\rho_\pi - \rho_B)} \approx 1 \Rightarrow$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{r^3}{(r + h)} \approx \frac{\rho_\pi}{(\rho_\pi - \rho_B)} R\delta = \frac{3}{2} \cdot 6,4 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 25 (\text{m}^2)$$

(here the numerical values ​​$R = 6,4 \cdot 10^6$ m, $\rho_\pi = 3 \cdot 10^3$ kg/m$^3$, $\rho_B = 1 \cdot 10^3$ kg/m$^3$, $\delta = 25$ m) are substituted. We will not solve this cubic equation for $r$, but will only estimate the value of $r$. To estimate, we use the fact that the function $r^3/(r + h)$ returns monotically for $r > 0$.

It is easy to verify by direct calculations that $r$ lies in the interval between 18 km and 20 km.

A. Stasenko

F535. The potential interaction energy of two identical charged small balls with charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ is equal to $W_1 = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 l}$, where $l$ is the distance between the chains of balls. If the balls are connected by wire, the charges of the balls will become equal, and the nominal energy of interaction of the balls will become equal to $W_2 = \frac{(q_1 + q_2)^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0 l}$. Since $(q_1 + q_2)^2 > 4q_1 q_2$ (at $q_1 \neq q_2$), then $W_2 > W_1$, then the nominal energy of interaction of charges returns!

Where does the extra energy come from?

The reasoning given in the conditions of the problem is not correct: they take into account only the potential energy of the interaction of the balls and do not take into account the energy of the power field of individual balls.

The energy of the electric field of an individual (isolated) ball with charge $q$ is equal to the work that is done when charging the ball. It can be considered as the energy of a spherical capacitor, the outer lining of which is infinitely further away (from the ball). This energy can be calculated using the formula $W = \frac{1}{2} qU$, where $q$ is the charge of the capacitor (charge of the ball), $U$ is the potential difference of its plates. In our case, $U$ is equal to the potential Ф of the ball with respect to infinity (where the potential is pleasant to zero): $U = \varphi = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r}$, where $r$ is the radius of the ball.

Thus, $W = \frac{1}{2} q\varphi = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$. The energy of the system before connecting the wire balls is $W_1 = \frac{q_1^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r} + \frac{q_2^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r} + \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 l}$, and after connecting -

$$W_2 = 2 \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r} + \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 l} = \frac{(q_1 + q_2)^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0 r} + \frac{(q_1 + q_2)^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0 l},$$

where $q = (q_1 + q_2)/2$.

So

$$W_1 - W_2 = \frac{(q_1 - q_2)^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{l} \right) > 0,$$

that is, $W_1 > W_2$. This is natural, since when connecting the wire balls, part of the energy was released in the form of a Joule body.
