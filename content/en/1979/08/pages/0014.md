From the dimension it follows that the area $S$ of a right triangle can be written as the product of the square of the hypotenuse and the neoctor function of one of the acute angles: $S=c^2f(\alpha)$. As another example, consider the problem of finding the period of external oscillations of a weight. First of all, let's find out what quantities can be included in the expression for the period. Since the forces acting on the weight are the gravitational force and the elastic force, it is natural to assume that the period of oscillation depends on the free fall root $g$ and the mass $m$ of the weight and on the stiffness of the spring $k$. Of course, such quantities as temperature and air viscosity will not be included in the problem if we neglect the damping of oscillations. (To simplify the problem, you need to know what can be neglected!) From the values ​​$g$, $m$ and $k$ you can make only one combination that has the dimension of time - $V/m/k$. Therefore, the period of $T$ is equal to

$$T=C\sqrt{m/k}.$$

And the dimensionless coefficients arising from the equation encountered in physics, as a rule, turn out to be of the order of unity *). An exact calculation gives the value 2π for $C$.

Thus, without calculations, using only dimensional analysis, we found the approximate value of the period of oscillation of the external weight.

In all areas of physics, there are problems associated with flasks of near-position systems. To understand multi-phenomena, and therefore for a qualitative analysis of these phenomena, you need to know the general properties of such systems. Systems that vibrate around an equilibrium position, regardless of their structure, are called oscillators. The simplest examples of oscillations are an external weight, a mathematical mat. A more complex example is a tense country; it can have many types of vibrations: a vibration with an antinode in the middle (fundamental tone), with one node, two nodes, etc. (overtones). Thus, a country is a set of oscillators of different frequencies. A similar example is the column of air in the organization of labor; it can be made to vibrate with the highest frequency (fundamental tone) or with a higher frequency, when at some points the air is motionless (analogous to the nodes in string vibrators).
