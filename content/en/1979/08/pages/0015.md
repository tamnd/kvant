where $q$ are quantities characterizing the deviation from the equilibrium position, and $\dot{q}$ is the rate of change of quantity $q$ in time (that is, $\dot{q} = \Delta q / \Delta t$, where $\Delta t$ is a small period of time). The term $\Pi = \gamma q^2 / 2$ is the potential energy of the oscillator; the coefficient $\beta$ is called the oscillator mass. The term $K = \beta \dot{q}^2 / 2$ is the kinetic energy of the oscillator; the coefficient $\beta$ is called the oscillator mass. (Remember how the energy of the simplest oscillator—an external weight—is written, and you will understand why $\gamma$ is called stiffness, and $\beta$ is the mass of the oscillator.)

No matter how the oscillator is specifically designed, its angular part $\omega = 2\pi/T$ is expressed as follows through rigidity $\gamma$ and mass $\beta$:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\gamma / \beta}.$$

The introduction of a “generalized” oscillator, the coordinate of which is not necessarily the distance from the equilibrium position, but can have any meaning, as long as the energy also has a structure like $E$ in expression (*), allows us to draw conclusions about the behavior of an oscillating system without a distance of physical nature and the origin of processes in it.

For clarity, let’s give another example of an oscillator, which is not similar to all the examples given above. Let us assume that there is an inductor $L$ made of well-conducting wire, the ends of which are connected to the capacitor emcapacity $C$, which has a charge $Q_0$. Electromagnetic oscillations arise in the circuit. If the coil is made of a superconductor (this requires low temperature), the oscillation will practically not be damped.

The energy of such a system consists of two terms: the energy of the magnetic field in the coil and the energy of the electric field in the capacitor. The energy of the electric field of the capacitor is proportional to the square of the charge $Q$, which is currently located on the capacitor plate: $\Pi = \frac{1}{C} Q^2 / 2$.

The energy of the magnetic field of the coil is proportional to the square of the current $I$ currently flowing through the coil: $K = L I^2 / 2$. But the current strength is equal to the rate of change of the capacitor charge over time: $I = \dot{Q}$. Thus, the energy of the magnetic field of the coil is $K = L \dot{Q}^2 / 2$.

Therefore, the energy of the system at any moment of time is equal to

$$E = \frac{1}{C} Q^2 / 2 + L \dot{Q}^2 / 2.$$

Our system is an oscillator; the role of the oscillation coordinate is played by the charge $Q$, the potential energy of the osillator is the energy of the capacitor, and the kinetic energy is the energy of the magnetic field of the coil. From the energy recording it is clear that the rigidity of this oscillator is $1/C$, and the role of the inductance mass is $L$. You can immediately write down part of the vibrations: $\omega = \sqrt{1/LC}$.
