Let the segment $KL$ (“staircase”) be fixed, and the lines $KO$ and $LO$ (“wall” and “floor”) rotate respectively around the points $K$ and $L$ so that the angle between them remains right. This time, the fact that the distance from the middle of the segment to the vertex $O$ of the right angle is always the same turns into Theorem A from paragraph 64 of “Geometry 7”: if two points $K$ and $L$ are given on the plane, then the points $O$, for which $K\hat{O}L=90^\circ$, lie on a circle with a diameter $KL$.

Problem 2. Point $O$ lies on the segment $AC$. Find the set of points $M$ for which $M\hat{O}C=2M\hat{A}C$ (Fig. 4).

Answer. Union of a circle with center $O$ of radius $AO$ (without point $A$) and ray $OC$ (without point $O$) - see figure 5.

Explanation. Let the point $M$ of the desired set not belong to the line $AO$. Let us prove that $|MO|=|AO|$. Let's construct a triangle $OAM$ (Fig. 6). By the theorem on the exterior angle of a triangle $O\hat{A}M+A\hat{M}O=M\hat{O}C$. By condition $M\hat{O}C=2M\hat{A}C$.

From the conditions of the problem we immediately obtain $O\hat{A}M=A\hat{M}O$; therefore, the triangle $AMO$ is equilateral, that is, $|OM|=|AO|$.

In the language of “movement”, the result of task 2 can be imagined as follows: let the straight line $AM$ rotate around the point $A$ with a constant angular velocity $\omega$ (that is, per unit time, rotate through an angle of magnitude $\omega$), and let the straight line $OM$ rotate around the point $O$ with an angular velocity $2\omega$, and at the initial moment time, both straight lines coincided with straight line $AO$ (Fig. 7). Then the point $M$ of intersection of these lines moves along a circle with center $O$.

From here we can extract the inscribed angle theorem (Theorem 62 from paragraph 111 of Geometry 8). If straight line $AM$ in time $t$ rotates from position $AM_1$ to position $AM_2$ by angle $\omega t$, then straight line $OM$ rotates through angle $2\omega t$, in other words, the inscribed angle $M_1AM_2$ is equal to the angular value of the corresponding central angle $M_1OM_2$ (Fig. 8).

This theorem can be formulated more clearly:

The theorem about quantity on a circle contains a small ring. A rod is rotated around the point $A$ of the circle and passes through this ring. If the rod rotates uniformly with angular velocity $\omega$, then the ring rotates uniformly around the circumference with angular velocity $2\omega$ (Fig. 9).

*) Note that the quantity theorem is in the literal sense: the imaginary “ring” is simply the intersection point of a line and a circle; if you make a wire model, then after one turn (in the other direction) the ring will get stuck.
