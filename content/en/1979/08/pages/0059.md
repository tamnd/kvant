⟦folio 57⟧

Several straight lines move in a circle.

This problem is closely related to problems 12*, 13* from paragraph 111 of “Geometry 8”.

Fig. 19.

Problem 5. Along a stationary circle, touching it from the inside, without sliding, the input circle is less than its radius. What line do we describe from point K of the moving circle (Fig. 14)?

The answer to this puzzle problem is simple: the point moves in a straight line, more precisely, along the diameter of a stationary circle (Fig. 15). This result is called Copernicus' theorem.

Try to verify the validity of Copernicus' theorem in practice. It is not difficult to prove - you just need to remember the inscribed angle theorem.

Let's compare the results of problems 1 and 5. In both problems, the solution involves a rather complex movement of a figure (in the first - a segment, in the second - a circle), but the trajectories of non-actor points turn out to be unexpectedly simple. It turns out that these two problems are similar not only externally, but also the movements themselves considered in them; match!

Indeed, a circle of radius d/2 rolls along a circle of radius d from the inside, and KL is empty - the diameter of this circle, rigidly connected to it. According to Copernicus' theorem, points K and L move along fixed straight lines (the diameter of the great circle is AA' and BB' of the corresponding Fig. 16). So, the diameter KL slides with its ends along two mutually perpendicular straight lines, that is, it moves like the segment in task 1.
