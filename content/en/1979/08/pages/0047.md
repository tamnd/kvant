At $x_1 \leqslant x_k$.
By the induction hypothesis
$$x_1 + \ldots + x_k - x_1 x_2 - \ldots - x_{k-1} x_k - x_k x_1 \leqslant [k/2].$$
Adding inequalities (2) and (3), we obtain
$$x_1 + \ldots + x_k + x_{k+1} + x_{k+2} - x_1 x_2 - \ldots - x_k x_{k+1} - x_{k+1} x_{k+2} - x_{k+2} x_1 \leqslant [k/2] + 1 = [k/2 + 1] = \left[ \frac{k+2}{2} \right],$$
so inequality (1) is also valid for $n=k+2$. Problem c) is solved.
Another, perhaps more “natural” solution to problem c) is the following:
Let us fix the variables $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{i-1}, x_{i+1}, \ldots, x_n$, and the variable $x_i=x$ will change (on the interval [0; 1]). Then
$$x_1 + x_2 + \ldots + x_n - x_1 x_2 - x_2 x_3 - \ldots - x_{n-1} x_n - x_n x_1 = kx + b,$$
where $k$ and $b$ are constant numbers. Obviously, it is also a linear function of $x$ defined on the interval [0; 1], takes the highest value at one of the ends of the segment. Since this reasoning can be applied sequentially to $x_1$, then to $x_2$, ..., to $x_n$, the maximum value of the expression $\Sigma = x_1 + x_2 + \ldots + x_n - x_1 x_2 - \ldots - x_{n-1} x_n - x_n x_1$ is achieved when some of the numbers $x_i$ are equal to one, and the rest are equal to zero. Note now that if two adjacent xxes are both equal to one (for example, $x_i$ and $x_{i+1}$), then one of them can be turned to zero without reducing the value of $\Sigma$. Thus, $\Sigma$ takes on the most meaning when some (say, $m$) does not create and the “xes” are equal to one. Since $m \leqslant [n/2]$, (“X’s” are not adjacent!), we get that $\Sigma_{\max} \leqslant [n/2]$. But it is easy to check that for $x_1=x_3=x_5=\ldots=0$, $x_2=x_4=\ldots=1$ the values ​​of $\Sigma$ are exactly equal to [n/2], which means that $\Sigma_{\max} = [n/2]$.

F533. A dumbbell of length $l$ fits in the knot formed by the flat planes (Fig. 10). The bottom ball of the dumbbell is moved horizontally for a very small distance, and the dumbbell begins to move. This is the speed of the lower ball at the moment when the upper ball moves away from the vertical plane.

Fig. 10.

Fig. 11.

Let the dumbbell make an angle $\alpha$ with the vertical plane (Fig. 11) at the moment the upper ball is lifted from the vertical plane, the speed of the upper ball is $v$, the speed of the lower one is $u$.

According to the law of conservation of energy
$$\frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{mu^2}{2} = mg\Delta h = mgl(1 - \cos \alpha),$$
or
$$v^2 + u^2 = 2gl(1 - \cos \alpha).$$
(1)
(m is the mass of each ball; $v, u, g$ - the modules correspond to each vector).
Since the rod is rigid,
$$v \cos \alpha = u \sin \alpha.$$
(2)
From equation (1) and (2) we find:
$$u^2 = 2gl(\cos^2 \alpha - \cos^3 \alpha).$$
(3)
Until the moment of lift-off, the center of mass of the dumbbell moves with horizontal acceleration (this acceleration was reported by the reaction force of the upper wall). Therefore, by the time the upper ball separates from the vertical wall, the speed $u$ is maximum. Let us find the value of $\cos \alpha$ at which the expression $\cos^2 \alpha - \cos^3 \alpha$ (see (3)) is maximum: $(x^2 - x^3)' = 2x - 3x^2 = 0$ ($x = \cos \alpha$) at $x = 2/3$, that is, $\cos \alpha = 2/3$.
Substituting this value $\cos \alpha$ into (3), we find:
$$u = \frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}gl}.$$

A. Zilberman
