6) For $\varphi = 90^\circ$, express the area $S$ of a convex quadrilateral in terms of the lengths $a, b, c, d$ of its sides. Note that in this case $a^2 - b^2 + c^2 - d^2 = 0$. Let $a$ be the smallest of the numbers $a, b, c, d$. A segment of length $a$ with ends on the ray $l_1$ and $m_1$ (Fig. 5) can be extended to a quadrilateral with given sides; By changing the position of the segment, we get a whole range of such quadrilaterals (with different angles at the vertices). Let us prove that the area of ​​all these quadrilaterals is not equal to the same number. To do this, we use the equality (Fig. 6)

$$S = \frac{1}{2} (ab \sin \varphi + cd \sin \psi),$$

where $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are the angles at the vertices $A$ and $C$ of the quadrilateral, let’s find what values ​​$S$ can take.

Angles $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are related by

$$a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos \varphi = c^2 + d^2 - 2cd \cos \psi$$

(cosine theorem).

We will assume that the angle $\varphi$ is a function of $\varphi$. From matching (*)

$$\psi' = \frac{ab \sin \varphi}{cd \sin \psi},$$

so for arbitrary $S'$ (by $\varphi$) we get:

$$S' = \frac{1}{2} (ab \cos \varphi + \psi' cd \cos \psi) = \frac{ab}{2} \left( \cos \varphi + \frac{\cos \psi}{\sin \psi} \sin \varphi \right) = \frac{ab}{2} \sin \psi \sin (\varphi + \psi).$$

We see that $S' = 0$ when $\sin (\varphi + \psi) = 0$, that is, when $\varphi + \psi = 180^\circ$. This means that a circle can be described around the corresponding quadrilateral.

We honor Schmeiner's famous theorem that of all quadrilaterals with a given side, the one around which a circle can be described has the largest area. Almost - because we still need to check that at the indicated zero of an arbitrary $S'$ the function $S = S(\varphi)$ really has a maximum. We will limit ourselves to checking this statement only for the case we are considering, when the diagonals of the quadrilateral are perpendicular (we have not yet used this circumstance).
