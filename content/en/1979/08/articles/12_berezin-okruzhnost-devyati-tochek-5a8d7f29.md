Circle
nine points
(see page 4 of the cover)

Let's construct an arbitrary triangle ABC and draw altitudes and medians in it. On figure 1 the main lines are L, M, N of segments connecting the orthocenter (the point of intersection of the heights) of the triangle with its vertices. Therefore, it is sometimes called the circle of the ninth point.

To prove all this, consider an arbitrary quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals. At figure 2 you see a quadrilateral ABCD in the culvert, but, generally speaking, it can be non-convex and even self-intersecting. Consider the middle

Fig. 1.

The heights (points D, E, F) and the bases of the medians (points K, P, G) are highlighted in red. It turns out that all these points lie on the same circle. It is often called the Euler circle. In addition to the indicated six points, the middle sides X, Y, Z, U lie on the same circle. It is easy to see that they are the vertices of a rectangle (prove this). Therefore, they lie on the same circle, and XZ and YU are its diameters. Let us drop from these points the perpendiculars XV, YW, ZT and US to opposite sides of the quadrilateral or their extension. It is clear that the points V, T, W and S lie on the same circle: real, for example, the angles XVZ and XTZ are right angles.

Now we can prove that on figure 1 the points D, E, F, K, P and G lie on the same circle. On it, upon closer examination, one can detect three non-convex quadrangles AHBC, ABHC, with perpendicular diagonals. For example, in the quadrilateral AHBC such diagonals are the segments HC and AB. Let us show with the help of this quadrilateral that points E, P, D and G really belong to the same circle. Point P is the midpoint of side BC, and point E is the base of a perpendicular drawn from it to the opposite side AH. Similarly, point D is the base of a perpendicular drawn from point G, the middle of side AC, to the opposite side BH of the same quadrilateral. In the same way, considering the quadrilateral ABCH, we are convinced that point F also lies on the same circle, etc.

It is easy to report that the radius of the Euler circle is equal to half the radius of the circle circumscribed about triangle ABC. Try to prove that the center of the Euler circle bisects the segment connecting the center of the circumcircle and the orthocenter H.
