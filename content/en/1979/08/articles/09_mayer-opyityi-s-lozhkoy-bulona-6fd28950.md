Experiments
with a spoon of broth

Please wash away the interesting phenomena in liquids that occur on its surface, where it borders on its vapor, on some other liquid or on a solid.

The surface layer of liquid is in special conditions compared to the rest of the liquid mass and therefore has special properties. First of all, this is surface tension. It is associated with such phenomena as, for example, wetting or non-wetting.

We suggest you conduct several experiments and study the behavior of one liquid on the surface of another.

Having taken a spoonful of good meat broth, do not rush to put it in your mouth. Look carefully at the broth - large droplets of fat are floating on its surface. Notice the size of these droplets. Pour a little broth from a spoon figuratively into a bowl and again look at its surface - droplets of fat have spread, becoming larger in diameter and smaller in thickness. What explains this interesting phenomenon?

First of all, let's find out under certain conditions a drop of fat can be on the surface of the broth without spreading. Look at figure 1 - a drop of liquid 2 (fat) lies on the surface of liquid 1 (broth). The drop shape resembles a lentil. On top is medium 3 - a mixture of air with liquid vapors 1 and 2. When the medium (1, 2 and 3) is in contact along a circle bounded by a drop. Let us select a small element of this circle (near the point O on figure 1) with length $\Delta l$. There are three forces of surface tension acting on it. At the interface between liquids 1 and 2 there is a force $\vec{F}_{12}$, directed tangentially to the interface and equal modulo

$$|\vec{F}_{12}| = \sigma_{12} \Delta l,$$

where $\sigma_{12}$ is the surface tension coefficient at the interface of media 1 and 2.

Similar forces $\vec{F}_{13}$ and $\vec{F}_{23}$ act at the interfaces 1, 3 and 2, 3:

$$|\vec{F}_{13}| = \sigma_{13} \Delta l$$

And

$$|\vec{F}_{23}| = \sigma_{23} \Delta l.$$

Here $\sigma_{13}$ and $\sigma_{23}$ are the corresponding surface tension coefficients.

Obviously, the drop will be in equilibrium if the sum of all three forces is zero:

$$\vec{F}_{12} + \vec{F}_{13} + \vec{F}_{23} = 0,$$

or - in projections on the coordinate axes $X$ and $Y$ (after substituting the corresponding abscissa values ​​and reducing to $\Delta l$) -

$$\sigma_{13} = \sigma_{12} \cos \theta_1 + \sigma_{23} \cos \theta_2,$$

($*$)

$$\sigma_{12} \sin \theta_1 = \sigma_{23} \sin \theta_2.$$

Here $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are the angles tangent to the surface of a drop of liquid 2 and the surface of liquid 1, they are called edge angles.

From equality (*) it is clear that equilibrium of a drop is possible if the surface tension coefficients are related by a certain correspondence:

$$\sigma_{13} < \sigma_{12} + \sigma_{23}.$$

Since the surface area in a liquid is small and depends on the presence of a gaseous medium above the liquid, we can practically assume that

$$\sigma_{13} = \sigma_1 \text{ и } \sigma_{23} = \sigma_2.$$

The quantities $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ are called surface tension coefficients

Liquid 1 and 2 respectively; in our case, these are the surface tension coefficients of the broth and fat.

Thus, a drop of fat will float on the surface of the broth without spreading if the surface tension coefficient of the broth is less than the sum of the surface tension coefficients of the fat and the broth-fat interface:

$$\sigma_1 < \sigma_2 + \sigma_{12}.$$

In the particular case, when the drop is very thin (almost flat), the angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are small ($\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$) and the equilibrium condition of the drop has the form:

$$\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 + \sigma_{12}.$$

At

$$\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 + \sigma_{12}$$

there are no values ​​of angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ for which equality (*) would hold. This means that in this case, liquid 2 does not collect in a drop on the surface of liquid 1, but spreads over it in a thin layer.

Now we will try to explain the results of experiments with a spoon of broth. Drops of fat float on the surface of the broth, which means that the correspondence (**) is fulfilled. Why, when the broth in a spoon decreases, do the drops spread over its surface, increasing in size? What does this change?

We offer this explanation. The coefficients of surface tension of fat $\sigma_2$ and the broth-fat interface $\sigma_{12}$ remain unchanged. It remains to assume that when pouring part of the broth from a spoon, the coefficient of surface tension of the broth $\sigma_1$ changes. But broth (to a first approximation) is water. Can the coefficient of surface tension of water change if the amount of water in the vessel is reduced? Apparently not. True, broth is not just water, but water covered with a thin layer of fat. When we pour broth from a spoon, we also pour out some of the fat, so that the thickness of the layer of fat covering the broth decreases. As a result, drops of fat spread across the surface of the broth.

To check, we suggest you check this experience yourself. Fill a clean saucer (without traces of fat) with clean tap water. Using a thin pipette or tube (you can use a clean ballpoint pen), drop a little sunflower oil on the surface of the water. You will see that the first drop grows completely on the surface of the water. The next drops no longer grow, but are formed by lentils. Carefully pour out some of the water - the drops will spread over its surface.

In conclusion, let’s return once again to the experience with a spoonful of broth. By carefully observing the behavior of fat droplets, you will be able to notice very interesting phenomena of droplet rupture and recovery.

Figures 2-8 show photographs of the results of one of these experiments. We poured tap water tinted blue (not containing alcohol) into a clean glass cup. A glass tube lightly moistened with sunflower oil was carefully touched to the surface of the water and eight small drops were obtained (see Fig. 2). Then, using a rubber bulb, they took some water from the cup - all the drops increased in size. After the next such operation, the drops increased even more noticeably, while they were somewhat distorted in shape (due to the flow of water) and nuclei of ruptures appeared on them (see Fig. 3).

All further changes occurred spontaneously, without outside interference. At first, the gaps in the drops grew, then they merged into one large gap - the drop turned into a stake. Finally, this stake was torn and again pulled together into a drop (see Fig. 4-8).
