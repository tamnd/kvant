Physics and mathematics

So, the task of a theoretical physicist is to obtain relationships between observable quantities using mathematical calculations. Doesn't this mean that theoretical physics is something of applied mathematics? This point of view is completely wrong. Both in the nature of the problems and in the methods of approaching problems, mathematics and theoretical physics are categorically different.

In mathematics, the most important role is played by the mathematical aspects, that is, the logical impeccability of all conclusions arising from the accepted axioms, and the study of all logical possible relationships. The task of physics is to construct, as far as possible, an accurate picture of the world without strict “rules of the game”, using all known experimental and theoretical facts, using the basic intuition of the contract, which will later be tested experimentally. Thus, a mathematician explores all logical possible types of geometry, while a physicist finds out what geometric relationships are realized in our world.

A mathematician, even if he deals with applied problems, that is, problems that do not come from mathematics, takes on solving only those problems that do not require additional unproven propositions. A physicist, as a rule, deals with problems in which there are insufficient initial data.

II. QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

Let's try to show with simple examples how a theoretical physicist works at the first, most important stage of the work, when a qualitative analysis of the problem is done. As we will see, at this stage of work, almost without any calculations, rough relationships between the quantities involved in the problem are obtained. The next stage of work - obtaining precise quantitative relationships using the mathematical apparatus of the theory - is entirely based on the first stage, during which the physical picture of the phenomenon is clarified and a draft of the expected solution arises. Without such a proposal, one cannot begin to search for an exact solution. Indeed, it is possible to prove only those statements that were guessed in advance. There was almost no exception to this rule.

One of the main elements of qualitative analysis is solving the problem using simplified models in which all non-existent ones are discarded. Making a problem more difficult to solve is much easier than resolving a complex one.

In some simple cases, a simple dimensional analysis clarifies a lot about the problem of magnitude and the possibility of relationships between them.

Generalization example - oscillator

In all areas of physics, there are problems associated with flasks of near-position systems. To understand multi-phenomena, and therefore for a qualitative analysis of these phenomena, you need to know the general properties of such systems. Systems that vibrate around an equilibrium position, regardless of their structure, are called oscillators. The simplest examples of oscillations are an external weight, a mathematical mat. A more complex example is a tense country; it can have many types of vibrations: a vibration with an antinode in the middle (fundamental tone), with one node, two nodes, etc. (overtones). Thus, a country is a set of oscillators of different frequencies. A similar example is the column of air in the organization of labor; it can be made to vibrate with the highest frequency (fundamental tone) or with a higher frequency, when at some points the air is motionless (analogous to the nodes in string vibrators).

III. ULTIMATE SIMPLIFICATION

Basic idea of ​​quantum mechanics

Let's try to show with another example how, by simplifying the problem to the limit, we can determine the main features of a phenomenon. This example will highlight what a qualitative approach to a problem is.

According to quantum mechanics, the energy of an electron in an atom can only take discrete values.

Possible values ​​of the electron energy in hydrogen atom are given by the expression

$$E_n = - \frac{me^4}{\hbar^2} \frac{1}{2n^2}.$$

(**)

The differences in the values ​​of $E_n$ for two different $n$ (n=1,2,3,...) are determined with great accuracy the frequencies of the spectral lines observed in the experiment.

The basic idea of ​​quantum mechanics is that each particle (in this case, an electron) is characterized by an infinite wave process with a wavelength

$$\lambda = \frac{2\pi \hbar}{mv},$$

where $m$ is the mass of the particle, $v$ is its speed. Discrete values ​​of the electron energy are obtained from the condition that along the length of the orbit along which the electron moves, there must be

$$W_n = m v_n^2 / 2 = \hbar^2 n^2 / 2 m r^2.$$

Another generalization - quantum oscillators

The problem of quantizing an oscillator is no more difficult to solve. At the same time, we do not know how the oscillator is implemented - whether it is represented by a load oscillating externally, or an electric oscillatory circuit.

Let us denote by $q$ the generalized coordinate of the oscillator. Let's write down the energy of the oscillator:

$$E = \gamma q^2 / 2 + \beta q^2 / 2.$$

One can imagine that an oscillator is a kind of “particle” with mass β, oscillating external rigidity γ. In order to formulate the basic idea of ​​quantum mechanics for this object, we take the wavelength λ of the wave process associated with our “particle”:

$$\lambda = 2\pi \hbar / \beta q.$$

The denominator is the product of “mass” and its speed. Since the speed changes as the particle moves, the wavelength also changes - it is the minimum value of the equilibrium position and grows in those areas where the speed of the “particle” is low.

Let the “particle” move in the region from - $q_0$ to $q_0$. (We emphasize that $2q_0$ is not the swing of the oscillating oscillator, but the region in which its coordinate “trembles.”) In order to form a standing wave, an integral number of half-waves must be placed on the “length” $2q_0$: $2q_0 = (n+1)\lambda/2$ ($n=0, 1, 2, \dots$). As an estimate of the speed, we take the value of the speed at $q=q_0$, that is, $(q)_0 = 2\pi \hbar / \beta \lambda = \pi \hbar (n+1)/2\beta q_0$. Substituting this value into the expression for kinetic energy, we find:

$$W_n = \frac{\pi^2 \hbar^2}{8\beta} \frac{(n+1)^2}{q_0^2}.$$

For the total energy we get:

$$E_n(q_0) = \frac{(\pi \hbar)^2}{8\beta} \frac{(n+1)^2}{q_0^2} + \frac{\gamma q_0^2}{2}.$$

For small values ​​of $q_0$ the energy is high due to the first term, and for large values ​​of $q_0$ - due to the second. The approximate value of $q_0$, and even the least energy, will be obtained if we equate both terms. From this condition we find:

$$q_0^2 = \frac{\pi \hbar (n+1)}{2\sqrt{\beta \gamma}}.$$

Substituting into the expression for energy, we get:

$$E_n = \frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \sqrt{\gamma/\beta} (n+1) = \frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \omega (n+1) \quad (n=0, 1, 2, \dots).$$

The value $\omega = \sqrt{\gamma/\beta}$ represents the frequency of the oscillating classical oscillator.

An accurate calculation for energy results in:

$$E_n = \hbar \omega \left( n + \frac{1}{2} \right).$$

Thus, we made a mistake in the numerical factor ($\pi/2$ instead of 1), as well as in the numerical value of the energy in the lowest state, that is, at $n=0$ ($\frac{\pi}{2} \hbar \omega$ instead of $\frac{1}{2} \hbar \omega$). But everything else worked out exactly! Now that the result has been obtained, we should think about what we used to obtain it and what follows from the expressions we obtained for the oscillator energy and for the value $q_0^2$.

First of all, we applied to our oscillator, without being interested in how it is structured, the principles of quantum mechanics, first established in relation to electrons. Of course, it is natural to expect that the general principles should be the same for other particles whose masses differ from the mass of the electron. Indeed, such a generalization was confirmed with great accuracy by experiment. But why can these principles be applied to such a system as a resonant circuit, where the role of the “coordinate” is played by the charge on the capacitor plates? Here we come to an important assumption, which is widely used and used in theoretical physics of the 20th century. If two systems have energy that equally depends on coordinates and velocities, then such systems have the same properties, despite the fact that “coordinates” and “velocities” may have completely different meanings in these systems.

There was not a single example where this assumption contradicted experience. Therefore, we have the right to consider that we have solved the problem of applying quantum mechanics for all possible oscillators at once.
