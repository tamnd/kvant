Straight and curved

Mathematics is a large and very unique world, surprising with the beauty of conquered and unconquered peaks, openwork places thrown over abysses and connecting one mountainous country of facts and methods to another. However, these horizons that generate imagination are opened only to those who have managed to penetrate the world of mathematics, have learned to see the beauty hidden from the uninitiated, and have mastered the difficult ability to see a wonderful kaleidoscope of figures, numbers and mathematical logic in the world around them. But penetrating the world of mathematical ideas is not easy: in addition to abilities and interest, you also need authenticity, will, desire and the ability to work hard. Only a lot of systematic work and, in particular, problem solving can lead a student to success in mathematics.

But everything is there: interest in mathematics, passion, the desire to solve problems (especially “difficult ones”), perseverance, and hard work. And then the main question will arise: what to read and what problems to solve? After all, if the book you read turns out to be not imbued with the modern “spirit” of mathematics and its applications, or if you solve problems with methods that promote the development of good taste and research and broaden your horizons, then something must be lost in the education and formation of the future mathematician. But if the chosen book is successful, then it turns out to be a kind and experienced teacher and mentor, and this can serve as a guarantee that it will help in cultivating good taste, acquiring an outlook and research skills.

In our opinion, the book by N.B. Vasiliev and V.L. Gutenmacher “Straights and Curves”* is precisely such a kind and experienced mentor for a young mathematician. The book contains an interesting selection of problems, by solving which the reader - perhaps without realizing it - will become familiar with the spirit and ideas of modern mathematics. Connected here (especially in the presented solutions to key problems, which serve as a kind of beacon for the reader) are the languages ​​of set theory and the coordinate method used by various displacements and kinematic messages (which the authors form in the form of “ring theorem”), vector methods for solving the problem and logical connections between the elements of the condition. At the same time, the same problem is often considered from different points of view, solved by different methods, which allows you to compare these methods, acquiring the necessary horizons.

As you can see, this book is not intended for light reading, on the contrary, it teaches hard work,

Let the segment $KL$ (“staircase”) be fixed, and the lines $KO$ and $LO$ (“wall” and “floor”) rotate respectively around the points $K$ and $L$ so that the angle between them remains right. Problem 2. Point $O$ lies on the segment $AC$. Find the set of points $M$ for which $M\hat{O}C=2M\hat{A}C$ (Fig. 4).

Answer. Union of a circle with center $O$ of radius $AO$ (without point $A$) and ray $OC$ (without point $O$) - see figure 5.

Explanation. Let the point $M$ of the desired set not belong to the line $AO$. Let us prove that $|MO|=|AO|$. Let's construct a triangle $OAM$ (Fig. 6). By the theorem on the exterior angle of a triangle $O\hat{A}M+A\hat{M}O=M\hat{O}C$. By condition $M\hat{O}C=2M\hat{A}C$.

From the conditions of the problem we immediately obtain $O\hat{A}M=A\hat{M}O$; therefore, the triangle $AMO$ is equilateral, that is, $|OM|=|AO|$.

In the language of “movement”, the result of task 2 can be imagined as follows: let the straight line $AM$ rotate around the point $A$ with a constant angular velocity $\omega$ (that is, per unit time, rotate through an angle of magnitude $\omega$), and let the straight line $OM$ rotate around the point $O$ with an angular velocity $2\omega$, and at the initial moment time, both straight lines coincided with straight line $AO$ (Fig. 7). Then the point $M$ of intersection of these lines moves along a circle with center $O$.

From here we can extract the inscribed angle theorem (Theorem 62 from paragraph 111 of Geometry 8). If straight line $AM$ in time $t$ rotates from position $AM_1$ to position $AM_2$ by angle $\omega t$, then straight line $OM$ rotates through angle $2\omega t$, in other words, the inscribed angle $M_1AM_2$ is equal to the angular value of the corresponding central angle $M_1OM_2$ (Fig. 8).

This theorem can be formulated more clearly:

The theorem about quantity on a circle contains a small ring. A rod is rotated around the point $A$ of the circle and passes through this ring. If the rod rotates uniformly with angular velocity $\omega$, then the ring rotates uniformly around the circumference with angular velocity $2\omega$ (Fig. 9).

Another version of the theorem about an inscribed angle and a ring on a circle:
Theorem on rotating lines.
If two intersecting lines $l_A$ and $l_B$ rotate in the plane of the circumferential points $A$ and $B$ with the same angular velocity $\omega$ (Fig. 10); in this case, of course, the angle between them remains constant), the shape of the point of intersection of these straight lines is a circle.

Explanation. Let's construct a circle $\delta$ passing through three points: $A$, $B$ and another position $M_0$ the point of intersection of lines $l_A$ and $l_B$ (Fig. 11). According to the theorem “about a ring on a circle,” the intersection point of the line $l_A$ and the circle $\delta$ moves along the circle $\delta$ uniformly with an angular velocity $2\omega$. The intersection point $l_B$ with the circle $\delta$ moves in exactly the same way. Since at one point in time these points coincide (position $M_0$), they coincide.

They give it at any other time. This implies the statement of the theorem.

Explanation. Draw a circle and divide it into 12 equal parts. Draw through one of the division points $A$ and the remaining division points are straight lines, as well as the tangent to the circle at the point $A$ (Fig. 12); you will get a bundle of 12 straight lines, dividing the plane into 24 equal angles). By moving the pencil around the circle, you can make sure that when moving from the division point $M$ to the next division point, the straight line $AM$ rotates through the same angle. Select another division point $B$ (say, the fourth from $A$) and construct for it the same bundle of 12 straight lines as for point $A$. For each division point $M$, mark an acute angle between lines $AM$ and $BM$ (all these angles are the same!).

From the theorem on rotating lines it follows that if we continue all 23 constructed lines to the intersection, then all 110 intersection points (not counting $A$ and $B$) lie on the same circle - ten on each.

Problem 5. Along a stationary circle, touching it from the inside, without sliding, the input circle is less than its radius. What line do we describe from point K of the moving circle (Fig. 14)?

The answer to this puzzle problem is simple: the point moves in a straight line, more precisely, along the diameter of a stationary circle (Fig. 15). This result is called Copernicus' theorem.

Try to verify the validity of Copernicus' theorem in practice. It is not difficult to prove - you just need to remember the inscribed angle theorem.
