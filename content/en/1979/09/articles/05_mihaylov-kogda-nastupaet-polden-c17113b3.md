When is noon?

Before answering this question, let’s ask another: what is noon? It would seem that the word speaks for itself: noon is half the day, that is, the average moment between east and sunset. But is it possible, based on such a definition, to accurately establish the moment of noon, for example, to knock with a hammer and say: “Here it is noon”? Obviously not. Firstly, the moments of sunrise and sunset can only be reliably noticed at sea, where the horizon is perfectly correct. Secondly, sunset will occur several hours later than noon, and therefore the moment when noon occurs can only be determined after the end of the day.

However, there is a phenomenon extremely close to noon that can be observed - this is the cult

mination of the Sun, that is, the moment the Sun passes through the meridian of a given place. So, for moderate people, the Sun rises every day in the eastern part of the horizon, rises above it, culminates, passes through the meridian, then begins to decline and sets in the western part of the horizon.

The culmination of the luminaries is observed using special instruments called passage instruments. Their main element is the critical tube, which can only rotate in height around a horizontal axis directed from east to west. Such a tube always faces the meridiana plane (Fig. 1). For the convenience of the observer, the tube is sometimes made broken, reflecting the light in the horizontal direction through the full axis of rotation, at the end of which there is an eyepiece (Fig.2). Thanks to this, the eye always observes in a constant position and looks in a horizontal direction.

In the field of view of the pipe, several vertical thin threads, usually spider webs, are stretched: the middle thread sets the direction of the meridiana. Having given the pipe the desired slope, observe

The body waits until the observed body, in this case the Sun, enters the field of view and crosses the middle thread. At this moment he presses a button that closes the contact, and the pen of the recorder, called a chronograph, makes a mark on the running paper tape parallel to the second markers of the watch. In this way, the moment of passage of the luminary through the meridian can be recorded with an accuracy of hundredths of a second. (The center of the Sun is not marked by anything, so the passage of its two edges is observed and then the results obtained are successful.)

The described method is strongly related to the speed of reaction observed. Now it has been significantly improved, up to the automatic recording of the moment of climax: light from the Sun enters through a narrow slit onto a photocell, the latter sends a signal to the chronograph.

Now it would seem that the clocks are slowing by 12 seconds per day. What's the matter?

In reality, it is not the clock that is to blame, but the unevenness of the apparent movement of the Sun along the ecliptic. More precisely, the elliptical earth's orbit, as a result of which (according to Kepler's second law) the Earth moves quickly only near perihelion, passing an arc of 1°1'10'' per day, which happens around January 2, and slowest near aphelion, around July 5, when this arc is reduced to 0°57'12". In addition, there is another, less influential, reason for the discrepancy between the moments culmination of the Sun. Thus, it turned out that the time given by the apparent movement of the Sun, called true solar time, is quadruple uneven and is not suitable for precise use. Therefore, it was necessary to introduce a new concept - mean solar time, which is based on the average duration of true solar days for the whole year. In other words, we can say that mean solar time is given by an imaginary point called the mean sun, which moves uniformly along the celestial equator, making one revolution during the year.

The difference between mean and true solar time is called the equation of time. Over the course of a year, the equation of time fluctuates between +14.3 minutes (mid-February) and -16.4 minutes (early November), passing through zero four times (around April 16, June 14, September 2, and December 25). The graph of the changing equation of time is shown in figure 4. For example, during the month of December, the equation of time changes rapidly in a positive direction, causing progressive contamination of half a day from day to day. The same thing happens with the sunrise and sunset. Therefore, when after December 22 the length of the day begins to slowly increase, this increase completely drops in the evening, and the morning continues to infect for several more days. So, in Moscow, the latest sunrise was about 30 days, and the earliest sunset was around December 15.

True solar time is determined by the sundial (Fig. 5). In them, a teng ostern, parallel to the axis of rotation of the Earth, or an oblique edge of a vertical plate falls on a horizontal, vertical or inclined dial with divisions, depending on its orientation. If you add the equation of time to the reading of such a clock, you get mean solar time. When the clock shows 12 o'clock at this time, it is local mean noon. We say “local” because noon does not occur at the same time in different places (it depends on the geographical location).

In the old days, each city lived according to its own local time, so, for example, in Moscow the clock always showed 29 minutes more than in St. Petersburg. This created more inconvenience and even confusion in transport and international relations. Therefore, it was proposed to introduce what is called *zone time*. Everywhere within the same time zone, the boundaries of which differ in longitude by approximately 15°, the time is considered the same; In neighboring villages the time differs by one hour. The original, zero one, will be encroached upon by the one whose intermediaries will continue the Greenwich meridan. The posyas count goes from it to the east, and the posyas number indicates how many hours its time is ahead of Greenwich time, called *universal time*. So, Moscow and Leningrad are in the second posyas, and therefore

In the USSR, regular time was introduced in 1919, but in 1930, in order to save electricity for lighting, one hour was added to the regular time; such time began to be called maternity leave. In particular, the maternity time of the second hour is called Moscow.

Thus, there are many different timekeeping systems. But for each event, time that is absent in one system can be associated with time in another system.

For example, let's calculate the onset of local true noon. By definition, this noon occurs at 12 o'clock true time. According to the average time, this will be 12 hours, the equation of time is η minutes. Universal time at this moment will be 1/15 λ hours (or 4 λ minutes) less. Here λ is the eastern longitude of a given place in degrees, and the set at λ is obtained from the calculation that the Earth rotates around its axis 360 degrees in 24 hours, so that 1° corresponds to 1/15 of an hour (or 4 minutes). To obtain the apparent time, you need to add n hours (or 60 n minutes) to the found universal time, where n is the number of the hour position. Finally, maternity time will be 1 hour (or 60 minutes) longer. Thus, true noon in a given place occurs according to decree time at the moment

t=12 h+η min -1/15 λ h+(n+1) h.
In this formula, only the equation η is variable, depending on the date; from year to year it repeats with small fluctuations associated with the incomplete coincidence of the duration of the calendar year with the year of nature (tropical year), equal to 365, 2422 days.
