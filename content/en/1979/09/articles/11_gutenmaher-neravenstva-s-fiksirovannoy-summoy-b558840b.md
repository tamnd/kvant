Problems about flour

In the conditions of the first problems we are talking about triplets of numbers a, b, c, related by the relation a+b+c=1, in the third part - about the method of depicting such tracks on a plane.

Problem 1. Three bags contain 1 cup of flour. In addition, it is known that there is no more flour in the first package than in the second, and in the second - no more than in the third.

a) Can the third bag contain 2/5 of flour?

b) Could the third bag contain 1/5 of a cup of flour?

b) How much flour, at least, can be in the third bag?

d) How much flour, at most, can there be in the third bag?

a) Answer. Maybe. Let's give an example. Let's distribute the flour like this: put 3/10 kg of flour in the first and second bags, and 2/5 kg in the third. Then all conditions are met: 3/10 + 3/10 + 2/5 = 1, and the first bag contains no more flour than the second (3/10 ≤ 3/10), and the second bag contains no more flour than the third (3/10 ≤ 2/5).

b) Answer. No, it can't. Let's prove it. Let’s say that the third bag contains 1/5 kg, then the second bag contains no more than 1/5 kg, and the first bag also contains no more than 1/5 kg. But then in all three packages there is no more than 3/5 ke of flour,

which contradicts the conditions. Therefore, our assumption (that there are $1/5$ kg in the third package) is not correct.

c) Answer. The third package contains, at most, $1/3$ kg. Let's prove it. Let us first show that the third package contains no less than $1/3$ kg. Let's assume the opposite: let there be less than $1/3$ kg of flour. Then both the second and first bags also contain less than $1/3$ kg, but this means that all three bags contain less than 1 kg of flour, which contradicts the conditions. So, the third package contains at least $1/3$ kg. Let us now show that the third bag can contain $1/3$ kg of flour. If you pour $1/3$ kg into each bag, then the total will be 1 kg and there will be no more flour in the first bag than in the second, and in the second – no more than in the third.

d) Answer. The third package contains, at most, 1 kg. In fact, according to the conditions, it cannot be more than 1 kg, and 1 kg will be obtained when there is no flour at all in the first two bags.

Instead of flour - numbers and angles

Let us turn again to problem 1. It can be formulated in another way:

Problem 4. About the numbers $a, b, c$ it is known that $a+b+c=1$ and $0 \leq a \leq b \leq c$.

a) Is it possible with equal $2/5$?

b) Is it possible with equal $1/5$?

b) Set the smallest value to $c$.

d) Set the smallest value to $c$.

The solution to problem 4 can be written as follows:

a) $c$ can be equal to $2/5$. For example, $c=2/5, a=3/10, b=3/10$. then $3/10+3/10+2/5=1$ and $3/10 \leq 2/5$.

b) $c$ cannot be equal to $1/5$. In fact, if $c=1/5$, then $b \leq 1/5, a \leq 1/5$, therefore $a+b+c<1$, which is impossible. So $c \geq 1/3$. Let us show that $c$ can equal $1/3$. In fact, if we put $a=b=c=1/3$, all conditions will be satisfied.

Problem 14. Given an equilateral triangle. Find the set of points inside it:

a) allocated from side $AB$ no more than from side $BC$;

b) allocated from side $BC$ no more than from side $AC$;

b) satisfying simultaneous conditions a) and b).

The answer to question a) is shown in color on Fig. 1a), to question b) - on Fig. 1b).

It turns out that problem 14b) is closely related to problem 1. In order to bridge the gap between these problems, we solve the following problem:

Problem 15. Prove that the sum of the distances from any point inside an equilateral triangle $ABC$ to its side is equal to the length $h$ of its altitude.

Solved and e. Take an arbitrary point $M$ inside the triangle $ABC$ and drop from it the redistributions of the lares on the sides $AB, BC$ and $AC$. Let the lengths of the resulting segments be equal to $h_1, h_2$ and $h_3$; these are the distances from point $M$ to the side. It is necessary to prove that $h_1+h_2+h_3=h$. Let's connect point $M$ with vertices $A, B$ and $C$. Obviously, $S_{ABM}+S_{CBM}+S_{CMA}=S_{ABC}$, where $S_{ABM}, S_{CBM}, S_{CMA}, S_{ABC}$ are the areas of triangles $ABM, CBM, CMA, ABC$, respectively. Let us denote the length of the side of the triangle $ABC$ by $a$ and rewrite this equality as follows:

$$\frac{ah_1}{2}+\frac{ah_2}{2}+\frac{ah_3}{2}=\frac{ah}{2}.$$

Hence we prove the equality: $h_1+h_2+h_3=h$.

Addition to problem 15. The statement of this problem is true not only for the interior points of the triangle, but also for the points lying on its sides and at the vertices. (At the same time, we assume that if a point lies on a side of a triangle, then its distance to this side is zero.) Prove this on its own.
