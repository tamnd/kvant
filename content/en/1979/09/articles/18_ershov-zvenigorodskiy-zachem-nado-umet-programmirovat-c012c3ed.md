Why do you need to know how to program?

The widespread use of computers led to the emergence of a new science and a new type of occupation, a new profession - programming.

It is important to immediately understand that programming is more than the ability to communicate commands to a machine. It is important for the person himself and has a noticeable impact on him. In a certain sense, the person himself and even groups of people often act according to certain programs. Moreover, we ourselves live in a world of programming, sometimes without realizing it.

World of the program
A program is a set of commands that has two important properties: completeness - each command is very simple and, being separate, can always be executed; certainty - at any moment it is known which command is executed next.

These two properties of the program are perhaps the most important: they explain why the program provides the computer with automatic and continuous operation, leading to a useful result.

Let us give an example of a purely mathematical program in which these two properties do not appear:

Initial state: given two natural numbers.

Step 1. If the numbers are equal, take the first number as the result and stop.

Step 2. If they are not equal, subtract the less from the large and the difference, the less of which is controlled by its complex programming. Whether we walk, breathe, read, endure the cold, toss and turn in our sleep - all this is the result of the action of many biological programs introduced into us genetically (inheritance), or formed under the influence of the environment, or developed consciously. It would not be a great exaggeration to say that almost everything we do is done according to a program, and when we reflect, we mainly adjust our old programs and make plans for the future.

The ability to plan one's actions in advance is the most important feature of human behavior. Outstanding programmers in this sense are sportsmen, astronauts, artists, surgeons - in a word, all people of constant, complex and error-free action. At the same time, driven programmers are also cautious, timid-looking guys who, when they find themselves in a new environment, overwhelm their elders with questions like “What’s next?”, “What if?”, “Where?”, “What then?” etc.

Since ancient times, it seemed that children learn mainly by imitation, by imitating the behavior of their elders. Only recently, when the learning process was smoothed out through the magnifying glass of logical analysis, it was discovered that not only mental, but even many motor skills of a person are developed only by understanding their nature, finding elementary movements and positions, realizing and consolidating them in consciousness in

Computer programs

Now is the time to ask what the special role of the computer is in this program world. The fact is that it was computers, with their appearance and existence, that made programs the subject of mathematical research and created programming as the most widespread mathematical profession.

Mathematicians and philosophers, in collaboration with biologists and engineers, have convincingly shown that the laws of control and information processing, algorithms and programs related to biological systems, on the one hand, and to automatic machines, on the other, are structured in the same way; the same mathematical abstractions and theories are suitable for them. Computers, thanks to their enormous speed and ability to execute any program, make it possible in principle to reproduce any “correct” action. This makes the computer not only our obedient and oaring helper, but also a judge of the canvases of our knowledge about the action that we want to transfer to the machine.

Let's return to our two-command program, which, as readers will probably agree, calculates the greatest common divisor of two numbers. In order for a computer to execute this program, it must, of course, “know,” for example, what it means to “subtract a smaller number from a larger one.” At the same time, she will always and successfully work to obtain a result, without having any idea what the greatest common divisor is. But the programmer, the rich program, must know very well what the machine will do and what properties the corresponding program has.

Task. Prove that the above program for any pair of original natural numbers produces a result equal to their greatest common division.

So, the program asks our complete knowledge about the problem, and, unlike a theorem or essay, in an active, repeatedly voluntary form. A machine powered by this program will calculate the greatest common divisor as well as the wisest schoolboy who has mastered argumentation. It is in the constant transfer of human ability to computers to consistently perform purposeful operations that the essence of the scientific and technological revolution associated with the automation of mental work lies.

"Second Literacy"

More than five hundred years ago, mankind invented printing. The immediate purpose of this invention was the desire to speed up the process of rewriting church books. Its objective result was an extraordinary increase in the speed of human progress through universal literacy and the spread of the printed word.

Electronic embroidery machines appeared approximately 35 years ago, at the height of the Second World War. The immediate purpose of their first initial use was to speed up calculations in the development of weapons systems. However, we can already see the time when computers, created in ten million copies, will be our faithful and understandable partners in all corners of the Earth and in every type of our activity.

By this time, programming, that is, the ability to express any “correct” process by means available for transmission to a machine, becomes the second literacy of each processed target. But this is in the future. And now the challenge facing LINK is to see the “second illiteracy.” You have to master the basics of programming.

To do this, first of all, it is necessary to answer the question - what to reduce computers?

Will machines just calculate?

But computing is no longer the only and, apparently, not the main area where a computer comes to the aid of humans.

The Sirena system sells tickets
Let's look at one example. As you know, modern airplanes can take you to the most isolated point in our country within a few hours. But issuing a plane ticket sometimes takes much longer than the flight itself. Booking seats is especially time-consuming if you need to transfer to another plane, for example, in Kyiv, Gorky or another large city. The Aeroflot agency where you booked your ticket sends a telephone message to the transfer point with a request to reserve seats for you on such and such a flight. The dispatcher responsible for this flight opens the corresponding table, cancels the reserved seats and compiles a response telephone number, on the basis of which tickets will be issued to you. All this takes three to four days - this is if everything is in order, not a single piece of paper is stuck anywhere and there are empty seats on the desired flight.

But if you have to transfer to another plane in Moscow, then issuing tickets will most likely take no more than two to three minutes. The fact is that ticket bidding in Moscow is carried out by the automated Sirena system, which is based on a powerful computer.

Special remote controls for communication with this computer - the so-called terminals - are installed in most large cities of our country. Each remote control has keys similar to the keyboard of a common typewriter, a small screen on which messages from the computer appear, and a printing device.

Let's assume that we came to the Novosibirsk operator of the Sirena system with a request to issue several tickets for one of the Moscow-Kharkov flights. The operator types the appropriate request on the keyboard, and after one or two seconds the machine’s response appears on the screen. During these seconds, a computer located several thousand kilometers from Novosibirsk looked through the schedule, chose a flight convenient for us, determined how many empty seats were on it, responded to the seats and reported their numbers to the Novosibirsk operator - in a word, it sold all the work that took several days.

If the seats offered by the machine accept us, the operator types a confirmation message on the console, and then the computer is filled in that these seats are reserved for passengers who will arrive on such and such a flight from Novosibirsk.

If we now remember how many passengers fly into and out of Moscow airports every day, and try to imagine how many people simultaneously contact Siren operators in different cities, then perhaps we will feel what a huge job the “brain” of the system does.

What does the computer do in the system?

Firstly, it must keep track of the schedule of all planes departing from Moscow, and an accurate list of free and occupied seats on these planes, at least two weeks in advance, in other words, the machine must store information about the schedule and allocation of seats on planes.

Secondly, the machine must receive from the terminals and respond to the request, that is, conduct a dialogue with a person (possibly with a large number of people at the same time) using terminals.

Thirdly, she must be able, if necessary, to name the information stored in memory: to note that the seats that were free are now reserved, that changes have been made to the schedule - that is, information processing is a central process.

The processor is the brain and heart of the computing system: it controls the entry of information into memory and reading it from memory, it receives requests from terminals and transmits human messages with their help, it performs its necessary processing of information stored in memory and coming from terminals.

While discussing the skills required by a computer to work in the Siren system, we said nothing about the ability to calculate; there is almost no need to calculate it - except perhaps to calculate the cost of a discount ticket for a schoolchild or the number of remaining empty seats on the plane.

In many other automated systems, very similar to “Sirena”, calculations come much more, but it is hardly possible to come up with a task for which the machine would only have to count: it is always necessary to remember what information (albeit a series of calculations and intermediate results), it is almost always necessary to accept (enter) some information (for example, initial data for a calculation) and report something to a person or a technical device (for example, another computer), that is, to output information.

And the man?

Now you can ask: what should a person who works with a computer and creates tasks for it—programs—should be able to do?

As an answer, we quote from an article by one of the authors “On human and aesthetic factors in programming”:

“Programmict must have a first-class mathematician's ability for abstraction and logical thinking, combined with an Edisonian talent for constructing anything from zero and one. He must combine the accuracy of an accountant with the insight of an intelligence officer, the imagination of the author of detective novels with the sober practicality of an economist. And in addition, Programmict must have a taste for teamwork, understand the interests of the user, and much more.”
