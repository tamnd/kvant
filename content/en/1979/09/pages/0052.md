⟦folio 50⟧

Special remote controls for communication with this computer - the so-called terminals - are installed in most large cities of our country. Each remote control has keys similar to the keyboard of a common typewriter, a small screen on which messages from the computer appear, and a printing device.

Let's assume that we came to the Novosibirsk operator of the Sirena system with a request to issue several tickets for one of the Moscow-Kharkov flights. The operator types the appropriate request on the keyboard, and after one or two seconds the machine’s response appears on the screen. During these seconds, a computer located several thousand kilometers from Novosibirsk looked through the schedule, chose a flight convenient for us, determined how many empty seats were on it, responded to the seats and reported their numbers to the Novosibirsk operator - in a word, it sold all the work that took several days.

If the seats offered by the machine accept us, the operator types a confirmation message on the console, and then the computer is filled in that these seats are reserved for passengers who will arrive on such and such a flight from Novosibirsk.

If we now remember how many passengers fly into and out of Moscow airports every day, and try to imagine how many people simultaneously contact Siren operators in different cities, then perhaps we will feel what a huge job the “brain” of the system does.

What does the computer do in the system?

Firstly, it must keep track of the schedule of all planes departing from Moscow, and an accurate list of free and occupied seats on these planes, at least two weeks in advance, in other words, the machine must store information about the schedule and allocation of seats on planes.

Secondly, the machine must receive from the terminals and respond to the request, that is, conduct a dialogue with a person (possibly with a large number of people at the same time) using terminals.

Thirdly, she must be able, if necessary, to name the information stored in memory: to note that the seats that were free are now reserved, that changes have been made to the schedule - that is, information processing is a central process.

The processor is the brain and heart of the computing system: it controls the entry of information into memory and reading it from memory, it receives requests from terminals and transmits human messages with their help, it performs its necessary processing of information stored in memory and coming from terminals.

While discussing the skills required by a computer to work in the Siren system, we said nothing about the ability to calculate; there is almost no need to calculate it - except perhaps to calculate the cost of a discount ticket for a schoolchild or the number of remaining empty seats on the plane.

In many other automated systems, very similar to “Sirena”, calculations come much more, but it is hardly possible to come up with a task for which the machine would only have to count: it is always necessary to remember what information (albeit a series of calculations and intermediate results), it is almost always necessary to accept (enter) some information (for example, initial data for a calculation) and report something to a person or a technical device (for example, another computer), that is, to output information.
