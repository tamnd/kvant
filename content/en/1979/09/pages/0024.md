Tasks

M581 — M585; F593— F597

M581. a) Is there a three-digit number whose cube ends in three sevens?
b) For any set of digits, the last of which is not 0, is there a cube ending with this set of digits?

A. Brotsky

M582. A quadrilateral with mutually perpendicular diagonals is inscribed in a circle with center O. Prove that the distance from point O to each side is equal to half the length of the opposite side.

A. Kelarev

M583. Sets of stones are considered, the mass of each of which is no more than 2 kg, and the total mass of the set is 50 kg. From such a set, several stones are selected, the total mass of which differs from 10 kg by the smallest possible number D for a given set. What is the largest value that the number D can take for all possible sets of stones?

A. Waitrob, A. Pechkovsky

M584. Is it possible to imagine the entire space as a union of lines, each two of which are intersecting (that is, do not lie in the same plane)?

F. Weinstein

M585. N scientists were present at the chemical conference - chemists and alchemists, and there were more chemists than alchemists. It is known that chemists answer the truth to any question, and alchemists sometimes tell the truth, sometimes they tell the truth. A mathematician who happens to be at the conference must determine for each scientist whether he is a chemist or an alchemist. To do this, he can ask any scientist the question: “Who is such and such a chemist or an alchemist?” (In particular: “Who are you?”). Prove that a mathematician can figure out everything that is required

a) for 4N questions; 6) for 2N - 2 questions;

b) * try to come up with a way to establish who is a chemist and who is an alchemist in fewer questions (the authors know of a rather cumbersome way that allows you to do this in [3N/2] questions).

S. Konyashin, P. Blecker
