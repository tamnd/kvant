1. Spring stiffness $k$ in the non-stretching position has a length $l_0$. A body of mass $m$ is suspended from the lower end of the spring, and the upper end of the spring is attached to a vertical axis around which the spring can rotate (Fig. 3). Draw a graph of the dependence of the angle $\alpha$ formed by the spring with the axis on the value of the angular velocity $\omega$ of the rotation of the spring. Assume that the angular velocity changes so slowly that at each value $\omega$ the motion has time to establish itself. Neglect the mass of the spring.

2. In a cylinder closed by a piston, at a temperature of $T_1$ there is $m$ kg of liquid water and water vapor (there are no other gases). The volume of the cylinder at temperature $T_1$ is equal to $V_1$. The piston is suspended externally with a rigidity of $k$, and the spring is in a relaxed state. The cylinder is then slowly heated to a temperature of $T_2$, and the water evaporates. Find the steady pressure under the piston. The area of ​​the piston is $S$, the density of saturated water vapor at a temperature is $T_1$ $\rho_{H1}$, the density of water at this temperature is $\rho_1$. Let us expand the cylinder when heated and neglect the three pistons against the cylinder walls. The molar mass of water is $\mu$.

3. A closed wire of length $l$ with a constant cross-section $\sigma$ and resistivity $\rho$ is rolled into a flat figure eight so that the ratio of the radii of the resulting circles is equal to 2. The resulting circuit is placed in an alternating uniform magnetic field, and the plane of the circuit is perpedicular to the field. Find the current flowing through the wire if the rate of change of the magnetic field is constant and equal to $k$.

equation of the line $l_i$ (the reader can easily find the numbers $b_i, c_i, d_i$, but we don’t need them). Let us set $F(x, y) = (b_1 x + c_1 y + d_1)(b_2 x + c_2 y + d_2)$ $G(x, y) = (b_3 x + c_3 y + d_3)(b_4 x + c_4 y + d_4)$ and consider the family of curves $\alpha F(x, y) + \beta G(x, y) = 0$ depending on the parameters $\alpha, \beta$. Let us substitute the coordinates of the point $E$ into this equation and then that both numerical coefficients $F(x_0; y_0), G(x_0; y_0)$ cannot vanish simultaneously (otherwise $E$ would coincide with one of the point $A, B, C, D$!). Therefore, it is possible to select the values ​​of the parameters $\alpha_0, \beta_0$ so that a numerical identity is obtained. Then it is clear that equation $\alpha_0 F(x; y) + \beta_0 G(x; y) = 0$ gives the required curve. We leave the proof of the uniqueness of the curve to the reader. (Ukaza and e. Assume that there are two of them - $F_1(x, y), F_2(x, y)$ - and show that for some $\alpha, \beta$ the second-order curve $\alpha F_1 + \beta F_2 = 0$ intersects a certain straight line at those points, which is impossible.)

The proof outlined above establishes a more general fact - the so-called Pascal's theorem: through any 5 points, no three of which lie on the same line, there is exactly one curve of the second order.

Let's now take a closer look at our figure. The poles are straight (our points $A, B, C, D$ connecting in pairs) and two parabolas (containing all 4 points) divide the plane into 5 parts: blue, pink, dashed (pink and blue) and white. The white part is filled with ellipses (one of them is shown), and the other four are filled with hyperbolas (two of them are shown).

Our last year's readers can take advantage of the well-known meaning of the degenerate case: two of the straight lines connecting 5 parts: “elliptic” and four “hyperbolic”.
