V. Birth of the theory of relativity

Many prominent physicists and mathematicians (especially Lorentz and Poincaré) thought about how to describe optical phenomena in a moving coordinate system. Einstein outlined his views on this issue in the article “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies.” It was received by the editor on July 30, 1905.

In mechanics, all inertial systems are equally oriented (Halley's principle). Einstein begins with a broader hypothesis: “... for all coordinate systems for which the mechanics equation is valid, the same electrodynamic and optical laws are valid...”. And further he describes the theory as follows: “We intend to turn this assumption (the content of which will be further called the “relative principle”) into a prerequisite and, in addition, make an additional assumption, which is with the first only in every contradiction, namely, that light in emptiness always propagates with a certain speed $v$, does not depend on the state of motion of the best body...”.

One of the main conclusions of the theory, which the following did not want to accept its critics, was that the duration of any physical process and the interval between two of its own turn out to be different from the point of view of reporting systems that move at different speeds. In short, in different frames of reference, moving in a different relative manner, time is marked differently. Now this is a firmly established fact. Mesons, “born” in the upper layers of the atmosphere at an altitude of, say, 10 km, reach the Earth’s surface. Moving at a speed close to the speed of light, mezons travel this distance in approximately $10^{-4}$ s, although at rest a mezon decays in $2 \cdot 10^{-8}$ s. From the point of view of the theory of relativity, everything is clear: in the system where mez ends, it has not yet had time to pass $2 \cdot 10^{-8}$ s. Having remained in the device on Earth, mezon decays (this is exactly how it was discovered). Many such examples can now be given. Einstein's discovery has firmly entered the foundation of modern physics.

Three portraits were brought into Einstein's office: Newton, Faraday and Maxwell. In his theory of relativity, he combined mechanics, created by Newton, and the theories of electricity and magnetism, created by Faraday and Maxwell, into a single science.
