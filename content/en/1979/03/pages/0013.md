The calculation shows that it will deviate from the straight line by 0.85." This small deviation was calculated back in 1801 by the German astronomer Zolder.

In 1915, Einstein obtained a different result: according to the theory of gravity he created, the deviation in the field of the Sun should be 1.7." The "doubling" was effective due to the fact that the laws of Euclid's geometry ceased to apply near the Sun and, as a result, Newton's law of universal gravitation itself changes. Bodies near the Sun are closer to it more strongly than follows from the law of universal gravitation.

In 1915, Einstein was no longer an unknown expert. The whole world listened to his words. Even as war raged in Europe, astronomers began discussing how to observe the predicted deviation. After all, in 1919 there should be a solar eclipse, during which it would be possible to check whether the effect predicted by Einstein exists. The eclipse was expected on May 29, when rays of light from a multitude of bright stars passed near the Sun. In April, two expeditions left England. One headed to Brazil, the second to the island of Prisilia off the western coast of Africa. On May 29, it rained all morning on the island, but just before the eclipse it needed to clear up, and the expedition decided to take photographs. Still, the weather did its job: the clouds obscured the stars, and when the images were processed days later, only one image gave good confirmation of Einstein’s effectiveness. Pictures from Brazil were transferred to England. Only four months later, on September 22 (regular postal communications were disrupted by the war!), a telephone message arrived from Lorenz from Holland: “Eddington discovered a mixture of stars at the edge of the Sun...”. Of course, no observation can prove a theory conclusively, but since then few have doubted Einstein's theory. Only a small group of old physicists tried (and with great persistence) to deny her conclusions.

But the time of classical physics has passed. Now there are many facts confirming the general theory of relativity. The most important thing for physicists of our time is that without this theory it is impossible to understand how the Universe develops.

XII. Evolution of the Universe

Perhaps the most impressive thing about the building built by Einstein is his cosmological work. In 1917, a work entitled “The Question of Cosmology and the General Theory of Relativity” was published.

This work stands out for its boldness. Like Newton, who declared that the law of the fall of a body on Earth is the same as the laws governing the motion of celestial bodies, Einstein declared that the laws of general relativity, tested within the solar system, are valid for the entire Universe.
