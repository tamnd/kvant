I remember, for example, that the Pythagorean theorem was shown to me by my uncle even before the bound book on geometry fell into my hands. With great difficulty I managed to “prove” this theorem using similar triangles; at the same time, it seemed to me, however, “obvious” that the ratio of the sides of a right triangle should be completely determined by one of its acute angles. In general, it seemed to me that it was necessary to prove only what was not “obvious” in this sense. And the objects with which geometry deals did not seem to me to be of a different nature than “visible and tangible” objects, that is, objects perceived by the senses. This primitive understanding is based, of course, on the fact that unconsciously taking into account the connection between geometric concepts and observable objects (length - solid rod, etc.) ...

Although it looked as if by pure reflection one could obtain reliable information about the objects observed, such a “miracle” was based on an error. Nevertheless, to those who experience this “miracle” for the first time, the very fact that a person is capable of achieving such a degree of reliability and purity in the abstraction of the mind that the Greeks first showed us in geometry seems surprising...

Between the ages of 12 and 16, I became familiar with the elements of mathematics, including the basics of differential and integral calculus. At the same time, in my experience, I came across books in which not too much attention was paid to logical structure, but the main idea was highlighted well. This whole activity was a fascinating concept; it contained uplifts that were as impressive as the “miracle” of elementary geometry—the basic idea of ​​analytical geometry, infinite series, the concepts of differential and integral. I was also lucky enough to get an idea of ​​the most important results and methods of the natural sciences from a very well-organized publication, in which the presentation is almost entirely limited to the qualitative side of the issue (Bernstein’s natural scientific books for the people - a work in 5-6 volumes); I read these books without taking a breath. By the time I entered the Zurich Polytechnic as a physics and mathematics student at the age of 17, I was already somewhat familiar with theoretical physics.

There I had excellent teachers (for example, Hurwitz, Minkowski), so, strictly speaking, I could receive a solid mathematical education. I do most of the intensive work in the physical laboratory, fascinated by direct contact with experience.
