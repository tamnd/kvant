Bose's stats, unconditionally accepting the hypothesis of light quanta and distributing them collected as an ideal gas, derived a formula for the distribution of quanta by energy in exactly the same way as formulas for an ideal gas are derived in kinetic theory.
At the same time, Bose believed (although he did not emphasize this in his work) that quanta are absolutely identical if they have the same energies. Planck's formula was obtained absolutely strictly. This means that Einstein’s hypothesis about the reality of quantum is actually proven by Planck’s formula, but no one was able to notice this. Einstein was delighted that quanta could be treated like atoms. He translated Bose's article into German and sent it himself to the magazine, where it was published. In a note, Einstein wrote: “Bose’s derivation of Planck’s formula is a great achievement. The method he used also gives the quantum theory of an ideal gas, which I will expound elsewhere."
He saw something completely new in Bose's formula, which led him to a new discovery.

IX. Bose-Einstein statistics

The idea that Einstein had when he read Bose's paper was that if, using the hypothesis of the identity of photons with the same energies, it was possible to derive Planck's formula, then one could try to apply the same method to an ideal quantum monatomic gas, the atoms of which have a non-zero mass.

The result was great. Srouz immediately came up with formulas that describe all the thermal properties of such a gas, and it was possible, continuing the analogy with photons, to think about the unexpected wave properties of particles, which Louis de Broglie wrote about in his dissertation in 1924.

In 1924, at a physics conference in the Austrian city of Isbruck, Einstein drew attention to the fact that de Broglie's theory implies the existence of diffraction and interference phenomena in molecular beams.

In the theory of monatomic gas, he effectively used the idea of ​​quanta to derive the equation of state of a quantum ideal gas and study its other properties.

The new theory was called Bose-Einstein statistics. It was created a year before complete quantum mechanics appeared. Further development of the theory led to the creation of the theory of superfluidity and many other amazing phenomena in the quantum world. But Einstein himself was not satisfied. He continued to believe that Bose's method "...is by no means indisputable, but seems justified only due to its success in the case of radiation...". Although Einstein was the first to introduce the quantum hypothesis in the theory of light, until the end of his days he could not apply the dual nature of particles and radiation, trying to find a different description of reality. Thus, until the end of his days, the founder of quantum theory was unable to apply it to modern quantum physics.
