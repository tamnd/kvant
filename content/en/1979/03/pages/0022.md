Now about the principle of conservation of mass. Mass is defined as a body's resistance to acceleration (inertial mass). It is also determined by body weight (heavy mass). The fact that two definition columns lead to the same value for body weight is in itself striking. According to the principle of conservation (namely: mass remains unchanged during any physical or chemical transformations), mass is an essential (due to its immutability) characteristic of matter. Heating, melting, evaporation, and the formation of chemical compounds should not change the total mass.

Physicists considered this principle to be valid several decades ago. However, he proved to be unsustainable in the face of the special theory of relativity. Therefore, it merged with the law of conservation of energy, just as approximately sixty years earlier the law of conservation of mechanical energy merged with the law of conservation of body. We could say that the law of conservation of energy, having previously absorbed the law of conservation of body, now includes the principle of conservation of mass and controls everything individually.

The equivalence of mass and energy is usually expressed (although this is not entirely accurate) by the formula $E = mc^2$, where $c$ is the speed of light, which is about 186,000 miles/sec, $E$ is the energy contained in a body at rest, $m$ is its mass. The energy corresponding to the mass $m$ is equal to this mass multiplied by the square of the speed of light, i.e., a huge amount of energy comes per unit of mass.

If every gram of a substance contains a columnar amount of energy, then why should this circumstance remain unchanged? The answer is quite simple: until the energy reaches the value $E/c^2$. I can easily impart energy to a certain body by heating it, for example, by ten degrees. So why has the mass gain, or weight gain, associated with this change never been replaced? The fact is that, in principle, mass a huge factor $c^2$ is included in the denominator of the fraction. The increase in mass is too small to be changed directly by even the most sensitive scales.

For the increase in mass to be modified, the change in energy per unit mass must be incredibly large. We know of only one phenomenon where such an amount of energy per unit mass is released; this is radioactive decay. Schematically, the process goes as follows: an atom with mass $M$ is split into two atoms with masses $M'$ and $M''$, which are separated with enormous kinetic energy. If we leave these atoms, i.e., take away the energy of motion from them, then in total they will have much less energy than the original atom. According to the principle of equivalence, the total mass of $M' + M''$ decay products should be somewhat less than the initial mass $M$ of decaying atoms, which contradicts the old principle of conservation of mass. The relative difference between these masses is approximately a fraction of a percent.
