Autobogafic notes

In 1949, Albert Einstein wrote Autobogaphic Notes. In them, he spoke about the basic scientific principles that guided his work. Shortly before his death, in 1955, Einstein also wrote a short, spectacled “Autobogathic Sketches” - memories of his student years and how the general theory of relativity was born. We present here excerpts from these works concerning the early period of the life of the great scientist.

...In 1895, at the age of sixteen, I came from Italy to Zorich, after spending a year in Milan with my parents without schools and without a teacher. My goal was to enter the polytechnic, although I did not have a very clear idea of ​​how this could be achieved. I was a wayward but modest young man who acquired my necessary knowledge sporadically, mainly through self-education. I thirsted for deep knowledge, but learning did not seem to me an easy task: I was poorly suited to memorization and had a poor memory. It was with a feeling of well-founded uncertainty that I took the advanced exam for the engineering department. The examination showed me the woeful inadequacy of my preparation, despite the fact that the examiners were lenient and full of sympathy. I understood that my failure was completely justified. The only gratifying thing was that the physicist G. Weber told me that I could listen to his colleagues if I stayed in Zorich. But the rector, Professor Albin Herzog, recommended me to the Kantenal School in Aaru, where, after a year of study, I took the matriculation exam. This school left its mark thanks to its liberal spirit and the humble service of teachers who did not rely on any ostentatious authority; comparison with six years of education in an authoritarian-run German gymnasium convincingly showed me how an education in two freedoms and a sense of personal responsibility is superior to an education that is based on drill, external authority and ambition. True democracy is not empty or an illusion.

That same year in Aaru, I had a question: if it were possible to chase a light wave at the speed of light, would we have a wave field in front of us that does not depend on time? This still seems impossible! This was the first children's thought experiment that related to the special theory of relativity. Discovery is not a matter of logical thinking, even if the final product is associated with a logical form.

1896-1900 — training at the department of teachers of special disciplines at the Swiss Polytechnic. I soon noticed that I was content with the role of a consistent student. In order to be a good student, you need to have ease of perception, a willingness to concentrate your energies on everything that is read in lectures, a love of order in order to write down and then conscientiously process what is taught in lectures. I was fundamentally lacking in all these qualities, as I regrettably discovered... I listened to some of the lectures with great interest. But usually I “walked around” a lot and spent time studying at home the luminaries of theoretical physics... This extensive self-sufficient training was a simple continuation of an earlier introduction.

I really did not have enough mathematical abilities to distinguish the central and Fundamental from the peripheral and unprincipally important.

During these student years, a real friendship developed with fellow student Marcel Grossman. Once a week, we triumphantly went with him to the Metropol cafe on the Limmat embankment and talked not only about studies, but, moreover, about all the things that might interest young people with open eyes. He was not as crazy and eccentric as I was, but he was connected with the Swiss environment and, to the extent possible, did not lose his independence. In addition, he had in abundance exactly the data that was lacking: quick recovery and order in all respects. He not only attended all the lectures that we considered important, but also presented them so wonderfully that if his periodicals had been reprinted, they could have been published in full. To prepare for exams, he lent me these terdis, which served as my lifeline; It’s better not to guess how I would live without them...

For me there is no doubt that our thinking proceeds mainly bypassing symbols (words), and, moreover, unconsciously. If this were different, then why should we sometimes be “surprised,” moreover, spontaneously, at this or that uprising? This “act of surprise” apparently occurs when ascension comes into conflict with the world of understanding that is sufficiently established in us. In cases where such a conflict is experienced acutely and intensely, it in turn has a strong impact on our mental world. The development of this mental world is, in a certain sense, an overcoming of the sense of wonder - a continuous flight from the “amazing”, from the “miracle”.

I experienced a miracle of this kind as a child of 4 or 5 years old, when my father showed me a compass. The fact that this arrow behaved so definitely did not fit in any way with the type of phenomenon that could find a place in my unconscious world (action through touch). I still remember now - or it seems to me that I remember - that this incident made a deep and lasting impression on me. Behind things there must be something else, deeply hidden. A person does not react this way to what he sees from an early age. The falling of bodies, the wind and the rain do not seem surprising to him, he is not surprised at the moon and the fact that it does not fall, he is not surprised at the difference between living and nonliving things.

At the age of 12, I experienced another miracle of a completely different kind: its source was a book on fold-out geometry on a plane, which fell into my hands at the beginning of the school year. There were statements, for example, about the intersection of three altitudes of a triangle at one point, which, although not very visible in themselves, could be proven with an increase, seemingly eliminating all doubts. This clarity and magnification made an indescribable impression on me. It didn't bother me that aximons should be accepted without proof. In general, it was enough for me if I

I remember, for example, that the Pythagorean theorem was shown to me by my uncle even before the bound book on geometry fell into my hands. With great difficulty I managed to “prove” this theorem using similar triangles; at the same time, it seemed to me, however, “obvious” that the ratio of the sides of a right triangle should be completely determined by one of its acute angles. In general, it seemed to me that it was necessary to prove only what was not “obvious” in this sense. And the objects with which geometry deals did not seem to me to be of a different nature than “visible and tangible” objects, that is, objects perceived by the senses. This primitive understanding is based, of course, on the fact that unconsciously taking into account the connection between geometric concepts and observable objects (length - solid rod, etc.) ...

Although it looked as if by pure reflection one could obtain reliable information about the objects observed, such a “miracle” was based on an error. Nevertheless, to those who experience this “miracle” for the first time, the very fact that a person is capable of achieving such a degree of reliability and purity in the abstraction of the mind that the Greeks first showed us in geometry seems surprising...

Between the ages of 12 and 16, I became familiar with the elements of mathematics, including the basics of differential and integral calculus. At the same time, in my experience, I came across books in which not too much attention was paid to logical structure, but the main idea was highlighted well. This whole activity was a fascinating concept; it contained uplifts that were as impressive as the “miracle” of elementary geometry—the basic idea of ​​analytical geometry, infinite series, the concepts of differential and integral. I was also lucky enough to get an idea of ​​the most important results and methods of the natural sciences from a very well-organized publication, in which the presentation is almost entirely limited to the qualitative side of the issue (Bernstein’s natural scientific books for the people - a work in 5-6 volumes); I read these books without taking a breath. By the time I entered the Zurich Polytechnic as a physics and mathematics student at the age of 17, I was already somewhat familiar with theoretical physics.

There I had excellent teachers (for example, Hurwitz, Minkowski), so, strictly speaking, I could receive a solid mathematical education. I do most of the intensive work in the physical laboratory, fascinated by direct contact with experience.
