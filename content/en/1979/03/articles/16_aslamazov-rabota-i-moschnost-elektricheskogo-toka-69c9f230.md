Work and power of electric current

When a charge $q$ moves in an electric field, work $A = q(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2) = qU$ is performed,

where $U = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ is the potential difference between the initial and final positions of the charge. Electric current is the directed movement of charges. During time $t$, a charge $q = It$ passes through the cross-section of the conductor, along which the conductor flows, through which the current $I$ flows. Therefore, the total work that an electric current can do in a conductor is equal to

$$A = qU = UIt.$$

This gives us the well-known formula for the total current power:

$$P = UI.$$

The amount of heat generated by the current in a conductor is determined by the Joule-Lenz law:

$$Q = I^2Rt.$$

Accordingly, the heat loss power is determined by the formula:

$$P_T = I^2R.$$

For a common conductor, for which Ohm’s law $U = IR$ is valid, formulas (1) and (2) are equivalent, and, therefore, all the work of the electric current turns into heat. In this case, for power you can also use the following formula:

$$P = P_T = \frac{U^2}{R}.$$

It is preferable to use any of formulas (1), (2) and (3) determined by the constant specificity of the problem.

Problem 1. Two light bulbs, extended to switch on seven with a voltage of $U = 220$ V, have powers of $P_1 = 25$ W and $P_2 = 100$ W. Which of the lamps will burn brighter if you turn on seven of them, connecting them in series?

Find the brightness ratio of each light bulb for common (single) and sequential switching on.

Consider that the brightness is proportional to the small power released in the light bulb.

When a light bulb is connected in series, the same current flows through them. Therefore, to compare the capacities allocated to them, it is more convenient to use formula (2):

$$\frac{P'_1}{P'_2} = \frac{I^2R_1}{I^2R_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_2}.$$

To find the resistance ratio, we fill in formula (3), since the known rated powers $P_1$ and $P_2$ correspond to the same voltage $U$ on the light bulbs:

$$\frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{U^2/R_1}{U^2/R_2},$$ and $$\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{P_2}{P_1}.$$

Thus we get:

$$\frac{P'_1}{P'_2} = \frac{P_2}{P_1} = 4$$

- a 25 W light bulb, when switched on in series, will burn 4 times brighter.

Let's compare the warm powers released in each light bulb with a common (single) and sequential switching on. In the first case

$$P_1 = \frac{U^2}{R_1}$$ and $$P_2 = \frac{U^2}{R_2}.$$

From here you can find the resistances of the light bulb $R_1$ and $R_2$:

$$R_1 = \frac{U^2}{P_1}$$ and $$R_2 = \frac{U^2}{P_2}.$$

When light bulbs are connected in series, a current $I = \frac{U}{R_1 + R_2}$ flows through them. The power released in light bulbs can be found using formula (2):

⟦folio 46⟧

$$P_1' = \frac{U^2 R_1}{(R_1 + R_2)^2} \text{ и } P_2' = \frac{U^2 R_2}{(R_1 + R_2)^2}.$$

Then for the power ratios we get:

$$\frac{P_1}{P_1'} = \left(1 + \frac{P_1}{P_2}\right)^2 = \frac{25}{16} \text{ и } \frac{P_2}{P_2'} = \left(1 + \frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)^2 = 25$$

- both bulbs, when switched on in series, burn less brightly, but for the second bulb the reduction in power is 16 times greater.

When a section of the circuit has an EMF source $\&$, the expressions for the total power and the power of heat losses (formulas (1) and (2)) become equivalent, and therefore the work of external forces $\& It$ must be taken into account in the energy balance. In this case, the direction $U$ in the section differs from the EMF $\&$ by the magnitude of the direction $IR$:

$$U = \& \pm IR$$

(the “minus” sign refers to the case when the direction of the current in the section coincides with the direction of the current created by the EMF, the “plus” sign refers to the opposite case). Correspondence, total current power $U_1$ is not equal to thermal power $I^2 R$:

$$U_1 = \& I \pm I^2 R.$$

The total power of the electric current in this case consists of mechanical and thermal powers:

$$UI = P_\pi + I^2 R$$

pose

$$P_\pi = UI - I^2 R = 1700 \text{ Вт}$$

$$\eta = \frac{P_\pi}{P} = \frac{UI - I^2 R}{UI} \approx 0,77 = 77\%$$

Problem 6. What useful power is developed by a motor connected to seven with a voltage of $U = 220$ V, with a current consumption of $I_1 = 10$ A? It is known that when the armature is completely frozen through the winding of the high current $I_2 = 40$ A.

When the motor comes to a complete stop, the induced emf in the winding is zero; hence $U = I_2 R$. From here we find the resistance $R$ of the mora winding: $R = U/I_2 = 5,5$ Ohm. The useful (mechanical) power at current $I_1$ is equal to

$$P_M = UI_1 - I_1^2 R = 1650 \text{ Вт.}$$

Now let's discuss the issue of heat losses when transmitting energy through wires over long distances. As is known, in order to minimize heat losses, high voltage is used in transmission lines. The transmitted voltage $U$ should not be confused—the potential difference between the wires of the power line and the voltage drop in line $\Delta U = IR$, which is associated with the current $I$ flowing through the wires and the line resistance $R$. The power transmitted through the wires is $P = UI$, and the heat loss is $P_T = I^2 R = \frac{\Delta U^2}{R}$. However, the same power $P$ can be transmitted with less current if the transmitted voltage $U$ is increased. In this case, the voltage drop in line $\Delta U$ and heat losses $P_T$ are reduced.
