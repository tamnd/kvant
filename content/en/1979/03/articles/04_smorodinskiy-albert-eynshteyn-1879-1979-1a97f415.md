Albert Einstein
(1879—1979)

A hundred years ago, in the German city of Ulm, a child was born who was destined to make one of the most remarkable discoveries in the history of the natural world - he was destined to become the father of the theory of relativity: Albert Einstein was born on March 14, 1879.

There was no other theory in the history of science that withstood so much controversy and dispelled such stubborn mistrust as the theory of relativity. Very few of Einstein's contemporaries were able to immediately accept the new teaching - it was so different from everything that nineteenth-century physicists knew. And a lot of time passed before the theory of relativity became the basis of all natural science.

But Einstein's words are based not only on this one discovery. When he was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1922, the theory of relativity was not yet universally accepted, and its application was crowned with the discovery of the laws of the photoelectric effect. It is now difficult for us to understand the indecisiveness of the Nobel Committee, but the work on the photoelectric effect opened a new era of physics. This work proved the existence of a quantum of light - the photon.

Einstein was always interested in the most general questions of physics, he tried to understand how our world works, and tried to find the global laws that govern it. “What infuriates me are the scientists,” he said, “who take a hole, choose the thinnest place in it, and then continue to make a lot of holes where it’s easy to drill.” Einstein himself spent his energy only where it was difficult to drill.

Let's try to talk about his works and various episodes from the life of the great physicist in a series of short, not very interconnected thirteen essays.

1. First works

Einstein wrote his first work in 1901. He sent copies of it to various laboratories, trying to get a job after graduating from the Zurin Polytechnic. These attempts were unsuccessful. (Many years later, those who did not open up to his request received him with the greatest honors.) Only in June 1902, two years after graduating from the polytechnic, Einstein was able to obtain a position as a technical expert at the Swiss Patent Office.

By the beginning of 1905, Einstein already had six published works. Einstein himself subsequently did not rate his first works highly. His first work was, indeed, a rather elementary study of the forces acting between molecules in capillary phenomena. The next four works were devoted to thermal phenomena. In them, Einstein develops a general theory of these phenomena. These works would have been of exceptional importance if... the general theory of thermal phenomena had not been formulated somewhat earlier by Gibbs. Young Einstein was not familiar with the scientific literature and did not know about the work of the great American.

The sixth work was called “Redefining the size of molecules.” It was sent to the editors of the German physics journal “Annalen der Physik” (“Annals of Physics”), in which Einstein’s previous five articles were published, on April 30, 1905. The work was devoted to diffusion in liquids, and in it, in essence, the first ideas of the theory of Brownian motion were laid.

II. Golden year

1905, the year of the first Russian revolution, turned out to be a golden year in the history of physics. This year, four more works by Einstein appeared one after another in the journal Annalen der Physik. During this period, the theory of the photoeffect was created. The second is the theory of Brownian motion. In the third, the basic formulas of mechanics and electrodynamics in the special theory of relativity were obtained. In the fourth article, one of the most significant formulas of the 20th century was obtained - a formula connecting mass and energy.

Each of these works ensured immortality for their author. And these discoveries were made by a man who worked completely alone, did not meet with physicists, and only told close friends about his ideas.

With these works of Einstein, a new century in physics began.

III. Photo effect

In 1900, Planck hypothesized that atoms emit light in separate portions - quanta, the energy of which is proportional to the frequency of light:

$$E = hv.$$

(1)

This proposal about the nature of the study of atoms allows us to explain some patterns in the study of a heated body.

Einstein went further than Planck. One of the questions that he examined was the question of the nomination of gases of light. It was known that light with a long wavelength does not nominate an atom: there is some critical wavelength $\lambda_0$ such that only light with wavelength $\lambda < \lambda_0$ can nominate an atom (for different atoms $\lambda_0$ is different). The mystery of this phenomenon disappears if we propose that light itself consists of portions - quanta, and the energy of these light quanta is associated with the frequency of light by the same correspondence $E = hv$. If we move from frequency v to wavelength $\lambda = c/v$, then correspondence (1) will take the form $E = hc/\lambda$. So light with a wavelength of $\lambda < \lambda_0$ is quanta whose energy is $E < E_0 = hc/\lambda_0$, where $E_0$ is the energy of induction of an atom.

Based on the assumption of the quantum nature of light, Einstein created a theory of the photoelectric effect that explains its basic laws: the existence for each substance of a minimum frequency of light, below which the photoelectric effect does not occur, and the independence of the kinetic energy of electrons emitted under the influence of light from the intensity of the light.

If an electron is located close to the surface of a body and in order to escape beyond its boundaries it needs to do work $P$ (against the forces of attraction to positive charges), then under the influence of light with frequency v (and, accordingly, with the energy of quanta hv) it will fly out with kinetic energy

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = hv - P.$$

If the energy of light quanta is $E = hv \leq P$, then $v = 0$—the photoelectric effect does not occur. The maximum energy of electrons emitted from a body under the influence of light does not depend on the intensity of the light - it is determined only by the frequency of the incident light.

Einstein came to the Fotoeffect formula while reflecting on the difference between the description of particles and light: “... it may turn out that the theory of light, operating with continuous spatial functions, will lead to a contradiction with experience when it is applied to the phenomena of the emergence and transformation of light,” he wrote at the beginning of the work under the title “On a heuristic point of view concerning the emergence and transformation of light” *), received by the editors of the mentioned journal on March 18 1905

For many years Einstein continued to discuss questions about whether

Eischstein's point of view turned out to be correct. In his work on the photographic effect, the concept of an elementary particle - a quantum of light - was born. The first elementary part was the electron, the second was the quantum of light - photon. Since then, the list of elementary particles has been continuously updated.

IV. Brownian motion

“On the movement of suspended particles, requiring a molecular-kinetic theory of heat” was the title of the second work of the “golden year” (it was received by the editor on May 11).

Eischstein compares the movement of a molecule in a liquid and the movement of a small floating ball inside this liquid (as they say, “suspended”). Both the molecule and the microsopper ball, subject to impacts from other molecules, move in the otic fluid. Considering this similarity in more detail, Eischstein, based on the most general laws of kinetic theory, showed that the average kinetic energies of a moving molecule and a ball should be the same.

“...according to the molecular theory of heat, bodies with dimensions of the order of 1/1000 mm, suspended in a liquid, undergo visible random motion, which is obligatory for the thermal motion of molecules. Such a movement is actually observed by biologists, who call it Brownian movement” (from a letter from Eischstein to his friend Habicht in the spring of 1905).

A ball in a liquid, moving randomly, will still slowly mix in the liquid, “diffuse”

Einstein describes a detailed theory of Brownian motion a little later (in 1906) in another article. In it, he reports that Brownian motion, which is observed by other physicists, is actually well described by his formulas. This work by Einstein put an end to many years of debate about the reality of the existence of molecules.

V. Birth of the theory of relativity

Many prominent physicists and mathematicians (especially Lorentz and Poincaré) thought about how to describe optical phenomena in a moving coordinate system. Einstein outlined his views on this issue in the article “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies.” It was received by the editor on July 30, 1905.

In mechanics, all inertial systems are equally oriented (Halley's principle). Einstein begins with a broader hypothesis: “... for all coordinate systems for which the mechanics equation is valid, the same electrodynamic and optical laws are valid...”. And further he describes the theory as follows: “We intend to turn this assumption (the content of which will be further called the “relative principle”) into a prerequisite and, in addition, make an additional assumption, which is with the first only in every contradiction, namely, that light in emptiness always propagates with a certain speed $v$, does not depend on the state of motion of the best body...”.

One of the main conclusions of the theory, which the following did not want to accept its critics, was that the duration of any physical process and the interval between two of its own turn out to be different from the point of view of reporting systems that move at different speeds. In short, in different frames of reference, moving in a different relative manner, time is marked differently. Now this is a firmly established fact. Mesons, “born” in the upper layers of the atmosphere at an altitude of, say, 10 km, reach the Earth’s surface. Moving at a speed close to the speed of light, mezons travel this distance in approximately $10^{-4}$ s, although at rest a mezon decays in $2 \cdot 10^{-8}$ s. From the point of view of the theory of relativity, everything is clear: in the system where mez ends, it has not yet had time to pass $2 \cdot 10^{-8}$ s. Having remained in the device on Earth, mezon decays (this is exactly how it was discovered). Many such examples can now be given. Einstein's discovery has firmly entered the foundation of modern physics.

Three portraits were brought into Einstein's office: Newton, Faraday and Maxwell. In his theory of relativity, he combined mechanics, created by Newton, and the theories of electricity and magnetism, created by Faraday and Maxwell, into a single science.

VII. Mass and energy

In the work we just talked about, Einstein derived an expression for the mass of the moving electron - formulas that determine the relationship between the mass of the electron and its speed.

If the force is perpendicular to the speed of the electron (the electron moves in a circle), then it behaves as if its mass ("transverse mass") is equal to $\frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$, where $m_0$ is the rest mass of the electron (nuto-

The quantity $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$ is now called the Lorentz factor.
If the force is directed along the velocity of the electron (the electron moves in a straight line), then it behaves as if its mass ("longitudinal mass") was $m_0\gamma^3/2$.

If there is arbitrary work done on an electron (for example, if it moves in an electric field), then its energy returns. Einstein showed that the kinetic energy $K$ of an electron is related to its speed ratio

$$K = m_0(\gamma - 1)c^2.$$

The quantity $m_0c^2$ is called rest energy, the quantity

$$W = m_0\gamma c^2 = \frac{m_0c^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$$

— the total energy of an electron. So the kinetic energy of an electron is equal to the difference between the total energy and the rest energy.

$$\Delta W = f\Delta x = ma\Delta x,$$

$$\Delta W = m\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\Delta x = m\Delta v \cdot v,$$

or, replacing the increment with differential ones,

$$dW/dv = mv.$$

On September 27, 1905, the editor of the same magazine received a very small note from Einstein, “Does the inertia of a body depend on the energy it contains?” If earlier it was said about the change in the mass of a body with increasing speed, then in this note it was proven that any change in the energy of a body changes its mass: “The mass of a body is a measure of the energy contained in it; if the energy changes by values ​​$L$, then the mass changes, accordingly, by values ​​$L/(9 \cdot 10^{16})$, and here the energy changes in joules, and the mass in kilograms.”

This establishes the formula $E = mc^2$, as it is written now (dimension $c$ - m/s).

The new formula was very difficult to test at the time. In his next work, Einstein, proposing a way to change the ratio of longitudinal and transverse masses, noted: “The author is not able to carry out such an experiment at first and will be glad if one of the physicists devotes resources to the proposed method.” Now the formulas of the theory of relative surfaces have been tested at accelerators, in many devices, but at the beginning of the century it was necessary to understand the full depth of the theory in order to believe in them.

VIII. Bose's work

In 1900, Planck announced the patterns of distribution of light intensity in the spectrum of a heated body. To do this, he had to make the assumption that light is emitted and absorbed by quanta. As we have already said, five years later Einstein proved that quanta exist in a free form and their behavior is very similar to the behavior of particles. From this point of view, the formula proposed by Planck can be remembered as a formula that determines how many quanta with a certain energy value are emitted from a heated body. Light from this point of view can be considered as a phoron gas, and the distribution of energy between individual photons of this gas (“how many phorons have a given energy?”) is described by Planck’s formula. But no one, not even Einstein himself, took advantage of this idea at that time.

In 1924, Einstein received a letter from a physicist in Dhaka (now the capital of the People's Republic of Bangladesh). Attached to the letter was an article in which Einstein's idea of ​​quanta was used in a clever, decisive way. Didn’t go into discussing subtle issues, the author

At the same time, Bose believed (although he did not emphasize this in his work) that quanta are absolutely identical if they have the same energies. Planck's formula was obtained absolutely strictly. This means that Einstein’s hypothesis about the reality of quantum is actually proven by Planck’s formula, but no one was able to notice this. Einstein was delighted that quanta could be treated like atoms. He translated Bose's article into German and sent it himself to the magazine, where it was published. In a note, Einstein wrote: “Bose’s derivation of Planck’s formula is a great achievement. The method he used also gives the quantum theory of an ideal gas, which I will expound elsewhere."
He saw something completely new in Bose's formula, which led him to a new discovery.

IX. Bose-Einstein statistics

The idea that Einstein had when he read Bose's paper was that if, using the hypothesis of the identity of photons with the same energies, it was possible to derive Planck's formula, then one could try to apply the same method to an ideal quantum monatomic gas, the atoms of which have a non-zero mass.

The result was great. Srouz immediately came up with formulas that describe all the thermal properties of such a gas, and it was possible, continuing the analogy with photons, to think about the unexpected wave properties of particles, which Louis de Broglie wrote about in his dissertation in 1924.

In 1924, at a physics conference in the Austrian city of Isbruck, Einstein drew attention to the fact that de Broglie's theory implies the existence of diffraction and interference phenomena in molecular beams.

In the theory of monatomic gas, he effectively used the idea of ​​quanta to derive the equation of state of a quantum ideal gas and study its other properties.

The new theory was called Bose-Einstein statistics. It was created a year before complete quantum mechanics appeared. Further development of the theory led to the creation of the theory of superfluidity and many other amazing phenomena in the quantum world. But Einstein himself was not satisfied. He continued to believe that Bose's method "...is by no means indisputable, but seems justified only due to its success in the case of radiation...". Although Einstein was the first to introduce the quantum hypothesis in the theory of light, until the end of his days he could not apply the dual nature of particles and radiation, trying to find a different description of reality. Thus, until the end of his days, the founder of quantum theory was unable to apply it to modern quantum physics.

X. Stimulated and spontaneous emission

In 1916, Einstein's thinking once again led him to remarkable results.

The question that interested Einstein was essentially simple. It was necessary to understand how the quantum hypothesis must represent the laws of emission and absorption of light.

If light consists of quanta - photons, then it is almost certain that the probability of absorbing light of a given particle should be greater, the more quanta of this particle the light emission of the given particle changes significantly?

Einstein's answer to this question becomes clearer if we consider the following example from the field of mechanical vibrations. How will an oscillating string behave if it is not continuously near

does another string vibrate at the same frequency? If the vibrations of the scab are in phase, the range of vibrations of the first string will increase; if the vibrations are in antithesis, the vibrations of the first string will die out. Both processes will be more intense the more amplitude the “donor” string vibrates, the oscillations of which are supported by some source of energy. (If there is no donor string, then the vibrations of the first scab will die out, even if there is no friction, and all its energy will be spent on creating sound in the air.)

An analysis of a similar situation for light and the atom led Einstein to the conclusion that if the probability of absorbing a quantum is proportional to the number of quanta present in a beam of light, then the probability of emitting a quantum consists of two terms. However, one of them describes induced radiation - radiation that is caused by the impact of light on the atom (which “rocks” the atom). This part is proportional to the number of quanta present in the light beam. The second term does not depend on the number of quanta and therefore does not vanish for an atom in vacuum. This term describes the so-called spontaneous, that is, spontaneous, radiation.

If a powerful light flux enters the medium, that is, a large number of quanta, the induced radiation transforms and becomes very intense.

The operating principle of a laser is related to stimulated emission. The effect predicted by Einstein in 1916 has now become the basis for a huge number of devices and installations.

X1. General relative theory and deflection of light in the field of the Sun

It is difficult to talk about the general relative theory, or the theory of gravity. Very few have understood its fundamental significance. Academician Landdu liked to say that although he himself deeply understood Einstein's general theory of relativity, he could not imagine how it was possible to agree upon it.

But one of the questions Einstein began with is quite simple: how does a gravitational field, for example, the field of the Sun, affect the propagation of light?

As someone remembered much later, people were interested in this question back in the 18th century, and then the first answer was received. The answer was also simple. If we agree with Newton that

light is a stream of corpuscles, then the corpuscles must fly near the Sun like comets. The trajectory of a comet that passes near the Sun is not difficult to calculate. The calculation shows that it will deviate from the straight line by 0.85." This small deviation was calculated back in 1801 by the German astronomer Zolder.

In 1915, Einstein obtained a different result: according to the theory of gravity he created, the deviation in the field of the Sun should be 1.7." The "doubling" was effective due to the fact that the laws of Euclid's geometry ceased to apply near the Sun and, as a result, Newton's law of universal gravitation itself changes. Bodies near the Sun are closer to it more strongly than follows from the law of universal gravitation.

In 1915, Einstein was no longer an unknown expert. The whole world listened to his words. Even as war raged in Europe, astronomers began discussing how to observe the predicted deviation. After all, in 1919 there should be a solar eclipse, during which it would be possible to check whether the effect predicted by Einstein exists. The eclipse was expected on May 29, when rays of light from a multitude of bright stars passed near the Sun. In April, two expeditions left England. One headed to Brazil, the second to the island of Prisilia off the western coast of Africa. On May 29, it rained all morning on the island, but just before the eclipse it needed to clear up, and the expedition decided to take photographs. Still, the weather did its job: the clouds obscured the stars, and when the images were processed days later, only one image gave good confirmation of Einstein’s effectiveness. Pictures from Brazil were transferred to England. Only four months later, on September 22 (regular postal communications were disrupted by the war!), a telephone message arrived from Lorenz from Holland: “Eddington discovered a mixture of stars at the edge of the Sun...”. Of course, no observation can prove a theory conclusively, but since then few have doubted Einstein's theory. Only a small group of old physicists tried (and with great persistence) to deny her conclusions.

But the time of classical physics has passed. Now there are many facts confirming the general theory of relativity. The most important thing for physicists of our time is that without this theory it is impossible to understand how the Universe develops.

XII. Evolution of the Universe

Perhaps the most impressive thing about the building built by Einstein is his cosmological work. In 1917, a work entitled “The Question of Cosmology and the General Theory of Relativity” was published.

This work stands out for its boldness. Like Newton, who declared that the law of the fall of a body on Earth is the same as the laws governing the motion of celestial bodies, Einstein declared that the laws of general relativity, tested within the solar system, are valid for the entire Universe. He wrote and solved the equation of the Universe. The solution that Einstein received was surprising: the Universe turned out to be closed...

However, when analyzing these equations, Einstein did not take into account all possible solutions. Five years later, Soviet physicist A. Friedman showed that Einstein's equations have other solutions, but these solutions are not statistical - the Universe is expanding. Einstein did not believe Friedman at first and declared that he had made a mistake. At that time, the Soviet physicist Yu. Krutkov was in Berlin, who convinced Einstein that he was wrong, and on May 31, 1923, Einstein published a small note: “... I believe,” he wrote, “Friedman’s results are correct and shedding new light. It turns out that the field equations also allow dynamic (i.e., variable with respect to time) centrally symmetric solutions for the structure of space to violate statistical ones.”
Friedman's solution did not require that the Universe be necessarily closed. Modern data rather indicate that the Universe is expanding infinitely, and the rate of its expansion increases with distance approximately according to the law

$$v = \frac{R}{T},$$

where $T$ is a constant equal to $2 \cdot 10^{10}$ years. This law is named after the astronomer Hubble who discovered it, and the constant $H = 1/T$ is called the Hubble constant. The expansion of the Universe has been confirmed by many observations, and Hubble's law has been tested to the distance that light travels in 10-12 billion years.

So Einstein assured the “conquest” of the laws of the Universe, begun by ATON. Man on Earth has understood the laws that govern the cosmos. “The most incomprehensible thing about nature is that it can be understood,” said Einstein.

XIII. Gravitational waves

Let's talk only very briefly about gravitational waves.

The first person to understand that gravitational interaction travels at the speed of light was Poincaré. The existence of a gravitational wave was a simple consequence of the equation of the theory of relative energy.

The theory did not reject the fact that no signal could be transmitted faster than the speed of light. In 1937 (together with Rosen) Einstein theoretically studied gravitational waves - his work is continued by many theorists today. Gravitational waves have not yet been discovered, but the day is not very far away when a gravitational signal from some great cosmic catastrophe - the birth of a supernova or something else of this kind - will be registered on Earth.

Epilogue

We end with 1937. Einstein did not stop working; Until his death on April 18, 1955, he became more and more immersed in new theories that are impossible to talk about. He wanted to find even more general laws than the laws of gravity - he worked on what he himself called a unified field theory. The last words in his last scientific article were: “... now nothing knows how to find a basis for such a theory.”

Einstein's work in recent years was treated differently. Many believed that success had betrayed him, and everything he did during these years was wrong. Many even believed that the task itself was inaction. Is this true? Can we say that now this significance no longer seems indisputable. The words “unified field theory” appear more and more often on the pages of the journal. Of course, time flows, and the new path is not at all the one that Einstein followed.

But Einstein's deep ideas do not disappear.

“Everything that came from his supremely Great intellect was only clear and red, like well-made art” - these words, written by Einstein in memory of Lorentz, fully apply to himself.
