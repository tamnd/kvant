The statement of the lemma follows from the fact that none of the above three first-graders can be a right vertun (Fig. 7).

So, after the disappearance of the island on the right, the length of the big one will move to the left with a speed of at least 3. Obviously, its left border cannot move to the left with a speed greater than 1. It follows that the length of the big one will decrease (at a speed of at least 2) and some time after it disappears. All first-class students will become right.

Solution problem. With a little more subtle reasoning, it can be shown that the movement can last no more than 2l + 2 seconds, where l is the maximum distance between the two left first-graders after the first turn (on the command left-VO).

Problem 1. Let n be the number of left first-graders after the first turn. Specify a function f(n) such that the time t of movement in a row cannot be greater than f(n), but t = f(n) is possible.

So, we have found a state of the string A₁ (all first-graders look to the right), any finite change of which (i.e., a 180° rotation of a finite number of first-graders) will disappear in a finite time (during evolution according to our rules).

It is clear that the state of the lines A₂ “everyone is looking to the right” also has this property: just as the “island” disappears in the “ocean,” the “lake” on the “mainland” will disappear.

These two states of the string have the property of being conservative: they do not change over time, and no matter what final change we subject them to, the string will return to its original state.

Problem 2. Prove that other conservative states do not exist.

Let us now consider a more general problem. Let A be some state of our system (i.e. some integer of symbols ⊥ and ⊥ infinite in both directions). Let us denote by A(t) the state in which the system (during evolution according to our rules) will transition in t seconds. We will call states A and B equivalent if over time they transform into the same state, that is, if there are t₁ and t₂ such that A(t₁) = B(t₂). We will say that state A is immunological (so they say, undeniably) if it is equivalent to any state A', which differs from A by the states of only a finite number of first-graders.

It is easy to see that all states equivalent to conservative ones (including conservative ones) are immune.
