Why is physics necessary for an engineer?

Starting with four lectures on physics, I would like, before moving on to a systematic presentation of the subject, to dwell today on one general question and share with you some messages about the position that physics occupies and, in my opinion, should occupy in a number of sciences, the study of which you are going to spend the next few years.

I would like to give you material on the basis of which you could see for yourself that an engineer always needs physics, throughout his activity, and that it cannot be looked at as a subject that is necessary - and is it even necessary? - pass it once, and then you can forget it, since anyway, what an engineer needs to know from physics is repeated once again when passing special subjects.

When I say that an engineer needs physics, I mean not only that he must be familiar with those individual phenomena and laws that he directly encounters in his practical activities. Such a statement would be self-evident. That a civil engineer, when calculating the strength of a structure, must be familiar with the basic laws of control, that an electrical engineer in designing, say, a lighting network must know Ohm's law relating current strength, resistance and electromotive force of a battery, etc. - this, of course, does not need proof. No, when I say that an engineer needs physics, I mean that he needs a broad knowledge of this subject in its entirety; I argue that he needs knowledge of physics in itself as a whole discipline with its specific methodology, and not just depending on current applications. I affirm, finally, that for this it is not enough for an engineer to know only the experimental part of it, but that he must also be thoroughly familiar with the theory.

Not in order to show that this is really so, we must first of all try to figure out for ourselves the structure of physics as a science and see in what correspondence the theory is with the experimental material that physics operates on.

Of course, we cannot resolve here the question of the existence of physical science in its entirety. This question, related to the theory of knowledge and of interest to both the physicist and the philosopher, is much more complex than it might seem at first glance. He always occupies a place in the history of physical thought. But for our purpose we don’t need to go too deep into it. It will be enough for us to know only one side of it, although, it is true, here too we will have to learn somewhat from afar.

There is no doubt that the only means by which we obtain our information about the world around us is our senses. But single sensory perceptions are too minimal and unstable to serve as material for further processing. And so a person identifies and records in memory those common features of individual perceptions that are repeated and which are practically important for him. This process, which is completely involuntary, leads to the formation of what in logic are called concepts.

In the formation of concepts, the first step along the path of knowledge of nature. They are the basis on which the future is built. But the initial concepts formed in this way have, it seems to me, the following property. They defy strict definition. We all know the concept of “light”. But we cannot explain in words, define one another being of this concept. To learn it, you need to have eyes, you need to see how everything around us is illuminated when the sun rises and how it plunges back into darkness when it sets. If you say it, you can learn the same thing by turning a light bulb on and off. But one thing is certain: words and definitions are powerless here. Try to explain to someone who has been blind since birth what light is.

So, initial physical concepts cannot be taught with words alone. This is why, let me emphasize this here, neither the textbook nor the teacher is sufficient to teach physics. The student must be willing to do a little experimental work himself. He must want to superficially, but to see for himself, to hear for himself, to realize for himself the phenomena that he is being told about.

We got a little sidetracked. Let's return to the first steps along the path of understanding nature, to the concepts directly imposed on us by nature. As humanity has developed its stock of concepts called experience (experiential knowledge), there is still a present need for their systematization, without which there is no way to understand the endless abundance of phenomena surrounding us. In this systemization, our ability to form another kind of concepts, concepts more definite than those discussed above, and less dependent on our feelings, is of great service. First of all, this includes the concept of number and those concepts that mathematics operates on. In the area of ​​these concepts, in other words, in the area of ​​mathematical thinking, we feel obviously more confident than when operating with material directly supplied to us by our senses. With the help of mathematical concepts, one can definitely form premises and also definitions and easily draw conclusions and conclusions from them. And so, knowing this power behind him, a person tries - first instinctively, and then with the development of science and consciousness - to adapt mathematical concepts and especially the concept of number to raw experimental material, to physical concepts.

This process of transition from qualitative to quantitative relationships will constitute the most important stage of scientific thought. Both the concept of measurement and the process of measuring physical quantities are based on it. We can safely say that any area of ​​physical phenomena generally becomes a science only from the moment we learn to see dimensions in it. So, for example, until there was a precise concept of temperature and they did not know how to measure it, the science of heat almost or even did not exist at all.

Using mathematics in the sense described, we are now trying to systemize the phenomena around us and make it easier for ourselves to understand them by looking for such mathematical formulas in the general sense of the word, not necessarily narrowly algebraic, that cover the entire possible number of units of facts or the general aspect of various phenomena. If such a formula is found, then we say that we have found a physical law. Let's take an example. The law of refraction of light when passing from one transparent medium to another states, as is well known, this: the incident and refracted rays lie in the same plane, and the ratio between the angle of incidence and the angle of refraction is constant. For example, for water and air this ratio is 1.33. What's before

what is this law? This is a formula covering a free set of single cases of refraction. It saves us from the need to make experiments in each individual case; it makes it unnecessary to fill out or fill in tables for each individual case with the angle of incidence and the corresponding angle of refraction of the ray. Knowing the law of refraction, you are sure that at any moment when you need it, with the help of simple calculations you will be able to solve any question that arises in this direction.

As physical knowledge has grown, as the number of laws found has increased, it has become more and more difficult to make sense of their varied abundance. Driven again by the need for better orientation in this enormous material, people tried to find such pictures, such points of view that would allow them to combine individual laws into one whole. This is how a physical theory, or rather a theory, is created.

Theory, therefore, stands in the same relation to individual laws as laws stand to individual phenomena. The systematizing role of theory, of course, does not exhaust everything, but still, as you can see, it is of enormous importance in the systematization of our knowledge. And here mathematics is a huge help. The correspondences just discussed between the various aspects of physics and their gradual development can be traced to any physical theory. It is very easy to do this, for example, on optics.

Optics, by the way, is one of the most ancient scientifically developed branches of physics. The study of optical phenomena already in ancient times led to the establishment of certain laws. The law of rectilinear propagation of light and the law of reflection from mirrors have been known for a long time. Later, in the 17th century, the law of refraction was discovered. Depending on which group of phenomena was supported by the study and from what points of view they were approached, various abstract laws were established. The phenomena of light diffraction, interference, etc. were discovered. But until there were promises, a unifying point of view, it was extremely difficult to understand the entire set of optical phenomena. Moreover, individual laws seemed to be in conflict with each other. The bending of light did not correspond, for example, with the rectilinear propagation of the beam.

This was the case until the efforts of a number of brilliant physicists, of whom Huygens and Fresnel should be named first of all, succeeded in finding the picture that we now call the wave theory of light and which allows us to quickly form one harmonious whole. And everything that seemed complex and contradictory became simple and clear.

And take Newton's theory of universal gravitation. Uniting with genius courage is a table of heterogeneous phenomena in the eyes of our senses, like the fall of a stone and the movement of heavenly bodies, it is gradual precisely because of its simplicity. In one simple formula it contains all the dynamics of the entire world of microdania.

I will limit myself to these examples. maybe there are some things in them that are unclear to you. It's nothing. An understanding of the factual side will come as you study physics, but I think that for you, at least in general terms, the correspondence between its experimental and theoretical sides has now been clarified.

These two sides, as you have seen, are interconnected; together they represent one whole. In achieving our ultimate goal - knowledge of nature - theory can help systematize our experience and give us the opportunity to use the material. The theory, and therefore the tool, which, as we have seen, she uses -

The presented view of the systematizing role of theory is very well illustrated by one beautiful comparison made by Poincaré.

Poincaré compares all of physics to a huge library. Individual experimental data, individual phenomena—these are the volumes that make up the library. Theory is the catalog of our library. Just as without a catalogue, a library, especially a large one, is only a collection of books, very valuable books that cannot actually be used productively, in the same way, physics without theory is not a science, but only a rather small conglomerate of individual facts, which there is no way to understand.

And now it will not be difficult for us to answer the question that we asked ourselves at the beginning. Does an engineer need physics for her purpose? Isn't it enough for him to know the individual facts that are necessary for his practical work? The answer, it seems to me, is clear.

To work productively - Poincaré allows me to speak in the language of comparison - it is not enough for an engineer to read and know several books from a huge library of knowledge. He should be familiar with, or at least be able to understand, the library catalogue.

History technically knows many examples of mysterious failures, repeated failures and sometimes having very unpleasant consequences. And very often it turns out that it is mysteriously determined not by the presence of the action of new, hitherto generally unknown factors, but by the lack of a broad physical horizon among those who deal with these issues. And when people with truly broad physical knowledge took on the solution, not only was the riddle clarified and a way was found to prevent failure, but often new paths were opened for further progress.

Let me conclude with one example that seems to me to illustrate the importance of a broad physical horizon in resolving technical questions.

I am referring to the question of optical instruments and, in particular, the question of microseals. You know that microsopop plays an extremely important role in many characteristics of applied knowledge. I will not remind you of its significance in medicine, hygiene, and sanitation. I can only say that in the study of metals microsopop is currently not replaceable. Microsopop became especially interested in the middle of the century, after its use in biology opened up completely new paths in the study of life phenomena. But after the first successful ones, it turned out that at that time the microorganisms were not very good and were not strong. The researchers clearly felt that if it were possible to construct microscopic structures with greater magnification, then at the same time it would be possible to penetrate even further into the essence of life. And prospects have always attracted people with special force. And imagination did not remain in the face of the construction of microsops, increasing by tens, hundreds of thousands and millions of times, they expected miracles from their use. Researchers expected that with their help they would penetrate into the most intimate details of the structure of living matter.

It is clear that with such a conjuncture, specialized designers of optical devices began to improve the microsop with their best efforts. And they considered it in principle possible to achieve

The fact is that at that time all calculations concerning optical instruments were carried out exclusively with the help of so-called geometric optics. The calculations were based on the microcoppa theory, which operates with light rays as straight lines (and which, however, is only taught in high school until late today).
And from the point of view of geometric optics, there really is no fundamental limit for the possible increase in microcoppa.

However, it soon became clear that the work aimed at establishing the microcoppa condition was far from yielding the results that seemed to be expected. There was a contradiction between what seemed accessible and what was achieved that was not obligatory.

This was the situation when the Jena mechanic Zeiss, who had a small mechanical and optical workshop and made microcoppas that were not bad at that time, invited the then young physicist Abbe as a consultant. Abbe had good theoretical training and was fluent in theoretical optics. He knew that geometric optics is only a convenient scheme for processing the classical phenomenon of refraction. He knew its value, because he himself had contributed a lot to this area. But he also knew that from the point of view of the wave theory of light, which serves as the basis for geometric optics, the latter is nothing more than an approximation. And he immediately approached the question of microdigging with a broad view, not bound by the narrow framework of geometric theory.

The results of this approach to business were not long in coming. One of the main results that Abbe came to was the following. He showed that the wave-like nature of light places a fundamental limit on the useful magnification that can be achieved with a microcoppa or, indeed, with any optical instrument. If the finer details of an object define values ​​or norms, then these details cannot be visible; no microcopy can reveal them. All references to an increase of 100,000 times or more and all hopes associated with them should be fundamentally abandoned, and the work of those who wanted to build such microcoppas is completely pointless. With brilliant experiments, Abbe confirmed the correctness of his theoretical conclusions.

If what I have said can help to strengthen this view in you, then my purpose has been achieved.
