This year marks the 100th anniversary of the birth of Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam (1879-1944), an outstanding Soviet physicist, academician, dedicated teacher, creator of one of the largest Soviet physical scientific schools.
In this issue we publish an article by one of Mandelstam’s students, Academician of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR V. A. Fabrikan, in which he shares his knowledge about L. I. Mandelstam and talks about one of the main discoveries of this visionary scientist. We also publish, with slight abbreviations, the introductory lecture given by L. I. Mandelstam in 1918 to students of the Odessa Polytechnic Institute. This lecture was devoted to a question that has not lost its activity today: why should a future engineer study physics?

V. Fabrikant

Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam

Mandelstam - teacher
I, a first-year student, first heard about Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam in 1925 at a meeting of the subject commission on physics at Moscow State University.
This commission, along with teachers, also included student representatives. The chairman reported that he had been able to invite a very prominent scientist, a famous physicist - L. I. Mandelstam, to work at Moscow State University. We students were naturally intrigued.

For some reason, someone who looked after me advised me to go to these seminars. He said: “At first you will understand little, but you will feel it as two real sciences.” Both parts of his prediction came true.

At L.I.’s seminars everything was unusual for us. First of all, the composition of the seminar. It was attended not only by undergraduate and graduate students, but also by many of our teachers and people who were completely strangers to us students. In this regard, the seminars were held in the largest auditorium. The “adults” occupied the first seats in the audience; the students sat somewhat further away.

At the seminar there was an atmosphere of friendly attention to the statements of any participant, regardless of his scientific “rank”. Niko was not afraid to ask “stupid” questions, many of which, after a follow-up, turned out to be far from being as “stupid” as it seemed at first. At the same time, any physical error was clarified and criticized in a clear, but not offensive, manner. The decisive role in creating such an atmosphere of goodwill and correctness was played by the personal qualities of L.I. It is difficult to convey his charm in words. Every meeting with him was a joy for us. When for some reason L.I. was absent from the seminar, the students said “tea without sugar.”

Usually L.I. prefaced the report of one of the participants in the seminar with several brief remarks, and after the report he summed up the results. I was amazed by his ability to present the most complex issues and, on the other hand, his ability to detect complexity in seemingly simple questions. It is not for nothing that in those years physicists coined the term “Mandelshtam’s clarity.”

When in my fourth year it was my turn to speak as a speaker at the seminar, I was personally convinced of the great work that L.I. and his staff did in preparing us for the presentation. L.I. himself was a lecturer and speaker. His student S. M. Rytov writes in his memoirs:

“I thought a lot about what the “secret” was of the very special impact of L.I.’s speeches, the emotional impact that they always evoked. His lectures and reports captivated the audience, made them forget about everything in the world and experience the conditions. This is what happens with the best works of theater or cinema, or art in general. But... L.I.’s “weapon” was not red-baitism or “theatricalism,” but a remarkable ability to amaze the listener, touch their heartstrings, arouse interest and force them to see questions where, it would seem, everything is already clear due to what was said earlier.”

I involuntarily, as a former student of L.I., began with the characteristics of Mandelstam the teacher, but this does not mean that teaching was the main thing for him. It would be more correct to say that L.I. had difficulty establishing a clear boundary between activity research and teaching.

Mandelstam - scientists

In 1929, L. I. Mandelstam was elected academician. An outstanding scientist, he created one of the largest Soviet scientific schools in the field of physics. His students are academicians A. A. Andronov and M. A. Leontovich, corresponding member S. M. Rytov, professors G. S. Gorelik, A. A. Witt, S. E. Khaikin, S. P. Strelkov. His close collaborators are academicians G. S. Landsberg and I. E. Tamp. It is impossible not to mention L.I.’s close friend, academician Nikolai Dmitrievchie Papaleksi, with whom he worked together for 45 years. All of these are big names in science. Now the scientific “grandchildren” and “great-grandchildren” of L.I. have successfully worked.

In order to understand how L.I.’s circle of scientific interests developed, it is necessary to provide some brief biographical information. Having graduated from hypnasium in 1897 with a gold medal, L.I. entered the mathematics department of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Novorossian University. The Department of Physics at this university was occupied by the outstanding scientist F. Brown, who received the Novel Prize in 1909 for his work on radiotelegraphy. He was also the director of education at the Physics Institute in Strasbourg at that time.

Brown very soon appreciated the exceptional abilities of L.I. and attracted him to scientific work in the field of physics of electromagnetic oscillations. L.I.'s first scientific article was devoted to determining the decay time of oscillations in a circuit.

After graduating from the university, L.I., as an assistant to F. Brown, continued active research in gravel work, combining it with part in factory testing of new radio equipment. During this period, L.I. made his first (out of 60!) invention, which allowed the creature to increase the sensitivity of receiving radio equipment. It is characteristic that this invention, which has great practical value, was made on the basis of the theoretical analysis carried out by L.I.

In his subsequent life, L.I. was mainly engaged in the physical and technical oscillations and waves of a very different nature, amazingly combining creative activities in a column of disparate areas, such as problems of theoretical physics, the subtleties of physical experiment and the solution of specific engineering problems. A. A. Andronov, in a report dedicated to the first anniversary of L. I.’s death, very correctly said: “... in our time of sharp division of physicists into theoretical and experimentalists, into “pure” physicists and “technical” physicists, L. I. is simultaneously a theoretical and experimentalist, a “pure” physicist and a “technical” physicist.” To these words he wants to add that L.I. knew how to appreciate creativity in any field, even very far from science and art. I remember with what admiration he spoke about the invention of the zipper, which was a novelty at that time, and wondered who made this ingenious invention.

The Strasbourg period also dates back to L.I.’s polemics with the already significant physicist at that time, the founder of quantum theory, Max Planck. L.I., despite his innate modesty and gentleness of character, firmly stood on the fact that Planck had a fundamental mistake in his work on the wave theory of the propagation of light in transparent media. After a long debate, Planck admitted that Mandelstam was right. This episode is also interesting because it demonstrated L.I.’s ability to find analogies between phenomena in areas of physics that are far from each other. In his dispute with Planck, apparently, L.I. used an analogy between neighboring atoms of a medium with electrons oscillating in them and closely located operating radio antennas.

In those same years, L.I. began a series of works on molecular light scattering.

It is known that the discovery of Raman scattering was made by Landsberg and Mandelstam in crystals and by Raman in liquids, independently of each other and almost simultaneously. However, in 1930, the Nobel Prize for this discovery was awarded to Raman alone, which was clearly unfair.

L.I., based on classical considerations, pointed out that Raman scattering in crystals, as well as the Mandelstam-Brillouin effect, arises due to the modulation of scattered light by the flasks of the crystal lattice. However, in this case, the role of modulation oscillations is played not by active, but by so-called optical oscillation solutions. According to the honest theory of modulation during Raman scattering, several symmetrically located lines—satellites—should appear in the spectrum of scattered light near each spectral line of the incident light. The satellite frequencies are obtained by combining the frequency of the incident light with the frequencies of intramolecular vibrations (hence the name of the phenomenon) and satisfy the simple relationship:

$$v_k = v_0 - v_M,$$
$$v_\phi = v_0 + v_M,$$

(1)

where $v_k$ is the frequency of the “red” satellite, that is, the linney, shifted relative to the main one (from the spectrum of the incident light) towards longer wavelengths, $v_\phi$ is the frequency of the “philot” satellite, that is, the linney, shifted towards shorter wavelengths, $v_M$ is the frequency of intramolecular vibrations (modulation frequency).

Correspondences (1) are in excellent agreement with experience. However, according to classical theory, the intensities of symmetrically located satellites should be the same. This conclusion is found as a result of the quadratic “red” satellite, which is many times greater than the intensity of the adjacent “philot” satellite (see Fig. 2, b). This intensity difference cannot be excluded from the classical modulation theory. It is necessary to take into account the quantum properties of light and matter.

According to classical concepts, two symmetrically located satellites arise when light is scattered by the same molecule. According to quantum concepts, a “red” satellite arises when light—photons—are scattered on molecules with a smaller internal energy reserve, and a “phylot” satellite arises when light is scattered on an excited ULE molecule with an increased energy reserve (in classical language, these molecules differ in the amplitude of intramolecular vibrations).
