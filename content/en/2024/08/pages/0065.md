The number of children is 们 1, 2, ..., 2$n$ in descending order of height. Then, if we arrange the children in the order $n+1$, $n+2$, ..., $2n$, $1$, $2$, ..., $n$, then all pairs $(i,j)$, where $i \leq n < j$, turn out to be good; There are only $n^2$ such pairs. Moreover, all pairs of the form $(i,i+1)$ also turn out to be good; There are only $2n-1$ such pairs. In this case, the pair $(n,n+1)$ is taught twice, so the total number of pairs is $n^2 + (2n-1)-1 = (n+1)^2 - 3$.

It remains to prove that there cannot be more than $(n+1)^2 - 3$ good pairs. We follow this by induction on $n$. For $n=1$ the statement is trivial, or there is only one pair of children.

Let now $n > 1$. Let us consider an arbitrary sheregi and choose a good pair $(a,b)$ in it, in which $|a-b|$ is the most; Let, for definiteness, $a < b$ and the child $a$ stand further to the left than $b$. Let's call a child $c$ beautiful if he forms good pairs with both $c$ and $c$.

Lemma. There are no more than two beautiful children.

Proof. If $c$ is beautiful, then by the choice of the pair $(a,b)$ we have $c-a \leq b-a$ and $b-c \leq b-a$, hence $a < c < b$. Such a child $c$ cannot stand between $a$ and $b$, otherwise the pair $(a,b)$ would not be good; this means that any beautiful child stands either to the left of $a$ or to the right of $b$.
