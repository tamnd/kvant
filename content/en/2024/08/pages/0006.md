From a formulaic point of view, each vertex $\varphi$ of the graph FS($X, Y$), i.e. the arrangement of people on the graph $X$ is a one-to-one mapping $\varphi : V(X) \rightarrow V(Y)$, which for each $i$ specifies the number $\varphi(i)$ – the number of the person standing at the $i$ vertex.

“Is such a cumbersome format really useful,” the reader will doubt. Yes! Sometimes useful! Here's a respectful example—and an amusing piece of duality.

Lemma 1. The graphs FS($X, Y$) and FS($Y, X$) are isomorphic.

Proof. We need to present an isomorphism of graphs, i.e. assign to the vertices of the graph FS($X, Y$) the vertices of the graph FS($Y, X$) so that two vertices from the graph FS($X, Y$) are connected by an edge if and only if the corresponding vertices of the graph FS($Y, X$) are connected by an edge. As already said, each vertex $\varphi$ of the graph FS($X, Y$) is a one-to-one mapping $\varphi : V(X) \rightarrow V(Y)$ (Fig.3,a). Let us match it with the figurative mapping $\varphi^{-1} : V(Y) \rightarrow V(X)$, in a natural way

Now let's look at the vertex $\psi$ (Fig.3,b), adjacent to $\varphi$ in the graph FS($X, Y$). It differs from $\varphi$ in one other exchange, i.e. in the graph $X$ there was an edge, say, between the $k$th and $l$th vertices, and the friends $A = \varphi(k)$ and $B = \varphi(l)$ standing at these vertices swapped places. But then in the graph FS($Y, X$) the vertices $\varphi^{-1}$ and $\psi^{-1}$ are also connected by an edge, which corresponds to the exchange of friends $k$, $l$ from the graph $X$, standing in the neighboring vertices $A$ and $B$ of the graph $Y$.

The above reasoning also works in the opposite direction: if the vertices $\varphi^{-1}$ and $\psi^{-1}$ are connected by an edge in the graph FS($Y, X$), then $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are connected by an edge in FS($X, Y$).

The lemma is proven.

Homework. There are $3n$ people standing in a circle - this is $n$ “mother-father-child” families. Any two people standing next to each other can switch places, except when a child switches places with one of their parents (this is not allowed). At what $n$ with the help of such exchanges can people be placed in a circle in any order? (Places along the circle are propoverized; permutations distinguished by a cyclic shift are considered different.)
