**Corollary** If the graph $\bar{Y}$ is connected, then fs($C_n, Y$) is divisible by $n$.

The “smallest” connected graphs on $n$ vertices are trees. For them, we can clarify the result of Lemma 2. We call the arrangement of people on the cycle $C_n$ “standard” if, starting from the first person, all the people are in a row in ascending numbers in a clockwise direction. We do not track which vertex of the cycle $C_n$ the first person is at, so there are $n$ different standard arrangements.

**Lemma 3. Let the graph $\bar{Y}$ be a tree. Then fs($C_n, Y$) = $n$ and from each arrangement of lods on the graph $C_n$ we can obtain a standard arrangement using other exchanges.

**Proof.** By induction on $n$ we prove that an arbitrary arrangement of boats on $C_n$ can be reduced to a standard one. The base for $n = 3$ is very given. To move from $n-1$ to $n$, consider the highest graph $\bar{Y}$, i.e. such a person $K$ who does not make friends with just one person from the company $Y$. “Pretending that $K$ has disappeared,” rearrange the remaining $n-1$ people in ascending order. This is done using a chain of exchanges, which is essential to the induction assumption. If the neighbors $K$, standing on opposite sides of $K$, need to change places, then in order to give those who are changing the opportunity to become side by side, we will exchange $K$ with the neighbor with whom he is friends. At the end of the exchanges, when all but $K$ are arranged in ascending order, we will bring $K$ into its place, moving it either clockwise or counterclockwise. Thus, any arrangement can be brought to standard by exchanges.
