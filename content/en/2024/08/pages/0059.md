Let us prove that $\alpha \in [1;2)$ is not suitable.
The receivers of the form $\left[2 - \frac{1}{n-1}; 2 - \frac{1}{n}\right]$ for $n = 2, 3, \ldots$ cover the receiver [1;2), so there is a natural $n \geq 2$ such that $2 - \frac{1}{n-1} \leq \alpha < 2 - \frac{1}{n}$. Then at $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n-1$ we have $2k-1 \leq 2k-\frac{k}{n-1} \leq k\alpha < 2k$, so $\lfloor k\alpha \rfloor = 2k-1$. Also, $2n-2 \leq 2n-\frac{n}{n-1} \leq n\alpha < 2n-\frac{n}{n}=2n-1$, therefore $\lfloor n\alpha \rfloor = 2n-2$. Thus, for the data $\alpha$ and $n$, the sum from the conditions of the problem will be equal to $1+3+5+\ldots+(2n-3)+(2n-2)=n^2-1$, which is not divisible by $n$.
This completes the decision.

3 (by decision of I. Chasovskikh). We will assume that initially the terms $a_1, \ldots, a_N$ are written on the board, and then we add terms $a_{N+1}, a_{N+2}, \ldots$ one at a time. We will depict the current situation by displacing some cells of a checkered square (i.e. a quarter of the plane, where each cell has natural coordinates $(x;y)$). Namely, for each $i$, if the number $i$ currently occurs $c_i$ times, then in the $i$th column $c_i$ lower cells will be marked (thus, the total number of marked cells is equal to the number of written numbers). Then at the next step, when the number $a_{n+1}$ is added, the following happens: if in the column with the number $a_n$ $k$ cells are marked, then at the new step the next cell is marked in the column $k$.
For the natural $t$ we denote by $S_t$ the set of cells in which at least one coordinate does not exceed $t$; then $S_t$ consists of a nodal square $t\times t$, a horizontal strip $\mathcal{H}_t$, in which only the ordinate does not exceed $t$, and a vertical strip $\mathcal{V}_t$, in which only the abscissa does not exceed $t$. Let us choose the number $M$ so that initially (at $n=N$) all marked cells lie in the corner square $M\times M$ (and therefore in the strip $S_M$).
Lemma 1. At any moment of time, all marked cells will be in $S_M$.
Proof. At $y>M$, each cell in column $y$ appears immediately after the moment when there are exactly $y$ cells in some column. If at some moment all marked cells lie in $S_M$, then all columns in which at least $y$ cells have abscissas do not exceed $M$, which means there are no more than $M$. Consequently, at the next step there cannot be more than $M$ cells in the $y$th column, i.e. the new marked cell cannot go beyond $S_M$.
Lemma 2. Starting from a certain moment, new cells will appear alternately in $\mathcal{H}_M$, $\mathcal{V}_M$, $\mathcal{H}_M$, $\mathcal{V}_M$,...
Proof. There is a finite number of cells in the square $M\times M$, so, starting from a certain moment, new cells will stop appearing in it, which means they will appear only in $\mathcal{H}_M$ and $\mathcal{V}_M$. Let's take our process from this point forward.
If at the next step cell $(x;y)$ appears in $\mathcal{V}_M$, then $y>M$, and the next cell appears in column $y$, i.e. it will lie at $\mathcal{H}_M$. Likewise, if the next cell $(x;y)$ appears in $\mathcal{H}_M$, then $y \leq M$ and the next cell appears in $\mathcal{V}_M$. From these two lemmas it follows that an infinite number of cells will appear in $\mathcal{V}_M$, i.e. some numbers will appear in the sequence an infinite number of times.
