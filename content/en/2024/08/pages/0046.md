wave zones are associated with corresponding observational astronomy: infrared, ultraviolet, x-ray and gamma. Using infrared radiation, surface temperatures of planets and exoplanets are measured, the composition of the atmospheres of planets and exoplanets, gas and dust comets and nebulae is determined, extraterrestrial life is searched for on other planetary systems, and objects of the early Universe are studied. Ultraviolet radiation is used to study the Sun, stars and interstellar gas. Gamma radiation is used to study supernovae, neutron stars, active galactic nuclei, and quasars. The Earth's atmosphere greatly complicates observations of celestial objects due to the scattering and absorption of radiation, and for X-ray and gamma radiation it becomes completely opaque. For this reason, it is necessary to use space telescopes or entire observatories, for example, in X-ray radiation Chandra, XMM-Newton, and in gamma radiation Fermi, placed outside the atmosphere. Currently, two unique space telescopes are operating simultaneously in space: Hubble (HST) and Webb (JWST). The first of them works in the near ultraviolet, visible and near infrared spectrum. The second is in the lower frequency range, from long-wave visible light (red) to mid-infrared (0.6–28.3 µm). Thanks to the use of these space telescopes and spectrographs, extremely important scientific results have been obtained about the structure and physics of the most distant and unusual astronomical objects located on the outskirts of our Universe in space and time, right up to the “dark ages” of the evolution of the Universe. The most remarkable thing is that all of the above-mentioned space telescopes, when analyzing the emission spectra from point-like (individual star) to extended (galaxy, nebula) astrophysical objects, use the same approach to determining the fine structure of the radiation being studied.
