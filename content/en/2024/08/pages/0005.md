The connected component of a graph is a vertex and all the vertices that can be reached from it by moving along the edges. A graph consisting of one connected component is called connected, and the remaining graphs are called not connected. A coincidence point in a graph is a vertex, which, when removed (along with the edges that go out from it), results in an incoherent graph. A vertex from which exactly one edge emerges is called hanging. It is customary to denote a cycle on $n$ vertices ($n \geq 3$) by $C_n$, and a path on $n$ vertices by $P_n$. A tree is a connected graph that has no cycles. A forest is a graph in which all connected components are trees.

The above formulation of the question “removed” from us an important object that describes the possibility of making other exchanges. Let's present it. Note that there are $n!$ ways to arrange our company in the vertices of the graph $X$. Let's consider a new huge graph, it will be denoted FS($X,Y$). Each way of arranging people in the graph $X$ will be considered a vertex of the graph FS($X,Y$) - thus, there are $n!$ vertices in the graph FS($X,Y$). The edges in this graph connect those (and only those) arrangements that are obtained from each other by one operation of another exchange. The constructed graph FS($X,Y$) will be called the graph of friends and strangers, and the number of connection components of this graph will be denoted by fs($X,Y$).

Now we can reformulate the main problem as follows: for given graphs $X$ and $Y$, find out whether it is true that the graph FS($X,Y$) consists of one connected component. And the perfectionist in each of us will add: if the graph is incoherent, find out how many connected components it has.

Example 1. Let $X = \textcircled{8}$, $Y = \textcircled{1}$$\textcircled{2}$$\textcircled{3}$. Then the graph FS($X,Y$) is depicted on figure 1. Here, six large circles are the vertices of the graph FS($X,Y$), indicating the possibility of placing three people at the vertices of the triangle.

Fig. 1. Graph FS($X,Y$), where $X = C_3$, $Y = P_3$

Example 2. The general problem of playing 15 in the language of others and strangers is formulated as follows. Let $X$ be a graph in the vertices of which we place people, and graph $Y$, which describes the friendship of these people, is a graph in which the $n$th vertex is connected to the rest and there are no other edges, such a graph will be denoted $K_{1,n-1}$. We need to find out for which graphs $X$ the graph of friends and strangers FS($X,K_{1,n-1}$) consists of exactly one connected component. (In this case we say that the Game of 15 globebreaker is being assembled.)

The answer to this question is given by Wilson’s theorem [3] (for a popular exposition, see [2]).

Theorema Wilson. Let the graph $G$ satisfy the following conditions:
1) is not dicotyledonous
2) is not a cycle $C_n$, $n \geq 4$,
3) does not contain matching washers,
4) does not coincide with the graph $\Theta$ (Fig. 2).
Then on graph $G$ the globular “Game of 15” coincides.

Fig. 2. Count $\Theta$

In fact, Wilson proved even more: that fs($\Theta,K_{1,n-1}$) = 6 and that fs($G,K_{1,n-1}$) = 2 if $G \neq C_n$ and $G \neq \Theta$ are a bipartite graph without a coincidence point.

This article will answer the same question: for which graphs $X$ is the graph of friends and strangers FS($X,C_n$) connected ($n \geq 3$)?
