The physical tradition is such that either a graph of the dependence of one observed parameter on one variable is depicted, or two or more graphs are combined on one figure - the dependence of several observed parameters on one variable parameter, or a “volumetric” figure - dependence on two parameters, it is also called the “response surface”. In all cases, the axes contain parameter values, but the values ​​do not always start from zero and move evenly along the axis. In this case, all sorts of features can be found on both the abscissa and ordinate axis. Now let's look at a few specific examples. The pictures are taken from various articles in the Journal of Technical Physics (No. 1, 2023). One could take any other physics journal, then the examples would be different, but the essence of the matter would not change. We do not consider the physical meaning of the results depicted; this is impossible in most cases at the school course level. We are now only interested in how the “picture” is constructed - the image of what happened on the graph. By the way, the ability to quickly “grab” a physical image, a picture with graphs, is also useful for serious work (which includes, among other things, reading physics magazines). figure 1 presents the simplest case - the surface roughness along a certain line is shown. This is the surface - don't get scratched! The abscissa axis shows the displacement along the surface (in micrometers), and the ordinate axis shows the surface height (micrometers). The scales are uniform, but the scale is different, so it’s not all that scary. Where on the axes is zero is not important in this case; the ruler in space can be positioned however you like. True, geographers count heights “from sea level,” and builders “from ground level,” i.e. they have, let's say, a natural zero. When depicting a surface profile, a “middle line” is often used and the height of the peaks and the depth of the pits are measured from it, but in this case the middle line was not drawn. By the way, if you were asked, how would you do it? At figure 2, the experimental points clearly lie on a straight line, four dependences of the capacitance in picofarads on the voltage in volts are shown, and there are two parameters for the dependences themselves. One parameter is shown in numbers, this is the temperature in Kelvin (420 K for the top dependence, 220 K for the other three). Another parameter is shown by the color and shape of the icon, in this case these are the frequencies at which the measurements were made, but this is not indicated on the figure itself. On the abscissa axis, zero is on the right, on the ordinate axis, zero is at the bottom, the scales are uniform.
