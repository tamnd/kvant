Kirchhoff Festival

200 years ago, on March 12, 1824, the great physicist, a native of Königsberg (now Kaliningrad), a graduate of the University of Königsber and a member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences Gustav $Кирхгофф^{1}$, was born. Schoolchildren and students know him as the author of the famous Kirchhoff laws for electrical circuits. But his more significant contribution to world science is research on thermal radiation, spectral analysis, electromagnetism, diffraction and mechanics of elastic media. The laws of thermal radiation discovered by Kirchhoff became the foundation on which modern ideas about the quantum nature of radiation were formed. His pioneering studies of emission and absorption spectra formed the basis of spectral analysis, which is important for its applications in physics, chemistry and astronomy. All the great discoveries of astronomy and cosmology of the 20th century are in one way or another connected with the use of spectral analysis to determine the composition of stars, interstellar matter, atmosphere

The significant date associated with the birth of Gustav Kirchhoff served as the basis for holding the “Kirchhoff Festival” in Kaliningrad. The main objective of the festival was to popularize the scientific discoveries of the great physicist and their role in the subsequent development of science. Using infrared radiation, surface temperatures of planets and exoplanets are measured, the composition of the atmospheres of planets and exoplanets, gas and dust comets and nebulae is determined, extraterrestrial life is searched for on other planetary systems, and objects of the early Universe are studied. Ultraviolet radiation is used to study the Sun, stars and interstellar gas. Gamma radiation is used to study supernovae, neutron stars, active galactic nuclei, and quasars. The Earth's atmosphere greatly complicates observations of celestial objects due to the scattering and absorption of radiation, and for X-ray and gamma radiation it becomes completely opaque. For this reason, it is necessary to use space telescopes or entire observatories, for example, in X-ray radiation Chandra, XMM-Newton, and in gamma radiation Fermi, placed outside the atmosphere. The most remarkable thing is that all of the above-mentioned space telescopes, when analyzing the emission spectra from point-like (individual star) to extended (galaxy, nebula) astrophysical objects, use the same approach to determining the fine structure of the radiation being studied.

And only Gustav Kirchhoff deserves the credit for revealing the subtle and deep meaning hidden behind the original ideas. His seminal work "On the relation between the emissive and absorptive powers of bodies for heat and light" was published in 1860. The basis for this article was a discovery made several months earlier by Gustav Kirchhoff himself, together with Robert Bunsen. They discovered that the Fraunhofer absorption lines in the solar spectrum, first noticed back in 1802–1814 by William Wollaston and Joseph Fraunhofer, coincide with the emission lines of known vapors and gases. This is how spectral analysis was born. Much more important for the development of astrophysics was the discovery by Kirchhoff and Bunsen of emission and absorption lines in the spectra of gaseous bodies. They themselves have already identified some strong lines in the spectrum of the Sun: mainly the lines of calcium and metals. In 1860, Gustav Kirchhoff received the first hint of the true nature of stars when he realized that dark lines in the spectrum of sunlight were caused by various elements absorbing specific wavelengths. Astronomers analyzed similar characteristics in the light of other bright stars and found that they were composed of the same substances found on Earth, and not some mysterious quintessence - a fifth essence, as the ancients believed. By deciphering the optical spectra of stars, we find out the temperature of their surface and determine the luminosity class, which allows us to determine the evolutionary status of the star using the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (“temperature – luminosity”). Systematic measurement of the spectrum makes it possible, based on the Doppler effect, to determine the nature of the spatial motion of the star and its orbital velocity in binary systems. And this is the only way to measure the mass of a star and identify its invisible satellites - exoplanets, white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes. It is impossible to list all the areas of modern astrophysics and the discoveries made in these areas, where spectral studies play a major role. It is a pity that Gustav Kirchhoff and his colleagues did not know what incredible opportunities science gained thanks to their discoveries.
