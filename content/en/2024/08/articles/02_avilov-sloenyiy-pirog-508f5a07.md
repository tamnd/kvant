The connected component of a graph is a vertex and all the vertices that can be reached from it by moving along the edges. A graph consisting of one connected component is called connected, and the remaining graphs are called not connected. A coincidence point in a graph is a vertex, which, when removed (along with the edges that go out from it), results in an incoherent graph. A vertex from which exactly one edge emerges is called hanging. It is customary to denote a cycle on $n$ vertices ($n \geq 3$) by $C_n$, and a path on $n$ vertices by $P_n$. A tree is a connected graph that has no cycles. A forest is a graph in which all connected components are trees.

The above formulation of the question “removed” from us an important object that describes the possibility of making other exchanges. Let's present it. Note that there are $n!$ ways to arrange our company in the vertices of the graph $X$. Let's consider a new huge graph, it will be denoted FS($X,Y$). Each way of arranging people in the graph $X$ will be considered a vertex of the graph FS($X,Y$) - thus, there are $n!$ vertices in the graph FS($X,Y$). The edges in this graph connect those (and only those) arrangements that are obtained from each other by one operation of another exchange. The constructed graph FS($X,Y$) will be called the graph of friends and strangers, and the number of connection components of this graph will be denoted by fs($X,Y$).

Now we can reformulate the main problem as follows: for given graphs $X$ and $Y$, find out whether it is true that the graph FS($X,Y$) consists of one connected component. And the perfectionist in each of us will add: if the graph is incoherent, find out how many connected components it has.

Note that if two people $A$ and $B$ are strangers, then at no point can they make a friendly exchange. Let's connect these two with a rubber thread running around the circumference in an arc from $A$ to $B$ clockwise. When the boats move, this thread will skim or stretch, but at any given time its length is strictly less than one turn. This means that at the end of the exchanges $A$ and $B$ have the same value. And since the graph $\bar{Y}$ is connected, any two people have the same place. But this is impossible: since the value of each person is not equal to 0, and the total value of all people is equal to 0, some people must have a positive sense, and the rest - a negative one. Contradiction.

**Corollary** If the graph $\bar{Y}$ is connected, then fs($C_n, Y$) is divisible by $n$.

The “smallest” connected graphs on $n$ vertices are trees. For them, we can clarify the result of Lemma 2. We call the arrangement of people on the cycle $C_n$ “standard” if, starting from the first person, all the people are in a row in ascending numbers in a clockwise direction. We do not track which vertex of the cycle $C_n$ the first person is at, so there are $n$ different standard arrangements.

**Lemma 3. Let the graph $\bar{Y}$ be a tree. Then fs($C_n, Y$) = $n$ and from each arrangement of lods on the graph $C_n$ we can obtain a standard arrangement using other exchanges.

**Proof.** By induction on $n$ we prove that an arbitrary arrangement of boats on $C_n$ can be reduced to a standard one. The base for $n = 3$ is very given. To move from $n-1$ to $n$, consider the highest graph $\bar{Y}$, i.e. such a person $K$ who does not make friends with just one person from the company $Y$. “Pretending that $K$ has disappeared,” rearrange the remaining $n-1$ people in ascending order. This is done using a chain of exchanges, which is essential to the induction assumption. If the neighbors $K$, standing on opposite sides of $K$, need to change places, then in order to give those who are changing the opportunity to become side by side, we will exchange $K$ with the neighbor with whom he is friends. At the end of the exchanges, when all but $K$ are arranged in ascending order, we will bring $K$ into its place, moving it either clockwise or counterclockwise. Thus, any arrangement can be brought to standard by exchanges.
