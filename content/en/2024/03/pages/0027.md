more “resonant” discoveries in biology of the 20th century - a discovery that truly exploded the scientific world and had, perhaps, the greatest influence on the development of biology in the second half of the 20th century. We are talking about the discovery of the spatial structure of the DNA molecule (Fig. 1), made by Francis Crick and James Watson a little more than 70 years ago (1953). Probably, almost everyone knows the phrase “double helix” - thanks to Watson’s book with that title. However, this was not at all the discovery of the “helical” structure of DNA that was the main thing in the discovery of Crick and Watson. The key here was the assumption that nitrogenous bases - the “building blocks” of which the DNA molecule is composed - are complementary in one chain, i.e. complementary to the bases opposite them in the other chain. Such complementarity, in fact, strict correspondence, meant that one strand of this “double helix” already contained all the necessary information about its structure. The second thread can be completed on the first in the same way as a cast iron casting is “completed” in a casting mold. Ideas about such a mechanism for copying hereditary information (recreating a new polymer molecule based on an existing one) were first expressed long before Watson and Crick (by the Russian biologist N.K. However, they found their visible embodiment and ceased to be just a speculative hypothesis only as a result of the discovery of the structure of DNA. This structure in Russian received a very unfortunate, albeit catchy name - “double helix”. Of course, DNA strands do not fold into a flat curve - a spiral, but into a helical line (Fig. 2 and 3). This unfortunate translation of the English word helix, which can sometimes mean a spiral, unfortunately, has become entrenched in the Russian language. It is curious that in one of his first articles, Francis Crick directly said what kind of curve should be understood by this term: “A helix is ​​often loosely called a spiral; the difference is that the helix winds not around a cone, but around a cylinder, as a spiral staircase usually does” (1954). Of course, the author took some liberties here; a spiral is not the curve itself wound around a cone, but its projection onto a plane perpendicular to its axis. But the article was published in the popular science magazine Scientific American, and such freedom was permissible.
