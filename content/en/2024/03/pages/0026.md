Helical lines, spirals and physical methods

V. PTUSHENKO

The rapid development of physics in the twentieth century gave it the opportunity to contribute to the development of other sciences, primarily through the emergence of many new research methods. One of them was the method of X-ray diffraction analysis, based on the diffraction of X-ray radiation on the atoms of the crystal lattice. Let us recall that the diffraction of a wave by a periodic obstacle leads to differences in the intensity of the wave scattered in different directions. One of the clear examples is the diffraction of light on the surface of a DVD disc, which in reflected light shimmers with all the colors of the rainbow. Any crystal is also a periodic structure and, therefore, can serve as a diffraction grating. However, the distance between the “lines” of this grating is too small for a visible diffraction pattern to arise when reflected from such a periodic structure of visible light. But X-ray radiation, the wavelength of which is close to interatomic distances, gives an excellent diffraction pattern. This phenomenon was discovered back in 1912 by Max Laue and father and son William and Lawrence Bragg, for which they were awarded Nobel Prizes (1914 and 1915). Relatively soon, X-ray diffraction analysis became (and still remains) one of the main methods for studying the structure of molecules, including biologically important macromolecules.
