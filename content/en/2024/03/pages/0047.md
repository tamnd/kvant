Try solving this problem yourself before reading further. We will present the solution to this problem here, because we will need this fact. The main thing to remember is that distance (displacement), just like speed, is a vector.

Let's choose a test star $C$. Let $\vec{v}_{AB}$ and $\vec{v}_{AC}$ be the velocities of stars $B$ and $C$, respectively, $\vec{v}_{AB} = H \cdot \overrightarrow{AB}, \vec{v}_{AC} = H \cdot \overrightarrow{AC}$, where $H$ is the proportionality coefficient, the Hubble constant, traditionally denoted by this letter in honor of the substitute American astronomer Edwin Hubble.2 Then

$$\vec{v}_{BC} = \vec{v}_{AC} - \vec{v}_{AB} = H \cdot \overrightarrow{AC} - H \cdot \overrightarrow{AB} = H \cdot (\overrightarrow{AC} - \overrightarrow{AB}) = H \cdot \overrightarrow{BC}.$$

Consequently, having recognized the planet of the star $B$, the astronaut will see that all the stars are moving away from the star $B$ with speeds proportional to their distances. And the proportionality coefficient is the same – $H$.

But what if, in the Newotian model of stationarity, we assume that at each moment of time galaxies are moving away from each other at speeds proportional to the distance between them? This is equivalent to the relative distances remaining the same.
