6) Given a regular tetrahedron. It is allowed to reflect it symmetrically relative to any of its faces. Is it possible to ensure that one of its vertices comes as angularly close to the origin of coordinates?
14. a) Prove that sin $n^2$ does not tend to 0 as $n \rightarrow \infty$.
6) Prove that the number $2^n$ can begin with any combination of digits.
15. Arrange on a straight system segments of unit length that do not have common points (including common ends), so that any infinite arithmetic progression has a common point with some segment of the system.
16. a) A circle of radius $1$ rolls along a circle of radius $\sqrt{2}$. At the initial time point, sticky red paint was applied at the point of contact. During the rolling process, the paint moves from one circle to another and back. How many dots will be colored after the small circle rotates 1000 times?
6) Three wheels of radii 1, $\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}$ touch at one point and rotate without slipping. At the initial moment of time, sticky red paint was placed at this point, which transfers from wheel to wheel during the rotation process. How many dots will be colored after the smallest wheel spins 1000 times?
17. a) Prove that, having written out the first digits of a billion consecutive powers of two, we will encounter the following section: 1, 2, 4, 8, 1, 3, 6, 1, 2, 5, 1.
6) Prove that this sequence is not rearranged.
c) A sequence is called uniform recurrent if it is almost periodic: for any $k$ one can find $n(k)$ such that any of its length $k$ can be found in any of its length $n(k)$. Prove that the sequence of the first digits of the fractional parts of the numbers $an$ is as follows.
d) If the sequence $W$ of letters is not periodic and uniformly recurrent, $U$ and $V$ are its different subwords, then he will find $S$ and $T$ such that $SUT$ is a subword, and $SVT$ is not a subword of $W$.

One of the authors of this article, Mark Valentinovich Sapir (02/12/1957 – 10/08/2022) was a remarkable mathematician. He graduated from Sverdlovsk (now Ural) University in 1978, defended his PhD thesis under the guidance of L.N. Shevrin. He worked at the Ural University, at the University of Nebraska at Lincoln, from 1997 until the end of his life he worked at Vanderbilt University, and was the editor-in-chief of the International Journal of Algebra and Computation. Had he remained editor longer, there is no doubt that this magazine would have been taken to new heights. In 2006, Mark Valentinovich was a guest speaker at the International Mathematical Competition in Madrid.

I promise in many ways with Mark. Thanks to him, I became interested in a lot, learned and understood a lot. For example, he interested me in the plot of “Robots in Mazes”, helped me understand the classic proof of Van der Waerden’s theorem, the operation of the Minsky machine; from him I learned a lot about the combinatorics of words and about Burside type problems. I am also grateful to him for many inspiring conversations related to discrete mathematics.

A. Belov
