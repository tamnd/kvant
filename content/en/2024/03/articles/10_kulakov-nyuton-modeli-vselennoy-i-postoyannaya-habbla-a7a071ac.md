Preface

After Newton's Principia and direct
derivation of elliptically planetary orbits
from Newton's laws and the universal law
Gravity by one of the representatives of the Bernoulli dynasty, physicists began to consider models of the Universe “as a whole.”

During Newton's time and until the twentieth century, the Universe seemed homogeneous, isotropic and Stationary. Homogeneity means that the same laws apply at all points of the Universe and that there is no single point in the Universe that can be embraced by the origin of coordinates - all points are equal. Isotropy means that all directions are equal - there is no single direction. Stationarity was understood as the immutability of the Universe as a whole, and from this it followed that each point on average should remain in its place or, what is the same, the distances between all objects in the Universe should remain unchanged. It was immediately discovered that only an empty Universe can have such properties, i.e. A universe in which there is no matter, or, what is the same, in which the density of matter is zero.

Indeed, let us assume that the density of matter in the Universe is ρ > 0. Then from homogeneity it follows that matter in the Universe is distributed uniformly with this density. Let us now consider some point A. Let us assume that this is the point of the Universe in which we live, planet Earth or the Solar system, and we will try to determine with what force the rest of the matter of the Universe acts on the material point A with mass m. We will come across two facts that were known to Newton.

1. A body located inside a sphere, over the surface of which the substance is distributed evenly, is not affected by any force boundary (all forces are balanced, complexed).

2. A sphere, the matter of which is distributed uniformly over the surface, acts on a body of mass m outside the sphere as if the mass were concentrated at the center of the sphere. As a consequence, the same is true for a ball, the density of the substance of which depends only on the radius (spherical symmetric distribution).

Task 1. Prove these facts.

Let us now choose point B, located at a distance R from point A. From homogeneity it follows that we can choose any point. Divide the Universe into spheres with a center at point A. According to the first fact, the force with which matter acts outside a sphere of radius R is equal to zero, and matter concentrated in a ball of radius R with a center at A will act on B with a force

$$F = -G \frac{mM_{AB}}{R^2}.$$

And this means that point $B$ in relation to point $A$ should experience rooting, i.e. move, which contradicts the stationarity and, as it seemed until the twentieth century, the single-circuit nature of the Universe.

The twentieth century made its own corrections to this contradiction: Einstein and Poincaré built the General Relative Theory (GTR), and Alexander Friedman took from GTR what is now called the “Standard Model of Modern Cosmology” - a model of the Universe as a whole.1 It turned out that this model contains three fundamentally different models and finding out which of the worlds we live in comes back to us. Moreover, all three models are nonstationary at the density of matter in the Universe $\rho > 0$.

What about homogeneity? In all three models, the galaxy recession velocities visible from the observed point $A$ turned out to be proportional to distances to $A$. Does this contradict homogeneity?

proportional to their distances. What picture will he observe if he flies to the planet of another star $B$ of this cluster?

Answer: absolutely the same!

Try solving this problem yourself before reading further. We will present the solution to this problem here, because we will need this fact. The main thing to remember is that distance (displacement), just like speed, is a vector.

Let's choose a test star $C$. Let $\vec{v}_{AB}$ and $\vec{v}_{AC}$ be the velocities of stars $B$ and $C$, respectively, $\vec{v}_{AB} = H \cdot \overrightarrow{AB}, \vec{v}_{AC} = H \cdot \overrightarrow{AC}$, where $H$ is the proportionality coefficient, the Hubble constant, traditionally denoted by this letter in honor of the substitute American astronomer Edwin Hubble.2 Then

$$\vec{v}_{BC} = \vec{v}_{AC} - \vec{v}_{AB} = H \cdot \overrightarrow{AC} - H \cdot \overrightarrow{AB} = H \cdot (\overrightarrow{AC} - \overrightarrow{AB}) = H \cdot \overrightarrow{BC}.$$

Consequently, having recognized the planet of the star $B$, the astronaut will see that all the stars are moving away from the star $B$ with speeds proportional to their distances. And the proportionality coefficient is the same – $H$.

But what if, in the Newotian model of stationarity, we assume that at each moment of time galaxies are moving away from each other at speeds proportional to the distance between them? This is equivalent to the relative distances remaining the same.

So, the first equation that the objects of our Universe must satisfy is

$$R' = H \cdot R,$$

Let the location at point $A$ be observed and the movement of point $B$ with mass $m_B$ located at a distance $R = R(t)$ from it be considered. Let us denote by $M_0$ the mass of the ball with center at point $A$ and radius $R$. The fact that it is necessary to consider just such a ball follows from facts 1 and 2, known to Newton. The radius $R = R(t)$ changes, but we believe that the mass $M_0$ of the substance inside this ball does not change, i.e. that matter neither appears nor disappears. The force acting on $B$ from a ball of radius $R$ centered at point $A$ is equal to

$$F = -G \frac{m_B M_0}{R^2}, \quad F = m_B R''.$$

So we need to solve the differential equation

$$R'' = -\frac{GM_0}{R^2}.$$

How would Newton solve this equation? The first thing he would do is rewrite it in differentials:

$$\frac{dR'}{dt} = -\frac{GM_0}{R^2}, \quad \text{или} \quad dR' = -\frac{GM_0 dt}{R^2}.$$

Then I would multiply both sides by $R'$:

$$R' dR' = -GM_0 \frac{R' dt}{R^2},$$

drove $R'$ under the differential on the right and left:

$$d \frac{(R')^2}{2} = -GM_0 \frac{dR}{R^2},$$

got an equation that can be integrated, and integrated it:

$$(R')^2 = 2GM_0 \frac{1}{R} + c.$$

Hence,

$$R' = \pm \sqrt{\frac{2GM_0}{R}} + c.$$

This equation is the law of conservation of energy divided by $m_B$. Note that $c$ is the square of the speed at infinity, i.e. at the moment when $R = \infty$, and that this constant of integration, generally speaking, can be different for each object $B$. In addition, $c$ can also be negative. Physically, this means that the object $B$ cannot reach infinity and at a finite distance $R_k$, defined by the equality $0 = \frac{2GM_0}{R_k} + c$, where $c < 0$, turns back and begins to approach. And although $R_k$ may differ for different objects, the moment in time at which this happens will be the same for everyone - this is the moment $H$ turns to zero.

In the last equation, the choice of the sign of the root is related to the choice of the sign of the time parameter $t$, so we will consider only the sign of the plane and introduce the constant $c_1 = 2GM_0$. Now let's separate the variables and integrate:

$$\frac{dR}{dt} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_0}{R}} + c,$$

$$dt = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{c_1}{R}} + c} dR,$$

$$t + t_0 = \int \sqrt{\frac{R}{cR + c_1}} dR.$$

The constant $t_0$ is associated with the choice of the absence of time and can be considered equal to zero.

Part 2

Part 3

Note that we have never used any connection between different points of our universe. All the uncertainty lies in the fact that we cannot determine the relationship between the constants $c$ for different initial points. And even if we consider the point $A$ at different times, we cannot say whether the constant $c$ will be the same for it or not. But we have another assumption about our universe, which in this case can be called an assumption about the state of the universe. This is the assumption that the density of the universe is the same at the initial point in time.

Problem 4. Prove that the change in density is given by the equation
$$\rho' = -3\rho \frac{R'}{R} = -3\rho H.$$

From problem 4 it follows that if at some moment in time the density $\rho$ and CONSTANTA $H$ are the same at all points, then at the next moment the density will be the same.

Let us now return to the obtained equal equations $R' = HR$, $R'' = -\frac{GM_0}{R^2}$. Differentiating the first equation with respect to time, we find
$$R' = HR$$
$$R'' = H'R + HR'$$
$$R'' = H'R + H^2R.$$

Substituting the expression below into the last equation
$$R'' = -\frac{GM_0}{R^2},$$
we get
$$-\frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho R = H'R + H^2R,$$
or $H' + H^2 + \frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho = 0,$
where
$$\rho = \frac{H' + H^2}{-\frac{4}{3}\pi G}.$$

From the last expression it follows that if at some point in time the density $\rho$ and CONSTANTA $H$ are the same at all points, then at the next moment CONSTANTA $H$ will be the same everywhere.

Finally, we differentiate the equation $H' + H^2 + \frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho = 0$ once again:
$$H'' + 2HH' + \frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho' = 0.$$
Substituting $\rho' = -3\rho H$ and $\rho = \frac{H' + H^2}{-\frac{4}{3}\pi G}$ here, we obtain a differential equation (not linear) with constant coefficients that satisfies $H$:
$$H'' + 5HH' + 3H^3 = 0.$$

The same differential equation is satisfied by Hubble's CONSTANTA in the Friedman model (the solutions are the same!).
