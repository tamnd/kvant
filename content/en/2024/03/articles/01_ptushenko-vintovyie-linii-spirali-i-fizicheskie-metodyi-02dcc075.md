Helical lines, spirals and physical methods

V. PTUSHENKO

The rapid development of physics in the twentieth century gave it the opportunity to contribute to the development of other sciences, primarily through the emergence of many new research methods. One of them was the method of X-ray diffraction analysis, based on the diffraction of X-ray radiation on the atoms of the crystal lattice. Let us recall that the diffraction of a wave by a periodic obstacle leads to differences in the intensity of the wave scattered in different directions. One of the clear examples is the diffraction of light on the surface of a DVD disc, which in reflected light shimmers with all the colors of the rainbow. Any crystal is also a periodic structure and, therefore, can serve as a diffraction grating. However, the distance between the “lines” of this grating is too small for a visible diffraction pattern to arise when reflected from such a periodic structure of visible light. But X-ray radiation, the wavelength of which is close to interatomic distances, gives an excellent diffraction pattern. This phenomenon was discovered back in 1912 by Max Laue and father and son William and Lawrence Bragg, for which they were awarded Nobel Prizes (1914 and 1915). Relatively soon, X-ray diffraction analysis became (and still remains) one of the main methods for studying the structure of molecules, including biologically important macromolecules. The key here was the assumption that nitrogenous bases - the “building blocks” of which the DNA molecule is composed - are complementary in one chain, i.e. complementary to the bases opposite them in the other chain. Such complementarity, in fact, strict correspondence, meant that one strand of this “double helix” already contained all the necessary information about its structure. The second thread can be completed on the first in the same way as a cast iron casting is “completed” in a casting mold. This structure in Russian received a very unfortunate, albeit catchy name - “double helix”. Of course, DNA strands do not fold into a flat curve - a spiral, but into a helical line (Fig. 2 and 3). This unfortunate translation of the English word helix, which can sometimes mean a spiral, unfortunately, has become entrenched in the Russian language. It is curious that in one of his first articles, Francis Crick directly said what kind of curve should be understood by this term: “A helix is ​​often loosely called a spiral; the difference is that the helix winds not around a cone, but around a cylinder, as a spiral staircase usually does” (1954). Of course, the author took some liberties here; a spiral is not the curve itself wound around a cone, but its projection onto a plane perpendicular to its axis. But the article was published in the popular science magazine Scientific American, and such freedom was permissible.
