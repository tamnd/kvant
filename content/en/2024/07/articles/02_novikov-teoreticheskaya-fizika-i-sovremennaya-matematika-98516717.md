Novikov is the creator of an outstanding scientific school, the author of fundamental monographs and textbooks. The problems posed by S.P. Novikov and the hypotheses put forward by him continue to have a great influence on the development of science. For more than 40 years, Sergei Petrovich's pedagogical activity was inextricably linked with the department of higher geometry and topology of the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics of Moscow State University, which he headed from 1982 until the end of his life. The memory of the great scientist, teacher and wonderful person will forever remain in our hearts.

For several centuries, the problem of solving (integrating) differential equations that describe natural phenomena has been central to the interaction of physics and mathematics. Everyone knows what role the famous two-body problem solved by Newton played in the development of mathematical methods of physics. For a long time after this, finding the exact analytical solution of differential equations remained the main tool of mathematical physics: if a problem was or seemed too difficult, then it was necessary to simplify it, and then look for an exact solution. Much effort has been spent searching for special “integrable cases” of famous problems such as the top motion problem. For this reason, in the 19th century, all mathematical methods were discovered and developed, including power and trigonometric series, the Fourier–Laplace integral transform, complex analysis, and symmetry considerations. Sometimes these methods led to remarkable negative results - evidence that this or that model is unsolvable in principle. In the 19th century, some strange integrable cases were discovered in which no obvious symmetry was visible: the integrability of a geodesic flow on two-dimensional ellipsoids in three-dimensional Euclidean space (Jacobi), the motion of a top with special parameters in a constant field of gravity (Kovalevskaya) and some others. What is the hidden symmetry behind all this? The answer was unclear until the advent of soliton theory. Solitons (or solitary waves) in water were known in the 19th century, but with the advent of the first computers in the 1960s, numerical experiments became possible that showed new remarkable properties of these nonlinear waves. Their mathematical explanation led to the creation of the theory of integrable soliton models, which connected various sections

In this series: nonlinear waves in a continuous medium (including plasma theory and nonlinear optics); quantum theory, scattering theory and periodic crystals; Hamiltonian dynamics; algebraic geometry of Riemann surfaces and Abelian varieties (theta functions). I will put forward the following thesis: a significant part of the most important discoveries in mathematics and mathematical physics were made in the process of developing the theory of integrable models. In the 1970s, important changes occurred in the collaboration between mathematicians and physicists. For example, topology results explained important experimental observations that had been pending for half a century. In the early 80s, physicists were unanimous in that the main thing that physicists have taken from mathematics over the past 10 years is topology. At the same time, discoveries made by physicists began to be applied in topology. Let us recall that from the theory of the self-dual Yang–Mills equation grew the theory of instantons with its remarkable applications to four-dimensional topology. The theory of the Yang–Baxter equation led to the theory of Jones polynomials in knot theory. The famous two-dimensional conformal field theories have produced many integrable models, which also represent new algebraic objects with deep connections to soliton theory and quantum groups. In the last decade, already in the 21st century, mathematical ideas that came from theoretical physicists had a great influence on the development of algebraic geometry. The interaction between mathematics and theoretical physics has not stopped; the achievements of each of these sciences enrich the other. In conclusion, I would like to make a wish to young mathematicians: expand the range of scientific seminars you attend in both mathematics and physics. Gain an understanding that will manifest itself, albeit over time.
