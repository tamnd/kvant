Foucauldian currents and gravity
S. GERASIMOV

It turns out that a very unusual force can act on the conductor in which Fuko currents flow. And you can find it in a regular educational laboratory.

There is every reason to be consistent in the fact that Foucault's currents are always closed. These currents are essentially ordinary induction

However, if the conductor and capacitor are placed in an alternating magnetic field, then a somewhat unusual phenomenon occurs, which causes genuine interest. To get acquainted with it, you can consider the forces with which the constructed magnetic field, homogeneous or inhomogeneous, acts on a symmetric rectangular frame closed to a capacitor (Fig. 1,a). opposite in direction to the force $\vec{F}_3$ with which the magnetic field acts on side 3. These forces do not produce anything interesting, except that they try to stretch or bend the conductor. Of greatest interest is the force $\vec{F}$, with which the magnetic field acts on section 2: this force does not have an antipode that would act on the conductor. The entire Ampere force, which at first glance is not compensated by anything, acting on the conductor $C$, can be calculated by summing the expressions $I[\Delta \vec{c} \times \vec{B}]$ over all elements of the conductor of length $\Delta c$. And since this is also true for a conductor located in a uniform magnetic field, then for this simple version of the problem we get $F = IcB$.

All this is true until the mixing current “flowing” between the plates of the capacitor is considered. The density of such a current is associated with an electrical direction arbitrary from the vector, and the weight of the mixing current $J$, of course, is equal to the current $I$. On the other hand, the density of the impulse transmitted to the alternating electromagnetic field is proportional to the vector product of the directivity of the electric field and the magnetic induction, which means that the total impulse of the electromagnetic field between the plates of the capacitor is also proportional to this vector product, and its arbitrary force, which is a force, is $F_J = -IcB$.

This force is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the total Ampere force acting on the conductor $C$.

It is further analyzed, but it has: if some body acquired an impulse, then a force must have acted on it. Similarly, if the electromagnetic field acquired an impulse, then something acted on it; this force can legally be considered reactive. It is this force that ensures the fulfillment of the law of conservation of momentum.

For now, we can only agree that the induction current may not be closed and a force may act on it that is not compensated by the usual effect on the conductor. For an ordinary turn with an ordinary capacitor, this force is extremely small: onioniotons and even less. This force is proportional to the direction of the electric field, which can be very strong at the ends or near the surface. This force is proportional to the strength of the current, which can be very large if the conductor is not a coil, but, say, a non-magnetic conducting disk located away from the source of the magnetic field (Fig. 1.6). A legitimate question: where can an alternating electric current with a density of $i$ come from in such a conductor?
