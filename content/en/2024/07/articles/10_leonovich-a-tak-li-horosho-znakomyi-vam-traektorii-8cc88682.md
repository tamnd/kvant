Are TRAJECTORIES so familiar to you?

It would seem that the concept of trajectory, introduced at the very first steps in mastering a physics course, could be simpler? However, even when describing various mechanical movements it “multiplies”, breaking the framework of the simple first set of straight lines and circles. Inevitably we have to turn to parabolas, spirals, cyclones when it is necessary to display the shape of the orbits of natural and artificial satellites, planets, comets and astrudes. And what are the trajectories of molecules in liquids and gases, charged parts in electric and magnetic fields?...

Yes, this, at first glance, a simple concept will accompany us for a long time when studying physics.

Questions and tasks

1. A black board is moving along a smooth horizontal table with speed v. What shape should chalk be left to this board, thrown horizontally with a speed u perpendicular to the direction of movement of the board, if: a) the friction between the chalk and/docker is negligible; b) is the training great?

2. What line does the midpoint of the segment between two pedestrians walking uniformly along parallel roads describe?

3. Two automations simultaneously start moving at the same speeds from the intersection of two mutually perpendicular roads. What trajectory will the second automatic period follow? And the first relative to the second?

4. Can the directions of the instantaneous velocity and acceleration of a matter point coincide when it moves along a curved path?

5. A wheel of radius R rolls without slipping along a horizontal road with a speed v₀. What trajectory does the point on the wheel rim follow? What is the radius of curvature at its vertex?

6. Two particles interacting with each other form a closed system, the center of mass of which ends. At some point in time, the position of the part and the trajectory of the part whose mass is m₁ are known. How to construct the trajectory of the second particle if its mass is m₂ = $\frac{1}{2} m₁$?

Microexperience

It's interesting that...
... when determining the trajectory of a horizontal thrown body, Galileo mistakenly considered it to be an arc of a circle. However, later he corrected his mistake and found that this trajectory was parabolic.
... Galileo and Kepler were the first to show that motion, free from the influence of forces, occurs directly. And Kepler came to the conclusion that the movement of planets in elliptical orbits clearly indicates the action of a force on them from the Sun.
... the cycloid was discovered, named and began to be studied again by Galileo, reflecting on the idea of ​​​​using a pendulum to regularize the clock. Geugens managed to prove that the period of oscillation of the pendulum will not depend on the amplitude when it describes a cycloid, and to apply this discovery in a clock.
... in order not to experience lateral shocks when a train moves from a straight section of the road to an arcuate one, it is necessary to connect them with rails running along a curve of variable curvature; the curves are also called clothids.
... the problem of determining the line of the shortest length connecting two given points of any surface (the so-called geodesic line) has found application in the study of the movement of bodies and particles in gravitational and electromagnetic fields in a continuous medium. It is best known as the problem of finding the shortest flight path of an aircraft between two airports.
... control of the trajectories of charged particles using electric and magnetic fields formed the basis for the creation of cathode ray tubes, used both in everyday life and in medicine and scientific instrument making.
... cosmic rays, produced more from the Sun, the so-called solar wind, are mostly streams of electrons and protons. At altitudes of tens of thousands of kilometers, the Earth's magnetic field blocks their path, calling the trajectories of particles onto their magnetic lines. The effect of “wind” on rarefied elephants will cause them to fade – the aurora borealis.
