The basis of the relative theory is made up of two Einstein steps and Lorentz transformations for the coordinates and times of two inertial reporting systems.

Einstein's first act. Physical phenomena occur in the same way in all inertial reporting systems.

Einstein's second act. The speed of light in a vacuum is the same in all inertial reporting systems and does not depend on the speed of the source or applicator.

Einstein's first step is a generalization of Galileo's mechanical principle of relative to all physical processes. An important conclusion follows from it: physical phenomena of the same nature must be described in inertial reporting systems by the same equations. The sameness of the occurrence of physical processes is equivalent to the sameness of quantitative (mathematical) correspondences between physical quantities.

The most revolutionary in its formulation is Einstein's second step, from which it follows that the classical law of addition of velocities is not observed for light. This, in turn, means the need to reconsider the classical ideas about the properties of space and time, since speed is a derivative of a quantity from coordinates and time.

Lorentz transformation. The outstanding Dutch scientist, Nobel Prize winner Hendrik Lorenz, was the first physicist who, based on Maxwell's equation of electromagnetism, obtained transformations of spatial and temporal variables that left the form of these equations unchanged when moving from one inertial reporting system to another. Now these transformations are called after him. They are a function of all modern physics. When used, the equations of mechanics, electrodynamics and quantum theory retain their mathematical form when moving from one inertial reporting system to another.

Let us write these transformations for the case of one-dimensional motion for the variables

$(x,t)$ and $(x',t')$, where $x$ and $x'$ are the coordinates of some points in two reporting systems, and the coordinate axis $x'$ moves along the $x$ axis at a constant speed $V$, $t$ and $t'$ are the times corresponding to the coordinates of the point $x$ and $x'$, $c$ – speed of light:

$$x = \frac{x' + Vt'}{\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2}}, t = \frac{t' + Vx'/c^2}{\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2}},$$

$$x' = \frac{x - Vt}{\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2}}, t' = \frac{t - Vx/c^2}{\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2}}.$$

Relations (1) are direct Lorentz transformations, relations (2) are inverse.

As the Lorentz transformation shows, the spatial and temporal variables of one reporting system are connected by linear correspondences with similar data of another. The consequence of this is the inequality of lengths of segments and time intervals in reporting systems moving relative to each other. This difference becomes more significant the closer the value of the relative speed $V$ is to the speed of light $c$. To verify the validity of this conclusion, we apply the Lorentz transformation for the case with a fixed position of the point of the relative moving reporting system and a fixed time of the event of the relative stationary reporting system.

In the first case, for $x_1' = x_2' = 0$ (the point remains at the origin of the moving coordinate system all the time) we have

$$\Delta t = t_2 - t_1 = \frac{t_2' - t_1'}{\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2}}.$$

This expression is nothing more than the time replacement coefficient. It shows that in a moving reporting system (one that moves at speed $V$) time flows more slowly than in a stationary system.

In the second case, under the condition $t_1 = t_2 = 0$ (stationary observable arbitrary moving reporting system, at the same point in time, which is necessary for accurate measurement of coordinates

Relativistic law of velocity addition. The relative theory is usually called relativistic theory. This name is associated with the English word relativity - relative. Let's use this term and we are talking about the law of addition of speed. This law connects the velocities of a point in two inertial reporting frames. Having specified the increments for the variables $(x, t)$ and $(x', t')$ using equation (1) and dividing these increments by each other, we obtain

$$v = \frac{v' + V}{1 + \frac{v' V}{c^2}}.$$

where $v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ and $v' = \frac{\Delta x'}{\Delta t'}$ are the speed of a point in a stationary and moving reporting system, $V$ is the speed of a moving system relative to a stationary one.

Expression (3) is the relativistic law of velocity addition. At low speeds, when $V \ll c$, it turns into the classical law of speed addition:

$$v = v' + V.$$

It is easy to see that this expression satisfies Einstein's second postulate. To do this, it is enough to equate $v'$ and $V$ to the speed of light, resulting in $v = c$.

Relativistic package. Now we have the necessary theoretical material to move on to a description of the movement of a relativistic package suitable for fast international flights. The main problem of relativistic packages is associated with the fantastically large kinetic energy of the packages. Already at a speed of 50,000 km/s, the kinetic energy of a mass of 1 kg is equal to $1,25 \cdot 10^{15}$ J. This is very high energy. It is more than a thousand times greater than the kinetic energy of the Apollo lunar module and is equivalent to the explosion of 20 atomic bombs like the one dropped on Hiroshima. To also impart kinetic energy to a mass of 1 kg due to jet propulsion, it will be necessary to expend energy many times greater (these are the features of jet propulsion). It is impossible to do this using chemical energy sources, since the energy output of chemical reactions is millions of times less than the required value. Moreover, it is impossible, using chemical energy sources, to impart a speed of 50,000 km/s to starships whose masses must be thousands of tons. To implement the flight mechanism, we need energy sources with the maximum energy output allowed by the laws of modern physics.

Flight modes of starships. In conclusion, we will consider the two main flight modes of starships. One is at constant acceleration, the other is at constant traction force. For simplicity, a discussion of starships with a maximum speed of 50,000 km/s, which in principle can be achieved using fusion engines. For such speeds, relativistic effects can be neglected, which will significantly simplify the analysis.

First, consider motion with constant acceleration $a$. In this case, the speed of the starship

$$v = at$$

and mass

$$m(t) = Me^{-\frac{v}{u'}} = Me^{-\frac{at}{u'}}.$$

Using this expression, we find the change in mass $\Delta m$ for time $\Delta t$:

$$\Delta m = M\left(e^{-\frac{a(t+\Delta t)}{u'}} - e^{\frac{at}{u'}}\right) = Me^{-\frac{at}{u'}}\left(e^{-\frac{a\Delta t}{u'}} - 1\right).$$

The exponent in parentheses is small due to the smallness of $\Delta t$. For small exponents, the exponent is equal to $1 - \frac{a\Delta t}{u'}$ with a good approximation. Taking this into account,

$$\Delta m \approx -\frac{a\Delta t Me^{-\frac{at}{u'}}}{u'}$$.

Dividing the model of this relationship by $\Delta t$, we obtain an explicit expression for fuel consumption:

$$\mu = \frac{aMe^{-\frac{at}{u'}}}{u'} = \frac{aMe^{-\frac{v}{u'}}}{u'}$$.

As can be seen, with constant acceleration, fuel consumption decreases according to an exponential law depending on the time and speed of the package. The maximum flow occurs at the moment of start, when it is equal to $\mu = \frac{aM}{u'}$.

Let us now consider the case of package motion with a constant traction force. Then the equation of motion of the packets takes the form

$$\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = m \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = ma = Me^{-\frac{v}{a'}} a = \mu u'$$,

$$a = \frac{\mu u' e^{\frac{v}{a'}}}{M}.$$

From this expression it follows that the acceleration of the starship will increase exponentially depending on the speed. The speed of the starship will increase according to the logarithmic law from the ratio of the initial and current masses, regardless of the acceleration mode.

The given expressions make it possible to calculate the body work costs required for interstellar flights based on the initial mass of the starship and its acceleration.
