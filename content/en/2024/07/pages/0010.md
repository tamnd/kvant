It is necessary to control the spatial configuration of the thermonuclear plasma in such a way as to ensure its thermal insulation from the material of the reactor walls and to form a flow of matter in the desired direction. Magnetic methods of plasma confinement and electromagnetic heating offer certain prospects for solving these issues. Without going into details of possible physical and technical approaches to solving the two identified problems using electromagnetic fields, we note the most important advantage of potential thermonuclear rocket engines. According to experts, these engines will be able to ensure the outflow of a plasma jet at a speed of about 50,000 km s, quite suitable for flights to the nearest stars. The value of this speed is mainly determined by the temperature inside the fusion reactor, namely the square root of this temperature. In this respect, thermonuclear rocket engines are similar to liquid-jet engines, where the speed of the jet stream is also proportional to the square root of the gas temperature. An exotic version of a thermonuclear engine could be engines based on the detonation of miniature hydrogen bombs. The idea of ​​such an engine was first proposed in the 1950s and 1960s in the Orion project. The hypothetical Orion engine was based on the idea of ​​using recoil impulses generated by the explosions of low-power atomic bombs. For such a pulse to work, the bombs must explode close to the massive plate located at the tail of the rocket. Moreover, the nuclear charge had to be designed so that the starship would absorb a significant portion of the explosion impulse.

Without dwelling on the technical problems associated with the implementation of the Orion project and later thermonuclear projects, we note the main conclusion. Explosive thermonuclear rocket engines are capable of accelerating spaceships only to a speed of 15,000 km s. This is a high speed, but it is clearly not enough for real interstellar flights. In such explosive starships, the values ​​of the final and initial masses of the rocket will vary greatly, since virtually all the mass will be used for acceleration. The same problem is inherent in plasma thermonuclear engines, but for them the ratio of the initial and final masses of starships is much more acceptable. So it will be possible not only to accelerate a plasma thermonuclear starship, but also to provide a favorable braking regime for the entire ship or its individual module. One solution to this problem of fuel shortage on thermonuclear starships could be the Bussard ramjet thermonuclear engine. In this engine, it is proposed to use interstellar matter, which mainly consists of hydrogen atoms, as thermonuclear fuel. Due to the very low concentration of interstellar matter, in order to create the required concentration of protons in a thermonuclear reactor, cosmic hydrogen will have to be captured over a large area using a special funnel. Estimates show that the outer diameter of this crater can reach hundreds and thousands of kilometers. This means that Bussard's starships must be gigantic in size, slowly accelerating with little jet thrust. Photon rockets.
