$\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = u \frac{\Delta M}{\Delta t}$.

The right side of this equation determines the magnitude of the reactive force, equal to
$F_p = u \frac{\Delta M}{\Delta t} = \mu u$,
where $\mu = \frac{\Delta M}{\Delta t}$ is the body’s work consumption to create a reactive impulse.

In order to obtain useful correspondences from the equation of motion of a relativistic reaction, it is necessary to move from finite differences to infinitesimal quantities - differentials. The result is a differential correspondence between the variables $M$ and $v$, which can be solved analytically. For the simplest case of motion with zero initial speed, the solution to the differential equation has the form
$$m = M \left( \left( 1 - \frac{v}{c} \right) / \left( 1 + \frac{v}{c} \right) \right)^{c/(2u')},$$
where $m$ is the mass of the reactant reaching the speed $v$, $M$ is the initial mass of the reactant. In the case of small final velocities, this formula transforms into the well-known Tsiolkovsky formula:
$$m = Me^{-v/u'}$$
A fair analysis shows that even for a speed of 200,000 km/s, the values ​​of $m$, calculated using the above formulas, differ from each other by no more than 20%. This means that the strong influence of relativistic effects in jet propulsion can only be felt at near-light speeds. For lower speeds, the classical theory of jet propulsion will work with a good approximation.
