M2802. Positive numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{2024}$ are considered around the circle in the indicated clockwise order. Let $A_i$ be the average argument of the number $a_i$ and the several following it in a clockwise direction. Prove that the largest of the numbers $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_{2024}$ is not less than the argumentative average of all numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{2024}$.

Let us put $n = 2024$ and let the argument mean of all numbers $\frac{a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n}{n}$ be equal to $a$. Let us divide the circle into arcs by points $K_1, K_2, \dots, K_n$ and assume that the number $a_i$ is on the arc between $K_i$ and $K_{i+1}$ (hereinafter we assume that $K_j = K_{j+n}$).

If in the number $A_i$ we take the argumentative mean of the numbers $a_i, a_{i+1}, \dots, a_{i+t}$, we will call this set the $\{a_i, a_{i+1}, \dots, a_{i+t}\}$ $i$th sausage. In this “sausage” terminology, we need to prove that the argumentative mean of the numbers in each sausage is not less than $a$. For each $i = 1, \dots, n$, draw the arrow along an arc from $K_i$ to $K_{i+t+1}$ clockwise (so that the arrow “covers” exactly the numbers of the $i$th sausage). Then we have a set of $n$ arrows, with exactly one arrow coming from each point $K_1, K_2, \dots, K_n$. It is clear that in such a directed graph there is a cycle (to find it, we can try using arrows, starting from some point, until we revisit some point).

Let's look at the arrows included in this cycle. The sausages corresponding to these arrows cover each of the numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ the same number of times. Let us denote the average arguments in these sausages as $S_1, S_2, \dots, S_k$, and the number of numbers in them as $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_k$. On the one hand, the sum of all the numbers in the sausages is equal to

$$S_1 t_1 + S_2 t_2 + \dots + S_k t_k.$$

On the other hand, all numbers appear in the sausages the same number of times, namely $\frac{t_1 + t_2 + \dots + t_k}{n}$ times. Therefore, the sum of the numbers in the sausages is

$$\frac{t_1 + t_2 + \dots + t_k}{n} \cdot na.$$

We got equality

$$S_1 t_1 + S_2 t_2 + \dots + S_k t_k = (t_1 + t_2 + \dots + t_k) a.$$

If all $S_i$ were smaller than $a$, then the left side would be smaller than the right. This means that some of $S_i$ is no less than $a$, which is what we needed to prove.
