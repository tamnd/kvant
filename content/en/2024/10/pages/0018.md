For $b = 1$ the following example works: 1, 2, 3, ..., 2024.
For $b = 2$ 1, 3, 5, ..., 2023, 2, 4, 6, ..., 2024 are suitable (in this case $a = 2023 + 2$).
Suppose that $x_1, x_2, ..., x_n$ (where $n = 2024$) is a permutation of the numbers 1, 2, ..., 2024 that satisfies the condition. Let us show that among the numbers $x_i$ there are no more than two remainders when divided by $b$. From here it will follow that for $b \ge 3$ the numbers 1, 2, 3 cannot be present in the line at the same time, i.e. there are no necessary examples for $b \ge 3$.
Note that if $x_i \equiv r(\bmod b)$, then either $x_{i+1} \equiv r(\bmod b)$ (in the case of $|x_i - x_{i+1}| = b$) or $x_{i+1} \equiv a - r(\bmod b)$ (in the case of $x_i + x_{i+1} = a$).
And similarly, if $x_i \equiv a - r(\bmod b)$, then either $x_{i+1} \equiv a - r(\bmod b)$ (in the case of $|x_i - x_{i+1}| = b$) or $x_{i+1} \equiv r(\bmod b)$ (in the case of $x_i + x_{i+1} = a$).
Consistently applying this message for $i = 1, 2, ...$, we understand that among the numbers $x_i$ there are no more than two remainders when divided by $b$.
M. Sagaftsan

M2804. Inside the triangle $ABC$, on the side $BC = 6$, there are two equal circles of radius 1, which touch each other, one inscribed in angle $B$, the other inscribed in angle $C$ (Fig. 1).
a) Prove that the point $M$ of intersection of the medians of triangle $ABC$ does not lie inside one of the given circles.
b) Prove that if $M$ lies on one of the circles, then the triangle $ABC$ is equilateral.

We will use the following well-known description of the point $M$. Let the triangle be divided by straight lines parallel to the sides into 9 equal triangles (Fig. 2). Then the only partition node located inside the triangle $ABC$ is $M$. (This description is not difficult to deduce from the fact that the medians are divided by the intersection point in a ratio of 2:1, counting from the vertex.)

Let's agree to consider the direction $BC$ horizontal, so that the circles are obtained from each other by an offset of 2 horizontally.
a) Line $AC$ touches the right circle. The straight line $C_1A_2$ is obtained from it by a shift of 2 to the left, which means that it touches the left circle (in other words, the left circle is inscribed in the triangle $BC_1A_2$). Thus, not a single point of the line $C_1A_2$ lies inside the left circle. In particular, $M$ does not lie inside the left circle. Likewise, it does not lie inside the right circle.
6) Once again, using the fact that $C_1A_2$ touches the left circle, we trample on the fact that $M$ lies on the left circle only if $M$ is the point of tangency. Ptak, let the left circle (inscribed in triangle $BC_1A_2$) touch its sides $C_1A_2$, $BA_2$, $BC_1$ at points $M$, $X$, $Y$ respectively. We know (from dividing into 9 equal triangles) that $MA_2 = MC_1$. Then from the equalities of the corresponding tangent segments we have
$$BC_1 = BY + YC_1 = BY + C_1M = BX + A_2M = BX + A_2X = BA_2.$$
We found that the triangle $BC_1A_2$ is equilateral: $BC_1 = BA_2$. Then the triangle $ABC$ is equilateral:
$$BA = \frac{3BC_1}{2} = \frac{3BA_2}{2} = BC.$$
Point 6) is resolved.
Note that point a) can be proven by other reasoning. Suppose that $M$ lies inside the right circle. Since $C_2$ is obtained from $M$ by shifting 2 to the left, then $C_2$ must be inside the left circle - a contradiction.
Note that this reasoning also works in a more general situation: we can replace the two conditions “$BC = 6$ and $M$ – the point of intersection of the medians” with one condition “$M$ – the midpoint of a segment of length 4, parallel to $BC$, the ends of which lie on the sides $AB$ and $AC$”.
E. Bakaev, P. Kozhevnikov
