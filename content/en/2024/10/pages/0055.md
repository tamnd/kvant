7. Let us note the angles of the parallelograms that are part of the angles of the polygon. Let the polygon have $n$ sides. Then the sum of the offset angles is $180^\circ(n-2)$. Each side of the polygon is adjacent to two offset angles (Fig. 11), their sum is obviously at least $180^\circ$. Summing such pairs on all sides, we obtain at least $180^\circ \cdot n$, i.e. at least $360^\circ$ more than when counted in another way. The excess occurs due to the fact that some angles are counted twice, namely those that are adjacent to two sides at once. Since each such angle is less than $180^\circ$, then there are at least three such angles. But the vertices of such angles are precisely the good vertices of the polygon.

8. For each figure, parallelogram or triangle, consider all the vertices of the figures on the boundary. For each figure, we draw vectors between adjacent points on the boundary so that their direction corresponds to traversing the figure’s boundary counterclockwise (Fig. 12).

Consider an arbitrary straight line $l$ and all vectors parallel to it. The sum of these vectors is zero. Indeed, each cut line parallel to $l$ is adjacent to sets of opposite vectors. If $l$ is parallel to some sides of the $100$-gon, then the sum of the vectors corresponding to the opposite sides of the $100$-gon is also zero, since they will be directed in opposite directions and equal in length. On the other hand, in every parallelogram the sum of the vectors corresponding to opposite sides is zero. Consequently, in two triangles the sum of all vectors parallel to $l$ will also be zero. Let us choose as $l$ a straight line parallel to some side of the first triangle. We obtain that the set of vectors of the second triangle parallel to $l$ are vectors opposite to the vector of the first triangle. Likewise for the other two sides. Therefore, for each side of the first triangle there is a side of the second triangle parallel and equal in length. Therefore, the triangles are congruent.
