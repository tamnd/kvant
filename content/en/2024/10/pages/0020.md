If we assume that the distance from the Earth to the Sun is constant and equal to 150 million km, then the speed of movement of the center of the Earth in the Copernican reporting system is 29.88 km/s.
In fact, the distance from the Earth to the Sun in the second half of June is $L_{\pi} = 152$ million km, and in the second half of January $L_{\alpha} = 147$ million km. Therefore, the speeds of the Earth's movement in the same reporting system at offset time intervals differ. In January the speed is approximately $v_{\alpha} = 30,489$ km/s, and in June $v_{\pi} = 29,487$ km/s.
This is due to the fact that the angular momentum (momentum) of the Earth is conserved as it moves around the Sun. The speed difference is approximately 1 km/s.
As a result, the time interval between two successive sunrises at the Earth's equator in January is longer than in June. In days $T = 24 \cdot 3600$ s.
During this period of time, the Earth manages to move in orbit a distance equal to the product of the speed of movement and the duration of the day. Consequently, to calculate the duration of the time interval between two successive sunrises, it is necessary to take into account that in relation to the stars the Earth rotates around an axis directed towards the northern Pole Star at an angle greater than $2\pi$. Due to the fact that the distances from the Earth to the Sun are different and the speeds of the Earth's orbit are also different, these additional rotations differ - for January they are greater than for June. The formula for estimating the difference in the length of the day is as follows:
$$\Delta T = \frac{T}{2\pi} \left( \frac{Tv_{\alpha}}{L_{\alpha}} - \frac{Tv_{\pi}}{L_{\pi}} \right) = 15,9 \text{ с} \approx 16 \text{ с}.$$
E. Sumkin
