This is due to the fact that fresh river waters flow into the seas and this reduces their salinity, and to the fact that water evaporates from the surface of the seas and oceans, and this, on the contrary, increases the salinity. If the density of water at a temperature of 4 q C and atmospheric external pressure $10^{5}$ Pa is equal to $10 00^{x}$ kg $м^{3}$, then salinity is usually called the number x, expressed in kilograms. When the density of salt water becomes equal to 1030 kg $м^{3}$, the salinity is 30, and at this value the feature of fresh water associated with the presence of a maximum in the graph of density versus temperature disappears. And at salinity values ​​greater than 30, the density of salt water decreases with increasing temperature. In the open seas (connecting with the oceans) and in the oceans themselves, the salinity of water is 32-35. In such reservoirs in cold seasons, convection of water leads to the fact that almost all of its mass, located below the layer of ice that appears on the surface, participates in the heat exchange process due to the convection mechanism. In addition, as is well known, in the oceans there are year-round, i.e. warm and cold currents independent of the season. Probably the most famous of the warm currents is the Gulf Stream. The presence of such currents and their stability is determined by the average annual temperatures of different places on the Earth's surface, the depths of the seas and oceans, the shapes of the continents and the straits connecting the seas and oceans.

For both freshwater reservoirs and saltwater reservoirs, the thickness of the layer of warming water in warm seasons is determined not only by air temperature, transparency and thermal conductivity of the water, but also by winds (storms and storms) above the surface of the water. At the same time, the flow of thermal energy from inside the Earth to its surface continues, only above the bottom of the sea or ocean it is not the solid soil that is heated, but liquid water. All of the above concerning the water part of the Earth’s surface makes it impossible to create a simple (at the school level) and unambiguous model. Therefore, from the entire wide palette of possibilities, we will select those situations that can still be described within the framework of simple models. Let's consider the situation with Lake Baikal. The water in Baikal has a mineralization of approximately 0.1. It is incorrect to use the word “salinity” in this case, since the composition of salts in Baikal is not the same as in sea water. The average annual surface temperature is close to zero (from 0.7 q C in the south to 3.6 q C in the northern part). In summer, the average air temperature over the lake is 20 q C, and in winter, respectively, 20 q C. The water in the lake is transparent - in summer, details on the bottom are visible from above to a depth of 5-8 meters. In winter, water transparency increases to 30–40 meters. During the winter season, a layer of ice 100–120 cm thick grows. It is also known that 4 q C is the water temperature in the deep layers of the lake. And the average water temperature of the surface layers in an open lake is approximately 8 q C. We will assume that due to the winds, the temperature over the entire surface of the water is the same, but these winds do not cause the water to mix, i.e. cold water in the warm season after the ice has melted warms up only due to the mechanism of thermal conductivity. Accordingly, in the cold season, when the water right up to the surface has acquired a temperature of 4 q C, the water freezes in the absence of convection of water under the ice layer.
