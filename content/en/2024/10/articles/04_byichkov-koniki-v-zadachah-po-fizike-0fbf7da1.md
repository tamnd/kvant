THE ANCIENT GREEKS WERE ALREADY Familiar with the figures that we today call second-order curves. They called them conic $сечениями^{1}$ or conics and devoted scientific research to them. In the 17th-18th centuries, these curves began to be used in mechanics: it turned out that projectiles fly along a parabolic trajectory, and planets rotate in elliptical orbits. Later, studying the movement of bodies in space, scientists found that the launch speed of an object determines its trajectory. It turned out that a body launched from the Earth at different speeds can move in space along various curves, which belong to second-order curves: ellipse, parabola and hyperbola. This article is devoted to the consideration of second-order curves and their application in physics problems. First, let us recall the definitions of second-order curves and some of their properties from a geometric point of view. A parabola is a set of points in the plane equally distant from a given point F and a given straight line l (Fig. 1). Point F is called the focus of the parabola, and line l is called the directrix. An ellipse is a set of points on a plane, the sum of the distances from which to two given points A and B is equal to a constant value (Fig. 2). These points are called the foci of the ellipse.

Problem 1. A person wants to cross the river in a rowing boat to point A, located exactly opposite the departure point B. The speed of the river is constant and equal to v. The boatman rows in such a way that his speed relative to the water is always directed towards point A and is equal to v. What trajectory is the boat moving along? Let us denote the width of the river d and consider a straight line l, perpendicular to its banks and located at a distance d downstream from the starting point A of the boat. The boat is initially at a distance d both from point B on the opposite bank and from line l. Since the speed of the water and the speed of the boat relative to the water are equal in magnitude and equal to v, the water carries the boat downstream the same distance that the boat travels in the direction of point B.
