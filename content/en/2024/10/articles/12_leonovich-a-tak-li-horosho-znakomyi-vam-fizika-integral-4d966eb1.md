Are you familiar with PHYSICS + INTEGRAL?

The derivative, as promised in our previous issue, passes the baton to its indifferent partner - the integral. But for the relationship between them to become akin to the connection between subtraction and addition, centuries had to pass, during which integral calculus developed independently of differential calculus. From Archimedes to Newton and Leibniz, eminent scientists, reflecting on practical problems, depicted ingenious ways of finding areas and volumes. By the way, we often use them even now, trying to “cheat the integral.”
Today we can compare different approaches to solving physical problems and evaluate the possibility and effectiveness of using the integral.

**Problem 10. Find the radius of curvature of the ellipse at its vertices if the semimajor axis is equal to $a$, small is equal $b$.**
Consider a circle of radius $b$ with center at the origin of the rectangular coordinate system $xy$. The equation of a circle is $x^2 + y^2 = b^2$. Let us stretch the circle in the direction of the $x$ axis by $k$ times. Then the point $A$ with coordinates $(x, y)$, belonging to the circle, will go to the point $A'$ with coordinates $x' = kx, y' = y$. Let $k = \frac{a}{b} > 1$. Let us rewrite the equation of the original circle in the form
$$\left(\frac{b}{a} x'\right)^2 + y'^2 = b^2, \frac{x'^2}{a^2} + \frac{y'^2}{b^2} = 1.$$
The result is an ellipse with semi-major axis $a$ and semi-minor axis $b$. Therefore, an ellipse is a curve that is obtained by stretching (or rolling) a certain circle in a certain direction.
Let us imagine that a material point is moving along a circle with a constant modulus speed $v$. At the same time, another material point moves along the ellipse. In this case, the line connecting these two points is always parallel to the $x$ axis (Fig. 15). Consider an infinitesimal arc $CED$ on a circle. When stretched, it turns into an arc $C'E'D'$ on an ellipse. The time of movement of points along these arcs will be the same.

**Fig. 15**
The velocities of points in positions $E$ and $E'$ will also be the same. However, the normal mixtures of points $FE$ and $F'E'$ differ by a factor of $k$. The normal accelerations of the points will differ by the same amount. At point $E'$the acceleration will be equal to
$$a_n = \frac{v^2}{b} k = \frac{av^2}{b^2}.$$
Therefore, the radius of curvature of the ellipse at point $E'$ is equal to
$$R_{\kappa p} = \frac{v^2}{a_n} = \frac{b^2}{a}.$$
Similarly, rationally, we can obtain that at the vertices of the ellipse lying on the axis $y$, the radius of curvature is equal to $\frac{a^2}{b}$.

Task 14. How can you use three audio signal receivers to determine the location of an enemy gun shot?

The method described below for determining the location of an object (enemy gun) was used during World War II (hyperbolic line method).

It is known that the speed of sound propagation in air (under normal conditions) is approximately $c = 330$ m/s. Let us assume that two signal receivers located at points $F_1$ and $F_2$ record the sound of the same shot of an enemy gun located at point $M$ (Fig. 21).

If receiver $F_1$ recorded the sound t seconds later, then the difference in distance $MF_1 - MF_2$ is equal to $c\tau$. Point $M$ is on the corresponding hyperbola with foci at points $F_1$ and $F_2$ (right branch of the hyperbola). Since in practice signal receivers are located at a considerable distance from the enemy gun, we can assume with high accuracy that the target point is located on asympto $a$, which is uniquely specified by the known parameters of the hyperbola. By placing another signal receiver at some point $F_3$, another asympita $b$ of the hyperbola with focuses at points $F_1$ and $F_3$ is similarly determined. The required point $M$ is located at the intersection of lines $a$ and $b$.
