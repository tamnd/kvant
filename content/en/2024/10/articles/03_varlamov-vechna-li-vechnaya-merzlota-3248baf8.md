Now the moment has come when we need to deal with the question posed in the title of the article: Is “permafrost” eternal? Related to this question is the following question: over what period of time, when the climate changes from “warm” to “glacial” or vice versa, is the steady-state thickness of the “permafrost” layer established?

Let us assume that the average pressure temperature in the territory of Yakutia during a long warm period was 0 °C and there was no permafrost. At a depth of about 1 km the temperature was +30 °C. And now the ice age has come with an average pressure of thermal conductivity, we will not stop, but will work with a simplified model situation.

Let's consider a simple version of the growth of the thickness of the permafrost layer: from the surface, where the temperature is –30 °C, to the place where the temperature is 0 °C, the temperature distribution is linear and the heat flow leads to the fact that the internal energy reserve of this layer decreases. And the heat flow from inside the Earth to the permafrost layer is considered small and does not affect the temperature distribution. In this case, we obtain an equation with a simple solution for small values ​​of x compared to the value of L:

$$dt \frac{S\lambda T}{x} - dt \frac{S\lambda T}{L-x} = dx \frac{SCT}{2}, t = \frac{C}{4\lambda} x^2.$$

Substituting x = 300 m, we obtain a time interval of $t_{300} \approx 960$ years. Since there are places in Yakutia where the thickness of the permafrost layer reaches 1.5 km, the corresponding period of time will be 25 times longer, i.e. the ice age lasted more than 20 thousand years.

The considered model does not take into account at all that the composition of the soil in its upper layers includes water, which turns into ice at temperatures below 0 °C. This simplification is valid if the mass fraction of water in the soil is very small. How will the model change and will it be able to account for thermal phenomena on the surface of a deep and wide body of water (lake, sea or ocean)?

It turns out that in this case the model loses its uniqueness. The fact is that fresh water in flowing lakes (Baikal, Ladoga, Onega and countless smaller lakes) or insufficiently salty water in some seas (Azov, Baltic and Caspian) has a peculiarity - the density of water reaches its maximum at a temperature of about +4 °C. This leads to the fact that in cold weather (when the air temperature drops below 0 ° C) water with maximum density accumulates near the reservoir and until the water in the reservoir reaches such a temperature, the water freezes on the surface

Let us consider a broken line running along the sides of the polygon along the left border from $N$ to $S$, the links of which are the segments $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{n-1}, a_n$ (Fig. 29). We will successively transform it into a broken line $\tilde{a}_n, \tilde{a}_{n-1}, \dots, \tilde{a}_2, \tilde{a}_1$, going from $N$ to $S$ clockwise along the right border of the polygon. The ends of the broken line remain fixed at the poles. At each step, two adjacent links, which are the sides of the parallelogram of the partition, will be replaced by two other links - two other sides of the same parallelogram. The segment $a_k$ will be located on one of the segments marked with the number $k$. It is argued that this can always be done.

Remark 6. The estimate proved in Proposition 9 shows that a double set of 5 balls cannot be sorted in less than 15 balls. Paper [3] proposed an algorithm that sorts a double set of $n$ balls in $\left\lfloor 2n^2/3 \right\rfloor$ balls. If $n = 2^k + 1$, then this algorithm works optimally and requires only $n(2n-1)/3$ balls. In addition, the authors of this article performed computer experiments and found that for $n \leq 20$, with the exception of the case of $n = 6$, sorting a double set of $n$ balls can indeed be done in $\left\lfloor n(2n-1)/3 \right\rfloor$ shifts. In this case, sorting occurs, generally speaking, no longer in the same way as it was in the case of $n = 5$. If $n = 6$, then you need one more shift than required by the lower estimate (23 instead of 22). There are also upper and lower bounds for the case when there are $k$ balls of each type, see [4].
