If we get two equalities, then in one of these pairs the masses of the coins are equal to 9 g, and in the other - to 10 g. Then the masses of the pairs {1, 3}, {2, 4}, {5, 6} are 19 g each. If $n \neq 3$, we divide the coins into pairs in an arbitrary way. Each pair has a mass of 18 g, 19 g or 20 g, with the pair weighing 18 g and 20 g equally. If for each pair we determine its mass, then we can add up to obtain the required division of coins. Indeed, pairs with a mass of 19 g can be left unchanged, and by combining one coin each from pairs with masses of 18 g and 20 g, we also obtain the desired pairs.

Let's compare the first pair with the second, then with the third, and so on until we get an inequality or run out of pairs. If the inequality is obtained, then from all the pairs that we compared, we will form the first pile. If there are pairs left, then we will act with them in the same way and create new piles. Let's start comparing groups: the first group is comparable to the second, then to the third, and so on until we get an inequality or the groups run out. If the inequality is obtained, for example, the first group turned out to be lighter than the $k$th (if vice versa, further reasoning is similar), then in the first group the mass of the light pair is 18 g, and in the $k$th group the mass of the heavy pair is 20 g. If $k$ is less than the number of groups, then we compare $X$ with all groups whose numbers are greater than $k$ - this is how we find out the masses of pairs in all piles, starting from the $(k+1)$th. If some pairs are not included in the piles, then compare one of them with half of the group $X$ (which includes one coin each from its constituent pairs). The result of the comparison allows us to find out the masses of all pairs that are not included in the piles.

Note that if the masses of two groups are equal, then in these groups the light pairs have the same mass and the heavy pairs too.
