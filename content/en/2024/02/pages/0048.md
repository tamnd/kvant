Rope slipping from the horizontal bar

The ideas on the basis of which the solution to the previous problem was based can be illustrated using the example of another well-known problem.

Problem 5. On a horizontal bar, which is a thin, smooth, horizontal rod of small radius, hangs a uniform soft rope of length L. At the initial moment, the lengths of the ends of the rope hanging from the horizontal bar are equal and it is at rest. A little milk sets the rope in motion. How far will either end of the rope move by the time the rope moves to interact with the horizontal bar? How does the pulling force of the rope on the large horizontal bar (at the level of its axis) depend on the distance x traveled by either end of the rope during the sliding process?

It may seem that the answer to the first question is $\frac{L}{2}$ (in other words, the rope will move with the horizontal bar when it slides completely off it), but this is not so. As we will see later, at some moments during the process of the rope sliding, the force of its pressure on the horizontal bar turns out to be zero, although the length of the hanging ends at this moment is not zero.

Naturally, we assume that the mechanical energy of the rope is conserved when sliding. Let the right end of the rope move downwards. Let us denote by $x$ the coordinate of this end at a non-coordinate moment in time (the axis $x$ is directed downwards, the floor of the axis coincides with the initial position of the ends of the rope; Fig. 9). The center of mass of the rope at the moment of the rope under consideration is lowered relative to the equilibrium position by a distance

$$x_c = \frac{1}{M} \left( \frac{Mx}{L} \cdot \frac{x}{2} + \frac{(-Mx)}{L} \cdot \frac{(-x)}{2} \right) = \frac{x^2}{L},$$

where $M$ is the mass of the rope. This formula is obtained based on the following reasoning. At the moment when the lower end of the rope passes the coordinate $x$, the rope can be represented as a superposition of the initial equilibrium state and two sections with zero total mass: a section with a negative mass $\frac{-Mx}{L}$, the center of mass of which is equally located at the point with the coordinate $\frac{-x}{2}$, and a section with a positive mass $\frac{Mx}{L}$, the center of mass of which is located at the point with the coordinate $\frac{x}{2}$. Thus, the law of conservation of energy takes the form

$$\frac{x'^2}{2} = \frac{gx^2}{L},$$ from where $x' = \sqrt{\frac{2g}{L}}x$.

Differentiating the last equality, we obtain formulas for acceleration:

$$x'' = \sqrt{\frac{2g}{L}}x',$$ $x'' = \frac{2g}{L}x$.

Let us consider the right vertical section of the rope as a system of variable state, then for the tension force $T_\pi$ acting on this section of the large horizontal bar, we have the equation

$$\frac{M}{L} \left( \frac{L}{2} + x \right) x'' + \frac{M}{L} x'^2 =$$

$$= \frac{M}{L} \left( \frac{L}{2} + x \right) g - T_\pi + \frac{M}{L} x'^2.$$

Solving this equation, we obtain the formula for the tension force:

$$T_\pi = 2Mg \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{x^2}{L^2} \right).$$

In exactly the same way, considering a light vertical section of the rope, we can obtain a formula for the force of tension $T_\pi$of the left section of the rope over the horizontal bar:

$$T_\pi = 2Mg \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{x^2}{L^2} \right).$$

As in the previous problem, the tension forces on the left and right of the bend turned out to be the same.
