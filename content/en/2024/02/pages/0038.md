Exercises

4. Prove the periodicity theorem.
5. a) Prove that the sum of the length of the period and pre-period in the sequence from the periodicity theorem does not exceed the number of elements of the set of states.
6) Each natural number $K < 100$ is assigned a corresponding natural number $F(K)$, also less than 100. The sequence $A(1) = 1$, $A(K+1) = F(A(K))$ is constructed. Prove that there is a number $N < 100$ for which $A(N) = A(2N)$.

A question arises. Reasonings 1 and 2 look quite convincing. Then formalize them, and in two more ways? Then pursue rigor when everything is already clear? After all, even in the school course there is no complete rigor. For example, important concepts such as “theorem” and “proof” are not strictly defined.

The fact is that in mathematics not only natural objects can be modeled, but also reasoning itself. Formalization is such modeling. Only it makes reasoning a mathematical object. This is often needed to prove the impossibility of something. For example, to prove the solvability of a cubic equation in radiacs, it is enough to derive a formula, but to prove the unsolvability of a general equation of the fifth degree in radiacs, it was necessary to formalize the very concept of solvability (which is what Abel did at the beginning of the last century). In order to construct a theorem from which its own unprovability would follow, and thereby an example of an unprovable and irrefutable statement, a formalization of the concept “theorem”, “proof” was needed. (It's amazing how difficult it turned out to be!)

In our case, it is not a matter of strictness: various formalizations make it possible to build on reasoning from different angles, which allows us to see it and apply it in different situations. Both methods of formalizing identity are often found: arrows in the theory of finite state machines, functions in the theory of dynamical systems. Let's give examples.

Exercise 6. In some companies, each person has exactly two friends. Prove that if all friends join hands, they will form one or more round dances.

Problem 7. Some combination of repetitions of faces brought the Rubik's cube out of the correct position. Prove that the cube can be solved again by repeating this combination several more times.

To solve this problem, it is enough to look at the combination of cube repetitions as a function that transforms one of its states into another. Since each repetition is invertible, this function itself is invertible and, therefore, one-to-one. Under the influence of such a function, the elements of a finite set “lead a round dance.”

What will happen in the case of an infinite set of states? Will there be anything new there other than “round dances”? An example of one of the possible situations is the following task.

Problem 8. Baron Monchhausen told Geord Kapitur that he could write natural numbers in a series without a unit, that all numbers, except for a few, would be no greater than their number in the series. Let's not forget the Baron?

Solution. Let's try to imagine the correspondence between numbers and their numbers. For example, like this:

$$f_1(1)f_2(2)...f_k(k)$$

The idea is that our numbers are the same numbers, just rearranged. Let at the initial moment the numbers be equal to numbers, and in place of the ones there is a “hole”:

$$\langle \rangle 2 3 ... k ...$$

$$1 2 3 ... k ...$$

Let's try to construct a baron sequence by rearranging the numbers. Let's put $f(1)$ in place of units, then in place $f(1)$ a “hole” will appear:

$$f(1) 2 ... \langle \rangle ... k ...$$

$$1 2 ... f(1) ... k ...$$
