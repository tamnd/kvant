Meitner (together with her nephew, physicist Otto Frisch) carried out the necessary calculations and showed that in the mass of uranium the nuclear fission reaction can be a chain reaction, and also for the first time estimated the energy that the products of this reaction would have - tens of millions of times more than in the chemical reactions of combustion and explosion. An article by L. Meitner and O. Frisch in the journal Nature, published on February 11, 1939, created a sensation in scientific circles. And it wasn’t just the amazing amount of energy. But soon Niels Bohr published a discouraging article in which he wrote about the practical impossibility of carrying out the fission process in a large mass of uranium. After all, this does not happen in nature. Natural uranium consists of two isotopes. The main isotope, uranium-238, has such a low probability of fission that a chain reaction will not occur. Another isotope, uranium-235, which is more capable of fission, is contained in natural uranium only 0.7%, and its isolation would require the creation of industrial production inaccessible to one country. It is well known what happened next. The Second World War has already begun. A group of European physicists, forced to emigrate to the USA from the horrors of the fascist regime, managed to understand that a country like the USA is capable of creating industrial production of the uranium-235 isotope. And America can and should overtake Germany in creating a “superbomb”. After all, it was in Germany that such an opportunity was discovered, and such work was probably already underway there. In 1944, Otto Hahn became a Nobel laureate “for his discovery of the fission of heavy nuclei.” It is difficult to overestimate the role of Lise Meitner in this epoch-making discovery.
