As a result, the number of vectors $\vec{a}_i$ reaches millions. To solve such systems, special algorithms are being developed that allow this to be done quite quickly. Once the linearly dependent vectors are found, it is enough to simply move the numbers corresponding to these vectors and get the desired square. This stage of Pomeran's quadratic solution is called matrix.

However, a problem may arise: large prime factors may appear in the expansion of the numbers $x^2 (\bmod r)$. then the system will be large and the method will be slow. Therefore, before the matrix stage, an evaluation process is done.

Let's proceed as follows: discard all sufficiently large factors. Namely, we will agree to consider only prime numbers less than some $B$, denoting their number by $\pi(B)$. It is not known in advance which factors may appear in the expansions. In order to, without decomposing the numbers, identify those that modulo $r$ in the expansion contain primes not larger than $B$, a process similar to the process in the Eratovfen sieve method is used.

Namely, for all numbers $x^2 (\bmod r)$ one checks in turn whether they are divisible by the next $p$ or not. If they are divided, then they are not crossed out (as in the Eratovfen sieve method), but divided by this $p$. They do this until they have gone through all the prime numbers less than $B$. Those numbers that will turn into 1 after this operation are suitable for us: they are decomposed into prime factors less than $B$. Enlightenment ends when the total number of numbers found is greater than the number $\pi(B)$. Then the system (***) will have more unknowns than equations, and it will have a solution.

In the example we are considering, $B = 11$, $\pi(B) = 5$. Enlightenment was also completed: we did not consider the squares $42^2$, $43^2$, $45^2$, $46^2$, $47^2$, $48^2$. Their remainders contain prime factors in the expansion greater than $B$.

Screening is the most labor-intensive process, after which it proceeds to the matrix stage.

The solution to RSA-129

The RSA-129 hacking project started on September 3, 1993 under the leadership of mathematicians A. Lenstra and D. Atkins. They united about 600 people in 24 countries, 1600 personal computers. Coordination is carried out via the Internet and was one of the first such projects.

For breaking RSA-129, the cutoff $B = 16333609$ was chosen and the factor base size was $\pi(B) = 524339$. Based on experience with many other factorizations, it was concluded that it is necessary to collect more than 8 million numbers such that $y = x^2 (\bmod r)$. By March 21, 1994, approximately 8.25 million numbers had been collected. This involved about 220 days of procession.
