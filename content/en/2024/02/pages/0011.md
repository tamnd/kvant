Nuclear physics

When Khariton’s laboratory (together with the Institute of Chemical Physics) separated from the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology, he himself regularly attended a seminar on nuclear physics, which Igor Vasilievich Kurchatov, head of the newly created laboratory of the atomic nucleus, began to teach at LPTI. It was 1932, a turning point in the physics of the microworld. The neutron was discovered, a proton-neutron model of the atomic nucleus was created, and work on artificial radioactivity began. Enrico Fermin in Italy, Federic and Irene Jollu-Curie in France have been working for several years on the interaction of neutrons with atomic nuclei

personal elements, including uranium nuclei. The awarding of Nobel Prizes to these scientists (Jollus-Curie in chemistry in 1935 and Fermin in physics in 1938) was recognition of the importance of these works both for the development of the theory of the atomic nucleus and for practical purposes - the obtained so-called local atoms.

In 1934, the German physicist and discoverer of the element rhenium, Ida Nodak, analyzing the results of the experiments of Jollu-Curie and Fermin, made the assumption that the nuclei of heavy elements, when hit by a neutron, can split into two or three parts having very high energy. Since she studied physical chemistry and electrochemistry, she published her reports in the journal of applied chemistry. But physicists did not read chemical journals, and chemists did not care about atomic nuclei. Therefore, Nodak's hypothesis passed independently. The German physicist and radiochemist Otto Hahn, director of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute of Chemistry, especially protested against this hypothesis.

But four years have passed and everything has changed. Otto Hahn and his assistant Fritz Strassmann, repeating Fermin with training using slow neutrons from uranium, proved the fact of the division of nuclei into two parts and the emergence of chemical elements in the middle of the periodic table from uranium nuclei. They dated the article about this discovery as December 17, 1938. Just two weeks later, on January 4, 1939, it was published in one of the most famous scientific journals - in the journal Natural Sciences, which was read by both chemists and physicists.

By this time, the talented author physicist and radiochemist Lise Meitner had been working with Otto Hahn for almost 30 years. It was she who convinced Hahn of the possibility of fission of the uranium nucleus. (Some histories of physics believe that it was she who made the experiments that led Hahn to believe in nuclear fission.) Soon after this, she was forced to leave Germany and settled in Stockholm.
