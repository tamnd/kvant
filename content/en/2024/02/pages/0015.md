JULIY BORISOVICH KHARITON

One of the points of Kurchatov's work plan was the creation of an atomic bomb design. It was clear to him that this would be a very complex structure that would combine nuclear physics, the physics of explosion and detonation, automation and mechanics. He knew only one specialist of such a broad and engineering profile. It was his colleague at the Leningrad Institute of Physics and Technology, Yuliy Borisovich Khariton. And when Kurchatov arrived from Kazan to Moscow, he found Khariton and invited him to head the design department of the laboratory. Khariton did not immediately agree. 45 years later, in his annual speech at the Sarov House of Scientists, he recalls: “One day in the summer of 1943, Igor Vasilyevich came to me and began to say that we needed to return to the interrupted work on the uranium problem. His words seemed like complete nonsense to me. The Germans are still deep in our territory, we need to help the army in every way possible. And here is the uranium problem. After all, it is clear that the war will end before we make atomic weapons. Once we finish the war, that’s another matter. Igor Vasilyevich was in no hurry. He invited me to attend seminars in his laboratory. I started going to seminars, at first occasionally, then more often. Thoughts began to gradually shift from TNT to uranium. I remembered our third (unpublished) article with Zeldovich and Gurevich, where we calculated the critical mass of the uranium-235 isotope. I also remembered my 1937 article on centrifugation of gas mixtures to separate gases. This method can be useful for obtaining the desired uranium isotope. And Zeldovich just arrived along with the Institute of Chemical Physics, which was determined to be located not in Leningrad, but in Moscow. And long conversations ensued. And it didn’t take much effort for Igor Vasilyevich to get me to join the team he was putting together.” But Khariton does not say that, at Kurchatov’s request, he wrote and on July 7, 1943 presented a report “On the question of the possibility of an explosion in the ocean.”

This report also contained some new calculation results, which for the first time made it possible to talk about the creation of an atomic bomb. It now exists in a single handwritten (by Khariton) copy marked top secret. In the autumn of 1943, Yu.B. Khariton began to closely prepare for his new work. A year later, he already clearly understood all the main areas of work and took part in the preparation of the decisive resolution of the State Defense Committee “On urgent measures to ensure the deployment of work carried out by Laboratory No. 2 of the USSR Academy of Sciences.” In this resolution, along with a detailed list of measures for the construction of laboratory buildings, a separate paragraph establishes “... To organize a design bureau with an experimental mechanical plant at Laboratory No. 2 and in a month to present a work plan for 1945.” In this Resolution, Khariton’s name does not yet appear, although there is evidence of the GKO’s fundamental agreement on the issue of his leadership of the newly created design bureau. And Khariton completed his work at the defense research institute-6 and began to work closely on the creation of an atomic bomb. In April 1945, he was allowed, along with Kurchatov, to get acquainted with the data received by intelligence about American work. On July 16, 1945, in the desert area of ​​New Mexico, for the first time in the world, an atomic bomb was tested - a monstrous explosion with an almost instantaneous release of energy of one hundred trillion $( 10^{14} )$ joules, which is equivalent in terms of the mechanical effect of the explosion (destruction and shock wave) to 21 thousand tons of TNT. But in addition to mechanical action, a nuclear explosion is accompanied by severe thermal effects (fires and burns) and radiation exposure.
