With much more accurate calculations, they reliably confirmed the estimated results that were given in the work of Meitner and Frisch. Like Niels Bohr, Khariton and Zeldovich came to the conclusion that the practical use of nuclear energy requires the hard-to-find isotope uranium-235 or an as yet unknown transuranium element, later called plutonium. Two articles by Khariton and Zeldovich, published in the Journal of Experimental and Theoretical Physics, greatly alarmed American physicists, who decided that work on creating an atomic bomb had also begun in the USSR. But they were wrong. All the forces of the state were aimed at producing modern aircraft, tanks and artillery pieces. The only significant thing was the discovery in 1940 of the phenomenon of spontaneous (without the participation of a neutron) fission of heavy nuclei (G.N. Flerov and others). This was very interesting and important for the development of the theory of the atomic nucleus, but had no practical significance. And work with irradiation of uranium with neutrons was only just beginning. In 1940, I.V. Kurchatov, at a session of the Department of Physical and Mathematical Sciences of the USSR Academy of Sciences, made a large report “On the Problem of Uranium,” in which he talks about the work of Khariton and Zeldovich on this problem. But no specific decisions were made to intensify work on uranium and allocate the necessary funds for this. But another commission of the Academy of Sciences was created on the problem of uranium. The commission consisted of 11 academicians and two senior researchers - Kurchatov and Khariton, included in it at the insistence of Ioffe. The critical mass of the uranium-235 isotope, necessary to initiate a chain reaction of uranium fission with a gigantic release of energy, has been reduced. But this work was not published then. War broke out. The first task already in the first month of the war was the evacuation of scientific equipment from Leningrad laboratories; all institutes of the Academy of Sciences moved to the east - to the Volga region, the Urals and Central Asia. Much valuable equipment was removed with the last echelons before the complete blockade of Leningrad. And the evacuation of the employees’ families was successful only because the leadership of this operation was entrusted to a wonderful woman, Maria Nikolaevna Khariton. After the resumption of work by the LFTI and the Institute of Chemical Physics in Kazan in 1942, all efforts were aimed at developing defense work. Ya.B. Zeldovich completed the development of gunpowder for rocket artillery (the famous “Katyusha”). Yu.B. Khariton continued the work begun before the war on the creation of new types of explosives for mines and other types of weapons, which used detonation rather than combustion. And most importantly, it was necessary to organize mass production of these substances. He was seconded to Moscow, to the secret military scientific institute NII-6, where his not only scientific but also organizational talents were fully demonstrated - the ability to lead large scientific teams. I.V. Kurchatov (together with A.P. Aleksandrov) was engaged in protecting ships from new German mines that reacted to the magnetic field created by the steel hull of the ship. For some time, nuclear physics was forgotten. But by the end of 1942, information began to accumulate that in the USA and Great Britain (and, possibly, in Germany) active work was underway to study the fission reaction of uranium. This information came through two channels.
