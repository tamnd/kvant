He who loves practice will achieve knowledge in art. Albrecht Durer

The book of the Polish mathematician Hugo Steinhaus “Mathematical Kaleidoscope” describes an interesting model of the dodecahedron [1]. The model is a development of two halves of a dodecahedron; they can be cut out of thick cardboard. To demonstrate, you need to put these two halves together and tighten them with an elastic stretchable “hoop” - an elastic band. If you toss this structure, the hoop, shortening, will pull the model into a three-dimensional body. The hoop cannot become any shorter, so it remains on the surface. This model is interestingly and clearly written about in the “Models” section of the “Mathematical Etudes” website [2]. The line along which the hoop is adjacent after folding is $является^{геодезической.}$ You can read about geodesic lines on polyhedra on pages «Kvant» in the article [3] and in the “Etudes” section of the “Mathematical Etudes” website [4]. Other regular polyhedra also have closed geodesics (green lines on figure 1), this suggests that for them it is possible to come up with models similar to the Steinhaus model. workpiece The work of solidifying the model by a small amount $dH$ is equal to the product of force and displacement, i.e. $FdH$. In this case, the hoop will lengthen by a small amount $dL$. For the force $F$ to overcome the force of urrugost $F_{yпр}$, it is necessary that $F_{yпр}dL < FdH$. According to Hooke's law, the strength of the elastic is $k(L-L_0)$, where $k$ is the coefficient of elasticity of the elastic band, and $L_0$ is its “investigated” length. So, in order for the model to form, it is necessary to skim it, fulfilling the conditions $|k(L-L_0)| < F\left|\frac{dH}{dL}\right|$. The mathematical quantities $\frac{dH}{dL}$ are the product of $H'(L)$. If you are not familiar with the understandable derivative, then it does not matter: the geometric quantities $\frac{dH}{dL}$ are the tangent of the angle of inclination (steepness) of the graph at the corresponding point. The rounder the graph and the weaker the tension of the hoop $k(L-L_0)$, the easier it is to compress the model. For example, we have already said that the tetrahedron and octahedron form more “capriciously”. This is due, in particular, to the fact that the graphs for them are larger and the total “stroke” of the rubber band is less.

Our physical model is highly idealized, since we neglected the thickness of objects, the resistance to bending, and, most importantly, we did not take into account the friction force. Train prevents both flattening and growth. It is quite difficult to accurately calculate the friction of the model's ribs against each other and the friction of the hoop against the ribs and faces.

It is precisely because of the friction that the model gets stuck halfway through the assembly. To reduce friction, you can cover the ribs of the model with as much tape as possible. If the elastic is too tight, you can prevent crumpled models by sewing the centers of the blanks with thread, but not too tightly.

Exercise 5. The assembled model was slightly flattened, the hands were removed, but the model did not fold. It’s easy to click on it and it folds. Why is this happening?

We hope that the reader will benefit from constructing the model, from independently deducing mathematical formulas, and from strictly justifying the physical conclusions. We suggest thinking about models for other polyhedra, not necessarily regular ones.

Note that there are other ways to obtain a variety of self-folding polyhedra using elastic bands, corners, frames, rods, etc. As a review article on these methods, we can recommend article [5].

One more observation. It is quite natural to treat a hoop on a polyhedron as a circle on a sphere. In particular, it is natural to denote by the letter $\pi$ the ratio of the length of the hoop to the height of the polyhedron, because for a sphere this will be real $\pi$.

Exercise 6. Calculate $\pi$ for tetrahedron, octahedron, quotient cube and dodecadron, and the arithmetic mean of these numbers.

Some numbers are noticeably smaller than the real $\pi$ (for those polyhedra whose height is the distance between the faces), others are larger. However, the arithmetic mean of the four worst $\pi$ is less than 0.001!

We will consider this coincidence as a guarantee that from the different types of geodesics we have chosen exactly the ones we need.

The vector flux is directionally independent of the radius of the sphere, and therefore of the shape of the surface within which $m$ is located. If we place arbitrarily located exact masses $m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_k$ inside a closed surface of any shape, then, in accordance with the principle of superposition, the total flux directed through this surface will be equal to

$$\Phi = -4\pi G \sum_{i=1}^{k} m_i.$$

This statement is called Gaus's law. If the Earth is considered a ball with spherical symmetrical mass distributions, then the gravitational potential of our planet is expressed as follows:

$$\varphi(r) = G \frac{M}{r},$$

where $r \geq R$, $R$ – radius of the Earth. Gravitational potential is a scalar quantity. According to the principle of superposition, the gravitational potential at a given point is equal to the algebraic sum of the gravitational potentials of all $n$ material points creating this field:

$$\varphi = G \left( \frac{m_1}{r_1} + \frac{m_2}{r_2} + \ldots + \frac{m_n}{r_n} \right) = G \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{m_i}{r_i}.$$

It is quite good to study the physical parameters of the gravitational field of the Earth, the Sun and other planets in the space of the solar system. However, there is very little scientific data characterizing the gravitational internal boundary characteristics of the Earth, which have been studied for more than 300 years. Based on theoretical and experimental geophysical studies, numerous models have been created that present the estimated values ​​of density, pressure, direction of the gravitational field (gravity acceleration) depending on the depth of the Earth (see table).

Let us consider a number of examples devoted to the analysis of the nature of the gravitational field of the inner Earth.

Example 6. Plot a graph of the Earth's gravitational field potential as a function of the distance to the center of the planet. The earth can be considered a homogeneous sphere.

Analysis. A body of mass $m$ is acted upon by a force $F(r) = -GmMr^3/R^3$ at $r < R$ (see example 4). This force is directed towards the center of the Earth. We use the correspondence $F = -\frac{dU}{dr}$, where $U$ is the potential energy of the body in the Earth’s gravitational field. Hence $U(r) = -\int Fdr + C$, or $U(r) = \int GmMrdr/R^3 + C$. The constant $C$ is found from the boundary condition $U(R) = -GmM/R$. After substitution we get $C = -3GmM/(2R)$, therefore,
$$U(r) = G \frac{mM}{R} \left( \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2 - \frac{3}{2} \right).$$
Since the potential of the gravitational field is numerically equal to the potential energy of a body of unit mass, we write the potential inside the Earth in this form:
$$\varphi(r) = G \frac{M}{R} \left( \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2 - \frac{3}{2} \right).$$
It is known that at $r \ge R$ the potential outside the Earth is equal to $\varphi(r) = -G \frac{M}{r}$. The dependences $\varphi(r)$ inside and outside the Earth are shown in figure 8. At the surface of the Earth, the potential of the gravitational field is $\varphi(R) = -G \frac{M}{R}$, and at the center of the Earth it is $\varphi(0) = -3G \frac{M}{2R}$.
