Let us now consider the random movement of atoms or molecules in a gas. On figure 2

Fig. 2. Motion of a selected Brownian particle

shows the trajectory of a certain particle, consisting of a large number of links $\vec{l}_{ij}$, where $\vec{l}_{ij}$ is a vector segment connecting two points of successive collisions, in which the direction of movement changes. Let after $n$ collisions (where $n$ is a very large number) the particle will move from the initial position to the point specified by the radius vector $\vec{r}_n$. Then

$$\vec{r}_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{l}_i.$$

Let's find the square of the particle's distance from the starting point:

$$\vec{r}_n^2 = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{l}_i \right)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{l}_i^2 + \sum_{i \neq j} \vec{l}_i \vec{l}_j.$$

Let us introduce the average length of a particle between two collisions:

$$l = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} l_i.$$

Taking this value into account, with a good approximation, the expression for the squared distance can be rewritten as

$$\vec{r}_n^2 = r_n^2 \approx nl^2 + l^2 \sum_{i,j} \cos \varphi_{ij},$$

where $\varphi_{ij}$ is the angle between vectors $\vec{l}_i$ and $\vec{l}_j$. The sum of the cosines of the angles between the vectors appears here. Taking into account the random nature of the movement of particles, $\sum_{i,j} \cos \varphi_{ij} = 0$, since the sum will equally likely contain angles of very different values. As a result we get

$$r_n^2 \approx nl^2.$$

The average length of a gas particle is related to the average time of free movement $\tau$ by the following relation:

$$\tau = \frac{1}{v},$$

where $v$ is the average thermal velocity of particles. Using the parameter $\tau$, we find the total number of collisions of the particle under consideration in time $t$:

$$n = \frac{t}{\tau} = \frac{vt}{l}.$$

Then

$$r_n^2 \approx nl^2 \approx vlt.$$

This expression can be written as

$$r_n^2 \approx Dt,$$

where $D = vl$ is the diffusion coefficient, characterizing the direction of movement of atoms or molecules in a gas due to chaotic thermal motion. The ≈ sign in the formulas indicates that the square of the radius vector for one particle is not exactly equal to the product $Dt$, but to an approximation. This is due to the random characteristics of the motion of a Brownian particle, when in some areas the particle (as shown in Figures 1 and 2) can move backwards or select. Rotations in the complex plane are given by multiplication by a complex number $t$, which according to the model is equal to 1. (For a detailed explanation of this fact, the reader is referred to article [1].) Then the vertices of the rotated polygon are expressed by the numbers $ta_1, \ldots, ta_n$, and the corresponding polynomial has the form

$$f_t(x) = \left( x - \frac{1}{2} \left( ta_1 + \frac{1}{ta_1} \right) \right) \cdot \ldots$$

$$\ldots \cdot \left( x - \frac{1}{2} \left( ta_n + \frac{1}{ta_n} \right) \right).$$

Theorem 3 is equivalent to the fact that all coefficients $f_t(x)$, except for the free term, do not depend on $t$. Let's prove it.

**Problem 3.** Prove that the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ in $f_t(x)$ is equal to zero for all $t$.

Consider the coefficient in $f_t(x)$ at $x^{n-k}$, where $0 < k < n$. He takes the form

$$\pm \frac{1}{2^k} \sum_{i_1 < \ldots < i_k} \left( ta_{i_1} + \frac{1}{ta_{i_1}} \right) \cdot \ldots \cdot \left( ta_{i_k} + \frac{1}{ta_{i_k}} \right).$$

Let's group this sum into powers $t$ (which vary from $-k$ to $k$) and consider the coefficient at $t^m$, $m \neq 0$. It will consist of the sum of all possible terms of the form

$$\frac{a_{i_1} \cdot \ldots \cdot a_{i_k}}{a_{i_{j+1}} \cdot \ldots \cdot a_{i_k}},$$

where $m = j - (k - j)$ and the index are chosen and divided into the set $i_1 < \ldots < i_j$ and $i_{j+1} < \ldots < i_k$ in all possible ways (the number of such ways is $C_n^k \cdot C_k^j$). Let us denote this coefficient by $S$. Generally speaking, the number $S$ is complex.

Let $\beta = e^{2\pi i/n}$ be the $n$th root of ones. Note the total of $S$ every $a_i$ by $a_i\beta$. Obviously, $S$ with such a substitution will turn into $S\beta^m$. But on the other hand, $a_1\beta = a_2, a_2\beta = a_3, \ldots, a_n\beta = a_1$, so $S$ will not change. Therefore, since $\beta^m \neq 1$, we have $S = 0$. So, the coefficients at $t^m$, $m \neq 0$ are equal to zero. This means that the coefficients for $x^{n-k}$, $0 < k < n$, do not depend on $t$. Q.E.D.

The reasoning we used with symmetric polynomials is fairly standard. Here's another, simpler example.

**Problem 4.** Let $a_1, \ldots, a_n \in \mathbb{C}$ be the vertices of a regular $n$-gon with center at zero. Prove that

a) $\sum_{i_1 < \ldots < i_k} a_{i_1} \cdot \ldots \cdot a_{i_k} = 0$ for any $k = 1, \ldots, n - 1$;

b) $\sum_{i \neq j} a_i^2 \cdot a_j = 0$ at $n > 3$.

**Chebyshev polynomials**

It turns out that there is a more explicit way to specify the polynomial $f_t(x)$, and this greatly simplifies the argument.1

Let us denote by $\tilde{t}$ the argument of the complex number $t$. By construction, $f_t(x)$ vanishes at $n$ (taking into account multiplicity) points of the form $x = \cos(\tilde{t} + 2\pi k/n)$ and only at them. We construct $f_t(x)$ from its roots, and they are all on the segment $[-1; 1]$, so we can put $x = \cos y$. Then the equality $f_t(\cos y) = 0$ is equivalent to the equality

$$\cos(y) = \cos(\tilde{t} + 2\pi k/n),$$

which holds exactly when the equality $\cos(ny) = \cos(n\tilde{t})$ is true. Thus, the functions $f_t(\cos y)$ have the same roots as the equations

$$\cos(ny) - \cos(n\tilde{t}) = 0.$$

**Theorem 4.** For each $n$ there is a real polynomial $T_n(x)$ such that

$$T_n(\cos y) = \cos ny.$$

The polynomials $T_n$ are called Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind. Their existence can be deduced from the Myabra formula:

$$(\cos y + i\sin y)^n = \cos(ny) + i\sin(ny).$$

Expanding the brackets on the left side by the binomial and dividing the expression into real and minimal parts, we obtain that $\cos(ny)$
