Prove that the lines PF, QE and AO intersect at one point.

The spelling should be $\angle FPB = \angle FMB$, which means $\angle FPE = \angle FMC$. On the other hand, $\angle FEP = \angle FCM$ (BCEF written), hence the similarity of $EPF \sim CMF$ and therefore $\frac{PE}{MC} = \frac{EF}{CF}$.

Likewise, $\frac{FQ}{BN} = \frac{EF}{BE}$. This means, since $MC = BN$, we have $\frac{PE}{BE} = \frac{QF}{CF}$ (i.e. points P and Q divide the heights BE and CF in the same ratio). Taking into account $\frac{BE}{CF} = \frac{AE}{AF}$ we get $\frac{PE}{FQ} = \frac{AE}{AF}$, therefore triangles AEP and AFQ are similar. We obtain $\angle FAQ = \angle EAP$, in other words, the lines AP and AQ are symmetrical with respect to the bicsectriciary angle BAC.

Now the solution can be completed using the isogonal theorem (see, for example, the article by A. Kulikov and D. Prokopenko “The isogonal theorem” in «Kvant» No. 4, 5 for 2018) for the four-sided EPFQ and the center A: pairs of straight lines AE and AF, AP and AQ are isogonal in the angle BAC (i.e. symmetric with respect to bicsectrics of angle BAC). This means that the lines AH and AG, where H = PE $\cap$ QF and G = PF $\cap$ QE, are also isogonal. But H is the orthocenter of triangle ABC, and, as is known (and easy to calculate in angles), lines AH and AO are isogonal with respect to angle BAC. It turns out that the lines AO and AG coincide, which completes the solution.

From the solution it is clear that the problem can be generalized: the bases of the heights E and F can be replaced by any points E and F on the sides AC and AB such that points B, C, E, F lie on the same circle, and point O by a point isogonally conjugate to the point PE $\cap$ QF.

Note also that the application of the isogonal theorem can be replaced by calculations, for example using the double ratio technique.

M2765*. These are 101 coins and two-share scales. In one weighing you can compare the weights of two coins. Let's call a subset of 51 coins of the same weight a large. What is the least amount of weighing that can be used to determine with harmony whether there is a majority among the given coins?

Answer: for 150 weighings.

Algorithm

Etman 1. At this stage we are looking for such a coin that if there is a majority, then it is in it. We will call such a coin enamel.

We will support the following situation: coins that have already participated in weighing are divided into groups. In each group, except perhaps one, exactly half of the coins have the same weight a, and the rest have weights different from a (nothing may be known about their mutual relationships). The remaining group (if there is one, let's call it artificial) is structured in the same way, but it contains more than half of the same coins. At first there are simply no groupies.

If there is no tempting group, compare two new coins. These coins form a new group: regular, if they are not equal, and tempting, if equal.

If there is a tempting group and most of the coins in it have weight a, compare one coin with weight a to a new coin. For any outcome, this coin is added to the group (which can rearrange more tempting ones).

The stage ends either when all the coins are in groups (from the parity messages, one of them must be exceptional), or when there is no tempting group, but one unweighted coin remains. Then we can consider that it forms a tempting group.

In total, at the end of the stage in each of the general groups there are no more than half of the coins of the same weight, and in the tempting group - more than half of the coins of a certain weight a. This means that these coins of weight a are enamel.
