Fig. 3. Galactic coordinates

The main one is the plane of the galactic disk (Fig. 3). This is a galactic coordinate system, and in it you can specify the position of a star using two angles:

• $l$ – galactic debt, the angle between the direction to the star and the direction to the center of the Galaxy (in projection onto the plane of the galactic disk);

• $b$ – galactic latitude, the angle between the direction towards the star and the plane of the galactic disk.

Formulas for the transition from the second equatorial coordinate system to the galactic coordinate system can be found on the Internet (https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gалактическая_systema_coordinат).

For the convenience of visual display of starship trajectories, all stars are projected onto the galactic plane using correspondence

$$r_{\pi} = r \cos b,$$

where $r = \frac{1}{\parallax} \cdot 3,26156$ is given in light years. The visualization of stars on figure is carried out using a Cartesian coordinate system:

$$x = r_{\pi} \cos l,$$

$$y = r_{\pi} \sin l.$$

The following calculations took into account the 10,000 stars closest to the Sun, which were contained in a sphere with a radius of about 100 light years. A starship that launched from Earth was used as a “random nearest particle.”
