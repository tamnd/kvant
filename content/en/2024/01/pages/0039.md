The vector flux is directionally independent of the radius of the sphere, and therefore of the shape of the surface within which $m$ is located. If we place arbitrarily located exact masses $m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_k$ inside a closed surface of any shape, then, in accordance with the principle of superposition, the total flux directed through this surface will be equal to

$$\Phi = -4\pi G \sum_{i=1}^{k} m_i.$$

This statement is called Gaus's law. If the Earth is considered a ball with spherical symmetrical mass distributions, then the gravitational potential of our planet is expressed as follows:

$$\varphi(r) = G \frac{M}{r},$$

where $r \geq R$, $R$ – radius of the Earth. Gravitational potential is a scalar quantity. According to the principle of superposition, the gravitational potential at a given point is equal to the algebraic sum of the gravitational potentials of all $n$ material points creating this field:

$$\varphi = G \left( \frac{m_1}{r_1} + \frac{m_2}{r_2} + \ldots + \frac{m_n}{r_n} \right) = G \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{m_i}{r_i}.$$

It is quite good to study the physical parameters of the gravitational field of the Earth, the Sun and other planets in the space of the solar system. However, there is very little scientific data characterizing the gravitational internal boundary characteristics of the Earth, which have been studied for more than 300 years. Based on theoretical and experimental geophysical studies, numerous models have been created that present the estimated values ​​of density, pressure, direction of the gravitational field (gravity acceleration) depending on the depth of the Earth (see table).

Let us consider a number of examples devoted to the analysis of the nature of the gravitational field of the inner Earth.

**Example 1.** Prove that the gravitational field that created a thin spherical layer of uniform density over the surface of the inner sphere surrounded by this layer is absent.

**Analysis.** Consider a spherical layer whose thickness is small. Let us place a point test mass $m$ (Fig. 2) at an arbitrary point of the inner sphere. Select on the surface
