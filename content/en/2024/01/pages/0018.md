Answer: $2^{n-1}$.
Let's number the rows from top to bottom and columns from left to right. All the hidden cells of the bottom row are located in one block adjacent to the right border. Indeed, cell $(n,n)$ (in the lower right corner) is definitely hidden. If there were such $i < j$ that cell $(n,i)$ is hidden, but cell $(n,j)$ is not (Fig. 1), then you can replace two hidden cells in columns $i$ and $j$ with two other cells in the same two rows and the same two columns, and the number of cells hidden in each line will not change (i.e. this kind of Sudoku is not suitable).

So, the path in the bottom line is hidden in cells $(n,j+1), (n,j+2), \dots, (n,n)$, but cells $(n,1), \dots, (n,j)$ are not. Then there are no riddle cells in rows $j+1, \dots, n-1$, otherwise such a riddle cell together with cell $(n,j+1)$ could be replaced by two other cells in the same two rows and the same two columns. Mentally cross out the last $n-j$ lines and get a Sudoku that satisfies the conditions for a Sudoku field with $j$ lines. Continuing further, we get a Sudoku that is uniquely hidden by cells on the main diagonal: if on the main diagonal cells $(j_1,j_1), (j_2,j_2), \dots, (j_k,j_k)$ are hidden for some sequences $0 = j_0 < j_1 < j_2 < \dots < j_k = n$, then all the hidden cells are located in lines $j_1,j_2, \dots, j_k$, and in line $j_t$ – in the last $(j_t - j_{t-1})$ cells (on figure 2) – example for $n = 8$, $\{j_1, \dots, j_k\} = \{2,4,5,8\}$). There are $2^{n-1}$ Sudokus found, since each diagonal cell, except $(n,n)$, may or may not be included in the set $\{j_1, \dots, j_k\}$.

On the contrary, each Sudoku of the above type is uniquely reconstructed by the number of hidden cells in the rows - this restoration is easily carried out row by row from top to bottom (discarding columns in which cells have already been found, from left to right).

M2767. It is easy to prove that in a right triangle, the sum of the radii of the inscribed and three outer circles is equal to the perimeter. Prove that the converse is also true.

We use standard notations for the elements of a triangle: $a, b, c$ – side lengths, $p$ – half-perimeter, $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ – angles, $R$ – radius of the circumscribed circle, $r, r_a, r_b, r_c$ – radius of the circumscribed and external circles, $I, I_a, I_b, I_c$ – their centers.

In any triangle

$$r_a + r_b = c \mathrm{ctg} \frac{\gamma}{2}.$$

This equality can be established directly by substituting the known formulas $r_a = \frac{S}{p-a}, r_b = \frac{S}{p-b}$ and performing the necessary trigonometric transformations. Geometrical equalities (1) are as follows. It is known that the midpoint $N$ of other ACBs of the circumscribed circle is the midpoint of the segment $I_a I_b$, which means that the distance from it to the line $AB$ is equal to the arithmetic mean of the distances from $I_a$ and $I_b$ to $AB$, i.e. equals $\frac{r_a + r_b}{2}$. On the other hand, from an equilateral triangle $ANB$ with angle $\angle ANB = \gamma$ this distance is $\frac{c}{2} \mathrm{ctg} \frac{\gamma}{2}$. Also true

$$r + r_c = (a + b) \mathrm{ctg} \frac{\gamma}{2}.$$

This can be proven in a simple way, using the fact that the midpoint of the segment $II_c$ is the midpoint of the other $AB$ circumcircles.
