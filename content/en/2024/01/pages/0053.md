2. A sealed vertical cylindrical vessel with a volume $V$ is divided by a thin weightless heat-conducting thermetic piston (a disk coaxial with the vessel) into two equal parts. The piston can stir without friction. At the top of the cylinder there is nitrogen, and at the bottom there is water and carbon dioxide. At the initial moment, the system was in equilibrium at room temperature $T_0$. In this case, the liquid occupied volume $V/4$. Then the cylinder was slowly heated to $T = 4T_0/3 = 373$ K. The steady-state volume of its upper part became equal to $V/6$. According to Henry's law, at a given temperature the amount $\Delta v$ of dissolved gas in a volume of liquid $w$ is proportional to the partial pressure $p$ of the gas: $\Delta v = kpw$. The volume of liquid remains practically unchanged. For carbon dioxide, Henry's CONSTANTA for a given room temperature is $k \approx 0.6 \cdot 10^{-3}$ mol/$\left(m^3 \cdot \Pi a\right)$. At the final temperature $T$ carbon dioxide practically does not dissolve in water. It can be assumed that $RT \approx 3 \cdot 10^3$ J/mol, where $R$ is the universal gas constant. Neglect the vapor pressure of water at room temperature and the change in liquid volume during heating. All gases are considered ideal.
