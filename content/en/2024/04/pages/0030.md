Us and models

PHYSICS ELECTIVE

L.ASHKINAZI

There are so many problems related to physics that I want to classify them. For example, you can divide them into “physics problems” and “physical problems”. The first are those problems that are in the problem book, offered in an exam or at an Olympiad. The second are those tasks that are dealt with, so to speak, by a “practicing physicist.” This division is not entirely strict. Thus, there are two problem books - by Jirl Walker and Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa, in which a significant part of the problems deserved the attention of physicists, including the authors of the problem books. Another thing is that for them these tasks were not, so to speak, Serious Scientific Problems, but rather pleasant entertainment. But if we take a closer look at them, we will see in these tasks an important feature that unites them with those very serious ones. You will meet them when you enter this great game - Physics. To the first level. This feature is not the obviousness of the model. When solving any problem, we take some things into account and some we don’t. School tasks are such that we always know what exactly we can take into account, since in addition to the problem book there is a textbook and everything is written there. In serious physics this is not the case; we are not limited by anything - except our current knowledge. However, there are traditions, and when considering, for example, the movement of the Earth or the Moon, we do not take into account the pressure of sunlight, and we do not even justify this. But with smaller object sizes, this pressure will have to be taken into account - although it has become smaller. (By the way, what size do you think? Try to do the math.) The simplifications we use can be divided into two groups. The former are more common in school problems; they radically simplify problem solving, and not

Examples are well known - neglect of air resistance when a body moves in the atmosphere, constancy of specific resistance, independence of friction from pressure and speed, equality of maximum static friction and sliding friction, ideality of gases, rigidity of supports, inextensibility and weightlessness of ropes, and so on. You yourself know all these examples, but after going through the problems you solved in your memory, remember others. Some of the “school” simplifications can be overcome quite easily. For example, if the maximum static friction and sliding friction are not equal, there is nothing complicated. Sometimes it is possible to consider a particular case, sometimes it is possible to build a computer model. Such work with simplifications is useful for a small but fundamentally important expansion of horizons. Among school simplifications there are those that, in principle, violate the logical coherence of the physics course.
