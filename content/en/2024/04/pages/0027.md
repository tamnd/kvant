It is difficult to find any details about the life of Maurice Krajcik. Apparently, Krajcik was a good chess player - in the 1930s, at the Brussels Chess Club, he gave sessions of simultaneous blindfold play on 15 boards. And one of his games, which ended in a quick victory, was published in the chess section of the Minsk Leaflet in 1902: 1. f4 e5 2. fe d6 3. f3 de 4. e5 d6 5. f3 h6 6. h3?? g3×. In addition, Krajcik gave performances where he surprised everyone with his mental arithmetic abilities.
Among his personal mathematical achievements, we can mention the Krajcik–Fermat factorization method, later improved by Karl Pomeratz and called the quadratic sieve. But even if Krajcik wrote only the book “Mathematical Entertainment”, this would already be enough for the grateful memory of posterity. The first (Brussels) edition of this book in 1930 contained 566 pages! Fortunately, he did and left behind much more. For example, in 1935 and 1937, it was he who organized international congresses on entertaining mathematics, timed to coincide with the World Exhibitions (in 1935 in Brussels, in 1937 in Paris) and published collections of reports and speeches from these congresses. The most interesting and mysterious (in every sense) of Krajcik’s children is the Sphinx magazine. Despite its uniqueness in history (how many magazines do you still know that are entirely dedicated to interesting mathematics?), there is still not a single scanned issue of the hundreds published on the Internet (1931–1939, 12 magazines per year). It was in this publication that the name `cryptarithm` first appeared for mathematical puzzles in which numbers are replaced by letters, and such puzzles began to be published regularly. The first problems with a tangram were also published there - a puzzle in which seven simple figures are asked to put together a variety of figure. All sorts of chess-related problems, problems about mathematical squares, transfusions and crossings, and much more that now seems to be an absolutely classic part of the problem in mathematical circles - all this was also pulled out of oblivion by the tireless Krajcik.
