F2787. Points A, B, C are located on the symmetry axis of a uniformly charged hemisphere (see figure): point A is larger than the “bottom” of the hemisphere, point B is at its center, point C is at a distance of the radius of the hemisphere from point B. From point A we start a charged particle with zero initial speed. The signs of all charges are the same. At point B the particle moves with speed $v_B$. Let's find the speed of $v_C$ particles at point C.

Let us prove that when moving from point A to point B and then from B to C, the potential is the same (to determine this, we assume that all charges are positive).

First way. The zero field at any point located in a uniformly charged sphere can be represented as a superposition of the fields of two hemispheres (we cut the sphere by the plane of a great circle perpendicular to the diameter on which this point lies). The resulting two hemispheres create directional vectors of equal magnitude, the sum of which is equal to zero at all points of an arbitrary diameter of the sphere. This means that the directionality $E$ of the hemisphere field is the same at any two points lying on the axis of symmetry, each of which is located at any distance $d < R$ from the center of the hemisphere. Hence the hemispheres $\varphi_A - \varphi_B = \varphi_B - \varphi_C$.

Second way. If you add a hemisphere to a sphere, then, according to the principle of superposition, the potential at the center of the sphere will double and be equal to $2\varphi_B$. The field inside the sphere is zero, therefore, the potential at point A will also be equal to $2\varphi_B$. This potential will be the sum of the potential $\varphi_A$ created at this point by the left hemisphere and the potential created by the right (added) hemisphere. The contribution of the right hemisphere, due to symmetry, is equal to the potential created by the left hemisphere at point C, i.e. $\varphi_C$. Thus, we obtain the correspondence $2\varphi_B = \varphi_A + \varphi_C$, hence $\varphi_A - \varphi_B = \varphi_B - \varphi_C$. Further, according to the theorem on the change in the kinetic energy of a material point, we have

$$\frac{mv_B^2}{2} = q(\varphi_A - \varphi_B),$$

$$\frac{mv_C^2}{2} = q(\varphi_A - \varphi_B + \varphi_B - \varphi_C).$$

It follows

$$v_C = \sqrt{2}v_B.$$

Note. Readers are recommended to study an example of solving an electrostatics problem by constructing a superposition of the fields of a sphere and hemispheres. Such a problem was published in the journal «Kvant» No. 6 in 2000 (problem F1762, its author is G. Grigoryan).

F2788. At sunset or sunrise, the visible shape of the full Moon differs little (almost noticeably) from the circle when observed from the surface of the Earth. But when shooting the same event from the board of the Luna space station, we take on the shape of a “bun”, indicating that the radius of curvature in its upper (above the Earth) part is much less than the radius of curvature in the lower (facing the Earth) side (see figure). How can this be explained?

The speed of light in a medium (in particular, air) is less than the speed of light in a vacuum. Therefore, when light passes through a boundary section with different speeds of light propagation, a change in the direction of propagation of the beam of light occurs in them (the beam is refracted if the boundary is sharp).
