In this case, the movement of light in the atmosphere changes the properties of the medium not sharply, but smoothly. Along with the change in air density as it approaches the Earth's surface, the vertical direction in which the density changes most rapidly also changes (i.e., the verticals at different places in the path of the light ray are not parallel lines). If, for example, we assume that the thickness of the atmosphere on Earth is approximately 32 km (at this altitude, the air density is approximately 1% of the air density near the Earth's surface at sea level), then with the Earth's radius being approximately 6400 km, the path length of light incident tangentially to the Earth's surface from the point of entry into the atmosphere to the Earth's surface is (without taking into account refraction) approximately 640 km. In this case, the verticals for the place of entry into the atmosphere and at the place where the light beam touches the surface of the Earth make up a very small angle - about 0.1 rad, or about 5.7q. When taking into account the phenomenon of refraction, the path length will be even greater, and the verticals in question will form an even larger angle. If the Earth were flat and there was an atmosphere above it with the same density near the surface as that of the real Earth, then in this case we would see luminous objects located at a solid angle equal to 2S. In this case, it would seem to us that the angle at which they are visible to us is less than 2S, i.e. the light of cosmic objects would come to us (located on the surface of the Earth) in directions that form an angle with the horizon no less than the angle of refraction. Let us estimate the magnitude of such an angle for a flat Earth. The refractive index of light in air under normal conditions is approximately n 1.00028. According to the law of refraction, the ratio of the sines of the angles of incidence and refraction is equal to the ratio of the speeds of light in the corresponding media. It follows that the minimum angle with the horizon, which is formed by rays of light coming from objects outside the atmosphere, for a flat Earth will be equal to arccos 1 n | 1.35 q 81 arcmin.
