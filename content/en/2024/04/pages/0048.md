24. At 49.
Each angle of a polygon has values ​​of either 90° - a of them are empty, or 270° - we will call them large and there are b of them empty, and a + b = 100. Let us write down the property of the sum of the angles of a polygon:

$$90^\circ a + 270^\circ b = 180^\circ (100 - 2);$$
$$(180^\circ - 90^\circ)a + (180^\circ + 90^\circ)b = 180^\circ (100 - 2);$$
$$(-90^\circ)a + (90^\circ)b = 180^\circ (-2);$$
$a - b = 4.$

Since a + b = 100, we find that there are only b = 48 large angles. Let's take any large angle. Let's extend one of its sides inside until it intersects again with the border of the polygon. Thus we get a cut that divides this large angle into two angles of 180° and 90°. In this case, the polygon was divided into two polygons, in which any two adjacent sides are perpedicular and the total number of large angles in them is at least one less than in the polygon before the cut. By poset, repeating this procedure as long as possible (no more than 48 times), we will obtain polygons with peredicular sides without large angles, i.e. rectilinear. Since at first there was one polygon and it was divided into two parts no more than 48 times, the result was no more than 49 rectangles.

Now we will show that there are polygons that cannot be cut into fewer than 49 rectangles. Let's consider a polygon in the form of a “staircase” (at figure 2 an example of such a 10-gon is shown; a 100-gon is constructed in a similar way). It will have 49 “steps” - horizontal sides, except for the bottom (at figure the “steps” are highlighted in red). No two of them can be applied to one rectangle, so at least 49 rectangles will be required.
