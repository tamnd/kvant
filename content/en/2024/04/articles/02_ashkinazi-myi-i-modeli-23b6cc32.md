Us and models

PHYSICS ELECTIVE

L.ASHKINAZI

There are so many problems related to physics that I want to classify them. For example, you can divide them into “physics problems” and “physical problems”. The first are those problems that are in the problem book, offered in an exam or at an Olympiad. The second are those tasks that are dealt with, so to speak, by a “practicing physicist.” This division is not entirely strict. Thus, there are two problem books - by Jirl Walker and Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa, in which a significant part of the problems deserved the attention of physicists, including the authors of the problem books. Another thing is that for them these tasks were not, so to speak, Serious Scientific Problems, but rather pleasant entertainment. But if we take a closer look at them, we will see in these tasks an important feature that unites them with those very serious ones. You will meet them when you enter this great game - Physics. To the first level. This feature is not the obviousness of the model. When solving any problem, we take some things into account and some we don’t. School tasks are such that we always know what exactly we can take into account, since in addition to the problem book there is a textbook and everything is written there. In serious physics this is not the case; we are not limited by anything - except our current knowledge. However, there are traditions, and when considering, for example, the movement of the Earth or the Moon, we do not take into account the pressure of sunlight, and we do not even justify this. But with smaller object sizes, this pressure will have to be taken into account - although it has become smaller. (By the way, what size do you think? Try to do the math.) Simplifications of the second group, “adult” ones, sometimes turn out to be quite serious problems, but even considering their formulation can be useful and interesting. Here are some examples with brief comments. Why do we apply the gravitational force mg at the center of gravity, i.e. at the point? Firstly, points exist only in mathematics, they do not exist in physics. Secondly, when a finite force is applied to a small area, large mechanical stresses arise. For example, when mg is applied to the center of gravity of a person, and not even to a point, but to an area, say, at $1 мм^{2}$, the mechanical stresses will be greater than the tensile strength. And this “point” will cut through your body and whistle towards the center of the Earth. And your torso, having lost weight, will stop pressing on the floor, the floor and your legs will unclench, and you will slowly fly upward. (By the way, this is a simple task - at what speed will you fly?) Of course, the reaction of the support, the friction force, and the capillary forces are not applied to the point.
