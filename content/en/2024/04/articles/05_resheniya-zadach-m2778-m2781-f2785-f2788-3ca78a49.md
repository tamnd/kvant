M2779. Prove that for every natural $k$ there is a natural $n$ such that

$$\text{НОК}(1,2,3,\dots,n) = \text{НОК}(1,2,3,\dots,n,n+1,\dots,n+k).$$

If from the series of the first $n (n \geq 2)$ natural numbers 1, 2, ..., $n$ we leave only powers of prime numbers (including the first power, i.e. the prime numbers themselves), and cross out the remaining numbers, then the LCM of the numbers remains the same, i.e. will be equal to LCM(1,2,3,...,n). Indeed, if we cross out the number $n = p_1^{\alpha_1} p_2^{\alpha_2} \dots p_k^{\alpha_k}$, where $k \geq 2$ (and $p_i$ are various primes included in the expansion with exponents $\alpha_i \geq 1$), then the LCM of the remaining numbers will be divisible by $n$, since the number $p_1^{\alpha_1}, p_2^{\alpha_2}, \dots, p_k^{\alpha_k}$ is in the remainder series.

This means that the equality

$$\text{НОК}(1,2,3,\dots,n) = \text{НОК}(1,2,3,\dots,n,n+1,\dots,n+k),$$

It is sufficient (and in fact necessary) that the numbers $n+1, n+2, \dots, n+k$ have no powers of prime numbers (including the first power).

This can be achieved as follows. Take $2k$ distinct primes $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_{2k}$. According to the Chinese remainder theorem, one can choose a natural number $n$ that simultaneously satisfies the following conditions: $n \equiv -1 (\bmod p_1 p_{k+1})$, $n \equiv -2 (\bmod p_2 p_{k+2})$, ..., $n \equiv -k (\bmod p_k p_{2k})$.

Then $n+i$ is divisible by $p_i p_{i+k}$ for every $i=1, 2, \dots, k$ and is therefore not a power of a prime number. This completes the solution to the problem.

Note that the same reasoning can be used to prove a stronger statement about the existence of $n$, for which none of the numbers $n+1, n+2, \dots, n+k$ is a prime number or an exact power (greater than 1).

In conclusion, we will say that the function $f(n) = \text{НОK}(1,2,3,\dots,n)$ plays an important role in number theory. It is clear that $f(n+1)$ is divisible by $f(n)$, and it is easy to establish that the part $\frac{f(n+1)}{f(n)}$ can only equal 1 or a prime number. The function $g(n) = \ln f(n)$ is known as the Chebyshev function. It is known to have linear growth, so $g(n) = n(1 + \alpha(n))$, where $\lim_{n \to \infty} \alpha(n) = 0$.

M2780. The chromatic number of a graph is the minimum number of vertices such that any two vertices connected by an edge are covered in different colors. Given a natural number $n \geq 3$ and a graph $G$ with a chromatic number $\chi(G) = n$, which has more than $n$ vertices. Prove that in $G$ there are two vertex-disjoint subgraphs $G_1$ and $G_2$ such that $\chi(G_1) + \chi(G_2) \geq n+1$.

A clique is a set of vertices in which any pair of vertices is connected by an edge. Let us choose the maximal clique $K$ in $G$, i.e. a clique consisting of the maximum number of vertices, and its vertices $A_1, \dots, A_k$ are empty. It is clear that $\chi(K) = k$. It follows from the condition that $2 \leq k \leq n$ and that $K$ do not coincide with $G$.

The rigid structure, indicated in figure by the values ​​of the powerful masses at the ends and the length of the sections of the imperfect rods, is held in place without movement, and the adopted length of the imperfect and inextensible rope is attached at the upper end to the grinding and we occupy a vertical position. The structure is released without giving it movement. With what accelerations will the loads move immediately after release?
Let us introduce a coordinate system with the cutoff origin at the point of attachment of the rope to the junction of the horizontal and vertical rods. Let one of the horizontal axes $x$ be directed along the horizontal rod from the first load to the second, the second horizontal axis $z$ is perpendicular to the horizontal rod, and the axis $y$ is directed downwards along the vertical rod. Along the $z$ axis, the weights and points of the rods do not move at all. And since it is indicated that the rope is long and at the initial moment is located vertically, this means that the load $M_3$ (low) and the places where the rope is attached to the rods at the initial moment can only move along the horizontal axis $x$. At the initial moment, all external forces acting on the system (gravity forces and the tension force of the rope) are directed vertically, which means that the center of mass of the motion system at the initial moment is in the vertical direction. As a result, we can assume that for the initial moment of motion there is a stationary center of rotation of the system, relative to which it begins to rotate with some angular acceleration, let’s denote it $\beta$. This instantaneous center of rotation is located on a vertical rod below the attachment point at a distance from the beginning of the cutoff equal to the vertical coordinate of the center of mass of the system
$$y_{\text{цм}} = \frac{M_3 L_3}{M_1 + M_2 + M_3}.$$
Relative to this instantaneous center of rotation, the center of mass of the system is at the same horizontal level with coordinate $x$ equal to
$$x_{\text{цм}} = \frac{-L_1 M_1 + L_2 M_2}{M_1 + M_2 + M_3}.$$
The moment of all external forces relative to the instantaneous center of rotation is equal to
$$\left( M_1 + M_2 + M_3 \right) g x_{\text{цм}} = \left( -L_1 M_1 + L_2 M_2 \right) g.$$
The moment of inertia of all system loads relative to the instantaneous center of rotation is equal to
$$J = M_1 \left( L_1^2 + y_{\text{цм}}^2 \right) + M_2 \left( L_2^2 + y_{\text{цм}}^2 \right) + M_3 \left( L_3 - y_{\text{цм}} \right)^2.$$

$$\beta = \frac{\left( -L_1 M_1 + L_2 M_2 \right) g}{J}.$$
The linear acceleration of each of the loads is equal in magnitude to the product of the angular acceleration $\beta$ and the distance $s$ from this load to the instantaneous center of rotation and is directed perpendicular to the segment connecting the instantaneous center of rotation with this load. F2787. Points A, B, C are located on the symmetry axis of a uniformly charged hemisphere (see figure): point A is larger than the “bottom” of the hemisphere, point B is at its center, point C is at a distance of the radius of the hemisphere from point B. From point A we start a charged particle with zero initial speed. The signs of all charges are the same. At point B the particle moves with speed $v_B$. Let's find the speed of $v_C$ particles at point C.

Let us prove that when moving from point A to point B and then from B to C, the potential is the same (to determine this, we assume that all charges are positive).

First way. The zero field at any point located in a uniformly charged sphere can be represented as a superposition of the fields of two hemispheres (we cut the sphere by the plane of a great circle perpendicular to the diameter on which this point lies). The resulting two hemispheres create directional vectors of equal magnitude, the sum of which is equal to zero at all points of an arbitrary diameter of the sphere. This means that the directionality $E$ of the hemisphere field is the same at any two points lying on the axis of symmetry, each of which is located at any distance $d < R$ from the center of the hemisphere. Hence the hemispheres $\varphi_A - \varphi_B = \varphi_B - \varphi_C$.

Second way. If you add a hemisphere to a sphere, then, according to the principle of superposition, the potential at the center of the sphere will double and be equal to $2\varphi_B$. The field inside the sphere is zero, therefore, the potential at point A will also be equal to $2\varphi_B$. This potential will be the sum of the potential $\varphi_A$ created at this point by the left hemisphere and the potential created by the right (added) hemisphere. The contribution of the right hemisphere, due to symmetry, is equal to the potential created by the left hemisphere at point C, i.e. $\varphi_C$. Thus, we obtain the correspondence $2\varphi_B = \varphi_A + \varphi_C$, hence $\varphi_A - \varphi_B = \varphi_B - \varphi_C$. Further, according to the theorem on the change in the kinetic energy of a material point, we have

$$\frac{mv_B^2}{2} = q(\varphi_A - \varphi_B),$$

$$\frac{mv_C^2}{2} = q(\varphi_A - \varphi_B + \varphi_B - \varphi_C).$$

It follows

$$v_C = \sqrt{2}v_B.$$

Note. Readers are recommended to study an example of solving an electrostatics problem by constructing a superposition of the fields of a sphere and hemispheres. Such a problem was published in the journal «Kvant» No. 6 in 2000 (problem F1762, its author is G. Grigoryan).
