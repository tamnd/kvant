MAURICE KRAJCZIK (21 APRIL 1882 – 19 August 1957) is generally considered a Belgian mathematician and popularizer of science. He was born in Minsk to a Jewish family (father - Boris Kraichik, mother - Sonya Kaufman). After graduating from school, he went to study mathematics in Belgium, where he entered the University of Lvezh. In 1923, he completed his dissertation on number theory. In 1922–1930 and after emigration, he wrote several books on number theory, and from 1931 to 1939 he published the journal Sphinx, dedicated to his mathematics. During World War II, he immigrated to the United States, where he taught a course on "Entertaining Mathematics" at the New School for Social Research in New York. After the war he returned to Europe. It is difficult to find any details about the life of Maurice Krajcik. Apparently, Krajcik was a good chess player - in the 1930s, at the Brussels Chess Club, he gave sessions of simultaneous blindfold play on 15 boards. And one of his games, which ended in a quick victory, was published in the chess section of the Minsk Leaflet in 1902: 1. f4 e5 2. fe d6 3. f3 de 4. e5 d6 5. f3 h6 6. h3?? g3×. In addition, Krajcik gave performances where he surprised everyone with his mental arithmetic abilities.
Among his personal mathematical achievements, we can mention the Krajcik–Fermat factorization method, later improved by Karl Pomeratz and called the quadratic sieve. But even if Krajcik wrote only the book “Mathematical Entertainment”, this would already be enough for the grateful memory of posterity. The first (Brussels) edition of this book in 1930 contained 566 pages! Fortunately, he did and left behind much more. For example, in 1935 and 1937, it was he who organized international congresses on entertaining mathematics, timed to coincide with the World Exhibitions (in 1935 in Brussels, in 1937 in Paris) and published collections of reports and speeches from these congresses. The most interesting and mysterious (in every sense) of Krajcik’s children is the Sphinx magazine. Despite its uniqueness in history (how many magazines do you still know that are entirely dedicated to interesting mathematics?), there is still not a single scanned issue of the hundreds published on the Internet (1931–1939, 12 magazines per year). It was in this publication that the name `cryptarithm` first appeared for mathematical puzzles in which numbers are replaced by letters, and such puzzles began to be published regularly. The first problems with a tangram were also published there - a puzzle in which seven simple figures are asked to put together a variety of figure. All sorts of chess-related problems, problems about mathematical squares, transfusions and crossings, and much more that now seems to be an absolutely classic part of the problem in mathematical circles - all this was also pulled out of oblivion by the tireless Krajcik. In probability theory, Krajcik’s “two envelope paradox” became famous. Here it is as formulated later by Martin Gardner:
Two people, equally rich, meet with a comparable amount in their wallets. Each does not know the contents of the other’s wallet (if the amounts are equal, nothing happens). Then each of the two can reason like this: “Let me have an amount S in my wallet. If I lose, it can be S. And if I win (with

2. Eight little spiderlings are on one of the vertical walls of the room, 80 inches above the floor point, which is exactly in the middle of this wall. The fly sits on the opposite wall of the room exactly 80 inches below the ceiling point, which is also in the middle of the wall. At the signal, all the spiderlings simultaneously begin to crawl towards their husband along eight different routes and reach her at the same time - each crawled τον 650 inches. Find the dimensions of the room.

3. The winemaker has prepared 45 barrels of wine, which he wants to divide among his five nephews so that each gets 9 barrels and an equal share of wine. Among these 45 barrels there are 9 empty, 9 quarter full, 9 half full, 9 three quarter full and 9 full. In addition, he wants everyone to get at least one barrel of each linen and that no two get the same set of barrels. How can he cope with the division? (Krajcik provides a complete solution to the problem, and does not just find one suitable option!)

4. Peter, Paul and their sons Tom and Richard bought books. When they left the store, it turned out that everyone had bought so many books, how much each one they bought cost, and each family spent the same amount on the purchase, less than 100 dollars. Peter bought 5 more books than Tom. Who is Richard's father?

5. Find the smallest natural number of the form 12n – 1, not divisible by 5, which is not prime. The same question for a number of the form 12n – 11.
