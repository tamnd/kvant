Grade. Consider apples of diameter $d$, where $2 \leq d \leq N$. Let such apples not be given to just two boa constrictors. The mouths of these boas can be $d - 1$ and $d$. Both boas will eat all the changing apples and leave all the big ones, so these boas cannot be distinguished. This means that for each $d$ from 2 to $N$ inclusive, apples of diameter $d$ require at least $N - 1$, and then in total at least $(N - 1)^2$ apples are needed.
Example. Let's give each boa constrictor, except the last one, apples of all diameters from 2 to $N$ inclusive. Having received such a set, deleting the size of your mouth: it is equal to the maximum diameter of the reduced apple or 1, if not a single apple is eaten. The size of the mouth of the last boa constrictor is determined by the method of exclusion.
Comment. It turns out that the same set of apples can be divided into boa constrictors like a charcoal, with only one condition: do not give the same apples to the same boa constrictor.
In fact, if there is a boa constrictor that has eaten an apple of diameter $N$, then its mouth will be of size $N$. Otherwise, a boa constrictor that has not received such an apple will have such a paste. Among the rest of the boas, using an apple of diameter $N - 1$, we will similarly find a mouth of size $N - 1$ and so on. The remaining boa constrictor at the end will have a size 1 mouth.

First way. The number $N$ is decomposable into the sum of various powers of two (binary representation). There are three possible cases.
1) This expansion does not have two terms (one or at least three). Then we first break it down exactly as in this double expansion, and then we turn each power of two into units.
2) $N = 2^a + 2^b$, where $0 < a < b$. Then $b \ge 3$, since $N \ge 10$. We break $N$ into pieces 1, $2^a$ and $2^b - 1$, the last of which into $b$ powers of two $(1, 2, \dots, 2^{b-1})$.
3) $N = 1 + 2^b$. Then $b \ge 4$, and we break $N$ into pieces 1, 2 and $2^b - 2$, the latter of which into $b - 1$ powers of two $(2, \dots, 2^{b-1})$.
