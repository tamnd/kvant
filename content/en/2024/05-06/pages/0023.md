Let us further assume that if $I$ and $A$ satisfy the listed conditions, then $I$ is the center of the incircle of one of the possible triangles $ABC$. Indeed, since $\angle EAI$ is acute, then $\angle AID = \angle EID - \angle EIA < (90^\circ + a) - 90^\circ = a = \angle EAI$. The resulting inequality means that the lines $AE$ and $DI$ intersect at some point (let’s call it $B$) belonging to the rays $AE$ and $DI$. Similarly, we obtain the point $C$ of intersection of the rays $AD$ and $EI$. Let $I'$ be the center of the inscribed circle of triangle $ABC$. then $AI'$ is the internal bissertis of the angle $BAC$, i.e. angle $DAE$, which means straight line $AI'$ coincides with $AI$. Also, $\angle BIC = \angle BI'C = 90^\circ + a$. But there is exactly one point with this property, lying on the bissertis of the angle $BAC$ inside the triangle $ABC$ (the intersection of the other $BIC$s with the bissertis). So, $I = I'$.

Now we fix the values ​​of the angle $a$, then $A$ and $I$ will be on fixed arcs $DAE$ and $DIE$; Let's call these arcs $\gamma$ and $\omega$. Let's start with an interesting special case $a = 30^\circ$. In this case, arcs $\gamma$ and $\omega$ together form one circle, and since $AI$ must pass through the midpoint $M$ of arc $\omega$, $I$ must coincide with $M$ (note that this case is very/no possible, for example when $A$ lies on the midperpendicular to $DE$).

Next, consider the case $a < 30^\circ$ (Fig. 2). In this case, the arc $\omega$ is outside the circle ($AED$). We know that from the point $I$ of the arc $\omega$ the point $A$ is uniquely reconstructed as the point of intersection of $IM$ with the arc $\gamma$. Let's find out which points $I$ of the arc $\omega$ are suitable for us, i.e. for which points $I$ arcs $\omega$ angles $DIM$ and $EIM$ acute. Let $S$ be the center of the circle ($EID$). Since $\angle EID = 90^\circ + a$, then $\angle ESD = 180^\circ - 2a$, which is equal to the angle $EMD$. We see that $S$ and $M$ are symmetrical with respect to $ED$, in other words, $DMES$ is a rhombus, the center of this rhombus is the middle $N$ of the segment $ED$. Let $KD$ be the diameter of the circle ($EID$), so that $S$ is the middle of $DK$ and $KE \perp ED$. Then $KEMS$ is a parallelogram (sides $EM$ and $KS$ are parallel and equal), and $KEMN$ is a trapezoid with bases $KE$ and $MN$ such that $KE : MN = 2$. Then $KM$ intersects the segment $EN$ at the point $P$ such that $EP : PN = 2$ (note that $P$ does not depend on the choice of angle $a$). Let ray $KM$ intersect arc $\omega$ at point $Q$. Since $KD$ is the diameter of circle $\omega$, we have $\angle PQD = 90^\circ$, in other words, $Q$ and $D$ are the points of intersection of the arc $\omega$ with the circle $\delta$, constructed on the segment $PD$ as a diameter. If $I$ lies inside $\delta$, then $\angle DIA = \angle DIM \ge 90^\circ$, which means that such points $I$ are not suitable for us. Similarly, we construct a circle $\delta'$, symmetrical to δ relative to the perpendicular bisector to $DE$. Then the points $I$ lying inside $\delta'$ are also not suitable for us. If the point $I$ lies on the arc $\omega$ strictly between the points $Q$ and $Q'$, where $Q'$ is the second intersection point of $\delta'$ and $\omega$ (Fig. 3), then $I$ is strictly outside the circles $\delta$ and $\delta'$, which means that both angles $DIM$ and $EIM$ are acute, and also points $I$ are suitable for us.

Now lets the angle $a$ change in the interval $(0^\circ; 30^\circ)$ and we get the set of points $I$: all points lying inside the circle $\Delta$ and outside $\delta$ and $\delta'$.
