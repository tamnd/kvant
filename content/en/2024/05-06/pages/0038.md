But as you move away from the Earth's surface, the air temperature decreases. Let us assume that the temperature decrease linearly depends on the radial distance, i.e. on height, and the dependence of temperature on height has the form
$$T - \Delta Th,$$
where $\Delta T$ is the change in temperature per unit length. Then the expression for $h$ will take the form
$$h = \ln \delta\rho \cdot \frac{k(T - \Delta Th)}{mg}$$

and the result of solving this equation for $h$ can be written in the form
$$h = \frac{\ln \delta\rho \cdot kT}{mg + \ln \delta\rho \cdot k\Delta T}.$$

Let's calculate the height at which the ball's movement will stop to realize earthly conditions. The linear dependence of temperature on height (radial distance) is maintained until the upper boundary of the troposphere, i.e. at least up to 10 km (from the Earth's pole). Consequently, if, as a result of the calculation for the takeoff of the ball from the pole, the value $h$ turns out to be greater than 10 km, it cannot be considered correct. First, let's find out what is the ratio of the acceleration of free fall at this height to the acceleration $g$ on the Earth's surface. The corresponding formula is
$$\delta g = \left(\frac{R}{R + h}\right)^2.$$
After substituting the numerical values ​​($R = 6357$ km, $h = 10$ km) we obtain
$$\delta g = 0,997.$$
Thus, the free fall acceleration for our conditions can be left equal to $g$. Now you can do the calculation. Let us accept (for convenience) $\delta\rho = e$, $k = 1,38 \cdot 10^{-23}$ J/K, $T = 273$ K (average temperature for the North Pole), $m = 4,82 \cdot 10^{-26}$ kg, $g = 9,82$ m/s², $\Delta T = 6,5 \cdot 10^{-3}$ K/m. As a result we get
$$h = 6692$ м ≈ 6,7 км.$$
This result did not go beyond the linear dependence of the temperature decrease on altitude, so it can be considered correct.

Let us follow the inverse problem: we determine at what $\delta\rho$ the ball at the North Pole rises to the upper boundary of the troposphere, i.e. up to a height of $h = 10$ km. From the formula
$$h = \ln \delta\rho \cdot \frac{k(T - \Delta Th)}{mg}$$

we get
$$\ln \delta\rho = \frac{hmg}{k(T - \Delta Th)},$$
where
$$\delta\rho = e^{\frac{hmg}{k(T - \Delta Th)}} = 5,2.$$
