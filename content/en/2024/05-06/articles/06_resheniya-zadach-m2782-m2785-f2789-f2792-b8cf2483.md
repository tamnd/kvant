According to the conditions of the problem, in the graph (cities - vertices, airlines - edges) between any two vertices there is a path of length no more than $n$. It is required to color the edges in $n$ colors (the color means belonging to the corresponding airline) so that for any two vertices between them there is a path in which there are no more than two edges of each color.
Let's take one of the vertices $A$ and “hang” the graph by this vertex, i.e. Let's carry out the following process. Let $A_0$ be a set of one vertex $A$. Let $A_1$ be the set of vertices from which one can, by walking along one edge, get to a vertex from $A_0$. And so on: let at the next step we have already defined the set of vertices $A_0, A_1, \dots, A_{k-1}$. Let us define $A_k$ as the set of vertices not included in $A_0, A_1, \dots, A_{k-1}$, from which one can, by walking along an edge, get to some vertex from $A_{k-1}$. Thus, the set $A_k$ is the set of vertices for which the shortest path to $A$ has length $k$ (in other words, $A_k$ are all the vertices located at a distance $k$ from $A$).
Then, by condition, all the vertices of our graph, except for the vertex $A$, will be distributed over $n$ “floors” $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$.
Let's color all the edges connecting a vertex from $A_{m-1}$ with a vertex from $A_m$ in the $m$th color, where $m = 1, 2, \dots, n$. (We show the remaining edges, i.e., the edges “inside the floors,” as corners.) We obtain that for any vertex $X$ there is a path from it to $A$, in which all edges have different colors. Indeed, this path can be constructed by moving from the “floor” $A_l$, on which $X$ is located, successively to $A_{l-1}, \dots, A_1, A_0$ (in fact, this is the shortest path from $X$ to $A$). Then for any pair of vertices $X, Y$ the path obtained as a union of the specified paths (without repeating edge colors) is suitable - from $X$ to $A$ and from $Y$ to $A$.

M2783. The sum of the digits of a natural number is $s$. What is the most possible sum of digits: $a$) for the square of the em number; $b$) for the fourth power of an emic number if $s \geq 4$?

First we will make some substitutions and prove the lemmas about the sum of digits of a sum and the product of numbers.

Let us show that the sum of the digits $s(n)$ of the number $n$ is the smallest $t$ such that $n$ can be represented as the sum $t$ of a power of ten. Indeed, firstly, using the decimal notation of the number $n$ one can easily obtain the required expansion, say for $n = 2024$ we have

$$n = 2 \cdot 10^3 + 2 \cdot 10^1 + 4 \cdot 10^0 =$$

$$= 10^3 + 10^3 + 10^1 + 10^1 + 10^0 + 10^0 + 10^0$$

– a total of 8 terms equal to the power of ten. Conversely, let $n$ be represented as the sum of several powers of ten. If the total contains 10 equal terms, replace them with one: $10 \cdot 10^m = 10^{m+1}$, and the number of terms will decrease (by 9). Continuing further, let's take it to counting, when $n$ is represented as a sum of several powers of ten so that there are no more than 9 copies of each addend in this sum. Then in this sum it is easy to group the terms and get a decimal representation of the number $n$, while $s(n)$ will be equal to the number of terms.

From the above “combinatorial definition” of the sum of digits, the well-known statements also easily follow:

$$s(m+n) \leq s(m)+s(n) \text{ и } s(mn) \leq s(m)s(n).$$

Indeed, let $s(m)=k$, $s(n)=l$, then $m$ and $n$ be represented as the sum of $k$ and $l$ powers of ten, respectively. Adding these sums, we find that $m+n$ is equal to the sum $k+l$ to the power of ten, hence $s(m+n) \leq k+l=s(m)+s(n)$. However, inequality $s(m+n) \leq s(m)+s(n)$ is easy to prove using column addition. At the same time, it can be noted that the case of equality $s(m+n) = s(m)+s(n)$ occurs if and only if when adding into a column there will be no transition through the digit.

Next we will deal with the product $mn$ and the variables of the sum of powers of ten, giving $m$ and $n$: $mn = \left(10^{a_1} + 10^{a_2} + \dots + 10^{a_k}\right)\left(10^{b_1} + 10^{b_2} + \dots + 10^{b_l}\right)$. F2789. The length of the boat is uniform and a flexible rope with a mass of $M = 3,2$ kg is thrown over a monk crossbar with a round cross section and we are in balance. The rope is moved a little and it begins to slide off the crossbar. What angle $\alpha$ with the vertical does this force form if the ratio of the diameter $D$ of the crossbar to the length $L$ of the rope is equal to $10^{-2}$?

We assume that the sections of the rope that do not lie on the crossbar are vertical. When one of the ends of the rope is moved $x > 0$ from the equilibrium position, the potential energy loss is equal to $\frac{M}{L} x^2 g$. There is no energy loss. The increase in kinetic energy is equal to that of potential energy:

$$\frac{1}{2} M v^2 = \frac{M}{L} x^2 g.$$

The speed increases in proportion to the coordinate: $v = \sqrt{2 \frac{g}{L}} x$, from where

$$a_x = \sqrt{2 \frac{g}{L}} v = 2 \frac{g}{L} x.$$

At the moment under consideration, at $x = \frac{L}{4}$, the acceleration module of the rope parts is $a = \frac{g}{2}$, the square of the rope speed is $v^2 = \frac{g L}{8}$.
