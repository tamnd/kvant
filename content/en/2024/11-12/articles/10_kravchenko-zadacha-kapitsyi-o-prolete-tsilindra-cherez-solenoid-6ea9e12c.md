Kapitsa problem on the flight of a cylinder through a solenoid

I. KRAVCHENKO

THIS ARTICLE INVITES THE READER, together with the author, to speculate on the problem of Academician Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa from the brochure “Physical Problems” (M.: Znanie, 1966). Its original condition looks like this:
Through the solenide, along which the mok is thrown, the conductive cylinder passes through. Determine the conditions under which the magnetic field prevents the cylinder from moving through the solenoid. The ohmic resistances of the cylinder and solenide can be claimed.

There are two significantly different ways of integrating this condition:

DOI: https://doi.org/10.4213/kvant20241103

• The cylinder flew up to the solenoid from afar and is already partially in it (“flies”), having a certain speed of movement.
• The cylinder, already entirely located inside the same solenoid, was given a certain speed of movement by a push (the same thing “flies”).

Name P.L. Kapitsa as a scientist is associated with fundamental discoveries in the field of low temperature physics and the explanation of superconductivity. Thus, the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to P.L. Kapitsa for “basic research and discoveries in the field of low temperature physics.” Pyotr Leonidovich was also a brilliant experimenter and engineer; his work went down in the history of the development of physical experimentation.

In this regard, the solenoid and cylinder mentioned in the conditions of the assignment can very appropriately be considered superconducting. And the electrical resistance of any superconductor must be zero ($R=0$). Although the recommendation does not take into account the resistance of conducting bodies in the conditions themselves, the requirement of superconductivity eliminates the need to take into account thermal energy losses in the system under consideration.

The superconductivity of an object assumes that the magnetic flux through any closed superconducting circuit remains unchanged (this is the so-called law of conservation of magnetic flux through a superconducting circuit). Superconductivity can also be “destroyed” - you can read more about this and more in the article by Yu. Shavrin “The Law of Conservation of Magnetic Flux” in «Kvant» No. 6 for 1970.

We will consider our superconducting cylinder not made of iron, otherwise it will be far from easy for us to understand its (iron’s) magnetic properties. The magnetic properties of materials are determined by a physical quantity called magnetic permeability and denoted by μ; in our consideration, for a cylinder it will be approximately equal to unity. (In general, dalle will proceed from the assumption that in any region of space μ = 1.)

2) Let the cylinder, initially accumulated relative to the solenoid and entirely located inside the solenoid, be given a point

4) We consider the solenoid and the cylinder to be lengths: this means that the length of the solenoid (cylinder) is much greater than the diameter of its cross-section. Denoting the lengths of the solenoid and cylinder by L and l, and the diameters of their sections, respectively, by D and d, the conditions for their “length” are written as follows: L >> D and l >> d. (In the case of a long solenode, when a “rotating” current flows through it, we can assume that the magnetic field generated by this current is uniform and concentrated entirely inside this solenode.)

5) The dimensions of the solenoid are much larger than the corresponding dimensions of the cylinder: L >> l and D >> d (in limiting cases, the solutions are always much simpler - here we get rid of unnecessary mathematics: the brochure from which the proposed problem is taken is not immediately called “Physical Problems”).

6) The solenoid and the cylinder, before they were inserted into each other, were “pumped” with magnetic fields of induction B_c and B_c, respectively (about the “pumping” of the field inside the superconductor, see the mentioned article by Yu. Shavrin).

So, the solenoid and the cylinder represent a system that we will consider isolated (energy does not come into the system from other bodies and does not leave the system to any bodies). Under the process in the problem, the concept of the movement of the cylinder after imparting speed to it is silently broken until it is placed outside the solenoid.

From the conditions of the problem under consideration, we can conclude that the cylinder will slow down. Therefore, the “non-process” condition can be formulated in general terms as follows: the magnetic field does not allow the cylinder to project through the solenoid in the event that the cylinder has an initial speed v that is not enough for it to exit the space inside the solenoid. The force that precedes the movement of the cylinder acts on it, of course, from the side of the solenoid (more precisely, the magnetic field of the solenoid). In this kind of situation, energy messages often come to the rescue. The energy of our system cannot change (the system is isolated), and we know the state of the system at the beginning and end of the process. Applying the law of conservation of energy, you can simply find the minimum initial speed $\vec{v}_{\min}$ of the cylinder that satisfies its exit from the solenoid, and then state that if the cylinder speed is less than $\vec{v}_{\min}$, the solenoid does not allow the cylinder to flow through it. Let's do so.

The energy of the system at the beginning of the process consists of the kinetic energy $E_{\kappa 0}$ of the cylinder, the energy $W_{\kappa 0}$ of the magnetic field of the internal solenoid outside the cylinder and the energy $W_{\pi 0}$ of the magnetic field of the cylinder:

$$E_{\kappa 0} + W_{\kappa 0} + W_{\pi 0}.$$

The energy of the system at the end of the process consists of the energy $W_{\kappa}$ of the magnetic field of the internal solenoid, in which there is no longer a cylinder, and the energy $W_{\pi}$ of the magnetic field of the cylinder, which is already outside the solenoid:

$$W_{\kappa} + W_{\pi}.$$

The law of conservation of energy assumes the equality of the initial and final energy:

$$E_{\kappa 0} + W_{\kappa 0} + W_{\pi 0} = W_{\kappa} + W_{\pi}.$$
