division itself, the equation of motion of a particle with mass $m$ and charge $q$ in a uniform gravity field $\vec{g}$ and in a magnetic field $\vec{B}$ perpendicular to it
$$m\vec{a} = q[\vec{v} \times \vec{B}] + m\vec{g}$$
after transition to the drift system, where $\vec{a}' = \vec{a}$, $\vec{v}' = \vec{v} - \vec{v}_{\pi}$, takes the form
$$m\vec{a}' = q[\vec{v}' \times \vec{B}] + q[\vec{v}_{\pi} \times \vec{B}] + m\vec{g} = q[\vec{v}' \times \vec{B}]$$
In other words, the equation of motion in the magnetic field alone becomes - the gravity field disappears, since the drift speed is chosen exactly so that $q[\vec{v}_{\pi} \times \vec{B}] + m\vec{g} = 0$.
As can be seen from the description of the experiment, the initial velocity of the particle was always directed towards the horizontal axis; we will further consider it to be the axis $x$, going in the direction $\vec{v}_{\pi}$. There are two possible cases here.
a) Speed ​​$\vec{v}_0$ is matched with $\vec{v}_{\pi}$. At $v_0 < v_{\pi}$ in a drifting system, the particle starts against the $x'$ axis with a speed $V = v_{\pi} - v_0$. Let's direct the axis $y$ vertically upward. It is clear that when moving at a drift speed, the Lorentz force is directed vertically; in this case, it is directed downward at the initial section of the trajectory (on a circle of radius $R = \frac{mV}{qB}$). Thus, the law of particle motion in this system is absent and takes the form
$$x'(t) = -R \sin \omega t$$
$$y'(t) = H - R + R \cos \omega t$$
(where $H$ is the initial height, frequency $\omega = \frac{qB}{m}$), and in the system connected with the planet there is no
$$x(t) = x'(t) + v_{\pi}t = R\left(\frac{\omega t}{1 - z} - \sin \omega t\right)$$
$$y(t) = y'(t) = H - R(1 - \cos \omega t).$$
Here the dimensionless variable $z = v_0/v_{\pi}$ is introduced.
Note that to analyze the dependence of the flight range on the value of the initial speed, it is convenient to write the radius $R$ in terms of quantities that are constant for a given experiment and this dimensionless variable:
$$R = \frac{m^2g}{q^2B^2} - \frac{mv_0}{qB} = \frac{q}{\omega^2}\left(1 - \frac{v_0}{v_{\pi}}\right) = \frac{g}{\omega^2}(1 - z).$$
Another convenient dimensionless variable is $h = \frac{H}{R} = \frac{\omega^2H}{q(1 - z)}$. As you can see, the frequency hits the surface of the planet only at $h \leq 2$, at the moment of falling $t_{\pi}$
$$\cos \omega t_{\pi} = \cos \varphi = 1 - h, \sin \varphi = \sqrt{h(2 - h)},$$
and the range of possible values ​​is $0 < \varphi \leq \pi$. This means that the coordinate of the impact point is
$$x(t_{\pi}) = \frac{g}{\omega^2}\left(\varphi - (1 - z)\sin \varphi\right).$$
This function monotonically increases with increasing $\varphi$, and the maximum flight range is achieved at the edge of the interval - at $\varphi = \pi$, i.e. each time you need to choose $H = 2R$. So, at $0 \leq v_0x < v_{\pi}$
$$L_{\max} = \pi \frac{g}{\omega^2}.$$
If $v_0x > v_{\pi}$, then in the drift system the Lorentz layer at the initial stage of movement is directed upward, i.e. during rotation, the particle turns above the initial position (with $v_0x = v_{\pi}$ rotation in general.net – a particle moving in a straight line). This means that for any $H > 0$ the particle does not fall on the “poll”.
6) The speed $\vec{v}_0$ is in the opposite direction to $\vec{v}_{\pi}$.
In this case, in a drifting system, the particle starts against the $x'$ axis with a speed $V = v_0 + v_{\pi}$.
The laws of particle motion both in a drifting system and in a stationary residual system in the previous paragraph:
$$x(t) = x'(t) + v_{\pi}t = R\left(\frac{\omega t}{1 + z} - \sin \omega t\right),$$
$$y(t) = y'(t) = H - R(1 - \cos \omega t)$$
And
$$R = \frac{mv_0}{qB} + \frac{m^2g}{q^2B^2} = \frac{g}{\omega^2}\left(1 + \frac{v_0}{v_{\pi}}\right) = \frac{g}{\omega^2}(1 + z).$$
As in case a),
$$\cos \omega t_{\pi} = \cos \varphi = 1 - h, \sin \varphi = \sqrt{h(2 - h)}, 0 < \varphi \leq \pi.$$
However, in case 6)
$$x(t_{\pi}) = \frac{g}{\omega^2}\left(\varphi - (1 + z)\sin \varphi\right),$$
and we find that at first the distance of the particle along the $x$ axis from the launch point increases in the region of negative $x$, reaches a maximum at $\cos \varphi = (1 + z)^{-1}$, and then decreases to zero and grows again - already in the region of positive $x$. The maximum deviation of a particle in the region of negative $x$ is
$$(-x)_{\max} = \frac{g}{\omega^2}\left(\sqrt{z(2 + z)} - \arccos \frac{1}{1 + z}\right).$$
The maximum deviation in the region of positive $x$ corresponds to $\varphi = \pi$ and is equal to
$$x_{\max} = \pi \frac{g}{\omega^2}.$$
To ensure maximum flight range, you need to select $H$, which corresponds to the larger of these values. Analyzing the behavior of the function in the expression for $(-x)_{\max}$, we establish that for small $z$ it is more profitable to choose $H = 2R$, and for $z > z_{\max}$ it is advantageous to launch the particle from a height $H = \frac{g}{\omega^2}z$. The “critical” value of the response speed $z_{\max}$, which is the root of the equation
$$\sqrt{z(2 + z)} - \arccos \frac{1}{1 + z} = \pi.$$
