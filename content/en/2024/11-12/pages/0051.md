2. Let's denote the white weights by the letters A, B, C, and the black ones by the letters a, b, c. Let's compare A and a first. There are three options.

I. The weights are equal in mass. Then they give the right pair. Let us now weigh B and b. If they are equal, then this is the second pair, and C and c are the third; otherwise B and c are the second pair, b and C are the third.

II. One of the weights is 2 g heavier. Let the white weight be heavier, for example. Then A has a mass of 103 g, a has a mass of 101 g. Now put weights A and B on one bowl, and C and b on the second. Let's make a table of the possible masses of these weights and find the difference in mass between the bowls (we consider it positive if the left bowl crossed, and imprinted if the right one):

| A | B | C | b | Difference |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 103 | 101 | 102 | 102 | 0 |
| 103 | 101 | 102 | 103 | -1 |
| 103 | 102 | 101 | 102 | 2 |
| 103 | 102 | 101 | 103 | 1 |

We see that the difference allows us to unambiguously restore the masses of weights B, C, b, but then we determine all the weights and distribute them according to parameters.

III. One of the weights is 1 g heavier. Let the white weight be heavier, for example. Then either A = 103 and a = 102, or A = 102 and a = 101. Let’s put weights A and a on one bowl, B and b on the second, and again look at the possibility of the difference:

| A | a | B | b | Difference |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 103 | 102 | 101 | 101 | 3 |
| 103 | 102 | 101 | 103 | 1 |
| 103 | 102 | 102 | 101 | 2 |
| 103 | 102 | 102 | 103 | 0 |
| 102 | 101 | 101 | 102 | 0 |
| 102 | 101 | 101 | 103 | -1 |
| 102 | 101 | 103 | 102 | -2 |
| 102 | 101 | 103 | 103 | -3 |

The difference is the same in two cases, but at the same time A = b, B = a (which means C = c) - the distribution parameter weights are the same. In other cases, all weights are again determined uniquely, and we add them into pairs.

3. $C_{98}^{49} = \frac{98!}{49!49!}$ ways.

Consider two 50x50 squares: the upper left corner and the lower right corner. They have exactly one common square - the central one. These two squares have a total of 100 offset squares, and a total of 99 offset squares. Therefore, the central square is offset, and there are no offset cells outside these two squares. (figure 3 shows a similar situation, but for the squares on the 9 and 5 sides the total is 99 and 50 respectively.) Looking at the similar squares in the upper right and lower left corners, the offset cells can only be in the “cross” formed by the central column and the central row (Fig. 4).

There are 99 cells in the central row. One of them is central, and the remaining cells are divided into pairs like this: a cell from the 1st column and from the 51st, from the 2nd and from the 52nd, from the 3rd and from the 53rd, ..., from the 49th and from the 99th. For two cells of one pair, there are two 50x50 squares that differ only in the cells of this pair (one square differs from the other by 1 horizontally). This means that these two cells are either sharpened, or both are sharpened. (On figure 4 cells from one pair are marked with the same letters. Two 5x5 squares are marked in red, differing only in cells of type b.)

A similar statement is true about the cells of the central column. This means that the entire arrangement of displaced cells is uniquely restored by filling 98 cells of the central column, which lie above or to the left of the central cell. It turns out that from these 98 cells you need to select 49 that are sharpened. This can be done in $C_{98}^{49} = \frac{98!}{49!49!}$ ways.
