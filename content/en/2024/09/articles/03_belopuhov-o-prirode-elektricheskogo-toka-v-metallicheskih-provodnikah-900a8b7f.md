About the nature of electric current
in metal conductors
L. BELOPUKHOV

From an early age we hear and remember the expressions “electric current”, “current flow through wires”. From school physics it is firmly established that electric current is the ordered direction of movement of electric charges.

The concept of “electric current” arose a long time ago, more than 200 years ago. In 1800, at a meeting of the Royal Society of London, the Italian physicist, 55-year-old professor at the University of Pavia, Alexandro Volleta (1745–1827), presented a report on the results of his research on electricity. He also demonstrated his invention, which had already received the name “volmov so.” For that time, this prototype of electric batteries was the most powerful source of current. In his report, Alexandro Volleta first used the terms “electric current, electromotive force, electrical capacity, electrical voltage.” And for two hundred years now, these terms have remained unchanged for electrical phenomena.

Volta was an uncharged scientist. He became interested in physical descriptions at the age of 13, and at the age of 34 he became a university professor, having worked in this position for more than 40 years. He communicated with many European physicists. With his knowledge and understanding of electrical phenomena, he charmed Napoleon, who awarded him the Legion of Honor and gave him the titles of count and senator. Italy considers him its national hero. And the unit of electrical voltage and electric motion of force in 1861 received the name volm.

Today, the legacy of the voltaic pillar is batteries and accumulators, which work on the same principle, but are much smaller in size. They are used as direct current sources in all devices to make life easier and better (gadgets) - from electric flashlights to sophisticated smartphones. But it is not easy to figure out how electric current is distributed in them - this is the field of electrochemistry. Therefore, we will teach from the most common case - electric current in conductors (metallax).

Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854) and other physicists quickly established that electric current in metals can be detected and changed by its action, primarily thermal. Electric current produces heat, heats the wire, which means it experiences resistance to its movement like mechanical friction. Ohm called this phenomenon in conductors electrical resistance and, based on experiments, established his significant law - the magnitude (strength) of the current in the conductor I is directly proportional to the voltage U at the ends of the conductor: I ~ U. He called the proportionality coefficient the conductivity of the conductor, and its inverse value - the resistance of the conductor (R).

For the simplest case (one-dimensional problem), Ohm established the dependence of resistance on the geometry and properties of the conductor: R = ρl/S, where l is the length of the conductor, S is its cross-sectional area, aρ is a value characterizing the property of the conductor material, called the specific resistance of the substance. In the technical (electrical) application of Ohm's law, conductors are wires with a diameter of the order of a millimeter, therefore in tables for resistivity the dimension Ohm mm²/m is more often used. The resistivity in this dimension is a million times greater than in SI units.

Resistivity is an amazing characteristic of a substance. For metals it has values ​​of the order of 10⁻⁸ Ohm m, for semiconductors ~1 Ohm m, and for dielectrics (glass, porcelain, hard rubber) ~10¹³–10¹⁴ Ohm m. No other characteristic of solids changes within such a wide range (10²² times!). Therefore, there cannot be a single explanation for the flow of electric current for all types of substances. The division of solids into three types according to their electrical properties is clearly visible. The structure of these types of solids has significant differences. We will consider here only metal conductors (metals and their alloys).

**Structure of metals**

The atomic-crystalline structure of metals was established back in the 19th century. The main credit for this goes to the Russian metallurgist and engineer D.K. Chernov (1839–1921), who laid the foundations of scientific metallography. Further development of the idea of ​​the structure of metals began to occur after the discovery of the electron and the creation of a planetary model of the atom. The fact that some of the atomic electrons are relatively easily separated from neutral atoms, turning them into positive ions, is proven by many phenomena (thermionic emission, external photoelectric effect, etc.).

Metals consist of many small crystals pressed into a total mass. In each crystal, positive ions are regularly located at lattice nodes, between which relatively free electrons move. The term “relatively free” electrons means that their freedom is limited by the internal space of the crystal and that they are not allowed to leave the crystal by attractive forces from the positive ions of the crystal lattice.

The attractive forces between ions and the generalized “gas” of free electrons represent the so-called metallic bond of particles in solids. Other types of bonds are ionic, covalent and hydrogen. Metallic bonds are present not only in those chemical elements that are adapted to metals (and there are about 90 of the 118 elements known today), but also in numerous metal alloys.

The ions of the crystal lattice are not stationary, they are in continuous oscillatory motion near the centers of equilibrium. This oscillatory movement is very complex - after all, each particle is connected to each neighbor in space by invisible “springs” of attractive and repulsive forces, and these “springs” are trillions of trillions. The energy of these vibrations represents the main part of the internal energy of a substance (proportional to absolute temperature).

In the simplest model, metal particles (ons) obey the Maxwell–Boltzmann energy distribution in the same way as particles of a polyatomic ideal gas, which have six effective degrees of freedom of vibrational motion. According to the principle of equal distribution of energy over the degree of freedom, the molar heat of the crystal lattice of metals should be of the order of 3 kN_A (k is Boltzmann’s constant, N_A is Avogadro’s number), which is 25 J/(mol K).

It turned out that the molar heat of many metals is close to this value. But how can this be? But doesn’t a gas of generalized free electrons have heat, i.e. does not change the state of chaotic motion of its particles when the temperature rises and the amplitude and speed of oscillations of the lattice from which electrons STALL increase?

Yes, it turned out that this is exactly the case. The principles of the classical statistical theory of gases are not applicable to electron gas in a metal. But this did not become clear immediately, but only after the creation of quantum mechanics.

Classical theory
electrical conductivity of metals

In the classical theory of electrical conductivity of metals, it is the movement of free electrons under the influence of an electric field that constitutes an electric current. The theory was created in 1900 by the German physicist Paul Drude (1863–1906) and a few years later it was improved by the famous Dutch physicist, 1902 Nobel laureate Hendrik Lorentz (1853–1928).

The model is like this. In the absence of a current source, the directionally averaged electric field acting on free electrons is zero. If this were not so, then all the free electrons would tend to cluster at one end of the conductor. But this is not the case - free electrons are, on average, evenly distributed throughout the conductor and a state with zero average directivity of the electric field is maintained inside the crystal. Electrons rush along zigzag trajectories, each time changing their direction as a result of collisions (interactions) with ions. These collisions are considered to be absolutely elastic. The concept of modern quantum theory of the structure of metals and their electrical conductivity

Such a large number of difficulties and shortcomings of the classical electronic theory of electrical conductivity required a change in the very model of electric current in a conductor. It was necessary to abandon the description of the ensemble of free electrons of a metal as an electron gas, subject to the classical molecular-kinetic theory of an ideal gas.

One of the first tasks facing the new physics, quantum mechanics, which was emerging in the 1920s, was its application to the question of the structure of metals. It was necessary to figure out why the electron gas in a metal has no heat capacity and does not obey the classical Maxwell-Bolshman statistics. Young physicists Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac independently proposed a different statistics based on the quantum Pauli principle in 1926. According to this principle, in an ensemble of particles having a spin number of 1/2, in particular electrons, there cannot be two particles in the same quantum state. Therefore, there cannot be more than two electrons with the same energy (one of them has a spin number of +1/2, the other has a spin number of -1/2).

Free electrons of a metal crystal can be considered a quantum ensemble, since they are in the general electric field of the crystal ions with the same average potential energy in the space between the ions (respectively, with an average zero voltage). Since the states of electrons must be different, the number of these states must correspond to the number of free electrons, i.e. be very big. In quantum physics of the creative body it is formulated that the set of energy values ​​of these electrons represents a non-energy zone. Perhaps the word “zone” is not a very good term, since in Russian this word is primarily associated with a spatial interval, and not an energy interval. But the term “energy zone” has been around for almost a hundred years, and it has firmly entered the physics of the created body, an entire section of which is called the “zone theory of created bodies.”

In band theory, it is important that in conductors (metals) the number of states occupied by electrons is always several times less than the number of possible states, i.e. electrons use only half or even a quarter of the width of the entire band, which is called the allowed band.

The part of the allowed band not occupied by electrons is called the conduction band. In the presence of an electric field (when a conductor is connected to a current source), all free electrons can receive energy from the field - the Pauli principle does not prevent this, since this happens almost simultaneously for all electrons, and the allowed energy band of these electrons is not completely filled with them. The receipt of energy by electrons from the field is electric current. The substance is a conductor.

In nonconductors (insulators and semiconductors), all possible states of external atomic electrons are fully used. In this case, the allowed zone is called the ballent zone. And the conduction band allowed by quantum theory is separated from the valence band by a band gap. This means that the electrons of the valence band either cannot receive energy from the field - these are dielectrics, or only a small part of the valence electrons (the most energetic) will absorb exactly the energy corresponding to the conduction band and become a charge carrier in the electric field - these are semiconductors.

But let's return to the free electrons of metals. In band theory, it is important that the width of the band occupied by free electrons in metals, i.e. The range of energies that a free electron can have is much greater than the value $kT$ at room and laboratory temperatures. Energy $kT$ is the energy that a free electron with energies $E$ could receive from the units of the crystal lattice during its chaotic collisions with them. However, it cannot pollute it, since the state with energies $E + kT$ is used by two other electrons. Only the most energetic electrons have this opportunity. But the number of such electrons does not mean anything that the main array of free electrons is insensitive to the energies of ion vibrations, i.e. to the temperature of the metal, or, in other words, has no heat capacity. Thus, Fermi-Dirac quantum statistics explains this difficulty in classical electronic theory.

How does quantum mechanics consider the nature of electric current in conductors and what is the electrical resistance of a conductor?

It was stated above that classical electronic theory considers electric current as a mixture of free electrons of a metal under the influence of an electric field (electron drift), and the resistance of a conductor as a result of the collision of free electrons with ions of the crystal lattice. The drift mixing $\Delta x$ could be visually represented on a vector diagram and expressed by the formula of classical mechanics - this is the “understanding” of what electric current is.

In quantum mechanics there is no such clarity. The concept of electron trajectory is fundamentally absent - both in an atom, and in a molecule, and in a metal crystal. There is no need (and it is impossible) to imagine the trajectory of a microparticle as a geometric image or formula.

But what is possible (and necessary)? In quantum mechanics, various mathematical approaches have been developed to solve the problem of the behavior of a microparticle. In all these approaches, the coordinate-time dependences of the probabilistic characteristics of the behavior of a microparticle are found and the receipt of recipes for obtaining from these probabilistic characteristics those quantities that are observed experimentally. In the simplest quantum theory it turned out to be convenient to express the interaction of electrons with the elements of the crystal structure as a result of the scattering of de Broglie electron waves on these elements. If the crystal lattice were ideal, the interaction would be simply spatial diffraction, similar to the diffraction of RENTEN RAYS, for which inexpensively available lattices are too small to respond. But the de Broglie wavelength of electrons coincides in order of magnitude with the crystal lattice constant (the average distance between pons) and for it lattice defects are significant. The theory uses the term “scattering of de Broglie waves by crystal lattice defects.” During scattering, the electron loses the energy it received from the external electric field and transfers it to the electron. As a participant in the general quantum ensemble of free electrons, its energy remains in the allowed energy band.

To harmonize theory with experiment, it turned out to be convenient to use the pri-function to express the average value of the speed of charge movement (density value $j$ of the electric current) from the electric field strength. The expression was obtained

This is a representation of Ohm's law in local form, from which one can easily obtain the general Ohm's law $I = U/R$, if the value $mu / \left( n^2 e \lambda \right)$ is denoted $\rho$ and called resistivity:

$$\rho = mu / \left( n^2 e \lambda \right).$$

In deriving this expression, Fermi-Dirac quantum statistics were used, according to which (as mentioned above) the average speed of free electrons in a metal is practically constant and does not depend on temperature,

in contrast to the Maxwell-Bolshman statistics adopted in the classical electronic theory of electrical conductivity of metals.

And the value $\lambda$ has a completely different meaning. In quantum theory, this symbol denotes the average length of scattering of electron de Broglie waves on defects in the crystal structure (but not the length of de Broglie waves itself!). Since the defect is the chaotic distribution of the oscillatory motion of the ins, the main theoretical dependence is the connection between $\lambda$ and the absolute temperature $T$. Despite the complexity of the calculations, the results are simple:

$$\lambda \sim 1/T.$$

Then, taking into account that $u \approx \text{const}$, for the specific heat we obtain the theoretical dependence:

$$\rho \sim T,$$

which coincides very well with experimental data.

Qualitative quantum theory also explains the temperature dependence of resistance at low temperatures and when the temperature tends to zero, when the main role in the scattering of electron waves begins to be played not by thermal motion, but by other defects of the crystal lattice. The quantum nature of the oscillatory motion of ins, which manifests itself at low temperatures, and the fact that even when the absolute temperature tends to zero, the oscillation energy tends not to zero, but to a certain minimum value, also plays a role. This also explains the greater resistance of metal alloys, in which higher concentrations of impurity atoms represent significant low-cost structures.

Thus, in this version of the quantum theory of electrical conductivity of metals, the electric current represents the distribution of de Broglie electronic waves along the conductor, and the electrical resistance is the result of the scattering of de Broglie waves on defects in the crystal structure, primarily on the statistical inconsistencies of the thermal vibrational motion of the lattice.

A regular hexagon on side n is divided into rhombuses on side 1 and an acute angle of 60°. The tiling of the interior tiling of the hexagon from side 1 can be changed, as shown in figure 21. Let us prove that a) from any arrangement of tiles, the maxim operation can be obtained by any other;

b) from the arrangement of tiles at figure 22 it is impossible to obtain the arrangement at figure 23 in less than $n^3$ operations.

First solution to problem 2: exit into space. When solving Problem 1, we proved that an arbitrary division of a regular hexagon into rhombuses can be dented as an image of the surface of a set of cubes complex in a box $n \times n \times n$. As before, we will remember figure 22 as an empty box, and figure 23 as a filled box. A flip corresponds to the addition of one cube to an empty “nest” or the removal of one cube on which no others lie. Then it turns out that to move from configuration figure 22 to configuration figure 23 you need to put $n^3$ cubes in a box, and this cannot be done in a smaller number of moves (the number of moves can be greater if some of them are useless, for example, this will happen if you take out already added cubes).

Let there be two partitions into rhombuses, which correspond to the spatial configurations of cubes $Q_1$ and $Q_2$. Then you can go from $Q_1$ to $Q_2$ in two different ways. You can first remove all the cubes from $Q_1$, and then first build $Q_1$ to a full box, and then remove the cubes so that you get $Q_2$. If you do these two actions sequentially, then in total $n^3$ cubes will be taken out and $n^3$ cubes will be added, i.e. a total of $2n^3$ operations will be performed. This means that at least one of these actions requires no more than $n^3$ operations.
