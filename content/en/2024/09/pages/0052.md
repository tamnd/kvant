The coefficient between the block and the wedge is $\mu = 0,6$. Find the model of acceleration a of the wedge. Neglect air resistance. Make figure indicating the forces acting on the block.

Possible answers:

A. $a = \frac{F - mg(\mu \cos \alpha - \sin \alpha)}{M + m}$;

B. $a = g \frac{\mu + tg \alpha}{\mu tg \alpha + 1}$;

C. $a = \frac{m}{M + m} \frac{\mu - tg \alpha}{\mu tg \alpha + 1} g$;

D. $a = g \frac{\mu - tg \alpha}{\mu tg \alpha + 1}$;

E. $a = g \frac{\mu + tg \alpha}{\mu tg \alpha - 1}$.

Some USE problems have an interesting feature: the presence of depicted data that should not be included in the answer. To successfully solve such problems, you must definitely learn to recognize this extra data.

Note that the conditions of this problem explicitly specify the numerical values ​​of four physical quantities ($M, m, \alpha$ and $\mu$) and the neutron acceleration of free fall ($g = 10 \text{ m/c}^2$). However, the numerical value of the force $F$ acting on the wedge is missing. Therefore, its value cannot be included in the answer, which means that answer A is incorrect. But what about the acceleration of the wedge without knowing the magnitude of the force $F$?

Here we will be helped by the fact that, according to the conditions of the problem, the block slides uniformly with the wedge. Consequently, the accelerations of the block and the wedge relative to the ground are the same. It turns out that we can not use Newton’s second law for the wedge and, therefore, the mass $M$ the wedge should not be included in the answer. However, the observant reader probably noticed that the conditions require avoiding drawings indicating forces acting only on the block. This hint confirms our assumption that the mass of the wedge will not be included in the solution.

Moreover, the answer should not include not only mass $M$ wedge, but also mass $m$ bar. Indeed, analyzing the possibility of answers and relying again on the dimension, it is not difficult to understand that to determine the acceleration $a$ in our location has only one quantity of the same location - the acceleration of free fall $g$ and two dimensionless quantities - friction coefficient $\mu$ and angle $\alpha$. The answer could also be the mass ratio $m/M$, but, as we found out, the mass $M$ the wedge should not enter it, which means that the mass of the block cannot enter either.

We are left with three “massless” answers. One of them – EG – is discarded, since it is highlighted as a “minus” in the denominator: when $\mu tg \alpha = 1$the acceleration of the wedge should tend to infinity.
