There are so many powers in physics: the speed of a falling body is directly proportional to time, the distance traveled is the square of time, the volume of the ball is the cube of the radius, ..., and the flow of emitted energy, it turns out, is proportional to the fourth power of temperature! Yes, anyone can be convinced of this if they know such fundamental constants as the Boltzmann constant $k$ (Ludwig Boltzmann, 1844–1906), Planck's constant $h$ (Max Planck, 1858–1947), speed of light $c$. Messages from the theory of dimensions and similarity will help here.

So, you need to get the energy flux density dependences $q$, its dimension $\frac{J}{\left(m^2 \cdot c\right)}$, from temperature $T$, expressed in degrees Kelvin, K. It is immediately clear that the corresponding formula should contain $kT$, J, to some extent $m$, and to get J to the first power, this value should be divided by $h^{m-1}$, J s. And opening the ligature $m^2$ in the denominator? Of course, divided by the square of the speed of light $c^2$, $m^2/c^2$. It turns out $q \sim \frac{(kT)^m}{c^2 h^{m-1}}$.

$$\frac{1}{c^2} \cdot c^{m-1} = c^{m-3} = c$$,
where $m = 4$. As a result we have
$$q \sim \frac{(kT)^4}{c^2 h^3} \sim T^4$$.

This is the famous Stefan-Boltzmann law (Josef Stefan, 1835–1893). A complex of fundamental constants, standing as a factor at $T^4$ and called the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, has the form
$$\sigma = \frac{2\pi^5}{15} \cdot \frac{k^4}{c^2 h^3} \approx 40 \cdot \frac{(1,4 \cdot 10^{-23})^4 \cdot Jx^4/K^4}{(3 \cdot 10^8)^2 m^2/c^2 \cdot (6,63 \cdot 10^{-34})^3 Jx^3 \cdot c^3} = 5,67 \cdot 10^{-8} \cdot Jx/\left(m^2 \cdot c \cdot K^4\right)$$.

All the energy emitted by the Sun is equal to $\sigma T_C^4 \cdot 4\pi R_C^2$, Where $R_C$ – radius of the Sun. Its part intercepted by the Earth's disk is equal to $\frac{\pi R_3^2}{4\pi L^2}$, Here $L$ – distance between the Sun and Earth. All energy emitted by the Earth is equal to $\sigma T_3^4 \cdot 4\pi R_3^2$. Equating the energy flows incident on the Earth's disk and emitted by its surface, we obtain

$$\sigma T_C^4 \cdot 4\pi R_C^2 \cdot \frac{\pi R_3^2}{4\pi L^2} = \sigma T_3^4 \cdot 4\pi R_3^2.$$

It can be seen that both sides of this equation can be reduced by $\sigma \cdot 4\pi R_3^2$. This means that only the fourth degree of temperature is important. In addition, the resulting isisability is the same for all planets. Attitude plays a major role

$$\frac{R_C^2}{4L^2} = \frac{(2R_C)^2}{16L^2} = \left(\frac{\alpha_C}{16}\right)^2$$,

where $\alpha_C$ is the angular size of the diameter of the Sun when observed from Earth, equal to approximately $10^{-2}$ radians. As a result we have

$$\sqrt{\alpha_C} / 2 \cdot T_C = T_3, T_C = 20T_3.$$

Taking the average temperature of the Earth to be 300 K, we get $T_C = 6000$ K.

Note that the angular size of the diameter of the Moon is the same as for the Sun. This allows us to observe both the total eclipse of our star and its corona.
