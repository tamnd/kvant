Selected problems of the XXIX Tournament
named after A.P. Savin

1 (grades 6-7). There are two natural numbers written on the board: one blue and one red. Petya has his own favorite natural number. He is our GCD with a blue number and his GCD with a red number. It turned out that if you add a red number to the first result, and a blue number to the second, then the sums will be equal. Prove that the red number is divisible by the blue number.
A. Peshin

2 (5-8 grades). a) Given an infinite square lattice. Is it possible to color the sides of the cells in two colors so that every two lattice nodes are connected by common colors? 6) Given an infinite triangular lattice. Is it possible to color the sides of triangles in three colors so that every two nodes of the lattice are connected by all three colors?
A. Gribalko

3 (6-7 grades). 100 wise men sit around a clearing, in the center of which a bazobab grows. They were told that they would put on caps with different numbers from 1 to 100 and everyone would see all the caps except their own and the cap of the sitting sage. Then, clockwise, starting with the oldest, everyone will have to name a natural number from 1 to 100. Before the test begins, the sages can agree on how to proceed. What is the largest number of them who are guaranteed to be able to name the number on their cap?
A. Gribalko

4 (8 grades). The mathematician has 100 weights, the masses of which are equal to $1, \frac{1}{2^2}, \frac{1}{3^2}, \dots, \frac{1}{100^2}$ kg, and an abciltonally accurate double-bar scale. can he place five weights on each of the scales so that equilibrium is established?
M. Evdokimov

5 (6-7 grades). The spectator shuffles a deck of 52 cards and gives the magician's assistant five cards. The assistant looks at the received cards, lays them out in a row from left to right, face down, and then, one after another, opens some three laid out cards. Can the magician and his assistant come to an agreement so that the magician can guess each of the two remaining cards?

M. Evdokimov

6 (7-8 grades). For what $n$ is it possible to fill the table $n \times n$ with various integers from 1 to $n^2$ so that every two consecutive numbers are in adjacent cells, and all exact squares are in different rows and in different columns?
A. Gribalko

10 (7-8 grades). Squared $3 \times 3$ natural numbers are written. During a move, you can add the same natural number to those numbers in one row or in one column. Prove that in a few moves it is possible to ensure that at least seven numbers become perfect squares.
