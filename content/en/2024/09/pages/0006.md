Classical theory
electrical conductivity of metals

In the classical theory of electrical conductivity of metals, it is the movement of free electrons under the influence of an electric field that constitutes an electric current. The theory was created in 1900 by the German physicist Paul Drude (1863–1906) and a few years later it was improved by the famous Dutch physicist, 1902 Nobel laureate Hendrik Lorentz (1853–1928).

The model is like this. In the absence of a current source, the directionally averaged electric field acting on free electrons is zero. If this were not so, then all the free electrons would tend to cluster at one end of the conductor. But this is not the case - free electrons are, on average, evenly distributed throughout the conductor and a state with zero average directivity of the electric field is maintained inside the crystal. Electrons rush along zigzag trajectories, each time changing their direction as a result of collisions (interactions) with ions. These collisions are considered to be absolutely elastic.
