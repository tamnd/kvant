Yuri knows that each cell of the table shows the sun or the moon and any two rows are different (at least in one column). Each cell of the table is covered with a sheet. The wind rose and blew away some of the sheets: two sheets from each line. Could it happen that now Yuri can find out at least about 10,000 rows what is depicted in each of the columns?

Answer: it could. Note that there are only $2^{200}$ different strings of length 200 in which each character is a sun or moon; This means that each such row appears in the table exactly once. Let's divide all positions into two halves of 100 columns - “left” and “right”. Suppose that in each row in which there are two suns in one half (let's call them solar), the wind blew away the leaves from one of these pairs of suns, and in each non-solar row - there are exactly $101^{2}$ such rows - the wind discovered the positions of all the suns (there are no more than two suns in a non-solar row, so the wind could do this). Then Yuri will realize that those lines where two suns are revealed in one half are exactly solar, which means that non-solar lines are exactly those $101^{2}$ lines in which the wind did not reveal two suns in one half. Each of them has all the suns open, so the images in these lines covered with leaves are the moon. The problem is solved. Comment. There are fundamentally other constructions that also allow one to define $101^{2}$ lines. Let's give one of them. Let's say that a row is monolithic if all the suns in it are in a row (a row of only moons is also monolithic). We call a monolithic string balanced if at least one of the two middle symbols is a sun, or if there are no suns at all. There are exactly $101^{2}$ balanced lines. Any unbalanced row contains either the sun standing to the left of the moon in the left half, or the moon standing to the left of the sun in the right half; a balanced string does not contain such pairs.
