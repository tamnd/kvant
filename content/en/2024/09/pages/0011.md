The part of the allowed band not occupied by electrons is called the conduction band. In the presence of an electric field (when a conductor is connected to a current source), all free electrons can receive energy from the field - the Pauli principle does not prevent this, since this happens almost simultaneously for all electrons, and the allowed energy band of these electrons is not completely filled with them. The receipt of energy by electrons from the field is electric current. The substance is a conductor.

In nonconductors (insulators and semiconductors), all possible states of external atomic electrons are fully used. In this case, the allowed zone is called the ballent zone. And the conduction band allowed by quantum theory is separated from the valence band by a band gap. This means that the electrons of the valence band either cannot receive energy from the field - these are dielectrics, or only a small part of the valence electrons (the most energetic) will absorb exactly the energy corresponding to the conduction band and become a charge carrier in the electric field - these are semiconductors.

But let's return to the free electrons of metals. In band theory, it is important that the width of the band occupied by free electrons in metals, i.e. The range of energies that a free electron can have is much greater than the value $kT$ at room and laboratory temperatures. Energy $kT$ is the energy that a free electron with energies $E$ could receive from the units of the crystal lattice during its chaotic collisions with them. However, it cannot pollute it, since the state with energies $E + kT$ is used by two other electrons. Only the most energetic electrons have this opportunity. But the number of such electrons does not mean anything that the main array of free electrons is insensitive to the energies of ion vibrations, i.e. to the temperature of the metal, or, in other words, has no heat capacity. Thus, Fermi-Dirac quantum statistics explains this difficulty in classical electronic theory.

How does quantum mechanics consider the nature of electric current in conductors and what is the electrical resistance of a conductor?

It was stated above that classical electronic theory considers electric current as a mixture of free electrons of a metal under the influence of an electric field (electron drift), and the resistance of a conductor as a result of the collision of free electrons with ions of the crystal lattice. The drift mixing $\Delta x$ could be visually represented on a vector diagram and expressed by the formula of classical mechanics - this is the “understanding” of what electric current is.

In quantum mechanics there is no such clarity. The concept of electron trajectory is fundamentally absent - both in an atom, and in a molecule, and in a metal crystal. There is no need (and it is impossible) to imagine the trajectory of a microparticle as a geometric image or formula.

But what is possible (and necessary)? In quantum mechanics, various mathematical approaches have been developed to solve the problem of the behavior of a microparticle. In all these approaches, the coordinate-time dependences of the probabilistic characteristics of the behavior of a microparticle are found and the receipt of recipes for obtaining from these probabilistic characteristics those quantities that are observed experimentally.
