In the technical (electrical) application of Ohm's law, conductors are wires with a diameter of the order of a millimeter, therefore in tables for resistivity the dimension Ohm mm²/m is more often used. The resistivity in this dimension is a million times greater than in SI units.

Resistivity is an amazing characteristic of a substance. For metals it has values ​​of the order of 10⁻⁸ Ohm m, for semiconductors ~1 Ohm m, and for dielectrics (glass, porcelain, hard rubber) ~10¹³–10¹⁴ Ohm m. No other characteristic of solids changes within such a wide range (10²² times!). Therefore, there cannot be a single explanation for the flow of electric current for all types of substances. The division of solids into three types according to their electrical properties is clearly visible. The structure of these types of solids has significant differences. We will consider here only metal conductors (metals and their alloys).

**Structure of metals**

The atomic-crystalline structure of metals was established back in the 19th century. The main credit for this goes to the Russian metallurgist and engineer D.K. Chernov (1839–1921), who laid the foundations of scientific metallography. Further development of the idea of ​​the structure of metals began to occur after the discovery of the electron and the creation of a planetary model of the atom. The fact that some of the atomic electrons are relatively easily separated from neutral atoms, turning them into positive ions, is proven by many phenomena (thermionic emission, external photoelectric effect, etc.).

Metals consist of many small crystals pressed into a total mass. In each crystal, positive ions are regularly located at lattice nodes, between which relatively free electrons move. The term “relatively free” electrons means that their freedom is limited by the internal space of the crystal and that they are not allowed to leave the crystal by attractive forces from the positive ions of the crystal lattice.

The attractive forces between ions and the generalized “gas” of free electrons represent the so-called metallic bond of particles in solids. Other types of bonds are ionic, covalent and hydrogen. Metallic bonds are present not only in those chemical elements that are adapted to metals (and there are about 90 of the 118 elements known today), but also in numerous metal alloys.

The ions of the crystal lattice are not stationary, they are in continuous oscillatory motion near the centers of equilibrium. This oscillatory movement is very complex - after all, each particle is connected to each neighbor in space by invisible “springs” of attractive and repulsive forces, and these “springs” are trillions of trillions. The energy of these vibrations represents the main part of the internal energy of a substance (proportional to absolute temperature).

In the simplest model, metal particles (ons) obey the Maxwell–Boltzmann energy distribution in the same way as particles of a polyatomic ideal gas, which have six effective degrees of freedom of vibrational motion. According to the principle of equal distribution of energy over the degree of freedom, the molar heat of the crystal lattice of metals should be of the order of 3 kN_A (k is Boltzmann’s constant, N_A is Avogadro’s number), which is 25 J/(mol K).

It turned out that the molar heat of many metals is close to this value. But how can this be? But doesn’t a gas of generalized free electrons have heat, i.e. does not change the state of chaotic motion of its particles when the temperature rises and the amplitude and speed of oscillations of the lattice from which electrons STALL increase?

Yes, it turned out that this is exactly the case. The principles of the classical statistical theory of gases are not applicable to electron gas in a metal. But this did not become clear immediately, but only after the creation of quantum mechanics.
