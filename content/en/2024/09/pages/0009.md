And some disagreements with experience are considered simply difficulties of the theory. In the educational literature this is entitled: “Difficulties of the classical electronic theory of electrical conductivity.” But it still seems that these are not just difficulties, but fundamental internal inconsistencies of this theory, far from the simple nature of electric current. Let's look at these discrepancies. 1) In classical electronic theory, the expression for the resistivity of conductors is derived using Newton's law and the laws of molecular physics for an ideal gas. Let's compare theory with experience. Calculation using the formula U 2mu $ne^{2} O$ gives the value $10^{6}$ Ohm m, while the real values ​​of this value for many metals are 100 times less. 2) The high resistance of many alloys compared to the resistance of the components of these alloys becomes completely incomprehensible. One of the most common alloys in heating devices, nichrome, contains 70% nickel and 25% chromium (and another 5% impurities to give the wires greater ductility). The resistivity of this alloy of 1.1 $10^{6}$ Ohm m is 14 times greater than that of nickel and 7 times greater than that of chromium. And, according to classical theory, it should have an intermediate value between the resistivities of nickel and chromium, i.e. 1.2 $10^{7}$ Ohm m. This is almost 10 times less than the real value. 3) How does the classical theory explain the dependence of the resistance of metals on temperature? The temperature effect can be neglected. The main temperature-dependent quantity in the classical theory is the average speed of chaotic (thermal) movement of free electrons, which is directly proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature: $u \sim T$ and tends to zero at low temperatures. Consequently, resistivity in the classical theory should also depend on temperature, i.e. $U \sim T$. What is experienced addiction? In school textbooks, the dependence of resistivity on temperature t, expressed in degrees Celsius, is usually given: $U U_{0} 1 D t$, where D is the temperature coefficient of resistance, equal to approximately 1,273. If temperature is measured in kelvins, then the experimental dependence on absolute temperature has the form $U U_{0} T 273$, i.e. $U \sim T$ rather than the square root of absolute temperature. A particularly strong discrepancy between theory and experiment occurs at low temperatures. The resistance does not tend to zero, but to a certain value, which can be called residual resistance. The classical theory does not explain this. Nor does it explain the phenomenon of superconductivity - the complete disappearance of resistance at some point.
