Before the school Olympiad

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. G. GAIN

Today's math club lesson should be considered as a preparatory lesson for the Olympiads.

For many schoolchildren, math Olympiads are the first step in getting to know foreign mathematics. At the Olympiads, children learn to apply their knowledge to solve non-standard problems; they perform with peers from other schools and cities. Typically, at the Olympiad, problems are offered that can conventionally be called algebraic, geometric, numerical and logical. We bring to your attention several such problems at the level of school and district olympiads (the number in brackets indicates the class in which the problem is proposed*)). You will find brief solutions and instructions for all problems in the next issue of the magazine.

Let's start with algebraic and geometric problems - they, please, do not need comments.

Numerical problems are mainly divisibility and equation solving problems in whole numbers. Such problems are practically not encountered in school, and participants in the Olympiad have to invent methods for solving them, which are not possible in a “combat” situation. Let's start with very simple tasks.

14. The sum of 1982 integers is odd. Prove that the product of these numbers is even. (7)

15. Prove that the number $2^9+2^{99}$ is divisible by 100. (7)

In problems involving integers, divisibility by 3 and 9 often helps.

16. Prove that for no natural values ​​$m$ and $n$ the number $10^m+1$ is divisible by $10^n-1$. (7)

17. Find all two-digit numbers whose sum of digits does not change when multiplied by 2. (7)

18. Prove that the equation $x^2+1=3y$ is not solvable in integers. (7)

The last problem uses the fact that if the sum of the squares of two integers is divisible by 3, then each term is divisible by 3. This statement is true for the number 7, and for the number 11, and in general for all prime numbers of the form $4k+3$. But this is already a “real” mathematical theorem, first proven by P. Fermat. As you can see, from an Olympiad problem to a mathematical theorem is one step. But how to do it?

19. Multiplying by 9 reverses the order of the digits of a certain four-digit number. Find this number. (7)

20. Find all natural numbers $x, y, z$ for which the equality $xyz=x+y$ holds. (7)

Often, tasks are proposed in one-day meetings, in which a description of a certain method of action is given and it is asked whether this or that result can be obtained as a result of these actions. The main method for solving such problems is to discover a property of the original object that does not change when performing the actions specified in the problem (this property is called an invariant). If the final object of the task does not have the found property, then it very/no cannot be obtained as a result of the described actions.

Here are a few similar tasks.

35. In the beginning, the gardener grew 25 bananas and 30 oranges in the apple tree. Every day he picks two fruits and immediately a new one grows on the apple tree, and if he picks two identical fruits, then an orange grows, and if he picks two different fruits, then a banana grows. What is the last fruit on this tree? (7)

36. Given three numbers: 2; $1+\sqrt{2}$; $1-\sqrt{2}$.

a) In one move you are allowed to write three new numbers, replacing each of the original numbers with the resulting other numbers. Is it possible, by continuing this operation several times, to arrive at set 1; $2+\sqrt{2}$; $2-\sqrt{2}$?

6) In one move, you are allowed to replace any two numbers with their sum divided by $\sqrt{2}$ and the difference divided by $\sqrt{2}$, leaving the third number unchanged. Is it possible, by continuing this operation several times, to arrive at set 1; $\sqrt{2}$; $1/\sqrt{2}$? (8)

37. Three blacksmiths play leapfrog: if the blacksmith from point $A$ conjugates through the blacksmith at point $B$, then he ends up at point $C$, symmetrical to point $A$ relative to point $B$. In the initial position, the blacksmiths occupy the three vertices of the square. Can they, playing leapfrog, get to its fourth vertex? (8)
