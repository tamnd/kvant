Another interesting feature of parabolas is that it makes it easy to determine the speed of a body’s movement. And to find the average speed, you need to check the chord, not the tangent, which is not very difficult.

In addition, we know that the pressure $p$ of the gas is equal by definition

$$p = \frac{F}{S},$$

where $F$ is the force acting on the flat surface $S$ perpendicular to it. This means we have to calculate the force $F$. We will do this using Newton's laws of mechanics.

Let's place our gas in a vessel in the form of a rectangular parallelepiped and direct the coordinate axes $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ along its edges, as shown in figure. Let's find the force with which the gas acts, for example, on the right wall of the vessel. Obviously, it must be denoted by $F_x$.

According to Newton’s third law, this wall itself acts on the application of a layer of gas to it with a force - $F_x$ (at figure this layer of gas is protected). From Newton's second law it follows that the force - $F_x$ must correspond as in the case of position and must not be in position.

So we were convinced that both the flow into the gas layer of the molecule and the flow out of it are imparted to it by impulses, the projection of which is equal to $mv_x$.

Assuming for simplicity that the values ​​of $mv_x$ are the same for all molecules, it is easy to calculate the change in momentum $\Delta P$ of the entire gas layer over time $t$. It is obviously equal to twice the product of $mv_x$ by the number $z$ of molecules that form a layer of gas in time $t$:

$$\Delta P = 2zmv_x.$$
