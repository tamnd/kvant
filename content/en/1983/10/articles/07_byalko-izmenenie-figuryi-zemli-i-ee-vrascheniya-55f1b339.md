Changes in the Earth's shape and rotation

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. V. BYALKO

Two years ago, a message appeared in the “Reports of the Academy of Sciences” and immediately in the journal “Science and Life”, which made the hearts of physicists beat faster. As shown by measurements carried out by Soviet scientists at three points on the globe - in Pottsdame, at the Ledovo station near Moscow and Novosibirsk, strong rooting in all these points over time regularly decreases by values ​​of the order of $10^{-7}$ m/s² per year. Recently, the famous geophysicists Yu. D. Boulanger and N. N. Parisky confirmed this amazing fact. It turns out that a decrease in rooting does not at all mean that the mass of the Earth is decreasing or that gravitation is constant. The reason for everything is a change in the shape of the Earth. Let's try to explain this.

As is known, the rotation of the Earth around its axis leads to the fact that our planet is not an exact sphere, but slightly flattened at the stripes: its equatorial radius is approximately 21.4 km larger than the polar one. In addition, because of this rotation, the rooting of strong gravity (more often called the rooting of free fall), measured in earthly conditions, that is, in the rotating reporting system, is not a purely gravitational rooting, but represents the difference between gravitational and centripetal rooting: $g = GM/R^2 - \omega^2 R\cos\varphi$.

Here $G$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ is the mass of the Earth, $R$ is the distance to the center of the Earth, depends on the spherical ones (see figure), $\omega$ is the angular velocity of the Earth’s rotation, $\varphi$ is the geographical latitude of the place. Thus, the variable rooting changes with latitude: it is minimum at the equator and maximum at the poles.

Laplace, the author of the classic Treatise on Celestial Mechanics, suspected that the rotation of the Earth was not entirely uniform in time. Now we know several reasons for this. First, the rotation is slowed by ocean tides, which are caused by the attraction of the Moon and the Sun. The magnitude of this constant deceleration, called secular (in contrast to other, more short-term changes in angular velocity), was found using data on total solar eclipses in ancient times. It turned out to be very small.

Information has been preserved that, for example, one of the total solar eclipses was observed by the inhabitants of Babylon on April 15, 136 BC. e. If we make the appropriate calculation under the assumption that the speed of the Earth’s rotation is constant, then it turns out that on the indicated day there should indeed be an eclipse, but not in Babylon, but approximately $49^\circ$ to the west of it. However, the eclipse was observed precisely in Babylon, from which we can conclude that the angular change in the eclipse band was caused by a significant change in the angular velocity of the Earth’s rotation. For evaluation, we will assume that the angular velocity decreased uniformly, that is, with a constant angular rooting $\beta = \Delta\omega/t$. About 2100 years have passed since the eclipse, or $t = 6,7 \cdot 10^{10}$ s. By analogy with uniform-speed rectilinear motion, we write the formula for the dependence of the angular change $\alpha$ ($\alpha = 49^\circ \approx 0,85$ rad) on time: $\alpha = \beta t^2/2$. Hence the angular rooting, that is, quickly changing the angular velocity, $\beta = \Delta\omega/t \approx 3,8 \times 10^{-22} \text{ c}^{-2}$.
