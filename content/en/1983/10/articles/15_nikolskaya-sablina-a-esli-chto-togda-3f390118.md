And if...,
what then?

Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences
I. L. NIKOLSKAYA,
N. G. SABLINA

At the end of the last lesson, Tatyana Ivanovna, their class teacher, entered the class where Petya is studying.

“Guys,” she said, “I have good news for you: twenty students from the class will go to Moscow in the summer.”

“Hurray!” everyone shouted.
— Who will go to Moscow? - Petya asked.

“In order to go to Moscow, it is enough to finish the school year without C grades,” answered Tatyana Ivanovna.

Petya came home excited. Kata immediately noticed this and asked what was the matter. Petya told her about the trip to Moscow.

“Well,” Kata drawled, “you’ll get a C in Russian this year, and maybe also in mathematics.”

“Katka is right,” thought Petya. “I can still get a B in math this year, but in Russian... I’ll go see Mitya; he is a student, he has to come up with something.

Mitya listened attentively to Petya and advised him not to give up too soon.

“Yes,” Petya said sadly, “Tatyana Ivanovna, you know what she’s like: if she’s already said it, she’ll certainly do it.”

“Wait, let’s figure it out,” said Mitya. “What does the phrase “In order to go to Moscow, all you have to do is finish the school year without C grades?” The same thing can be said like this:

“If a student finishes the school year without C grades, he will go to Moscow.” This complex sentence is formed using a logical connective “if... then...” from two simple sentences: “The student will finish the school year without C grades” and “He will go to Moscow.” Let's denote the first of these sentences by the letter A, and the second by the letter B. However, it’s so easy - after all, the truth or falsity of these sentences depends on which student they apply to: one student can finish the school year with C grades, and another without C grades; one will go to Moscow, and the other will not

det. Thus, these phrases are sentences with a variable, not a statement; put it - did you read about this in Algebra 6? Let us denote them not by $A$ and $B$, but by $A(x)$ and $B(x)$, where $x$ can be any student in the class. Now, using the signs of mathematical logic, we will write Tatyana Ivanovna’s phrase like this:

$(\forall x)[A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)]$; (1)

The sign $\forall$ is used in mathematics together with the words “any”, “any”, “every”. Thus, Tatyana Ivanovna’s phrase in form (1) can be read as follows: “Any student who finishes the school year without tracks will be picked up in Moscow.” However, in the verbal formulation the words “any”, etc. are usually omitted - they are implied. That’s why we can say: “If a student finishes the school year without tracks, then he will be rejected in Moscow,” that is, $A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)$ (2)

Instead of “If $A(x)$, then $B(x)$” they also say “From $A(x)$ follows $B(x)$”. When do you think we can say that the teacher didn't keep her promise?

“If there is a student who finishes the year without a track, but in Moscow he doesn’t fail,” answered Petya.

— That’s right, for such a student the statement $A(x)$ is true, and $B(x)$ is false; therefore statement (2) is false. Now tell me in what cases Tatyana Ivanovna will fulfill her promise.

— If all students who completed the school year without tracks are taken to Moscow.

- Right. What should Tatyana Ivanovna do with students who have an annual “C”? If she does not take such a student in Moscow, she, of course, will not break her promise (thus, when $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ are false, $A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)$ is true). But, if Tatyana Ivanovna still takes such a student in Moscow, she will not break her promise either! Remember what she said: “In order to go to Moscow, it is enough to finish the school year without tracks.”

Enough, but not necessary! You can be different in some other matter. But you are our sport!

- What is this happening! - Petya exclaimed. — Let's apply $A(x)$ and true $B(x)$ again $A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)$ is true? This means that I still have a chance to go to Moscow.

“So,” Mitya continued, “statement (1) is false only in one case: if there is a $x$ for which $A(x)$ is true and $B(x)$ is false.” Let's record our “research” in a table:

| $A(x)$ | $B(x)$ | $A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| AND | AND | AND |
| AND | L | L |
| L | AND | AND |
| L | L | AND |

It is in this sense that the connective “If..., then...” is used in mathematics: the statement $A \Rightarrow B$ is false if and only if $A$ is true and $B$ is false; in the other three cases it is true. Our table precisely specifies the definition of the conjunction “If..., then...”. Take, for example, the statement “Every number $n$ divisible by 4 is even.” You, of course, understand that it has the form (1). In form (2) it would look like this: “If $n$ is divisible by 4, then it is even.” Do you think this is true?

“I see, that’s right,” answered Petya, “even a third grader knows that.”

- Well, then tell me, is the statement “If 18 is divisible by 4, then 18 is even” true?

“But 18 is not divisible by 4,” Petya doubted.

“Well, what’s not shared,” Mitya objected, “that’s not what I’m sharing!” After all, you yourself said that the statement “If $n$ is divisible by 4, then it is even” is true, that is, true for any value of $n$. And if so, then the statement “If 18 is divisible by 4, then 18 is even” (third row of the table), and the statement “If 17 is divisible by 4, then 17 is even” (fourth line of the table), and the statement “If 16 is divisible by 4, then 16 is even” (first line of the table) is true. There will be no “A(n) true, B(n) false” (second row of the table) for this statement for any value of n; that is why this statement is true. In other words, from the statement “The number n is divisible by 4” the statement “The number n is even” follows. What do you think, does the first of these statements follow from the second?

“No,” Petya said quickly, “after all, 2 is even, but not divisible by 4.”

“Well done,” friend Mitya congratulated him.

“Thunderbolt will strike me if I don’t correct the C in mathematics!” Petya shouted joyfully.

“Of course, you will correct the C in mathematics and the thunder will not strike you,” Mitya laughed. “Let’s hope that your exclamation is a true statement with false A and B. The wisest Kozma Prutkov loved to utter such true ones.” “If the shadows of objects did not depend on the size of these latter, but rather on their own arbitrary growth, then perhaps soon there would not be a single bright place left on the entire globe,” he used to say.

But let's return to mathematics. I see you understand well what “From A follows B” means. If each of two statements follows from the other, then such statements are called equivalent.

You know, my parents once told me, my sister Galya and my brother Kolya: “If we go to a rest home, then you will go to a camp.” At school they asked us where we would go in the summer. “If we go to the camp, then the parents will go to the rest home,” Galya answered. Kolya said, “If Mama and Mama don’t go to the rest home, then we won’t go to the camp.” “You got everything mixed up,” I said. “It was said: “If we don’t go to the camp, then the parents won’t go to the rest home.”

“Yes, but you also didn’t say it like your parents,” Petya noted.

“That’s right,” answered Mitya, “but one and the same thing can be said in different ways.” Let's make the table again. Let A stand for the statement “The parents will go to the holiday home”, and B stand for “We will go to the camp”. Then what the parents said will be written as “If A, then B,” Gali’s answer will be written as “If B, then A,” Kolya’s answer will be written as “If not A, then not B,” and my answer will be written as “If not B, then not A.”

Let's now make a table, let's look at the previous one. Since again each of the statements A, B can be true or false, it will again have 4 lines. To make it easier for us to count, we write after the columns for A and B the columns for “not A” and “not B” (“not A” is true when A is false, and false when A is true; similarly, “not B”). We simply rewrite the column for “if A, then B” from the previous table. And the columns for the other three statements are filled in according to the general rule: “L” is written if and only if the premise (what stands between “if” and “then”) is true, and the conclusion (what stands after “then”) is false. As a result, we get the following table:

| A | B | not A | not B | if A then B | if B then A | if not A, then not B | if not B, then not A |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| AND | AND | L | L | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND |
| AND | L | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND |
| AND | L | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND |

You see that the statement “If A, then B” (what the parents said) is only equivalent to the statement “If not B, then not A” (what I said). So only I correctly conveyed the meaning of what my parents said. Galya and Kolya said the same thing, but not like their parents.

“Ah,” Petya drawled, “that means I shouldn’t have been offended by Katya.” Mom told me: “If you go to the cinema this week, then do all your homework first,” and Katka said: “If you don’t do your homework, then you won’t go to the cinema.” It turns out they said the same thing!

“Of course,” Mitya smiled, “They both said that in order to go to the cinema, you must first do all your homework.”
