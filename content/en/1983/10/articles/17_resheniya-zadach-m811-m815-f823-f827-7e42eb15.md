F823. A meteorite flying directly to the planet (no straight line passing through the center of the planet) fell into an automatic space station, orbiting the planet in a circular orbit of radius R. As a result of the collision, the meteorite got stuck in the station, which moved to a new orbit with a minimum distance to the center of the planet R/2. Determine the speed of the meteorite before the collision. The mass of the station is 10 times the mass of the meteorite. The mass of the planet is M. The gravitational constant is G.

Let u be the speed of the meteorite, v₁ the speed of the station before the collision, v₂ the speed of the station and the meteorite immediately after the collision, m the mass of the meteorite, 10m the mass of the station.

Let us write down the equation of motion of the station before the collision:

$$10m \frac{v_1^2}{R} = G \frac{10mM}{R^2}.$$

From here we find the speed v₁:

$$v_1 = \sqrt{G \frac{M}{R}}.$$

According to the law of conservation of velocity momentum $\vec{u}, \vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ are related by the relation

$$m\vec{u} + 10m\vec{v}_1 = 11m\vec{v}_2.$$

Let's write the law of conservation of momentum in projections on the X and Y axes (see figure):

$$10mv_1 = 11mv_2x,$$

$$mu = 11mv_2y.$$

After the collision, the station goes into an elliptical orbit. The mechanical energy of the station with the meteorite in it while moving along an elliptical orbit remains constant. Hence,

$$-G \frac{11mM}{R} + \frac{11m}{2}(v_2^2 + v_1^2) = -G \frac{11mM}{R/2} + \frac{11m}{2}V^2,$$

where V is the speed of the station at the moment of greatest approach to the planet. According to Kepler's second law, the speed V and the speed V₂ of the station after the collision are related by the relation

$$VR/2 = v_2xR.$$

Solving equations (1)–(4) together and taking into account that $v_1 = \sqrt{G \frac{M}{R}}$, we find the speed of the meteorite u:

$$u = \sqrt{58G \frac{M}{R}}.$$

A. I. Buzdin

F827. A cube with an edge of length a moves at a speed v = 0.8 s (c is the speed of light) in a direction perpendicular to one of the faces of the cube. What happens in a photograph of a cube taken by a remote camera with a very short exposure time if the optical axis of the camera is perpendicular to the face of the cube and intersects the trajectory of its center (Fig. 1)?

Let us mentally reverse the process of photographing in time: let the cube move at a speed of $-\vec{v}$, at some moment the camera emits a light flash that reaches the cube at the time when it crosses the optical axis. The points of intersection of the wave front with a point on the cube will be those points whose images are obtained in the photograph (this is due to the fact that light travels different distances from different parts of the cube to the camera, and therefore in different times).

Near the cube, the wave can be considered flat (since the camera is removed from the cube). Light travels the distance a from the nearest to the far edge of the cube in time $\tau \approx a/c$.

Fig. 2.

In the photograph, the images of the close and distant edges of the cube will be mixed in the direction of movement of the cube at a distance s=vτ=0.8a relative to each other. The cube in the photograph will appear to be surfaced at an angle of $\varphi = \arcsin \frac{v}{a}$ (Fig. 2).
