At the beginning of 1783, Princess Ekaterina Romanovna Dashkova, who 20 years earlier had been the closest associate of Catherine II in the days of her accession to the Russian throne, was appointed director of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences. Known for her inventiveness, the princess comes up with a sure-fire move that should convince the academician of her commitment to science. She persuades the deceased Euler, who had long been at odds with the academic authorities and did not attend academic conferences, to accompany her on her first visit to the Academy. Blind Euler appears accompanied by his son and grandson. Dashkova recalled in remembrance: “I told them that I asked Euler to join me in the ambush, because, despite my own ignorance, I believe that by such an act I solemnly testify to my respect for science and enlightenment.”

And just a few months later, in the minutes of the Academy it was written: “At the conference meeting on September 11, 1783, Academician N. I. Fuss took on the duties of secretary *), absent due to the death of his famous father, Mr. Leonhard Euler, who died of an applix stroke on September 7 at 11 pm, at the age of 76 years, 5 months and 3 days, having completed his long and brilliant journey and who made his immortal name known throughout Europe.”

Euler's exceptional personality and his irresistible role in the history of the Academy forced us to look for unconventional ways to honor his memory. On October 23, Academician N. I. Fuss, Euler’s student and the husband of his grandchildren, delivered a “Laudatory speech.” The academicians decided, at their own expense, to make a bust of the “Immortal Euler, equally worthy of admiration for his genius and his merits,” and their “illustrious leader” (Dashkova) “added to this a magnificent column, which serves as the base of this bust”; First, the bost was installed in the library, and then - opposite the presidential cross in the meeting room (and remained in the library

The service of honoring the scientist spread further beyond the borders of Russia. The Permanent Secretary of the French Academy of Sciences, the Marquis Condorcet (less than 10 years later, he would take part in the revolution and his name would be deleted from the lists of the St. Petersburg Academy for “reprehensible behavior... against the sovereign”) said in his “Eulogy”: “So, the people who at the beginning of this century received the Zavarars are now setting an example for civilized Europe - how to celebrate great people during their lifetime and respect their memory in their lifetime.” death: and in this case, other nationalities come to realize that they not only could not warn Russia in this regard, but are not even able to imitate.” Although in distant Paris the situation in St. Petersburg seemed more prosperous than it actually was, the attitude towards science over the 60 years of the existence of the St. Petersburg Academy became unrecognizable.

The first years of the Academy

Peter I thought about organizing an Academy in Russia in the last years of the 17th century. Beginning in 1711, he discussed his plans with Leibniz three times and even enlisted the latter in Russian service.

Leibnitz did not consider the lack of science in Russia to be an obstacle to the creation of the Academy and even to find some advantages in this. However, few people in Russia shared this optimism. There were many difficulties along the way of the project, but in 1724 the Senate decided to create the Academy of Sciences. At this time, even the word “science” did not yet exist in the Russian language and the Academy was called “Di Sciences Academy”.

In 1725, Peter died without waiting for the opening of the Academy. Catherine I, not without hesitation, carried out her husband’s plan, although she did not share his interest in science (as a contemporary writes, “the laudable recipes of scientists were incomprehensible to Her Majesty”). The fate of the Academy was always hanging in the balance. It was perceived as an exclusively German phenomenon, and the Russian party, in particular Menshikov, was opposed to it. The academicians tried to establish contacts with the Russian public, who well understood the functions of the Academy. Twice a week its doors opened to visitors. Sometimes you could see something amazing there.

On February 24, 1729, “Professor Leitman managed to change the image of the state coat of arms (using prizes) into a portrait of the reigning emperor.” The academies have somewhat established themselves with their success in organizing “amusing lights” and illuminations, in combination with solemn od. High-quality materials were not held in high esteem, except when drawing up “land maps” and some recommendations to seafarers.

In 1727, Euler attempted to occupy the chair of physics in Basel, which was doomed to failure. Meanwhile, he successfully participates in a competition of the French Academy of Sciences on the best way to arrange masts on a ship. It is noteworthy that “in mountainous Switzerland, from which Euler had never left before, he, of course, had the opportunity to see the ship only in pictures...” (Academician A. N. Krylov).

Daniil Bernoulli fulfilled the promise he made when leaving for St. Petersburg: even before Euler tried to get a job in Basel, he learned about the opportunity to get a position as an adjunct in physiology with a salary of 200 rubles. Bernoulli hurries, recommends to “exit this winter.” Euler was not embarrassed that he had to practice medicine. In those years, medicine was not perceived as a science, far from mathematics. It’s not far to look for examples: his teacher I. Bernoulli combined mathematics with medical practice (as, indeed, with teaching Greek). Euler begins to study anatomy and physiology; Later he surprised those around him with his medical skills. The departure did not succeed as quickly as D. Bernoulli wanted, but in the spring of 1727 Euler received “thirty rubles in a bill of exchange for travel money” and left for Russia. He arrived in St. Petersburg on the day of the death of Catherine I.

In his first Petersburg years, Euler hardly thought that his life would be so firmly connected with the Academy. The very continued existence of the Academy seemed extremely problematic at that time. Then N.I. Fuss will write: “Euler was an adornment to our weak Academy for fifty years. Before his eyes, she began to exist, died several times and was restored.” Euler felt very uncomfortable when the death of the Academy seemed real to him. At one of the most difficult moments, when, after the death of Peter II in 1730, a mass exodus of academicians from Russia began, Euler was clearly negotiating to enter the naval service. But it wasn't necessary. On the contrary, a vacancy became available allowing Euler to take the position of professor (academician) in the physics department (albeit with a relatively low salary of 400 rubles). And two years later, D. Bernoulli left Russia and Euler took over his chair of mathematics (although his salary - 600 rubles - was only half the salary that D. Bernoulli received in this place).

Over the years, Euler became a prominent figure in the Academy. Most academicians were not too zealous about their responsibilities, which, moreover, had not yet been clearly defined. Euler did not neglect any assignments: he constantly made reports at academic conferences, gave public lectures, wrote a textbook on arithmetic for academic hypnasia and popular science articles for the “Notes” to the “St. Petersburg Gazette”, he conducted various examinations on the study of fire pumps, scales, “saw machines” and magnets. Euler detailed numerous technical projects. Looking ahead, we can recall Euler's research on hydraulic turbines and conclusions on the designs of places across the Neva, including the single-arch wooden place of I.P. Kulibin, who worked at the Academy as a mechanic.

Beginning in 1733, Euler participated in the “examination” of maps, and gradually participation in cartographic activities came to the fore among his academic duties. The question arises of drawing up a general map of Russia on the basis of already compiled provincial maps, and Euler proposes his project, accompanying it with the words: “I am sure that Russian geography, through my and Professor Gensinus’s works, has brought the city into a better condition than the geography of the German land.” Sharp disagreements with academician Delius led Euler to the decision in 1840 to stop cartography. So, Euler is in Berlin. Gradually he becomes involved in Berlin life. The assignment here is no different than in St. Petersburg: he recommends books on ballistics to the king and himself publishes three volumes of works on this topic, examines the leveling of the canal between Havel and the Oder and the state of affairs in the salt mines, participates in the organization of state lotteries and the reform of cash registers, and gives feedback on many projects. Everyone quickly realized that he could do a variety of things well and did not refuse anything. After the organization of the Berlin Academy of Sciences (1744), Euler became director of its mathematical department.

However, relations with the king did not work out in the best way. It is significant that Euler's salary was half the salary of the president of the Maupertius Academy. Euler rarely received a royal reap.

In Berlin, it was believed that the duties of scientists include serving in living rooms and delighting with pleasant conversation. The French scientists Maupertius and Darjean mastered this art brilliantly, but Euler did not. In 1746, Friedrich’s brother August-Wilhelm met Euler, and he shared his impressions with the king: “...Mr. Maupertius introduced me to the mathematician Euler. I found it to affirm the truth that all things are imperfect. Thanks to diligence, he developed logical thinking and thereby acquired a name, but his appearance and impossible manner of expression obscure all his wonderful qualities and prevent him from enjoying them.”

Euler divided his time between science and home, but he did not belong to the category of scientists, was not interested in external events and avoided making promises with people. His scientific knowledge was encyclopedic, he knew a lot about rich man, chimini, anatomy, medicine, a good knowledge of “ancient” and “oriental”, and spoke Russian. After his death, they recalled that he knew well “the best writers of the ancient world,” “ancient literature on mathematics,” and “the history of all times and peoples.” N. I. Fuss wrote in his memoirs that Euler knew to study “Enenda”, and he noted with what verse each page of his copy began and ended. After the end of the Seven Years' War (1763), Euler was still thinking decisively about returning to Russia. In 1746 and 1750 he had already received an invitation through the representative of the Academy, Count K. G. Razumovsky, but then he politely postponed the decision for an indefinite period.

With the rise of Catherine II in Russia (1762), negotiations on the move resumed with renewed vigor. Catherine II really wanted to get Eilar. Euler almost left for Russia in 1763, but still refused to move. However, two years later, Euler aroused the king’s anger by interceding for the academic treasurer during an audit.

Euler informs Russia of his conditions: salary of 3,000 rubles. (a representative received such a salary; an academician’s salary usually did not exceed 1,200 rubles), a place as an academician in physics for his son Johann Albrest, a suitable place for other sons (an artilleryman and a doctor), an apartment free of soldiers’ quarters, and, finally, the establishment for him of the post of vice president with the appropriate rank. Once upon a time, Friedrich refused to give Euler the vacant post of chairman of the Berlin Academy. Now Euler wanted to at least partially implement his pure plans in St. Petersburg (he did not apply for the position of chairman, believing that in Russia it should be filled by a nobleman). On January 6, 1766, Catherine writes to Chancellor Count Vorontsov: “Mr. Eilar’s ​​letter to you brought me more convenience, because I learned from it about his desire to re-enter my service. Of course, I find it perfect for the desired title of Vice-President of the Academy of Sciences, but for this some measures must be taken before I establish this title - I say I will establish it, since its bottom did not exist. In the current state of affairs, there is no money for a desire of 3,000 rubles, but for a person with such merits as Mr. Euler, I will add to the academic desire from state income, which together will amount to the required 3,000 rubles. He will have a government card and not the slightest shadow of sweets. ...I am confident that my Academy will be reborn from the ashes of such an important acquisition, and I congratulate myself in advance for returning a great man to Russia.” Having learned of Eilar’s ​​desire to take part in the restructuring of the Academy, the Empress promises “not to make any changes at the Academy before his arrival, in order to better negotiate with him about improvement...”. With great diplomatic skill, Euler is denied a rank: “I would give him a rank when he wants, if I were not afraid that this rank would compare him with many people who are not worthy of Mr. Eilar. Since his fame is the best way to show him due respect.” Euler probably quickly realized that the generous empress had to clearly explain the boundaries of what was permitted, agreed to all the conditions and decided to “end his days in the service of this unjust sovereign.”

Back in Russia

Euler arrived in St. Petersburg on July 17, 1766. He had been gone for exactly 25 years and was approaching his sixtieth birthday. At first, Euler always accepted Catherine's offer to take part in the reorganization of the Academy. He brought with him a detailed project, and he did not strive for the autonomy of the Academy, but, for example, focused on the intertwining of the activities of the Academy and development institutions. However, it gradually became clear that the Empress was not inclined to entrust Eulerr with the leadership of the Academy. Euler learned another lesson that enlightened monarchs like to be taught their place.

Euler brought with him to St. Petersburg a pile of manuscripts that could not be published in Berlin due to the almost ceased publishing activity during the war. But he brought even more ideas into his head that were almost ripe, but not realized. And life told the scientist that he had to bargain. Soon after his arrival, he only loses sight in his second eye, but does not stop working, discussing his works to a boy who does not have the slightest idea about mathematics. The ophthalmologist Baron Wentzel, invited by the empress, removed a cataract in one eye, but warned that overload would inevitably lead to the return of blindness. So it was an accident soon, or Euler preferred loss of vision to passivity. He tried to involve other scientists in the work: his son, academicians Kraft, Fuss and Leksel, but most of all, what was interesting was what he knew and wanted to tell people. Over the course of a decade and a half, he produced more than 400 articles and 10 large books. Deafness began to creep in. In 1766, his wife died and Euler married her sister (this was the easiest way to maintain order and accept the house). The house and most of the property burned down.
