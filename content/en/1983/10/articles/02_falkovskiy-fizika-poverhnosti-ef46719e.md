What does the surface of a solid look like? The answer to this question, please, is very clear: the type of surface depends on the method of its preparation. If you take a crystal and walk along its edge with sandpaper, the picture under a microscope will resemble the view of mountain ranges from an airplane window. And before processing with sandpaper, the surface of a good crystal seems to us to be ideally robust, mirror-like. In reality, there are nerves on such a surface, and its mirror image is only that the size of the nerves does not exceed the wavelength of visible light - our instrument. Light does not “see” defects whose size is relative to its wavelength. Just as we do not notice the nervousness on the road that is in comparison with our step. How to detect these defects? What will we see on the surface with higher magnification? In order to answer this question, various and powerful research methods have now been developed. But before we talk about them, a few words about why detailed information about the structure of the surface is needed.

At first glance, it may seem surprising that in our space research and elementary particle physics, the “nearby” field of science - physics, chemistry and surface mechanics - is experiencing rapid development. This is dictated, of course, by the demands of new technology. The behavior of small-sized elements used in microelectronics is largely determined by the properties of their surface. The problem of plasma confinement requires clarification of the mechanism of particle interaction and study with the boundaries of the volume where the plasma is located. The design of vacuum pumps, as well as the separation and purification of gases, faces the problem of adsorption, that is, the absorption of gas by a branched surface of a solid. On the influence of the surface on the properties of conductors

It is well known that the resistance of a wiring is determined by its length $L$ and diameter $d$:

$$R = \rho L / S = 4 \rho L / \pi d^2.$$

The question that interests us is the following: can we assume that the resistivity $Q$ depends only on the wiring material and does not depend, for example, on the diameter $d$? We now see that to answer this question it is important to know the nature of the reflection of conduction electrons from the inner surface of the conductor.

Let the electron be reflected specularly, that is, the projection of its momentum parallel to the surface, as well as the energy do not change upon reflection (figure 4, a). This means that the presence of a surface has no effect on the motion of an electron in a direction parallel to the surface. We consider the electric field, as is usually the case, to be directed along the surface. Therefore, the resistivity $Q$ also necessarily - from the “mirror” inner surface – must coincide with the resistivity of the unconfined sample. This resistance depends on the concentration of free electrons and on the characteristics of the free electrons and on the characteristics of motion within the conductor. As a simple calculation shows (we will do it in the Appendix at the end of the article), the resistivity of an unbounded conductor is equal to

$$\varrho_0 = m / e^2 n \tau,$$

(1)

where $m, e$ is the mass and charge of the electron, $n$ is the concentration of electrons in the region

$$\varrho_0 = \rho_0 \left( 1 + q \frac{l}{d} \right)$$

(2)

(We will also derive this formula in the Appendix). Here $l$ is the average free electron length, that is, the average distance traveled by an electron between two successive collisions with defects, and $q$ is a value determined by the nature of the collision of the electron with the surface. The values ​​of $q$ lie in the interval between 0 and 1. With purely specular reflection $q = 0$, and we arrive at the previous formula (1). The relative correction to it, that is, the value $\varrho_0 = 1 + q l / d$, contains the ratio of the length of the free gap $l$ to the thickness of the sample $d$. This is quite natural, since the electron “mirrors” the surface at a distance of $l$ from it, and the current flows across the entire cross section of the sample.

For only enough samples, that is, at $d \gg q l$, formula (2) practically coincides with formula (1). In the opposite limiting case, when $d \ll q l$, the resistivity is determined only by the diameter $d$ and does not depend at all on the length of the free gap $l$. This happens because the electron often collides with the surfaces of the sample and is diffusely reflected from them. In this case, the role of the free gap length is played by the diameter of the sample.

Dependence (2) is often observed in experiments, but deviations from it are also known. The situation deserves special attention when the length of the free space $l$ is very large compared to the thickness $d$, and the reflection is close to specular. One should not think that an almost mirror image is not real. In the limiting case of an almost grazing incidence, that is, when the angle between the direction of motion and the surface tends to zero, the reflection is necessarily specular. Everyone is familiar with this phenomenon: if you look at an asphalt highway from a small enough angle, for example, while going up a mountain, then it will sparkle like a mirror at the moment when the line of vision passes tangentially to the highway.

Thus, we can, somewhat arbitrarily, divide electrons into two groups: sliding, which are reflected almost specularly, and the rest, reflecting diffusion. Each of these groups contributes to the resistance of the conductor.

Now we understand that the conditions of reflection from a surface are determined by the state of the surface itself and the angles at which the electrons approach the surface. The value $q$ in formula (2), which characterizes the interaction of electrons with the surface, at small sliding angles $\theta$ (at angles of incidence close to $\pi/2$) can be represented as $q = Q\theta$, where $Q$ are some coefficients determined by the quality of the surface. (This formula for $q$ most simply satisfies the requirement $q \rightarrow 0$ for $\theta \rightarrow 0$, but it can also be obtained by simple calculations.)

As calculations show, the contribution made by sliding electrons to the resistance of a thin plate of thickness $d$ turns out to be equal to

$$\varrho_{\text{ск}} = \varrho_0 \sqrt{Ql/d}$$

(3)

(for thin wire the formula is different). The contribution of the remaining electrons reflected by diffusion gives the second terms in formula (2), where $q$ corresponds to large grazing angles ($\Theta \approx \pi/2$). For sufficiently small thicknesses, values ​​(3) are less than this second term (it is easy to see that for the smallest $x \sim \sqrt{d}/l \ll 1$ the inequality $x \gg x^2$ holds). The total resistance of the sample is calculated as the resistance of two conductors connected in parallel.

Application

Consider a conductor of length $L$ with a flat cross-section $S$, to which a voltage $U$ is applied. The electric field of the inner conductor is $E = U/L$.

In the absence of a field, the electrons in the conductor moved randomly and their average speed was zero. As a result of the action of the electron field, an average speed $\bar{u}$ (the so-called drift speed) appears from zero. This speed can be estimated by assuming that it is completely lost when an electron collides with a salt atom, with a defect, or with a used atom. If the average time between two collisions of an electron is $t$, then at the moment of collision the average speed of the electron is equal to

$$\bar{u} \approx a\tau = \frac{eE}{m}\tau = \frac{eU}{mL}\tau$$

where $a$ is the acceleration acquired by the electron in the field $E$.
