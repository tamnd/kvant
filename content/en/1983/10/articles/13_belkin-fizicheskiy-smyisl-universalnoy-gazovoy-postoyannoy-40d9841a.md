$$\Delta P = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} n S v_x t \cdot m v_x = n S m v_x^2 t.$$

So we have found where the impulse from our layer comes from, directed along the $X$ axis and equal to $F_x t$. So, $F_x t = n S m v_x^2 t$.

Reducing by $t$ and dividing both sides of this equality by $S$, we obtain an expression for the gas pressure on the right wall of the vessel:

$$p = \frac{F_x}{S} = n m v_x^2.$$

(1)

Repeating our reasoning for walls perpendicular to the $Y$ and $Z$ axes, we obtain

$$p = n m v_y^2,$$

(2)

$$p = n m v_z^2.$$

(3)

The pressures $p$ on all walls are, of course, the same. Adding equalities (1), (2) and (3) term by term, we obtain

$$3 p = n m \left( v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2 \right),$$

or

$$p = \frac{1}{3} n m \left( v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2 \right).$$

(4)

In fact, the values ​​of $v_x$, $v_y$ and $v_z$ are different for different molecules (due to the randomness of their motion). Therefore, on the right side of equality (4) it is necessary to substitute their average values ​​$\bar{v}_x^2$, $\bar{v}_y^2$ and $\bar{v}_z^2$ instead of $v_x^2$, $v_y^2$ and $v_z^2$. The sum of $\bar{v}_x^2 + \bar{v}_y^2 + \bar{v}_z^2$ is equal to $\bar{v}^2$, then finally

$$p = \frac{1}{3} n m \bar{v}^2 = \frac{2}{3} n \frac{m \bar{v}^2}{2}.$$

Physical meaning of the universal gas constant

The equation of state of an ideal gas (Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation)

$$p V = \frac{m}{M} RT$$

includes the universal gas constant $R$. It is known to be equal to the product of two other constants—Avogadro’s number $N_A$ and the constant $k$:

$$R = N_A k.$$

The constants $N_A$ and $k$ have a clear meaning: $N_A$ is the number of molecules or atoms in a unit of substances, that is, mole; $k$ is a constant that defines the relationship between temperature in kelvins and temperature in energy units. But the combination of $N_A$ and $k$—the constant $R$—has a certain physical meaning (perhaps it will seem unexpected to many).

Let us imagine that 1 mole of an ideal gas is in a vessel with a movable piston and that by supplying a body to it or removing a body from it, its temperature is changed by 1 kelvin. Due to the fact that the vessel is closed by a movable piston, the gas pressure will remain constant (and equal to the external pressure).

Let us write the equation of state of the gas before and after heating:

before heating $p V_1 = RT$,

after heating $p V_2 = R (T + \Delta T)$, where $\Delta T = 1$ K. Subtracting the first equality from the second, we get

$$p \left( V_2 - V_1 \right) = RT + R \Delta T - RT = R \Delta T.$$

The left side of this equation represents the work done by the gas pressure force (or an external force against the pressure force) when, at constant pressure $p$, the gas volume increases (or decreases) from $V_1$ to $V_2$. Therefore, the gas constant $R$ is equal to the work done by 1 mole of an ideal gas, expanding when heated by 1 K at constant pressure.

Of course, with isobaric cooling of 1 mole of gas by 1 K, the external force acting on the piston is also equal to perfection.
