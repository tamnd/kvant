So, Euler is in Berlin. Gradually he becomes involved in Berlin life. The assignment here is no different than in St. Petersburg: he recommends books on ballistics to the king and himself publishes three volumes of works on this topic, examines the leveling of the canal between Havel and the Oder and the state of affairs in the salt mines, participates in the organization of state lotteries and the reform of cash registers, and gives feedback on many projects. Everyone quickly realized that he could do a variety of things well and did not refuse anything. After the organization of the Berlin Academy of Sciences (1744), Euler became director of its mathematical department.

However, relations with the king did not work out in the best way. It is significant that Euler's salary was half the salary of the president of the Maupertius Academy. Euler rarely received a royal reap.

In Berlin, it was believed that the duties of scientists include serving in living rooms and delighting with pleasant conversation. The French scientists Maupertius and Darjean mastered this art brilliantly, but Euler did not. In 1746, Friedrich’s brother August-Wilhelm met Euler, and he shared his impressions with the king: “...Mr. Maupertius introduced me to the mathematician Euler. I found it to affirm the truth that all things are imperfect. Thanks to diligence, he developed logical thinking and thereby acquired a name, but his appearance and impossible manner of expression obscure all his wonderful qualities and prevent him from enjoying them.”

Euler divided his time between science and home, but he did not belong to the category of scientists, was not interested in external events and avoided making promises with people. His scientific knowledge was encyclopedic, he knew a lot about rich man, chimini, anatomy, medicine, a good knowledge of “ancient” and “oriental”, and spoke Russian. After his death, they recalled that he knew well “the best writers of the ancient world,” “ancient literature on mathematics,” and “the history of all times and peoples.” N. I. Fuss wrote in his memoirs that Euler knew to study “Enenda”, and he noted with what verse each page of his copy began and ended.
