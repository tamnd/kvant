No new forces act on the person standing in the back (for simplicity, we will assume that the friction forces are small), and the person begins to move relative to the truck with acceleration. It is clear that it is impossible to simply accept Newton’s laws in this system: there is acceleration, but no forces. That is why it is necessary to introduce fictitious forces - inertial forces.

Another example of a non-inertial reporting system, which we will examine in more detail, is a moving accelerated elevator. When the elevator moves upward, you feel “heaviness” during acceleration and “lightening” during sudden braking. If you use Newton's laws in the elevator system, then this cannot be understood. Indeed, the body is acted upon by gravity forces $m\vec{g}$ and reaction forces $\vec{N}$. Since the person will die, the reaction force must be equal in magnitude to the force of gravity. But from experience it is clear that this is not so. Therefore (if you count in an elevator), you need to add some fictitious force to the force of gravity when accelerating the elevator and subtract it when separating. This is the force of inertia.

What is the force of inertia? In order to find out this, we first find the reaction force acting on a person when accelerating and decelerating the elevator in a stationary reporting system. Newton's second law is valid for such a system

$$\pm ma = N - mg,$$

where the “+” sign corresponds to the acceleration of the elevator (acceleration is directed upward), and the “-” sign refers to the case of its deceleration (acceleration is directed downward). Hence the reaction force

$$N = mg \pm ma.$$

In a system connected to an elevator, the reaction force must be the same, so it is necessary to add the inertial force to the force of gravity when accelerating the elevator and subtract it when decelerating. From the last equality we obtain that the inertial force acting on a person in the elevator is equal to

$$\vec{F}_n = -m\vec{a}.$$

Now everything is in order: when the elevator accelerates, inertia seems to make the body heavier, and when it slows down, on the contrary, it lightens it (Fig. 2). Please note that physical inertia is similar in appearance to gravity: both are proportional to the mass of the body.

Sometimes you hear, for example, that light inertia throws passengers back when the train accelerates and only them forward when it slows down, that is, they talk about inertial forces as ordinary forces. In this case, it is necessary to add that the movement is considered relative to the accelerated moving reporting system (in this case, relative to the train). But this system should not forget about the origin of inertial forces. These forces are fundamentally different from ordinary forces: they are not related to the interaction of bodies. This is why they are often called fictitious forces or pseudo forces. The inertial force is introduced artificially in order to describe the change in the acceleration of a body in a non-inertial reporting system compared to an inertial one for the same real forces driving the body.
