Another interesting feature of parabolas is that it makes it easy to determine the speed of a body’s movement. It is known that if the motion graph is represented by a straight line (rectilinear uniform motion), then the speed is determined by the angle of inclination of the straight line to the $t$ axis. The graph of rectilinear uneven movement is always a curve. If we select a section on this curve corresponding to the time interval $\Delta t$ and the change in coordinate $\Delta x$ (Fig. 4), then the ratio $\Delta x/\Delta t$ will be equal to the average speed for time $\Delta t$. As can be seen from figure 4, it is determined by the angle of inclination of the chord,

If we infinitely reduce the time interval $\Delta t$, directing it to zero, then the chord will become tangent to the curve at the point corresponding to the moment of time $t$, and the angle of its inclination instantly determines the speed at this moment of time. Thus, in order to find the instantaneous speed of a motion graph at some point in time, you need to check the tangent to the graph at the point corresponding to this moment in time; the tangent may or may not be drawn. This feature of paraboles (square trinomials) is associated with the obstruction that in rectilinear equilibrium motion, the speed for any period of time is equal to the resulting initial and final speed. Therefore, in order to use a parabola to instantly find the speed at some point in time, you need to perform a period of time for which the moment of interest to us serves as the middle. Then the average speed for this interval turns out to be equal to the instantaneous speed in the middle of the interval. And to find the average speed, you need to check the chord, not the tangent, which is not very difficult.
