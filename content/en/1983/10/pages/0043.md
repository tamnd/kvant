⟦folio 41⟧

There will be no “A(n) true, B(n) false” (second row of the table) for this statement for any value of n; that is why this statement is true. In other words, from the statement “The number n is divisible by 4” the statement “The number n is even” follows. What do you think, does the first of these statements follow from the second?

“No,” Petya said quickly, “after all, 2 is even, but not divisible by 4.”

“Well done,” friend Mitya congratulated him.

“Thunderbolt will strike me if I don’t correct the C in mathematics!” Petya shouted joyfully.

“Of course, you will correct the C in mathematics and the thunder will not strike you,” Mitya laughed. “Let’s hope that your exclamation is a true statement with false A and B. The wisest Kozma Prutkov loved to utter such true ones.” “If the shadows of objects did not depend on the size of these latter, but rather on their own arbitrary growth, then perhaps soon there would not be a single bright place left on the entire globe,” he used to say.

But let's return to mathematics. I see you understand well what “From A follows B” means. If each of two statements follows from the other, then such statements are called equivalent.

You know, my parents once told me, my sister Galya and my brother Kolya: “If we go to a rest home, then you will go to a camp.” At school they asked us where we would go in the summer. “If we go to the camp, then the parents will go to the rest home,” Galya answered. Kolya said, “If Mama and Mama don’t go to the rest home, then we won’t go to the camp.” “You got everything mixed up,” I said. “It was said: “If we don’t go to the camp, then the parents won’t go to the rest home.”

“Yes, but you also didn’t say it like your parents,” Petya noted.

“That’s right,” answered Mitya, “but one and the same thing can be said in different ways.” Let's make the table again. Let A stand for the statement “The parents will go to the holiday home”, and B stand for “We will go to the camp”. Then what the parents said will be written as “If A, then B,” Gali’s answer will be written as “If B, then A,” Kolya’s answer will be written as “If not A, then not B,” and my answer will be written as “If not B, then not A.”

Let's now make a table, let's look at the previous one. Since again each of the statements A, B can be true or false, it will again have 4 lines. To make it easier for us to count, we write after the columns for A and B the columns for “not A” and “not B” (“not A” is true when A is false, and false when A is true; similarly, “not B”). We simply rewrite the column for “if A, then B” from the previous table. And the columns for the other three statements are filled in according to the general rule: “L” is written if and only if the premise (what stands between “if” and “then”) is true, and the conclusion (what stands after “then”) is false. As a result, we get the following table:

| A | B | not A | not B | if A then B | if B then A | if not A, then not B | if not B, then not A |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| AND | AND | L | L | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND |
| AND | L | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND |
| AND | L | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND | AND |

You see that the statement “If A, then B” (what the parents said) is only equivalent to the statement “If not B, then not A” (what I said). So only I correctly conveyed the meaning of what my parents said. Galya and Kolya said the same thing, but not like their parents.

“Ah,” Petya drawled, “that means I shouldn’t have been offended by Katya.” Mom told me: “If you go to the cinema this week, then do all your homework first,” and Katka said: “If you don’t do your homework, then you won’t go to the cinema.” It turns out they said the same thing!

“Of course,” Mitya smiled, “They both said that in order to go to the cinema, you must first do all your homework.”
