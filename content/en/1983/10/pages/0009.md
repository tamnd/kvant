The situation deserves special attention when the length of the free space $l$ is very large compared to the thickness $d$, and the reflection is close to specular. One should not think that an almost mirror image is not real. In the limiting case of an almost grazing incidence, that is, when the angle between the direction of motion and the surface tends to zero, the reflection is necessarily specular. Everyone is familiar with this phenomenon: if you look at an asphalt highway from a small enough angle, for example, while going up a mountain, then it will sparkle like a mirror at the moment when the line of vision passes tangentially to the highway.

Thus, we can, somewhat arbitrarily, divide electrons into two groups: sliding, which are reflected almost specularly, and the rest, reflecting diffusion. Each of these groups contributes to the resistance of the conductor.

Now we understand that the conditions of reflection from a surface are determined by the state of the surface itself and the angles at which the electrons approach the surface. The value $q$ in formula (2), which characterizes the interaction of electrons with the surface, at small sliding angles $\theta$ (at angles of incidence close to $\pi/2$) can be represented as $q = Q\theta$, where $Q$ are some coefficients determined by the quality of the surface. (This formula for $q$ most simply satisfies the requirement $q \rightarrow 0$ for $\theta \rightarrow 0$, but it can also be obtained by simple calculations.)

As calculations show, the contribution made by sliding electrons to the resistance of a thin plate of thickness $d$ turns out to be equal to

Application

Consider a conductor of length $L$ with a flat cross-section $S$, to which a voltage $U$ is applied. The electric field of the inner conductor is $E = U/L$.

In the absence of a field, the electrons in the conductor moved randomly and their average speed was zero. As a result of the action of the electron field, an average speed $\bar{u}$ (the so-called drift speed) appears from zero. This speed can be estimated by assuming that it is completely lost when an electron collides with a salt atom, with a defect, or with a used atom. If the average time between two collisions of an electron is $t$, then at the moment of collision the average speed of the electron is equal to

$$\bar{u} \approx a\tau = \frac{eE}{m}\tau = \frac{eU}{mL}\tau$$

where $a$ is the acceleration acquired by the electron in the field $E$.
