Centrifugal forces of inertia are encountered in many practical problems. At the same time, when making calculations, they often forget about their origin and treat them as ordinary forces. For example, it is believed that the centrifugal force of inertia exists more in centrifuges, separates isotopes, and helps train astronauts; it can also destroy the turbine rotor and cause wheel runout during flat alignment. Let us note once again that such statements make sense only in a rotating reporting system. But even there we must remember that the force of inertia is a fictitious force that is introduced in order to describe the change in the acceleration of the body caused by the rotation of the reporting system. All the described phenomena can be binding by considering them in an inertial system, where Newton’s laws are valid. True, the obligation there will be more difficult.

Korpolis power

Now let’s discuss the case when a person not only rides on a carousel, but also decides to move from one chair to another (see Fig. 3), that is, begins to move in a circle with a certain speed $v_0$ in the carousel system, for example, in the direction of rotation. (The experiment is purely mental, since this is strictly forbidden by the authorities.) It turns out that then it is necessary to introduce another inertia, also directed from the center of rotation.

Let us first consider the movement of a person relative to the Earth. The total speed of movement $v$ is the sum of the linear speed of the carousel $\omega r$ and the speed of relative movement $v_0$:

$$v = \omega r + v_0.$$

Centripetal acceleration is determined by the well-known formula

$$a_u = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{v_0^2}{r} + \omega^2 r + 2v_0\omega.$$

According to Newton's second law

$$ma_u = Q,$$

where $Q$ is the reaction force acting on a person from the side of the carousel.

Now let's look at the same movement in a carousel system. There the speed is $v_0$, and the centripetal acceleration is $a_u' = v_0^2/r$. Using the previous two equalities, we can write

$$ma_u' = \frac{mv_0^2}{r} = Q - m\omega^2 r - 2mv_0\omega.$$

If we want to use Newton's law in a rotating system, we must control the inertial forces

$$F_{ин} = -(m\omega^2 r + 2mv_0\omega),$$

where the sign “—” indicates that this force is directed from the center of rotation. The first term is the already familiar inertial force $F_{ин6}$ of the Roblis center. The faster the rotation and the further from the center, the greater it is. The second force is called the Korpolis force $F_k$ (named after the French scientist Korpolis, who first calculated this force). It has to be introduced only when the body moves in the rotating system. It does not depend on the position of the body, but depends on the speed of movement of the body and on the speed of rotation of the reporting system.

What if the body in the rotating system does not move along a circle, but, for example, along a radius (see Fig. 3)? It turns out that in this case it is also necessary to drive the Korpolis forces, but they will not be directed along the radius, but perpendicular to it. And in general, during any movement in a rotating system, the Korpolis force is directed perpendicular to the axis of rotation and the speed of the body. Surprisingly, but true: when moving in a rotating system, the inertial force seems to be only sideways.
