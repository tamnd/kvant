⟦folio 17⟧

Leonard Euler

(on the 200th anniversary of his death)

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. G. GINDIKIN

So Euler stopped calculating and living.
Condorcet

At the beginning of 1783, Princess Ekaterina Romanovna Dashkova, who 20 years earlier had been the closest associate of Catherine II in the days of her accession to the Russian throne, was appointed director of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences. Known for her inventiveness, the princess comes up with a sure-fire move that should convince the academician of her commitment to science. She persuades the deceased Euler, who had long been at odds with the academic authorities and did not attend academic conferences, to accompany her on her first visit to the Academy. Blind Euler appears accompanied by his son and grandson. Dashkova recalled in remembrance: “I told them that I asked Euler to join me in the ambush, because, despite my own ignorance, I believe that by such an act I solemnly testify to my respect for science and enlightenment.”

And just a few months later, in the minutes of the Academy it was written: “At the conference meeting on September 11, 1783, Academician N. I. Fuss took on the duties of secretary *), absent due to the death of his famous father, Mr. Leonhard Euler, who died of an applix stroke on September 7 at 11 pm, at the age of 76 years, 5 months and 3 days, having completed his long and brilliant journey and who made his immortal name known throughout Europe.”

Euler's exceptional personality and his irresistible role in the history of the Academy forced us to look for unconventional ways to honor his memory. On October 23, Academician N. I. Fuss, Euler’s student and the husband of his grandchildren, delivered a “Laudatory speech.” The academicians decided, at their own expense, to make a bust of the “Immortal Euler, equally worthy of admiration for his genius and his merits,” and their “illustrious leader” (Dashkova) “added to this a magnificent column, which serves as the base of this bust”; First, the bost was installed in the library, and then - opposite the presidential cross in the meeting room (and remained in the library

*) He was Johann Albrecht, the eldest son of L. Euler.
