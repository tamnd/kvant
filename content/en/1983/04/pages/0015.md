— Andrey Nikolaevich, often comes to refer to the increasing specialization of science. At the same time, it is known that you were engaged in such areas of mathematics as the theory of probability and algebraic topology, mathematical logic and the theory of dynamical systems. What do you think is the future of science—universality or specialization?

— Mathematics is great. One person is not able to study all its ramifications. In this sense, specialization is inevitable. But at the same time, mathematics is a single science. More and more new connections arise between its sections, sometimes in unexpected ways. One sections serve as tools for other sections. I think confining mathematicians within too narrow limits must be disastrous for our science. The situation is made easier by the fact that work in the field of mathematics is, in principle, collective. There must be a certain number of mathematicians who understand the mutual connections between the most diverse areas of mathematics. On the other hand, you can work with great success in some very narrow branch of mathematics. But in this case, you also need, at least in general terms, to understand the connections between your special field of research with related fields, to understand that, in essence, scientific work in mathematics is a collective work.

— What can you say about the correspondence and connections between applied and pure mathematics?

— First of all, it should be noted that the very distinction between applied and pure mathematics is extremely arbitrary. Questions that seem to belong to pure mathematics and have no applications very often unexpectedly turn out to be important for the development of applications. On the other hand, when studying applied mathematics, a scientist almost inevitably looks at related questions that are solved using the same methods, attracting him with their logical beauty, but, strictly speaking, no longer receiving direct applications. If funny questions that are empty and have no immediate application are attractive, if only because of the beauty and naturalness of the problems that arise, then, of course, they also need to be addressed.

— Norbert Wiener writes in his autograph book that Percetal was engaged in functional analysis when he felt that “Kolmogorov was pressing on my heels.” How do you feel about competition in mathematics?

— Wiener’s statement is not entirely clear to me. I have done little in functional analysis. The most interesting work on functional analysis is called “The Wiener Spiral and Some Other Interesting Curves in Globert Space.”

As for competition, competition can have other abbreviations, then it differs little from cooperation. Close contention, when two mathematicians think simultaneously and in parallel about the same problem, can sometimes be very productive. But at the same time, sometimes it happens that the participation of one of the practical employees turns out to be unnecessary and then it is reasonable for him to leave the reports aside without offense.
