⟦folio 39⟧

Tasks

M796—M800; F808—F812

We have been running this section from issue to issue since the founding of the magazine. The problems published in it are not standard, but solving them does not require knowledge beyond the scope of the school curriculum. More difficult tasks are given asterisks. After firming up a task, we usually indicate who proposed it to us. Of course, not all of these problems are published for the first time. Solutions to problems from this issue can be sent no later than June 30, 1983 to the address: 103006, Moscow, K-6, st. Gorky, 32/1, «Kvant». In the “To” column, write: “Problem «Kvant» No. 4 - 83” and the numbers of the problems whose solutions you are sending, for example “M796, M797” or “F808”. Solutions to problems from different issues of the magazine or in different subjects (mathematics and physics) are assigned in different envelopes. Include an envelope with your address written on it (in this envelope you will receive the results of the decision verification). The conditions of each original problem proposed for publication are assigned in a separate envelope in duplicate along with your solution to this problem (on the envelope place: “Problem «Kvant», new problems in physics” or “...new problems in mathematics”). At the beginning of each letter, please indicate the school number and the class in which you study.

M796. The point $P$ is located inside the square $ABCD$ such that $|AP| : |BP| : |CP| = 1:2:3$. Find $\widehat{APB}$.

L. D. Kurlyandik

M797*. It is known that the last digits of the squares of integers can only be the numbers 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 and 9. Is it true that before the last digit they can contain any group of digits, that is, that for any set of $n$ digits $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ one can find an integer whose square ends with the digits $a_1 a_2 \dots a_n b$ (where $b$ is one of the digits listed above)?

D. B. Fuchs

M798*. Mark $4k$ points on the circle and color them alternately red and blue; then $2k$ red points were randomly connected in pairs $k$ by red segments, and $2k$ blue ones by $k$ blue segments (the three segments do not intersect at one point). Prove that there are at least $k$ points of intersection of the red segments with the blue ones.

S. V. Fomin
