Thus, the discreteness of the values ​​of the kinetic energies of molecules was simply an intermediate computational technique, devoid of physical meaning. Despite the formal nature of the technique used by Bolshoi, it obviously played a significant role in the formation of the views of Planck, who suggested that the energy of oscillators also forms a discrete series of values ​​that are multiples of the same value. Attempts to direct this value to zero did not give the desired result, that is, it did not lead to formula (1). Then Planck abandoned these attempts. In a letter to the famous American physicist Wood, Planck wrote: “In short, I can call this whole thing an act of despair. Because I think I am peace-loving and not inclined to risky adventures. But I had already been working on the problem of balance between radiation and matter since 1894, without achieving any success; I knew that this problem was of fundamental importance for physics, and I knew the formula that reproduces the distribution of energy in the normal spectrum; a theoretical explanation had to be found at any cost, and no price was too high.” And Planck paid this price by introducing into physics a perfect idea of ​​the discreteness of possible values ​​of the energy of oscillators, no longer as an intermediate stage due to the convenience of calculation, but as an essential element of the entire consideration of problems. This violated the basic principle of classical physics, according to which physical quantities always change in a continuous manner. Everyone believed in the truth of Aristotle's saying: “Nature makes no leaps.”

Taking into account the restrictions indicated by Wien for the type of dependence $I_v(T)$, Planck was forced to assume that portions of the energy of the oscillators should be proportional to the frequency:

$$\varepsilon = h\nu .$$

This is how a new function, the constant $h$, came into physics - Planck’s constant. The quantity $\varepsilon$ allows was called a quantum of energy. Using condition (2), Planck obtained an expression for the energy of the oscillator $E_v$, and then moved on to the integrability of radiation:

$$I_v = \frac{2\pi h\nu^3}{c^2(e^{h\nu/kT}-1)},$$

where $c$ is the speed of light, $k$ is Bolshiman’s constant.

Planck spoke repeatedly about the “tortuosity” of the path he had to take. Indeed, we were talking about deriving formula (1'), which represents an improvement of Wien’s formula. This was done through energy quantization. But Wien's formula diverged from experiment in the region of low frequencies, where $\varepsilon$ is small and quantization is of little importance. In the region of high frequencies, where $\varepsilon$ is large and the discreteness of the oscillary energy values ​​is significant, Wien’s formula gave good agreement with experiment. The appearance of unity in the denominator of Planck's formula ensured that the results of theoretical calculations coincided with experimental data in the low-frequency region. At the same time, in this unit the germ of a new technical branch was laid - quantum electronics, that is, the possibility of creating masers and lasers. But that, as I say, is another story.

No matter how important Planck's formula is, the idea of ​​quantization used in the process of its derivation and the introduction of a new fundamental constant turned out to be even more important. This was a real revolution in physics, which also had consequences such as the creation of the quantum theory of matter and radiation. The presence of the quantity $h$ in the physical formula means that this formula can only be obtained from quantum concepts.

The path that led Planck to complete victory was essentially illogical.
