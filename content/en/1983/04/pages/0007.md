In addition, the plasma structure can have a noticeable physical effect on the walls of the alloy, because today’s plasma torch is used for cutting the most durable metals and alloys. So, the alloy material must withstand temperatures of 3000–4000°C and at these temperatures be resistant to physical influences.

In practical nature there are no materials that in their pure form could satisfy these conditions. Therefore comes the use of principles to cool the alloy material during fuel combustion. The most suitable material is tungsten, whose melting point is 3400°C. However, it is impossible to obtain a solid profile from it and with sufficiently thin (from the minimum weight message) walls using traditional metalworking methods. It is very difficult to obtain products by casting, and the quality of the metal will be low. In addition, even if this were possible, tungsten would not be able to work, since the temperature of the exhaust gases during the combustion of some types of modern fuel is higher than its melting point.

This is where powder metallurgy, combined with known physical principles, comes to the rescue. The method of powder metallurgy, which consists of placing metal powder into the cavity of the tool, and then subjecting it to pressure and sintering, produces a porous structure of the “pumice” type product. Then this “pumice” is impregnated with copper (its melting point is only 1083°C) and the resulting material can work for a short time, even if the temperature of the exhaust gases is about 4000°C. Paradox? Not at all. Remember when you walk out to the shore on a hot summer day. You're cold. Why? As water evaporates, it takes heat away from you and cools your body. So here, copper evaporates and cools the tungsten frame, and since it is all penetrated by channels filled with copper, it always protrudes on the surface of the alloy and cools it.
