⟦folio 38⟧

In game the beginner will win. He must first take enough beads so that the remaining number is divisible by $p+1$ (that is, take $r$ beads, where $r$ is the remainder of $N$ divided by $p+1$), and then continue this tactic further.

If the number $N$ is divisible by $p+1$ (say, $N=k (p+1)$), then the player who goes second wins. No matter how his opponent starts, he will be able to get into winning positions all the time: $(k-1)(p+1), (k-2)(p+1)$, etc.

— Can “Nehru for Beginners” be changed in any other way?

Master's degree Yes, for example, there is a game called “Evens Win.” In this game, two players also take turns from 1 to $p$ beads from a pile that initially has an odd number of beads. This time the winner is the one who, when the table is empty, collects an even number of beads. This game is much more interesting - it is much more difficult to win.

— Is it true that, nevertheless, you will always win in this game?

Master's degree If I am given the right to choose whether I start the game or go second, then I will always win. I know the theorem - in games of this type*) like these, one of the players has a winning strategy. This wonderful fact of game theory gave me confidence, and while preparing for the game, I found the secret to winning**).

I will quote a verse about this from a friend of the Master of the Game, Joseph Knecht:

You write on a piece of paper, and the meaning is familiar and fixed by the wanderings of the pen,
For the following it is completely clear:
The game ends with the rules***

*) We are talking about games that specialists call “finite games of the infinite with complete information.” You can read about them in the book by E. S. Ventzel “Elements of Game Theory” (M., “Nauka”, 1959) or in «Kvant», 1981, No. 1, p. 12.

**) This secret was also unraveled by the guys from the Small Academy of Sciences of Crimea. «Kvant» will soon return to this game.

***) From the mentioned book by Hermann Hesse “The Glass Bead Game” (translation of poems by S. Averintsev).
