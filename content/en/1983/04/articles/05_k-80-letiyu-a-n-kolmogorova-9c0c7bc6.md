On April 25, Academician Andrei Nikolaevich Kolmogorov, one of the founders of the magazine «Kvant» and permanent first deputy editor-in-chief, will turn 80 years old.

The life of A. N. Kolmogov is inextricably linked with Moscow University, where he became a student in 1920 and where he was later a professor and dean; Now Andrey Nikolaevich heads the Department of Mathematical Logic and the Department of Mathematics of the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics.

By universal recognition, A. N. Kolmogov is one of the leading mathematicians of our time. Andrei Nikolaevich's works relate to a wide variety of branches of mathematics and its applications, ranging from the most abstract sections to such applied areas as hydrodynamics and control theory. During the Great Patriotic War, A. N. Kolmogov made a great contribution to the theory of artillery firing calculations. However, his work on probability theory brought him the most fame - Andrei Nikolaevich put this science on a solid axiomatic foundation and significantly enriched many of its sections (limit theorems, Markov processes, complexities of numerical sequences).

A. N. Kolmogov is not only an outstanding mathematician, but also an excellent teacher who trained many talented scientists. Among his students are academicians A. I. Maltsev, S. M. Nikolsky and Yu. V. Prokorrov, corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences I. M. Gelfand, and among mathematicians of the younger generation - doctors of physical and mathematical sciences V. I. Arnold, Ya. G. Sinai and the untimely death of V. M. Alekseev.

The pedagogical talent of A. N. Kolmogov manifested itself not only in the education of mathematical research: Andrei Nikolaevich taught a lot in a general secondary school, founded a boarding school at Moscow State University and taught there for 15 years; in the sixties, Andrei Nikolaevich headed the Commission for the reform of mathematical education and wrote a number of school textbooks.

The editorial board, the editorial board and the editorial board of the magazine «Kvant» congratulate Andrei Nikolaevich on his birthday and wish him good health and new successes in his teaching activities and in science.

Conversation with Andrei Nikolaevich Kolmogorov

We are in an old wooden house in the village of Komarovka near Moscow, where Andrei Nikolaevich usually spends the end of the week. Bright, modestly furnished room. In one of the corners there is an old but high-quality record player with special shelves for records. The walls are lined with shelves of books. In the middle of the room there is a large table with many books, reprints of articles, manuscripts, and art albums. Andrei Nikolaevich sits by the window at a small desk. Next to a lush typewriter and neatly folded sheets of paper covered with writing, there is a tape recorder on which our conversation is recorded. We bring to your attention a transcript of this conversation.

— Andrey Nikolaevich, often comes to refer to the increasing specialization of science. At the same time, it is known that you were engaged in such areas of mathematics as the theory of probability and algebraic topology, mathematical logic and the theory of dynamical systems. What do you think is the future of science—universality or specialization?

— Mathematics is great. One person is not able to study all its ramifications. In this sense, specialization is inevitable. But at the same time, mathematics is a single science. More and more new connections arise between its sections, sometimes in unexpected ways. One sections serve as tools for other sections. I think confining mathematicians within too narrow limits must be disastrous for our science. The situation is made easier by the fact that work in the field of mathematics is, in principle, collective. There must be a certain number of mathematicians who understand the mutual connections between the most diverse areas of mathematics. On the other hand, you can work with great success in some very narrow branch of mathematics. But in this case, you also need, at least in general terms, to understand the connections between your special field of research with related fields, to understand that, in essence, scientific work in mathematics is a collective work.

— What can you say about the correspondence and connections between applied and pure mathematics?

— First of all, it should be noted that the very distinction between applied and pure mathematics is extremely arbitrary. Questions that seem to belong to pure mathematics and have no applications very often unexpectedly turn out to be important for the development of applications. On the other hand, when studying applied mathematics, a scientist almost inevitably looks at related questions that are solved using the same methods, attracting him with their logical beauty, but, strictly speaking, no longer receiving direct applications. Probably, in practical work mathematics needs to be demonstrated

proper width. There is no doubt that mathematicians must, it is their duty, deal with all those questions that are strictly called questions of practice. If funny questions that are empty and have no immediate application are attractive, if only because of the beauty and naturalness of the problems that arise, then, of course, they also need to be addressed.

— Norbert Wiener writes in his autograph book that Percetal was engaged in functional analysis when he felt that “Kolmogorov was pressing on my heels.” How do you feel about competition in mathematics?

— Wiener’s statement is not entirely clear to me. I have done little in functional analysis. The most interesting work on functional analysis is called “The Wiener Spiral and Some Other Interesting Curves in Globert Space.”

As for competition, competition can have other abbreviations, then it differs little from cooperation. Close contention, when two mathematicians think simultaneously and in parallel about the same problem, can sometimes be very productive. But at the same time, sometimes it happens that the participation of one of the practical employees turns out to be unnecessary and then it is reasonable for him to leave the reports aside without offense.

From early childhood it was known that I died a good death and that I was interested in mathematical problems of an arithmetic nature; I also became acquainted with the beginnings of algebra relatively early. — When do you usually show aptitude for mathematics? Were you tortured, like you, at an early age?

— I taught quite a lot in high school. I have the impression that interest in mathematics in middle school, at the age of twelve or thirteen, often turns out to be temporary and disappears completely by high school. This happens especially often in girls. In my opinion, those schoolchildren who are passionate about mathematics at the age of 13-14-15 are worth working with. With skillful cultivation, their abilities gradually develop and, as a rule, are no longer lost. There are, of course, many more skillful things. Of course, a serious interest in mathematics may appear later.

— Which mathematicians of the older generation had the most influence on you?

— During my student years, I was a student of Nikolai Nikolaevich Luzin. Besides him, Vyacheslav Vasilievich Stepanov, Alexander Yakovlevich Khinchin, Pavel Sergeevich Alexandrov and other mathematicians of their generation had a great influence on me.

— What would you like to say about your students and which of them would you like to mention?

— I was lucky to have talented students. Many of them, having started working with me in some area, then moved on to a new topic and, completely independently of me, received wonderful results. It would be difficult to single out the most worthy of mention.

I will say only as a joke that at present one of my students controls the dark atmosphere, and the other controls the oceans *).

— Andrey Nikolaevich, what is your daily routine?

— Naturally, during my rather long life, the daily routine at different periods was different. I will, perhaps, only describe the daily routine that Pavel Sergeev Aleksaandrov and I established for ourselves for those 3-4 days a week that we spent outside the city, near Moscow, in the village of Komarovka.

The day starts at 7 am. The first hour was devoted to gymnastics and jogging. At 8 o'clock we had breakfast and got to work at the table - with or without a typewriter. At one or two o'clock there was an afternoon snack consisting of milk or kefir with bread. After afternoon tea we worked a little more, but usually went for a long walk on foot or - in winter - on ski slopes, until 4 o'clock in the afternoon. Then we snuck off to sleep for half an hour. At 5 o'clock there was lunch. After lunch, we sometimes did some more work, some minor community work: copying or the like. The evening was dedicated to reading, music, and receiving guests. Before going to bed, we also liked to take a short walk. We went to bed around 10 o'clock.

But, of course, when you work and begin to achieve a solution to some important problem, everything disappears into the background, there was no longer any daily routine.

— Do you go in for sports? Which one?

— I have never been involved in competitive sports. If I'm not mistaken, I've only competed in a 10K flat race three times in my life.

But I have always been very fond of walking and boating, and making long journeys by canoe or boat. I really like swimming, it’s similar in the mountains. In all these activities, I appreciate not only their health benefits, but the joy of the promise of nature that they bring.

I have always loved swimming in the surf. On sunny skiing days I like to do more skiing in just shorts. During such ski runs like this, I like to swim in the middle of the sun-drenched sgugovs in the river that has just opened up from the ice. However, I do not advise you to oblige you to imitate me in all this - you can simply enroll in some sports section that attracts you.

— Andrey Nikolaevich, what would you like to wish our readers?

— I myself am a scientist, and, of course, first of all, I wish our readers to make one or another contribution to science, big or small. I note, however, that if all our readers began to write independent scientific works, then scientific journals would not withstand such pressure. Therefore, I have a statement and a more modest wish - that your school passion for mathematics will be useful to you in later life. In «Kvant» we are just trying to show you (perhaps not enough) how diverse the applications of mathematical science are.
