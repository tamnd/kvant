Unique laboratory

Interview
with the pilot-costonaut of the USSR
A.A. Serebrov

On August 19, 1982, in accordance with the space research program in the Soviet Union, the Soyuz T-7 spacecraft was launched.
On August 20, the Soyuz T-7 spacecraft docked with the Salyut-7 - Soyuz T-5 orbital complex, and the Salyut-7 - Soyuz T-5 - Soyuz T-7 rafted research complex began to function in near-Earth space.
On August 27, after completing an extensive scientific research program, the crew of the Soyuz T-7 ship safely returned to Earth.
The editors «Kvant» met with one of the participants in this expedition, a pilot-costonaut of the USSR
Hero of the Soviet Union A. A. Serebrov and asked him to answer a number of questions of interest to our readers.

Alexander Alexandrovich, as far as we know, you are a physics engineer by education, graduated from the Moscow Physico-Technical Institute and worked there for a long time. Which area of ​​physics is closest to you?

My main specialty is thermodynamics, the study of heat and mass transfer: experimental study of the characteristics of the movement of masses of liquid and gases in given temperature and pressure regimes *). I took part in the creation of the Salyut-6 and Salyut-7 stations. And some of the results of my student research “fly”, providing the necessary tell mode for the Soyuz and Soyuz-T.

What is your opinion, as a physicist, about long-term space flights?

Long-term space stations are unique physical laboratories that make it possible to create conditions that are not possible on Earth. At the same time, as a physicist, I believe that before talking about setting up scientific physical experiments at the station, you need to study the specifics of the spacecraft as a laboratory.

After entering orbit, the station with all its contents begins to “fall” to Earth almost freely, except for braking in the atmosphere, and this “fall” occurs throughout the entire flight until the engines are turned on. So everything on the station is in a state of weightlessness. However, not everything is as simple as we read from science fiction writers.

As the spacecraft moves in orbit, its distance from the Earth changes, which affects the magnitude of the gravitational force acting on the station. Of course, the magnitude of the gravitational force is also influenced by the low accessibility of the Earth. In addition, the station body provides ventilation for the operation of fans, without which it is impossible to ensure both the thermal operating conditions of the equipment and human life.

*) For work in this area, the team of authors, which included A. A. Serebrov, was awarded the Lenin Komsomol Prize in 1976.

Filling a vessel with water will cause the influence to flow out of it, and together with the influence the drops of water suspended in it will “spit out”.

If a low-speed jet is directed directly at the wall of the vessel, then the water, wetting the wall, will stick to it, and there will be no suspended drops (at least until the vessel is shaken). In this way, you can pour liquid into a vessel without loss, pushing the influences out of it.

And then the second question arises: how can you take the liquid “floating” in it from the vessel?

Of course, if there is a centrifuge, then the problem is solved simply: by rotating the vessel, the liquid will be “pressed” against the wall farthest from the axis, and from there it can be taken out using a syringe. If there is no centrifuge, you can press the liquid against the wall, moving the vessel with a slight linear acceleration. This is exactly what they usually do.

Apart from weightlessness, are there any significant differences between a space station and an earthly laboratory?

In the Earth laboratories in which I had previously worked, the issue of equipment placement was always secondary, messages of convenience prevailed there. On a space station, any movement of equipment causes a displacement of the station’s center of mass, which immediately affects the nature of its translational and rotational motion and, as a consequence, the operation of the equipment itself. These circumstances are taken into account when choosing the optimal station position correction mode.

Tell us more about the Tavria experiment your experiment conducted during the flight.

“Tavria” belongs to the category of biotechnical experiments (this term was proposed by the Vice-President of the USSR Academy of Sciences, Academician Yu. A. Ovchinnikov). This is an experiment to separate biological matter into factions - groups of cells that are similar in their physical properties. In particular, it is possible to separate cells of the same type even by age.

A tube made of different materials, in which the substance under study is located, is placed in an electric field of the order of 10 V/cm, directed along the tube. Under the influence of this field, the substance is divided into homogeneous fractions along the length of the tube. Under terrestrial conditions, due to convection movement, such a separation is impossible in many cases. In a state of weightlessness, it was possible to distinguish ten factions where, under terrestrial conditions, the rekord division contained only three factions, and even then with blurred boundaries.

What is the practical value of such an experiment?

The fact is that different fractions of the same substance, when introduced into the body of an animal, affect different organs - one on the liver, another on the heart, etc. When treating one organ, it is important to prevent a negative impact on another. In particular, the limited use of a substance such as interferon, prepared under terrestrial conditions, is associated with possible undesirable side effects.

What is Vaucher's partial in the experiment?

In addition to performing camera work—installation and debugging of the device, filming with a laser head on board the station—while still on Earth, I introduced some conditions into the experimental methodology. Thus, along with the previously provided round tube, the recording of processes in which is difficult due to the refraction of light in cylindrical glass, at my suggestion, a chamber with flat-parelle stacks was used. Since such an experiment was carried out on board the station for the first time, it was necessary to select and ensure the optimal operating mode of the installation, taking into account the impact on it. This was also my task.

What are your future plans?

My specialty is a space technology testing engineer, which says a lot. I want to continue my test work related to the development of existing and the creation of new effective space technology. I would like to take a more hands-on approach to the issues of physics on board a spacecraft.

What would you wish for the readers of our magazine?

Readers «Kvant» are young, inquisitive, creative people. I wish them to discover new horizons in understanding the world around them and, of course, I wish them genuine scientific discoveries. And the main thing is the desire to strive and be able to put into practice what was created with pen and thought.
