BOARD UNDER PROTECTION

In the last issue of the magazine, we talked about combinatorial problems on a chessboard related to the placement of a large number of pieces that do not threaten each other. Another common type of chess puzzle consists of arranging the smallest number of pieces that keep all the free squares of the board covered, counting the number of such arrangements, and generalizing the problems to $n \times n$ boards.

Queens. Five queens on squares c6, d3, e5, f7, g4 guard the entire chessboard. The number of such formations is 4860. It is curious that the heels of the queens are also obtained for the guard docks $9 \times 9$, $10 \times 10$ and even $11 \times 11$. In the general case, the problem of the number of queens that keep all the free squares of the board $n \times n$ under fire has not been solved; there are only some estimates (see «Kvant», 1977, No. 12).

Rooks. There is complete clarity here. On the board $n \times n$ you need to place exactly $n$ rooks so that they fire at all squares of the board, the number of placements is $2n^n$—n! (n! is the product of all integers from 1 to $n$).

Elephants. $n$ bishops placed along one of the central verticals (horizontals) of the board will guard all the free fields of the board, that is, on a promise board 8 bishops will cope with their task. The formula for the number of possible arrangements of $n$ bishops on a board $n \times n$ has a rather cumbersome form: $((2k-1)!(4k^2+k))^2$ for $n=4k+2$, $2(k!)^2(k+2)$ for $n=2k+1$ (k>0).

Kings. Any natural number $n$ can be represented in one of the following ways: $n=3k, n=3k-1, n=3k-2$, where $k$ is some natural number ($k=[(n+2)/3]$). The smallest number of kings guarding the entire $n \times n$ chessboard is $k^2$. In particular, for $n=8$ we have $k=3$, and 9 kings (for example, on squares b2, b5, b8, e2, e5, e8, h2, h5, h8) will fulfill their funkini. The formula for the number of arrangements is unknown.

Connie. In the general case, for the board $n \times n$ the problem is not solved. On the promise board you can place 12 knights guarding all the free squares of the board (b6, c2, c3, c5, c6, d3, e6, f2, f3, f6, f7, g2).

Pawns. There are 29 pawns on the promise board for security. They can be placed on the first horizontal and on verticals a, d and g. Try to find the number of arrangements in the general case (on the board $n \times n$) yourself.

In non-octal tasks about protecting the board $8 \times 8$ it is required that not only free squares are under battle, but also occupied by pieces. Five queens satisfying this condition can be placed on squares a2, c4, d5, e6, g8. Eight rooks standing along the front rank or vertical also cover all squares of the board. As for the other pieces, a little more is required to protect all the boards: knights - 14, words - 10, kings - 12.

In such tasks it is not necessary to have unambiguous figures. Five queens cope with the chessboard, but two of them can be replaced with rooks (queens b7, d1, f7, rooks d3, h5) or even rooks and a bishop (queens b4, f2, h6, rook d8, bishop e5), and in the first arrangement all 64 squares of the board are covered. The queens can be accompanied by a knight or a king (queens d6, e4, f5, g7 and knight cl or king b2).

Is it possible to place a complete set of pieces (without pawns) on a chessboard so that they keep all 64 squares under fire?

Oddly enough, eight pieces can guard the entire board only if the bishops are of the same color.

If the bishops are of different colors, then one square is always left unguarded, and it has been proven that the other square of the board can be unguarded. If we want free squares of the board to be under the defense, then seven pieces are enough: Kb4, Qg2, Rf8, Rh1, Nc7, Ke5, Cc5.
