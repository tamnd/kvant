Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. L. GUTENMACHER

We invited the Master of the Game *), a well-known specialist in game theory, to the editorial office - he knows a lot of old games and comes up with new ones.

— Dear Master, please tell us about your goals.

Master's degree Let's start with the simplest game, which we will call.

A game for beginners. There are 66 beads on the table. The first player can take from 1 to 9 beads, the second player takes from 1 to 9 beads from the remaining ones, and so on. The one who takes the last beads wins.

The game is called a “Game for Beginners” - this has a double meaning. Firstly, the game is simple and using its example it is convenient to demonstrate methods of reasoning for novice mathematicians. Secondly, in this game, if played correctly, the starting player wins - the one who goes first.

— How will you care if you start?

Master's degree I will take 6 beads - there will be 60 left. No matter how the opponent goes, the next move I can make is that there will be 50 beads left, then 40, then 30 and so on until there are 0 beads left. I won.

If I don’t start the game, then the opponent who knows the described method of play will win. Therefore, it is enough for two masters to throw a coin and

— What to do if the enemy started to fire first, but doesn’t know how to shoot correctly?

Master's degree We know “winning positions” - when the number of beads that remains after a move is a multiple of 10. So you need to move to such a position as soon as the opportunity arises.

— How did you unravel the secret of this cerra?

Master's degree I began to reason from the end of the game, and not from its beginning. Let's say I won, that is, I took the last beads. I could only do this if the opponent had previously left 1 to 9 beads on the table. To force him to do this, I had to leave 10 beads on the table after my previous move. This is a winning position. In the same way, the next (actually the previous) winning position is to leave 20 beads. Winning positions are then periodically repeated. The very last, that is, the very first winning position is to leave 60 beads on the table. We know that you can get into it with the first move - by taking 6 beads.

As you can see, my secrets are very simple: think from the end of the game and find winning positions. And, of course, notice how they repeat it periodically.

- Thank you, Master. Would you mind offering us other cerra?

Master's degree The considered game is very easy to generalize: let's assume that at the beginning there are N beads on the table and that each player can take from 1 to p beads. For specific N and p we get various specific games. Specifically, at p=9, N=66 is our beginner's game.

- Are there rules that would be suitable for any such cerra?

— Can “Nehru for Beginners” be changed in any other way?

Master's degree Yes, for example, there is a game called “Evens Win.” In this game, two players also take turns from 1 to $p$ beads from a pile that initially has an odd number of beads. This time the winner is the one who, when the table is empty, collects an even number of beads. This game is much more interesting - it is much more difficult to win.

— Is it true that, nevertheless, you will always win in this game?

Master's degree If I am given the right to choose whether I start the game or go second, then I will always win. I know the theorem - in games of this type*) like these, one of the players has a winning strategy. This wonderful fact of game theory gave me confidence, and while preparing for the game, I found the secret to winning**).
