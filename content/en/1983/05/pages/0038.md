Obviously, the widest interference pattern will be in the place where the largest intersection area of ​​the interfering beams is. From figure 2 it is clear that it will be at a distance

$$L \approx \frac{D}{4 \text{tg} \beta} \approx \frac{D}{4 \beta} \approx \frac{Dn}{4 \alpha} \approx 112.5 \text{ см.}$$

In this case, the width of the entire interference pattern is equal to $b = D/2 = 3 \text{ см.}$

Each of the intersecting rays has a width of $d = (D/2) \cos \beta \approx D/2$. The wave surfaces of the beams form an angle between themselves equal to $2\beta$; therefore, the maximum difference in the path of the beams is

$$\Delta l = d \text{tg} 2\beta \approx d \cdot 2\beta.$$

The path difference corresponding to two adjacent maxima (or minima) is equal to the wavelength $\lambda$. Thus, the number of interference fringes formed on the screen is equal to

$$N = \frac{\Delta l}{\lambda} \approx \frac{2d\beta}{\lambda} \approx \frac{D\alpha}{n\lambda} \approx 800,$$

and the width of one strip is

$$b_0 = \frac{b}{N} \approx \frac{D/2}{D\alpha/(n\lambda)} \approx \frac{n\lambda}{2\alpha} \approx 37.5 \text{ мкм.}$$

Problem 3. A radio scene receiver monitoring the appearance of the Earth's satellite from behind the horizon is located on the shore of a lake at a height of $H = 3$ m above the water surface. As the satellite rises above the horizon, there will be a periodic change in the intensity of the received signal. Determine the frequency of the satellite's radio scene if intensity maxima are observed, in particular, at the satellite's elevation angles above the horizon $\alpha_1 = 3^\circ$ and $\alpha_2 = 6^\circ$. The surface of the lake can be considered a perfectly reflective mirror.

The receiver receives rays that indefinitely come from the satellite and are reflected from the surface of the lake. On figure 3 these are rays 1 and 2 respectively. The difference between them

$$\Delta l = |AC| - |AB| = \frac{H}{\sin \alpha} - \frac{H}{\sin \alpha} \cos 2\alpha$$
