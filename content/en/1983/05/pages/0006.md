⟦folio 4⟧

If in the solar system the planets move under the influence of gravitational Newtonian forces, then in the atom electrons move around the charge under the influence of electric, Coulomb forces.

The “sun” was called the nucleus of an atom, which has a positive charge equal to the sum of the charges of the electrons rotating around it (so that the atom remains electrically neutral). This is the so-called Rutherford planetary model of the atom. The size of the nucleus does not exceed 10 thousandths of the size of the atom itself (just as the size of the Sun is much smaller than the size of the entire solar system). This is how a new term characterizing atom was born - the atomic core. The discovery of the nucleus marked the beginning of a new branch of physics, now called nuclear physics.

Studies of α-particle scattering on various elements of the periodic table led to an important discovery: the positive charge of the nucleus is numerically equal to the product of the serial number of a given element in the periodic table and the charge of the electron. This shed new light on the great law of Mendeleev, giving it a deep physical meaning. It turned out that the number of a chemical element in its value table is equal to the number of electrons rotating around the nucleus. It was also found that the nucleus contains positively charged particles - protons, the charge of which is equal to the charge of the electron, and the mass is approximately 1800 times greater than the mass of the electron.

At first, the study of the atomic nucleus was carried out only in a very small number of laboratories in the world. These are Rutherford's laboratorium in Cambridge, Madame Curie's laboratorium in Paris and D. V. Skobeln's laboratorium in Leningrad. However, by the end - the beginning of the thirties - the basic properties of the atoms themselves were well studied, and many physicists around the world turned their attention to the atomic nucleus.

In 1932, one of the largest discoveries in nuclear physics of the 20th century was made: the neutron was discovered - an electrical neutral particle whose mass is approximately equal to the mass of a proton. It became clear that the nucleus consists of protons and neutrons.

Indeed, by that time it was known that atoms having the same number in the periodic table can have different masses. Such atoms are called isotopes of a given element. The nuclei of atoms isotopes thus contain the same number of protons, but a different number of neutrons.

In 1934, the spouses Irene and Frederic Jolnot-Curie made an equally important discovery of artificial radioactivity. It consisted in the fact that light elements (for example, beryllium), when irradiated with alpha particles, themselves became radioactive, emitting neutrons.

When heavy elements are irradiated, such nuclei are not observed. The famous Italian physicist Fermi agreed that heavy elements have too much nuclear charge, and an α-particle having a charge of the same sign cannot come very close to it. A neutron that has no charge will not be acted upon by a positive charge by Coulomb forces. Fermi accomplished a series of wholesale goals of irradiating various elements with neutrons and discovered that virtually all elements become artificially radioactive after irradiation. After irradiation with a large source, α- and β-particles, γ-rays, and neutrons are emitted. When α- and β-particles were emitted, new chemical elements were formed.

Soon Fermi published a large work, which presented the results of his studies of almost all elements of the periodic table, which became radioactive after irradiation with neutrons. At the same time, he gave an almost complete description of the artificial radioactivity of each element. Thus, a new ensuing transformation of elements with appropriate irradiation was discovered.
