High hydrogen contains two atoms of heavy hydrogen (an isotope of hydrogen - deuterium - with a larger mass). By placing blocks of natural uranium in heavy water, the neutrons can be replaced and a controlled, self-sustaining fission chain reaction of natural uranium can occur. This served as the basis for the creation of nuclear reactors using thermal neutrons.

In a nuclear reactor, uranium is placed in the form of separate blocks (usually cylindrical), between which there is a moderator - graphite or heavy water. By gradually increasing the number of such blocks, you can approach a critical mass. In this case, it is no longer possible to control the number of neutrons released during the splitting of uranium nuclei in blocks, and the chain reaction does not turn into an explosive one. In an atomic bomb, two separate pieces of uranium are quickly combined, each of which is slightly less than critical mass. It is clear that the total mass of the connected parts quickly becomes above the critical mass. As a result, an explosion will occur. A nuclear reactor can serve as a long-lasting energy source.

During the operation of nuclear reactors, another important phenomenon occurs: part of the excess neutrons, interacting with $^{235}$U nuclei, leads to the formation of an isotope of a new element of the periodic table with atomic number 94 and nuclear mass 239. Such an element does not exist in nature, and it is only obtained artificially by irradiating uranium with neutrons. This element is called plutonium. Pluttonium, like $^{235}$U, when irradiated with neutrons, splits (fissions) with the formation of an excess number of neutrons. Its critical mass is several times less than that of uranium, and this is its significant advantage compared to $^{235}$U. Pluttonium is chemically different from uranium, which makes it possible to isolate it from the mass of uranium and obtain it in its pure form chemically.
