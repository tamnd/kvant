a) Let the screen be close to the hole. To a first approximation, the Sun can be considered the exact source of S (Fig. 6). The perfect spot on the screen is the central projection of the hole onto the plane of the screen. The center of the projection S is located very far away, so all the rays are practically parallel, and the shape and size of the illuminated area coincide with the shape and size of the hole. When the normal to the screen plane is tilted by an angle β (relative to the direction to the Sun), the size of the spot in the corresponding direction increases: $d' = d/\cos \beta$. When the normal to the hole plane is inclined by an angle γ, the spot size decreases: $d' = d \cos \gamma$. In the general case $d' = d \cos \gamma/\cos \beta$. Due to the finite angular diameter of the Sun ($\alpha \approx 0,01$), the boundaries of the shadow from the edges of the hole will be blurred. Penumbra width (Fig. 7) $d'' = \alpha L$. 6) Let the screen be further away from the hole. Then the hole can be considered accurate to a first approximation; its field shape does not play a role. The dark spot on the screen is the image of the Sun in the so-called obskrura camera. It represents the flow of a circular cone of solar rays with the apex in the hole and the angle $\alpha$ at the apex equal to the angular diameter of the Sun. If the screen is perpendicular to the axis of the cone of rays, then the spot is a circle of diameter $d' = \alpha L$. When the screen is tilted at an angle of $\beta$, this circle turns into an ellipse with a major axis of $d'' = d'/\cos \beta = \alpha L/\cos \beta$. As the size of the hole increases, the truth is illuminated, but at the same time its edges become more blurred. This is the placement of hole diameter $d$. Therefore, this case is realized when $d \ll \alpha L$, that is, $L \gg 100d$. For screen $c d \sim 10$ cm $L \gg 10$ m.
