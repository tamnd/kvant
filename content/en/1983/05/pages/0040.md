Discovery of a new particle

January 1983 was marked by a major event in the history of physics. In Geneva, the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN) announced the discovery of a new particle - the W boson. The particle was born during the collision of a proton and an antiproton, accelerated to an energy of 270 GeV (1 GeV = 10³ MeV = 10⁹ EV) each.

This phenomenon is extremely rare. During the two months of operation of the registration facility, there were only six cases of W-boson production (two more cases were reported by another group of physicists).

The discovery of the new particle was highly anticipated. And it is no coincidence: its existence was theoretically predicted several years ago. The fact is that bosons are the “main characters” of the theory of electroweak interaction, a unified theory of electromagnetic and weak processes (its authors S. Weinberg, S. Glashow and A. Salam received the Nobel Prize in 1979). They are associated with the field of weak interaction - interaction that manifests itself, for example, during the beta decay of nuclei. These particles are quanta of a weak field, similar to how photons are quanta of an electromagnetic field.

The family of weak quanta consists of three particles: W⁺, W⁻ and Z⁰; the first two are charged, the last is neutral. With their help, many transformations are carried out in the world of elementary particles.

When I spoke about new parts, I never saw them in any experiment, and this greatly upset the scientists. However, the theory predicted not only the fact of their existence, but also their mass: 78 GeV for W⁺ and W⁻ and 89 GeV for Z⁰ (remember that in particle physics mass is usually expressed not in units of mass, but in units of energy).

Preparation for the experiments lasted two years, and finally the first bazons were recorded by instruments. The experiments (their leader was the Italian physicist Rubbin) were carried out with colliding points of protons and antiprotons, in collisions of which, in violation of the other particles, W-bosons were supposed to be born:

$$p + \bar{p} \rightarrow W + \text{что угодно.}$$

What was “caught,” of course, was not the boson itself, but its subsequent decay (which is why the more complete name of the new particle is an intermediate boson). For example, a born positive W boson decays into a positron and a neutrino:

$$W^{+} \rightarrow e^{+} + v.$$

It is surprising that the experimental estimates of the mass of the W boson - (81 ± 5) GeV - turned out to be very close to the theoretical ones (the addition of ±5 GeV is an estimate of the accuracy of the experiment; the actual value lies in the range from 76 to 86 GeV). This makes it possible to consider the weight experiments carried out as plausible.

We can say that now the theory of electromagnetic and weak interaction has received experimental justification. Although, of course, there is still a lot of work ahead - we need to accumulate similar cases of the production of W-bosons, clarify their properties and, finally, we need to “catch” the neutral boson too.
