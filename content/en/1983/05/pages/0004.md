⟦folio 2⟧

Physics and scientific and technological progress

Academician I. K. KIKOIN

The first part of this article, dedicated to the influence of physical discoveries on the development of technology in the 19th century, was published in the 3rd issue of our magazine.

In 1896, the French physicist Anise Becquerel, learning about Roentgen's discovery, proposed that the source RENTGENA represented the best case of fluorescent substances*. Becquerel had a whole collection of such substances, among which equilibrant salt fluoresced most intensely. He conducted a number of experiments. In Becquerel's experiments, the fluorescent equalizing salt actually illuminated the photographic plastic, including black paper. But one day he accidentally left an unlit preliminary equalizing salt near the photographic plastic. It turned out that even under these conditions, in the absence of fluorescence, the equalizing salt illuminated the photographic plastic protected with black paper. Becquerel decided that equilibrated salt emits rays similar to RENTGENA. Thus, a phenomenon was discovered, a better name for preactivity (from the Latin word radius - ray).

French physicists Maria Sklodowska-Curie and Pierre Curie became interested in this phenomenon.

*) Fluorescence is the glow of certain substances that arise after irradiation with light. Fluorescence has a finite duration.

The Unnies began a detailed study of uranium and waste from its factory production and for the first time discovered that this waste produces radiation that is much more intense than the radiation of uranium itself. As a result of enormous work, Marie and Pierre Curie, for the first time in the world, isolated from this waste two new hitherto unknown elements - posium and radium.

The radiation intensity of radium (it was actually radium chloride) turned out to be a million times higher than the radiation intensity of uranium. I dreamed that every gram of radium emits about 580 joyls within an hour. The continuous emission of the chemical element of energy was completely incomprehensible and, at first glance, seemed to contradict the law of conservation of energy.

It was radium's ability to emit, albeit very slowly, significant amounts of energy that most interested physicists. What causes radioactive substances to emit rays? What is the nature of these rays? Where do radioactive substances get the energy that they give off in the form of their tanning rays? Such questions worried physicists. This was the radium mystery. Many physicists have puzzled over its solution.

Experimental studies of radium radiation were continued by Marie and Pierre Curie. They placed radium in a magnetic field (between the strips of an electromagnet) and noticed an amazing phenomenon - the radium radiation noticeably weakened. The Curies agreed that the weakening of radium radiation in a magnetic field is associated with the deflection of some loaded particles in this field. These particles were called β particles. It is now known that β particles are electrons.

Rutherford discovered that in addition to the β particle, radium also emits a positively charged particle. They are called α particles. Finally, the part of radium radiation that is not affected by the magnetic field at all is called γ particles.
