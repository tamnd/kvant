The layered reception of television stations located several thousand kilometers away from the reception site is explained by the reflection of waves from this layer. Attempts to use this phenomenon for regular television reception, as well as to combat it as a nuisance, were unsuccessful.

The study of signals at distances of the order of 200÷800 km from the transmitter led to the discovery of a previously unknown phenomenon, which is now called “long-range tropospheric VHF propagation.” This propagation is associated with the re-emission of radio waves in the air envelope of the Earth, in other words, in the troposphere. Re-emission is explained by the non-hydrogen structure of air. The electrical capacity of air depends on humidity, temperature and pressure. There are always non-hydrogens in the air - areas in which these meteoparameters, and therefore the electrical permeability, differ from the parameters of the surrounding air. A striking example of non-hydrogenity visible to the naked eye is oblaska. Non-hydrogen content of the air leads to partial dissipation and reflection of wave energy. This is shown schematically in figure 6. On the right side of the figure is a random dependence of the electroscopic influence on height. Teeth on the dependence indicate the presence of non-hydrogenity. The shape of non-hydrogens and their relative position can be very different. Complex hydrocarbons play a major role. Their length ranges from hundreds of meters to several kilometers. Ultrashort waves reflected from mineral deposits can be received at a great distance from the transmitter. The variability of heterogeneities over time leads to interference of incoming waves and, as a consequence, to rapid signal fading. The re-radiated power is only $10^{-6} \div 10^{-8}$ part of the transmitted signal power.

The described phenomenon is used in tropospheric radio relay lines. Such lines require the installation of powerful transmitting devices and diversity reception systems for signal fading. And yet they turn out to be appropriate for radiotelephone communications in hard-to-reach areas. The advantage of tropospheric lines is the large interval between neighboring stations. When using decimeter waves, the interval length is equal to $250 \div 300$ km, but in some cases it can be greater. For example, on the well-known tropospheric radio link “Soviet Union - Induction” the distance between corresponding points is about 700 km.
