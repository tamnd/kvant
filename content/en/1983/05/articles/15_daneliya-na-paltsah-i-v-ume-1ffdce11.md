On your fingers and in your mind

R. S. DANELIA

In childhood, to add numbers, we all used a computer designed by nature itself - ten fingers. It turned out that the same “device” can also be used when multiplying numbers. We'll show you how to multiply any two numbers from 6 to 10 inclusive. To do this, let's number the fingers: minus - 6, ring - 7, middle - 8, index - 9, thumb - 10. Let's say we need to find the product 7x8. To do this, on the left hand we bend fingers 6, 7, on the right - 6, 7, 8, then multiply the number of bent fingers of both hands by ten and add the product of the number of digit fingers of the hand by the number of digit fingers of the left: 5 10 + 2 3 = 56.

After practicing a little or teaching this simple art to your younger brother, who still knows the multiplication table well, you can check that this method of multiplication always gives the correct result. Why?

The required number is the product ab, where 6≤a≤10, 6≤b≤10. It is clear that there are a-5 fingers on the left hand, and b-5 on the right hand. Thus, the number of bit fingers is a + b - 10. There are 10-a fingers on the left hand and 10-b fingers on the right hand. Their product is equal to (10-a)(10-b).

According to what has been said, we need to find the sum:

(a+b—10)·10+(10—a)(10—b).

By opening the brackets, we make sure that the sum of the real is equal to a·b, that is

⟦folio 41⟧

$$a \cdot b = (a + b - 10) \cdot 10 + (10 - a) \cdot (10 - b).$$

Thus, the finger multiplication method we use is justified.

* * *

Looking carefully at the same action (1), it is easy to understand that instead of the number 10 you can put any number $m$, that is

$$ab = (a + b - m)m + (m - a)(m - b).$$

Check it out! Using this identity, you can quickly find the product of two such numbers that have the same number of tens, hundreds, ...

Let's look at examples.

1. Let us calculate the product 16 · 17. In the same formula (2) we take $m = 20$. Then

$$16 \cdot 17 = (33 - 20) \cdot 20 + 4 \cdot 3 = 272.$$

2. Let's find the product 42 · 44. Take $m = 50$. Then

$$42 \cdot 44 = (86 - 50) \cdot 50 + 8 \cdot 6 = 1848.$$

Of course, there can be other meanings for $m$. For example, it will be nice if we take 40 as $m$.

In this case we have

$$42 \cdot 44 = (86 - 40) \cdot 40 + 2 \cdot 4 = 1848.$$

3. To find the product 121 · 103, in the same equation (2) we take $m = 100$. Then

$$121 \cdot 103 = (224 - 100) \cdot 100 + 21 \cdot 3 = 12463.$$

* * *

You may know the rule for squaring two-part numbers ending in 5. To find $(10x + 5)^2$, just multiply the number $x$ by the number following it, and add 25 to the resulting product. For example,

$$35^2 = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 10 + 25 = 145.$$

This rule is a consequence of the equality

$$(10x + 5)^2 = 10x(x + 1) + 25,$$

which, in turn, is a special case of identity (2).

* * *

Finally, consider the case where $a = 10x + y$, $b = 10x + z$. Then we have

$$(10x + y)(10x + z) = x(10x + y + z)10 + yz.$$

If $y + z = 10$, we get an identity similar to identity (3):

$$(10x + y)(10x + z) = 100x(x + 1) + yz.$$

Based on this, the following rule can be formulated: in order to find the product of two numbers, the number of tens of which is the same, and the sum of the units is 10, it is necessary to multiply the number of tens by the subsequent number and add the product of units to the resulting number (if one is unambiguous, 0 is assigned before it). For example:

1. $48 \cdot 42; 4 \cdot 5 = 20, 8 \cdot 2 = 16; 48 \cdot 42 = 2016;$
2. $99 \cdot 91; 9 \cdot 10 = 90, 9 \cdot 1 = 09; 99 \cdot 91 = 9009;$
3. $102 \cdot 108; 10 \cdot 11 = 110, 2 \cdot 8 = 16, 102 \cdot 108 = 11016.$

By practicing these multiplication techniques, you will learn how to quickly multiply many numbers in your head. And if you can think of other examples that reduce multiplication, write to us.
