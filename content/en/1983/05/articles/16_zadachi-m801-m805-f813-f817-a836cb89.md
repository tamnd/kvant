M788. a) On graph $y = x^2$, points $A$ ($a; a^2$) and $B$ ($b; b^2$) are offset. Place between them a point $M(m; m^2)$ for which the sum of the areas of the two segments bounded by the graph and the segments $AM$ and $BM$ is the smallest.
b) On the graph, the points $A$ and $B$ are offset by the differentiable function $y = f(x)$. It is known that the graph and the segment $AB$ limit the convex-physis. Let $M$ be a point on the graph located between $A$ and $B$ for which the sum of the areas of the two segments bounded by the graph and the segments $AM$ and $BM$ is the smallest. Prove that the tangent to the graph at point $M$ is parallel to chord $AB$.

Let us first present the solution for the general case (point 6), and then apply the result to the functions $f(x) = x^2$ (point a).

Since by condition the segment $AB$, bounded by the segment $AB$ and the graph $y = f(x)$, is a convex function, its area is equal to the sum of the areas of the triangle $ABM$ and the segments $AM$ and $MB$ (see figure). Therefore, if the sum of the areas of segments $AM$ and $MB$ is minimal, the area of ​​triangle $ABM$ is maximum. Divide this triangle into two - $AM_1M$ and $BM_1M$ - by a straight line $MM_1$, parallel to the ordinat axis. Let $a, b$ and $m$ be the abscissas of the points $A, B$ and $M$, and, for definition, $a < b$. Then

$$S_{ABM} = S_{AM_1M} + S_{BM_1M} = \frac{1}{2} (m - a) \cdot |MM_1| + \frac{1}{2} (b - m) \cdot |MM_1| = \frac{1}{2} (b - a) \cdot |MM_1|,$$

The most significant value of the area of ​​the triangle $ABM$ is achieved when the maximum length of the segment $MM_1$. Let us denote by $l(x) = kx + c$ the linear function whose graph is the straight line $AB$, then $|MM_1| = l(m) - f(m)$. (We assume that the chord $AB$ is located above the graph - see figure; otherwise $|MM_1| = f(m) - l(m)$.) By Fermat’s theorem (see Algebra and Analysis 9-10, paragraph 26), at the maximum point of the function $g(x) = l(x) - f(x)$ its arbitrary one is equal to zero, that is, $g'(m) = l'(m) - f'(m) = 0$ and $f'(m) = l'(m) = k$. But $f'(m)$ and $k$ are the angular coefficients of the tangent to the graph $y = f(x)$ at point $M$ and the straight line $AB$, and the equality of the angular coefficients implies the parallelism of the corresponding lines.

In particular, if $f(x) = x^2$, the slope of the line $AB$ is equal to $(b^2 - a^2) / (b - a) = a + b$, and an arbitrary function $f(x)$ at the point $m$ is equal to $2m$. The figure bounded by the parabola and its chord, as is easy to see, is convex. Therefore, the abscissa of the point $M$, for which the sum of the areas of the segments $AM$ and $MB$ is maximum, satisfies the conditions $2m = a + b$, that is, the point $M$ has coordinates $(\frac{a + b}{2}; (\frac{a + b}{2})^2)$.

E. D. Moskovsky. Yu. V. Chinayaev

F799. The height of a vertical water fountain from the level of the pump outlet pipe is equal to H. How many times should the pump power be changed so that the total height of the fountain remains the same after connecting a vertical pipe of the same diameter with a height h<H to the pump outlet pipe?

The mass of water passing per unit time through the cross-section of the pump outlet pipe is equal to m=qvs, where q is the density of water, v is the speed of water flow in the pump outlet pipe, s is the cross-sectional area of ​​the pipe. The power of the pump is equal to the energy “carried away” by water per unit time, that is, equal to N=mv^2/2. In order for the water in the fountain to rise to a height H, its speed at the height from the pipe must be equal to v=\sqrt{2gH}.

Therefore, the pump power should be equal
$$N=\frac{mv^2}{2}=\frac{1}{2}qs(2gH)^{3/2}.$$
We obtain a similar expression for power in the case when an additional pipe of height h is connected to the output pipe of the pump. The “output” water speed in this case should be $v'=\sqrt{2g(H-h)}$, and the power should be
$$N'=\frac{1}{2}qs(2g(H-h))^{3/2}.$$
Thus, in order for the total height of the fountain to remain the same, the pump power must be reduced by
$$k=\frac{N'}{N}=\left(1-\frac{h}{H}\right)^{3/2}.$$
P. I. Zubkov

F802. The heat-conducting mass cylinder $m$, closed at the ends, is partitioned by a stationary mass piston $M$. On both sides of the piston there is one mole of an ideal gas, the internal energy of which is equal to $U = cT$. With a short blow, the cylinder imparts speed $\vec{v}$, directed along its axis. How much will the gas temperature change after the oscillating piston oscillates? There is no friction between the piston and cylinder.

Let $u$ be the speed at which the cylinder and piston move after the oscillating piston has died down. According to the law of conservation of momentum

$$mv = (M + m) u \Rightarrow u = \frac{m}{M + m} v.$$ (6)

Reducing the kinetic energy of the system -

$$\Delta K = m \frac{v^2}{2} - (M + m) \frac{u^2}{2} = \frac{Mm}{M + m} \frac{v^2}{2}$$

- is due to the transition of part $\Delta K$ of this energy into heat and is equal to the increase in the internal energy of the gas in the cylinder (we consider the heat capacity of the cylinder and piston to be negligible compared to the heat capacity of the gas). Since the piston, as in the beginning, is in equilibrium, both moles of gas (to the right and to the left of the piston) again have the same volume, pressure and temperature. Thus, the change in internal energy of the gas is equal to

$$\Delta U = 2c \cdot \Delta T = \frac{Mm}{M + m} \frac{v^2}{2}.$$ (7)

From here the gas temperature is found:

$$\Delta T = \frac{Mm}{M + m} \frac{v^2}{4c}.$$ (8)

G. V. Meledin
