Mass radio broadcasting began on long waves. In the Soviet Union, two Moscow radio transmitting stations began almost simultaneously broadcasting regularly (1924). However, of these, the one located in Sokolniki had low power and was intended for radio amateurs in Moscow and the region. The other is the radio station named after. Comintern - was located on Shabolovka and was significantly superior in power to the Sokolnich station. Its radius of action included the central regions of the European part of the country.

In order to cover the entire vast territory of Russia with broadcasting, it was necessary either to create a super-powerful station, or to build a network of low-power stations that could broadcast Moscow and conduct their own programs. The second way turned out to be more reasonable; use it now.

In a network of stations, some of them, located far from each other, can operate on the same frequencies without mutual interference. But in most cases, each station is allocated its own frequency band, in other words, its own frequency channel. And then it became clear that the long-wavelength range has a very limited number of channels. The entire range occupies a frequency band of 270 kHz. To transmit one broadcast program, a frequency channel with a width of 9 kHz is required*). It’s easy to calculate - the DV band contains only $\frac{270}{9} = 30$ broadcast channels.

Another disadvantage of wavelengths is the weak directivity of antennas.

It is not difficult to understand qualitatively what directionality is. The receiving antenna picks up vibrations, moving away from the source in a certain direction. The more part of the energy emitted by the transmitting antenna is distributed in a given direction, the better - this allows you to reduce the transmitting power, and on the receiving side, you can “tune out” from interference. Therefore, it is important that the transmitting antenna “direct” the radiation, and the receiving antenna “catch” the radiation, essentially in a given direction.

The required directional properties of the antenna are not difficult to obtain when the antenna dimensions exceed the wavelength. It is difficult to do this at long wavelengths, to put it mildly.

Reception of signals in the DV range is often accompanied by crackling and rustling sounds. These are atmospheric and industrial spaces. The source of atmospheric conditions is nearby and further thunderstorms.

Medium waves

Along with the DV band, the station uses medium waves for radio broadcasting. And here it was very soon noticed that the reception conditions for radio stations operating in the CB range are different at noon and at night. During the day, the range of such stations is hundreds of kilometers. Butchio, especially in winter, the reception range is significantly greater. Sometimes you can “catch” stations that are several thousand kilometers away. Another feature of CB reception was also replaced: those stations that were received steadily during the day, but were often subject to fading - the sound volume gradually increased and decreased from time to time.

When these phenomena were discovered, the original ideas that radio waves would travel only along the earth's surface were greatly shaken. It became obvious that two waves can arrive at the receiving point: one “earthly” - a wave propagating along the earth’s surface, and the other “heavenly” - a wave reflected from the nosophere (figure 1).

Let us recall that the ionosphere is a layer of ionized gas in the upper part of the atmosphere - at altitudes from 60 km to 450 km. Gas ionization occurs under the influence of ultraviolet rays and a stream of charged particles emitted by the Sun.

Medium waves are reflected from a layer located at an altitude of 100–120 km (this layer is designated by the letter “E”). But reflection is only possible at night. During the day, in the atmosphere at an altitude of 60–80 km, under the influence of solar radiation, a layer appears in which medium waves are strongly absorbed (this layer is designated by the letter “D”). So in the daytime, only the ground wave arrives at the antenna, for example. At night, in those reception areas where interference of the earth and sky waves occurs, the signal fades (at figure 1 this occurs at the reception point closest to the source; the earth wave does not reach the far point - it is attenuated, and at this point only the reflected wave is received). If the phases of the waves coincide, then the resulting directivity of the field returns the loudness of the sound. If the waves compensate each other, the station’s audibility deteriorates. The trajectory of the reflected wave and the degree of its reflection changes all the time, so the nature of the fading is random.

The study of the ionosphere has led to a change in view of the propagation of long waves. According to modern concepts, long radio waves propagate in a kind of spherical waveguide. The lower wall of the waveguide is the earth's surface, the upper wall is the ionosphere.

Short waves

The short-wave range is nine times wider than the range of long and medium waves. To operate on these waves, directional antennas can be created; at the same time the level of interference is less. The dimensions of transmitters and antennas operating on KB are relatively small. However, short waves did not immediately become a reality. According to the theory of diffraction, large transformations in the path of propagation of short waves (large houses, forested areas, hills) shield the transmitting antenna. Therefore, it was believed that reception on HF was only possible in the line of sight zone, and these waves were given away by the radio amateur for close communications and experimentation. In the presented range, radio amateurs began to set unprecedented communication records. So, in January 1925, radio amateur F.A. Lobov, using a 15 W transmitter, transmitted signals from Nizhny Novgorod that were received in Bardade, Paris and London. In 1928, the short-hauler E.V. Andreev, while sailing on the training ship “Vega” around Europe, maintained continuous contact with

In the Soviet Union, since 1923, the research of short waves has been carried out, in particular, by the famous Nizhny Novgorod Radio Laboratory named after. V.I. Lenin. As one would expect, the propagation of short waves over long distances was due to reflection from the inoosphere.

Reflection can occur at altitudes of 100÷400 km. It is easy to calculate that the maximum reception range with a single reflection can be 2000÷4000 km (figure 2). Short waves are capable of being reflected from the surface of the Earth and then again from the inoosphere, so they can “squeeze” the entire globe and return to the transmission point (“steep-light echo”). On the other hand, in some middle parts of the jumping route, signal reception turns out to be difficult. This area is called the “silent zone”.

Several waves always arrive at the receiving point, having traveled different paths and, accordingly, having different physical properties. Due to their interference, reception on short waves is always accompanied by rapid fading. To combat fading in radio applications, a system of automatic control of conditions is provided.

The principle of its operation is simple: as the signal decreases, the success rate of the receiver increases, and as it increases, it decreases. On short-wave communication lines, so-called “diversity reception” is used to combat fading.
With this type of reception, a signal from one transmitter is received by two or three antennas, which are installed across the path and separated from each other by distances exceeding several wavelengths. The signals at the outputs of the receiving antennas change asynchronously, and it is always possible to select the best signal (this choice will be made, of course, automatically).

Since short-wave antennas have a fairly sharp directivity, several stations remote from each other, broadcasting in different directions, operate at the same frequency without mutual interference. To date, the short wave range has been used to capacity. You can imagine how many radio stations there are now, if in 1940 the number of officially registered stations, not counting amateur and military radio stations, was 28,407!

The main advantage of the VHF band is its width, almost 30,000 MHz! In this range, you can easily transmit signals containing a wide spectrum (set) of frequencies, for example, radio signals of a television image. Creating highly directional antennas in this range does not pose any difficulties. Signal reception is highly stable, practically independent of the time of year and day, and is not affected by atmospheric and industrial interference. But all these advantages are realized mainly in the presence of direct visibility between the antennas of the transmitter and receiver. If there is an obstacle in the path of ultrashort waves, it partially screens the transmitting antenna, and the direction of the field of the received signal is significantly reduced. The reception quality is deteriorating.

Obviously, the line-of-sight distance depends on the height of the antennas (figure 3). As a result of refraction (refraction) in the air, waves propagate along a slightly curved path. The study of signals at distances of the order of 200÷800 km from the transmitter led to the discovery of a previously unknown phenomenon, which is now called “long-range tropospheric VHF propagation.” This propagation is associated with the re-emission of radio waves in the air envelope of the Earth, in other words, in the troposphere. Re-emission is explained by the non-hydrogen structure of air. The electrical capacity of air depends on humidity, temperature and pressure. There are always non-hydrogens in the air - areas in which these meteoparameters, and therefore the electrical permeability, differ from the parameters of the surrounding air. A striking example of non-hydrogenity visible to the naked eye is oblaska. Non-hydrogen content of the air leads to partial dissipation and reflection of wave energy. This is shown schematically in figure 6. On the right side of the figure is a random dependence of the electroscopic influence on height. Teeth on the dependence indicate the presence of non-hydrogenity. The shape of non-hydrogens and their relative position can be very different. Complex hydrocarbons play a major role. Their length ranges from hundreds of meters to several kilometers. Ultrashort waves reflected from mineral deposits can be received at a great distance from the transmitter. The variability of heterogeneities over time leads to interference of incoming waves and, as a consequence, to rapid signal fading. The re-radiated power is only $10^{-6} \div 10^{-8}$ part of the transmitted signal power.

The described phenomenon is used in tropospheric radio relay lines. Such lines require the installation of powerful transmitting devices and diversity reception systems for signal fading. And yet they turn out to be appropriate for radiotelephone communications in hard-to-reach areas. The advantage of tropospheric lines is the large interval between neighboring stations. When using decimeter waves, the interval length is equal to $250 \div 300$ km, but in some cases it can be greater. For example, on the well-known tropospheric radio link “Soviet Union - Induction” the distance between corresponding points is about 700 km.
