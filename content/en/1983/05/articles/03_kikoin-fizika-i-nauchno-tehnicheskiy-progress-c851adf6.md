Physics and scientific and technological progress

Academician I. K. KIKOIN

The first part of this article, dedicated to the influence of physical discoveries on the development of technology in the 19th century, was published in the 3rd issue of our magazine.

In 1896, the French physicist Anise Becquerel, learning about Roentgen's discovery, proposed that the source RENTGENA represented the best case of fluorescent substances*. Becquerel had a whole collection of such substances, among which equilibrant salt fluoresced most intensely. He conducted a number of experiments. In Becquerel's experiments, the fluorescent equalizing salt actually illuminated the photographic plastic, including black paper. But one day he accidentally left an unlit preliminary equalizing salt near the photographic plastic. It turned out that even under these conditions, in the absence of fluorescence, the equalizing salt illuminated the photographic plastic protected with black paper. Becquerel decided that equilibrated salt emits rays similar to RENTGENA. Thus, a phenomenon was discovered, a better name for preactivity (from the Latin word radius - ray).

French physicists Maria Sklodowska-Curie and Pierre Curie became interested in this phenomenon.

*) Fluorescence is the glow of certain substances that arise after irradiation with light. Fluorescence has a finite duration.

The Unnies began a detailed study of uranium and waste from its factory production and for the first time discovered that this waste produces radiation that is much more intense than the radiation of uranium itself. As a result of enormous work, Marie and Pierre Curie, for the first time in the world, isolated from this waste two new hitherto unknown elements - posium and radium.

The radiation intensity of radium (it was actually radium chloride) turned out to be a million times higher than the radiation intensity of uranium. I dreamed that every gram of radium emits about 580 joyls within an hour. The continuous emission of the chemical element of energy was completely incomprehensible and, at first glance, seemed to contradict the law of conservation of energy.

It was radium's ability to emit, albeit very slowly, significant amounts of energy that most interested physicists. What causes radioactive substances to emit rays? What is the nature of these rays? Where do radioactive substances get the energy that they give off in the form of their tanning rays? Such questions worried physicists. This was the radium mystery. Many physicists have puzzled over its solution.

Experimental studies of radium radiation were continued by Marie and Pierre Curie. They placed radium in a magnetic field (between the strips of an electromagnet) and noticed an amazing phenomenon - the radium radiation noticeably weakened. The Curies agreed that the weakening of radium radiation in a magnetic field is associated with the deflection of some loaded particles in this field. These particles were called β particles. It is now known that β particles are electrons.

Rutherford discovered that in addition to the β particle, radium also emits a positively charged particle. They are called α particles. Finally, the part of radium radiation that is not affected by the magnetic field at all is called γ particles.

⟦folio 4⟧

If in the solar system the planets move under the influence of gravitational Newtonian forces, then in the atom electrons move around the charge under the influence of electric, Coulomb forces.

The “sun” was called the nucleus of an atom, which has a positive charge equal to the sum of the charges of the electrons rotating around it (so that the atom remains electrically neutral). This is the so-called Rutherford planetary model of the atom. The size of the nucleus does not exceed 10 thousandths of the size of the atom itself (just as the size of the Sun is much smaller than the size of the entire solar system). This is how a new term characterizing atom was born - the atomic core. The discovery of the nucleus marked the beginning of a new branch of physics, now called nuclear physics.

Studies of α-particle scattering on various elements of the periodic table led to an important discovery: the positive charge of the nucleus is numerically equal to the product of the serial number of a given element in the periodic table and the charge of the electron. This shed new light on the great law of Mendeleev, giving it a deep physical meaning. It turned out that the number of a chemical element in its value table is equal to the number of electrons rotating around the nucleus. It was also found that the nucleus contains positively charged particles - protons, the charge of which is equal to the charge of the electron, and the mass is approximately 1800 times greater than the mass of the electron.

At first, the study of the atomic nucleus was carried out only in a very small number of laboratories in the world. These are Rutherford's laboratorium in Cambridge, Madame Curie's laboratorium in Paris and D. V. Skobeln's laboratorium in Leningrad. However, by the end - the beginning of the thirties - the basic properties of the atoms themselves were well studied, and many physicists around the world turned their attention to the atomic nucleus.

In 1932, one of the largest discoveries in nuclear physics of the 20th century was made: the neutron was discovered - an electrical neutral particle whose mass is approximately equal to the mass of a proton. It became clear that the nucleus consists of protons and neutrons.

Indeed, by that time it was known that atoms having the same number in the periodic table can have different masses. Such atoms are called isotopes of a given element. The nuclei of atoms isotopes thus contain the same number of protons, but a different number of neutrons.

In 1934, the spouses Irene and Frederic Jolnot-Curie made an equally important discovery of artificial radioactivity. It consisted in the fact that light elements (for example, beryllium), when irradiated with alpha particles, themselves became radioactive, emitting neutrons.

When heavy elements are irradiated, such nuclei are not observed. The famous Italian physicist Fermi agreed that heavy elements have too much nuclear charge, and an α-particle having a charge of the same sign cannot come very close to it. A neutron that has no charge will not be acted upon by a positive charge by Coulomb forces. Fermi accomplished a series of wholesale goals of irradiating various elements with neutrons and discovered that virtually all elements become artificially radioactive after irradiation. After irradiation with a large source, α- and β-particles, γ-rays, and neutrons are emitted. When α- and β-particles were emitted, new chemical elements were formed.

Soon Fermi published a large work, which presented the results of his studies of almost all elements of the periodic table, which became radioactive after irradiation with neutrons. At the same time, he gave an almost complete description of the artificial radioactivity of each element. Thus, a new ensuing transformation of elements with appropriate irradiation was discovered.

If it were possible to isolate practical $^{235}\text{U}$ in its pure form, then, by ensuring its critical mass, it would be possible to obtain a huge amount of energy. As calculations have shown, the process of distribution of practical pure $^{235}\text{U}$ under the influence of neutrons occurs very quickly and has the character of an explosion (such calculations were carried out by Ya. B. Zeldovich, Yu. B. Khariton in the USSR and a number of foreign physicists). Moreover, for this purpose a special source of neutrons is not even required, because in the Earth’s atmosphere there is always a small amount of neutrons of cosmic origin, which is sufficient to cause an explosive reaction if uranium reaches a critical mass. It became clear that in this way it was possible to create a formidable weapon - an atomic bomb.

Many physicists around the world have begun a detailed study of the fission process of uranium nuclei. Almost every issue of physics journals around the world published scientific articles or notes elucidating details regarding the distribution of uranium nuclei. But obtaining pure $^{235}\text{U}$ in the required quantities, on the order of tens of kilograms, seemed fantastic at that time.

However, it soon became clear that a natural mixture of uranium isotopes could be subject to a nuclear chain reaction. To do this, you just need to somehow reduce the energy of the neutrons emitted during the fission of the $^{235}\text{U}$ nucleus. Fortunately, it turned out that the speed of neutrons can be adjusted to decrease it in a very simple way. To do this, it is enough to pass neutrons through a substance consisting of atoms, the mass of which differs little from the mass of the neutrons themselves. Then, when neutrons collide with such atoms, they quite quickly transfer part of their energy to these atoms, thereby reducing their speed to the level of the speed of thermal motion of the atoms.

Substances whose atomic mass is not too large are suitable for replacing neutrons. It is only necessary that these substances do not absorb neutrons, only the ability to replace them and distribute the corresponding uranium nuclei. Heavy water, which contains two atoms together, has the ability to absorb neutrons little.
