This, at first glance, mysterious phenomenon is explained by the electrical properties of the tip. Since charges of the same sign repel, they, flowing off the conductor, tend to move away from it as far as possible and accumulate in large numbers at the end of the tip. These charges attract charges of the opposite sign from the air, which “sit” on various parts suspended in the air. Having touched the tip, the particles are immediately recharged and, having acquired a charge of the same sign as on the conductor, fly away from the tip. The flow of these parts creates the thunderous wind.

The described property of the tip can be used to construct an electric analogue of the Segner wheel, which has been known since ancient times (rotating with the help of reactive force, water flowing out of its nozzles). Take the cork, hollow out its middle so that a metal thimble fits in there. Outside, insert several metal rods, bent in the form of hooks, placing them symmetrically. Now install this “wheel” on a metal vertical axis connected to the conductor of the electrophore machine. When the handle of the machine is rotated, the charges are deposited on the tips and, as in the previous experiment, a stream of recharging particles will be received from them. These flows cause the “wheel” to rotate.

The action of a lightning rod is based on this same property. Its inventor, Benjamin Franklin, extremely obscurely demonstrated the principle of operation of a lightning rod as follows. It is known that thunderclouds are always very uneven, some of them are located at different distances from the ground, some of them sometimes pass very low above the ground. Franklin believed that such descending “links” of a thundercloud are steps that clothe the lightning’s choice of path. To demonstrate this, he hung several cranberry papers from the conductor of the electrophore machine, attaching them to each other. When a charge was applied to them, the sticks were pulled towards the ground. Traying to the bottom of the cranberry needle, Franklin forced it to rise to the previous one and did this until all the paper returned to the conductor, as if he had not been charged.

The phenomenon of interference is easily demonstrated for water waves or radio waves. Observing interference patterns in optics is not easy, since conventional light sources (not lasers) emit oscillations with rapidly and randomly changing phases and are therefore loose. However, if the light from one source is divided into two beams and then these beams are superimposed on each other, then a fairly clear interference pattern can be obtained $^*$).

Note that for a correct description of the interference phenomenon, the error in determining the oscillation phase difference must be significantly less than $\pi$ (otherwise it will be impossible to distinguish the maximum from the minimum). In this case, the error in changing the wave path difference should be much less than the wavelength $\lambda$ (for light waves this error should be much less than 0.1 μm).

Problem 1. A plane electromagnetic wave of frequency $v$, generated by a horn antenna, falls perpendicularly onto a flat screen reflector. Determine the amplitude of the reflected wave if you change the direction of the electric field when moving between the screen and the horn, fixed by the maximum field amplitude $A_1$ and minimum $A_2$. Also determine the distance between two adjacent field maxima.

In the space between the horn and the reflecting screen, a superposition of the incident and reflected waves occurs. At the maximum points, the phases of the oscillations caused by these waves coincide, and, consequently, the resulting number of occurrences with the amplitude

$$A_1 = A_{\text{пад}} + A_{\text{отр}}.$$

At the minimum points, the incident and reflected waves are in antiphase, and the total amplitude of oscillations is equal to

$$A_2 = A_{\text{пад}} - A_{\text{отр}}.$$

From these two equalities we find

$$A_{\text{отр}} = \left( A_1 - A_2 \right) / 2.$$

Let the oscillations at the point with coordinate $x$ caused by the incident wave be described by the expression

$$a_{\text{пад}} = A_{\text{пад}} \cos \omega \left( t - \frac{x}{c} \right) = A_{\text{пад}} \cos \left( \omega t - \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} x \right).$$

If the distance from the horn to the screen is $l$, then the reflected wave travels a distance $2l - x$ to this point, and the oscillations caused by the reflected wave have the form

$$a_{\text{отр}} = A_{\text{отр}} \cos \omega \left( t - \frac{2l - x}{c} \right) = A_{\text{отр}} \cos \left( \omega t + \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} x - \frac{4\pi l}{\lambda} \right).$$

(In fact, when the wave is reflected, the phase of the oscillations may change to the opposite, but this cannot affect our further reasoning.)

Let two adjacent maxima be observed at points with coordinates $x_1$ and $x_2$. The phase differences between the oscillations caused by the incident and reflected waves at these points are equal, respectively

$$\left( \Delta \varphi \right)_1 = \left( \omega t - \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} x_1 \right) - \left( \omega t + \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} x_1 - \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} l \right) = - \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} x_1 + \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} l$$

And

$$\left( \Delta \varphi \right)_2 = - \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} x_2 + \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} l.$$
