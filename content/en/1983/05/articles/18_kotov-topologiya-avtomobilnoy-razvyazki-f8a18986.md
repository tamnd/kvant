⟦folio 52⟧

Topology
road junction

Four points on the border of a circle or square (for example, the vertices of a square) cannot be connected in pairs by non-intersecting lines (paths) drawn inside the figure. It is not difficult to verify this by picking up a pencil and paper, although knowledge from the field of topology is required for a solid document. If you replace the square with a piece of surface with a figured cutout and a “bridge”, like figure 1, the problem is easily solved.

Such topologically complex surfaces are not only of speculative interest - they are useful in the practice of designing and constructing automation, railway tracks, city street networks, etc. Looking at figure 1, the reader probably recognized the modern automatic cloverleaf interchange. At first glance, the ability to connect four points in pairs by non-intersecting paths means that it is possible to organize the continuous movement of automatic flows.

However, if you look more closely, you will notice that the diagram shown in figure 1 is not accurate enough. In fact, traffic flow is two-way, and oncoming flows should also not intersect, so the number of points and paths should be reduced. As shown in figure 2, each of the “departure” points $A, B, B, \Gamma$ should be connected by an oriented path to each of the “arrival” points $A', B', B', \Gamma'$. And on the surface of the “cloverleaf” it is impossible to do this without intersection.

Red circles on figure indicate points of intersection of traffic flows. True, these intersections occur in places where the flows are similar (for example, an automatic driver moving from point $A$ to point $B'$ must change lanes on the spot, since his lane crosses that of an automatist moving from point $B$ to point $B'$), but with heavy traffic, lane changes are also undesirable.

How are things? Perhaps it is possible to direct traffic flows through such an interchange differently? No, it is impossible to intersect in this way - this is a theorem that can prove topology.

But the same topological messages also tell us a way out of this situation: we must determine the “topological structure” of the junction. However, a solution that allows all traffic flows to be directed continuously is shown on the fourth page of our cover. Here, in the middle part of the surface, another structure with a figured cutout is formed, which connects the surfaces of the “bridge” with the transverse road located below.

It is possible that the solution shown will not satisfy the automatist’s designers. But in the practice of modern construction there are examples of topologically weighted nodes and junctions.
