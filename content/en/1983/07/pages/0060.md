Richard Mises attempted to put his concept of randomness into the basis of the theory of probability, but ran into gray logical difficulties. A convincing criticism of his approach was given by the Soviet mathematician A. Ya. Khinchin, and a satisfactory justification for the theory of probability was obtained in 1933 by A. N. However, the direction of research generated by Mises, dedicated to the division of the sequence

Richard Mises was born 100 years ago in Lviv. After graduating from the University of Vienna, he was then a professor at the Universities of Strasbourg and Berlin. Since 1933, Mises worked as a professor at Istanbul University, and since 1939 at Harvard (USA). Mises died in 1953.

On integer points of curves of the form

$$x^n + y^n = c^n$$

If $c$ is an integer, then the circle

$$x^2 + y^2 = c^2$$

(1) necessarily passes through points with integer coordinates, for example through points $(0, \pm c)$ and $(\pm c, 0)$, lying on the coordinate axes. Circle (1) can also pass through integer points that do not lie on the coordinate axes, for example, at $c = 5$ - through points $(3, 4), (4, 3)$ *).

Can a curve

$$x^n + y^n = c^n$$

(2) (where $n \geqslant 3, c > 0$) pass through the integer point $(x, y)$ for $x > 0$ and $y > 0$? In general, the answer to this question is unknown, but we will prove the following statement:

For any positive integer $c$, one can specify a number $N$, starting from which (that is, at $n \geqslant N$) curve (2) does not pass through integer points,

not lying on the coordinate axes.

Why is this so? Studying the graph of curve (2) at $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ is called the idea that for a sufficiently large $n$ this curve is pressed against the broken line $ABC$, the links of which go along the straight lines $AB$ and $BC$, given respectively by the equations $y = c$ and $x = c$, where $c$ is an integer (Fig. 1). But inside the “red corridor” of width 1, bounded by the broken line $ABC$, the coordinate axes and the broken line $A_1B_1C_1$ (see Fig. 1), there are no longer any integer points, and our curve lies entirely in this corridor with a sufficiently large $n$.

Based on these visual messages, we now present a rigorous proof of the formulated statement.

Let the given integer $c > 0$; curve (2) intersects the coordinate axes at points $(c, 0), (0, c)$. Its intersection point $T$ with the bissertric of the first square has the abscissa $x = \frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}$. In order for the curve to be entirely inside the “red corridor” (Fig. 2), it is enough to require that $c - 1 < \frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}$.

And the last inequality will be satisfied if we choose

$$n > \frac{\ln 2}{\ln c - \ln(c - 1)},$$

which can always be done, since the rights part of inequality is constant. There are no integer points in the “corridor”. This means that our curve does not pass through integer points that do not lie on the coordinate axes, which is what was required.
