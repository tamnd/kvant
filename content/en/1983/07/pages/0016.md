⟦folio 14⟧

CSE participants to the taiga. The specialist scientists first looked at them condescendingly, then good-naturedly, then with respect. Based on the results of the research, dissertations were written and only works were published. Now this is no longer a CSE, but a permanent group of researchers, led by the Commission on Meteorites and Cosmic Dust of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences and the Tomsk Branch of the All-Union Astronomical and Geodetic Society.

The CSE participants set a task: to build a mathematical model of the forest fall field. To do this, they systematically, dividing the taiga into squares, measured the azimuths of the fallen trees. The azimuths of 100 trees were successfully averaged. Each fall point corresponds to a certain average azimuth. Then mathematicians V. G. Fast and A. P. Boyarkina processed the results on a computer. The results are as follows: on average, the fallout is radial (which means there was an explosion!), but traces of symmetry are also observed. The axis of symmetry runs from east - southeast to west - northeast. It can be assumed that this direction corresponds to the azimuth of the trajectory of the Tunguska body. The overall shape of the bulge filled the butterfly. That's how it was called - a butterfly.

Studying the area of ​​“dead” forest in the center of the butterfly made it possible to estimate the height of the explosion at 5-10 km.

The construction of a forest fall model took almost 10 years of dedicated work. Now we have imagined using this model to try to reconstruct the picture of the explosion - to determine the slope of the trajectory, the intensity of energy release along it, and finally, the height of the explosion and its energy, and from it - the mass and speed of the Tunguska body.

This problem has been solved.

Calculations carried out on a computer for the following results: explosion energy - 4·10^16 J; speed of the Tunguska body - 40 km/s; the height of the explosion was estimated at 5-10 km, which was in good agreement with the data determined from the radius of the “dead” forest and from seismograph processing data.

The mass of the Tunguska body was estimated back in 1961 independently by two methods. Academician V.G. Fesenkov drew attention to the observations of the American astronomer Charles Abbott, who two weeks after the fall of the Tunguska meteorite recorded a weakening of solar radiation over California. Believing that during this time a cloud of dust formed during the explosion should have been there, V. G. Fesenkov was able to calculate the mass of the exploded body by weakening sunlight. It turned out to be about a million tons.

In the same year, the author of this article calculated the entry into the atmosphere of a family of bodies of different masses at different speeds (within the speed range characteristic of meteorites - from 15 to 60 km/s). It turned out that only a body with an initial mass of one million tons, flying at a speed of about 25 km/s, could reach a height of 5-10 km above the Earth and produce an explosion of energy that corresponded to the explosion of a forest: 10^16 J. As can be seen, both estimates coincided with the results of calculations using the explosion model: with an explosion energy of 4·10^16 J and a speed of 4·10^3 m/s, the body mass should be ~10^9 kg.

So, the explosion mechanics were knocked out. But his physics were all there
