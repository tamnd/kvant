Revolutionary
in science and life
V. P. LISHEVSKY

Many scientists are people of amazing destiny, but few have lived such an exciting, interesting, adventurous life as Gaspard Monge. A remarkable scientist (mathematician, mechanic, chemist and metallurgist) was also an active participant in the Great French bourgeois revolution: it was he who approved the death sentence of Louis XVVI with his signature. The son of poor parents, the revolutionary and Jacobin Monge, who fought against the privileges of knowledge, becomes a count and personal friend of Emperor Napoleon. After the restoration of the monarchy, the scientist was expelled from the French Academy of Sciences and ended his days in exile.

Gaspard Monge was born on May 10, 1746 in the small town of Beaune, located in eastern France. His father was an illiterate street vendor, but he was committed to giving his children the best education available at that time to a representative of the third estate. Gaspard's two brothers also became professors: the youngest, Jean, was a mathematician, hydrographess and navigation, the middle one, Louis, was a mathematician and astronomy. Interestingly, Louis Monge was a member of the La Perouse expedition - one of the three who survived it.

Considering Gaspar started at the age of six and soon became the best student at the school, its pride. After graduating from school in 1762, he entered the College of the Holy Trinity in Lyon, where he simultaneously studied and taught physics. The future scientist, as always, spent the summer of 1764 at home. And here his fate comes together

During his rest days, Gaspard, together with one of his friends, drew a plan of his hometown of Beaune. This plan was seen by the lieutenant colonel of the engineering service before Vinko and invited Monge to enter the Mezières school, which he headed, in the department that trained masters and voluntary engineers. Another department trained military engineers, but only children of nobles were accepted into it.

While studying at school, Monge became interested in a problem that has always faced military engineers: how to place fortifications on the ground so that they are least susceptible to destruction by enemy artillery, located at a certain point. Monge quickly solved the problem, but the school teachers did not look at his solutions, believing that the student could not cope with the complex problems required.

The French Legislative Assembly declares: “The Fatherland is in danger!” On August 10, 1792, a new stage of the revolution begins - the king is overthrown and the Provisional Executive Council, consisting of ministers elected by the Legislative Assembly, comes to power. This council includes Gaspard Monge as Minister of the Navy.

On September 21, 1792, at its first meeting, the newly elected Convention decided to govern the monarchy and proclaim a republic. The king was put on trial and sentenced to death. The sentence was approved by Monge, who at that time used the duties of chairman of the Provisional Executive Council (the position of chairman of the Council was held by ministers in turns).

The French Republic is in a difficult situation. Exhibition of weapons and food. The flop-trained, under-equipped and hungry soldiers fought on numerous fronts against overwhelmingly superior enemy forces. On behalf of the revolutionary government, Monge organizes the production of gunpowder, rifles, cables, and cannons. He is looking for reserves of salttire necessary for the production of gunpowder; under his leadership, iron factories and factories are switching to the production of weapons (the production of guns in Paris alone reaches 1000 pieces per day); Monge creates literary workshops for casting cannon tables. He trains the workers, takes care of their food, and he himself lives from hand to mouth. On Thermidor 9 (July 27), 1794, he carried out a counter-revolutionary coup. The leaders of the Jacobin dictatorship - Robespert, Saint-Just and others - were government officials. Monge, as an active Jacobin, had to hide for some time.

The convention closed the Academy of Sciences, the school of higher and secondary specialists. The production of weapons is being reduced, many workshops and factories are being rearranged - now Monge only teaches.

Polytechnic school

With the active participation of Monge, the Central School of Public Works was created, which was later transformed (September 1, 1795) into the Ecole Polytechnique - the famous Polytechnical School, the director of which Monge was for many years. This is his favorite child. Monge gives all his free time and even money (for scholarships to needy students) to the Polytechnic School. And the school lived up to his hopes. Over the years, such outstanding scientists as Ampère, Corilis, Gay-Lussac, Becquerel, Arago, Fresnel, Poinsot, Poisson, and many generations of top-class engineers have embroidered from it.

“No one taught as well as he,” Monge’s student, the famous engineer Brisson, later recalled. - Gestures, posture, voice modulation - everything served him to develop his thinking. He always followed the eyes of the ministers and knew how to guess the degree of understanding of each of them... We recognized Monge, this kindest man, attached to youth and devoted to science. He was always among us; After lectures on geometry, analysis and physics, private conversations were arranged, which further expanded and strengthened our abilities. He was a friend to every student, encouraged us to work, always helped and was always happy about our successes.”

Monge and Napoleon

Meanwhile, gatherings in the country went their way. In February 1796, the Directory appointed 26-year-old General Bonaparte commander-in-chief of the French troops fighting in Italy. In May of the same year, Monge left for Italy on instructions from the Directory. Here he met with the future emperor, which played such a big role in the life of the scientist.

Bonaparte and Monge had met once before (when the latter was Minister of the Navy), but the scientist did not remember the next visitor. Now Bonaparte, smiling, recalled the witness: “One young artillery officer... made a request to the Minister of the Navy in 1792. Perhaps the minister did not remember this incident - you never knew how many petitioners he had then... As for the unknown officer, he will never forget his attention.”

A trusting relationship was immediately established between the commander and the scientist. It was mutual sympathy between two smart people, which later turned into sincere friendship. Bonaparte did not let Monge leave him even one step. And although their relationship was not always cloudless, in the person of Monge - this disinterested, simple-minded scientist, incapable of the slightest falsehood, insincerity, intrigue, Napoleon Bonaparte found a true friend. The future emperor was not mistaken in his choice. Monge remained faithful to this friendship until his death.

When Bonaparte began his Egyptian campaign (1798-1799), Monge took part in it. This campaign almost ruined his life: he almost died during a plague epidemic. Only thanks to the chemist Bertola, who selflessly looked after the big Monge, did he recover.

The words of Napoleon, spoken by him in a moment of danger, refer precisely to the Egyptian campaign: “Donkeys and scientists in the middle!” Some people try to interpret this phrase as disrespect for scientists. Giving the data to the number of the future emperor, we nevertheless note that he ordered those whom he valued most to be placed in the middle of the square: scientists and animals who transported weapons, food and water.

In Egypt, Monge and other scientists who were part of Bonaparte's expedition carried out scientific work. Their goal was, as Monge defined it, to maintain a program for the education of Egypt. For this purpose, the Capri Institute is created, of which Monge is elected president, and Bonaparte is elected vice-president. French scientists compiled a “Description of Egypt,” studied ancient monuments and agriculture, and worked on a canal project that would create the Mediterranean and Red Seas.

The position of the French troops is deteriorating. And not only in Egypt. Suvorov liberates Italy from the French. The situation is difficult on other fronts. Under these conditions, Napoleon decides to return to Paris. In August 1799, he left the army and sailed to France. Together with him are Monge, Bertolet, Murat and other close people. On October 16, Bonaparte and his companions leave for Paris. He is greeted by enthusiastic crowds of people.
