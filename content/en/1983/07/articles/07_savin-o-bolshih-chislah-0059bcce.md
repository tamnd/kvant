About large numbers

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. P. SAVIN

Once, at a pioneer camp, I had the opportunity to attend a question and answer evening called “And who is the most...?” There were many different questions: “Who has the longest tail? What star is closest to Earth? But one girl asked:
- Which number is the largest?
Instead of answering, I suggested that she play this game.
“Here is a candy,” I said, “whoever names the highest number gets this candy.” Tell me your number!
“One hundred thousand million,” the girl blurted out.
“There is no such name,” I answered.

“No, there is,” insisted the girl.
- Okay, then I wished for two hundred thousand million. Shall we play again?
The rest of the guys made noise and started talking, interrupting each other.
“Quiet,” I said, “let’s talk one at a time, otherwise we won’t understand anything.” Of the ten guys who immediately raised their hands, I chose a short boy with glasses.
“The game is unfair,” he said, “no matter what number Masha names, you will name a number one more and go higher.” And in general, there is no such thing as a number: one can be added to any number.

- What if there are no zeros at the end? - one of the boys asked me.

“The fact is,” I said, “that in physical and other measurements, as a rule, only the first two or three digits are correct.” To obtain large numbers of correct signs for each number, for example, the mass of a planet or its distance, special precautions and special, very precise instruments are required. Therefore, in large numbers obtained from a regular experiment, only the first two or three digits are left, and the rest are replaced with zeros.

Imagine a board with 400 light bulbs arranged in a square $20 \times 20$. It’s easy to imagine; similar boards are found in large airports and train stations; there, with the help of illuminated lights, announcements about the arrival and departure of trains or planes are displayed. Now let’s think about how many different ways there are to light up the scoreboard $20 \times 20$.

Let's start counting the number of states of our scoreboard. For order, we number the light bulbs with numbers from 1 to 400. The first light bulb can be in two states: off and on. Two light bulbs can already be in four states. If we use the o sign to indicate an extinguished light bulb, and the + sign to indicate a lit light bulb, then these four states can be listed: 00, +0, 0+, ++0, 00+, +0+, 0++, +++. The amount of state satisfies the first two lamps when the third lamp is extinguished, plus the same amount of state when the third lamp is lit. It is easy to notice that when adding four lamp states, satisfying and becomes equal to $2^4 = 16$ again, with five lamp states it will be $2^5 = 32$, with ten – already $2^{10} = 1024$, and with 400 lamps – $2^{400}$. Let us show that this number is greater than a googol. Note that $2^{10} = 1024 > 10^3$, therefore $2^{400} = 2^{10 \cdot 40} > 10^3 \cdot 40 = 10^{120}$. So, with the help of our simple scoreboard, we were able to surpass googol.
