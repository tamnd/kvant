Equation of gas state

V. E. BELONUCHKIN

The equation of state of an ideal gas (or simply the equation of gas state, or the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation) describes the relationship between the state parameters - pressure, temperature and volume - of one of the simplest physical systems. The object is simple, so the equation of its state is quite simple:

$$pV = \frac{m}{\mu} RT,$$

where $p$ is the gas pressure, $V$ is its volume, $m$ is the mass, $\mu$ is the molar mass, $T$ is the temperature, $a$ is the universal gas constant.

So, according to the equation, the pressure of an ideal gas is proportional to its temperature. How should this be understood? Does this mean, for example, that when the temperature increases, the gas pressure always increases, and when the temperature decreases, it decreases? Of course not. This is certainly true only if neither the volume, nor the mass, nor the molar mass changes. Further, for a fixed mass of the gas being determined, with increasing temperature, the pressure may not increase, but decrease, if the volume occupied by the gas grows sufficiently quickly. Other situations are also possible. Please, it is most convenient to discuss them on specific problems (all problems are taken from the libraries of admission exams to the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology).

In particular, is the pressure in the vessel higher or lower than atmospheric?

In this case, the volume of the gas does not change, and the pressure is not proportional to the temperature. Is this possible?

Let us turn to the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation. In addition to the parameters p, V and T, it also includes m and μ. The molar mass of the gel should not change - the gel gas is monatomic and cannot dissociate. There shouldn't be any condensation either, as the temperature rises. Apparently, the only possibility is to change the mass m of the gaseous gel.

Since the pressure increases more slowly than it would in an isochoric process for a constant mass, the mass of the gas must decrease; that is, the gas must flow out of the vessel. This means that the pressure in the vessel is greater than atmospheric pressure, otherwise the gas would not flow out.

The Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation allows you to compare not only different states of one gas, but also the states of different gases. Here's an example.

Problem 3. Ball lightning is a faintly luminous ball of gas floating freely in the air. Created by the Stakhanov model (one of the models built to explain the nature and behavior of ball lightning), the gas inside the ball is a complex compound, part of which consists of a nitrogen ion associated with several water molecules. The electrons lost by nitrogen are captured by water, so that each complex molecule as a whole turns out to be neutral. Determine how many molecules of water bind each nitrogen, if the temperature inside the ball is t=600°C, and the temperature of the surrounding air is t0=20°C.

Since ball lightning floats in the air, the density of its constituent gas is equal to the density of the surrounding air. The pressure in lightning is apparently also equal to atmospheric pressure. These two conditions together give

$$\frac{p}{\varphi} = \frac{p_0}{\varphi_0},$$ or $$\frac{pV}{m} = \frac{p_0V_0}{m_0}$$

(here all quantities without an index refer to the compound, and with an index - to air).

Using the equation of gas state, we obtain

$$\frac{T}{\mu} = \frac{T_0}{\mu_0},$$

from where, knowing the molar mass of air ($\mu_0=29 \cdot 10^{-3}$ kg/mol), we find the molar mass of the complex compound:

$$\mu = \mu_0 \frac{T}{T_0} \approx 86 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ кг/моль.}$$

The molar mass of atomic nitrogen is $14 \cdot 10^{-3}$ kg/mol, and that of water is $18 \cdot 10^{-3}$ kg/mol; therefore, each nitrogen binds four water molecules.

The external conditions in which the gas is located can be changed in various ways, but each time the state of the gas can be described by the equation of gas state.

Problem 4. A cylindrical joint S=10 cm² is closed by a massive piston. There is gas inside. The vessel begins to be lifted with an acceleration of 2g. When the gas temperature became equal to the initial one, the volume of gas under the piston decreased by 1.5 times. Find the mass of the piston. External pressure $p_0=10^5$ Pa.

In a stationary state, the force of the piston balances the difference in gas pressure forces inside the vessel and outside:

$$mg = (p - p_0)S.$$

When the connection moves, the force acting on the piston gives it an acceleration of 2g, directed upward:

$$2mg = (1,5p - p_0)S - mg,$$ or

$$3mg = (1,5p - p_0)S$$

(at a constant temperature, the volume will decrease by 1.5 times; this means that the pressure will also increase by 1.5 times). Solving the two equations obtained jointly, we obtain

$$m = \frac{p_0S}{3g} \approx 3,4 \text{ кг.}$$

We have looked at several examples of using the equation of gas state (of course, they do not exhaust the range of problems in which the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation is used). However, no less important is the question of when this equation cannot be used, or, in other words, what is the scope of its application.

The ideal gas model was based on the assumption that the interaction energy of molecules is small compared to their kinetic energy. Molecules are considered as elastic balls that interact only through direct contact. The dimensions of these balls are negligible compared to the length of their free space.

Of course, such a model is very difficult. Indeed, how, for example, can we determine the sizes of molecules? Of course, it is reasonable to understand the so-called effective diameter of molecules as the distance at which the movement of molecules noticeably changes. It is clear that with such a definition, the size of the molecules must depend on many conditions, in particular on temperature. Experience confirms this - it shows that with increasing temperature the effective diameter of molecules decreases. This is understandable: with increasing temperature, the kinetic energy of molecules increases, the molecules must come close to each other so that the potential energy of their interaction is of the same order of magnitude as the kinetic energy (their motion will not change noticeably). True, it turns out that this estimate is weak and it is not so pointless to talk about specific constant values ​​of molecular sizes.

Significant deviations from the behavior of an ideal gas in real gases begin when it cannot be assumed that molecules do not interact most of the time, that is, when the length of the free space of molecules - from content to content - becomes comparable to the size of the molecules. In this case, the molecules are mainly located close to each other, and their interaction can no longer be neglected.

Under what conditions does this happen? Before answering this question, consider the following approach.

Task 5. Estimate the free length of air molecules under normal conditions. The diameter of the molecules is taken to be equal to $d = 3,7 \cdot 10^{-10}$ m.
