Black holes

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
Y. A. SMORODINSKY

General relativity has always sparked imagination with its predictions of amazing phenomena. Among all her predictions, the record beats the conclusion that there may be black holes in the Universe - celestial bodies with high density, the depths of which fall, causing almost everything in the vicinity to fall through.

It is still difficult to say whether the existence of black holes has been proven, and although natural scientists speculated about them more than 200 years ago, based on Newton’s theory of gravity, their discovery only began in our days, when they realized that they could be “seen.”

If black holes are real, then there is something for them in the Universe: they could take on the role of giant energy generators that ensure the evolution of the Universe. Perhaps astronomers recently saw such a generator when they received a photograph of the center of our galaxy. The science of black holes is just beginning. It is impossible to say what successes there will be and where disappointment awaits us, but physicists can tell a lot of interesting things about the properties of black holes.

Movement of bodies in the vicinity of a black hole

Consider a celestial body (star) with radius $R$ and mass $M$.

Let us assume that there are no other bodies of use and the gravitational field is created only by them alone. Let some other body (for simplicity of reasoning, whose mass and radius will be considered small compared to $M$ and $R$, respectively), marveling from infinity, falls into this gravitational field.

According to Kepler's laws, it will either move around the star in an ellipse, or, coming from infinity, it will go around the body along a parabola or hyperbola and go back to infinity, leaving the star forever.

Similarly, if a body begins its movement from the surface of a star, where it is given a certain speed, then it (unless, of course, it falls on the star) will either become a satellite of the star or will forever leave it.

In order to leave the star, the body must have enough energy to do work against gravitational forces. In other words, its kinetic energy $mv^2/2$ must be no less than $GmM/R$ (because this is the work that must be done to move the mass $m$ from the surface of the star to infinity*)):

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} \geq G \frac{mM}{R}.$$

From here you can find the minimum speed that allows the body to leave the star; it is called the second escape velocity:

$$v_{\text{II}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}.$$

If the radius of the star $R$ decreases, and its mass $M$ remains unchanged, then the speed $v_{\text{II}}$ increases and becomes much greater if $R$ tends to zero. In other words, a body falling in a straight line onto a point star acquires, formally, an infinitely greater speed.

*) See, for example, Appendix I to the article by K. Kikon “What is a potential well” («Kvant», 1982, No. 8) or paragraph 61 of the textbook “Algebra and the beginnings of analysis 9-10” (M., “Prosveshcheniye”, 1982).

This result contradicts the statement of the theory of relativity that no body can have a speed greater than the speed of light.

Our formula is not always correct and can be used only when the speed $v_{II}$ turns out to be much less than the speed of light $c$:

$$\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} \ll c.$$

This inequality can be written differently:

$$R \gg \frac{2GM}{c^2}.$$

The quantity on the right side plays an important role in the theory and is given a special name - the gravitational radius of the star:

$$R_{rp} = \frac{2GM}{c^2}.$$

Now the condition for the applicability of classical mechanics to the description of the motion of celestial bodies in the gravitational field will look like this:

$$R \gg R_{rp}.$$

The gravitational radius of most celestial bodies is very small. For the Sun it is approximately 3 km, for the Earth - 0.8 cm, that is, it is significantly smaller than their linear dimensions ($R_C = 7 \cdot 10^5$ km, $R_3 = 6,4 \cdot 10^3$ km). So usually the speed of a body, even falling from infinity on the surface of a star (or planet), is very small:

$$v_{II} = \sqrt{\frac{R_{rp}}{R}} c \ll c.$$

Is this inequality always preserved or is it possible that it would violate it? Of course, it is possible, but for this it is necessary, first of all, that the radius of the star - the source of the gravitational field - be at least equal to the gravitational radius. The sun, for example, would fall for this purpose approximately 230,000 times. Its density would be unimaginably large: $\sim 2 \times 10^{19}$ kg/m$^3$ (average density of the Sun $\sim 1,4 \cdot 10^3$ kg/m$^3$), greater than the density of an atomic nucleus (density of atomic nuclei $\sim 10^{17}$ kg/m$^3$). Nevertheless, these are precisely the objects that are meant when people talk about black holes—structures of incredible rolls of materials.

What distinguishes the behavior of the body near these objects? If a body moves from afar, as long as the distance $r$ to it is much greater than the gravitational radius: $r \gg R_{rp}$, its motion obeys Kepler’s law. If the body gets to a closer distance such that the ratio $R_{rp}/r$ is not too small, the movement takes on a completely different characteristic. (The formal reason for this is that the force of attraction ceases to obey the inverse square law - the law of universal gravitation. And this immediately violates the orderly classical picture of motion.) Finally, in the vicinity of a black hole, that is, at distances comparable to the gravitational radius, the attraction to the black hole will be so great that no body can pass through the mismo - it pulls it in and disappears in its “black” depths. In classical Putean mechanics, a comet, for example, can fly as close as possible to the center of attraction and still go away from it to infinity. A black hole gathers around itself everything that flies relatively close. The ray of light also behaves in an untrained manner. Passing around a black hole, the beam is deflected so strongly that its trajectory twists into a spiral and, thus, it is also captured by the black hole.

Can any body (or at least a light beam) leave a black hole? It turns out not, because even the speed of light is not enough to fly out. No body energy is enough to overcome the colossal gravitational field of a black hole.

A little history

The theory of black holes has a long history. The unexpected side of it is that the connection between the radius and mass of the black hole was discovered years ago. And although the grounds for this discovery were false, it deserves to be told about it.

In the 18th century in England, scientific communications were sent in the form of letters and read at a meeting of the Royal Society (the so-called English Academy of Sciences). However, such a letter was sent by one of the members of this society, Bachelor of Wealth John Michel, to another member - the famous Henry Cavendish *). It was read at a meeting on November 27, 1783. As we now know from the Cavendish Notes, published by Maxwell, Cavendish also drew attention to the possibility of the existence of objects that we now call black holes. Maybe that’s why Michel reported his messages to him.

Let us quote an excerpt from his letter, which was published in the famous journal Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society in 1784: “... if the radius of a ball, which has the same density as the Sun, exceeds the radius of the Sun in the ratio of 500: 1, then a body falling on the ball from an infinitely great height will acquire on its surface a speed exceeding the speed of light. It can be concluded, assuming that light, like other bodies, attracts with a force proportional to its mass, that the light emitted by such a ball will be forced back due to its own gravity. This was the first theoretical proposal.

Where did Michel get his assessment from? Let's try to answer this question by solving the inverse problem - we will find what the ratio of the radii of the ball in question and the Sun should be so that the cosmic speed for the ball becomes equal to the speed of light.

We will proceed from the expression for the second escape velocity, in which the mass of the body $M$ will be expressed through its density $q$:

$$v^2 = \frac{2GM}{R} = \frac{8\pi G}{3} qR^2.$$

This shows that when constructing the density, $v_{\text{II}}$ is proportional to $R$. Therefore, in order for the speed $v_{\text{II}}$ to be equal to the speed of light $c$, the radius of the ball $R_{\text{ш}}$ must be as many times greater than the radius of the Sun $R_{\text{с}}$ as the speed of light is greater than the second cosmic speed for the Sun:

$$\frac{R_{\text{ш}}}{R_{\text{с}}} = \frac{c}{v_{\text{II}} c}.$$

Or, using the formula $v_{\text{II}} c = \sqrt{R_{\text{rp}} C / R_{\text{с}}} c$, -

$$\frac{R_{\text{ш}}}{R_{\text{с}}} = \sqrt{\frac{R_{\text{с}}}{R_{\text{rp}} C}}.$$

Knowing that $R_{\text{с}} \approx 700\ 000$ km, and $R_{\text{rp}} C \approx 3$ km, we obtain for the ratio of radii a value of ≈ 480, which is indeed close to 500.

Note that Michel, when calculating the second escape velocity for the Sun, of course, could not use the understandable boundary of the radius (at that time such a point simply did not exist). How did he count?

Michel knew the speed of the Earth around the Sun: $v = 30$ km/s and the ratio of the radius of the Earth’s orbit $R$ to the radius of the Sun $R_{\text{с}}$:

$$k = \frac{R}{R_{\text{с}}} \approx \frac{150 \cdot 10^6 \text{ km}}{0,7 \cdot 10^6 \text{ km}} \approx 214.$$

According to Newton's second law, the Earth's orbital speed

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}.$$

To leave the solar system from Earth's orbit, you need speed

$$v' = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}.$$

which is $\sqrt{2}$ times the speed $v$.

Black hole radiation

As we have already said, nothing comes out of the depths of a black hole, which is why it is called black. Nevertheless, the location of the black hole makes itself known by a powerful flux of radiation. Distant celestial bodies - quasars, located at a distance of billions of light years from us, emit energy hundreds of times greater than the energy emitted by entire galaxies. We don’t know what’s happening in these areas. There is a hypothesis that the sources of energy are giant black holes, the mass of which exceeds the mass of the Sun by 10⁶—10⁹ times and which were born along with the Universe. Unfortunately, testing this hypothesis is very difficult.

Astronomers (assume that in the center of our Galaxy there is a black hole with a mass of about 10⁹ solar masses. Another one revolves around it - approximately the same (or even more) mass, but with a larger radius. In this system, flows of matter arise, rushing in the direction of the black hole. These are both small particles and large bodies - the sedges of stars torn apart by the huge gravitational forces of the black hole. They generate flows gravitational and electromagnetic waves.

Radiating energy, the particles are constantly approaching the surface of the black hole and at the same time losing their kinetic energy. But the supply of this energy is constantly replenished due to a decrease in potential energy. (The potential energy on the surface of a black hole can be estimated using the Newtonian formula:

$$E_{\pi} = -G \frac{mM}{R_{\text{гр}}} = -\frac{1}{2} mc^2.$$

When calculated using the formulas of the general relative theory, the coefficient 1/2 disappears.) Therefore, a body falling from an infinitely large distance (when $E_{\pi} = 0$) has an energy reserve equal to the sum of the kinetic energy $mv^2/2$ (provided that $v \ll c$) and the rest energy $mc^2$. During the fall, this reserve is almost completely lost.

Thus, from a theoretical point of view, a black hole turns out to be a mechanism that is capable of “processing” the entire energy of particles, including rest energy, into radiation. Such an “engine”, capable of operating with an efficiency coefficient very close to unity, is unique. It would be unjust if nature did not realize this opportunity.
