Let's heat the water from above

V. I. PENTEGOV

People heat water every day and almost always place the heater below, under a vessel with water. This is understandable - the convection of the liquid occurring throughout the entire volume of the vessel ensures uniform heating of the entire liquid to the required temperature.

What happens if the heater is placed on top? This question is not different. Let us remember that 70% of the earth's surface is covered with water, for which the main source of energy is the Sun. So in nature, on a huge scale, water is heated from above. Many of the processes associated with this are so complex that there is still no consensus on their causes. For example, relatively recently it was found that the temperature of water in the ocean changes with depth not by swimming, but by jumps. These jumps occur in a very narrow gap between layers in which the temperature is constant. Within each layer, the density and “salinity” (percentage of soli) of water are also constant. Moving Border

After observing the interface between different layers of liquid for some time, we will find that it is moving. Experimental graphs of the dependence of the thickness of the upper layer of water $h$ on time $t$ and the speed of movement of boundaries $v$ on the thickness of the upper layer, more precisely, on the difference in levels $h-h_0$ (see Fig. 1), are shown in Figures 3 and 4 (measurements were carried out for a 3-liter jar at a heater power $P \approx 120$ W).

Please note that noticeable movement of the boundaries occurs only when the temperature of the upper layer is close to or equal to the boiling point - as soon as the heater is turned off, the movement of the boundaries stops. It can be assumed that the movement of the boundaries is a consequence of intense movement in the hot layer (which will occur precisely at high temperatures). To check this, let's do the following. Turn off the heater (the movement of the boundaries will stop) and begin by some mechanical means to carefully move the water in the upper layer. As we expected, the border is starting to move again.

Based on the above facts, we can offer the following explanation for the movement of borders. Let's look at the interface between the layers of hot and cold water at some point in time and see a very small area on it (Fig. 5,a; here the red dots represent hot water molecules, blue dots represent cold water molecules). As a result of the process of diffusion and coduarized molecules, “hot” molecules appear under the interface. A thin layer of water becomes hot and after some time is “washed away” by convective flows of hot water (Fig. 5, b and θ). This microprocess leads to the downward movement of boundaries.

So, in the case of heating a liquid from above, heat is transferred not only through thermal conductivity (which is completely natural), but also

Let's try to answer one more question that arose during the experiments: does the movement of the boundary depend on the heating power?

Obviously, the thickness of the upper layer cannot be less than the depth of level $a-a$ (see Fig. 1), since convection will always occur above. At the same time, at first it cannot be greater than the depth of level $b-b$, since there is no convection below yet (with flat or precise heating, levels $a-a$ and $b-b$ coincide). Consequently, there is a certain initial thickness of the top layer, determined mainly by the depth of heating immersion. The movement of the boundary begins with the beginning of intense movement of liquid in this layer. As already mentioned, this will occur at a temperature close to the boiling point. This means that the heater must ensure heating of a layer of liquid of initial thickness almost to boiling in the presence of heat losses in the surrounding space. These conditions determine the minimum heating power at which the boundary begins to move.

From the above experiments, several main conclusions can be drawn. Here they are:

1) The interface between warm and cold water can exist for a long time without collapsing only in the presence of convective fluid flows at the boundaries: these flows.link show the consequences of diffusion, maintaining the boundary.

2) The border moves, and the speed of movement depends on the temperature difference. As the difference decreases, the speed decreases until the boundaries stop, but the boundary itself is preserved if the fluid flow at the boundaries continues.

3) Large temperature differences (and high water heating temperatures) in the experiments were necessary only so that convection currents would arise in the hot liquid.

Guess and assumptions

Now let's get back to the conversation about solar heating of water in the ocean and try to answer some questions. Why do the boundaries between different layers in the ocean not move over time, although there is a gradual equalization of temperature and “salinity”? Why does the leveling of “salinity” occur much slower than the equalization of temperature?

This can again be explained by convection, which exists in all layers of the ocean (and not just in the hot layer, as in our experiments).

Let's consider two adjacent layers. As a result of diffusion and collision between molecules, both “cold” and “hot” molecules appear on both sides of the interface. They are picked up by convective currents and carried away from the boundaries. Moreover, if convection is equally intense on both sides of the boundary, the boundary itself does not move, although energy transfer occurs and temperatures gradually equalize.

As for equalizing the “salinity” of the water, this process is much slower. This is due to the surface tension that the interface has. It is known that the coefficient of surface tension of the solution layer is greater than that of pure water. The boundary, like any physical system, tends to have minimal surface energy. Therefore, the molecules into which the molecules break up in water are mostly located far from the boundaries, and their diffusion begins to noticeably manifest itself only when the surface tension of the boundary decreases significantly.

The appearance of the boundary between warm and cold water can probably be attributed not only to the layering of the ocean. It is possible that this phenomenon necessitates the existence of four and stable boundaries of ocean currents and the recently discovered giant outlets in the ocean, as well as much, much more.

Application

Let us denote by $W$ the power of the heater (let it be greater than the minimum). Some part of this power is wasted on the evaporation of the liquid, but if the temperature is constant, then these losses are also constant. Therefore, for convenience, we introduce the value $W_1$, equal to the power of the heater and the power looking for the evaporation of the liquid and we will consider it constant.

It is natural to assume that the power $W_2$, finishing in the surrounding space through the side surface of the vessel, is proportional to the thickness $h$ of the hot layer: $W_2 = kh$. The proportionality coefficient $k$ depends only on the experimental conditions and does not change during the experiment.

Let the boundary move a distance $dh$ during time $dt$. This means that a layer of liquid with a thickness of $dh$ was heated from the temperature of the cold layer to the temperature of the hot layer, that is, by $\Delta T$ degrees. This requires an amount of heat $dQ = cQ\Delta Tdh = k_1dh$ (here $c$ is the specific temperature of the liquid, $Q$ is its density, $S$ is the horizontal sectional area of ​​the vessel).

According to the law of conservation of energy,
$$W_1dt = dQ + W_2dt,$$
or
$$W_1dt = k_1dh + khdt.$$
Let's rewrite this equality differently:
$$\frac{dh}{W_1 - kh} = \frac{dt}{k_1},$$
Let’s integrate it and obtain the dependence of the thickness of the hot layer $h$ on time $t$:
$$h = a - be^{-at},$$
where $a = W_1/k$, $b = W_1/k - h_0$ and $\alpha = k/k_1$ are constant coefficients, $h_0$ is the initial depth at which the boundary is formed.

Now let’s find the speed of movement of the boundaries $v$:
$$v = \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{W_1 - kh}{k_1}.$$
From this it can be seen that the speed of the boundary linearly depends on the thickness of the layer, and at the next thickness ($\frac{равной}{W_1/k}$) the speed should become equal to zero. This happens because as the thickness increases, energy losses increase and there must be a moment when all the energy from the heater goes into the surrounding space.

At the moment the boundary stops, the convective flows of the gliding do not weaken so much that the movement of liquid practically stops, and due to diffusion and thermal conductivity, the boundary will gradually “disintegrate.”
