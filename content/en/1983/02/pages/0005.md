⟦folio 3⟧

First of all, we must not forget that universal literacy did not exist from time immemorial. Like programming, it had its own origin, emergence and development. We are accustomed to the fact that the USSR is a country of practical complete literacy; But 100 years ago in Russia only about 20% were literate. There are still about 800 million illiterate people on Earth.

Further, both the basis for universal literacy and the basis for the widespread dissemination of the program is a technical invention: the printing press and the computer, respectively. The development and spread of printing led to universal literacy. The development and spread of computers will lead to universal programming.

Just as the word “literacy” now has a much broader meaning than just the ability to write and read, the word “programming” takes on a broader meaning. This is not just the ability to write computer programs in some specialized language, but the ability to draw up clear, targeted rules for action in a wide variety of situations. In this broad sense, programs are as much an organizational ability of a person as literacy. This ability is prepared by the organization of the human nervous system; it is inherent in people at work, when communicating with each other, in contemplating nature and in interacting with it.

Finally, although literacy and programming can, of course, be compared in many ways, finding many analogies between them, the most important thing, perhaps, is that they complement the others and together form a new idea of ​​the harmony of the human mind.

It’s worth dwelling on the last point a little.

We all remember the simple and succinct words of Maxim Gorky: “Love books, the source of knowledge.” We understand literacy as the ability to express knowledge in the form of text. However, the problem remains: how to move from knowledge to action. "Inform

actions with words, and words with action,” says one of Shakespeare’s heroes. It is here - in the transition from knowledge to action - that programming arises.

In the pre-book period, the unity of word and deed was realized naturally and clearly. With words and example, the master (teacher) taught the younger (student) the job. Understanding was not a purely mental insight, but the achievement of a set goal, mastery of the techniques of the matter being studied.

The appearance of the book dealt a severe blow to the unity of word and deed. The process of accumulating knowledge was separated from its application, new forms of thirst for knowledge and new desires for realizing the truth appeared. All languages ​​have developed special expressions for people who prefer books in their quest for knowledge. In these expressions there was a tinge of disdain, generally unfair, but sometimes deserved.

Gradually it became clear that the bookish accumulation of knowledge required new mechanisms for transforming this knowledge into action. Thus, the task of programming appeared long before the computer. The advent of computers only made the problem of programming relevant, just as printing made the problem of literacy relevant.

But literacy and the ability to act by themselves do not know anything. In this contrast, it is difficult to say from which we suffer more: from ignorance or inactivity. Goethe warned: “There is nothing more terrible than activity.” However, experience suggests that it grows when the active life position of educated and cultured people is insufficient. Hence our desire for a second literacy - not only for learning to write commands, but for the perception of a person who is decisive and prudent at the same time.
