Waves underwater

From the photograph shown in figure 2.6, it is clear that transverse waves can occur at the interface. (By the way, they said that the border can be seen without tinting - due to the scattering and reflection of light from the waves on it.) Therefore, the border has surface tension. A clear illustration of this can be, for example, the reflection of vortex wheels from boundaries.

Turn off the heater (to reduce the movement of liquid in the hot layer) and drop ink from a pipette from a height of 1-2 cm above the water level. A vortex ring is formed in the water, moving downward. If the energy of the ring is not very high, it will be elastically reflected from the boundaries (in this case the boundary will bend somewhat) and then collapse; if the energy of the ring reaches more, it can pass through the boundary, but immediately after that it will collapse (Fig. 2,θ).

Moving Border

After observing the interface between different layers of liquid for some time, we will find that it is moving. Experimental graphs of the dependence of the thickness of the upper layer of water $h$ on time $t$ and the speed of movement of boundaries $v$ on the thickness of the upper layer, more precisely, on the difference in levels $h-h_0$ (see Fig. 1), are shown in Figures 3 and 4 (measurements were carried out for a 3-liter jar at a heater power $P \approx 120$ W).

Please note that noticeable movement of the boundaries occurs only when the temperature of the upper layer is close to or equal to the boiling point - as soon as the heater is turned off, the movement of the boundaries stops. It can be assumed that the movement of the boundaries is a consequence of intense movement in the hot layer (which will occur precisely at high temperatures). To check this, let's do the following. Turn off the heater (the movement of the boundaries will stop) and begin by some mechanical means to carefully move the water in the upper layer. As we expected, the border is starting to move again.

Based on the above facts, we can offer the following explanation for the movement of borders. Let's look at the interface between the layers of hot and cold water at some point in time and see a very small area on it (Fig. 5,a; here the red dots represent hot water molecules, blue dots represent cold water molecules). As a result of the process of diffusion and coduarized molecules, “hot” molecules appear under the interface. A thin layer of water becomes hot and after some time is “washed away” by convective flows of hot water (Fig. 5, b and θ). This microprocess leads to the downward movement of boundaries.
