⟦folio 13⟧

In the 18th century in England, scientific communications were sent in the form of letters and read at a meeting of the Royal Society (the so-called English Academy of Sciences). However, such a letter was sent by one of the members of this society, Bachelor of Wealth John Michel, to another member - the famous Henry Cavendish *). It was read at a meeting on November 27, 1783. As we now know from the Cavendish Notes, published by Maxwell, Cavendish also drew attention to the possibility of the existence of objects that we now call black holes. Maybe that’s why Michel reported his messages to him.

Let us quote an excerpt from his letter, which was published in the famous journal Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society in 1784: “... if the radius of a ball, which has the same density as the Sun, exceeds the radius of the Sun in the ratio of 500: 1, then a body falling on the ball from an infinitely great height will acquire on its surface a speed exceeding the speed of light. It can be concluded, assuming that light, like other bodies, attracts with a force proportional to its mass, that the light emitted by such a ball will be forced back due to its own gravity. This was the first theoretical proposal.

Where did Michel get his assessment from? Let's try to answer this question by solving the inverse problem - we will find what the ratio of the radii of the ball in question and the Sun should be so that the cosmic speed for the ball becomes equal to the speed of light.

We will proceed from the expression for the second escape velocity, in which the mass of the body $M$ will be expressed through its density $q$:

$$v^2 = \frac{2GM}{R} = \frac{8\pi G}{3} qR^2.$$

This shows that when constructing the density, $v_{\text{II}}$ is proportional to $R$. Therefore, in order for the speed $v_{\text{II}}$ to be equal to the speed of light $c$, the radius of the ball $R_{\text{ш}}$ must be as many times greater than the radius of the Sun $R_{\text{с}}$ as the speed of light is greater than the second cosmic speed for the Sun:

$$\frac{R_{\text{ш}}}{R_{\text{с}}} = \frac{c}{v_{\text{II}} c}.$$

Or, using the formula $v_{\text{II}} c = \sqrt{R_{\text{rp}} C / R_{\text{с}}} c$, -

$$\frac{R_{\text{ш}}}{R_{\text{с}}} = \sqrt{\frac{R_{\text{с}}}{R_{\text{rp}} C}}.$$

Knowing that $R_{\text{с}} \approx 700\ 000$ km, and $R_{\text{rp}} C \approx 3$ km, we obtain for the ratio of radii a value of ≈ 480, which is indeed close to 500.

Note that Michel, when calculating the second escape velocity for the Sun, of course, could not use the understandable boundary of the radius (at that time such a point simply did not exist). How did he count?

Michel knew the speed of the Earth around the Sun: $v = 30$ km/s and the ratio of the radius of the Earth’s orbit $R$ to the radius of the Sun $R_{\text{с}}$:

$$k = \frac{R}{R_{\text{с}}} \approx \frac{150 \cdot 10^6 \text{ km}}{0,7 \cdot 10^6 \text{ km}} \approx 214.$$

According to Newton's second law, the Earth's orbital speed

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}.$$

To leave the solar system from Earth's orbit, you need speed

$$v' = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}.$$

which is $\sqrt{2}$ times the speed $v$.
