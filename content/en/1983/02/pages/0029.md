We have looked at several examples of using the equation of gas state (of course, they do not exhaust the range of problems in which the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation is used). However, no less important is the question of when this equation cannot be used, or, in other words, what is the scope of its application.

The ideal gas model was based on the assumption that the interaction energy of molecules is small compared to their kinetic energy. Molecules are considered as elastic balls that interact only through direct contact. The dimensions of these balls are negligible compared to the length of their free space.

Of course, such a model is very difficult. Indeed, how, for example, can we determine the sizes of molecules? Of course, it is reasonable to understand the so-called effective diameter of molecules as the distance at which the movement of molecules noticeably changes. It is clear that with such a definition, the size of the molecules must depend on many conditions, in particular on temperature. Experience confirms this - it shows that with increasing temperature the effective diameter of molecules decreases. This is understandable: with increasing temperature, the kinetic energy of molecules increases, the molecules must come close to each other so that the potential energy of their interaction is of the same order of magnitude as the kinetic energy (their motion will not change noticeably). True, it turns out that this estimate is weak and it is not so pointless to talk about specific constant values ​​of molecular sizes.

Significant deviations from the behavior of an ideal gas in real gases begin when it cannot be assumed that molecules do not interact most of the time, that is, when the length of the free space of molecules - from content to content - becomes comparable to the size of the molecules. In this case, the molecules are mainly located close to each other, and their interaction can no longer be neglected.

Under what conditions does this happen? Before answering this question, consider the following approach.

Task 5. Estimate the free length of air molecules under normal conditions. The diameter of the molecules is taken to be equal to $d = 3,7 \cdot 10^{-10}$ m.

A molecule collides with another molecule if the distance between their centers is less than the diameter of the molecules $d$. Let the molecule travel along paths $l$ over some time. At the same time, she collided with molecules whose centers are located in a broken cylinder (kinks at the points of collision) of length $l$ and cross-section $\pi d^2$. The number of molecules in this volume $n l \pi d^2$ ($n$ is the number of molecules per unit volume) is the total number of hundredths. Let's calculate from one collision to another, that is, the length of the free collision of molecules:

$$\lambda = \frac{l}{n l \pi d^2} = \frac{1}{n \pi d^2} = \frac{RT}{N_A p \pi d^2} \approx 8,75 \cdot 10^{-8} \text{ м}$$

(here pressure $p = 10^5$ Pa, temperature $T = 273$ K, Avogadro number $N_A = 6,02 \cdot 10^{23}$ mol$^{-1}$). The experimental value for such conditions is $6,20 \cdot 10^{-8}$ m. The divergence of the connection is mainly due to the fact that we considered all molecules, except the chosen one, to be immobile. A detailed analysis, beyond the scope of a school course, shows that taking into account the relative motion of molecules leads to a change in the length of the free gap by a factor of $1/\sqrt{2}$. Multiplying the result we obtained by this moment, we get $6,19 \cdot 10^{-8}$ m.
