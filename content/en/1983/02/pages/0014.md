⟦folio 12⟧

This result contradicts the statement of the theory of relativity that no body can have a speed greater than the speed of light.

Our formula is not always correct and can be used only when the speed $v_{II}$ turns out to be much less than the speed of light $c$:

$$\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} \ll c.$$

This inequality can be written differently:

$$R \gg \frac{2GM}{c^2}.$$

The quantity on the right side plays an important role in the theory and is given a special name - the gravitational radius of the star:

$$R_{rp} = \frac{2GM}{c^2}.$$

Now the condition for the applicability of classical mechanics to the description of the motion of celestial bodies in the gravitational field will look like this:

$$R \gg R_{rp}.$$

The gravitational radius of most celestial bodies is very small. For the Sun it is approximately 3 km, for the Earth - 0.8 cm, that is, it is significantly smaller than their linear dimensions ($R_C = 7 \cdot 10^5$ km, $R_3 = 6,4 \cdot 10^3$ km). So usually the speed of a body, even falling from infinity on the surface of a star (or planet), is very small:

$$v_{II} = \sqrt{\frac{R_{rp}}{R}} c \ll c.$$

Is this inequality always preserved or is it possible that it would violate it? Of course, it is possible, but for this it is necessary, first of all, that the radius of the star - the source of the gravitational field - be at least equal to the gravitational radius. The sun, for example, would fall for this purpose approximately 230,000 times. Its density would be unimaginably large: $\sim 2 \times 10^{19}$ kg/m$^3$ (average density of the Sun $\sim 1,4 \cdot 10^3$ kg/m$^3$), greater than the density of an atomic nucleus (density of atomic nuclei $\sim 10^{17}$ kg/m$^3$). Nevertheless, these are precisely the objects that are meant when people talk about black holes—structures of incredible rolls of materials.

What distinguishes the behavior of the body near these objects? If a body moves from afar, as long as the distance $r$ to it is much greater than the gravitational radius: $r \gg R_{rp}$, its motion obeys Kepler’s law. If the body gets to a closer distance such that the ratio $R_{rp}/r$ is not too small, the movement takes on a completely different characteristic. (The formal reason for this is that the force of attraction ceases to obey the inverse square law - the law of universal gravitation. And this immediately violates the orderly classical picture of motion.) Finally, in the vicinity of a black hole, that is, at distances comparable to the gravitational radius, the attraction to the black hole will be so great that no body can pass through the mismo - it pulls it in and disappears in its “black” depths. In classical Putean mechanics, a comet, for example, can fly as close as possible to the center of attraction and still go away from it to infinity. A black hole gathers around itself everything that flies relatively close. The ray of light also behaves in an untrained manner. Passing around a black hole, the beam is deflected so strongly that its trajectory twists into a spiral and, thus, it is also captured by the black hole.

Can any body (or at least a light beam) leave a black hole? It turns out not, because even the speed of light is not enough to fly out. No body energy is enough to overcome the colossal gravitational field of a black hole.

A little history

The theory of black holes has a long history. The unexpected side of it is that the connection between the radius and mass of the black hole was discovered years ago. And although the grounds for this discovery were false, it deserves to be told about it.
