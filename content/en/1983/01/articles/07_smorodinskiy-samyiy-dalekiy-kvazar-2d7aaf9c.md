The most distant quasar

At the beginning of last year, a message appeared in the press that English and Australian astronomers had discovered a new luminous object, which is located farthest from us from all other celestial objects. It turned out that this is a quasar located in the constellation Sagittarius (its equatorial coordinates: declination +33° and right ascension 1.3 s).

Quasars (quasi-stars) were discovered about twenty years ago. This is how distant cosmic bodies began to be called, which in appearance in photographs resemble stars, but their radiation in power is comparable to the radiation of entire galaxies. (For example, the quasar discussed here emits 10¹⁴ times more energy than the Sun.) Quasars are not located in our Galaxy, but very far beyond its borders, at distances of several billion light years.

How can you determine the distance to a quasar? It turns out that to do this you need to measure its red shift. Let's explain what this means.

When comparing the spectra of the chemical elements that make up quasars with the spectra of the same elements on Earth, it was discovered that the lines in the spectra of quasars are strongly shifted towards longer wavelengths, that is, towards the red end of the spectrum. If we denote the observed wavelength by λ, and by λ₀ it is called the redshift. It is characteristic that for all lines of the spectrum of a given quasar z is the same. On the other hand, it is known that the wavelength emitted by a source changes as the source moves and is observed relative to each other. (This effect is called the Doppler effect.) In particular, if the source moves away from the observer, the observed one detects a longer wavelength.

Thus, it was concluded that quasars are moving away from us, and moving away at very high speeds. To do this, one should refer to the formula

$$\lambda = \lambda_0 \sqrt{\frac{c+v}{c-v}},$$

where c is the speed of light, and v is the speed of the source relative to the observer. Given the redshift z, the velocity v can be found from this formula. Thus, the speed of the external quasar is v=0.92 s. This is the fastest speed ever observed.

Now that the speed of the quasar is known, the distance to it can also be estimated. To do this, we need to take advantage of the fact that the indicated speed v is related to the distance R to the quasar by the Hubble law (the law of the “scattering” of galaxies):

$$v = HR.$$

The quantity H is called the Hubble constant. Its exact meaning is difficult to change; currently, based on long-term observational values, H≈2.5·10⁻¹⁸c⁻¹.

For a quasar moving away from us at a speed of 0.92 s, we obtain R≈1.1·10²⁶m≈1.2·10¹⁰ sv. years.

In other words, we can say that we see this quasar as it was approximately 12 billion years ago. If we remember that the age of the Universe is estimated at 13-15 billion years, the “antiquity” of the discovered quasar, of course, should be highly respected.
