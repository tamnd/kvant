Pyramids and cube

Y. A. SMORODINSKY

I wanted to play with a simple but beautiful puzzle that I came across in one of Martin Gardner's articles.

I went to a mechanical workshop and asked them to make three parts for me according to two drawings (Fig. 1). The mechanic I contacted was almost offended, thinking that I was playing him stupidly. But I didn’t even think of joking - the drawings were made according to all the rules of technical drawing (see Fig. 2).

(Without looking any further, try to draw these details or imagine what they look like.)

Trying to make the task easier, I decided to make only three parts together from six according to a different drawing (Fig. 3). Since this drawing turned out to be difficult to decipher right away, I did not test the mechanic’s patience and glued together models from paper myself, from which it would be easy to make them from metal. But they never got around to it: the paper parts were quite suitable for the puzzle. (You can make these parts using the reamer shown in figure 4).

The parts themselves are shown on figure 5. The reader will figure out which drawing is used to glue which part.

Take a cube made of three parts of the second type with two fingers, as shown in figure 6, and carefully rotate it around the resulting axis (this axis is the spatial diagonal of the cube). When rotated by 120° and 240°, each of the three parts will move to its place of settlement. In such cases, they say that the figure (cube) has an axis symmetrical to the triangle (see Fig. 6).

It is easy to imagine how to divide an apple into three identical parts - “thirds”; but such a “third” - one third of a cube - has probably never been seen before.

This “third” has a symmetry plane - it can be cut by a plane perpendicular to the square base so that this base is divided into two congested triangles. It turns out that there are two prims - congruent, but not identical; they are mirrored, that is, they relate to each other, like an object and its image in a mirror.

It's funny that it is usually difficult for a person to distinguish mirror-symmetrical objects. Take two primids from set figure 5 and, placing them far from each other, ask a friend whether they are the same or mirrored. You will see how this question will not be at all simple for many.

You can test yourself this way. You probably have a dice. Everyone knows that the sum of points on opposite sides is seven. Therefore, by looking at one face, you can immediately tell how many points are scored on the opposite face. But you can ask a slightly more difficult question: how many points should be put on the side with the opposite sign (Fig. 7)? Try to guess and compare with your dice.

* * *

Answering the last question, you probably noticed that, in principle, you can arrange the glasses in two ways - mirror each other. This means that there are two types of cups - “right” and “left” (Fig. 8). In games, they usually use the “left” dice - at least, they say that this is the case in gambling houses. In children's games, there is also a “right” cube - not everyone knows that there is a difference.
