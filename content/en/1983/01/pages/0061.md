River meanders

Let us find the shape of the surface of a liquid rotating in a glass with an angular velocity $\omega$. Let's turn to figure 1 (see p. 18). If the linear size of the selected cube is $\Delta r$, then its mass is equal to

$$\Delta m=\varrho \cdot \Delta s \cdot \Delta r.$$

Using this expression for $\Delta m$, from equation (1) and (2) (see p. 18) we obtain:

$$\Delta h=(\omega^2/g)r \cdot \Delta r.$$

Thus, the tangent of the angle $\alpha$ tangent to the curve depicting the cross section of the free surface of the liquid at figure 1 increases in proportion to the distance from the center:

$$tg \alpha=\Delta h/\Delta r=(\omega^2/g)r.$$

If the height of the liquid level on the rotation axis is $h_0$, then in order to find it at a distance $r$, you need to add to $h_0$ the average value $tg\alpha$ multiplied by $r$. The average value of a quantity that changes according to a linear law can be easily found by taking the result obtained.

$$h(r)=h_0+\frac{\omega^2 r}{2g} r.$$

As you can see, the dependence $h(r)$ is square, and its graph is represented by a parabola. (Those familiar with integral calculus will, of course, understand that we calculated the integral in this way.)

It is curious that the parabolicity of the shape of a rotating fluid was used in practice to create a telecopole. The famous American physicist Robert Wood built a telecopole with a parabolic mirror by placing a vessel with mercury at the bottom of a rotating well.
