The blades of a working fan are practically invisible to us - they merge into one translucent disk. And with the help of a stroscope you can see them without leaving the fan. This is why a stroscope is useful because it allows you to observe only fast processes that our eye, due to its inertia, is not able to follow. The diagram of one of these experiments is shown in figure 1.

But it’s even more convenient to work with a stroscope, which creates intermittent lighting (Fig. 2). To do this, a rotating cardboard disk with slots must be placed in front of a light source. An ordinary round flashlight powered by three batteries is suitable as a light source. It produces enough light and its light beam is well focused. The disk can be driven by a motor from an electric constructor. To change the rotation speed, you need to turn on the rheostat at 40-50 Ohms and change its resistance. Observations are carried out in the dark - the object under study is illuminated only by the flashing light of a stroscope.

Using such a stroscope, you can, for example, change the speed and acceleration of bodies moving forward (this is described in the physics textbook for grade 8: “Physics 8”, 1982, p. 39). But please, the most effective experience is to observe the disintegration of a thin stream of liquid into droplets. Imagine, for example, a gentle stream of water flowing from a water tap. From the hydrodynamic principle of jet continuity it follows that the free jet, as the velocity of the liquid increases (in the earth's field of gravity), should decrease over the flow. And if it were not for the damaged tension, the cross-section of the jet would decrease indefinitely. The initial tension damage leads to the fact that, with a very small cross-section, the jet begins to disintegrate into drops. This is explained by the fact that the energy of the damaging tension of a spherical drop is less than that of a cylindrical jet, and any system always tends to a state with the lowest energy.
