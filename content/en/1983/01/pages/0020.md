Let us mentally select a cube of mass $\Delta m$ inside the liquid at a distance $r$ from the axis of rotation (at figure 1 such a cube is highlighted in a different color). At an angular velocity of rotation $\omega^2 r$, this acceleration is created by the difference in pressure forces acting on the side faces (left and right at figure 1) of the cube. Hence,

$$\Delta m \cdot \omega^2 r = F_1 - F_2 = (p_1 - p_2) \cdot \Delta s,$$

(1)

where $\Delta s$ is the area of ​​the side face of the cube. Pressures $p_1$ and $p_2$ are determined by distances $h_1$ and $h_2$ to the free surface of the liquid -

$$p_1 = \rho gh_1, \quad p_2 = \rho gh_2,$$

(2)

where $\rho$ is the fluid density, $g$ is the acceleration of free pressure. Since the force $F_1$ must be greater than the force $F_2$, then, consequently, $h_1$ must be greater than $h_2$, that is, the free surface of the liquid during rotation should bend as shown in figure 1. The greater the rotation speed, the more the surfaces are distorted.

You can find the shape of a curved liquid surface. It turns out to be a parabola, that is, a surface whose cut is a parabola. (Show this yourself. You can check whether your reasoning is correct by looking at p. 59).

While we stir the tea with a spoon, we keep the liquid swirling. But if you take the spoon out of the glass, then due to friction between the individual layers of liquid (viscosity) and friction against the walls and up to the glass, the knitetic energy will be converted into heat, and the liquid will gradually remain.

As the rotational speed decreases, the surface of the liquid straightens. In this case, output flows arise inside the liquid, the direction of which is shown in figure 2. The origin of output flows is associated with unequal braking of the liquid at the bottom of the glass and at the surface. At depth, due to the high friction on the bottom of the glass, the liquid slows down more strongly than at the surface. Therefore, for partial flows of communication, it is unequally decelerated by the liquid - the closer you are to the bottom of the glass, the lower the speed. And the equal forces of “lateral” pressure acting on such particles are the same. This force can no longer cause the necessary deceleration of the liquid throughout its entire depth (as in the case of rotation of a mass of liquid with the same angular velocity). At the surface, the angular velocity is too high, and water particles are thrown towards the walls of the glass; at the bottom, the angular velocity is small, and the resulting pressure force forces the water to move towards the center.

Now it’s clear why the jars gather in the center at the bottom of the glass (figure 3) - they are carried away by the vortex flows that arise during braking. Of course, this consideration is quite simplified, but it correctly reflects the essence of the phenomenon.
