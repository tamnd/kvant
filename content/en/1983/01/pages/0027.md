Task 7. At least one of the tetrahedra $K_0L_0M_0N$ or $K_0L_0M_0N'$ has a perimeter greater than the tetrahedron $K_0L_0M_0N_*$).

And since each maximal tetrahedron has a vertex that coincides with one of the points $A, B, C$ or $D$, then we only need to consider this degenerate case.

Task 8. Let each of the points $K, L, M$ and $N$ coincide with one of the points $A, B, C$ or $D$. Then

$$P_{KLMN} < \frac{4}{3} P_{ABCD}$$

Thus concludes Spivak's decision. We see that for a professional mathematician it can be concentrated in one phrase: due to Weierstrass’s theorem, a solution to the problem of a complex tetrahedron of maximum perimeter exists, and due to the convexity of the distance functions, this solution is concentrated at the vertices of the tetrahedron ABCD, which makes the general problem degenerate (problem 8).

Note that the author's solution was also based on Problem 7: its slightly more subtle application allows us to avoid reference to Weierstrass's theorem**).

We could end here, but a few more words - incidental. The last solution reflects an important principle for the maximum and minimum problem: the maximum value of a convex function on a polygon (if it exists) is achieved at the vertex of the polygon. This principle plays an important role in linear programming - a branch of mathematics that has great application in economics (see about this, for example, in article 3.
