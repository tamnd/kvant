1. Consider the ring $K$ sliding along the wire (Fig. 5). Two forces act on it: the tension of the cable $\vec{T}$ and the reaction of the wire $\vec{F}$, directed perpendicular to the wire (since there is no tension). Under the influence of these rings, it slides along the wire with acceleration. If we determine from the kinematic messages the acceleration of the ring at a given time in the conditions, then it will be possible to find the desired tension of the cable.

Let us introduce an oblique coordinate system by directing the $X$ and $Y$ axes, as shown in figure 5. At any time, the distance between

the ring and the dog are equal to the length of the cable $l$, and the coordinates $x$ of the ring and $y$ of the dog are related to $l$ by the cosine theorem:

$l^2 = x^2 + y^2 - 2xy \cos 120^\circ = x^2 + y^2 + xy$.

Let us differentiate this equality twice in time:

$2(x')^2 + 2xx'' + 2(y')^2 + 2yy'' + 2x'y' + xy'' + yx'' = 0$.

Since the dog runs at a constant speed $v_0$, at any moment of time $y' = v_0$ and $y'' = 0$. Since the cable is unreactive, the projections of the speeds of the ring and the dog on the direction of the cable $KC$ are the same, and at a given moment of time the modules of these velocities are also the same: $v = v_0$, $x' = -v_0$ (the “minus” sign is associated with the choice of the positive direction of the axis $X$). coordinates $x$ and $y$ can be related to the cable length $l$ using the sine theorem:

$x = y = l \text{tg} 30^\circ$.

Thus, the acceleration of the ring at the considered moment in time is equal to
