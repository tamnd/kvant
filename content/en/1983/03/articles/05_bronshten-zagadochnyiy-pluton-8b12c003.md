Mysterious Pluto

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. A. BRONSHT.EN

In 1930, that is, a little over fifty years ago, the ninth planet of the solar system, the most distant from the Sun, Pluto, was discovered. Relatively quickly, astronomical definitions determine the elements of the orbit of a new planet - the average distance to the Sun, the degree of elongation (eccentricity) of the orbit and the inclination to the plane of the Earth's orbit.

It turned out to be much more difficult to determine the physical characteristics of Pluto - its size, mass, density. For a long time, for some reason, it was believed that Pluto was practically no different in size and mass from the Earth. Such estimates were even included in astronomical reference books. However, in 1950, astronomical measurements measured the diameter of Pluto’s disk and found it to be approximately 5000 km (recall that the diameter of the Earth is ≈ 12,750 km). Fifteen years later, observations of the occlusion of a faint star by Pluto showed that the diameter of the planet was even smaller, in any case not exceeding 4400 km.

In the early 70s, scientists tried to clarify the mass of Pluto by the appearance it created in the movement of the neighboring planet, Neptune. They arrived at a mass estimate of 0.11 Earth masses. But it is immediately clear that this estimate is dependent, since with a diameter of even 4400 km it led to an unrealistically large value for the average density of Pluto - about 15 g/cm³ *) (of the planets of the Solar system, the Earth has the highest density - 5.5 g/cm³).

The discovery of Pluto's moon, named Charon, in 1978 made it possible to determine the mass of the planet based on its orbital period and the radius of its satellite's orbit. It turned out to weigh only 0.002 times the mass of the Earth (that is, six times less than the mass of the Moon). That is why all attempts to determine the mass of Pluto by appearing in the movement of Neptune were in advance turned to failure - with such an insignificant mass, Pluto could not in any way influence the movement of its 7500 times more massive body.

Recently a new way of measuring the diameters of celestial bodies was invented. This made it possible to determine the size of Pluto with greater accuracy than before. It turned out that its diameter ranges from 3000 to 3600 km (this is about one and a half times less than the diameter of Mercury, which was considered the smallest planet in the solar system).

From estimates of mass and diameter it follows that Pluto’s density is from 0.5 to 0.8 g/cm³. Until then, Saturn was considered the most dense planet: its average density is 0.7 g/cm³. Clarifying the size of Pluto will show whether it will take precedence over Saturn in terms of lowest density.

And another unique record was “set” by Pluto. Its satellite Charon is the only planet in motion in terms of mass. Previously, it was believed that the highest ratio of the mass of the planet to the mass of the satellite is in the Earth-Moon system, where it is equal to 81.

More recently, it was discovered that Pluto’s brightness is fluctuating and will also weaken intensively, despite the planet’s approach to the Sun (it will pass perigelian in 1990). So far there is no explanation for this. Possibly future research into the problem with not being there yet.

*) In astronomy, it is customary to express the densities of celestial bodies in g/cm³, and not in SI units. (Editor's note)
