Conducting sphere in power machine problems

S. A. YURSKY

The spherical surface is one of the simplest in geometry. Close to its surface in nature there are many bodies: the Sun and the Earth, raindrops and rowan berries, and even electrons in our imagination are drawn as small balls.

Spherical bodies are part of the problem in power machine problems. This is often due to the fact that spherical symmetry allows a significant use of many mathematical calculations without compromising the physical content.

Before proceeding to the analysis of specific tasks, we will discuss some general issues of power machines.

In an isolated system of bodies, no matter what changes are made in it, the algebraic sum of positive and negative charges remains constant.

Electrical interactions can be described quantitatively. In the simplest case of two exact charges $q_1$ and $q_2$, located in a vacuum at a distance of $r$ from each other, the force of interaction between them is determined by Coulomb’s law

$$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2},$$

where $k$ is a numerical coefficient, depending on the choice of system of units. In SI it is written as $k = 1 / (4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ (here $\varepsilon_0$ is the electrical constant). Imprecise charges, of course, also act on each other, but their interaction strength is no longer determined by the given formula. In this case, charged bodies should be mentally divided into exact charges, find the interaction force for each pair of charges, and then add all the resulting values.

As you know, electric charges interact through the electric field they create. To quantitatively describe this field, merging and energy characteristics are used: field strength and its potential. The voltage $\vec{E}$ of the field at a given point is equal to the force acting on a unit positive charge placed at this point, and the potential $\phi$ of a given point is equal to the work done by the field to move a unit positive charge from this point to infinity, or, which is the same, the work of external forces to move a unit positive charge from infinity to a given point. By “infinity” we mean a region of space that is truly remote from all electric charges and that there is no field in it.

From the above definition of potential it is clear that only the potential difference between the points under consideration is of practical importance, and not the absolute value of the potentials of the points. Therefore, the beginning of the potential report can be chosen arbitrarily. As a rule, the potential of a point at infinity or the potential of any point connected by a conductor to the Earth (that is, a grounded point) is taken as zero. In this regard, let's talk a little about grounding bodies.

The earth is a conducting body of enormous size. It has a significant negative electrical charge. A different positive space charge is contained in the atmosphere, in a high layer of the order of tens of kilometers. At the Earth's surface, the field strength is ≈ 130 V/m. Considering the Earth to be a conducting ball and knowing the field strength at the surface, we can estimate the magnitude of the Earth's charge:

With such a connection, the charge and potential of the Earth practically do not change, although some charge can transfer from the body to the Earth or vice versa. Since the Earth is compared to any terrestrial body of space to infinity and its potential is constant, we agreed to take this potential as zero. Thus, grounding the conductor means giving it zero potential.

To obtain charge voltage model

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$$

and potential

$$\varphi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}.$$

All experimental facts indicate that electric fields have a substitutive property: the electric field of a system of sources is the sum of the fields of individual sources. In other words, the field of each fixed charge is independent of the presence of other charges. To find the resulting intensity at a certain point, it is enough to vectorially add the intensity of the fields created at this point by individual charges. To find the total potential, it is enough at a given point to take the algebraic sum of the potentials of the fields created by each charge separately, and the point of zero potential must, of course, be common to all charges. This principle of finding the resulting field is called the superposition principle.

The electric field is often depicted graphically either using tension lines (field lines) or using equivalent potential surfaces. Lines of force are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces, therefore, knowing one picture, you can easily obtain another.

The word “conductor” usually means a conducting, that is, containing free charges, body of any size and shape, most often metal. Inside a conductor placed in an electric field, the direction of the field is always zero, and all points of the conductor have the same potential. The absence of a field in a conductor does not mean that an external field does not penetrate into it. Quite the contrary, thanks to the penetration of the field into the conductor, charges appear on its surface, the field of which inside the conductor compensates for the external field. Thus, free charges in a conductor can only be located on its surface.

To characterize the ability of conductors to accumulate electrical charges, a special physical quantity is introduced - electrical capacity. A capacitor has a large electrical capacity - a system of two conductors (separated by a thin layer of dielectric) charged with equal but different charges. The capacitance of the capacitor $C$ is called the ratio of the charge $q$ of one of the conductors to the potential difference $\Delta \varphi$ between them: $C = q / (\Delta \varphi)$. It depends on the size, shape and relative position of the conductors, as well as on the electrical properties of the dielectric.

Problem 3. Two concentric metal spheres of radii $R_1$ and

$R_2$ have charges $q_1$ and $q_2$. Draw graphs of tension and potential versus distance $r$ to the center of the system.

Consider the case of a spherical capacitor ($q_1 = -q_2$) and find its em-capacity.

Let $q_1 > q_2$. Then

$$\begin{align*}
\text{при } 0 < r < R_1 & \left\{ \begin{array}{l}
E = 0, \\
\varphi = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{q_1}{R_1} + \frac{q_2}{R_2} \right);
\end{array} \right. \\
\text{при } R_1 < r < R_2 & \left\{ \begin{array}{l}
E = \frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} r^2, \\
\varphi = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{q_1}{r} + \frac{q_2}{R_2} \right);
\end{array} \right. \\
\text{при } r > R_2 & \left\{ \begin{array}{l}
E = \frac{q_1 + q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} r^2, \\
\varphi = \frac{q_1 + q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} r.
\end{array} \right.
\end{align*}$$

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} r^2, \varphi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{R_2} \right).$$

Problem 4. An uncharged metal ball of radius r surrounding a conducting shell of radius R with potential φ. What will the shell potential be equal to if the ball is grounded (Fig. 9)?

Before grounding, the shell potential φ is created by some charge q: φ = $\frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R}$. Since the ball is not charged, it has the same potential. After grounding, the potential of the ball became zero. This means that some kind of charge has appeared on it. We find its values ​​$q'$ from the condition that the total potential created by charges q and $q'$ on the surface of the ball is equal to zero:

$$\frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R} + \frac{q'}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r} = 0, \text{ и } q' = -\frac{r}{R} q.$$

Now it is easy to find a new shell potential, which is created by the same charges q and $q'$:

$$\varphi' = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R} + \frac{q'}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R} = \frac{q(R-r)}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R^2} = \frac{R-r}{R} \varphi.$$
