Mössbauer effect or resonant nuclear absorption of γ quanta in crystals

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences Yu. A. SAMARSKY

A little about resonance

Even people far from physics are well aware of a wide variety of resonance phenomena. Who hasn’t fiddled with the radio dial, tuning to the desired wave? Or didn’t the glass in the window frame become woody due to the mismo passing of the tram? Many probably remember the folklore story about how a company of soldiers, marching in formation around a place and accidentally falling in time with its own vibrations, collapsed along with the place into the river.

In these and many other cases, resonance—a sharp increase in the amplitude of oscillations—occurs when the frequency of the external influence on the system coincides with the natural frequency of oscillations of this system. If you play a note on a musical instrument near a piano, the piano will respond with the sound of that particular note: the frequency of the sound emitted by the instrument (the frequency of the external influence) coincides with the natural frequency of vibrations; the strings of the piano correspond.

For the receiver circuit of a radio receiver, the external influence is radiovolts from the transmitting station, and the natural frequency is determined by the parameters of the circuit - resistance, inductance and capacitance, which we change by rotating the tuning knob.

Using radio reception as an example, it is convenient to discuss another side of the resonance phenomenon. In addition to the frequency at which we want to tune the receiver, the accuracy of the setting is very important. With a good receiver, even a slight turn of the knob dramatically changes the conditions of the receiver. This means that the receiving circuit has a sharp resonance. The quantity characterizing the sharpness of the resonance is called the quality factor Q and is determined by the ratio of the resonant frequency vₚ to the width Δv of the frequency interval in which the resonant phenomenon can be observed (figure 1):

$$Q = \frac{v_p}{\Delta v}.$$

In modern radio circuits, the quality factor is approximately 200÷300.

Resonance in the atom

Typical resonant systems are also atoms, molecules and atomic nuclei.

Nayka about the laws of motion of microparts - quantum mechanics - was able to explain many phenomena that occur in the atom, and, in particular, the ability of atoms to absorb and emit photons only with a certain energy.

This energy is usually between several hundred and several million electrons, that is, many orders of magnitude greater than the energy of visible light. This is how γ-radiation arises. The observed spectrum of γ-rays is always discrete, which allows us to speak about the discreteness of nuclear levels. The scheme of energy levels for nuclear transitions is depicted in exactly the same way as the scheme of transitions in the atom. As in the atom, due to the finite lifetime, the excited levels of the nucleus have a natural width Γ (see figure 3). The blurring of the excited levels of the nucleus leads to the appearance of the width of the spectral lines of γ-radiation.

The nuclei of atoms can not only emit, but also absorb photons. This process is possible only for γ-quanta of certain energy and, therefore, also has a resonant nature.

It would seem that resonant absorption of γ-rays should be easily observed. To do this, it is enough to pass a stream of γ-quanta emitted by a radioactive source through an absorber substance containing the same nuclei in an unexcited (ground) state. However, this is not true. The fact is that the energy $E_\gamma$ carried away by the γ-quantum turns out to be less than the transition energy between levels, and it is not enough to excite the absorber nucleus. Although a small, but noticeable fraction of the energy is carried away by the source nucleus, which, according to the law of conservation of momentum, begins to move in the direction opposite to the direction of emission of the γ-quantum (like ordine after the shot).

Let us carry out some simple estimates for a free nucleus (atom).

Let the emitting nucleus be free and initially end, and the emitted γ-quantum correspond to the nuclear transition from the first excited level to the ground level (figure 3). According to the law of conservation of momentum, after the emission of a γ-quantum, the nucleus will receive a recoil momentum equal in absolute value to the momentum of the $\gamma$-quantum: $p_\gamma = p_\gamma = E_\gamma/c$. As a result, the nucleus begins to move at a speed of $v_\gamma = p_\gamma/m_\gamma$, carrying away the recoil energy $R$, equal to

$$R = \frac{m_\gamma v_\gamma^2}{2} = \frac{p_\gamma^2}{2m_\gamma} = \frac{E_\gamma^2}{2m_\gamma c^2}. \quad (4)$$

According to the law of conservation of energy, $E_\gamma + R = E_{21}$, where $E_{21}$ is the distance (difference in energy) between the ground and first excited levels of the nucleus. Since always $R \ll E_\gamma$, in formula (4) $E_\gamma$ can be replaced by the transition energy $E_{21}$:

$$R \approx \frac{E_{21}^2}{2m_\gamma c^2}. \quad (5)$$

Let us estimate the values ​​of $R$. As an example, let us consider the distance between the nucleus of the isotope $^{119}\text{Sn}$, which has $E_{21} = 2,38 \cdot 10^4$ eV, the lifetime of the excited level is $\tau = 1,8 \cdot 10^{-8}$ s, and the natural width of the level is $\Gamma_2 = h/2\pi t = 3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ eV. The recoil energy in this case is

$$R \approx \frac{E_{21}^2}{2m_\gamma c^2} \approx \frac{(2,38 \cdot 10^4)^2 (\text{эВ})^2}{2 \cdot 119 \cdot 10^9 \text{эВ}} \approx 2,5 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{эВ}.$$

The energy that is spent on the recoil of a nucleus that has absorbed a γ-quantum turns out to be exactly the same. This picture is shown in figure 4: the emission line is shifted by amounts $R$ to the left of $E_2$, and the absorption line is shifted by the same amount to the right of $E_2$ (distances along the $E$ axis given).

What effect does the shift $R$ have on the resonant absorption of γ quanta? The magnitude of $R$ in itself does not seem to be of significant interest; it is important to correspond to the width Г of the resonance line. It is easy to see that resonant absorption is possible only if the emission and absorption spectra overlap, that is, provided

$$2R \leq \Gamma. \quad (6)$$

And this condition is almost never satisfied for γ-transitions in free

How is γ-resonance observed? In principle, it is possible to compensate for the 2R energy shift using the Doppler effect, which consists of changing the frequency of radiation, and therefore the energy of photons, when the source (or absorber) moves *). Indeed, if the recoil movement destroys the resonance, then the opposite movement, compensating for the recoil, should restore it. But what should the speed of movement be?

Let us consider the case when the absorption core ends, and the emitting core moves towards it. Let the frequency of the stationary source nucleus be equal to $v_\gamma$. Then, when this nucleus moves with a speed of $v$, the frequency of its radiation will change, and in relation to the stationary absorber core it will be equal to $v = (1 + v/c) v_\gamma$. Accordingly, the energy of emitted photons will be $E = hv = (1 + v/c) hv_\gamma = (1 + v/c) E_\gamma$, where $E_\gamma = hv_\gamma$ is the energy of photons that are emitted by a stationary absorber core.

So, when the source nucleus moves “toward,” the energy of the photns emitted by it and falling on the stationary absorber nucleus increases by amounts

$$\delta E = E - E_\gamma = E_\gamma \frac{v}{c}.$$  (7)

If it turns out that $\delta E = 2R$, then γ-resonance should be observed. Using formula (7), it is easy to calculate at what speed $v$ this condition is satisfied:

$$\dot{v} = c \frac{2R}{E_\gamma}.$$  (8)

Thus, for $^{119}$Sn nuclei this speed is approximately 60 m/s.

Experiments on other nuclei based on this idea were carried out in 1953 by P. Moon and A. Storust. They looked at a source with excited mercury nuclei on the arm of a centrifuge. The arm was rotated, and then a resonance was observed in the resting mercury absorber. The speed of movement required to compensate for the nuclear recoil in mercury was on the order of the speed of sound! Similar experiments on γ-resonance had limited application, were technically complex and therefore were carried out extremely rarely.

This continued until 1958, when the position of the radial changed thanks to the discovery of the young German physicist Rudolf Mössbauer. The discovery was that Mössbauer had found how to avoid spending some of the energy on recoil of nuclei and observe γ-resonance without recoil. If the experiments of Munn and Storust showed how to compensate for recoil, then the experiments of Mössbauer showed how to destroy it.

In order to save the gun from moving when it is protruding and thereby transfer all the energy of the charge to the bullet, it is very important to support the gun and connect it to some massive body. But what should we “rest” the nucleus against, emitting or absorbing a γ-quantum, and how can we make it motionless? It turns out that under certain conditions the nuclei can be “fixed” in the crystal lattice. It was the crystal lattice, in which the nuclei were firmly “bound” to each other by chains of chemical bonds, that played the role of a massive body preventing separation.

How does the process of absorption and emission of γ-quanta of the nucleus occurring in the crystal lattice occur?

A much simpler case is when the energy of separation exceeds the binding energy of the nucleus in the lattice, which is quite large - it amounts to 10–30 eV. Formula (4) shows that such a case is realized only at high energies of γ-quanta ($E_\gamma > 1$ MeV). In this case, binding the nuclei becomes unimportant, and we are essentially dealing with a free kernel.

When γ-quanta with $E_\gamma < 1$ MeV are emitted, the energy of the individual is not retained to tear the nucleus out of the crystal lattice, and the momentum of the nucleus in one form or another is transferred to the entire crystal. Most often, the energy of the particle turns into sound vibrations of the lattice. This process is very visible. As a result, the core is separated from its place, but does not lose connection with the lattice. It transfers its impulse to neighboring nuclei, they transmit it to their neighbors - and a mechanical wave appears in the crystal lattice.

It is known that a crystal lattice is a quantum system, which, like an atom, cannot arise in an arbitrary manner. “Due to the quantization of the crystal’s own energy, it can absorb energy only in discrete portions. When the temperature decreases, the probability of the emergence of the crystal’s own levels decreases, therefore, when γ-quanta of low energy ($E_\gamma \lesssim 100$ keV) are emitted, some of the transitions occur in such a way that the impulse is separately perceived by the entire crystal as a whole,” wrote R. Mösbacher in his country. Thus, a case has arisen in the crystal when no vibrations occur at all! Then the entire grid as a whole experiences recoil. In formula (5), which determines the energy of a separate nucleus, the mass of the entire crystal should now be substituted. As a result, the energy of the separation is understood to be 20 orders of magnitude and turns out to be much less than the linewidth Γ. In this case, γ resonance is relatively easily observed.

This elastic, that is, without separation, emission and absorption of γ-quanta in crystals is called the Mösbacher effect.

Of course, we cannot trace every single atom or γ-quantum taken. All processes considered are random in nature. Therefore, it is clear that even at low temperatures, not all nuclei in a crystal emit γ quanta without separation, but only a small part of them. The lower the temperature, the greater the proportion of the nucleus that emits “resonant” γ quanta. In the first experiments of Mösbacher, where radioactive pridinium $^{191}$Ir was used as a source of γ-rays, at a temperature of liquid nitrogen $T \approx 80$ K the fraction of resonant quanta did not exceed several processes. At present, radioactive compounds are known whose radiation, even at room temperature, accounts for almost half of the total radiation. To carry out γ-resonance optics with such sources, they do not require cooling at all.

A remarkable feature of γ-resonance is its extremely high quality factor. Thus, for the tin isotope $^{119}$Sn with a γ-quanta energy $E = 2,38 \cdot 10^4$ eV and a resonance level width $\Gamma = 3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ eV, the quality factor is equal to

$$Q = \frac{E}{\Gamma} \approx 0,8 \cdot 10^{12}.$$

This is almost 20 thousand times better than the value for optical resonance.

The high quality factor of the γ-resonance turned it into an instrument of exceptional precision. For example, for tin, it is enough, using the Doppler effect, to change the energy of γ quanta by one part-trillion in order to go beyond the natural width of the excited level and destroy the resonance. The method for observing the resonant absorption of γ quanta, proposed by Mössbauer, is based on this “detuning” condition. Let's consider it.

Let the source and absorber nuclei be in identical crystals and at the same temperature. Then the emission line completely overlaps with the linear absorption line, and the number of γ-quanta resonantly absorbed by the absorber will be maximum when the source and absorber are stationary ($v=0$). If now the absorber (or source) is given a small speed, then a shift in the emission and absorption lines will occur (it is called an additional shift), and the resonance will be disrupted. This destruction of resonance occurs even at negligible speeds.

Let us estimate the velocity values ​​for $^{119}$Sn. Substituting into formula (8) instead of the natural $R$ line width $\Gamma=3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ eV, we find that to disrupt the resonance it is enough to mix the absorber or source at a speed of the order of a millimeter per second! (Instead of 60 m/s to obtain resonance on free nuclei.) Thus, by changing the energy of γ quanta using the Doppler effect, one can escape from resonance or restore it.

Such a curve is called the Mössbacher or γ-resonance spectrum. The resonance line width measured in the experiment is the result of the superposition of the source and absorber lines. With a thin absorber and source and in the absence of vibration, the width of the lines is equal to twice the natural width 2G. Further work showed that any external influence that changes the initial energy of the nuclei can be compensated by relative motion. Then the magnitude of the change can be determined by the value of the relative speed required to restore resonance.

Thus, Mössbacher’s discovery should contribute to the registration of energy changes (namely changes, and not the absolute value of this energy) of γ-quanta and formed the basis of a whole new field - Mössbacher spectroscopy, in which Soviet scientists made a significant contribution.

Currently, the Mössbacher effect has been discovered in more than 40 elements of the periodic table.

Applications of the Mössbacher effect are numerous and are mainly based on the use of Mössbacher spectra as sources of valuable information about the object under study. Let's look briefly at some applications.

Until quite recently, it was difficult to imagine that one could notice changes in the energy of a nuclear transition depending on the nature of the chemical environment of the nucleus. However, it turned out that when γ quanta are emitted and absorbed by nuclei that are part of chemical compounds, the maximum of absorption lines can be observed at a speed different from zero. This is explained as follows. The energy of a nuclear transition depends on the electrostatic forces of interaction between the nucleus and the electrons surrounding it. But the contribution of this interaction is so small that it cannot be directly observed. However, in experiments studying the Mössbacher effect, the situation changes. In this case, the electrostatic forces on the energy levels of the nucleus should be compared not with the energy of the γ-transition, but with the width of the lines of the resonant position.

The mixing of the maximum of the Mössbacher spectrum is replaced. Such changes therefore provide information about the nature of the chemical properties.

The application of the Mössbacher effect in solid state physics turned out to be important and fruitful. Atomic nuclei are small magnets that interact with the magnetic and electric fields of surrounding electrons. Such interactions significantly affect the Mössbacher spectra and therefore can be studied in detail.
