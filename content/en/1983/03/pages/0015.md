The high quality factor of the γ-resonance turned it into an instrument of exceptional precision. For example, for tin, it is enough, using the Doppler effect, to change the energy of γ quanta by one part-trillion in order to go beyond the natural width of the excited level and destroy the resonance. The method for observing the resonant absorption of γ quanta, proposed by Mössbauer, is based on this “detuning” condition. Let's consider it.

Let the source and absorber nuclei be in identical crystals and at the same temperature. Then the emission line completely overlaps with the linear absorption line, and the number of γ-quanta resonantly absorbed by the absorber will be maximum when the source and absorber are stationary ($v=0$). If now the absorber (or source) is given a small speed, then a shift in the emission and absorption lines will occur (it is called an additional shift), and the resonance will be disrupted. This destruction of resonance occurs even at negligible speeds.

Let us estimate the velocity values ​​for $^{119}$Sn. Substituting into formula (8) instead of the natural $R$ line width $\Gamma=3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ eV, we find that to disrupt the resonance it is enough to mix the absorber or source at a speed of the order of a millimeter per second! (Instead of 60 m/s to obtain resonance on free nuclei.) Thus, by changing the energy of γ quanta using the Doppler effect, one can escape from resonance or restore it.
