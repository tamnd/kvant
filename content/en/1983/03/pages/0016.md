The measurement results are presented on figure 6.

Such a curve is called the Mössbacher or γ-resonance spectrum. The resonance line width measured in the experiment is the result of the superposition of the source and absorber lines. With a thin absorber and source and in the absence of vibration, the width of the lines is equal to twice the natural width 2G. By measuring this width, one can determine the lifetime of the excitation state.

Further work showed that any external influence that changes the initial energy of the nuclei can be compensated by relative motion. Then the magnitude of the change can be determined by the value of the relative speed required to restore resonance.

Thus, Mössbacher’s discovery should contribute to the registration of energy changes (namely changes, and not the absolute value of this energy) of γ-quanta and formed the basis of a whole new field - Mössbacher spectroscopy, in which Soviet scientists made a significant contribution.

Currently, the Mössbacher effect has been discovered in more than 40 elements of the periodic table.

Applications of the Mössbacher effect are numerous and are mainly based on the use of Mössbacher spectra as sources of valuable information about the object under study. Let's look briefly at some applications.

Until quite recently, it was difficult to imagine that one could notice changes in the energy of a nuclear transition depending on the nature of the chemical environment of the nucleus. However, it turned out that when γ quanta are emitted and absorbed by nuclei that are part of chemical compounds, the maximum of absorption lines can be observed at a speed different from zero. This is explained as follows. The energy of a nuclear transition depends on the electrostatic forces of interaction between the nucleus and the electrons surrounding it. But the contribution of this interaction is so small that it cannot be directly observed. However, in experiments studying the Mössbacher effect, the situation changes. In this case, the electrostatic forces on the energy levels of the nucleus should be compared not with the energy of the γ-transition, but with the width of the lines of the resonant position.

The mixing of the maximum of the Mössbacher spectrum is replaced. Such changes therefore provide information about the nature of the chemical properties.

The application of the Mössbacher effect in solid state physics turned out to be important and fruitful. Atomic nuclei are small magnets that interact with the magnetic and electric fields of surrounding electrons. Such interactions significantly affect the Mössbacher spectra and therefore can be studied in detail.
