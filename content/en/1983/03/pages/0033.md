Conducting sphere in power machine problems

S. A. YURSKY

The spherical surface is one of the simplest in geometry. Close to its surface in nature there are many bodies: the Sun and the Earth, raindrops and rowan berries, and even electrons in our imagination are drawn as small balls.

Spherical bodies are part of the problem in power machine problems. This is often due to the fact that spherical symmetry allows a significant use of many mathematical calculations without compromising the physical content.

Before proceeding to the analysis of specific tasks, we will discuss some general issues of power machines.

In an isolated system of bodies, no matter what changes are made in it, the algebraic sum of positive and negative charges remains constant.

Electrical interactions can be described quantitatively. In the simplest case of two exact charges $q_1$ and $q_2$, located in a vacuum at a distance of $r$ from each other, the force of interaction between them is determined by Coulomb’s law

$$F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2},$$

where $k$ is a numerical coefficient, depending on the choice of system of units. In SI it is written as $k = 1 / (4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ (here $\varepsilon_0$ is the electrical constant). Imprecise charges, of course, also act on each other, but their interaction strength is no longer determined by the given formula. In this case, charged bodies should be mentally divided into exact charges, find the interaction force for each pair of charges, and then add all the resulting values.

As you know, electric charges interact through the electric field they create. To quantitatively describe this field, merging and energy characteristics are used: field strength and its potential. The voltage $\vec{E}$ of the field at a given point is equal to the force acting on a unit positive charge placed at this point, and the potential $\phi$ of a given point is equal to the work done by the field to move a unit positive charge from this point to infinity, or, which is the same, the work of external forces to move a unit positive charge from infinity to a given point. By “infinity” we mean a region of space that is truly remote from all electric charges and that there is no field in it.

From the above definition of potential it is clear that only the potential difference between the points under consideration is of practical importance, and not the absolute value of the potentials of the points. Therefore, the beginning of the potential report can be chosen arbitrarily. As a rule, the potential of a point at infinity or the potential of any point connected by a conductor to the Earth (that is, a grounded point) is taken as zero. In this regard, let's talk a little about grounding bodies.

The earth is a conducting body of enormous size. It has a significant negative electrical charge. A different positive space charge is contained in the atmosphere, in a high layer of the order of tens of kilometers. At the Earth's surface, the field strength is ≈ 130 V/m.
