There is another type of “self-filling” tube - wicks. If the vessels are connected together by wick tubes, the liquid, albeit slowly, will flow through it. This is where capillary phenomena come into play.

It remains to be said that the tube itself is not always required. Thus, in J. Walker’s book “Physical Fireworks” you can read about “self-flowing” liquids: “Some liquids, for example liquid polystylene, can flow out of the vessel themselves, if only you allow the action of such a “siphon”, pouring out part of the liquid...”. Since there is no certainty that liquid polystylene is stored in your home, it is advisable to try other liquids. You may be able to achieve the desired effect.

Let us also note that there is one concept of a wonderful liquid that can flow out of a vessel, itself rising along its walls. This is liquid helium, or more precisely, helium II. This is what this liquid is called at temperatures below 2.17 K, when it develops absolutely amazing properties. And above all - superfluidity, that is, the complete absence of fluidity *). Here is what L. D. Landdu and A. I. Kitaigorodsky write about this phenomenon in the book “Physics for Everyone”:

“... Helium II is capable of “climbing out” from a glass or test tube on its own, where it is linted.
