All experimental facts indicate that electric fields have a substitutive property: the electric field of a system of sources is the sum of the fields of individual sources. In other words, the field of each fixed charge is independent of the presence of other charges. To find the resulting intensity at a certain point, it is enough to vectorially add the intensity of the fields created at this point by individual charges. To find the total potential, it is enough at a given point to take the algebraic sum of the potentials of the fields created by each charge separately, and the point of zero potential must, of course, be common to all charges. This principle of finding the resulting field is called the superposition principle.

The electric field is often depicted graphically either using tension lines (field lines) or using equivalent potential surfaces. Lines of force are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces, therefore, knowing one picture, you can easily obtain another.

The word “conductor” usually means a conducting, that is, containing free charges, body of any size and shape, most often metal. Inside a conductor placed in an electric field, the direction of the field is always zero, and all points of the conductor have the same potential. The absence of a field in a conductor does not mean that an external field does not penetrate into it. Quite the contrary, thanks to the penetration of the field into the conductor, charges appear on its surface, the field of which inside the conductor compensates for the external field. Thus, free charges in a conductor can only be located on its surface.

To characterize the ability of conductors to accumulate electrical charges, a special physical quantity is introduced - electrical capacity. A capacitor has a large electrical capacity - a system of two conductors (separated by a thin layer of dielectric) charged with equal but different charges. The capacitance of the capacitor $C$ is called the ratio of the charge $q$ of one of the conductors to the potential difference $\Delta \varphi$ between them: $C = q / (\Delta \varphi)$. It depends on the size, shape and relative position of the conductors, as well as on the electrical properties of the dielectric.
