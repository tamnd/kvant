⟦folio 24⟧

Optical illusions

“If you read the inscription “buffalo” on an elephant’s cage, do not believe your eyes,” advised the actual state councilor, director of the Assay Office, Kozma Petrovich Prutkov.

The wise man was not entirely right. Basically, you can and should trust your eyes, since a person receives the most information about the world around him through vision. True, sometimes this information seems completely implausible, and sometimes it turns out to be completely incorrect.

Let's do a very simple experiment. Place your thumb and index finger so that a gap forms between them, and look through it at the light of the lamp. When the gap becomes very narrow, a black stripe will appear in the center. What is this - an optical illusion?

Let's condition the experience a little. To do this, open up a piece of aluminum foil (for example, a chocolate candy wrapper) and make a 4-5 cm long cut in it with a razor. If you now look through this cut with one eye at the light of a frosted light bulb, you can also see a black stripe in the center. In addition to it, weaker lateral dark stripes will also be visible.

The experiments are really very simple, but explaining them is far from easy. It is pleasant to think that the passage of dark communication bands with the diffraction of light - the bending of waves - is transformed. (It is possible, however, that a physiological effect also plays a significant role here.)

But when do the eyes really deceive us? Why is this happening? What goals does nature pursue?*)

Let us say right away that deceptions should be attributed not to the optical system of the eye, but to the fact that the nervous system, the human brain, “corrects” and “invents” a lot of things. Thus, from the flat figure a volumetric cone (Fig. 4) appears or contrasting stripes appear on the border of black and white, which seem to be trying to make sharper transitions of illumination.

To observe these bands (called Mach bands), we will do the following experiment. A disk made of white cardboard with a drawing painted on it in black ink (Fig. 5) will be brought into rapid rotation. It is natural to expect to see a white circle in the center, then gray and finally black with relatively smooth transitions of illumination. However, in reality the picture appears to be somewhat different: the central white circle is surrounded by a narrow bright light ring, and on the border of the black ring a black stripe of maximum density is visible. These are the Mach bands.

In some cases, their appearance is very useful. For example, they give us the opportunity to read and clearly perceive individual letters. The whiteness of the paper next to each letter is brighter, and the borders of the letters are blacker than they really are.

Since we are talking about rotating disks and the optical illusions associated with them, we cannot help but recall another disk - the so-called Benham disk. It can be used to create red, blue and green colors by mixing only white and black colors. This effect is often written about in various popular publications (see, for example, the magazine “Science and Life”: 1970, No. 11 or 1971, No. 10), since it is very easy to observe.

*) For those who are always interested in this and similar questions related to the physical average, read the book by S. I. Vavilov “The Eye and the Sun”. The latest edition of this wonderful book was published just recently.
