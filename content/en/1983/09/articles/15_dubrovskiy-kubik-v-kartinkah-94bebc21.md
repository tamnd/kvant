Each assembly stage is specified by drawings that show the initial position of the cubes that are rearranged at this stage, the sequence of turns of this stage and the appearance of the cube after its completion.

Let's agree that when assembling, the edge with the blue central square will always be correct. The central square of the opposite face is different in different copies of the cube; for the purpose of definition, we will consider it green. So, as a result of assembly, the top edge of the cube should become blue, the bottom should become green. The front face during assembly can be any of the other four (side) faces.

If you want to round the cube a little, you realized that the central cubes of all faces can immediately be considered standing in their place (since their relative positions are rigidly established by the design of the cube), and for each of the remaining cubes the final position is completely determined: each face of the cube must apply (by side or vertex) to the central square of the same color.

We will solve the cube last - first the bottom layer (first step), then the middle layer (third step) and, finally, the top layer (last four steps).

The operations of the first two steps are very short. We advise you to do them slowly, turn by turn, and follow the movements of those cubes that are installed in the correct position with their help - you will understand how these operations “work” and learn to perform them without thinking. Only one operation can be remembered for each of the following steps; the second (where it exists) can always be replaced with the first one done twice. Let us also note that the last 5 steps of the operation $M_1$ (3rd step) coincide with the first 5 moves $F$ (4th step) and that the operation $Z$ (7th step) is obtained from $T$ (6th step) by replacing the first move $\Pi^2 = \Pi' \Pi'$ with $\Phi \Pi'$ and the last two - $\Phi^\circ \Pi$ - to $\Phi' \Phi' = \Phi^2$. So, in essence, you only need to subtract three basic operations $M_1$, $C$, $T$ and some of their variants.

However, it is much more interesting and useful to penetrate into the “principles of action” of these operations.

Let us first consider the operation $M_1 = \Phi' B \Phi \cdot B \cdot \Pi B' \Pi'$. Its first three turns $\Phi' B \Phi$ are similar to the three-moves of the 2nd step and transfer the cube $\phi n$ (for the method of designating cubes, see Fig. 0) from the lower layer to the upper one, and so that it is correctly presented to the corresponding rib cube (check!); the last three turns $11B'11'$ are similar to them, returning the cube $\phi n$ (along with the presence of the costal one) in place; intermediate turn $B$ prepares the starting position for the last three turns. Analyze on your own the operation $M_2$ and the operation $M_3 = \Phi' B \Phi B^2 \Pi B^2 \Pi'$, which can be used together with $M_1$. After this, try on the same basis to come up with operations similar to $M_1$ and $M_2$, for the case when the cube $\phi n$ is in its place, but is oriented incorrectly. You can go further and develop operations that allow you to combine the 2nd and 3rd steps, that is, simultaneously with each lower corner cube, the adjacent rib cube of the middle layer is installed.

The operations of the 4th and 5th steps are built on the basis of so-called commutators, that is, a sequence of the form $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ ($x^{-1}$ is the rotation inverse to $x$: $\Phi^{-1} = \Phi'$, $(\Phi')^{-1} = \Phi$, $(\Phi^2)^{-1} = \Phi^2$). If $x$ and $y$ are rotations of two adjacent cube faces $X$ and $Y$ (to $\pm 90^\circ$ or $180^\circ$), then the cyclic commutator $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ represents three edge cubes: one from face $X$, the other from $Y$, the third - on the common edge $X$ and $Y$ (check!); At the same time, some corner cubes also appear, but here this is of almost no interest to us.
