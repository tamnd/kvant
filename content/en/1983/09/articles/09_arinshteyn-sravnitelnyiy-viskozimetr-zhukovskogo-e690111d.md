Comparative Zhukovsky viscometer

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences A. E. ARINSHTEIN

Everyone is well aware that, according to Newton's laws, no forces are required to maintain the movement of a body at a constant speed. However, in practice we are convinced of just the opposite: if forces are not applied to the moving body to support this movement, then the body will sooner or later leave.

The gnawing contradiction is very easy to explain. And the point, of course, is not a violation of Newton's law

on. Simply, as a result of practical situations, any moving body is subject to resistance forces directed against the movement. The nature of these forces may be different, but the result of their action is the same: additional force is needed to maintain movement, otherwise it will only stop.

One of the properties of body materials that leads to the emergence of a resistance force to movement is that, according to Bernoulli’s law, the fluid pressure must be the same along the entire length of the pipe. In reality, the pressure drops along the direction of fluid movement, and in order to maintain a stable flow at the ends of the pipe, a certain pressure difference must be maintained. This is due to the fact that the speed of movement of the liquid at different distances from the walls of the pipe is not the same, and forces called viscous forces arise between the individual layers of the moving liquid. They tend to slow down the one of the two contacting layers that moves faster, and accelerate the one that moves slower.

This effect manifests itself differently in different liquids. In thick liquids like honey, it is more pronounced than in liquids like water. The first liquids are called, accordingly, more viscous, and the second - less viscous. (For bodies in an amorphous state, a viscous flow, although very weak, also occurs. It is known, for example, that after several tens, and sometimes hundreds, of years, a vertical plate placed takes the form of the force of an elongated drop. The same happens with stalactites - deposits of a bizarre formula hanging from the ceiling and walls of caves.)

There is also a quantitative assessment of viscous properties - viscosity, a value that can be changed (and this is very important for physicists). What is this concept?

Let the entire system initially come to an end, and at some moment the upper plate begins to move parallel to the lower one at a constant speed $v_0$. Thanks to the fluidity, the liquid will also begin to move, and the layer adjacent to the top plate will move at the same speed as the plate, the next layer at a lower speed, the next at an even lower speed, and so on until the very bottom layer, which seems to stick to the bottom plate and remains at rest. It turns out that in order to keep the top plate moving at a constant speed, a constant force must be applied to it. As was experimentally established by Newton, the model $F$ of this force is greater, the larger the area $S$ of the plate and the speed $v_0$ of movement and the smaller the distance $d$ between the plates (in other words, the force per unit area of ​​the surface of the plate depends on how quickly the speed of the fluid flow changes when moving from layer to layer):

$$F \sim S \frac{v_0}{d},$$

The proportionality coefficient $\eta$ is the viscosity of the liquid. In SI units it is measured in kg/($\text{m} \cdot \text{c}$).

For different liquids its values ​​are different, and for any given liquid the viscosity depends quite strongly on temperature. So, in life we ​​often notice how, as the temperature drops, many liquids thicken, that is, become more viscous. Therefore, when setting the viscosity of a substance, they always specify the temperature, and if necessary, set different viscosity values ​​depending on the temperature or even a graph of viscosity versus temperature (for example, Figures 2 and 3 show such graphs for water and olive oil).

Viscosity can be measured, for this there are special instruments called viscometers. There are several different types of viscometers, and we want to talk about one of them.

Direct change in viscosity - the processor is quite thin. But if you have a liquid whose viscosity is known, then you can literally use scrap materials to make a device with which you can easily compare the viscosity of two liquids, and therefore determine the viscosity of the interspersed liquid. We are talking about the Zhukovsky comparative viscometer, named after the remarkable Russian scientist N. E. Zhukovsky, who invented and described this device.

The comparative viscometer (Fig. 4) consists of a large vessel $K$ filled with water, the temperature of which is kept constant and controlled by a thermometer $T$. There are two pear-shaped vessels in the water (for example, milk bottles)

Curved tubes 1 and 1', hanging in the necks of the vessels, are connected via a tee to a rubber ball W (suitable, for example, for a football camera or a medical syringe). Straight tubes 2 and 2' one end reach the bottom of the corresponding vessel, and the other enters beakers M and M' (programmed in cubic centimeters). The protruding sections of these tubes can be smoothly bent so that their ends are almost level (slightly higher) with the liquid level in vessels B and B'. This is done in order to reduce the measurement error due to different densities of the liquid (there is no need to overcome the different pressure forces of the liquid carriers themselves).

Let's fill vessel B with a liquid of known viscosity, and vessel B' with the liquid under study, lower them into vessel K and pour water of a certain temperature into it. After some time, when the entire contents of vessel K reach the same temperature, we begin to remove the ball Ш and drive air from it into vessels B and B'. It enters the upper parts of the vessels and presses on the liquid with equal pressure forces. Under air pressure, liquids are partially forced out of the vessels and enter beakers through straight tubes.

As experience shows, the volume V of liquid flowing through a tube of constant cross-section over a period of time t is greater, the lower the thickness of the liquid, that is

$$\frac{V}{\tau} = \frac{A}{\eta},$$

where coefficient A depends on the experimental conditions, which include pressure transfer and tube parameters (more details about this will be discussed in the Appendix to the status). If tubes 2 and 2' are the same, then the ratio of the volumes of displaced liquid will be equal to the inverse ratio of their volumes:

$$\frac{V}{V'} = \frac{\eta'}{\eta}$$

(after all, all the conditions for the flow of both liquids are the same). From here, knowing the thickness η of a known liquid and measuring the volumes V and V' on the test, it is easy to find the thickness of the liquid under study:

$$\eta' = \eta \frac{V}{V'}$$.

But what if it is impossible to guarantee the identity of tubes 2 and 2' (doing this at home is truly, practically impossible)? This difficulty can be overcome by slightly changing the experimental method.
