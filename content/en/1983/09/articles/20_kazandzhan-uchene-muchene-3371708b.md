Firstly, not all examinees firmly understood the fact that force arises only when bodies interact. A flying body interacts only with the Earth, and it is affected only by the force of gravity towards the Earth. Of course, when the body was thrown, some force was applied to it (it could be called the throwing force). However, we will consider the body not during the throwing, but after it has been thrown, that is, during the flight. It is important to remember that force cannot be “accumulated”: there is interaction between bodies - there is a corresponding force, when the interaction ends - the force immediately disappears.

Secondly, there is a fairly widespread misconception that the nature and trajectory of a body’s movement are determined only by the forces acting on it. In reality, force determines the acceleration of a body, that is, a change in speed. The speed itself at each moment of time is determined by two factors - the change in speed and the initial speed. In other words, the nature of the body’s motion depends not only on the forces acting on the body, but also on the initial conditions. In all problems of mechanics, the initial conditions are the initial coordinate and initial velocity of the body. In this case, the initial conditions should be understood as the angle at which the body was thrown and the model of the speed with which it was thrown.

Question 4. A body (a stone on a rope) moves in a circle in a vertical plane. What forces act on the body? How are they directed at the moment when the body finds itself at point A of the trajectory (Fig. 1, a)?

Typical wrong answer. Three forces act on a body: force

$$\vec{F}_T, \vec{N}, \vec{F}_T'$$

the elastic force of the rope and the centripetal force $\vec{F}_T$, which holds the body on the circle (Fig. 1, b).

Error analysis. The body in question interacts with the Earth (the result is the gravity force $\vec{F}_T$) and with the rope (the result is the elastic force of the rope $\vec{N}$). Thus, only two forces act on the body. The force that holds the body on the circle and gives it centripetal acceleration $a_u$ is the sum of the projections of these forces onto the direction of the radius (Fig. 1, c).

$$m a_u = N - F_T \cos \alpha.$$

These same forces, more precisely, the sum of the projections of these forces onto the direction of the tangent to the circle, impart tangential acceleration $a_k$ to the body:

$$m a_k = F_T \sin \alpha.$$

Question 6. Does the melting of ice floes floating along the river during ice drift increase the water level in the river?

Typical wrong answer. Yes, it does.

Error analysis. When an ice floe floats in water, its gravitational force is balanced by the Archimedean force, equal to the gravitational force of water in the volume displaced by the ice floe. After the ice floe melts, it turns into water, the volume of which is exactly equal to the volume of the underwater part of the ice floe. It follows that when the ice floes melt, the water level in the river does not change.

Question 7. The gas in the sauce with a volume of $V_0$ at a pressure of $p_0$ is opened with a piston pump having a working chamber with a volume of $V$ (Fig. 2). How many piston strokes must be made for the gas pressure in the sauce to rise to $p$?

Typical wrong answer. Let the required number of moves be $n$. Considering that the volume of gas increased by $nV$, and the temperature of the gas remained unchanged, in accordance with the Boyle-Mariotte law we obtain
$$ p_0V_0 = p(V_0 + nV). $$
This is where we find $n$.

Error analysis. The Boyle-Mariotte law is valid only when not only the temperature, but also the mass of the gas remains unchanged. In this case, after each stroke of the piston, part of the gas leaves the system; when the piston moves from right to left, it closes cap A and opens cap B, through which the gas leaves the system (see Fig. 2). Therefore, it is necessary to consider each of the piston strokes separately. Let's start with the first move. For the mass of gas that was initially in the sauce, we write the Boyle-Mariotte law:
$$ p_0V_0 = p_1(V_0 + V), $$
where $p_1$ is the gas pressure when the piston occupies the extreme right position. When the piston returns to its original left position, a mass of gas remains in the vessel, changing compared to the initial one, but its pressure is $p_1$. For this mass of gas
$$ p_1V_0 = p_2(V_0 + V), $$
where $p_2$ is the gas pressure at the end of its piston stroke. Considering successively $n$ strokes of the piston, we obtain a system of $n$ equations:
$$ \begin{align*}
p_0V_0 &= p_1(V_0 + V), \\
p_1V_0 &= p_2(V_0 + V), \\
p_2V_0 &= p_3(V_0 + V), \\
p_{n-1}V_0 &= p(V_0 + V);
\end{align*} $$
Solving this system, we find
$$ p = p_0(V_0 / (V_0 + V))^n, $$
where
$$ n = \log_{V_0 / (V_0 + V)} (p / p_0). $$

Question 8. What will the voltmeter show in the circuit at figure 3 (see p. 56)? The resistance of the voltmeter is considered infinitely large, and the resistance of the connecting wires can be exceeded.

Typical wrong answer. The voltmeter will show the EMF of the element: $U = \mathcal{E}$.

Error analysis. In circuit ABCD (see Fig. 3) current flow
$$ I = 2\mathcal{E} / (2r) = \mathcal{E} / r, $$
where $r$ is the internal resistance of each element. A voltmeter connected to the element terminals shows the voltage
$$ U = \mathcal{E} - Ir = \mathcal{E} - (\mathcal{E} / r)r = \mathcal{E} - \mathcal{E} = 0. $$

Question 9. You flip the switch, and... the light bulb suddenly burns out. What explains this?

Typical wrong answer. Due to the phenomenon of self-induction, when disconnected from the network, an additional current (so opening) will arise in the same direction as the main current. As a result, the total current through the light bulb suddenly returns, and the light bulb may burn out.

Question 10. How many images of the point S will be obtained in a system of two mutually perpendicular plane mirrors (Fig. 4, a)?

Typical wrong answer. Two images: $S_1$ and $S_2$ (Fig. 4, b).

Error analysis. In addition to the images $S_1$ and $S_2$, formed by rays reflected from only one mirror, there is another image - $S_3$ (Fig. 4, c). It is formed by rays reflected first from one mirror and then from another.

Question 11. Why is energy released when a uranium nucleus fissions?

Typical wrong answer. Each particle in the nucleus has a binding energy of about 7.5 MeV; the total binding energy is about 1800 MeV. Thus, enormous energy is concentrated in the core. It is released when the core is destroyed.

Error analysis. By definition, binding energy is the energy that must be expended to separate a nucleus into its constituent particles. Or, in other words, this is the energy that is released during the formation of a nucleus from individual particles.

What energy is released during the fission of a uranium nucleus? The fact is that the binding energy per particle in the uranium nucleus is 7.5 MeV, and for each particle of shards formed during the fission of uranium, there is about 8.5 MeV. Thus, the decomposition of a uranium nucleus requires 7.5 MeV of energy per particle, and the formation of shards releases 8.5 MeV of energy per particle. The difference between these energies, which is approximately 200 MeV per nucleus, is released during the fission of the uranium nucleus.
