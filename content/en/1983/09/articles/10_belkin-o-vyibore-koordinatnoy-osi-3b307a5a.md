About choosing a coordinate axis

When considering the rectilinear motion of a point, the X coordinate axis is usually directed along the direction of motion, that is, along the direction of the displacement vector (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

Then the projection $S_x$ of the vector $\vec{S}$ onto the axis of space is equal to the modulo $S$ of this vector:

$$S_x = x_2 - x_1 = S.$$

In the case shown at figure 1,

$$S = 8\text{ м} - 2\text{ м} = 6\text{ м}.$$

But you don’t need to think that such a choice of axis is mandatory. The $X$ axis can be directed in any way, at any angle to the direction of the $\vec{S}$ vector. For example, as shown in figure 2, where the axis $X$ forms an angle $\alpha = 60^\circ$ with the vector $\vec{S}$. As a result, the result (modulo of the displacement vector) cannot change. From figure 2 it is clear that

$$S_x = x_2 - x_1,$$

A

$$\frac{x_2 - x_1}{S} = \cos \alpha,$$

or $x_2 - x_1 = S \cos \alpha,$

where

$$S = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{\cos \alpha}.$$

In our case

$$S = \frac{4\text{ м} - 1\text{ м}}{0,5} = 6\text{ м}.$$

Thus, the result does not depend on which way the coordinate axis is directed. The choice of axis direction is dictated only by convenience.

A simple way to determine molecular sizes

In molecular physics, the main “actors” are molecules, the inconceivably small particles that make up all matter in the world. It is clear that to study many phenomena it is important to know what molecules they are. In particular, what are their sizes.

When talking about molecules, they are generally considered to be small, elastic, hard balls. Therefore, knowing the size of molecules means knowing their radius.
