Metals have memory!

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. A. ZAIMOVSKY

The phenomena of living and inanimate nature of the present are diverse and so closely intertwined with each other that, when faced with something new in the inorganic world, we involuntarily look for an analogue with the manifestations of human life and activity. This desire is reflected in scientific terminology. Terms such as “lifetime” or “settled life time” (in relation to parts), “survivability”, “fatigue” (in relation to structural materials) are considered strict and have become generally accepted along with hundreds of others similar to them in this sense. It is no coincidence that the relatively recently discovered property of metal alloys is called “shape memory.” Can metals remember, remember, score? Let's try to attribute these abilities of living beings to metals, and then we will see that metals are even capable of being themselves!

Elastic and plastic deformation

First, let us remember what we know from the school physics course about the deformation of metals, and agreements on some notations.

If a load is applied to a metal sample—an external force that imparts acceleration to one part of the body relative to another—then the sample will change its size and shape—it will deform. Deformation is usually characterized by the ratio of the change Δl in the sample size to the initial size l: ε = Δl/l. The specific measure of load is stress - the ratio of the magnitude of the force acting on the sample to the cross-sectional area of ​​the sample.

The relationship between σ and ε is determined only by the properties of materials and for common metals and alloys is displayed by the curve shown in figure 1.

When they say: “bend it like a ram’s horn,” they mean precisely such large deformations that the elastic component can already be neglected due to its smallness compared to the residual one. Between the lines of this decisive expression you can read: “you won’t be able to speed up anymore.” Indeed, in order to eliminate the consequences of plastic deformation and return the body to its original shape, it is necessary to forcefully deform it plastically in the opposite direction. If you heat plastic deformed metal, then its shape remains the same - there is no reason for a decrease or increase in the magnitude of the residual deformation.

To better understand the essence of effective shape memory in metals, it is necessary to understand the mechanism of their plastic deformation. More precisely, we need to consider only the geometric aspect of this mechanism. Being crystalline bodies, plastic metals are deformed by shear

Are there shear processes in metals that can occur without the occurrence of an external force? It turns out that they exist and have been using humans for 3000 years. Even in ancient Greece and Rome, the phenomenon of hardening steel was used to give it special hardness and strength. However, only in the 20th century it became clear that this change in the properties of steel was associated with the transformation of one crystalline modification of iron into another.

G.V. Kurdimov predicted, and already the next year, together with his collaborator L.G. Khandros (now Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences), experimentally observed a new type of martest transformation in aluminum bronze. In 1980 it was declared open. The authors of the discovery received a corresponding diploma, and the phenomenon itself was called the “Kurdimov effect.” Its essence is as follows. Under a favorable combination of certain conditions (in particular, the difference in phase volumes must be small), the transformation of austenite (A) into marstestite (M) acquires special features. It becomes, as G.V. Kurdimov put it, “thermoelastic.” This term reflects many unannounced features of such a martest transformation. In particular, he supports the fact that if you stop cooling, the transformation immediately stops at the stage at which it was stopped by the stopping temperature, and if you start heating the sample, then the transformation soon begins to go in the opposite direction. This means that martestite again turns into austenite, all shear mixing of atoms goes in the opposite direction, and the atoms themselves return exactly to their original positions corresponding to the austenite lattice.

In alloys that have undergone a thermoelastic transformation, the application of external stress can cause an increase in the temperature of the martest transformation, but the main thing is that in these alloys the presence of an external load especially sharply changes the geometric picture of the transformation. If the voltage is applied as shown in figure 4, b, then the proportion of crystals 1, in which the direction of the shear atoms is consistent with the direction of the load, increases, and the proportion of crystals 2 decreases. In the limiting case, during the A → M transition, only one version of atomic mixing is realized (figure 4, b), and we obtain a significant overall deformation of the sample, which corresponds to a relative strain of 10% or more.

In its magnitude, this deformation is much higher than the εmax of elastic metals, but it accumulates not due to irreversible shifts, but due to the directed transformation A → M. An external force orders the mixing of atoms during this transformation. If we now induce the reverse transformation M → A, then all this enormous deformation will disappear, since the atoms return to their original position, which they occupied in the austenite lattice.

It turns out that a plate made of such an alloy and bent into a ring remembers its original flat shape, and can store it in its memory for quite a long time. The process of “remembering” is realized when heated, and now we can “recall” the metal at the time and in the area when we need it.

Currently, hundreds of alloys are known that exhibit the Kurdimov effect. By adjusting their composition, we can shift the interval of A → M → A transformations along the temperature scale. For example, let’s make sure that the martest point Mₙ is slightly below room temperature, and Aₙ—the temperature at which the reverse M → A transformation begins when heated—is slightly higher. Then small stresses applied at room temperature will cause the transformation A → M and a significant change

How is metal memory used?

The possibilities for practical applications of alloys with a unique shape-filling property are extremely diverse and tempting. The building before the designers is a wide field of activity, dotted with fundamentally new engineering solutions. For example, in space technology, these alloys effectively solve the traditional problem of saving space in a building. Antennas, energy emitters, etc., folded or twisted into a compact shape and placed in the small bottoms of the space corpus, are straightened after the vehicle is launched into orbit from the action of solar heat. Memory alloys can be used to create a space radiotelexan - a compact motor of thin wiring unfolds when heated into a circle with a diameter of about 2 km!

Of course, memory alloys have many uses on Earth as well. Their ability to lift loads when heated opens up the possibility of creating engines for the spicy transformation of the body into mechanical work. Models of such engines have already been built. Their efficiency is low, but for their operation it is possible to use low-temperature heat sources - solar energy, thermal waste from industrial enterprises, etc.

This same property of memory alloys has found application in creating joints in a way that replaces welding, soldering and other traditional methods. Let's say we need to connect two tubes to create, say, an airplane engine fuel line. We take a bushing made of a low-temperature memory alloy, the inside diameter of which is 4% less than the outer diameter of the three-piece connection (figure 5). In liquid nitrogen (-196 °C) we deform the bushing so that its inner diameter becomes 4% larger than the outer diameter of the three-hole bushing. Now we can insert bushings into the ends of the three-pieces, which, upon warming up to room temperature, recalls its previous shape, bends and compresses the ends of the three-pieces, providing a firmly sealed connection. Here, perfect from an engineering point of view, especially the manifestations of metal memory are used. After all, all normal, that is, “forgetful” metals, when heated, expand in all directions. And we made the memory alloy shrink when heated! Of course, it is compressed only along two axes, and along the third - along the triangular axis - it is stretched, since the volume of materials during the $M \rightarrow A$-transformation changes little.
