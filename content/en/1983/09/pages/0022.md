A similar picture occurs when liquid is poured out. The unperturbed lower bound corresponds to the zero value $R$ (point 0 on figure 4). When waves with $R < R_{\text{кр}}$ arise, the surface of the liquid will tend to return to an undisturbed state. But as soon as $R$ exceeds $R_{\text{кр}}$, the amplitude of the waves will tend to increase without limit. The decrease in energy due to the movement of the center of the heavy stack turns out to be greater than the increase in energy associated with an increase in surface area. Therefore, it is energetically beneficial for the wave to increase its amplitude. This will lead to liquid pouring out of the vessel.

Let us now calculate the critical value $R_{\text{кр}}$. From figure 4 it is clear that the maximum potential energy is achieved only at one point. Those who already have explored the function for maximum and minimum using arbitrary ones, it is easy to find that

$$R_{\text{кр}} = \sqrt{\frac{(\pi-2)}{2} \frac{\sigma}{\rho g}}.$$

For those who do not have integration, the difficulties would become insurmountable, and we sought to simplify all calculations as much as possible. However, substituting the numerical values ​​for water - $\rho = 10^3$ kg/m³ and $\sigma = 73 \cdot 10^{-3}$ J/m² - gives a critical radius value of $R_{\text{кр}} = 2 \cdot 10^{-3}$ m=2 mm. Considering that the wavelength is $\lambda = 4R$, we find that water will flow out of an inverted vessel if the size of the response exceeds 8 mm, which is in reasonable agreement with experiment.
