⟦folio 51⟧

On the first page of the cover you see a photograph of a plaster model of a third-order surface (cubic surface) - a surface defined by a third-degree polynomial in three variables. It does not show 27 primroses lying entirely on it.

The theorem stating that, in the general case, on any cubic surface there are 27 new prims (if we also count the “minimum” and “infinitely distant” prims), will appear on one of the “Geometric pages”, which from the next issue will be regularly printed in «Kvant». On each such page, in addition to the drawing with explanations, there will be tasks - graphic and mathematical.

Geometric pages can decorate a school's mathematical classroom or be enriched with students' independent graphic and mathematical work, appearing in the school wall newspaper.

At one time, the 27-prime theorem was a major mathematical sensation. Here is what the English mathematician A. Henderson wrote about this in his book: “In 1849, Cayley and Salmon discovered 27 primomas. In 1869, Christan Wiener made a model of a cubic surface showing 27 active prims on it. The outstanding geometer Sylvester considered that Wiener’s work belongs to the number of discoveries that “inscribed the year 1869 in the Annals of Hayka for all eternity.” At the 1894 World's Fair in Chicago, Klein demonstrated a complete set of cubic surfaces, including the X. Wiener surface."

Since then, there has been a buzz around the sensation, and there has been a modo on plaster surfaces - the specimen we photographed is now considered unique (it is kept in the Department of Differential Geometric Mechanics - Faculty of Mathematics of Moscow State University): As the Soviet mathematician Yu. I. Manin wrote today, “Henderson’s book was published in 1911, when Sylvester’s redness and plaster models were leaving along with the Victorian era.”

But the beauty discovered in this era by 27 primos is eternal.
