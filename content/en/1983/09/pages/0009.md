⟦folio 7⟧

G.V. Kurdimov predicted, and already the next year, together with his collaborator L.G. Khandros (now Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences), experimentally observed a new type of martest transformation in aluminum bronze. In 1980 it was declared open. The authors of the discovery received a corresponding diploma, and the phenomenon itself was called the “Kurdimov effect.” Its essence is as follows. Under a favorable combination of certain conditions (in particular, the difference in phase volumes must be small), the transformation of austenite (A) into marstestite (M) acquires special features. It becomes, as G.V. Kurdimov put it, “thermoelastic.” This term reflects many unannounced features of such a martest transformation. In particular, he supports the fact that if you stop cooling, the transformation immediately stops at the stage at which it was stopped by the stopping temperature, and if you start heating the sample, then the transformation soon begins to go in the opposite direction. This means that martestite again turns into austenite, all shear mixing of atoms goes in the opposite direction, and the atoms themselves return exactly to their original positions corresponding to the austenite lattice.

Fig. 4. Martestin transformation during cooling (a) and under the influence of increasing external force (b).

In alloys that have undergone a thermoelastic transformation, the application of external stress can cause an increase in the temperature of the martest transformation, but the main thing is that in these alloys the presence of an external load especially sharply changes the geometric picture of the transformation. If the voltage is applied as shown in figure 4, b, then the proportion of crystals 1, in which the direction of the shear atoms is consistent with the direction of the load, increases, and the proportion of crystals 2 decreases. In the limiting case, during the A → M transition, only one version of atomic mixing is realized (figure 4, b), and we obtain a significant overall deformation of the sample, which corresponds to a relative strain of 10% or more.

In its magnitude, this deformation is much higher than the εmax of elastic metals, but it accumulates not due to irreversible shifts, but due to the directed transformation A → M. An external force orders the mixing of atoms during this transformation. If we now induce the reverse transformation M → A, then all this enormous deformation will disappear, since the atoms return to their original position, which they occupied in the austenite lattice.

It turns out that a plate made of such an alloy and bent into a ring remembers its original flat shape, and can store it in its memory for quite a long time. The process of “remembering” is realized when heated, and now we can “recall” the metal at the time and in the area when we need it.

Currently, hundreds of alloys are known that exhibit the Kurdimov effect. By adjusting their composition, we can shift the interval of A → M → A transformations along the temperature scale. For example, let’s make sure that the martest point Mₙ is slightly below room temperature, and Aₙ—the temperature at which the reverse M → A transformation begins when heated—is slightly higher.
