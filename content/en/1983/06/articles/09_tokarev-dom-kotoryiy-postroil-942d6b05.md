The house that...
A. V. TOKAREV

The outstanding Italian architect of the 16th century, Andrea Palladu, believed that in every building three things must be observed, without which no value can deserve approval. This is creep (and convenience), beauty and durability. Let's leave aside messages of convenience and beauty and talk about durability, or, in other words, about the reliability of the building.

Probably each of you knows that the construction of any structure begins with laying the foundation. A good, solid foundation is the key to further success. But what does this mean - a solid foundation?

Many questions come to be solved by specialists. One of them is the fight against foundation settlement. Particular difficulties associated with this problem arise when structures arise on permafrost. Houses built there often develop cracks due to uneven settlement of the foundation caused by the subsidence of the soil underneath.

Is it possible to avoid thawing of the soil under houses built on permafrost, or at least reduce thawing?

Let's try to solve this problem based only on knowledge of the simplest physical laws and patterns.

First of all, let's analyze the conditions of the problem. Why might the soil under the house begin to thaw? Obviously, because the foundation transfers some heat to it. So it's all about the foundation. Then, the first thing to do is to reduce the area of ​​contact between the foundation and the ground. It was for this purpose that tasks began to be built on connections, abandoning the usual solid foundation. But this turned out to be insufficient.

How else can you reduce the prioctella to the ground? In this case, the prioctella is transmitted only by thermal conductivity; Consequently, the material binds to conduct heat as poorly as possible. What should the ties be made from?

To quantify the thermal conductivity of various substances, a special physical quantity is used - the thermal conductivity coefficient. It is different for each substance. Metals have the highest thermal conductivity, liquids have a non-VELICA thermal conductivity, and gases have even less. For our reasoning it is not important how exactly

The thermal conductivity coefficient is determined and in what units it is measured. We are only interested in comparing the abilities of different materials to conduct heat. Therefore, we will use the reference book *) and compile a small table of relative thermal conductivity coefficients, choosing the thermal conductivity of water as a unit:

| Substance | Relative thermal conductivity | Substance | Relative thermal conductivity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Petrol | 0,2 | Iron | 122 |
| cotton wool | 0,07 | Kerosene | 0,2 |
| Water | 1 | Brick | 1,1 |
| Air | 0,04 | Ice | 3,7 |
| Felt | 0,1 | Cork | 0,07 |
| Tree | 0,2—0,6 | Alcohol | 0,3 |

Let's take a close look at this table. Among solid materials, cotton wool and cork have the best thermal conductivity, followed by wood and brick. However, first of all, the connection must be strong, so all the listed materials are not suitable. What if the connection is made of metal, for example iron, and cotton wool, felt or some other porous substance containing a lot of air is placed inside it? This is the way out!

So, the bond must be made of durable hard materials and filled with some kind of porous substance.

Analyzing the obtained result, we make a choice: due to low thermal conductivity, connecting the specified structure will actually reduce the flow of heat from the surrounding air to the group (that is, from top to bottom) in the warm season. And in the cold? It would be nice to make sure that in winter, during severe frosts, the binding helps to cool the group (that is, it transfers heat upward). This would increase the strength of the group and reduce the extent of its thawing in the summer.

Let us formulate more clearly what physical processes occur in fresh soil and in the soil during summer and winter. In the warm season, the upper part is fresh, in contact with air, and heats up. Gradually, the fresh part that is in the ground will also heat up. The less heating the lower end of the fresh, and therefore the adjacent groups, the better. In winter, the upper part is freshly cooled by contact with cold air. The lower part is gradually cooled and the groups around it are fresh. The lower the temperature to which the group is cooled, the better.

Thus, connecting the due with the following properties:

a) if the temperature of the upper part is higher than that of the lower part, then the pile should conduct heat poorly;

b) if the upper end is cooler than the lower end, then the pile should conduct heat well.

Or in other words:

heat transfer from top to bottom should be bad, and from bottom to top - good.

As is known, in solids heat conduction occurs only due to thermal conductivity, which does not depend on direction. This means that a bond with the properties listed above cannot be made from a solid. Our previous model - a metal connection filled with porous materials - is not suitable (the porous “inside” will conduct heat more efficiently not only in summer, but also in winter, when it is desirable for the groups to be well cooled).

But what if the durable shell is fresh, able to withstand heavy loads, filled with liquid or gas? In this case, heat transfer will be carried out not only by thermal conductivity, but also by convection. What will this lead to?

In winter, the upper layers of liquid or gas will cool and, being denser, will sink down. The place will be occupied by less dense lower elephants, which will, in turn, cool and sink down, etc. As a result, the fresh one and the groups around it will take on the temperature of the surrounding air, that is, they will be cooled due to natural frosts. In summer, the upper layers of liquid or gas will be

Of course, heating with the surrounding air, but, being less dense, they will not go down. Heat transfer will be carried out only by thermal conductivity, and this is very small for liquids and gases.

This means that tying such a structure in summer will conduct heat poorly, and the soil around it will warm up slightly.

So, we come to the conclusion: a connection made of durable materials must be filled with liquid or gas.

The last question remains - what exactly should I fill the connection with? In winter, this is all the same, since during a long cold period the ground temperature will have time to rise to the ambient temperature. In summer, it is important that the soil warms up as little as possible. This means that it is necessary to select a body that, upon receiving the body, would heat up the least. From the formula

$$Q = cm(t_2 - t_1)$$

we find that the change in temperature $(t_2 - t_1)$ depends not only on the amount of heat $Q$ received, but also on the remote heat capacity $c$ of the body and on its mass $m$.

The mass of a liquid is always greater than the mass of a gas of the same volume. From these reports, preference follows the liquid department. Now let’s compare the specific heats, using the already mentioned reference book:

| Substance | Specific heat, kJ/(kg K) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Petrol | 1,4 |
| Water | 4,2 |
| Air | 1 |
| Glycerol | 2,4 |
| Kerosene | 2,1 |
| Machine oil | 1,9 |

It turns out that the specific heat is greater for liquids than for gases.

Therefore, the connection must be filled with liquid. But which one? Water, although the cheapest and most accessible, is not suitable because it freezes in winter. Glycerin and machine oil thicken at low temperatures, which makes convection difficult. What remains is gasoline and kerosene. Both liquids freeze at temperatures below -50°C, so each could “withstand” severe winter frosts. However, we still choose kerosene, since it has a higher specific heat and is cheaper than gasoline.

Finally, we can formulate the final answer to the problem.

So, in order to reduce the number of houses built on permafrost, buildings must be built on stilts; The ties, made of durable solid materials, must be filled with kerosene inside.

Note that this method of strengthening frozen foundations (reduced supply of soil under the foundation) is not fantasy, but reality. It has been developed in detail theoretically and tested under arbitrary conditions of operating conditions “violating” the law of nature. Listen to this described in the book by A.P. Nechaev “Miracles without miracles”:

“...The samovar was served. Steam was pouring out from under the lid. They started making tea, and it turned out that the water was cold!”

Let us only add that the described incident occurred at the end of the 19th century, when samovars, unlike modern electric ones, were coal-fired.
