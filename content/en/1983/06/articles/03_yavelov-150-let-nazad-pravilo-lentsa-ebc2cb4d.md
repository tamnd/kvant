The CO₂ content in the atmosphere is now 0.0337%. And just 20 years ago it was less than 0.0318%. This increase will occur due to the ever-increasing displacement of organic fuels: oil, coal, gas. So won't the greenhouse effect continue to increase? Isn't the Earth suffering from overheating?

Of course, the additional carbon depletion of the atmosphere contributes to the climate change. The climate, however, depends not only on it. In addition, it should be noted that the amount of CO₂ in the atmosphere increases relatively quickly as fuel is burned - the vast majority of the additional carbon dioxide is dissolved in the ocean and absorbed by plants during photosynthesis. The ocean contains 30-100 times more dissolved carbon dioxide than its atmospheric mass and is capable of absorbing much more. The increase in the proportion of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is due to the fact that the process of its dissolution in the ocean is slow compared to the supply of CO₂ from the combustion of organic fuels. In the next 50-100 years, oil resources will be depleted, and humanity will be forced to switch to nuclear energy. Then the problem of atmospheric carbon dioxide will be solved automatically.

Lenz's rule

In 1831, the English scientist Faraday discovered the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction, a phenomenon that clearly illustrated the possibility of “converting magnetism into electricity.” In Faraday's experiments, an induced current arose either when the conductors moved relative to each other, or when the conductor and magnet moved relative. To determine the direction of the current, Faraday formulated two corresponding rules: 1) an electric current causes a current in the opposite direction in the parallel wiring approaching it, and a current in the same direction in the one removed; 2) a magnet causes a current in a conductor moving near it, depending on the direction in which the conductor, in its movement, crosses the magnetic lines of force.

Having become acquainted with the works of the English scientist, the extraordinary academician of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences Emilius Khristanovich Lenz (1804-1865) regarded these rules as unsatisfactory. Indeed, they had a purely geometric content, described only special cases and did not cover all its phenomena as a whole.

Instead, Lenz proposed one general rule with which one can determine the direction of the induced current, regardless of how the current is induced - by the movement of a conductor or magnet, or by turning on or off the current in an adjacent conductor. This is the rule as Lenz formulated it on November 29, 1833, when he first reported his discovery at a meeting of the St. Petersburg Academy:

“If a metal conductor is moved by a galvanic current or a magnet, then a galvanic current will arise in it in such a direction that if the conductor were stationary, the current could move it in the opposite direction; it is assumed that a conductor at rest can only move in the direction of movement or in the opposite direction.” Lenz reliably confirmed the rule he rightly proposed through a whole series of convincing experiments.

Of course, this formulation is not as concise as the equivalent modern formulation of Lenz's rule.
