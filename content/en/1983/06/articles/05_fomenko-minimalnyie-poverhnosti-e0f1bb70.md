When the White physicist Joseph Plateau began experiments on studying the configuration of soap films in the 19th century, he hardly imagined that they would serve as an impetus for the emergence of an entire scientific field that has been rapidly developing up to the present day and is known today as the “Plateau problem.” The experiments that Plato once carried out are well known to us from childhood - hardly anyone has not had fun at one time or another by blowing soap bubbles or constructing soap films that tighten a wire circuit.

If you want to make beautiful soap films at home (like the ones discussed in this article), here are some practical tips. You need: flexible thin wire, tuplet soap, a glass or bowl of water and glycerin (you can do without it, but films with it

more stable). Make small shavings from four pieces of soap, dissolve in warm water and add a spoonful of glycerin (if you have it). Make a closed loop with a handle from the wire, lower it into the solution and carefully remove it; By intricately bending the contour, you can diversify the shape of the films.

When we remove the wire circuit from the soapy water, there is a beautiful rainbow soap film hanging on it, bounded by this circuit. The dimensions of the film can be quite significant, but, of course, the larger the film, the easier it is to burst under the influence of gravity. If, on the contrary, the dimensions of the contour are small, then in many questions related to the study of soap films, gravity can be neglected. We will use this circumstance in the future.

When soap molecules are added to water, they rush to the surface and fill it with a uniform layer, each of them oriented with its non-polar end outward (Fig. 1.6). The physical principle underlying the formation of soap films and regulating their behavior and properties is extremely simple: a physical system retains a certain configuration only when it cannot easily change it to a position with a lower energy value. Energy

surface (soap film), often described in terms of surface tension of a liquid, is due to the presence of attractive forces between individual molecules and the imbalance of these forces at the surface boundary. The beginning of unbalanced forces entails the following interesting effect: the liquid film turns into an elastic surface, tending to minimize its area and, therefore, minimize the tension energy per unit area. In this case, we neglect gravity and air pressure.

Thus, the mathematical model of a soap film is a smooth surface of minimal area subtending a given contour: mathematicians call it a minimal surface. The classical mathematical theory of these surfaces belongs to the so-called calculus of variations, a field of analysis that arose in the 18th century. Nowadays, more modern means of topology and differential geometry are used to develop this theory. Of course, using “school” mathematics cannot present the theory itself; but its results are easy to visualize and can even be verified experimentally. The simplest contours

Let us first consider the case when the contour is not too strongly curved - namely, when it can be one-to-one*) projected onto a convex contour lying in a certain plane. Then it follows from mathematical theory that there is one and only one minimal surface that spans the given contour. When this contour is flat, the “existence and uniqueness theorem” seems obvious: the contour can be enclosed by a piece of the plane that it bounds, and any other surface subtending it will have a larger area (Fig. 2).

Minimal surfaces in nature

It turns out that minimal films are widespread in nature as the most economical surfaces that form the skeletons of neotourian living organisms. The most effective example is the skeletons of radiolarians, microsoptic marine animals with the most diverse and exotic forms. Apparently, the first person to notice that surface tension plays a significant role in creating the shape of these creatures was the English scientist D'Arcy Wentworth Tompson, author of the book "Growth and Form." Radiolariン consists of small lumps of protoplasm enclosed in foam-like forms, similar to soap bubbles and films. Since these organisms are quite complex, the minimal surfaces included in them, generally speaking, have many branching points and branching ribs, on which the bulk of the liquid that makes up the organism is concentrated. This concentration of liquid along the branching ribs and near the special points is reflected in real soap films: the liquid flows freely from the soap film until

meets an edge on which three sheets of film converge. Here the liquid slows down and settles, forming water segments that visually reveal special edges of the minimal surface. The same process actually occurs in radiolarians. The concentration of liquid along the branching ribs causes solid frakines of seawater and salt to settle along these ribs and gradually form the solid skeleton of the animal. The geometry of this skeleton can be visualized if, in the mass of soap foam, one pays attention to the branching ribs, that is, to the segments along which different bubbles join. These segments are connected to each other (sometimes in a complex way) and form the “liquid backbone” of the foam. After the death of the animal, the soft tissues gradually disappear and a hard skeleton remains, formed in the manner described.

So, it is nothing more than a visual frozen image of a system of branching edges and singular points that arose in a complex minimal surface, which also included closed volumes limited by the semblance of soap bubbles. In addition, cell walls, which also accumulate seawater salts, also play a role in the formation of the solid skeleton of radiolaria.

The role of the minimal surface in chemistry is important, where interaction surfaces at the boundaries of different media determine the nature and rate of many chemical transformations. Examples of minimal surfaces are well-known membranes to all of us - this is the eardrum in ours; these are membranes that serve as boundaries of living cells; these are membranes in living organisms that separate one organ from another, etc.

This interesting topic attracts a variety of specialties in biology, chemistry, and physics. As we have seen, a number of interesting mathematical problems are associated with minimal surfaces. Their correct formulation and study allows us to better understand this amazing (albeit simple in appearance) natural phenomenon.
