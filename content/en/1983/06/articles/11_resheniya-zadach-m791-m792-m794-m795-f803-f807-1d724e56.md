M792. Solve equations in natural numbers
a) $3^x + 1 = 2^y$;
b) $3^x - 1 = 2^y$.
b) * Find all positive integers n for which both numbers $\frac{1}{n}$ and $\frac{1}{n+1}$ are expressed as finite decimals.
e) * Prove that for any prime p>3 and natural m>1, none of the numbers $p^m+1$ and $p^m-1$ can be a power of two.

S. B. Gashkov

a), 6) Let’s give the answers to the first two points of the problem:
a) $x=1, y=2$; 6) $x=1, y=1$ or $x=2, y=3$.
It is easy to check that these numbers satisfy the corresponding equations, and in part d) we will show that there are no other solutions.

b) Answer: $n=4$. If $1/n$ is a final decimal fraction, then $1/n=m/10^s$, where m and s are natural numbers, therefore, $10^s$ is divisible by the integer n, that is, $n=2^a \cdot 5^b$ and similarly $n+1=2^c \cdot 5^d$, where a, b, c, d are non-negative integers. From here we obtain the equality $2^a \cdot 5^b+1=2^c \cdot 5^d$, which, obviously, can only hold for a=0, d=0 or b=0, c=0; in the first case $5^b+1=2^c$, in the second $5^d-1=2^a$. In part d) we will show that the equation $5^x \pm 1=2^y$ has no solutions for x>1. It remains to note that $5+1 \neq 2^y$, and $5-1=2^2$, whence a=2, b=0 and n=4.

d) We must prove that the numbers $p^m+1$ and $p^m-1$ each have at least one odd divisor (greater than one).

Let m be even, m=2n. Since p is a prime number and p>3, $p^n=2s+1$, where s>1. Therefore, $p^m=(p^n)^2=4s^2+4s+1$ and $p^m+1=2(2s^2+2s+1)$, and $p^m-1=4s(s+1)$. But the number $2s^2+2s+1$ and one of the numbers s and s+1 are odd, which is what we needed.

Let m be odd. Then $p^m-1=(p-1)(p^{m-1}+p^{m-2}+...+1)$, and $p^m+1=(p+1)(p^{m-1}-p^{m-2}+...+1)$. The second factors in these two expansions are the sums of an odd number of odd terms, and are therefore odd.

It is easy to see that these calculations are also valid for all numbers of the form $3^m \pm 1$ ($m>1$), with the exception of $3^2-1=2^3$. It follows that the equation of points a) and b) has no solutions different from those we wrote out above.

Note also that in fact we have proved statement d) for all odd (not necessarily prime) $p>3$.
S. N. Bichkov, V. V. Prasolov, L. D. Kurlyandchik

F805. An alternating current $i(t) = I_0 \cos \omega t$ flows through a flat capacitor filled with a dielectric with dielectric constant $e$ and resistivity $Q$. Determine the voltage amplitude across the capacitor. The distance between the plates is $d$, the area of ​​each plate is $S$.

Like all implementations of capacitors, this capacitor can be replaced by the equivalent circuit shown in figure 1. In this circuit, the resistance $R$ of the resistor is equal to the ohmic resistance of the capacitor, which is due to the presence of conductivity of the medium filling the space between the capacitor plates. In our case

$$R = \varrho \frac{d}{S}.$$

The capacitance $C$ of an “ideal” capacitor (without ohmic resistance) is equal to the capacitance of a real capacitor, that is

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon S}{d}.$$

So $I(t)$ flowing through a real capacitor is equal to the sum of the current $I_R(t)$ flowing through the resistor and the current $I_C(t)$ flowing through an ideal capacitor.

All these three currents are out of phase with respect to each other. Let's construct a vector diagram of the amplitude values ​​of currents in the circuit and voltage. Let us plot the current amplitude $I_R(t)$ (Fig. 2) horizontally in the form of a vector $\vec{I}_{0R}$. The current and voltage across the resistor are in phase. Therefore, the vector $\vec{U}_0$ of the voltage amplitude on the resistor, and therefore on the real capacitor, coincides in direction with the vector $\vec{I}_{0R}$. Considering that the voltage on the capacitor lags behind the current in phase by $\pi/2$, let us plot the current amplitude vector $\vec{I}_{0C}$ vertically.

From the diagram it can be seen that the amplitude $I_0$ of the current flowing through a real capacitor is equal to

$$|\vec{I}_0| = \left(|\vec{I}_{0R}|^2 + |\vec{I}_{0C}|^2\right)^{1/2}.$$

Considering that $I_{0R} = U_0/R$, $I_{0C} = U_0 \omega C$, we get

$$|\vec{I}_0| = \frac{|\vec{U}_0|}{R} \left(1 + (\omega RC)^2\right)^{1/2}.$$

From here we find the voltage amplitude on the capacitor:

$$U_0 = \frac{I_0 R}{(1 + (\omega RC)^2)^{1/2}} = \frac{I_0 \varrho d}{S (1 + (\omega RC)^2)^{1/2}}.$$

V. V. Mozhaev

F806. In the circuit shown in figure, immediately after turning off the external magnetic field in which the inductance coil $L$ is located, a current $I$ flows through the resistor with resistance $R_1$. Neglecting the ohmic resistance of the coil, determine how much calves are released on the resistor with resistance $R_1$ and on the resistor with resistance $R_2$. The magnetic field was directed perpendicular to the plane of the coil turns.

During the change in the external magnetic field, an inductive emf will act in the circuit, which is caused, on the one hand, by a change in the magnetic inductance flux of the external magnetic field, and on the other hand, by a change in the magnetic inductance flux of the coil’s own magnetic field (self-induction emf).

When connecting resistors $R_1$ and $R_2$ in parallel, at any moment in time the connection between the currents flowing through the resistors will have the form $I_1(t) \cdot R_1 = I_2(t) \cdot R_2$. Therefore, immediately after turning off the external field, the current through resistor $R_2$ is equal to

$$I_2 = IR_1/R_2.$$

So $I_L$ flowing through the coil immediately after turning off the external magnetic field is equal to

$$I_L = I + I_2 = I \frac{R_1 + R_2}{R_2}.$$

After the field is turned off, the system is isolated. At the first moment, all the energy of the system is concentrated in the magnetic field of the coil:

$$W = \frac{LI_L^2}{2} - \frac{LI^2}{2} \frac{(R_1 + R_2)^2}{R_2^2}.$$

In the future, this energy will be released in the form of calves on resistors. Since the voltage drop across the resistors will be the same all the time, the amount of voltage released on the resistors will be inversely proportional to their resistance:

$$Q_1 \sim 1/R_1, Q_2 \sim 1/R_2.$$

Solving a system of equations

$$\begin{cases} Q_1 + Q_2 = \frac{LI^2}{2} \frac{(R_1 + R_2)^2}{R_2^2}, \\ Q_1/Q_2 = R_2/R_1, \end{cases}$$

we get

$$Q_1 = \frac{LI^2}{2} \frac{(R_1 + R_2)}{R_2}, Q_2 = \frac{LI^2}{2} \frac{(R_1 + R_2)R_1}{R_2^2}.$$
