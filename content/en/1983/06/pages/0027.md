The same process actually occurs in radiolarians. The concentration of liquid along the branching ribs causes solid frakines of seawater and salt to settle along these ribs and gradually form the solid skeleton of the animal. The geometry of this skeleton can be visualized if, in the mass of soap foam, one pays attention to the branching ribs, that is, to the segments along which different bubbles join. These segments are connected to each other (sometimes in a complex way) and form the “liquid backbone” of the foam. After the death of the animal, the soft tissues gradually disappear and a hard skeleton remains, formed in the manner described.

So, it is nothing more than a visual frozen image of a system of branching edges and singular points that arose in a complex minimal surface, which also included closed volumes limited by the semblance of soap bubbles. In addition, cell walls, which also accumulate seawater salts, also play a role in the formation of the solid skeleton of radiolaria.

The role of the minimal surface in chemistry is important, where interaction surfaces at the boundaries of different media determine the nature and rate of many chemical transformations. Examples of minimal surfaces are well-known membranes to all of us - this is the eardrum in ours; these are membranes that serve as boundaries of living cells; these are membranes in living organisms that separate one organ from another, etc.

This interesting topic attracts a variety of specialties in biology, chemistry, and physics. As we have seen, a number of interesting mathematical problems are associated with minimal surfaces. Their correct formulation and study allows us to better understand this amazing (albeit simple in appearance) natural phenomenon.
