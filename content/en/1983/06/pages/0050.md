the divisor can be written as

$$\sigma(n) = \frac{n}{d_{1}} + \frac{n}{d_{2}} + \dots + \frac{n}{d_{s}} = n \left( \frac{1}{d_{1}} + \frac{1}{d_{2}} + \dots + \frac{1}{d_{s}} \right).$$

where $d_{1}, \dots, d_{s}$ are all divisors of the number $n.$ Thus, the value $\sigma(n)/n$ is equal to the sum of several numbers from the finite sequence $1, 1/2, \dots, 1/n$ and it will be greater, the more numbers with small values ​​are included in this sum.

a) Let us prove that $\sigma(n) > 2n$ for any $n = 6k$, where $k > 1$ (of course, there are infinitely many such numbers). Since $n$ is divisible by 1, 2, 3, 6 and $k$, from (1) we obtain

$$\sigma(n) \geq n \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{k} \right) > 2n.$$

6) Let $n$ be any number that is a multiple of the product $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \dots \cdot 16 = 16!$. then $n$ is divisible by all natural numbers from 1 to 16 (we already have enough of these divisors) and by virtue of (1)

$$\sigma(n) \geq n \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \left( \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} \right) + \left( \frac{1}{5} + \dots + \frac{1}{8} \right) + \left( \frac{1}{9} + \dots + \frac{1}{16} \right) \right) > n \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \frac{1}{8} \cdot 4 + \frac{1}{16} \cdot 8 \right) = 3n.$$

Similarly, we can prove that for any $a$ the inequality $\sigma(n) > an$ holds for an infinite set of numbers $n.$ Note also that, as a more accurate estimate shows, in our proof for $a = 3$ the divisor is 16! can be replaced by 7!

b) From formula (1) it follows that

$$\sigma(n) \leq n \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n} \right).$$

Let us estimate the sum on the right side. Let $2^{k} \leq n < 2^{k+1}$, then $k \leq \log_{2}n$ and $1 + \dots + \frac{1}{n} < 1 + \left( \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} \right) + \left( \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{7} \right) + \dots + \left( \frac{1}{2^{k}} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1} \right) + \frac{1}{2^{k+1}} < 1 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 4 + \dots + \frac{1}{2^{k}} \cdot 2^{k} + 1 = k+2$, that is, $\sigma(n) < n \cdot (k+2) \leq n(\log_{2}n+2).$

d) Let us estimate the sum $\frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}$ in inequality (2) differently than in point c) - using an integral. Each term $1/k$ in it can be represented as the area of ​​a rectangle, the base of which is the segment $[k-1; k]$ of the axis $Ox$ (see figure), and this rectangle is located under the hyperbola $y = 1/x$. Therefore, the total area of ​​all rectangles for $k = 2, \dots, n$ is less than the curved trapezoid bounded by the hyperbola $y = 1/x$, the axis $Ox$ and the straight lines $x = 1$ and $x = n$. Thus,

$$\frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n} < \int_{1}^{n} \frac{dx}{x} = \ln n,$$

and therefore $\sigma(n) < n(\ln n+1).$
