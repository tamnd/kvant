This pump allowed the concept of gas pressure in the pumped out tube up to several millimeters of the path column. If, at this pressure, a voltage of the order of 1000 V is applied to the electrodes placed in the gas, then a glow discharge (Fig. 2) will flare up in the tube.

In order for a noticeable electric current to flow in a gas, it is necessary that there be a sufficient number of current carriers - ions and free electrons. How do they arise?

When a voltage is applied to the electrodes, the small number of charged particles that always exist in the gas are accelerated by the electric field and form new electrons and ions. Basically, this process occurs in two ways: firstly, due to the impacts of energetic electrons on gas molecules and their ionization and, secondly, due to the fact that the accelerated ions bombard the cathode and knock out new free electrons from it.

In addition to the ionization of gas molecules, other processes occur in the discharge - excitation of neutral molecules and recombination of ions and electrons. They cause the gas to glow.

The glow discharge can be very red. You can easily verify this by looking at gas-light advertising lamps. In them, neon burns with a bright red-orange light, and argon with a blue-green light.

But let's return to our light bulb. It turns out that if you start to bring the electrode and cathode closer together in the tube, the glowing column will first decrease and then completely continue to be positive. It is in this “shortened” form that a discharge exists in a light bulb.

In order for it to light up at a not very high voltage, the electrodes of the light bulb are coated with a special composition containing lithium and barium salts. Under the influence of ion impacts, such a cathode easily gives up electrons, and the ignition voltage decreases. For the TH-0.2-2 light bulb it is only 80-90 V. The color of its glow can be red or reddish.
