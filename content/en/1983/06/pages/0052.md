Thus,

$$m\omega^2 \cdot |CO| = 2G \frac{mM}{x^3} \cdot |CO|,$$

or

$$m \frac{2GM}{R^3} \cdot |CO| = m \frac{2GM}{x^3} \cdot |CO|.$$

This equality holds either for $|CO| = 0$ or for $x = R$. In the first case, the planet is located at the center of mass of the stars - at point $O$ (the triangle $ABC$ “degenerates” into the segment $AB$). In the second case, the planet is located at the vertex of an equilateral triangle (point $C$ or $C'$ on figure), at the other two vertices of which there are stars.

It is remarkable that even with different masses of stars $A$ and $B$ the “equilibrium positions” turn out to be the vertices $C$ or $C'$ of an equilateral triangle (you can check this fact yourself; but the calculations will be quite cumbersome).

F805. An alternating current $i(t) = I_0 \cos \omega t$ flows through a flat capacitor filled with a dielectric with dielectric constant $e$ and resistivity $Q$. Determine the voltage amplitude across the capacitor. The distance between the plates is $d$, the area of ​​each plate is $S$.

Like all implementations of capacitors, this capacitor can be replaced by the equivalent circuit shown in figure 1. In this circuit, the resistance $R$ of the resistor is equal to the ohmic resistance of the capacitor, which is due to the presence of conductivity of the medium filling the space between the capacitor plates. In our case

$$R = \varrho \frac{d}{S}.$$

The capacitance $C$ of an “ideal” capacitor (without ohmic resistance) is equal to the capacitance of a real capacitor, that is

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon S}{d}.$$

So $I(t)$ flowing through a real capacitor is equal to the sum of the current $I_R(t)$ flowing through the resistor and the current $I_C(t)$ flowing through an ideal capacitor.

All these three currents are out of phase with respect to each other. Let's construct a vector diagram of the amplitude values ​​of currents in the circuit and voltage. Let us plot the current amplitude $I_R(t)$ (Fig. 2) horizontally in the form of a vector $\vec{I}_{0R}$. The current and voltage across the resistor are in phase. Therefore, the vector $\vec{U}_0$ of the voltage amplitude on the resistor, and therefore on the real capacitor, coincides in direction with the vector $\vec{I}_{0R}$. Considering that the voltage on the capacitor lags behind the current in phase by $\pi/2$, let us plot the current amplitude vector $\vec{I}_{0C}$ vertically.

From the diagram it can be seen that the amplitude $I_0$ of the current flowing through a real capacitor is equal to

$$|\vec{I}_0| = \left(|\vec{I}_{0R}|^2 + |\vec{I}_{0C}|^2\right)^{1/2}.$$

Considering that $I_{0R} = U_0/R$, $I_{0C} = U_0 \omega C$, we get

$$|\vec{I}_0| = \frac{|\vec{U}_0|}{R} \left(1 + (\omega RC)^2\right)^{1/2}.$$

From here we find the voltage amplitude on the capacitor:

$$U_0 = \frac{I_0 R}{(1 + (\omega RC)^2)^{1/2}} = \frac{I_0 \varrho d}{S (1 + (\omega RC)^2)^{1/2}}.$$

V. V. Mozhaev
