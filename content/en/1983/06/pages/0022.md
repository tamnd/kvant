surface (soap film), often described in terms of surface tension of a liquid, is due to the presence of attractive forces between individual molecules and the imbalance of these forces at the surface boundary. The beginning of unbalanced forces entails the following interesting effect: the liquid film turns into an elastic surface, tending to minimize its area and, therefore, minimize the tension energy per unit area. In this case, we neglect gravity and air pressure.

Thus, the mathematical model of a soap film is a smooth surface of minimal area subtending a given contour: mathematicians call it a minimal surface. The classical mathematical theory of these surfaces belongs to the so-called calculus of variations, a field of analysis that arose in the 18th century. Nowadays, more modern means of topology and differential geometry are used to develop this theory. Of course, using “school” mathematics cannot present the theory itself; but its results are easy to visualize and can even be verified experimentally. However, beautiful geometric problems arose here along the way, which can be solved using elementary mathematics.
