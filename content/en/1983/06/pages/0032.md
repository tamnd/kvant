Observations over a large amount of numerical material support the hypothesis that there is an infinite number of numbers $D$, not divisible by the square of the prime, for which the length of the continued fraction period $\sqrt{D}$ exceeds $D^{\frac{1}{2}} - \varepsilon$ for any fixed positive $\varepsilon$.

The results used above that the period of the continued fraction $\sqrt{p}$ for primes $p$ of the form $4k+1$ has an odd length was proved in 1785 by Legendre. Later, the German mathematician Dirichlet established a similar theorem for the numbers $D=p \cdot q$, where $p, q$ are prime numbers with certain conditions. Only recently, in 1980, the American mathematician Lagaris found an algorithm to determine the solvability in number terms of equation (9) or, equivalently, the oddness of the length of a continued fraction for $\sqrt{D}$, in about $(\log D)^{5+\varepsilon}$ operations.

Continued fractions for specific numbers

We saw above that square irrationalities and only they can be expanded into periodic continued fractions. Thus, there is a certain clarity in the structure of continued fractions of quadratic irrationality. One will naturally ask what classes of other irrational numbers have equally good visible expansions into continued fractions. There is no satisfactory answer to this question.

For example, for the number $\sqrt[3]{2}$ do схх it is still unknown whether the incomplete partials $a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots, a_k$, ... its expansion into a continued fraction will be limited or not

$$\sqrt[3]{2} = a_0 + \frac{1}{|a_1|} + \frac{1}{|a_2|} + \dots$$

The first few thousand numbers $a_0, a_1, \dots$ were calculated on the computer

Here are the very first of them:

$$\sqrt[3]{2} = [1; 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 8, 1, 14, 1, 10, 2, 1, 4, 12, 2, 3, 2, 1, 3, 4, 1, 1, 2, 14, 3, 12, 1, 15, 3, 1, 4, 534, 1, 1, 5, 1, 1, 121, 1, 2, 2, 4, 10, 3, 2, 2, 41, 1, 1, 1, 3, 7, 2, 2, 9, 4, 1, 3, 7, 6, 1, 1, 2, 9, 2, 3, 3, 1, 1, 69, 1, 12, \dots]$$

From this decomposition it is clear that the sequence $a_0, a_1, \dots$ behaves as a limited sequence and only “outliers” (for example, 534, 121) in certain places violate this picture.

Moreover, until cxx not a single algebraic number is known that is not a square irrationality, about whose continued fraction there would be enough information.

Of particular interest was the continued fraction expansion of neo-shak classical constants. The number of such constants for which expansion into a continued fraction is known is very small.

L. Euler found the expansion of the number $e$ into a continued fraction

$$e = 2 + \frac{1}{|1|} + \frac{1}{|2|} + \frac{1}{|1|} + \frac{1}{|1|} + \frac{1}{|4|} + \dots,$$

where the law of conversion of incomplete quotients $a_0, a_1, \dots$ is:

$$a_0 = 2, a_1 = 1, a_2 = 2, a_3 = 1, a_4 = 1, a_5 = 4, \dots, a_{3m} = a_{3m-2} = 1, a_{3m-1} = 2m, m = 1, 2, \dots$$

Let us remind you that

$$e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k!} = 2,718281828459045 \dots$$

The derivation of correspondence (10), although not difficult, is not elementary. Note that no one knows the same “good” continued fraction expansion for the number π.
