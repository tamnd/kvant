Note that a cone with a vertex at the origin will not be a minimal surface. figure 10, e shows how the cone is deformed, trying to occupy a position with the smallest area (with a fixed boundary). The configuration corresponding to this position is the catenonde. This circumstance is connected with the effects with which we have already become acquainted when studying the decay of fourfold points into the sum of threefold points. In fact, figure 7 can be seen as the process of transforming a “one-dimensional cone” formed by two intersecting segments (the diameters of a point circle) into a more complex curve with two threefold singularities. In principle, a similar phenomenon occurs in the case of an ordinary two-dimensional cone, but only here its apex (singular point) inflates into a whole circle - the neck of the catenon.

In both cases, the process of deformation of the film (one-dimensional curve) reduces its original area (or length in the one-dimensional case) and forces the surfaces (curve) to take an optimal position, characterizing the smallest area (long).

Minimal surfaces in nature

It turns out that minimal films are widespread in nature as the most economical surfaces that form the skeletons of neotourian living organisms. The most effective example is the skeletons of radiolarians, microsoptic marine animals with the most diverse and exotic forms. Apparently, the first person to notice that surface tension plays a significant role in creating the shape of these creatures was the English scientist D'Arcy Wentworth Tompson, author of the book "Growth and Form." Radiolariン consists of small lumps of protoplasm enclosed in foam-like forms, similar to soap bubbles and films. Since these organisms are quite complex, the minimal surfaces included in them, generally speaking, have many branching points and branching ribs, on which the bulk of the liquid that makes up the organism is concentrated.
