Maybe that’s why the game of “tic-tac-toe” on an “endless” (that is, unlimited) board is spreading among students and high school students. It is easy to see that if on such a board we again consider “three in a row” to be a win, then the beginner will easily win. Check that in the game “four in a row” a beginner can win quite easily. Usually they play “five in a row”. Practice shows that in this case the beginner will win (we do not have proof of this statement).

From a logical point of view, renju is an intermediate option between the two descriptive varieties of tic-tac-toe. Board - 15x15. The beginner puts a “cross” in the central cell. Then the second player places a “zero” in any of the 224 free cells. On the third move, the first player places a “cross” in any free cell that is not located in the central 5x5 square (this is the only restriction recognized to balance the chances of the parties). Then, one by one, the player places “toes” and “crosses” in any free cells. The winner is the one who builds a row of five “crosses” or, accordingly, “toes”, and longer rows do not count. Bot and all rights Renju.

It only remains to add that the Renjiists themselves speak a different – ​​quite equivalent – ​​language.

The game "Renju" originated in Central Asia more than four thousand years ago. It was played by the ancient Armenians and Incas of pre-Columbian America. At the beginning of our history, Renju came to Japan, where it became most widespread. In the 20th century, Renju spread throughout the world.

How is Renju played? Co-players place their checkers at the intersection points of the square line on the side of 14 cells (Fig. 2); on each side of such a square there are 15 points, a total of 15x15. The beginner (“black”) places a black checker at the central point. Then the second player (“white”) places a white checker on any of the 224 free points. On the third move, Black places a black checker on any free point lying outside the central square (in the pictures it is drawn in the same color), containing 5x5 points (this restriction* applies only on the third move!). Then white and black take turns placing their checkers on any free points. The winner is the one who builds a row (vertically, horizontally or diagonally)

*) It was invented several years ago in our country. They also try to balance the chances of the parties with other restrictions (see, for example, “Science and Life”, 1980, No. 9).
