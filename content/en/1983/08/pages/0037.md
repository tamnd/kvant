Let's return to the question of achieving the minimum value of the functions $f(M)$. The analysis proves that a continuous numerical function defined on a segment must take its maximum and minimum values ​​at some of its points $^*$). A similar theorem is also true for functions of several variables, for example, for functions on the plane; in particular, a function continuous on a polygon always achieves its largest and smallest values ​​on it. However, this theorem is not directly applied to our problem—the function $f(M)$ is not defined at the vertices of triangle ABC. Moreover, it is even impossible to redefine it at points $A, B, C$ so that it becomes continuous on the entire triangle (including the corners, we get a hexagon on which our function will already be continuous and, therefore, reaches its minimum value. It can be shown that near the boundary of the triangle $f(M) > 2r$. Therefore, if cutting off the corners is small enough, the smallest value $f(M)$ on the hexagon, and therefore on triangle, is achieved at $M=I$ and is equal to $2r$.

The considered proof of inequality (13) is indirect, in contrast to the so-called direct ones, when it is directly proven that (in our case) $f(M) \geq f(I)$ for all points $M$ inside triangle ABC. Indirect evidence must be carried out with some caution, because the highest or lowest value of functions is not always achieved. You don’t have to look far for an example: the function $f(M)$ itself does not take on its greatest value; the reader is invited to prove that $f(M) < l$, where $l$ is the length of the longest side of the triangle, for all points $M$ of triangle ABC (except the final vertices), and the value $f(M)$ can be as close as possible to $l$.

Let's move on to the proof of inequality (14). It will also be indirect: we will show that the maximum point $M$ of the lying part of this inequality (if it exists!) coincides with $I$.

We draw a circle around the triangle BMC and continue cutting
point $AM$ until the secondary intersection with it at point $A_2$ (Fig. 6). Let us apply Ptolemy’s theorem to the quadrilateral $BMCA_2$ - “the sum of the products of opposite sides of a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle is equal to the product of its diagonals”: ​​$|BM| \cdot |A_2C| + |CM| \cdot |A_2B| = |BC| \cdot |A_2M|$.

This theorem can be proven by expressing the area of ​​a quadrilateral in two ways: if $KLPQ$ is a cyclic quadrilateral and the chord $KK'$ is parallel to the diagonal $LQ$, then $S_{KLPQ} = S_{K'LPQ}$ (Fig. 7); at the same time $2S_{KLPQ} = |KP| \cdot |LQ| \cdot \sin \alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the diagonals $KP$ and $LQ$, and $2S_{K'LPQ} = |K'L| \cdot |LP| \sin K'LP + |K'Q| \cdot |QP| \times \sin K'QP = (|KQ| \cdot |LP| + |KL| \cdot |QP|) \times \sin \alpha$, or $K'LP = \alpha$ - see Fig. 7.

Considering that the lengths of the chords of one circle are proportional to the lengths of the angles resting on them, we obtain

$$|BM| \sin A_2MC + |CM| \sin A_2MB = |A_2M| \sin BMC,$$

or

$$|BM| \sin AMC + |CM| \sin AMB = |A_2M| \sin BMC.$$

Comparing the last equality with inequality (14), we see that the left side of the inequality is equal to $|AA_2| \sin BMC$. This means that straight line $AM$ must pass through the center of the circle described about BMC, since otherwise, by moving $M$ along the arc $BC$, we can increase the left side of inequality (14). Further reasoning (including proof of the existence of a maximum point) is carried out in the same way as in problem (13).
