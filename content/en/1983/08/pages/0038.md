⟦folio 36⟧

The so-called Steiner-Lemus problem has long been considered one of the most difficult to prove statements of this kind.

(15) Prove that if two bisectors in a triangle are equal, then the triangle is equilateral.

This problem is widely known*), but the following amusing variation on the same theme is almost unknown even among geometry lovers.

(16) It is known about this triangle that the triangle formed by the base of its bisectors is equilateral. Is it possible to say that this triangle is equilateral?

It turns out that this is, generally speaking, impossible to say! More precisely, let $A_1, B_1, C_1$ be the bases of the bisectors of the triangle $ABC$. If $|A_1B_1| = |A_1C_1|$, and the triangle $ABC$ is not equilateral, then its angle $A$ tuna and cos $\hat{A}$ lies in the interval $] - \frac{1}{4}; \frac{\sqrt{17}-5}{4}$ [, which for the angle $A$ itself corresponds to the interval $102^\circ 40'; 104^\circ 28'$ [(values ​​are approximate in degrees). Conversely, for any angle $a$ from this interval one can specify a unique (up to similarity) unequal triangle $ABC$ with $\hat{a} = a$ for which $|A_1B_1| = |A_1C_1|$. A detailed solution to this problem is given in I. F. Shargin’s book “Problems in geometry (planometry)” (M., Nauka, 1982), p. 157. Unfortunately, the author was unable to construct a specific example of a triangle (that is, accurately indicate the values ​​of all its angles or side lengths) with such exotic properties. Maybe the readers of the magazine will succeed?
