⟦folio 10⟧

2°. $M_B=0$, that is, the center of gravity coincides with the metanet (Fig. 2, a). Then the gravitational force $\vec{P}$ and the supporting force $\vec{\gamma}$ act in one straight line and no restoring moment occurs. This case is called the case of zero stability of the ship; practical vessel is unstable.

3°. $M<0$, that is, the center of gravity lies above the methantor (Fig. 2, b). Then the restoring moment acts in the same direction as the fastening moment - in the direction of increasing roll; the ship is unstable.

When the vessel is anchored at a large angle $\theta$, the physical essence does not change, only the formula for finding the shoulder $GK$ of stability takes on a more complex form.

Thus, the stability of the ship must be reduced according to certain laws of ship design (it was precisely these calculations by engineer Reed that were neglected when the decision was made to build the battleship “Captain”).

But when making these calculations, designers must take into account that excessive stability also cannot be considered a positive phenomenon, since a ship with excessive stability experiences sharp roll, which negatively affects the well-being and performance of the crew and the operation of ship mechanisms.

And when operating a ship, every captain remembers that any reasonably stable ship can be rendered unstable by unreasonable actions. For example, if the captain of a fishing vessel, carried away by the LOB (such a rich school was caught!), does not ensure that the catch is processed and placed in triomes in a timely manner, too many fish will accumulate on his finger. As a result, the ship's center of gravity rises above the thrower and the ship's stability will be shaken.

When working on ships, it should be taken into account that when accepting and removing cargo, the relative position of the center of gravity and the center of magnitude changes, which entails a change in stability. Therefore, during cargo operations at sea and in port, the captain must monitor and regulate the stability of the vessel. To do this, in the process of cargo operations, stability is calculated using certain formulas and diagrams, taking into account everything and the location of the fuel reserves, drinking water, ballast, food supplies and the cargo being accepted or removed on the ship.

Unsinkability and the sinking of the Titanic

Each vessel, naturally, must be unheatable, that is, the ability to remain afloat and not capsize parts of its compartments when flooded. In other words, when one or more compartments are flooded, the ship must remain buoyant and stable.
