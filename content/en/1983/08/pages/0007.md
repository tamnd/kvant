It is impossible to tell in detail about all the research conducted in the ocean within the framework of a journal article. This includes research into the solution and the study of its structure; study of the laws of sediment accumulation and study of heat flows from the bottom; determining the chemical composition of ocean waters and studying vortices in ocean waters, and much, much more. Therefore, I will try to tell in more detail only about the research related to the study of sound dispersion of maritime organizations.

Several decades ago, it was discovered that in almost all areas of the World Ocean, except for its polar regions, there are clusters of small marine organizations localized vertically. These aggregations usually consist of small fish a few centimeters in size. They form one or several layers decades meters thick. During the day, these layers are located at depths of 200-1000 m, and at night they rise, or, as biologists say, settle down, to the surface. These clusters dissipate from them much more than from a thickness that does not contain marine organizations (that is, the fraction of sound energy scattered when a signal passes through a cluster of fish is much larger than in the case of “clean” water). Such clusters were precisely discovered by sound scattering, which was registered ecologically as a “false bottom.” They were called sound-bearing layers (abbreviated 3RS). The number of organizations and their composition varies greatly from area to area, and since these small fish serve as substitutes for larger commercial fish, information about 3RS allows us to judge the distribution of commercial fish. This information is obtained using action waves.

The sound emitter generates vibrations of a certain part. When vibrations, propagating through the water column, encounter clusters of marine organizations, a small portion of the energy is dissipated in all directions, including in the opposite direction. This scattered energy is captured by the sound receiver, which is located next to the emitter. Knowing the emitted power and measuring the intensity of the scattered signal, it is possible to determine what fraction of the energy is dissipated in the 3RS.
