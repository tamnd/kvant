Around the bisector

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
I. F. SHARYGIN

This article collects some geometric facts directly or indirectly related to the bisector of a triangle. Among them, the reader will find simple but often used “lemmas”, more solid and difficult “theorems” and simply beautiful “tasks”. But we will not classify them in any way, but will simply number them in the order of appearance in the statistics. Statements that are given in the article without proof are automatically included in the exercises. However, we tried to present the evidence available in the article as concisely as possible in order to leave the reader room for thought.

Everyone should know this

Let us first recall some generally accepted notations: $ABC$ is a given triangle, $S_{ABC}$ is its area, $|BC|=a$, $|CA|=b$, $|AB|=c$, $2p=a+b+c$, $O$ and $R$ are, respectively, the center of the circumscribed circle and its radius, $I$ and $r$ is the center and radius of the inscribed circle. In addition, the triangle has three outer circles, each of which touches one side of the triangle and is extended by the other two sides. Their centers and radii will be denoted, respectively, by $I_a$, $I_b$, $I_c$, $r_a$, $r_b$, $r_c$ ($I_a$ is the center of the out-of-circle tangent to the side $BC$ and the extended side $AB$ and $AC$, $r_a$ - its radius).

All other symbols will be explained in accordance with the location.

(1) Let the bisector of the angle $A$ intersect the side $BC$ at the point $A_1$. Then
$$\frac{|BA_1|}{|A_1C|} = \frac{|BA|}{|AC|} = \frac{c}{b}.$$

(2) Let the bisector of the external angle at the vertex $A$ intersect the line $BC$ at the point $A_2$. Then
$$\frac{|BA_2|}{|A_2C|} = \frac{|BA|}{|AC|} = \frac{c}{b}.$$

(3) $S_{ABC} = pr.$

(4) $S_{ABC} = (p-a)r_a.$

(5) If $M$ is the point of tangency of the inscribed circle with sides $AB$, then $|AM| = p-a$. (Similarly,

(6) If $M$ is the point of tangency of the outer circle with center $I_a$ and line $AB$, then $|AM| = p$.

(7) The vertices $B$ and $C$ lie on a circle with diameter $II_a$; the center $L$ of this circle lies on the circumcircle (Fig. 1).

Thus, the center of the incircle $I$ has the following property: the lines $AI$, $BI$ and $CI$ (that is, the bicsetrices) pass through the centers of the circumcircles of the triangles $BIC$, $CIA$, $AIB$, respectively. The converse theorem is also true.

(8) If the lines $AM$, $BM$ and $CM$ pass through the circumcentres of triangles $BMC$, $CMA$ and $AMB$, then $M$ is the incircle center of triangle $ABC$.

Indeed, let $M_a$, $M_b$, $M_c$ be the points of intersection of the lines $AM$, $BM$ and $CM$ with the corresponding circles, different from $M$ (Fig. 2). Then $MM_a$, $MM_b$ and $MM_c$ are the diameters of these circles, so $M_aA$, $M_bB$ and $M_cC$ are the altitudes of triangle $M_aM_bM_c$. From here we get $B\hat{A}M = B\hat{M}_cM = 90^\circ - B\hat{M}_aC = C\hat{M}_bM = C\hat{A}M$, that is, $M$ lies on the bicsetrice of the angle $A$ and, similarly, on the bicsetrices of the angles $B$ and $C$.

(10) $|OI_a|^2 = R^2 + 2Rr_a$.

(11) $|II_a|^2 = 4R(r_a - r)$.

Two extremal properties of the center of the incircle

Take an arbitrary point $M$ inside the triangle $ABC$. There are quite a few inequalities regarding the distances from this point to the vertices of the triangle. We will consider two such inequalities that are relevant to the topic of the article.

(13) Let us denote by $A_1$ the point of intersection of the straight line $AM$ with the circumscribed circle. Then

$$\frac{|BM| \cdot |CM|}{|A_1M|} \geq 2r.$$

(14) $|AM| \sin B\widehat{MC} + |BM| \times \sin C\widehat{MA} + |CM| \sin A\widehat{MB} \leq p$.

In denote by the straight line the inequalities are achieved if $M$ coincides with $I$ - the center of the inscribed circle.

Let us assume that the smallest value of the expression $f(M) = \frac{|BM| \cdot |CM|}{|A_1M|}$ on the left side of inequality (13) is achieved at some point $M$ inside the triangle $ABC$. We will show that $M = I$. And since $f(I) = 2r$ (this follows, for example, from the similarity of the right angle triangles $BID$ and $I_aIC$ on figure 4*)), it will follow that if $f(M)$ belongs to its smallest value inside the triangle $ABC$, then $f(M) \geq 2r$. The highlighted proposal is non-trivial and very significant. We'll discuss it a little later.

Let us describe a circle (Fig. 5) around the triangle $AMC$. All triangles $CMA_1$, which when moving the point $M$ along its arc $AC$, are similar to each other (why?), therefore, the relation $|CM| / |A_1M|$ will be the same for them. Therefore, if $M$ is the minimum point of $f(M)$, the line $BM$ must pass through the center of the circumcircle of triangle $AMC$, otherwise we could reduce $|BM|$ while keeping the ratio $|CM| / |A_1M|$ constant. Let now $B_1$ and $C_1$ be the points of intersection of the lines $BM$ and $CM$ with the circle,

$$\frac{|BM| \cdot |CM|}{|A_1M|} = \frac{|CM| \cdot |AM|}{|B_1M|} = \frac{|AM| \cdot |BM|}{|C_1M|}.$$

Consequently, straight lines $AM$ and $CM$ must also pass through the center

Let's return to the question of achieving the minimum value of the functions $f(M)$. The analysis proves that a continuous numerical function defined on a segment must take its maximum and minimum values ​​at some of its points $^*$). A similar theorem is also true for functions of several variables, for example, for functions on the plane; in particular, a function continuous on a polygon always achieves its largest and smallest values ​​on it. However, this theorem is not directly applied to our problem—the function $f(M)$ is not defined at the vertices of triangle ABC. Moreover, it is even impossible to redefine it at points $A, B, C$ so that it becomes continuous on the entire triangle (including the corners, we get a hexagon on which our function will already be continuous and, therefore, reaches its minimum value. It can be shown that near the boundary of the triangle $f(M) > 2r$. Therefore, if cutting off the corners is small enough, the smallest value $f(M)$ on the hexagon, and therefore on triangle, is achieved at $M=I$ and is equal to $2r$.

The considered proof of inequality (13) is indirect, in contrast to the so-called direct ones, when it is directly proven that (in our case) $f(M) \geq f(I)$ for all points $M$ inside triangle ABC. Indirect evidence must be carried out with some caution, because the highest or lowest value of functions is not always achieved. You don’t have to look far for an example: the function $f(M)$ itself does not take on its greatest value; the reader is invited to prove that $f(M) < l$, where $l$ is the length of the longest side of the triangle, for all points $M$ of triangle ABC (except the final vertices), and the value $f(M)$ can be as close as possible to $l$.

Let's move on to the proof of inequality (14). It will also be indirect: we will show that the maximum point $M$ of the lying part of this inequality (if it exists!) coincides with $I$.

We draw a circle around the triangle BMC and continue cutting
point $AM$ until the secondary intersection with it at point $A_2$ (Fig. 6). Let us apply Ptolemy’s theorem to the quadrilateral $BMCA_2$ - “the sum of the products of opposite sides of a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle is equal to the product of its diagonals”: ​​$|BM| \cdot |A_2C| + |CM| \cdot |A_2B| = |BC| \cdot |A_2M|$.

This theorem can be proven by expressing the area of ​​a quadrilateral in two ways: if $KLPQ$ is a cyclic quadrilateral and the chord $KK'$ is parallel to the diagonal $LQ$, then $S_{KLPQ} = S_{K'LPQ}$ (Fig. 7); at the same time $2S_{KLPQ} = |KP| \cdot |LQ| \cdot \sin \alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the diagonals $KP$ and $LQ$, and $2S_{K'LPQ} = |K'L| \cdot |LP| \sin K'LP + |K'Q| \cdot |QP| \times \sin K'QP = (|KQ| \cdot |LP| + |KL| \cdot |QP|) \times \sin \alpha$, or $K'LP = \alpha$ - see Fig. 7.

Considering that the lengths of the chords of one circle are proportional to the lengths of the angles resting on them, we obtain

$$|BM| \sin A_2MC + |CM| \sin A_2MB = |A_2M| \sin BMC,$$

or

$$|BM| \sin AMC + |CM| \sin AMB = |A_2M| \sin BMC.$$

Comparing the last equality with inequality (14), we see that the left side of the inequality is equal to $|AA_2| \sin BMC$. This means that straight line $AM$ must pass through the center of the circle described about BMC, since otherwise, by moving $M$ along the arc $BC$, we can increase the left side of inequality (14). Further reasoning (including proof of the existence of a maximum point) is carried out in the same way as in problem (13).

Prove on your own that if $M$ coincides with $I$, then $|AA_2| \sin BMC = p$; To do this, you can use, for example, the given (6) and (7) and the fact that $BIC = 90^\circ + \hat{A}/2$.
