Floating and the fate of the bark "Zvezda"

The property of a vessel's floatability, known to every schoolchild, is based on the property of a vessel's floatability - the ability to float with a given draft, carrying on the outside the loads necessary for normal operation.

Two types of forces act on a ship in calm water (Fig. 1, a): the gravitational force $\vec{P}$, which is applied at the center of gravity $G$ of the vessel and directed vertically downwards (towards the center of the Earth), and the hydrostatic pressure force $\vec{\gamma}$, which is applied at the so-called center of magnitude $C$ and is directed vertically upward. Under the influence of the latter force, the ship tends to float, which is why it is called the supporting force.

If all the ships have large supporting forces, the ship will naturally sink. By the way, Benito de Soto wanted to take advantage of this by “loading” the barque “Zvezda” with sea water.

To ensure the floatability of the vessel during its operation, the navigator is required to use many mathematical concepts and calculation techniques. In particular, this requires a drawing of the vessel and associated coefficients, parameters, determination of the landing of the vessel, the equilibrium equation of the vessel; the entire vessel and the coordinates of its center of gravity, volumetric displacement and coordinates of the center of magnitude, the change in the recess due to the reception of cargo are calculated; the number of tons per unit of sediment is determined. Thus, the stability of the ship must be reduced according to certain laws of ship design (it was precisely these calculations by engineer Reed that were neglected when the decision was made to build the battleship “Captain”).

But when making these calculations, designers must take into account that excessive stability also cannot be considered a positive phenomenon, since a ship with excessive stability experiences sharp roll, which negatively affects the well-being and performance of the crew and the operation of ship mechanisms.

And when operating a ship, every captain remembers that any reasonably stable ship can be rendered unstable by unreasonable actions. For example, if the captain of a fishing vessel, carried away by the LOB (such a rich school was caught!), does not ensure that the catch is processed and placed in triomes in a timely manner, too many fish will accumulate on his finger. As a result, the ship's center of gravity rises above the thrower and the ship's stability will be shaken.

When working on ships, it should be taken into account that when accepting and removing cargo, the relative position of the center of gravity and the center of magnitude changes, which entails a change in stability. Therefore, during cargo operations at sea and in port, the captain must monitor and regulate the stability of the vessel. To do this, in the process of cargo operations, stability is calculated using certain formulas and diagrams, taking into account everything and the location of the fuel reserves, drinking water, ballast, food supplies and the cargo being accepted or removed on the ship.

Unsinkability and the sinking of the Titanic

Each vessel, naturally, must be unheatable, that is, the ability to remain afloat and not capsize parts of its compartments when flooded. In other words, when one or more compartments are flooded, the ship must remain buoyant and stable.

What was the service with the Titanic, which received damage to its hull? According to one of the witnesses of the disaster, at first this giant appeared on a slight swell, shining with the lights of portholes and windows, only slightly tilting on its nose. Then the trim began to increase sharply

Yes, after the damage, the floating island stayed on the water for only two hours. And this is an accident because during the design and construction of the Titanic, the basic requirements of unsinkability were violated: the transverse waterproof bulkheads separating one section from another were not extended to the upper deck, and the gun faces were not waterproof. In this regard, and bearing in mind the decision of the London court that took place after the sinking of the Titanic, academician Alexei Nikolaevich Krylov wrote: “Thus, all the blame is placed entirely on the captain, who also died at his post; the design flaws themselves, which caused his death with relatively minor damage, are not mentioned in a single word in the court ruling.”

Ship safety today

But perhaps the achievements of science and technology in the field of navigation and shipbuilding have significantly reduced concerns about the seaworthiness of the ship and, thus, reduced the importance of mathematics? And is mathematics necessary in other important areas of a navigator’s work?

Nowadays the sea element remains as formidable and insidious as it has always been. The achievements of modern science and technology, of course, have increased the degree of safety of navigation, but, on the other hand, the volume, variety and complexity of the tasks that today's marines solve have sharply increased.

So, at the beginning of 1982, the large-scale vessel “Serebryanka” led an industrial beast through the Arctic ice environment. When the external movement of the pack ice occurred, the skill of the captain and the sailors led by him made it possible to avoid disastrous consequences, but the ship still received two problems: in the auxiliary engine room and in the first three holds. The sailors managed to seal the holes. But in the process of fighting for the survivability of the ship, the captain and his assistant had to quickly make numerous complex and accurate mathematical calculations, showing what exactly needs to be done to reduce the dangerous trim in the event of flooding of damaged compartments (of course, taking into account the distribution of the cargo on the ship).
