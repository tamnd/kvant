Unusual crystal generators

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
I. V. KARPOVA

Imagine an electrical target consisting of a specially prepared semiconductor chip, a resistor, and a DC voltage source (such as a flashlight battery) connected in series. It turns out that under certain conditions electrical vibrations can occur in such a circuit. This was first predicted theoretically and then confirmed experimentally.

Vibrations with an amplitude of several volts were detected in the experiment; the vibration frequencies ranged from $10^2$ to $10^7$ Hz. The lasing sample was a rectangular bar with face sizes from several mm to tens of microns, cut from a single crystal of germanium or silicon.

It is characteristic that vibrations occur under the most general conditions: at room temperature, without additional pressure, in the absence of strong electric or magnetic fields. All features are related only to the composition of the crystal. What are these features?

It is known that in semiconductors, charges are transferred by two types of carriers: electrons and holes, which differ from each other, first of all, in the sign of the charge and the direction of ordered movement in the electric field. Electrons and holes can be in two states - free and bound. The transition from the first state to the second is called recombination. In this case, the transition of charge carriers is captured by so-called traps, which usually serve as impurity atoms or defects in the crystal lattice of the semiconductor. Traps are usually charged, so it is clear that electrons and holes are captured by the same trap with different probabilities. Consequently, the time during which they participate in current transfer (lifetime) is also not the same. So, if suitable impurities are introduced into the crystal, such that the lifetime of electrons and holes, as well as their concentrations, satisfy a certain ratio, then the generation of electrical oscillations can occur in the crystal. Let's try to explain this.

Let us assume that somewhere in the semiconductor a fluctuation (inhomogeneity) of the electron density has accidentally arisen. Since the semiconductor as a whole is electrically neutral, the resulting negative charge must be compensated by the positive charge of the corresponding hole fluctuation. Let the traps introduced into the crystal be such that almost all the excess holes will be bound and the electrons will be free.

If an external electric field of direction $E_0$ is applied to the sample, it polarizes the fluctuations of holes and electrons so that, as a result, the total electric field has a dip

(see figure). Let us distance the flows of free holes flowing into and out of the field wire region. The flow of holes, that is, the number of holes passing every second through a unit cross-sectional area perpendicular to the direction of the holes, is equal to $q_p=n_pv$, where $n_p$ is the concentration of holes, $c$ is their speed. In a homogeneous crystal, the concentration of free holes is always directed (similar to the directional speed of free holes in a metal). On the left slope of the field wire, the inflow of holes is greater than the outflow, so the holes will accumulate on the left slope, that is, they will grow on the traps. On the right slope of the field wire, fewer holes flow in than flow out, that is, here the holes will be produced from traps. Thus, the hole fluctuation will move to the left.
