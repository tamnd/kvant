Some generalizations

If you carefully analyze the progress of the proof of the “horse” theorem, you will find that it is not so important that the points A and A' are diametrically opposed. All reasoning remains valid, for example, when instead of the center O we take an arbitrary internal point O₁ of the circle and under A and L' we place the ends of the chord passing through O₁. One can proceed similarly with the zero field theorem and with the Borsuk-Ulam theorem (Fig. 8). The important thing here is that with a new definition of the point P' the property (P'')=P is preserved.

Any continuous mapping P→P' that has this property is called an involute. The Soviet mathematician A. Fet, using subtle and powerful means of topology, discovered that the Borsuk-Ulam theorem (hereinafter in its n-dimensional version) remains in force if an arbitrary involution P→P' is given on the sphere S. Here is the exact formulation of this statement for three-dimensional space:

Let an arbitrary involution P→P' be given on the sphere S. For each pair of functions f(P) and g(P) continuous on S, there is a point P₀∈S such that f(P'₀)=f(P₀), g(P'₀)=g(P₀).

The reader who has a desire to become more familiar with the basic concepts of topology, we recommend reading the excellently written popular book by N. Steenrod and W. Chinn “First Concepts of Topology” (M., Mir, 1967) *. This book contains a more complete and rigorous proof of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem, as well as a number of other “appetizing” theorems (for example, the “pancake theorem”, “the ham cutting theorem”).

Laws of electrolysis

At the very beginning of 1833, Faraday reported to the Royal Society of London about his experiments, which for the first time proved the exact existence of various types of electrecent - “generally”, arose as a result of friction, zalvanic, thermoelectricity, electrecent obtained by induction, and the so-called animal electrecent (found in some fish and marine animals). The essence of the proof of a state is to demonstrate the sameness of “actions”, arbitrary electric forces of various processes.

Among other actions (such as tell, physiological or magnetic), Faraday also studied chemical action. This research led him to the conclusion, which later received the name Faraday’s first law for electrolysis: the chemical effect of the electric current is directly proportional to the absolute amount of passage of the electric current (naturally, Faraday’s formulation is somewhat different from the modern one). The law was formulated on the basis of a series of carefully thought out and masterfully conducted experiments, during which Faraday discovered another law, now called Faraday's second law: electrochemical equivalents correspond to chemical equivalents.

Faraday finished writing a report on these experiments on December 31, 1833, and published it already in 1834. That is why they later write that Faraday's laws were discovered in 1833, and others - in 1834.

Establishing precise quantitative laws of electrolysis in those days was not an easy task. Faraday managed to do this, in many cases with the invention of a device - the voltazlektrometer, which for the first time made it possible to change the amount of electricity.

Let us also note that it was during the description of these studies of Faraday that the terms necessary for us now, such as electrolyte, electrolysis, aode, cathode, cathode, etc., were also introduced into physics.

In addition to the fact that Faraday’s laws formed the basis of electrochemistry, they played a very important role in the history of the development of physics: for the first time, the idea of ​​​​the discrete nature of the electric energy, the existence of the “electric energy atom” (in modern terms - electron).
