The picture of parallel horizontal layers so beautifully drawn by us is actually distorted in space by the existence of deep, so-called “internal” waves caused by the oscillations of layers of water of different densities. Vertical oscillations can have periods of the order of a day and reach a range of 100 m. The thickness of ocean waters can be affected by waves caused by underwater earthquakes—tsunamis.

Reception of weak signals is complicated by the presence of ambient noise. This background consists of noise caused by dynamic processes, mainly wind waves, biological noise (it is known that marine animals widely use acoustic waves), seismic background, noise of ships passing, etc.

Further, from this brief listing of factors influencing the propagation of sound in the ocean, the complexity of the process of transmitting and receiving acoustic signals in the ocean is visible. One can imagine the difficulties encountered when studying the laws of sound propagation in the ocean. But experiments are carried out in a very wide frequency range and at distances from hundreds of meters to thousands of kilometers.

The study of acoustic fields in the ocean is only part of the problems that ocean acoustics studies. There are tasks that are commonly called processing tasks. The nature of propagation, reflection, and scattering of acoustic waves provides information about the properties of the medium, about objects that reflect and scatter sound. Determining the characteristics of the ocean from such information is the ultimate goal of the study. For example, the propagation time of an echo sounder pulse to the bottom and back allows you to change the depth of the ocean. Acoustic methods of ocean research replace traditional “contact” methods with express “non-contact” ones.

It is impossible to tell in detail about all the research conducted in the ocean within the framework of a journal article. This includes research into the solution and the study of its structure; study of the laws of sediment accumulation and study of heat flows from the bottom; determining the chemical composition of ocean waters and studying vortices in ocean waters, and much, much more. Therefore, I will try to tell in more detail only about the research related to the study of sound dispersion of maritime organizations.

Several decades ago, it was discovered that in almost all areas of the World Ocean, except for its polar regions, there are clusters of small marine organizations localized vertically. These aggregations usually consist of small fish a few centimeters in size. They form one or several layers decades meters thick. During the day, these layers are located at depths of 200-1000 m, and at night they rise, or, as biologists say, settle down, to the surface. These clusters dissipate from them much more than from a thickness that does not contain marine organizations (that is, the fraction of sound energy scattered when a signal passes through a cluster of fish is much larger than in the case of “clean” water). Such clusters were precisely discovered by sound scattering, which was registered ecologically as a “false bottom.” They were called sound-bearing layers (abbreviated 3RS). The number of organizations and their composition varies greatly from area to area, and since these small fish serve as substitutes for larger commercial fish, information about 3RS allows us to judge the distribution of commercial fish. This information is obtained using action waves.

The sound emitter generates vibrations of a certain part. When vibrations, propagating through the water column, encounter clusters of marine organizations, a small portion of the energy is dissipated in all directions, including in the opposite direction. This scattered energy is captured by the sound receiver, which is located next to the emitter. Knowing the emitted power and measuring the intensity of the scattered signal, it is possible to determine what fraction of the energy is dissipated in the 3RS. By changing the oscillation frequency of the emitter, you can “tune” it to the natural frequency of the swimming belly of a fish. In this case, the share of dissipated energy

Already today there are ideas on how to obtain almost continuously information about the characteristics of the World Ocean throughout its entire water area. This method has received praise as active tomography. Its essence is as follows. Along the perimeter of the ocean area, the characteristics of which need to be known, several systems are firmly installed. The systems alternately study action impulses and apply them. The received signals carry information about temperature, salinity and other parameters of the environment through which they propagated, that is, about the region of the ocean “outlined” by the systems. As a result, it will be possible to continuously obtain characteristics of the ocean environment. The value of such information is very clear.

There are, of course, many difficulties on the way to solving this problem. First, we need to solve it theoretically; secondly, it is necessary to permanently install many quite powerful emitting systems throughout the World Ocean and control their changes. Thirdly, it is necessary to create a fleet of powerful computers that could process a huge amount of information in a relatively short time. And much, much more...

This task is undoubtedly of an international scale and is not yet today. However, I have no doubt that humanity will solve it.
