What was used with the light bulb?

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. N. PEGOEV

Of course, a light bulb burning out is a minor thing. Please, even the word “event” seems inappropriate here. However, the conversation about why and how filaments in incandescent lamps break down is worthy of attention.

Our industry produces billions of lamps a year. After serving for several hundred or thousands of hours, they all burn out, causing mild annoyance to some, and surprise and curiosity to others. Sometimes the sight of a light bulb burning out puzzles even an experienced electrician.

But before we talk about lamp burnout, a few words about their structure. The main part of the lamp (Fig. 1) is the incandescent body. Typically this is a tungsten filament in the shape of a helix or double helix. The ends of the thread are connected to the ends of the inputs. The middle part of the inputs is fixed in a glass blade. The thread is supported by inputs and wire holders, the latter are formed into a glass rod. This design will be placed in a glass flask. A cycol is glued to the flask, to the contact of which the outer ends of the inputs are soldered. Their diameter is slightly smaller than the diameter of the inner part of the bushings. Inside the cycol there is a spout - the sealed end of a glass tube through which air is released from the flask. In some types of lamps, the bulb is guided by an inert gas (argon, crypt), sometimes with the addition of halothenes.

The filament is the most unreliable element of the lamp. The current flowing through the filament heats it up to almost 3000 K. Increasing the voltage by 15% of the rated voltage leads to rapid destruction of the filament. Further, increasing the voltage by 1% reduces its service life by 15%, although it increases the light output by 4%.

As already mentioned, the sight of a light bulb burning out can be surprising, and sometimes it can also be challenging. Let us identify several types of lamp destruction based on external signs, formulate the corresponding questions, and then answer them.

The life of most lamps ends after prolonged burning. At the last moment, the light flickers, its color may change, sometimes a quiet hum is heard, and the thread breaks.

Question 1: Why after a long screen there is a section of thread, while the rest of the thread is still running thread, usually near the input.

In everyday life, up to a quarter of lamps burn out the moment they are turned on. Immediately after the circuit is closed, the lamp flashes brightly and goes out. It is typical that several sections of the thread are destroyed, usually near the input.

Question 2: why does the probability of filament burnout return at the moment of switching on, when the filament temperature has not yet reached the operating value?

Sometimes the filament burns out when turned on and is accompanied by a flash in the cycle. In this case, one of the inputs in the damaged part burns out, and evaporated metal is deposited on the glass of the flask. The flask may become disconnected from the cycle and hang.

In some cases (this applies mainly to powerful lamps), when the lamp is turned on, a significant part of the filament straightens out. Frozen drops of tungsten, their charred traces, and unmelted pieces of thread can be found on objects that were under the lamp.

* * *

Answer to question 1. It is natural to look for the reason for the destruction of a small section of the thread in the fact that the thread cannot be completely homogeneous. Let's imagine the thread as a series connection of small sections, shown in the diagram as resistors with a resistance of $R_i$ each (Fig. 3).

The electrical resistance of a section of thread is determined by its length $l_i$, diameter $d_i$ and resistivity of materials $Q_i:R_i = 4Q_i l_i / (\pi d_i^2)$. Let us choose the lengths of the sections to be the same and assume that the resistivities of all sections are the same, then the difference in resistance will be associated only with the difference in the diameters of the sections. The path along the thread is current $I$, which is determined by the direction $U$ applied to the thread and the resistance of the thread $R$ (that is, the sum of the resistances of all sections): $I = U/R$. Power $P_i = I^2 R_i$ is allocated at each site. Under the current regime, this power is spent on light and thermal radiation, as well as on heat removal through adjacent areas and inputs and through the gas in the flask.

The filament temperature $T_2$ is almost 3000 K, then, immediately after switching on, the resistance of the filament is approximately 10 times lower than in the heated state. Consequently, at the initial moment the current flowing through the filament exceeds the rated value by almost 10 times (Fig. 4). Then, as the filament heats up, the current decreases.

The increased current value in itself does not destroy the filament, but creates the preconditions for temporary overheating of its individual sections. Indeed, a weak section of a thread with a reduced diameter, as we have already found out, has increased resistance and increased power is released on it. In the unsteady mode, power is spent, in addition to radiation and heat removal, on heating the filament. Since the heat of the weak section is reduced compared to other sections, and the power is increased, this section heats up faster than the others. This, in turn, leads to an increase in the resistance of the weak section and to an additional acceleration of heating. As a result, its temperature reaches the operating level before the entire thread heats up. At this moment, the current still exceeds the rated value, so the weak section overheats for a while (see Fig. 4). This may be enough for tungsten to evaporate from the surface of the thread and the thread in this place decreases in diameter. With each subsequent switching on, the weakening of the thread progresses until, finally, at the next switching on, overheating leads to the destruction of the weak area.

Thus, the dependence of the thread resistance on temperature leads to overheating and destruction of the weak section of the thread at the moment of switching on, when the main part of the thread has not yet warmed up.

Lamps in theater lights, for example, which rise smoothly, are not threatened by such destruction. And flashing, partly switched on lamps must have particularly uniform filaments or work with underheating. The same applies, for example, to lamps for traffic lights.

Answer to question 3. We have already found out how the filament burns out when the lamp is turned on. Why might the input be destroyed? It is clear that the current that destroys the input does not flow through the filament, since the main part of the filament (with a relatively small melting current) has been preserved. It is also clear that this current flows inside the lamp, since the destruction of the input is the binding of the filament.

It turns out that the reason for everything is an arc discharge between the inputs inside the bulb. When a weak section of the filament is destroyed, a cloud of tungsten vapor, partially ionized, will appear in the flask. Sometimes a breakdown occurs through this cloud, and an electric arc lights up between the inputs. The arc current destroys the input in its weakened damaged part (Fig. 5). Traces of the arc remain in the form of a bluish coating on the inputs inside the bulb.

It is interesting that such an inconspicuous detail of the lamp design as the difference in the diameters of the inputs of the inner and outer bulb is important. If the diameter of the damaged input section was the same or larger than the diameter of the internal section, then it would be possible for the input lamp to evaporate. And this is fraught with the explosion of the flask.

Answer to question 4. Sometimes, after the destruction of a weak section of the thread, a breakdown will occur not between two inputs, but between one of the inputs and the rest of the thread. This leads to the melting of a large section of the filament and the possibility of explosion of the lamp bulb (Fig. 6). Is it possible

Let's look at two more unusual and interesting cases of lamp burnout.

Once, at the moment of switching off, the bulb of the lamp broke. It turned out that during prolonged burning the thread gradually sagged under the influence of gravity and approached the bottom

Now let's talk about a case of simultaneous death of twenty lamps connected in series. At a time when there were no Christmas tree garlands on Father Frost’s schedule, a chain of twenty low-power automatic lamps, rated at 12 V each, was compiled. When turned on with a voltage of 220 V, the lamps blinked and burned out (all twenty!), and a short piece of filament evaporated in each lamp. This is as amazing as if, when stretching a cord knitted from twenty pieces, it would break in twenty places - neatly between the knots (Fig. 7, a).

Why did all the lamps burn out at the same time? The reason for this is the following. The destruction of a weak section of the filament in one of the lamps led to the ignition of an electric arc between the inputs (Fig. 7, b). The resistance of the gap contract is significantly less than the resistance of the filament in operating mode, as a result of which the voltage on the remaining lamps has increased. This accelerated the death of the next lamp: the ignition of an arc in it (Fig. 7, c). The voltage on the increased lamps increased further, and the baton of destruction was passed from one lamp to another until there were no weak points left in the circuit. So the burnout of one weak lamp caused the destruction of the others. Such cases are, of course, rare, but quite possible.
