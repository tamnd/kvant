Flaring X-ray stars

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. D. CHERNIN

In 1975, a team of Soviet astronomers reported observing short, powerful bursts of cosmic X-ray radiation. Soon, American scientists discovered flashes of X-ray radiation in the center of one of the star clusters of our Galaxy. Thus began the history of the study of bursters—flaring X-ray stars. Their name comes from the English word burst, which means explosion, flash. More than 30 such stars are now known; 8 of them belong to star clusters.

The fact that stars are capable of emitting not only visible light, but also X-rays, was discovered in the last 15-20 years, when special X-ray telesons appeared, carried on high cylinders, rockets, and satellites beyond the Earth’s atmosphere (which does not allow cosmic X-ray radiation to reach us). Cosmic sources emit X-rays in the wavelength region from $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-10}$ m. Quantums corresponding to this range of electromagnetic waves have energies from 0.1 to 30 keV or from $10^{-17}$ to $3 \cdot 10^{-15}$ J.

The signals received by X-ray telescopes during the observations of different bursters differ in energy by approximately ten times. The values ​​of the energy flux density, that is, the energy arriving per unit surface per unit time, lie within $F = (3 \cdot 10^{-11} \div 3 \cdot 10^{-12}) \text{ Дж}/(\text{м}^2 \cdot \text{с})$.

If the distance to the source is $R = 3 \cdot 10^{20}$ m, we can find its luminosity, that is, the total energy emitted by the source per unit time in all directions:

$$L = 4\pi R^2 F = (3 \cdot 10^{30} \div 3 \cdot 10^{31}) \text{ Дж}/\text{с}.$$

Using the same method, we can find the background, non-vanishing luminosity of the burster. Its value is approximately the same for all bursters: $L_0 \approx 10^{30} \text{ Дж}/\text{с}.$

To understand the nature of bursters, it is very important that during flashing the burster glows in the same way as the surface of a body heated to a temperature of ten to thirty million degrees would glow. This was established by the type of X-ray spectrum of the burster, that is, by the characterization of the emitted energy depending on the wavelength $^*$). The average energy $\varepsilon$ of the emitted quantum is related to the temperature $T$ of the body by an approximate correspondence $\varepsilon \approx kT$, where $k = 1,4 \cdot 10^{-23} \text{ Дж}/\text{K}$ is Boltzmann’s constant. At a temperature of $T = (1 \div 3) 10^7$ K, the actual value of $\varepsilon \approx (1 \div 4) 10^{-16}$ J lies in the energy range characteristic of X-rays.

According to one of the laws of thermodynamics - the Stefan-Boltzmann law, the surface of a body heated to a temperature of $T$ emits per unit time from a unit area an amount of energy proportional to the body temperature: $W = \sigma T^4$. The proportionality coefficient $\sigma = 5,7 \times 10^{-8} \text{ Дж}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{с} \cdot \text{К})$ is called the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. The reedus ball $r$ has a surface area $4\pi r^2$; this surface, if heated to a temperature of $T$, creates luminosity

$$L = \sigma T^4 \cdot 4\pi r^2.$$

The cause of the flares is the transformations experienced by the matter that falls on the surface of the neutron star.

The material of the companion star captured by the burster has a general stellar chemical composition: (70-75)% hydrogen (by mass), about (25-30)% gel, approximately 1% heavier elements. When it hits the surface of a neutron star, the gas is compressed and heated to such an extent that thermonuclear reactions converting hydrogen into helium become possible - the same as in the depths of the Sun and other “normal” stars.

The energy released in the reactions is added to the energy imparted to the surface of the accretion star. This addition, however, is not very large. Indeed, in nuclear transformations - empty even before complete “combustion”, that is, the transformation of hydrogen into iron - no more than 0.01 $c^2$ energy is released per unit mass of matter. Therefore, the energy released per unit time during thermonuclear combustion of the accreted substance does not exceed 0.01 $c^2 j$. Comparing this with our estimate of the kinetic energy of the accreted substance, we find that thermonuclear reactions produce a surge of energy that is approximately thirty times less than the accretion energy.

Others are more important here. As a result of thermonuclear reactions of hydrogen combustion, a layer of gel is formed on the surface of the neutron star. It turns out that it is in the gel layer that the processes that cause outbreaks develop.

More than 30 years ago, L. E. Gurevich and A. I. Lebedinsky, developing a theory of the late stages of stellar evolution, discovered that under certain conditions thermonuclear explosions can occur on stars. In a star that has exhausted nuclear fuel in its central region, there are most often layers close to the surface where the matter is still rich in hydrogen, gel and other light elements. Let's imagine a situation where the temperature in these layers of weight is high, but still not enough for nuclear reactions to occur. Let, further, for some reason the temperature in one of these layers rises and suddenly turns out to be above the critical limit from which nuclear combustion becomes possible. What will happen next?

Obviously, he will learn nuclear reactions with the release of heat. But will the temperature of the layer remain sufficiently high? Will it go up or down? This depends on the correspondence between the temperatures of heat release in nuclear reactions and the temperatures of heat from the layer to the outside. If heat leaves the layer very quickly, almost instantly, then it is clear that, having barely begun, nuclear reactions will immediately die out, since the temperature of the layer will immediately drop and become critical.

A different result should be expected if the heat released during nuclear reactions does not have time to quickly leave the layer, so that its temperature rises further. As the temperature increases, nuclear reactions proceed more and more efficiently, heat release increases rapidly, and as a result, the temperature of the layer continues to increase. But then nuclear reactions almost slow down, the heat supply increases, etc. We can say that the process of nuclear energy release develops in this case on an avalanche basis, supporting and accelerating itself. And this is an explosion. In the case of nuclear energy release from a star, this process develops because the rate of nuclear reactions varies very quickly with temperature: the higher the temperature, the more efficient the nuclear reactions, the faster the heat is released.

Why does a neutron star manifest itself in some cases as a barstra, and in others as a pulsar?

Or in other words: why doesn’t the burster pulsate and the pulsar doesn’t flare up?

It is believed that X-ray pulsars are also part of test binary systems with flow of matter and accretion. It is possible that magnetic fields play a significant role in this case. The magnetic field of a neutron star, if it is strong enough ($10^{12}$ times stronger than the average magnetic field of the Sun, equal to $10^{-4}$ T), can change the nature of accretion - the matter is directed by the field predominantly to the magnetic poles of the star. Because of this, heating turns out to be uneven: the temperature at the poles is significantly higher than on the rest of the surface of the star. Hot spots at the poles create mainly radiation (remember that luminosity strongly depends on temperature, it is proportional to its four-degree). If a star rotates rapidly and at the same time the magnetic axis is inclined to its rotation axis (figure 3), then a lighthouse effect occurs: a bright spot is sometimes visible, sometimes not visible, and the radiation appears intermittent, pulsating.

The barster's magnetic field is believed to be weaker than that of a pulsar; it does not affect accretion, which occurs more or less uniformly in all directions, so that all surfaces of the neutron star are heated equally and radiate equally (figure 4). Then the rotation of the star, which can be fast here too, will not appear in the radiation. If so, then it becomes clear why the burster does not pulsate. But why doesn’t the pulsar produce a flash? It is assumed that the magnetic field that controls accretion is capable of somehow preventing thermostatic outbursts on the surface of a neutron star.

These guesses and hypotheses still need to be verified by further theoretical calculations and astronomical observations.
