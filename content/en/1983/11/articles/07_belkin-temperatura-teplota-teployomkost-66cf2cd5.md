The term “temperature” appeared in physics about 200 years ago, in the second half of the 18th century, and it remained in physics as a memory of those ideas about the body, cold, and temperature that now seem like pages, which then existed in science.

Since the 17th century, there has been a struggle in physics between two ideas about the nature of tellotes. This struggle became legal relatively recently - in the middle of a century; as a result, one of the tellote theories was completely rejected, and the second only partially triumphed.

The first theory (more precisely, a hypothesis) is that tellota is a special substance that can penetrate any tello. The more of this substance in the body, the higher its temperature. The experimental fact on which this idea was based was that when two differently heated bodies come into contact, the hotter one cools and the colder one heats up. It really looks as if nothing is flowing from a thicker body into a colder one. This something, a kind of solid substance, was called differently, but most often - caloric. It was believed that every body appears

is a mixture of the substance of the body itself with caloric, and the temperature, changed by a thermometer, characterizes the concentration of caloric in the body. The word “temperature” in Latin means mixture. Bronze, for example, is called a temperature (mixture) of copper and tin.

The second theory (hypothesis), first proposed at the beginning of the 17th century by the English scientist Francis Bacon, argued that heat is the movement of small particles inside a body (molecules, atoms, or, as they said then, insensitive particles). This hypothesis is also based on experienced observers showing, for example, that heating can be caused by movement. This theory had many supporters, and even very important ones, such as Descartes, Boyle, Hooke, Lomonsov.

Both theories, for all their differences, also had something in common. Both agree that heat is something contained in the body. According to the first hypothesis, the body contains caloric, according to the second - particles with their “living force” (as kinetic energy was then called). They also agreed that heat does not disappear and does not appear: if during contact of two bodies one of them loses heat, then the other receives it, so that what is lost by one is replaced by the other. Nevertheless, the overwhelming majority of researchers up to the 19th century adhered to the so-called material theory of heat, and the 18th century was, of course, the century of the triumph of this particular idea of ​​heat.

The introduction of the concept of thermal conductivity of dough is associated with the solution of problems of heat distribution during contact between different bodies.

Based on the first hypothesis and the fact that the thermometer shows the concentration of caloric in the body, the English mathematician Taylor showed that if, for example, the mass $m_1$ of water is displaced at a temperature $t_1$, the mass $m_2$ at a temperature $t_2$, $m_3$ at a temperature $t_3$, etc., then the temperature of the mixture turns out to be equal

$$t = \frac{m_1 t_1 + m_2 t_2 + m_3 t_3 + \dots + m_n t_n}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3 + \dots + m_n}.$$  (1)

The German physicist Fahrenheit, at the suggestion of the Inderland physician and chemist Burgrave, conducted experiments with two equal masses of water and rutui and showed that the temperature of the mixture (rhythm and water do not mix, but by meeting them they can still be brought to the same temperature) is not equal to the temperature $t$, calculated by formula (1), that is, that formula (1) for a mixture of different liquids is not correct. It turned out that if the water temperature is higher than the temperature of the rutui, then the temperature of the mixture is higher than calculated. This seemed to the countries, because if a thermometer actually shows the concentration of caloric in a body, then with equal masses any body receives the same amount of heat (tellorade) when heated by the same number of degrees.

The English chemist Black brought clarity to this issue in the mid-18th century. The Fahrenheit-Burgrave experiment, Black explains, shows that it takes less heat to heat a certain mass of rutui than to heat the same mass of water by the same number of degrees. In Black's own words: "Mercury therefore has less capacity for heat (if I may use that expression) than water, less heat being required to raise the temperature of the mercury by the same number of degrees."

This is how the concept of “heat capacity” (telloradiness) first appeared. Thus it was clearly shown that heat and temperature are not the same thing. But what was known before remains true: the amount of heat received by one body lost by another. In mathematical form, Black's idea is expressed by an equality now known to every schoolchild:

$$c_1 m_1 (t - t_1) = c_2 m_2 (t_2 - t).$$  (2)

It is called the heat balance equation, which expresses the idea of ​​heat conservation.

From equation (2) it is impossible to find the specific heat capacities $c_1$ and $c_2$ from

In particular, from experiments it turned out that the heat of water is 20 times greater than the heat of mercury (according to modern data - more than 30 times). In general, the specific heat of water is the most average of liquid and solid substances.

Some time later, it was proposed to take the specific heat of water as a unit (on this basis, the unit of heat was introduced - caloria, which has become obsolete), and then it became possible to measure the heat of any substance.

Around the same time, Black made another important discovery, which showed especially clearly that there is a difference between the amount of heat and temperature. The fact is that if a thermometer measures the concentration of heat (tellorade), then the supply of heat to the body will cause an increase in body temperature, and the removal of heat will cause it to decrease. Meanwhile, Black discovered that when a solid (ice) melts, the temperature remains unchanged, despite the supply of heat to it. The temperature of the water does not change when it hardens, although heat is removed from the water. Black called this heat, which is “not noticed” by the thermometer, the latent heat of fusion or solidification (teler simply calls it the heat of fusion). He also established the existence of latent heat of vaporization.

What settled the dispute about the nature of heat? Which of the two hypotheses ate? Work related to heat capacity, and other thermal studies of the 18th century, could not resolve the dispute. Experiments were needed to show that body temperature can increase without the supply of heat, due to mechanical work. It was also necessary to obtain more detailed information about atoms and molecules (“insensitive particles”), which were “suspected” of being present in heat. All this was done in the 19th century. As a result, it was found that the thermorad theory has nothing to do with reality and that heat is indeed associated with the movement of a particle of the internal body, but not in the way it was previously imagined. What the internal heat of bodies is “contained” is not heat, but internal energy (that is, the kinetic energy of the translational random motion of particles, which determines the temperature of the body, the kinetic energy of the rotational and vibrational motion of particles, as well as the potential energy of their interaction). Tellota is the energy of random motion of a particle of a body, transferred (for example, upon contact) to another body or bodies. Equation (2), in particular, does not express, as previously thought, the law of conservation of heat quantities (there is no such law!). This is simply a special case of the law of conservation of energy for thermal processes (that is, the first law of thermodynamics) $Q = \Delta U + A$. They are valid when the system itself or external forces on the system do not perform mechanical work ($A = 0$), and the change in the internal energy of the system $\Delta U$ is directly related to the heat transfer process.
