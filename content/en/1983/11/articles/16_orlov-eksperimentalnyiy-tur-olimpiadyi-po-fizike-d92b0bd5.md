Experimental tour of the Olympiad in Physics

Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences
V. A. ORLOV

The implementation of experimental tasks of the final round of the XVII All-round Physics Olympiad for schoolchildren took place in three universities in Vilnius: eight classics worked at the Pedagogical Institute, nine classics worked at Vilnius University. We present the conditions of all experimental tasks and options for their solution proposed by Olympiad students.

8th grade
Task 1
1) Investigate the dependence of the speed of movement of a body in a tube with water on the angle of inclination of the tube and plot this dependence.
2) Estimate the coefficient of friction between the body and the wall of the tube with water.
3) Assuming that the resistance force when moving a body in a liquid is proportional

speed: $F_c = \alpha v$, determine the proportionality coefficient $\alpha$.

Equipment: glass tube with water and a body (volume and density of the body are given), ruler, tripod, stopwatch, graph paper, mathematical tables.

Experiments show that the body begins to move only after the tube reaches a certain minimum inclination $\beta_0$. Let us apply to this limiting case Newton’s second law, written in projections onto the coordinate axes (Fig. 1):

$$F_A \sin \beta_0 - F_{\tau p} - mg \sin \beta_0 = 0,$$
$$F_A \cos \beta_0 - mg \cos \beta_0 - N = 0.$$

Taking into account that $F_{\tau p} = \mu N$, we get

$$(F_A - mg) \sin \beta_0 = \mu (F_A - mg) \cos \beta_0,$$

from where we find the coefficient of friction between the body and the wall of the tube with water:

$$\mu = tg \beta_0.$$

When the angle of inclination of the tube $\beta$ is greater than $\beta_0$, the body begins to move at uniform speed. But since a resistance force appears, proportional to the speed, the body quickly, as follows from experience, reaches a constant speed. It is easy to determine by the formula $v = l/t$, where $l$ is the length of the path traveled by the body at a steady speed, $t$ is the time of movement. By measuring the speed of the body in this way at different angles of inclination $\beta$, it is possible to construct an experimental graph of the dependence $v = v(\beta)$. In figure 2 this graph is shown in blue.

Students who compared the experimental curve with the theoretical one received extra points. Let us write down Newton’s second law for the case of body motion at an arbitrary angle $\beta$ and at an angle $\beta = \pi/2$:

$$(F_A - mg) \sin \beta - \mu (F_A - mg) \cos \beta - \alpha v = 0,$$
$$F_A - mg - \alpha v_{\pi/2} = 0.$$

From here

$$v = v_{\pi/2} (\sin \beta - \mu \cos \beta) = v_{\pi/2} \frac{\sin(\beta - \beta_0)}{\cos \beta_0}.$$

Graphically, this dependence is shown on figure 2 black line.

The coefficient $\alpha$ is easy to determine for the case of a body in the vertical direction:

$$\alpha = \frac{V(\rho_0 - \rho) g}{v_{\pi/2}}.$$

where $V$ is the volume of the body, $\rho$ and $\alpha$ are the densities of water and the corresponding body.

Due to the asymmetry of the body, and therefore different conditions of flow around it, when the tube is turned over, the coefficient $\alpha$ may become different. Those who studied this and found two meanings of $\alpha$ received extra points. True, as experience has shown, the difference in values ​​was within the measurement error.

Task 2
1) Determine the spring stiffness and the friction coefficient of the glass on the glass.
2) Using a vertical “myakkoo shock absorber” device, determine the energy loss of the ball during the first collision of the ball and the spring.

3) For the case of a ball falling from the top point of the tube onto a spring, draw a graph of the projection of the ball’s acceleration onto the vertical axis as a function of the distance from the top point.

Equipment: stabilizer spring, glass tube, stabilizer ball with a known mass, ruler, graph paper.

Determining the spring stiffness was not difficult: $k = mg/\Delta l$, where $\Delta l$ is the deformation of the spring when the ball is placed on it (for stability, the spring is placed in a vertical glass tube). To determine the sliding friction coefficient of a stable ball on glass using the formula $\mu = tg \beta_0$ (where $\beta_0$ is the minimum angle of inclination at which sliding begins), it was necessary to force the ball to slide along the glass. For this purpose, it was possible, for example, to clamp a ball between the coils of a spring. Some people took advantage of the fact that the spring is stable and determined (in the same way) the sliding friction coefficient of the spring on the glass.

Most of the puppies determined the loss of energy of the ball from the expression $\Delta E = mg(h_2 - h_1)$, where $h_2$ and $h_1$ are the final and initial heights of the ball above the table (that is, after and before the first collision of the ball with the spring). Some defined energini by the maximum slope of the spring.

When plotting the dependence of the projection $a_x$ of the acceleration of the ball on the vertical axis from the distance $x$ of the ball to the top point of the tube (Fig. 3), the main drawback was a purely theoretical solution to the problem:

$$a_x = g - \frac{k}{m} (x - x_0) = g - \frac{k}{m} \Delta x = g \left(1 - \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta l}\right).$$

In fact, all the points should have been plotted on the graph after changing the corresponding values, taking into account the adjusted scale.

Unfortunately, most of the puppies were limited to one cycle of the ball falling onto the spring, while the ball, after interacting with the spring, bounced off it and rose to a new height, fell again, dropping the spring, rose again, and so on until it stopped completely. It was necessary to show all these points on the graph.

Evaluating the assignments for grade 8 as a whole, it should be noted that they are quite interesting, but the amount of work was large. No one completed all the tasks completely. The best result (2.1 points out of 3) was shown by D. Kalavinskaya and G. Mechetsos (Vilnius). Typical shortcomings were: performing only one measurement, sparse reporting, lack of tables with experimental data, and incorrect determination (and sometimes simply absence) of measurement errors.

9th grade

Task 1

Determine the specific heat capacity of the unknown liquid.

Equipment: heat-lined vessel (thermostat), capillary with a bubble at the end, resistor, vessels with water and non-stellar liquid, changing cylinder, plate with terminals, graph paper, battery of galvanic cells, capacitor, wires, plates.

When completing this assignment, students encountered a number of problems:

a) How to change the temperature without a thermometer?

b) How to calculate the energy of an electric current without a stopwatch?

b) How to eliminate the unknown heat capacity of the thermostat?

d) How to evaluate the heat transfer of a vessel with liquid into the surrounding space?

Only by answering these questions correctly could one choose the optimal method for performing the work.

a) To measure temperature, you need to make a gas thermometer. For this purpose, a capillary with a bubble at the end is heated by hand, and the end of the capillary is lowered into the water so that the capillary water enters it. The volume of air in a bubble under a column of water is proportional to the absolute temperature of the medium in which the capillary is located:

$$\frac{V}{V_0} = \frac{T}{T_0} \Rightarrow \Delta T = \frac{\Delta V}{V_0} T_0.$$

The ambient current temperature formula $W = U^2 t/R$ cannot be used, since the resistance value is not known.

To simplify, we can select values ​​of $n_1$ and $n_2$ such that temperature changes $\Delta T_1$ and $\Delta T_2$ are the same in general experiments ($\Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2 = \Delta T$). You can also pour equal volumes of liquid into the thermostat ($V_B = V_x = V_\text{ж}$). Then, excluding the heat of the thermostat, for the specific heat of the liquid we obtain

$$c_x = c_B \frac{Q_B}{Q_x} - \frac{CU^2/2(n_1 - n_2)}{V_\text{ж} \Delta T_\text{ж}}.$$

However (due to the fact that it is not so easy to achieve the same temperature increase), it is more expedient to set the same energy of the electric current, that is, the same number of switchings $n$. In this case

$$c_x = c_B \frac{Q_B}{Q_x} + \frac{nCU^2/2}{Q_x V_\text{ж} \Delta T_2} - \frac{nCU^2/2}{Q_B V_\text{ж} \Delta T_1}.$$

Both methods were considered correct if they gave a correct estimate of the specific heat of the liquid (alcohol).

d) To estimate the optimal number of switchings ($n=1$ - clearly few, $n>100$ - obviously many), it was useful to estimate the time of noticeable cooling of the liquid in the thermostat, that is, the heat sensor in the surrounding space. In addition, it is important to estimate the discharge time of the capacitor (meaning its capacitance and the resistance of the resistor) in order to determine the required switching frequency of the capacitor.

When performing this task, G. Baranauskaya (Shlya), A. Korol (Novokuznetsk) and S. Orlov (Moscow) showed better results.

Characteristics of the shortcomings: neglect of the thermal capacity of the thermostat, single discharge of the capacitor, as well as insufficient movement of liquid into the vessel.

10th grade
Task 1
Determine the emf and internal resistance of the current source.
Equipment: the current source under study, a current source with a known emf and a known internal resistance, a spiral of nichrome wire, a more sensitive non-radiating device for measuring current, connecting wires.
The first part of this assignment is no different from the second assignment for grade 9.
To measure the unknown internal resistance of a current source $r_x$, a galvanometer with a shunt ($R_1$) and an additional resistance ($R_2$) should be connected first to the source under study, then to the known one:
$$I_3 = \frac{\mathcal{E}_x}{r_x + \frac{R_r R_1}{R_r + R_1} + R_2}, \quad I_4 = \frac{\mathcal{E}_x}{r + \frac{R_r R_1}{R_r + R_1} + R_2}.$$
From here, reasonably neglecting the resistance $R_r R_1/(R_r + R_1)$ (since it is much less than $R_2$), the desired value $r_x$ is found.
A method for simultaneously determining $r_x$ and $\mathcal{E}_x$ was proposed by A. Gnilovskaya (Leningrad). Connecting a galvanometer to a known source with a shunt, first of additional resistance, and then with additional resistance $R$, such that the readings of the galvanometer in the second case are 2 times less than in the first: $I_2 = I_1/2$, we obtain that $R = r$ (since $R_r \ll R$). Now, knowing the value of resistance $R$, connect with a shunt and with additional resistance $R$, as well as without it to an unknown source:
$$I'_2 = \frac{\mathcal{E}_x}{r_x + R}, \quad I'_1 = \frac{\mathcal{E}_x}{r_x},$$
where
$$r_x = \frac{I_2}{I_1 - I_2} R, \quad \mathcal{E}_x = I_1 r_x.$$

To determine $\mathcal{E}_x$ it is necessary to know the abscilliton value of the current $I_1$. For this purpose, you need to irradiate the galvanometer, knowing that when the device is fully deflected without additional resistance connected to a known source, the current strength is equal to $I = \mathcal{E}_x/r$.
