Electrical vibrations.

Oscillatory circuit

In 1853, the famous English physicist William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, published an article entitled “On Unsteady Electric Currents.”
In it, he showed that in a circuit composed of a capacitor with a capacitance of $C$ and a coil with an inductance of $L$ (Fig. 1), electromagnetic oscillations with a period of $T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}$ should occur.

When we hear the word “oscillations,” we immediately imagine the image of an oscillating mathematical matrix (Fig. 2) or a weight attached to a circle (Fig. 3). But vibrations represent not only the mechanical movement of a body “here and there.”
By oscillations one must understand any periodic change in any quantity, that is, a change in which the value of this quantity is repeated after a certain period of time, called the period of oscillation.

During mechanical oscillations of a material point periodically, the changing quantity is the coordinate $x$ of the point. In our circuit, this is the charge $q$, which was imparted to the capacitor. During the oscillation period, the charge on the plate gradually disappears, changes its sign and is restored again. Both the voltage $U = q/C$ between the capacitor plates and the current $I$ in the circuit oscillate, since the correspondence is valid at any time.

$$U = L \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$$

The voltage $E$ of the electric field in the capacitor (because $E = U/d$, where $d$ is the distance between the plates) and the induction $B$ of the magnetic field of the coil (because it is proportional to the current) also fluctuate.

This is similar to how during mechanical vibrations, not only the coordinate of a point changes periodically, but also its speed, acceleration, kinetic energy, etc. All these quantities undergo oscillations.

The school textbook "Physics 10" talks about the analogy between mechanical and electrical vibrations. This analogy can be made more strictly if we notice that equation (1) coincides in form with the equation expressing Newton’s second law for a body oscillating under the influence of others:

$$F = m \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t},$$

where $F$ is the force of the equations acting on the body, $m$ is the mass, $\Delta v/\Delta t = a$ is the acceleration of the body. Therefore, equation (1) can be studied according to the principle “identical equations have identical solutions.”

A comparison of equations (1) and (2) shows that the voltage $U$ corresponds to the force equations $F$, the current $I$ in the coil corresponds to the speed $v$ of the body's movement, and the inductance of the coil $L$ corresponds to the mass of the body $m$. Finally, from the correspondence

$$U = \frac{q}{C} \text{ и } F = k\lambda$$

it follows that the reciprocal of the capacitance of the capacitor ($1/C$) corresponds to the spring stiffness $k$. A summary table summarizing this analogy is provided in Physics 10.
