How crystals are arranged

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
R. V. GALIULIN

Since ancient times, crystals have amazed the human imagination with their exceptional geometric perfection. Our ancestors saw in them the creations of angels or underground spirits. The first attempt to scientifically understand the shape of crystals is considered to be the work of Johannes Kepler “On Hexagonal Snowflakes” (1611). Kepler proposed that the shape of snowflakes (ice crystals) is a consequence of the special arrangements of their constituent particles. Three centuries later, it was finally established that the specific features of crystals are associated with special arrangements of atoms in space, which are similar to the patterns in kaledoscopes. All the various laws of such arrangements were derived in 1891 by our remarkable correspondence, the founder of modern crystallography, E. S. Fedorov (1853-1919). The regular forms of crystalline polyhedra can easily be determined within the framework of these laws. And these laws themselves are so beautiful that they did not serve as the basis for creating works of art (see screensaver)*.

From a geometric point of view, the location of atoms in space is represented by a system of points corresponding to their centers. Therefore, the problem can be formulated as follows: it is required to find geometric conditions that distinguish systems of points with a “crystalline structure”, and these conditions must be physically justified. There is a lot of evidence, so we quickly want to identify the reasons for the ordered arrangement of atoms in crystals.

the centers of atoms in any atomic aggregates (and not just in crystals) is discreteness.

Discreteness condition. The distance between any two points of the system is greater than some fixed value r. The physical value of this condition is beyond doubt.

The desire of atoms to be evenly distributed in space can be reflected by the following restriction on the corresponding system of points:

Coverage condition. The distance from any point in space to the point of the system closest to it is less than a certain fixed value R.

The name of this condition is explained by the fact that if a system of points satisfies it, then balls of radius R with centers at these points cover the entire space (prove it!).

The discreteness condition does not allow the points of the system to be located too densely, and the coverage condition prevents them from being too sparse. Together, these two requirements ensure an approximately uniform distribution of points in space. Systems of points that satisfy these two conditions simultaneously are called Delaunay systems, in memory of our famous geometer B. N. Delaunay (1890-1980), who first identified these systems.

The simplest example of a Delaunay system (on a plane) is the set of nodes of an infinite sheet of checkered paper. In crystallography, systems of this type play a very important role and we will talk about them in detail later. From this system one can obtain a more general Delaunay system if each node is arbitrarily connected to a distance not exceeding, say, 1/3 of the distance between neighboring nodes (Fig. 1).

So, Delaunay systems corresponding to crystals must be symmetric. Such systems can also be described based on the concept of an equal environment. To do this, we connect an arbitrary point A of the Delaunay system with all its other points (Fig. 3). The resulting infinite set of segments is called the eglobal star of point A in a given system. In the general case, global stars at different points in the system are not equal to each other. But it is clear that if the system contains at least two points with equal global stars, the system will already be symmetric. The converse statement is also true: every smetrine Delaunay system contains points with equal global stars. Thus, the equality of the global stars at the two points of the Delaunay system is a necessary and sufficient condition for the smettriness of this system.

But not every symmetric Delaunay system corresponds to the center of atoms in crystal structures. The symmetry of crystals is specific. For example, among crystalline polyhedra there are no regular dodecadrons and icosadras, or in general polyhedra that have 5th order symmetry axes (that is, “self-aligning” when rotated through an angle of 2π/5 about these axes). How to oblige such fastidiousness of crystalline forms?

In 1783, the French abbot R. J. Gayun, a recognized mineralogist, proposed that every crystal is composed of parallel equal particles adjacent along entire faces (Fig. 4). In 1824, a student of the Great Gauss, professor of physics in Franburg, L. A. Seeber, to explain the expansion of crystals when heated, proposed replacing the Gayun polyhedra with their centers of gravity. Such systems of points were called "lattices".

More strictly, a lattice is the set of all points with integer coordinates relative to an arbitrary (optional rectangular) coordinate system (Fig. 5,a-c). Lattice points are called nodes. It is obvious that the coordinate system uniquely determines the lattice. But the converse statement is not true: in a given lattice, the coordinate system that defines it can be chosen in an infinite number of ways (Fig. 5, b). It is easy to check that the lattices satisfy the conditions of discreteness and coverage, that is, they are Delaunay systems. Let us now prove their symmetry. The following is true.

Lemma about the lattice. Any lattice transforms into itself upon parallel translation to a vector connecting any two of its nodes, as well as upon a central symmetric relative to any node.

Let us now prove that a crystal cannot have a 5th order symmetry axis. Let us assume that such a crystal exists. Then the corresponding lattice also has a 5th order axis $l$. Let us draw a plane perpendicular to $l$ through any node and select a node $A$ in this plane, close to $l$ (the existence of such a node is easy to deduce from the discreteness condition). Since the lattice transforms into itself upon rotation through angles divisible by 2π/5 around the $l$ axis, the images of the point $A$ upon rotation are lattice bonds. They form a regular pentagon $ABCDE$ (Fig. 6). If the lattice is shifted by the vector $\overrightarrow{AB}$, then by the lattice lemma the node $E$ must go to some node $N$ lying inside the pentagon. But this is impossible, since point $N$ is located closer to the axis $l$ than $A$.

But not all facts known about crystals fit within the framework of the lattice model. One such fact is the existence of non-centrosymmetric crystalline polyhedra, such as the crystals of the gemstone thermaline (Fig. 7) (by the lattice lemma, all lattices are centrosymmetric). To explain such phenomena, it was necessary to expand the arsenal of permissible arrangements of particles in space. The famous German crystallographer L. Soncke suggested in 1879 that particles in crystals are arranged in regular systems.

A Delaunay system is called *correct* if from each of its points the system is visible equally, that is, if the global stars of all points of this system are equal to each other (Fig. 8,a-c). If the observed person fell asleep at some point of the correct system and at that time he was transferred to another point of this system, he would not even notice it. In other words, any point of the correct system can be transferred to any other transformed system. Groups of systems of regular systems are called *Fedorov or spatial crystallographic groups*. There are 230 different Fedorov groups (there are significantly fewer flat crystallographic groups - only 17; the self-alignment pattern depicted on the splash screen represents one of these groups). They set the laws for the arrangement of atoms in crystal structures, which we mentioned at the beginning of the article.

From the lattice lemma it follows that any lattice is a regular system. The converse is not true, but it can be shown that every regular system is composed of congested and parallel lattices (Fig. 8,c). The proof of this not simple fact was outlined by E. S. Fedorov in his famous book “The Beginnings of the Study of Physics,” work on which he began as a 16-year-old boy. This proof was carried out by A. Schonflies, but it turned out to be so complicated that in the first edition of the book on symmetrine crystal structures in 1891, he placed this proof at the very end, so as not to frighten the reader. B. N. Delaunay, together with his student M. I. Shtogrin, simplified this proof, but did not insist that it could be presented here.

At the beginning of our contribution, it is exemplarily confirmed that atoms in crystal structures form one or more regular systems with a common Fedorov group (Fig. 9). But this statement does not reveal the reasons for ordering, but only states the fact of its existence. The founder of Soviet crystallography, Academician A.V. Shubnikov (1887-1970), spoke about this: “We know well how a crystal is structured, but why it is structured this way, no one is doing this on the server.”

Let's imagine a growing crystal. Here is another atom being included in its structure. What makes this atom take its strictly defined place? In order not to violate the correctness of the system (in the sense of the definition given above), he must “know” and “learn” the positions of all other atoms, including those very far away. It would be quite natural to require that each atom should be equally surrounded by all the atoms removed from it by some comparative arrangement of chemical bonds.
