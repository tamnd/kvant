Probably, many of you in the physics classroom noticed a device that looked like a TV with a small round screen. When it works, various glowing lines “run” across its screen. This device is called an oscillogram. It is used to record on the screen rapidly occurring physical processes invisible to the human eye. Thus, the device expands the possibility of our perception in understanding natural phenomena. For this feature, the oscilloscope is often called the “electronic eye.”

The main thing is the tube

The main element of an oscilloscope is a cathode ray (or simply “electronic”) tube. It is a specially shaped glass cylinder from which almost all the air has been pumped out. Inside the cylinder there is an “electron gun” for producing electron beams and a mechanism for controlling this beam (figure 1). We'll talk about how the tube works later. First, a little history.

The prototype of a modern electron tube was a gas-discharge tube, with the help of which the Englishman W. Crookes studied the processes of passage of electric current through rarefied gases. The tube is a glass container with electrodes molded into it; the cylinder is connected to a pump that pumps air out of it. Crookes discovered that when the electrodes were connected to a voltage source, unknown rays appeared inside the tube.

25 years later, the French physicist J. Perrin proved that these rays are a stream of negatively charged particles, the source of which is the packed surface of the negative electrode - the cathode. Perrin placed in front of

In his experiment, Thomson pioneered the electrical method of controlling the flow of electrons. By placing two parallel plates in front of the cathode, connected to the poles of an electric battery, he saw that the rays entering the electric field between the plates deviated from their original path.

Electron gun

We have already said that the source of electrons in the tube is a negatively charged electrode - the cathode. Any conductor contains a huge number of electrons that are in constant motion. Most electrons move at almost identical speeds in absolute value. But there are electrons whose speed is much greater than the average speed. Such electrons, if they find themselves close to the surface of a conductor, can “escape” through the surface and “evaporate” (this situation is similar to the process of evaporation of a liquid). At room temperature, the fraction of these electrons has no effect. The evaporation of electrons from the surface of a hot conductor is very intense.

The cathode of the electron tube (a thin conductor) is heated red-hot by passing an electric current through it, and electrons evaporate from the surface of the cathode.

You can verify that electrons will evaporate when the conductor is heated using a simple experiment. For the experiment you need an incandescent lamp, a piece of foil and an electric juicer. You can do the electronics yourself. To do this, take a bottle and select a cork for it (preferably rubber; you can make a cork from an eraser). Pass a fairly long star through the cork; Attach tissue paper petals to the sharp end of the star. Close the bottle with the cork so that the petals are inside the bottle. The electric juicer is ready.

Cut a strip of foil and wrap it tightly around the storage lamp bulb. Use a thin wire to connect the foil to the electric juice.

Now charge the electric juicer positively (for example, using a glass rod rubbed against a crack) and turn on the lamp. You will see the electrojuice petals falling off.

The foil connected to the electric juice has a positive charge. Electrons evaporating from the lamp filament are attracted by this charge, settle on the lamp bulb and charge its inner surface negatively. This negative charge neutralizes the positive charge of the foil and, therefore, the electric juice.

How to collect all the electrons “evaporating” from the cathode into a thin beam?

Opposite the cathode, a second electrode is placed - the aode. As a rule, it has the shape of a disk. A large voltage is created between the cathode and the electrode (from several hundred to several thousand volts). The electric field between the electrodes directs electrons emitted from the surface of the cathode to the electrode and accelerates them to a very high speed. And to collect these electrons into a thin beam, they do the following.

A metal “shirt” is placed on the cathode - a cylindrical glass with a small hole in the bottom. She is given a negative charge. This charge removes electrons escaping from the metal surface towards the axis of the “jacket”-glass, so that most of the electrons fly out of the “shirt” through a small hole. By changing the charge on the “shirt”, you can regulate the number of electrons escaping from it. Due to this property, the metal “jacket” is called the control electrode. A narrow beam of electrons emitted from the control electrode is directed to the electrode and passes through a narrow hole in it.
