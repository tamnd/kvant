Great Legacy

Euler's scientific legacy is striking in its completely unchallenged dimensions. During his lifetime, his 530 books and articles were published. The last years of his life, academic publications could not cope with the flow of scientific production of the blind scientist, and he playfully promised Count V.N. Orlov that his works would fill the “Commentaries” of the Academy for 20 years after his death. This assessment turned out to be “optimistic”: the Academy had been publishing Euler’s works for 47 years. The number of works reached 771, but the bibliography compiled by Enster in 1910 contained 886 titles, divided into headings: philosophy, mathematics, mechanics, astronomy, physics, geography, agriculture. Since 1910, the Swiss Society of Natural Sciences has been publishing a collection of Euler's works, distributed by international subscription: according to preliminary estimates, it will amount to 75 large volumes. Paradoxically, it can be said without much exaggeration that Euler worked almost exclusively in mathematics throughout his life. In other areas of science (for example, mechanics or astronomy), it was prematurely associated with the use of mathematical methods. His philosophical position, against the backdrop of his entire life, was that natural discoveries should be obtained through theoretical (largely mathematical) processing of a small number of general, undoubted principles. In his Swiss dissertation, nineteen-year-old Euler writes: “I did not consider it necessary to confirm this new theory with experiment, because it is completely derived from the most reliable and irrefutable principles of mechanics and, thus, doubt as to whether it is correct and whether it has a place in practice simply cannot arise.” Next, Euler tries to derive Newton's laws from more general principles, and in celestial mechanics he strives not to obtain empirical formulas from processing observational results, but to draw conclusions directly from the law of universal gravitation. As a rule, he moved from theory to practice. Although Euler was associated with experiment all his life, this was not his strong side. Academician S.I. Vavilov wrote: “... Euler’s genius was essentially a mathematical one... he had a poor understanding of experiment (although he experimented himself) ...”; elsewhere: “The mathematical genius Euler lacked the physical intuition of Newton and Huygen, which allowed him to guess the solution in the absence of an exact mathematical formulation of the problem or methods for solving it.”

Apprentice Farm

Euler's first publications on arithmetic (number theory) date back to 1732, the fifth year of his stay in St. Petersburg. Euler had two great ideas in arithmetic: the Greek Diophantus (3rd century) and the Frenchman P. Fermat (1601-1665). Euler begins by proposing and providing proof of Fermat's assertions. His considerations are so systematic that it seems that these are preparations for the first textbook of higher arithmetic (which was not written by him).

A 1732 paper examines the question of the divisibility of “Fermat numbers” (that is, numbers of the form $F_n = 2^{2n} + 1$, where $n \in \mathbb{N}$) and “Mersenne numbers” (that is, numbers of the form $M_p = 2^p - 1$, where $p$ is a prime). Numerous questions relate to such numbers that direct enumeration was inaccessible even to such a virtuoso computationalist as Euler.

Euler proved that the divisors of the numbers $F_n$ and $M_p$ can only have weights. Special view. Namely, the divisors of the number $F_n$ have the form $k \cdot 2^{n+2} + 1$; Thanks to this, Euler discovered that $F_5$ is divisible by $641 = 5 \cdot 2^7 + 1$, disproving Fermat's claim that all numbers $F_n$ are prime. The divisors of the number $M_p$ must be both $2pk - 1$ and $8l \pm 1$. Helped by this, Euler proved the primeness of the number $M_{32} = 2147483647$. Since then, no Fermat primes have been discovered, and records in the world of Mersenne primes are constantly being improved (the 1983 record is obtained at $p = 86243$). Regarding the Mersenne numbers, Euler also filled the gap left by Euclid. Euclid knew that if $M_p$ is a prime number, then $M_p \times (M_p + 1)/2$ is a perfect number (that is, a number equal to the sum of its own divisors). Euler proved that every even perfect number can be represented in this form (it is still not known whether odd perfect numbers exist).

Gradually, convenient numbers are becoming less and less common. In the first batch there were 62 of them, but Euler stubbornly continued his calculations, confidently hoping to notice a pattern. He found only three more convenient numbers: 1320, 1365, 1848, although, without losing patience, he went through all the numbers up to 10,000 and a little further. Euler had every reason to hypothesize that the set of convenient numbers was limited to the 65 numbers he found. Gauss made Euler's considerations more correct, but did not find new convenient numbers. The finiteness of the set of convenient numbers has now been proven, but it is not known whether there are convenient numbers greater than 1848.

This search is very characteristic of Euler's creative method, which continued enormous experimental computational work both to test hypotheses and to increase new patterns. Of the great mathematicians, please, only Gauss also mastered this inductive method.

Analytic number theory

Number theory owes Euler an idea that soon completely changed its face. We are talking about the use of mathematical analysis in arithmetic. It was difficult to imagine such a possibility. At first, she surprised Euler himself: “And although we are here considering the nature of integers, to which the Beskono-Malch Calculus seems inapplicable, nevertheless, I came to my conclusion with the help of differentiation and other tricks.”

Additive number theory

The problem of number representation of natural numbers in the form of sums of terms of a certain nature (as Euler said, the question of the “partition of numbers”) should be at the center of his attention. Perhaps the initial inspiration for the task is contained in a letter to Node, whose surname means nothing to our contemporary. Euler applied the apparatus of infinite products to these problems. Here are some examples. Euler shows that

$$\begin{align*}
(1+x)(1+x^2)(1+x^3) &\dots = \frac{1}{(1-x)(1-x^3)(1-x^5)}.
\end{align*}$$

The reasoning is that if you multiply the left side of the sequence by 1−x, 1−x^3, 1−x^5, ..., then all non-zero powers will gradually disappear, and this means identity (this reasoning can be made rigorous using the theory of limits). After opening the brackets on the left side, we get the series 1+a₁x+a₂x²+a₃x³+..., where aₖ is the number representing k as a sum of different natural terms. The right side, using the sum of an infinite geometric progression, is written in the form

$$\begin{align*}
(1+x+x^2+x^3+...) (1+x^3+x^6+x^9+...) \times (1+x^5+x^{10}+x^{15}+...) \cdot ...
\end{align*}$$

from which we again obtain the series 1+b₁x+b₂x²+b₃x³+..., where bₖ is the number representing k as a sum of odd terms, among which there may be identical ones (why?). Euler concludes that the representation number a_k=b_k coincides. Try to prove this coincidence in a non-representational way and you will see that it is not clear how it fits into this problem.

The following reasoning comes from the point

$$\begin{align*}
(1+x)(1+x^2)(1+x^4)(1+x^8) &\dots = \frac{1}{1+x+x^2+x^3+...} \\
= 1+x+x^2+x^3+...
\end{align*}$$

(to verify its plausibility, you can multiply both sides by 1 − x and observe how the non-Lu powers of x consistently disappear in both sides). It immediately follows from it that each number is represented in one and only one way as a sum of different powers of two (the numbers of such representations are coefficients in the power series obtained after transforming the left product).

Briefly about other works

In mathematics, Euler was primarily an analyzer. A significant part of his life was devoted to the creation of a systematic (semitic) course in mathematical analysis. Euler was the first to work with the general concept of a function defined by an arbitrary curve (graph), and he connects the general concept of a function with the problem of the number of strings. Euler did a lot in considering special classes of functions important for applications. Here is the famous Γ-function, which takes the values ​​n! at point n of the natural series, its twin the B-function (Eulerian integrals), cylindrical and elliptic functions. Euler systematically carried out analytical considerations in a complex of one area, discovering a substitutional connection between the complex exponential function and trigonometric functions. His studies in geometry were more sporadic. They mainly relate to problems related to cartography, or to the application of analysis in geometry (beginning with differential geometry). But we can also recall the meaning of Euler’s formula for polyhedra: B+Γ=P+2, where B is the number of vertices, Γ is the number of faces, P is the number of edges, as well as the problem of the seven Könggerg places, in which Euler correctly sensed the problems of the new geometry (topologists).
