So, a soliton is a solitary wave propagating in a nonlinear dispersive medium with negligible friction, completely retaining its shape.

The fact that the properties of a solitary wave largely depends on the properties of the medium in which the wave propagates was established theoretically at the end of the last century of mathematics by D. J. Kortever and G. de Vries. They showed that among the solutions to the equation of wave motion in a straight channel with water there are those that describe nonlinear dispersive solitary waves, the shape of which is maintained indefinitely during the propagation process in the absence of friction. These are solitons.

It should be emphasized that the soliton wave is only one of quite a few solutions to the Kortever-de Vries wave theory. Among other solutions, we encounter other, including periodic, types of waves propagating along the water surface in different directions and at different speeds. This circumstance is completely consistent with the results of experimental observations. For example, when a ship moves at speeds different from the critical value, periodic wave structures are formed, the crests of which are directed almost parallel or perpendicular to the side of the ship (usually they are called “oblique” and “transverse” waves, respectively).

Generally speaking, solitary wave solutions exist for many systems. However, the solitary waves that Kortever and de Vries studied have some distinctive properties. These differences are especially evident when two such waves interact. Let's imagine two solitary waves with different amplitudes, moving in the same direction at different speeds (figure 2, a).
