You can see the effect of a magnetic field on the flow of electrons by holding a magnet close to the side of your TV. The image on the screen will shift up or down depending on which strip you hold the magnet towards.

In 1897, the German physicist K. Braun created a magnetically controlled ray tube in the shape

Fig. 2.

similar to a modern electron tube.

In the same year, the English physicist J. J. Thomson, using this tube, showed that the rays emanating from the cathode are a stream of electrons. In his experiment, Thomson pioneered the electrical method of controlling the flow of electrons. By placing two parallel plates in front of the cathode, connected to the poles of an electric battery, he saw that the rays entering the electric field between the plates deviated from their original path.

You can see how the electric field acts on charged particles by continuing this experiment. Take two aluminum plates or pot lids with insulated arms and strengthen them parallel to each other.

Make a round ball (or sleeve) from the file and attach a thread to it. Then take an ebonite stick and rub it with a piece of silk; in this case the stick becomes negatively electrified. First, with a light touch of the stick, charge the ball, and then one of the plates. Then rub the sticks with a piece of paper. In this case, it is positively electrified. Charge the sticks with the second plate. Now everything is ready for the experiment. Lower the ball on the string between the plates. You will see that the ball is deflected towards the striping charged plate.
