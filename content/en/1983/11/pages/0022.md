He shows that this property can sometimes be used to prove the primality of $n$: for example, the number 1,000,009 is composite because along with the representation $1000^2+3^2$ there is a representation $235^2+972^2$.

Euler is concerned with the simplicity of numbers that admit a unique representation in the form $x^2+2y^2, x^2+3y^2$ and, in general, $x^2+Dy^2$ (D is a fixed integer). He proves their simplicity for $D \leq 10$, but for $D=11$ he discovers a composite number that admits a unique such representation. The situation intrigued Euler. It was called $D$ convenient if only prime numbers can be represented in the form $x^2+Dy^2$ in a unique way. Euler received a criterion for checking the convenience of numbers, and with curiosity he wrote down the convenient numbers one after another; after 10 come 12, 13, 15, 16, 18, 21, 22, 24...
Gradually, convenient numbers are becoming less and less common. In the first batch there were 62 of them, but Euler stubbornly continued his calculations, confidently hoping to notice a pattern. He found only three more convenient numbers: 1320, 1365, 1848, although, without losing patience, he went through all the numbers up to 10,000 and a little further. Euler had every reason to hypothesize that the set of convenient numbers was limited to the 65 numbers he found. Gauss made Euler's considerations more correct, but did not find new convenient numbers. The finiteness of the set of convenient numbers has now been proven, but it is not known whether there are convenient numbers greater than 1848.

This search is very characteristic of Euler's creative method, which continued enormous experimental computational work both to test hypotheses and to increase new patterns. Of the great mathematicians, please, only Gauss also mastered this inductive method.
