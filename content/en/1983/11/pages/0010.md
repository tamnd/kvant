Let's talk about another practical use of the properties of solitons. A solitary wave, which does not change its shape when moving, is an ideal information carrier that can be irreplaceable in the creation of logical devices, memory elements, and in the transmission and processing of information signals. To create logical devices for processing and filling information, they are now beginning to use magnetic solitons in complex superconducting structures. It turns out that solitons in the form of “clumps” of magnetic flux can move along an thick structure formed by superconducting electrodes and an insulating layer with a thickness of 20-40 angstroms (1 angstrom = 1Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m). The bunch can be moved along the superconductor-insulator-superconductor contact (along the X axis at figure 5), passing a current through the thick structure. Such a lump is localized in space (figure 6) and carries a small but strictly defined portion of the magnetic flux Φ₀, equal to h/2e = 2.07 × 10⁻¹⁵ Wb (h is Planck’s constant, e is the electrode charge). The quantity Φ₀ is called magnetic flux. The untrained nature of the superconductor-insulator-superconductor structure is that with a small thickness of the insulating layer, due to the quantum nature of superconducting electrons, it is possible for them to leak through the insulator layer without losses, that is, without disrupting the superconductivity of the system. The appearance and movement of a magnetic flux bunch under the action of an external magnetic field is accompanied by a sharp decrease in Iₖ and a change in the energy of the system as a whole. Therefore, the quantum flux Φ₀ inherent in a single information: for example, in its absence, the energy of the system can be assigned the number “0”, and in its presence - the number “1”. The question, however, is how to maintain the magnetic structure. It turns out that this can be done if a structural defect is artificially created in the area of ​​the insulating layer, for example, a straight metal channel between the superconducting plates, having transverse dimensions of the order of several tens of angstroms. Interacting with such a channel, the bunch will “catch” on it and remain in the thick structure after the magnetic field is turned off. Such an element can serve as a cell for processing and filling information with two stable states: one in the presence of a bunch with a single quantum of magnetic flux Φ₀, the other in its absence. A large number of appropriately connected elements makes it possible to create systems with a large memory and a variety of operations for processing incoming information.
