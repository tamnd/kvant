Paradoxically, it can be said without much exaggeration that Euler worked almost exclusively in mathematics throughout his life. In other areas of science (for example, mechanics or astronomy), it was prematurely associated with the use of mathematical methods. His philosophical position, against the backdrop of his entire life, was that natural discoveries should be obtained through theoretical (largely mathematical) processing of a small number of general, undoubted principles. In his Swiss dissertation, nineteen-year-old Euler writes: “I did not consider it necessary to confirm this new theory with experiment, because it is completely derived from the most reliable and irrefutable principles of mechanics and, thus, doubt as to whether it is correct and whether it has a place in practice simply cannot arise.” Next, Euler tries to derive Newton's laws from more general principles, and in celestial mechanics he strives not to obtain empirical formulas from processing observational results, but to draw conclusions directly from the law of universal gravitation. As a rule, he moved from theory to practice. Although Euler was associated with experiment all his life, this was not his strong side. Academician S.I. Vavilov wrote: “... Euler’s genius was essentially a mathematical one... he had a poor understanding of experiment (although he experimented himself) ...”; elsewhere: “The mathematical genius Euler lacked the physical intuition of Newton and Huygen, which allowed him to guess the solution in the absence of an exact mathematical formulation of the problem or methods for solving it.”

Euler's first publications on arithmetic (number theory) date back to 1732, the fifth year of his stay in St. Petersburg. Euler had two great ideas in arithmetic: the Greek Diophantus (3rd century) and the Frenchman P. Fermat (1601-1665). Euler begins by proposing and providing proof of Fermat's assertions. His considerations are so systematic that it seems that these are preparations for the first textbook of higher arithmetic (which was not written by him).

A 1732 paper examines the question of the divisibility of “Fermat numbers” (that is, numbers of the form $F_n = 2^{2n} + 1$, where $n \in \mathbb{N}$) and “Mersenne numbers” (that is, numbers of the form $M_p = 2^p - 1$, where $p$ is a prime). Numerous questions relate to such numbers that direct enumeration was inaccessible even to such a virtuoso computationalist as Euler.

Euler proved that the divisors of the numbers $F_n$ and $M_p$ can only have weights. Special view. Namely, the divisors of the number $F_n$ have the form $k \cdot 2^{n+2} + 1$; Thanks to this, Euler discovered that $F_5$ is divisible by $641 = 5 \cdot 2^7 + 1$, disproving Fermat's claim that all numbers $F_n$ are prime. The divisors of the number $M_p$ must be both $2pk - 1$ and $8l \pm 1$. Helped by this, Euler proved the primeness of the number $M_{32} = 2147483647$. Since then, no Fermat primes have been discovered, and records in the world of Mersenne primes are constantly being improved (the 1983 record is obtained at $p = 86243$). Regarding the Mersenne numbers, Euler also filled the gap left by Euclid. Euclid knew that if $M_p$ is a prime number, then $M_p \times (M_p + 1)/2$ is a perfect number (that is, a number equal to the sum of its own divisors). Euler proved that every even perfect number can be represented in this form (it is still not known whether odd perfect numbers exist).
