In particular, from experiments it turned out that the heat of water is 20 times greater than the heat of mercury (according to modern data - more than 30 times). In general, the specific heat of water is the most average of liquid and solid substances.

Some time later, it was proposed to take the specific heat of water as a unit (on this basis, the unit of heat was introduced - caloria, which has become obsolete), and then it became possible to measure the heat of any substance.

Around the same time, Black made another important discovery, which showed especially clearly that there is a difference between the amount of heat and temperature. The fact is that if a thermometer measures the concentration of heat (tellorade), then the supply of heat to the body will cause an increase in body temperature, and the removal of heat will cause it to decrease. Meanwhile, Black discovered that when a solid (ice) melts, the temperature remains unchanged, despite the supply of heat to it. The temperature of the water does not change when it hardens, although heat is removed from the water. Black called this heat, which is “not noticed” by the thermometer, the latent heat of fusion or solidification (teler simply calls it the heat of fusion). He also established the existence of latent heat of vaporization.

Black's discoveries were undoubtedly the most important in the 18th century in the field of thermal physics. Their fate is interesting and unknown. Avoiding popularism, Black did not publish articles about his work, but reported on them only in lectures and reports. What settled the dispute about the nature of heat? Which of the two hypotheses ate? Work related to heat capacity, and other thermal studies of the 18th century, could not resolve the dispute. Experiments were needed to show that body temperature can increase without the supply of heat, due to mechanical work. It was also necessary to obtain more detailed information about atoms and molecules (“insensitive particles”), which were “suspected” of being present in heat. All this was done in the 19th century. As a result, it was found that the thermorad theory has nothing to do with reality and that heat is indeed associated with the movement of a particle of the internal body, but not in the way it was previously imagined. What the internal heat of bodies is “contained” is not heat, but internal energy (that is, the kinetic energy of the translational random motion of particles, which determines the temperature of the body, the kinetic energy of the rotational and vibrational motion of particles, as well as the potential energy of their interaction). Tellota is the energy of random motion of a particle of a body, transferred (for example, upon contact) to another body or bodies. Equation (2), in particular, does not express, as previously thought, the law of conservation of heat quantities (there is no such law!). This is simply a special case of the law of conservation of energy for thermal processes (that is, the first law of thermodynamics) $Q = \Delta U + A$. They are valid when the system itself or external forces on the system do not perform mechanical work ($A = 0$), and the change in the internal energy of the system $\Delta U$ is directly related to the heat transfer process.
