It turns out that it is precisely under these conditions that an electric domain arises in the sample. Let's figure out how this happens.

We will assume that the electric field in the sample is uniform. Let us assume that due to fluctuation*) in some place of the sample the density of electric charges at some moment turns out to be greater than the average charge density in the rest of the volume. The charged electric field in this place also returns. The drift speed of the electrons located in this region of the strong field becomes less than the drift speed of the electrons behind and ahead of the fluctuation (we must remember that the electrons and, therefore, the fluctuation moves from the κατάka to the anode of the sample). Thus, the electrons located along a given field will move more and more slowly, an ever larger number of electrons will catch up with it, and an ever larger number of electrons will begin to return the electric field to the fluctuation region. (At this stage of the formation of the electric domain, effective dispersion is clearly manifested: different regions of the fluctuation move at different speeds.) This process will continue until the electric field ahead and behind the fluctuation decreases to such an extent that the drift speed of the electrons in these regions begins to decrease. (It is clear that the decrease in speed with a decrease in the field begins from the moment when the direction of the field outside the fluctuation becomes less than $E_{пор}$. From this moment on, the effective dispersion)

*) Fluctuations are random deviations of any physical quantity characterizing a system of a large number of particles from its average value. The fluctuation of charge density, which we will talk about, can arise due to the thermal movement of electrons.

When the velocities of the electrons in the strong field region and outside it become equal, the electric domain can be considered formed. In the future, it will move along the sample at a constant speed.

Thus, in the example with an effective Gunn, all the properties inherent in a soliton are manifested: the presence of a stable formation - a solitary wave (electric domain) - which appears in a sample with dispersion and strong non-captivity and, while maintaining its shape unchanged, moves at a constant speed (figure 4).

It is easy to see that such formation and movement of domains is accompanied by electric current vibrations. When an electric field $E \geq E_{пор}$ is applied to the sample, the value κατάστα appears as a domain with a high field direction; Consequently, in the rest of the sample the field becomes smaller and the electric current decreases. When the domain near the anode is destroyed, the field in the volume of the sample returns, therefore, the drift velocity of the electrons and the electric current decreases. However, returning the field to the value $E_{пор}$ will lead to the formation of a domain near the κατάσταс, the process of its movement from the κατά to the anode will be repeated. This periodic repetition causes an electric current to oscillate in the sample, the period of the oscillation being directly proportional to the length of the sample $d$.

The Gunn effect is now actively used for practical purposes - on its basis, industrial samples of small-sized semiconductor generators of ultrahigh-frequency electromagnetic flasks have been developed and produced. Such generators are widely used in radio communication systems, signal reception and processing, and radar.
