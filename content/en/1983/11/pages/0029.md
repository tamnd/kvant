The second theory (hypothesis), first proposed at the beginning of the 17th century by the English scientist Francis Bacon, argued that heat is the movement of small particles inside a body (molecules, atoms, or, as they said then, insensitive particles). This hypothesis is also based on experienced observers showing, for example, that heating can be caused by movement. This theory had many supporters, and even very important ones, such as Descartes, Boyle, Hooke, Lomonsov.

Both theories, for all their differences, also had something in common. Both agree that heat is something contained in the body. According to the first hypothesis, the body contains caloric, according to the second - particles with their “living force” (as kinetic energy was then called). They also agreed that heat does not disappear and does not appear: if during contact of two bodies one of them loses heat, then the other receives it, so that what is lost by one is replaced by the other. Nevertheless, the overwhelming majority of researchers up to the 19th century adhered to the so-called material theory of heat, and the 18th century was, of course, the century of the triumph of this particular idea of ​​heat.

The introduction of the concept of thermal conductivity of dough is associated with the solution of problems of heat distribution during contact between different bodies.

Based on the first hypothesis and the fact that the thermometer shows the concentration of caloric in the body, the English mathematician Taylor showed that if, for example, the mass $m_1$ of water is displaced at a temperature $t_1$, the mass $m_2$ at a temperature $t_2$, $m_3$ at a temperature $t_3$, etc., then the temperature of the mixture turns out to be equal

$$t = \frac{m_1 t_1 + m_2 t_2 + m_3 t_3 + \dots + m_n t_n}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3 + \dots + m_n}.$$  (1)

The German physicist Fahrenheit, at the suggestion of the Inderland physician and chemist Burgrave, conducted experiments with two equal masses of water and rutui and showed that the temperature of the mixture (rhythm and water do not mix, but by meeting them they can still be brought to the same temperature) is not equal to the temperature $t$, calculated by formula (1), that is, that formula (1) for a mixture of different liquids is not correct. It turned out that if the water temperature is higher than the temperature of the rutui, then the temperature of the mixture is higher than calculated. This seemed to the countries, because if a thermometer actually shows the concentration of caloric in a body, then with equal masses any body receives the same amount of heat (tellorade) when heated by the same number of degrees.

The English chemist Black brought clarity to this issue in the mid-18th century. The Fahrenheit-Burgrave experiment, Black explains, shows that it takes less heat to heat a certain mass of rutui than to heat the same mass of water by the same number of degrees. In Black's own words: "Mercury therefore has less capacity for heat (if I may use that expression) than water, less heat being required to raise the temperature of the mercury by the same number of degrees."

This is how the concept of “heat capacity” (telloradiness) first appeared. Thus it was clearly shown that heat and temperature are not the same thing. But what was known before remains true: the amount of heat received by one body lost by another. In mathematical form, Black's idea is expressed by an equality now known to every schoolchild:

$$c_1 m_1 (t - t_1) = c_2 m_2 (t_2 - t).$$  (2)

It is called the heat balance equation, which expresses the idea of ​​heat conservation.
