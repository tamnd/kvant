But not every symmetric Delaunay system corresponds to the center of atoms in crystal structures. The symmetry of crystals is specific. For example, among crystalline polyhedra there are no regular dodecadrons and icosadras, or in general polyhedra that have 5th order symmetry axes (that is, “self-aligning” when rotated through an angle of 2π/5 about these axes). How to oblige such fastidiousness of crystalline forms?

In 1783, the French abbot R. J. Gayun, a recognized mineralogist, proposed that every crystal is composed of parallel equal particles adjacent along entire faces (Fig. 4). In 1824, a student of the Great Gauss, professor of physics in Franburg, L. A. Seeber, to explain the expansion of crystals when heated, proposed replacing the Gayun polyhedra with their centers of gravity. Such systems of points were called "lattices".

More strictly, a lattice is the set of all points with integer coordinates relative to an arbitrary (optional rectangular) coordinate system (Fig. 5,a-c). Lattice points are called nodes. It is obvious that the coordinate system uniquely determines the lattice. But the converse statement is not true: in a given lattice, the coordinate system that defines it can be chosen in an infinite number of ways (Fig. 5, b). It is easy to check that the lattices satisfy the conditions of discreteness and coverage, that is, they are Delaunay systems. Let us now prove their symmetry. The following is true.

Lemma about the lattice. Any lattice transforms into itself upon parallel translation to a vector connecting any two of its nodes, as well as upon a central symmetric relative to any node.
