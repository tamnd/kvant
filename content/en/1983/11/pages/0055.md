3) For the case of a ball falling from the top point of the tube onto a spring, draw a graph of the projection of the ball’s acceleration onto the vertical axis as a function of the distance from the top point.

Equipment: stabilizer spring, glass tube, stabilizer ball with a known mass, ruler, graph paper.

Determining the spring stiffness was not difficult: $k = mg/\Delta l$, where $\Delta l$ is the deformation of the spring when the ball is placed on it (for stability, the spring is placed in a vertical glass tube). To determine the sliding friction coefficient of a stable ball on glass using the formula $\mu = tg \beta_0$ (where $\beta_0$ is the minimum angle of inclination at which sliding begins), it was necessary to force the ball to slide along the glass. For this purpose, it was possible, for example, to clamp a ball between the coils of a spring. Some people took advantage of the fact that the spring is stable and determined (in the same way) the sliding friction coefficient of the spring on the glass.

Most of the puppies determined the loss of energy of the ball from the expression $\Delta E = mg(h_2 - h_1)$, where $h_2$ and $h_1$ are the final and initial heights of the ball above the table (that is, after and before the first collision of the ball with the spring). Some defined energini by the maximum slope of the spring.

When plotting the dependence of the projection $a_x$ of the acceleration of the ball on the vertical axis from the distance $x$ of the ball to the top point of the tube (Fig. 3), the main drawback was a purely theoretical solution to the problem:

$$a_x = g - \frac{k}{m} (x - x_0) = g - \frac{k}{m} \Delta x = g \left(1 - \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta l}\right).$$

In fact, all the points should have been plotted on the graph after changing the corresponding values, taking into account the adjusted scale.

Unfortunately, most of the puppies were limited to one cycle of the ball falling onto the spring, while the ball, after interacting with the spring, bounced off it and rose to a new height, fell again, dropping the spring, rose again, and so on until it stopped completely. It was necessary to show all these points on the graph.

Evaluating the assignments for grade 8 as a whole, it should be noted that they are quite interesting, but the amount of work was large. No one completed all the tasks completely. The best result (2.1 points out of 3) was shown by D. Kalavinskaya and G. Mechetsos (Vilnius). Typical shortcomings were: performing only one measurement, sparse reporting, lack of tables with experimental data, and incorrect determination (and sometimes simply absence) of measurement errors.

Task 1

Determine the specific heat capacity of the unknown liquid.

Equipment: heat-lined vessel (thermostat), capillary with a bubble at the end, resistor, vessels with water and non-stellar liquid, changing cylinder, plate with terminals, graph paper, battery of galvanic cells, capacitor, wires, plates.

When completing this assignment, students encountered a number of problems:

a) How to change the temperature without a thermometer?

b) How to calculate the energy of an electric current without a stopwatch?

b) How to eliminate the unknown heat capacity of the thermostat?

d) How to evaluate the heat transfer of a vessel with liquid into the surrounding space?

Only by answering these questions correctly could one choose the optimal method for performing the work.

a) To measure temperature, you need to make a gas thermometer. For this purpose, a capillary with a bubble at the end is heated by hand, and the end of the capillary is lowered into the water so that the capillary water enters it. The volume of air in a bubble under a column of water is proportional to the absolute temperature of the medium in which the capillary is located:

$$\frac{V}{V_0} = \frac{T}{T_0} \Rightarrow \Delta T = \frac{\Delta V}{V_0} T_0.$$

The ambient current temperature formula $W = U^2 t/R$ cannot be used, since the resistance value is not known.
