But if the matter were limited to the existence of solitons only on the water surface, “solitonics” would hardly attract the attention of specialists studying wave processes in various fields of physics. Meanwhile, solitons are found in many media and systems in which the existence of waves is generally possible: in plasma - an ionized gas with a high density of electrons, in semiconductors and superconductors, in chains of series-connected atoms, etc. Although the physical nature of the waves that arise in these different systems may be different, the equations describing the propagation of these waves turn out to be similar or even identical in appearance. (Of course, in each specific case the parameters included in these equations have their own physical meaning.)

A typical example of solitons in solids are the so-called electrical domains that form under certain conditions in semiconductors. They were discovered in the early sixties by the English physicist Gann. Using the behavior of Arsend Gall samples—a semiconductor with electronic conductivity (i.e., $n$-type)—in sufficiently strong electric fields, Gunn found that when the field direction in the sample reached a value of $E \geq 3 \cdot 10^5$ V/m, strictly periodic current oscillations with large amplitude arose in it; the oscillation frequency reached the value $\sim 10^9$ Hz$^*$). Further experimental studies clarified the picture of the appearance of oscillations. It turned out that they are associated with movement along the pattern of a localized region of a strong electric field; This region is called the electrical domain $^{**}$.

*) This effect, called the “Gunn effect,” was described in an article by M. E. Levinshtein in the 10th issue of “Kvanat” for 1982.

**) A domain in physics is a name for limited areas whose properties differ sharply from the properties of their surrounding environment.

What is the reason for the emergence and movement of electrical domains and why do they not form in all semiconductors? What distinctive properties does the $n$-type arsend galla have?

It turns out that, unlike other semiconductors, Gall's arsenal with electronic conductivity has a very unique current-voltage characteristic. As the electric field increases, the current through the sample first increases, as predicted by Ohm's law. However, upon reaching a certain threshold field strength $E$, the current begins to decrease and decreases until it reaches a certain minimum value determined by the properties of the sample; with a further increase in the electric field, the current begins to increase again (figure 3,a). This “nonlinear” current-voltage characteristic is due to the dependence of the action of electrons in the sample on the field strength in the sample. The curve describing this dependence (figure 3,b) has the same $N$-shaped shape as the current-voltage characteristic at figure 3,a.
