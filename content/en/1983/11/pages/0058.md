The most accurate way to determine the refractive index of the plate under study turned out to be the “school” method. The plate was placed on graph paper and the path of the rays was determined by combining parallel grid lines spaced 5 mm from each other. By replacing angular measurements with linear ones, it was possible to achieve a measurement error not exceeding 5%.

Another method, leading to errors of up to 10%, was as follows: two parallel lines, the distance between which was l = 2 cm, were examined through the plate so that one line aligned with the other (Fig. 7). By measuring the distances H, L, l and d, it is easy to calculate the desired refractive index n = sin α/sin β.

The third method, leading to errors of up to 10%, consisted of the following reflection of light from two surfaces of the plate (Fig. 8). By measuring the distance l, the refractive index of the plate substance can be calculated. This method is also suitable for the case when the lower surface of the plate is blackened and therefore the first two methods are not used.

Other methods were also proposed, but they were inferior in measurement accuracy to those described above.

The second part of the task presented greater difficulties. Let's describe two ways to do it.

The first, most rational method, applied by A. Alekseev (Leningrad), A. Burshchenko (Novosibirsk) and M. Savrov (Azov), is as follows. The light bulb is located near the eye at a height H from the plate. If you look at the plate in a vertical direction, you can see two images of a light bulb filament. By placing a strip of graph paper under the plate and marking two points on it at a distance x from each other, you can select a height H such that two images of the light bulb will be at a given distance. By calculating the refractive index of the plate substance n, we can estimate the angle δ between the planes of the plate and indicate the direction of the wedge edge (Fig. 9):

$$\alpha \approx \frac{x}{H}, \alpha=n\delta \Rightarrow \delta \approx \frac{x}{nH}.$$

The second method, applied by B. Bankkov (Aleksin) and M. Dyachkov (Chernogolovka settlement, Moscow region), is based on observing images of the luminous filament of a light bulb after reflection from the top and bottom faces. When the plate is rotated, the distance between the images of the light bulb changes from zero to a maximum value. The position of the plate at maximum or minimum deposition between the sprouse images already allows us to draw a conclusion about the position of the wedge edge and where the thickness of the plate is maximum and where it is minimum. It is more convenient to calculate the angle δ when combining both images (Fig. 10). By measuring the distances h, l and L, the desired angle δ can be calculated.
