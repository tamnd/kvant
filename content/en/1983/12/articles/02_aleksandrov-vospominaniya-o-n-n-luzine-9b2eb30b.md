N. N. Luzin

(on the centenary of his birth)

Celebrating the centenary of the outstanding scientist, the creator of the Soviet school of set theory and functions, Academician N. N. Luzin, we publish the memoirs of two of his students, Academician P. S. Aleksadrov and corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences L. A. Lyusternik. Let us briefly fill in the main stages of N. N. Luzin’s life path.

Nikolai Nikolaevich Luzin was born in 1883 in Tomsk, studied at Tomsk and Irkutsk gymnasiums. In 1901 he entered the mathematical department of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Moscow University. In 1905, while a student, he went to Paris, where he gave lectures at the Sorbonne by E. Borel, A. Poincaré, J. Hadamard and J. Darboux. They returned to Moscow, successfully passed the state exams and was left at the university to prepare for a professorship. In the fall of 1910, the faculty sent him to Gottingen and Paris, where Luzin wrote his first independent research works. In 1915, after returning to Moscow, N.N. Luzin completed the work “Integral and Trionometric Series”, for which he received the degree of Doctor of Pure Mathematics (1916). From 1918 he worked at the Ivanovo Polytechnic Institute, and from 1922 at Moscow University. After being elected academician (1929), he headed the department of function theory at the Mathematical Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences. V. A. Steklova. N. N. Luzin died in Moscow in 1950.

From memories
P. S. Aleksadrova*)

I first met N.N. Luzny in 1914, while a second-year student at Moscow University. The impression from this meeting was, one might say, stunning and haunting to me.

The two most outstanding mathematics professors who taught at Moscow University in the first third of this century: D. F. Ego-

Rov and N.N. Luzin, as lecturers and, in general, university professors, belonged to two opposite types. D. F. Egorov belonged, I would say, to the classical type, and N. N. Luzin to the romantic type. In the lectures of D. F. Egorov, always impeccably carefully prepared, all the proofs were thoroughly detailed and presented in an absolutely strict form. There was no room for any element of improvisation, and they corresponded to the training and level of understanding of those services for which they were intended.

In contrast, N.N. Luzin was a prominent representative of the “romantic” type of university professor. All his teaching was extremely experiential. The form of this teaching was not always impeccable. N. N. Luzin was encouraged to come to a lecture poorly prepared and then, standing at the dock in front of the students, improvise a proof that was not yet ready. At the same time, he often made mistakes, got confused in calculations and put off until the next lecture material intended for this lecture, but which turned out to be unprepared. In short, his teaching could not be called exemplary. At the same time, he sometimes became somewhat flirtatious with this “unmodelling” of his. But Luzin’s lectures, during the heyday of his teaching activity at Moscow University, were full of new and interesting ideas that could encourage the service to further independent studies. N. N. Luzin possessed the gift of captivating and inflaming hearts to the highest degree. Naturally, the results of this passion and ignition were different depending on the combustible material on which the sparks of inspiration thrown into it fell. Capable young aspiring mathematicians, and there were many of them among the students, under the service of N.N. Luzin, were encouraged to undertake serious and deep research of their own, to further advance in mathematical science.

No less important than the nature of Luzin’s lectures, which encourages and, I would say, inspires ministers to independent research in the field of mathematics, was the completely new style of interaction between professor and students introduced by Luzin into the practice of our faculty. It gave birth to great freedom and ease, the absence of any formality and the replacement of external manifestations of respect on the part of students with real deep respect, often turning into enthusiastic admiration. This respect made it impossible for the freedom and spontaneity in the relationship between N.N. Luzin and his students to degenerate into any kind of familiarity.

* * *

My acquaintance with Luzin occurred quite precisely in the middle of the decade in which he obtained his most significant results. He then lived completely alone in furnished rooms (Kokorevskoe farmstead on Balchug), lived only by science. I remember one phrase of his, said during one of our many meetings: “I think day and night about Zermelo’s axiom (the axiom of arbitrary choice in set theory). If only someone knew what this thing is!”

Seeing Luzin during these years, I really saw what can be called my new attitude towards science, and I not only taught him mathematics, but also received a lesson in what a university professor should be.

Then I realized that science and the acquisition of new young people to it are two sides of the same activity - the activity of a scientist. All this remains with me for the rest of my life.

This is how the famous Lusitania arose - actually young mathematicians, welded together not only by technical other relations between its members, but also by ardent love and a lively, unselfish interest in mathematical science.

Lusitania immediately recognized itself as something whole and proclaimed itself an “order” with “commander” N.N. Luzny and “grandmaster” D.F. Egorov. Lusitania was a collective of great labor, creative and emotional upsurge, reflecting in the “microcosm” of the then mathematical faculty of Moscow University the grandiose and comprehensive upsurge experienced by the entire country in those years, in the first years of the revolution, the years of the formation of a new life.

An essential element of N.N. Luzin’s communication with his students were the meetings and conversations that N.N. Luzin had with very small groups of them (about 1-3 people dealing with some topic). In addition, there was a daily general training day for all Nikolai Nikolaevich’s students. However, these were Thursdays, then Wednesdays.

These meetings with N.N. Luzin consisted of two parts. First there was a mathematical part in Nikolai Nikolaevich’s office, a very cozy room, in which, as, indeed, in the entire apartment of N.N. Luzin at that time (Arbat, 25, third floor), there was (at the owner’s request) kerosene lighting. I will never forget those conversations, rich in the most lively mathematics, that took place then. These conversations sometimes dragged on for a long time, but whenever they ended, they were followed by tea and the invariable very tasty nut cake. Over this tea - no longer in the office, but in the dining room of the Luzins' apartment - the conversations took on a different, non-mathematical character and touched on a variety of issues of cultural and social life. Sometimes something was read dry. So, for example, N.N. Luzin with great skill and artistry read the works of Korney Chukovsky, which were intended specifically for children. I remember once reading Anatole France’s story “The Miracle of St. Nicholas.”

The meeting at N.N. Luzin’s ended late at night. After their completion, the participants of the large group went out onto the Arbat and its alleys and gradually, in various sequential combinations, seeing each other off, finally dispersed - in person by morning - to their homes.

Lusitania lived not only for others, she lived cheerfully, and how cheerfully! This fun even chokes the “seniors”, and not only N.N. Luzin, but also D.F. Egorov, who take, so to speak, full participation in our expectations of a cheerful meeting. These gatherings were numerous and took place on various occasions. New Year's Eve and Tatiana's Day, the name days of D. F. Egorov and N. N. Luzin, the birthdays of individual Lusitanians - everything gave a reason for our meetings.

The Luzin school, Lusitania, was one of a kind, a unique group of young, mostly gifted and joyful mathematicians.

Why does Dean's machine instantly become a world sensation with an unkind memo, while the problem of high-temperature superconductivity barely attracts lukewarm, condescending attention?

The story began in America. Without going into details, the gist of it was this. A certain Dean designed a rather crude and simple mechanical device; it seems he even received a patent. I emphasize that there were no subtleties in his system. But for a person who poorly understands the laws of mechanics, the behavior of the “machine” during operation could seem strange. She, the machine, either twitched or did something similar.

I don’t want to go into details, and a little later it will become clear why.

So, instead of turning to a literate person for an explanation of the principles of its operation, Dean himself, or perhaps some journalist, took it and announced: “The machine shows that Newtonian mechanics is grossly erroneous. The mechanical equations need to be significantly corrected.”

Sensation! First class. That's where it started. Now it is difficult, and even uninteresting, to establish how many articles were devoted to the “great find.” And, of course, there were hints: arrogant professional physicists do not even want (and perhaps cannot) understand the new genetic theory.

Why bother?

Any physicist knew in advance, completely uninterested in the details: all this was empty nonsense. And you shouldn’t even understand it, except maybe shoot it as a fun problem for schoolchildren.

“This is where the arrogant existing professional came to light,” Newton’s mechanics might shout and deny. “Caste organization. Lack of faith in human genius."

Not at all. Nothing like that came of it. But quite the opposite - a scientific mindset or, if you like, common sense appeared. Tens, no, hundreds of thousands of experiments have shown us that Newtonian mechanics works flawlessly in its field. Thousands of famous and unknown nearby physicists tested its laws, tested it for a variety of phenomena with an accuracy of many signs. And now we can absolutely confidently state: for macroscopic bodies at speeds that greatly change the speed of light, Newtonian mechanics works great. In this area, any more general theory can only slightly “correct” Newton, showing that differences should appear in the eighth or tenth digit. But no experiment or theory can cross out Newtonian mechanics as an impeccable practical guide to the calculation of millions and millions of problems. Physicists have accepted the theory of relative theory and quantum mechanics, but, of course, the first and main requirement for new theories is that, in a known, well-tested area, their predictions coincide with classical physics and, of course, with Newtonian mechanics.

This is a general requirement for any new theory.

It can be assumed that the relative theory will be replaced by a new one, but the results of the relative theory will be included in the new scheme and, within its scope, will remain unchanged (up to possible small corrections).
