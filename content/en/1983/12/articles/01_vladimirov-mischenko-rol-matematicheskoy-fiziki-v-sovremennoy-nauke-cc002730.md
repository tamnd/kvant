The very name “mathematical physics” suggests that this discipline occupies an intermediate area between mathematics and physics, being under the direct and strong influence of both. What exactly does this “intermediate field” of science do? Speaking briefly and perhaps not entirely clearly, this question can be answered like this: “Mathematical physics is the theory of mathematical models of physical phenomena.” To understand this brief definition, let us turn to some (well-known to schoolchildren) examples of such mathematical models.

A characteristic feature of these methods is the construction of mathematical models in the form of partial arbitrary differential equations - equations that include an unknown function that depends not on one, but on several variables, and its partial arbitrary ones (that is, arbitrary in individual variables). In the 19th century, a great contribution to the development and ideas of methods of mathematical physics was made by Russian scientists M. V. Ostrogadsky (1801-1862), P. L. Chebyshev (1821-1892), A. M. Lyapunov (1857-1918), N. E. Zhukovsky (1847-1921), V. A. Steklov (1864-1926) and S. A. Chaplgiin (1869-1942).

The remarkable Russian and Soviet mathematician, founder of the St. Petersburg School of Mathematical Physics V. A. Steklov wrote about classical mathematical physics in 1922:

“The solution of problems of mathematical physics leads to the determination of one or more quantities that characterize a particular physical process occurring in a given medium (in a given body) depending on the position of each point of this medium and time using one or more differential equations.

These equations are derived with the help of a small number of possible simple hypotheses, which are used as the basis of the theory of each physical one and are presented as the result of a generalization of a long series of wholesale and observed physical processes that can be divided into those that actually originate in the nature around us or are created artificially.

As a result of such abstraction (generalization), a small number of basic provisions (hypotheses) are created, which should be independent of each other and not contradict any of the currently known facts of reality.

These hypotheses are used as the basis for the theory of one or another physical phenomenon, and all theories are then developed detective-wise using the axioms of mathematics and the basic laws of promised mechanics using the methods of differential and integral artificiality.

In this way, based on the physical data of each problem, differential equations are compiled that characterize the essence of the process under consideration for each point in the medium and for each moment in time.

The problem comes down to determining, as a function of time, the coordinates of each point of the medium in which the phenomenon being studied occurs, and the quantities that determine the physical properties of the medium, those unknowns that appear in the resulting differential equations, that is, to the integration of these equations.

At the same time, we obtain in this way a solution that must satisfy all the data that is obtained as a result of direct observation of the process being studied.”

The field of mathematics used in constructing mathematical models is also expanding. Today's mathematical physics, thanks to its deep connections with all branches of mathematics, occupies a central, central role in the overall structure of modern mathematics.

With its dark connections with physics and other natural sciences, mathematical physics has always attracted the attention of many prominent scientists. This she stimulated the development of mathematics. It is well known that during the last decade many prominent representatives of “numerical” mathematics began to engage in mathematical physics. However, the reverse influence of mathematics on physics is often underestimated. There are misconceptions that “real” mathematics has nothing to do with solving problems that are interesting for practice”, that mathematics is needed by physics only as a means for calculation, that physics only needs “simplified” mathematics, that one can even get around it with just a computer. In fact, the city's mathematicians are deeper. Many examples are well known from the history of science when, within the framework of a successfully chosen mathematical model, with the help of only reasoning and calculations, as they say, “at the tip of the pen,” it was possible to imagine new physical phenomena or the existence of new physical objects. And these ideas were subsequently more brilliantly confirmed by experience.

* * *

One of the most vicarious examples of theoretical representation is the discovery of the planet Neptune in the solar system. In 1781, the English astronomer William Herschel discovered a new celestial body using the telexan he himself built. At first, he predicted that the body he discovered was a comet. But a thorough study of the movement of this object, which was carried out by Russian astronomer Andrei Ivanovich Leksel for two years, showed that the body discovered by V. Herschel is a new planet. This planet was called the calculation of the orbit of Uranus and found that its movement was not consistent with theoretical calculations. He suggested that irregularities in the movement of Uranus are caused by the attachment of another previously unknown planet, formed at a greater distance from the Sun. The search for this unknown planet began. More than half later, J. Adams, an English astronomer, completed calculations of the orbit of this new planet in September 1845 and reported the result of his calculations to the director of the Greenwich General Hospital, J. Erie. However, the latter did not organize his time to search for a new planet. Simultaneously with J. Adams, similar calculations of the arbitrary in France were carried out by Urbain Le Verre. On September 18, 1846, W. Lever sent a letter to the Berlin Astronomical Observatory in which he reported the location of the hypothetical planet. The letter was received by the German astronomer Johann Halle on September 23, 1846. That same evening, I. Galle discovered the planet. It was located at the calculated location only 52 arc minutes away.

The theoretical presentation of a substantially new planet, Neptune, was a triumph of 19th-century scientific thought.

Let us give another example from very recent times. In January 1983, in Geneva, at the European Organization for Nuclear Research, the discovery of a new part was announced - the W boson *). The discovery of this part has long been expected - its existence was predicted several years ago. Bosons are the “main characters” of the theory of electroweak interaction, a unified theory of electromagnetic and weak processes (its authors S. Weinberg, S. Glashow and A. Salam received the Nobel Prize in 1979). The theory predicted not only the very existence of W bosons, but also their mass. Preparation for the experiments lasted two years, and finally the first bazons were registered with instruments. This phenomenon is extremely rare - and during the two months of operation of the installation, only six cases of W bosons were recorded. The most remarkable thing is that the experimental estimates of the mass of the part turned out to be very close to the theoretical ones - practically being within the limits of experimental accuracy. Thus, the theory of electromagnetic and weak interaction received experimental justification.

In modern mathematical physics, it has become customary to establish certain physical effects using theory, and then confirm these effects in experiments. Outstanding mathematicians and physicists have repeatedly spoken about this enormous influence of mathematics on physics. Thus, at the World Congress of Mathematicians in 1900, the great German mathematician D. Hilbert, putting forward his famous mathematical problems (using the name “Hilbert’s problems”), called the problem “the axiomatization of those physical sciences in which mathematics plays an important role.” The formulation of this problem, in conflict with other key problems of “pure” mathematics, testifies to the important role that Hilbert attached to mathematics for the progress of physics, for its theoretical formulation. The same idea was expressed by V. A. Steklov in 1921, justifying the current need to organize a physics and mathematics institute at the Academy of Sciences: “Not one of the natural sciences, if it is not about its own material, but about real creativity, can do without mathematics, the matter of all sciences. As for physics, which is placed ahead of all other sciences..., at present mathematics and physics have merged into one whole to such an extent that it is sometimes difficult to separate where mathematics ends and physics begins.”

The outstanding theoretical physicist and mathematician P. Dirac said even more definitely in a famous article in 1930, in which he theoretically predicted the existence of the antiparticle: “The progress of physics requires for its theoretical formulation more and more “high” mathematics... It seems likely that this process of continuous abstraction will continue in the future and that the success of physics must rely to a large extent on continuous modifications and generalizations of the axioms on a mathematical basis...”
