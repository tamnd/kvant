Where it's thin, that's where it breaks

V. V. MAISKY

This article can be considered as an example of setting up any physical experiment. First of all, the problem is clearly formulated and an idea for its solution is developed. Then the experiment itself is carried out: a setup is selected and appropriate measurements are made. After which the results obtained are processed and theoretically applied.

When Vsevolod Maisky wrote his article, he was in tenth grade at Moscow School No. 179. Now he is a student at the Moscow Institute of Chemical Engineering.

“Durable”, “fragile” - we often use these terms in everyday life and in technology, characterizing various objects and materials. What is “strength” and how can it be measured?

Fishermen, for example, are well aware of a simple method for determining the strength of a fishing line: a piece of fishing line tied at one end is loaded with various weights; The maximum load a line can withstand is its tensile strength. This method can be used to test the strength of other materials.

What determines strength, such as tensile strength? Firstly, from the cross-sectional area. Thus, to break a fishing line with a diameter of 0.1 mm, a force of about 50 N is required, and a fishing line with a diameter of 0.5 mm can withstand the weight of an adult. In order to eliminate dependence on the thickness of the sample, a special concept is introduced - tensile strength. This is the maximum stress that the sample can withstand without failure.

Secondly, the strength of materials depends on its temperature. For metals, say, as the temperature increases, the tensile strength decreases (correspondence to the graphs of the dependence of the tensile strength on temperature for various metals is given, for example, in the “Handbook on Elementary Physics” by N. I. Koshkin and M. G. Shirkevich).

Could strength depend on... length? At first glance, it seems that no, and for large plums this is indeed true. But there is a material whose strength depends on the length of the sample. This material is wool.

One of the correspondence problems of the IV Tour of Young Physicists is based on the small properties of six-blade. Here are its terms:

Wool thread is woven from many individual fibers. Investigate the tensile strength of such a thread depending on its length. Explain the results of the experiment.

The first thing required was to select a suitable object for research. After several first experiments, the choice fell on a thin but quite strong six-piece thread (a force of 2 to 4 N was required to break it). During these experiments it was established:

During the search, various devices were invented and tested. This includes hanging a load from a thread attached to one end, and using a lever with a variable arm ratio, and using a solenoid with a core (the so-called electromagnetic balance).

After many tests, the choice fell on one of the simplest settings, made on the basis of a regular school diameter meter (maximum reading 4 N, division value 0.1 N). A ruler was attached to the base of the diameter meter to measure the length of the threads. On the conducting rod, which ends with the diameter meter spring, a slider was mounted that could move along the scale with a slight tremor. With its help, the diameter readings were recorded. A bunch of threads (10-15 pieces) was tied to the hook of the diameter meter. One thread was selected from the bundle, its length was measured using a ruler, and then the thread was sharply pulled at the free end. The arrow, moving along the diameter scale, pushed the engine. After the threads broke, the arrow returned to its original position, and the slider indicated the value of the force at which the thread broke, that is, the sample was strong.

For experiments, threads of lengths 2, 3, from 5 to 30 were used with an interval of 5 and, finally, 40 cm. For each length, 10-15 experiments were carried out and the average reading of the thread was found. Based on the data obtained, a graph was constructed of the dependence of the sixth thread on its length (Fig. 1). The graph shows that as the length of the sample increases, the thread strength decreases. How can this be explained?

It turns out that the whole point is in the internal structure of the six threads. Each thread consists of a large number of individual fibers 30-50 microns thick, twisted together. The length of different villi is different: from 4 to 40 cm. During the experiment, the length of the villi was changed, while the average length turned out to be 8.1 cm, 89% of the total number of villi fell into the range from 4 to 13 cm, and there were very few very short and very long villi.

The fibers are twisted together so that in each section of the thread there are a number of areas (we will call them defects) where, for one reason or another, the thickness of the thread is reduced.
