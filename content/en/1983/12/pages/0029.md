Deformation without acceleration. Distance is now such a case. A body of mass $m$ (Fig. 2) is suspended from a non-versoarm, the upper end of which is attached to a fixed stand. Under the influence of gravity, the body, of course, begins to fall. Together with it, the lower end of the spring also begins to move. Therefore, the cause of deformation is the elastic force acting on the upper end of the body. Since the force of gravity acts on all parts of the body, and the force of elasticity only on its upper end, different parts of the body will move differently and only end up deformed. The elastic force resulting from this will act on the lower end of the spring, etc.

It is easy to understand that eventually the weapon and the body will stop. This means that the sum of the forces acting both externally and on the body will be equal to zero. The external elastic force acts from the side of the deformed support and the elastic force of the deformed body (forces $\vec{f}_1$ and $\vec{f}_2$ on figure 2). The body is subject to gravity forces $m\vec{g}$ and elastic forces of the deformed spring $f$. All bodies of our system are deformed, but none of them has acceleration.

Obviously, this is exactly the situation that is meant when they talk about the static action of a force, that is, the action of a force leading to deformation, but not acceleration (indeed, in our example we can talk about this). However, we must remember that in cases like the one considered, there is no acceleration because two forces act on both the body and the external one and their sum is equal to zero. Of course, under no circumstances can one force (or several forces, if their sum is not equal to zero) deform a body without imparting acceleration to it as well. A force (alone!) cannot have a static action. The force is of diamic magnitude.

About surface tension forces

The special properties of the surface of a liquid are due to the fact that the molecules on the surface have many neighbors on one side, deep in the liquid, and few on the other, above its surface. Due to this, the molecules located on the surface are subject to forces directed deep into the liquid, perpendicular to its surface. These forces add up and exert pressure on the fluid, which is balanced by the pressure caused by the elastic forces of the fluid itself.

Meanwhile, numerous experiments, for example those described in the textbook “Physics 9,” indicate that forces act on the surface of a liquid, directed not perpendicularly, but tangentially to the surface (dividing it into an elastic film). This is also reflected by navani - surface tension forces. It turns out that the real forces acting on the molecules of the surface are directed perpendicular to it, and in reality there are no forces of surface tension?

It turns out that forces directed tangentially to the surface are real; they do not arise only when the surface of the liquid is torn by some foreign bodies, solid or liquid. In fact, any molecule inside a volume of liquid is surrounded on all sides by other molecules acting on it with forces evenly distributed in all directions, so these forces are balanced.
