But any sounds also differ in volume. The sound of the same source, the same tone and timbre can be more or less loud. Let's discuss this in more detail.

A sound wave carries with it energy - the vibrational energy of a particle of the medium in which it propagates. The volume of sound is associated with this energy. But this connection is not as simple as it might seem.

Each unit of volume of the medium in which a sound wave propagates has, as can be shown, energy $w = \varrho v_m^2/2$, where $\varrho$ is the density of the medium and $v_m$ is the amplitude of the velocity oscillations of the oscillating particle. The amplitude of the velocity is equal to $\omega x_m$, where $\omega$ is the cyclic frequency of oscillations and $x_m$ is the amplitude of particle oscillations (see “Physics 10”), so the energy per unit volume, that is, the energy density, is equal to

$$w = \frac{\varrho \omega^2 x_m^2}{2}.$$

But the wave propagates with a certain speed $v$ (this speed is usually large compared to the speed $v_m$), so the energy is like a flow in the direction of propagation of the wave. If you mentally imagine a flat area $S$, perpendicular to the direction of propagation of the wave, then in time $t$ the energy contained in the volume $Svt$ and equal to $\varrho \omega^2 x_m^2 Svt/2$ “flows” through it. It follows that in a unit of time the energy passing through a plane unit of wave is equal to

$$I = \omega v = \frac{\varrho \omega^2 x_m^2}{2} v.$$

The value $I$ is called sound integrity. It is measured in J/($m^2 \cdot c$), or in W/$m^2$.

The human ear is especially sensitive to frequencies around 1000 hertz. At this frequency, the integrativeness of the weakest sound that can still be heard is only $10^{-12}$ W/$m^2$. During normal conversation, integrativeness does not exceed $10^{-5}$ W/$m^2$. The most powerful sounds generate the required power per square meter of the order of $10^3$ W/$m^2$. Sound intensity of 1 - 10 W/$m^2$ causes pain in the ears and is felt on the skin. Thus, the human hearing organ perceives sounds whose integrities range from $10^{-12}$ to 10 W/$m^2$. The most powerful sound is 10$^{13}$ times more integrative than the weakest one!

However, we never have the feeling that one sound is millions or even billions of times louder than another. It turns out that we need to distinguish between the integrity of a sound and its volume. If the integrativeness of one sound is ten, a hundred or a million times greater than the integrativeness of another sound, this does not mean at all that it is just as many times louder. The loudness of sound is assessed physiologically, with the participation of the human nervous system and brain. And physiological hearing is such that doubling the integrativeness of a sound is not perceived as doubling its volume. Connecting volume with the integrity of sound is what it is.

According to the logarithmic law. Let's look first at sounds with a frequency of 1000 Hz. The weakest sound that can still be perceived at this frequency, called the hearing threshold, has an integrity of $10^{-12}$ W/$m^2$. We will assume that the volume of this sound is zero. The volume of a sound of the same frequency, but with 10 times more integrity, is equal to one unit, called “bel”, or ten units, called “decibel” (short designation is dB). The volume of a sound whose integrativeness is 10 times greater increases by another 10 decibels, etc. In other words, when the integrativeness of a sound is multiplied, the volume increases.*)

*) This law was discovered by the German anatomist and physiologist E. Weber (1795-1878), and the German physicist and psychologist G. Fechner (1801-1887) gave it a mathematical form, which is why the law is called the Weber-Fechner law. It states: the strength of sensation is proportional to the logarithm of the force of separation. This is true not only for the perception of sound, but also for other human sensations.
