Mathematical this law looks like this. Let us denote the intensity of some sound of frequency 1000 Hz by $I$, and the intensity at the threshold of those referring to the same frequency by $I_0$. Then the loudness of the sound $L$ intensity $I$ expressed in white is equal to

$$L_6 = \lg \frac{I}{I_0},$$

and in decibels -

$$L_{\lambda 6} = 10 \lg \frac{I}{I_0}.$$

A sound with a frequency of 1000 Hz can be part of the sounds produced by a person conducting a quiet conversation and a person screaming at the top of his voice. In the first case, the sound intensity is approximately $10^{-8}$ W/m², in the second - $10^{-4}$ W/m², the volume of the first sound is 40 dB, the second 80 dB. This, however, does not mean that the volume of a scream is twice as loud as a quiet conversation. These two sounds are said to be 40 dB louder than the other. Note, by the way, that a volume difference of 1 dB is the smallest difference in volume that can be satisfied by the human ear.

The universal unit of loudness is background. The above logarithmic law is true and applies not only to sounds with a frequency of 1000 Hz. But for other frequencies the intensities at the threshold of those referring are different. So, for example, at a frequency of about 100 Hz it is equal to $10^{-8}$ W/m², that is, $10^4$ times more than for a frequency of 1000 Hz. According to the formula for loudness, this means that if the volume of some sound is at a frequency of 1000 Hz, the intensity of the sound is at the reference threshold at a frequency of 1000 Hz.
