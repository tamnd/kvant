⟦folio 9⟧

D. E. Menshova, A. Ya. Khinchin, M. Ya. Suslin and me. By 1919-1920 it was replenished with a number of young mathematicians: P. S. Uryson, L. A. Lyusternik, M. A. Lavrentyev, N. K. Bari, several other students and students of N. N. Luzin. This is how other teams arose, which were soon joined by two such outstanding mathematicians as A. N. Kolmogorov and L. G. Shnirelman. Among the mathematicians of the older generation, V. V. Stepanov immediately joined this team. Somewhat later, this team included P. S. Novikov and L. V. Keldysh. This is how the famous Lusitania arose - actually young mathematicians, welded together not only by technical other relations between its members, but also by ardent love and a lively, unselfish interest in mathematical science.

Lusitania immediately recognized itself as something whole and proclaimed itself an “order” with “commander” N.N. Luzny and “grandmaster” D.F. Egorov. Lusitania was a collective of great labor, creative and emotional upsurge, reflecting in the “microcosm” of the then mathematical faculty of Moscow University the grandiose and comprehensive upsurge experienced by the entire country in those years, in the first years of the revolution, the years of the formation of a new life.

An essential element of N.N. Luzin’s communication with his students were the meetings and conversations that N.N. Luzin had with very small groups of them (about 1-3 people dealing with some topic). In addition, there was a daily general training day for all Nikolai Nikolaevich’s students. However, these were Thursdays, then Wednesdays.

These meetings with N.N. Luzin consisted of two parts. First there was a mathematical part in Nikolai Nikolaevich’s office, a very cozy room, in which, as, indeed, in the entire apartment of N.N. Luzin at that time (Arbat, 25, third floor), there was (at the owner’s request) kerosene lighting. I will never forget those conversations, rich in the most lively mathematics, that took place then. These conversations sometimes dragged on for a long time, but whenever they ended, they were followed by tea and the invariable very tasty nut cake. Over this tea - no longer in the office, but in the dining room of the Luzins' apartment - the conversations took on a different, non-mathematical character and touched on a variety of issues of cultural and social life. Sometimes something was read dry. So, for example, N.N. Luzin with great skill and artistry read the works of Korney Chukovsky, which were intended specifically for children. I remember once reading Anatole France’s story “The Miracle of St. Nicholas.”

The meeting at N.N. Luzin’s ended late at night. After their completion, the participants of the large group went out onto the Arbat and its alleys and gradually, in various sequential combinations, seeing each other off, finally dispersed - in person by morning - to their homes.

Lusitania lived not only for others, she lived cheerfully, and how cheerfully! This fun even chokes the “seniors”, and not only N.N. Luzin, but also D.F. Egorov, who take, so to speak, full participation in our expectations of a cheerful meeting. These gatherings were numerous and took place on various occasions. New Year's Eve and Tatiana's Day, the name days of D. F. Egorov and N. N. Luzin, the birthdays of individual Lusitanians - everything gave a reason for our meetings.

The Luzin school, Lusitania, was one of a kind, a unique group of young, mostly gifted and joyful mathematicians.
