Why is a nearby inhabitant of our planet so excited by the idea that somewhere in the mountains - either in the Himalayas, or in the Cordillera, or (why are we worse?) in the Pamirs and the Caucasus - is a wild great ape roaming? Why doesn’t the discovery of the t-particle or the W-boson, or, at worst, the discovery of pulsars, arouse a tenth or a hundredth of such keen interest?

Why does Dean's machine instantly become a world sensation with an unkind memo, while the problem of high-temperature superconductivity barely attracts lukewarm, condescending attention?

The story began in America. Without going into details, the gist of it was this. A certain Dean designed a rather crude and simple mechanical device; it seems he even received a patent. I emphasize that there were no subtleties in his system. But for a person who poorly understands the laws of mechanics, the behavior of the “machine” during operation could seem strange. She, the machine, either twitched or did something similar.

I don’t want to go into details, and a little later it will become clear why.

So, instead of turning to a literate person for an explanation of the principles of its operation, Dean himself, or perhaps some journalist, took it and announced: “The machine shows that Newtonian mechanics is grossly erroneous. The mechanical equations need to be significantly corrected.”

Sensation! First class. That's where it started. Now it is difficult, and even uninteresting, to establish how many articles were devoted to the “great find.” And, of course, there were hints: arrogant professional physicists do not even want (and perhaps cannot) understand the new genetic theory.

Why bother?

Any physicist knew in advance, completely uninterested in the details: all this was empty nonsense. And you shouldn’t even understand it, except maybe shoot it as a fun problem for schoolchildren.

“This is where the arrogant existing professional came to light,” Newton’s mechanics might shout and deny. “Caste organization. Lack of faith in human genius."

Not at all. Nothing like that came of it. But quite the opposite - a scientific mindset or, if you like, common sense appeared. Tens, no, hundreds of thousands of experiments have shown us that Newtonian mechanics works flawlessly in its field. Thousands of famous and unknown nearby physicists tested its laws, tested it for a variety of phenomena with an accuracy of many signs. And now we can absolutely confidently state: for macroscopic bodies at speeds that greatly change the speed of light, Newtonian mechanics works great. In this area, any more general theory can only slightly “correct” Newton, showing that differences should appear in the eighth or tenth digit. But no experiment or theory can cross out Newtonian mechanics as an impeccable practical guide to the calculation of millions and millions of problems. Physicists have accepted the theory of relative theory and quantum mechanics, but, of course, the first and main requirement for new theories is that, in a known, well-tested area, their predictions coincide with classical physics and, of course, with Newtonian mechanics.

This is a general requirement for any new theory.

It can be assumed that the relative theory will be replaced by a new one, but the results of the relative theory will be included in the new scheme and, within its scope, will remain unchanged (up to possible small corrections).
