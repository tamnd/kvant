⟦folio 6⟧

Vella made numerous predictions, which were later confirmed. The most important takeaway from Maxwell's theory is the establishment of the electromagnetic nature of light. The fact that light rays are nothing more than high-frequency electromagnetic waves was proven by an unrepresented experimental test by the German physicist Hertz.

* * *

Let us give another example from very recent times. In January 1983, in Geneva, at the European Organization for Nuclear Research, the discovery of a new part was announced - the W boson *). The discovery of this part has long been expected - its existence was predicted several years ago. Bosons are the “main characters” of the theory of electroweak interaction, a unified theory of electromagnetic and weak processes (its authors S. Weinberg, S. Glashow and A. Salam received the Nobel Prize in 1979). The theory predicted not only the very existence of W bosons, but also their mass. Preparation for the experiments lasted two years, and finally the first bazons were registered with instruments. This phenomenon is extremely rare - and during the two months of operation of the installation, only six cases of W bosons were recorded. The most remarkable thing is that the experimental estimates of the mass of the part turned out to be very close to the theoretical ones - practically being within the limits of experimental accuracy. Thus, the theory of electromagnetic and weak interaction received experimental justification.

* * *

In modern mathematical physics, it has become customary to establish certain physical effects using theory, and then confirm these effects in experiments. Outstanding mathematicians and physicists have repeatedly spoken about this enormous influence of mathematics on physics. Thus, at the World Congress of Mathematicians in 1900, the great German mathematician D. Hilbert, putting forward his famous mathematical problems (using the name “Hilbert’s problems”), called the problem “the axiomatization of those physical sciences in which mathematics plays an important role.” The formulation of this problem, in conflict with other key problems of “pure” mathematics, testifies to the important role that Hilbert attached to mathematics for the progress of physics, for its theoretical formulation. The same idea was expressed by V. A. Steklov in 1921, justifying the current need to organize a physics and mathematics institute at the Academy of Sciences: “Not one of the natural sciences, if it is not about its own material, but about real creativity, can do without mathematics, the matter of all sciences. As for physics, which is placed ahead of all other sciences..., at present mathematics and physics have merged into one whole to such an extent that it is sometimes difficult to separate where mathematics ends and physics begins.”

The outstanding theoretical physicist and mathematician P. Dirac said even more definitely in a famous article in 1930, in which he theoretically predicted the existence of the antiparticle: “The progress of physics requires for its theoretical formulation more and more “high” mathematics... It seems likely that this process of continuous abstraction will continue in the future and that the success of physics must rely to a large extent on continuous modifications and generalizations of the axioms on a mathematical basis...”

*) About this, see the article “Opening a new part” («Kvant», 1983, No. 5).
