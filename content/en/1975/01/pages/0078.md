a metal cylinder (or ball) with a small hole. In this case, the influence of the charged body on other bodies is almost excluded, therefore, on the inner surface of the cylinder (or ball) a charge will be induced, almost equal in magnitude to the charge of the ball, but opposite in sign, and on the outer surface - the same in magnitude and sign. If a charged body touches the inner wall of the cylinder, charges of opposite sign are neutralized, and charges of the same sign will remain on the cylinder as were on the charged body (Fig. 5). Thus, it is possible to transfer almost completely the charge from one body to another.

Now let's try to answer the second question posed at the very beginning. Let us have: a charged ball $A$, an uncharged ball $B$, a hollow metal ball and an electroscope on which we want to accumulate a charge greater than the charge of the ball $A$.

First of all, let’s charge the ball $B$ by induction (naturally, with a charge of the opposite sign). Let's bring ball $B$ inside the grounded ball for a while, and then remove both the grounding and ball $B$. A charge will remain on the ball, equal in magnitude to the charge of the ball $B$, but opposite in sign, that is, coinciding with the sign of the charge of the ball $A$.
