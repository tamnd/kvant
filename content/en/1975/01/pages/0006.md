The larger of the segments CD, EH is called the major axis of the ellipse, and the smaller is called the minor axis. This means that if $k > 1$, then $a$ is the semiminor axis, and $b$ is the major one, and if $k < 1$, then vice versa. In both cases $k = \frac{b}{a}$.

The point $o$—the center of the circle—at PC transforms into itself, this point $O$ is called the center of the ellipse, the points $C, D, E, H$ are its vertices. The square $pqrs$ circumscribed around the circle (the "mother" of the ellipse) so that $pq \parallel rs \parallel l$ and $qr \parallel sp \perp l$ are transformed into the rectangle $PQRS$ on the sides $2a$ and $2b$ circumscribed around the ellipse; it is called the fundamental rectangle for our ellipse. The sides of this rectangle touch the ellipse at its vertices.

How to measure an ellipse

If the axes of the ellipse are given, then the easiest way is to measure the ellipse by hand by first constructing a fundamental rectangle and then an oval curve into it. But this method is not accurate, there are many oval curves with the same axes of symmetry (several such curves are shown on figure 4), but only one of them (on figure 4 - red) is an ellipse. You can accurately construct as many points of the ellipse as you like. Most commonly used

Having made such a construction for several points $m_1, m_2, m_3, \dots$ of the circle, one can obtain the corresponding points of the ellipse. All that remains is to connect them in a smooth line by hand or using a pattern.
