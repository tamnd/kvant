If the point $M'(X', Y')$ does not lie on the ellipse, then the point $m'(x', y')$, transferred to $M'$ as a result of PC, did not lie on the circle, and the equality $(x')^2 + (y')^2 = a^2$ is false, which means that the equality

$$(X')^2 + \frac{(Y')^2}{k^2} = a^2.$$

In accordance with the definition of the equation of a line, we obtained the following equation of an ellipse: $x^2 + \dfrac{y^2}{k^2} = a^2$. Since $ka = b$, the last equation reduces to the form

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1. \tag{Э}$$

This is the equation of the ellipse in the most convenient (so-called *canonical*) form.

**Inclined sections of a circular cylinder**

Intersecting a circular cylinder with planes inclined to its base at an acute angle $\alpha$, we obtain oval curves. Let us prove that they are ellipses. To do this, it is enough to establish that each such oval is obtained from a circle as a result of RS (namely, stretching) with a coefficient $k = \dfrac{1}{\cos\alpha}$.

Let us take (Fig. 8) at the base of the cylinder there are two diameters: $cd$ - parallel and $eh$ - perpendicular to the line of intersection of the base plane with the section plane of the cylindrical

Each ellipse with a minor semi-axis $a$ and a major semi-axis $b$ can be obtained as a section of a circular cylinder, the radius of which is equal to the semi-minor axis of the ellipse. To do this, it is enough to cut the cylinder with a plane inclined to the base of the cylinder at an angle whose cosine is equal to $\dfrac{a}{b}$. This is always possible since there is $\dfrac{a}{b} < 1$.

**Projection of a circle - ellipse**

If we project a circle lying in a certain plane onto a second plane intersecting the first at an acute angle $\alpha$, then the projection will result in an ellipse. The reader can easily prove this himself, using figure 8, which must now be looked at from a different point of view: we will consider the line $CEDH$ to be a circle ($OC = OD = OE = OH = a$), and the projecting cylinder not to be circular, but oval. Here $cd = CD = 2a$, $eh = EH \cdot \cos\alpha = 2b$. It is necessary to prove that $cedh$ is an ellipse (the surface in this case is called *an elliptical cylinder* - due to the shape of the perpendicular section).
