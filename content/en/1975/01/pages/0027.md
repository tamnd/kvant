So, it means that logic is still not indifferent to the question of *truth*? Of course not. This is essentially the most important question for her. But, unlike ordinary conversational practice, we will always strive for maximum accuracy and therefore will be forced to distinguish between several types of truth (respectively falsity).

The so-called *factual truth* of sentences, understood as “correspondence to reality,” is accepted in logic without any discussion and without interest in a way to verify it. The fact that the Volga flows into the Caspian Sea is true, but it is true purely in fact, and it is a matter of geography, not logic, to guarantee this truth. The statements “The Volga flows into Lake Michigan” and “The Amazon flows into the Klyazma” are, of course, false - but again, purely factually: this does not follow from any “laws of logic”. The propositions “If the Volga flows into the Caspian Sea, then the Volga flows into the Caspian Sea”, “If the Volga flows into Lake Michigan, then the Volga flows into Lake Michigan” and “If the Amazon flows into the Klyazma, then the Amazon flows into the Klyazma” - all three are equally *logically true*, that is, true, so to speak, solely by virtue of their structure, regardless of the truth or the falsity of their premises and conclusions. The truth of Newton's laws or Coulomb's law is confirmed by experience, but not by logic, from which none of the specific physical laws follows. The conclusion “The Greeks are people, Socrates is a Greek, therefore Socrates is a man,” on the contrary, is correct in itself, but

not by virtue of the truth of the first premise, certified by world history, or the second, stating a certain fact in the history of philosophy.

Strictly speaking, the previous phrase is very conditional: in this case we simply tacitly agree to mean *that* Socrates, and not, say, Chernyshevsky’s father-in-law (Nikolai Gavrilovich’s wife was named Olga Sokratovna) or a cat named “Socrates” (in the latter case, conclusion (II) would not, of course, become more legitimate, but in the correct conclusion (III) both premises would be true). Thus, the actual truth of statements often depends on a number of additional (not always explicitly stated) agreements and conditions, as a result of which in such cases we sometimes talk about the *possible truth* - as opposed to the *necessary truth* of statements like “12 is divisible by 3 or not” and “12 is divisible by 5 or not”, true without regard to our arithmetic knowledge and even to what number system the numbers 12, 3 and 5 are written in.

Thus, *logical truths* - unlike the experimental, factual truths of concrete sciences and everyday practice - *do not bring us any information about the real world*. As Leibniz said, these are *truths in all possible worlds*. Therefore, they are also called *always true*, or *identically true* statements. Or even shorter and more expressively: *tautologies*. This is what (literal translation from Greek: “the same word”) is called in everyday speech meaningless repetitions that do not tell us anything new. Take a closer look at logically true sentences like “From $A$ follows $A$”, “$B$ is true or $B$ is false”, “If statements $C$ and $D$ are true, then statements $D$ and $C$ are true”, etc., and you will agree that the term is very clear and relevant.

Not all tautologies (in the logical sense of the word) are so immediately obvious - sometimes their logical truth cannot be immediately discerned due to the purely external complexity and cumbersomeness of their structure.
