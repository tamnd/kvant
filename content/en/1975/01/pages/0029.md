1. All integers are rational.
2. π is an integer.  (VI)
3. Therefore, π is a rational number.

1. All tigers are striped.
2. Shere Khan is a tiger.  (VII)
3. Therefore, Shere Khan is striped.

etc. - they all have a “general appearance”

1. All $x$ have the property $P$.
2. The item $y$ is $x$.  (VIII)
3. Therefore, $y$ has the property $P$.

But what can you say about the next series of examples\*)

1. Columbus discovered America.
2. There is a portrait of Columbus hanging on the wall.  (IX)
3. Consequently, there is a portrait of the discoverer of America hanging on the wall

1. Pushkin wrote "Eugene Onegin".
2. There is a portrait of Pushkin hanging on the wall.  (X)
3. Consequently, there is a portrait of the author of Onegin hanging on the wall.

1. Bulat Okudzhava wrote Hamlet.
2. There is a portrait of Okudzhava hanging on the wall.  (XI)
3. Consequently, there is a portrait of the author of Hamlet hanging on the wall.

1. Malyuta Skuratov created the theory of relativity.
2. There is a portrait of Malyuta hanging on the wall.  (XII)
3. Consequently, there is a portrait of the creator of the theory of relativity hanging on the wall.

So far everything is exactly the same, isn't it? (Of course, it is already clear to you that we have no need to discuss here the issue of the authorship of “Hamlet”, “Onegin” and the theory of relativity.) Is it also possible to include this “syllogism” in the same group:

1. Some man invented the samovar.
2. There is a portrait of a man hanging on the wall.  (XIII)
3. Consequently, a portrait of the inventor of the samovar hangs on the wall.

---

\*) Inspired (like most of this presentation in general) by the introduction to the book by A. Cherch “Introduction to Mathematical Logic”, vol. 1, translation from English, M., IL, 1960.

⟦column⟧

So much for “identity of form!” (But “formally”, it would seem, everything here is the same as in examples (I), (III), (V)-(VIII) - who can stop us, as before, from denoting the subjects in premises and conclusions through $x$ and $y$?)

Of course, there is no “mystery” here - you can easily explain that in Russian, proper nouns and common nouns with indefinite pronouns as subjects are far from equal (and in English, French and in general in all Germanic and Romance languages, such misunderstandings cannot arise with the correct use of definite and indefinite articles). But I would like to draw your attention not only to the simplicity and naturalness of the formulation of problems of formal logic, but also to the fact that its true “formalization” can be complicated by precisely those qualities of our spoken language that make it such a convenient, rich and flexible means of communication between people: the abundance of semantic shades of words and the absence of a rigid, “standard” structure (form) of sentences.

In modern formal logic \*) these difficulties are overcome by using special *formalized languages*. In these languages, as can be read in many popular books, all the inaccuracies and ambiguities of ordinary spoken languages ​​are carefully eliminated (of course, the language is impoverished in this case - but we are not going to write ordinary good prose in it, not to mention poetry). But, on the other hand, in all the essential features of their structure, these formalized languages ​​follow the laws well known to us all.

---

\*) Often (for obvious reasons) called *symbolic logic*, or *mathematical logic*.
