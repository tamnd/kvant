## WHERE DOES LOGIC START?
Yu. A. Gastev

1. All people are mortal. (I)
2. Socrates is a man.
3. Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

Almost every logic textbook at one time began with such an example of a *syllogism* (in a more understandable language - *inferences*). “Well, that’s all right,” says today’s schoolchild, who has not studied any such special logic. “However, the science is small: everything just hurts.”

Take your time!

Here’s another example for you (in Eugene Ionesco’s wonderful play “Rhinoceros” it is uttered by an unpleasant character called “the logician”):

1. All cats are mortal.
2. Socrates is mortal. (II)
3. Therefore, Socrates is a cat.

“What nonsense,” you say, “what kind of cat is he?” But is *that* why “syllogism” “logic” is bad?!

⟦column⟧

Please evaluate two more examples:

1. All cats are mortal.
2. Socrates is a cat. (III)
3. Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

1. All people are mortal.
2. Socrates is mortal. (IV)
3. Therefore, Socrates is a man.

So which example is “nonsense”? (III), where is the nonsense about the cat repeated again? Nothing of the kind! What is wrong is precisely the conclusion (IV), in which everything *individually* is true: both *premises* 1 and 2, and *conclusion* 3 - but the conclusion does not *follow* from the premises at all! And this is also why, from the point of view that interests us now, inference (II) differs from (I).

So, logic is interested not so much in itself in the truth and falsity of individual statements, but primarily in the connection between different

⟦folio 24⟧

true statements, laws (rules), according to which from some true statements one can with complete confidence obtain new true statements, that is, as they say, *norms of correct reasoning*.

So, it means that logic is still not indifferent to the question of *truth*? Of course not. This is essentially the most important question for her. But, unlike ordinary conversational practice, we will always strive for maximum accuracy and therefore will be forced to distinguish between several types of truth (respectively falsity).

The so-called *factual truth* of sentences, understood as “correspondence to reality,” is accepted in logic without any discussion and without interest in a way to verify it. The fact that the Volga flows into the Caspian Sea is true, but it is true purely in fact, and it is a matter of geography, not logic, to guarantee this truth. The statements “The Volga flows into Lake Michigan” and “The Amazon flows into the Klyazma” are, of course, false - but again, purely factually: this does not follow from any “laws of logic”. The propositions “If the Volga flows into the Caspian Sea, then the Volga flows into the Caspian Sea”, “If the Volga flows into Lake Michigan, then the Volga flows into Lake Michigan” and “If the Amazon flows into the Klyazma, then the Amazon flows into the Klyazma” - all three are equally *logically true*, that is, true, so to speak, solely by virtue of their structure, regardless of the truth or the falsity of their premises and conclusions. The truth of Newton's laws or Coulomb's law is confirmed by experience, but not by logic, from which none of the specific physical laws follows. The conclusion “The Greeks are people, Socrates is a Greek, therefore Socrates is a man,” on the contrary, is correct in itself, but

⟦column⟧

not by virtue of the truth of the first premise, certified by world history, or the second, stating a certain fact in the history of philosophy.

Strictly speaking, the previous phrase is very conditional: in this case we simply tacitly agree to mean *that* Socrates, and not, say, Chernyshevsky’s father-in-law (Nikolai Gavrilovich’s wife was named Olga Sokratovna) or a cat named “Socrates” (in the latter case, conclusion (II) would not, of course, become more legitimate, but in the correct conclusion (III) both premises would be true). Thus, the actual truth of statements often depends on a number of additional (not always explicitly stated) agreements and conditions, as a result of which in such cases we sometimes talk about the *possible truth* - as opposed to the *necessary truth* of statements like “12 is divisible by 3 or not” and “12 is divisible by 5 or not”, true without regard to our arithmetic knowledge and even to what number system the numbers 12, 3 and 5 are written in.

Thus, *logical truths* - unlike the experimental, factual truths of concrete sciences and everyday practice - *do not bring us any information about the real world*. As Leibniz said, these are *truths in all possible worlds*. Therefore, they are also called *always true*, or *identically true* statements. Or even shorter and more expressively: *tautologies*. This is what (literal translation from Greek: “the same word”) is called in everyday speech meaningless repetitions that do not tell us anything new. Take a closer look at logically true sentences like “From $A$ follows $A$”, “$B$ is true or $B$ is false”, “If statements $C$ and $D$ are true, then statements $D$ and $C$ are true”, etc., and you will agree that the term is very clear and relevant.

Not all tautologies (in the logical sense of the word) are so immediately obvious - sometimes their logical truth cannot be immediately discerned due to the purely external complexity and cumbersomeness of their structure.

Is it possible to achieve the same “level of fluency” in the question of the correctness of inferences and the logical truth of statements as when performing and checking ordinary arithmetic operations, no matter how complex the heaps of which now do not frighten us at all? Can. And it is precisely the identification of the rules that ensure such “fluency” that constitutes the primary task of logic as a science. Again, everything is the same as in arithmetic and algebra: the task is not to learn to “automatically” (that is, without special thinking) add $2+3$, $3+5$ or even $678\ 930\ 567+809\ 376\ 542$, etc., namely $a+b$ for *arbitrary* $a$ and $b$.

For algebra and arithmetic, two expressions that have the same form (see the previous paragraph) are in a certain sense equivalent - even if they have different “quantities”. Likewise, if we want to "algebraize" logic,

But what is the *form of a sentence* (statement)? If statements or their individual “elementary parts” are designated, as is customary in modern logic, (like ordinary algebra) by letters, then the identity (or difference) of their form is usually not difficult to discern. For example, the sentences “From *A* and *B* follows *A*” and “From *C* and *F* follows *C*,” differing only in the designation of “component,” are naturally considered equal, which cannot be asserted (in any case, without additional clarifications and agreements), for example, about the sentences “From *X* follows *Y*” and “From not-*Y* follows not-*X*.” But when we compare sentences in ordinary language, things are not always so simple. For example, conclusions (I), (III), as well as the following reasoning, clearly have the same form:

1. All integers are rational.
2. 7 is an integer.    (V)

Of course, there is no “mystery” here - you can easily explain that in Russian, proper nouns and common nouns with indefinite pronouns as subjects are far from equal (and in English, French and in general in all Germanic and Romance languages, such misunderstandings cannot arise with the correct use of definite and indefinite articles). But I would like to draw your attention not only to the simplicity and naturalness of the formulation of problems of formal logic, but also to the fact that its true “formalization” can be complicated by precisely those qualities of our spoken language that make it such a convenient, rich and flexible means of communication between people: the abundance of semantic shades of words and the absence of a rigid, “standard” structure (form) of sentences.

In modern formal logic \*) these difficulties are overcome by using special *formalized languages*. In these languages, as can be read in many popular books, all the inaccuracies and ambiguities of ordinary spoken languages ​​are carefully eliminated (of course, the language is impoverished in this case - but we are not going to write ordinary good prose in it, not to mention poetry). But, on the other hand, in all the essential features of their structure, these formalized languages ​​follow the laws well known to us all.

I would especially like to emphasize this circumstance, often left in the shadows by the authors of popular books, sometimes too persistently *contrasting* natural and artificial languages: failure to understand the deep kinship of these groups of languages ​​(with all their obvious differences) makes it very difficult to master the most basic, “elementary” concepts of modern logic.

Consideration of this “ABC of Logic” is very reminiscent, as you will see, of the syntax and morphology of ordinary Russian (or any other) language. This is partly why the term “*syntax*” often generally refers to the study of the *form* (structure) of formalized languages. These concepts fall into two main groups, the first of which very closely corresponds to the “parsing by sentence members” that you know well from school. Another part of the syntax of formalized languages ​​is very similar to that part of school grammar, which is devoted to the issue of the formation (construction) of complex sentences from simple ones. Unlike the usual scheme for studying grammatical concepts, it turns out to be more convenient to start with the second group of concepts - it is simpler and more elementary, so it is easy to consider independently of the first. Moreover, it is customary to construct a special language corresponding to this group of grammatical categories. This language, the basic “atomic” unit of which is the *statement* - that is, the sentence about which it makes sense to say that it is true or false - is called: *propositional calculus*. There is no “parsing according to the members of a sentence” in propositional calculus - for the simple reason that the internal structure of sentences = “atoms” is not considered here at all: “atoms” are still considered “indivisible”.

The next stage in the theory of formalized languages, at which, on the contrary, people are already interested in the “structure of the atom” and only with the help of which we will be able - but not this time - to complete the analysis of the above formal examples (I) - (XIII) - this is the so-called *predicate calculus*. Predicate calculus is usually structured in such a way that it includes propositional calculus; in the same way, when studying the structure of the atom in physics and chemistry, we do not need to forget what we learned earlier about the connection of atoms with each other.

So, propositional calculus. I repeat: a statement is any sentence (of natural or formalized language - it makes no difference) about which it makes sense to say that it is true or false. To speak, or rather even to ask, but not to answer at all. *Logic* itself, as we have said more than once, simply *does not have the means to answer* many *such questions* (although not all): logic (propositional calculus) is ready to reckon with the fact that the statements “The Volga flows into the Caspian Sea” and “$2 \times 2 = 4$” are true, and the statements “The Volga flows into the Indian Ocean” “$2 \times 2 = 5$” are false. But her conclusions do not depend in any way on these facts and would remain true if a geological catastrophe changed the course of the Volga or if we changed the usual writing of numbers, conventional, like any designations.

Moreover, propositional logic (as is often said instead of “propositional calculus”) can (and in fact does) do without answering the questions *what* “truth” and *what* “false” are. Instead, it is enough to simply talk about two different *truth values* (or *truth values*), without asking the question “what does this mean.” One of these meanings is usually called “selected” and denoted through “and” (from “true”), “*t*” (from “true”) or, for example, simply “1” (without any “natural analogies” with ordinary logical intuition), and the second is “unselected” -

⟦folio 29⟧

through “l” (“false”), “f” (“false”) or “0”. Each statement, like the statements of which it is composed (and on which, depending on the way they are connected, its meaning depends), can take on two different meanings, not necessarily called “truth” and “false” and associated with these concepts. Thus, propositional calculus can be understood as “*the algebra of logic*” - the study of functions that, like their arguments, take on two different meanings. These values ​​can (but, we repeat, not necessarily) be called truth and false. If we not only accept these names, but are also interested in their connection with the “ordinary” concepts of truth and falsehood (and try to clarify them), then we are engaged in *semantics*.

Otherwise, while remaining within the framework of pure *syntax*, we can completely forget about the origin of our “logical” terminology. Such “pure” propositional calculus is simply a section of *combinatorics*, and the “logical problems” considered in it are, in principle, no different from ordinary combinatorial problems.

So, in addition to the statements themselves, propositional calculus studies the various functions between them - the various ways of forming “complex” statements from “simple” ones. Within semantics, these functions are very reminiscent of ordinary conjunctions in Russian (or any other) language, with the help of which complex sentences are built from simple ones. But we will postpone this connection with ordinary language (and with “ordinary logic”) until the next conversation. Therefore, we will end our article with how such articles usually begin: by defining several “basic” such functions, or, as they are called, *logical operations*.

Since we are talking about functions that take, like their arguments, a finite number of values ​​(namely

two), we will be able to specify the functions we are interested in by explicitly indicating what values ​​they take for all possible distributions of argument values ​​(“simple constituent statements”). It is convenient to represent such definition tasks in the form of so-called *truth tables*, the “inputs” of which indicate the values ​​of the original statements, and the “outputs” (in the cells of the table itself) indicate the values ​​of the resulting statement.

These are the logical operations:

1) The *negation* of a true statement is false, and the negation of a false statement is true. This operation, denoted by the symbol “$\overline{\phantom{A}}|$”, corresponding to the particle “not” in Russian, is given by the following truth table:

| $A$ | $\overline{\phantom{A}}|\,A$ |
|---|---|
| And | l |
| l | And |

2) The *conjunction* of two true statements (corresponding to the conjunction “and” between them, denoted by $\wedge$) is true, but if at least one of them is false, it is false:

| $A$ | $B$ | $A \wedge B$ |
|---|---|---|
| And | And | And |
| And | l | l |
| l | And | l |
| l | l | l |

| $A$ | $B$ | $A \vee B$ |
