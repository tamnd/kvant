## ELLIPSE
I. N. Bronstein

Three remarkable curves - the ellipse, hyperbola and parabola, collectively called “conic sections” - were known back in Ancient Greece. They were discovered around 360 BC by Menaechmus, one of the students of the famous astronomer, physician and mathematician Eudoxus. The works of Menaechmus have not reached us, but we know the remarkable work of Apollonius, devoted to the same issue and written about 200 years after Menaechmus.
In this issue we begin a series of articles on conic sections.

**Conjugate diameters of the ellipse**

Let us note the following interesting property of the ellipse: *the midpoints of all parallel chords of the ellipse form a segment passing through the center of the ellipse* (Fig. 6). This property is obvious for a circle: for it, the midpoints of parallel chords lie on a diameter perpendicular to these chords. During compression, parallel chords of a circle turn into parallel chords of an ellipse, and the midpoints of the chords of a circle turn into the midpoints of the ellipse chords. This is where the validity of the formulated property follows.

The chord passing through the center of the ellipse is called its *diameter*. In an ellipse, the lengths of the diameters are, generally speaking, not the same. The largest of them is the major axis of the ellipse, and the smallest is the minor axis. If we draw a second series of chords in the ellipse parallel to the resulting diameter, then their midpoints lie on the diameter belonging to the first series of chords (Fig. 6). The two resulting diameters are called *mutually conjugate*. For each diameter of an ellipse there is a conjugate to it. In a circle, each pair of conjugate diameters is mutually perpendicular, but in an ellipse there is only one pair of mutually perpendicular conjugate diameters, these are the major and minor axes.

*The equation of a line* is such an equation for $x$ and $y$, which turns into an identity when substituting instead of $x$ and $y$ the values ​​$x_0$, $y_0$ if and only if the point with coordinates $x_0$, $y_0$ lies on this line. Let the given ellipse with semi-axes $a$ and $b$ be obtained as a PC of a circle of radius $a$ with a coefficient $k = \dfrac{b}{a}$ (Fig. 7). Let us introduce a coordinate system on the plane containing both of these lines as follows: the $Ox$ axis is directed along the PC axis, and the $Oy$ axis is directed through the center of the circle $o$, which is also the center of the ellipse, perpendicular to the $Ox$ axis. Let $m$ be a point on the circle, and $M$ be the point of the ellipse to which $m$ will go at RS.

We denote the coordinates of the point $m$ by $x_{\text{окр}}$, $y_{\text{окр}}$, and the coordinates of the points $M$ by $x_\text{э}$, $y_\text{э}$. By definition RS $x_\text{э} = x_{\text{окр}}$, $y_\text{э} = k y_{\text{окр}}$, from where

$$x_{\text{окр}} = x_\text{э}, \quad y_{\text{окр}} = \frac{y_\text{э}}{k} \, . \qquad (*)$$

If we substitute the coordinates of any point $(x_{\text{окр}},\ y_{\text{окр}})$ into the equation of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$, then the equality $x_{\text{окр}}^2 + y_{\text{окр}}^2 = a^2$ will be true, and, therefore, based on $(*)$ the equality will be true

$$x_\text{э}^2 + \frac{y_\text{э}^2}{k^2} = a^2.$$

Focal property of an ellipse

We move on to the most important property of the ellipse - focal (focal is an adjective from the word focus**); this is the name given to two replacement points lying on the major axis symmetrically relative to the center of the ellipse).

We will consider an ellipse as a section of a circular cylinder - we have seen that we always have the right to do this. Now let’s roll the ball (of the same radius as the cylinder) into the cylinder from both ends so that the ball hits the plane of this section (Fig. 9), in geometric language - we’ll fit the balls into the cylinder so that they touch the plane of the section.

The touching points $F_1$ and $F_2$ will be the focal points of our ellipse.

Each ball touches the cylinder in a circle (red). Let's take any point $M$ of the ellipse and mark the generator $PQ$ of the cylinder on which point $M$ lies ($P$ and $Q$ are the points of this generator lying on the red circles). It is clear that $MF_1 = MQ$, since these are the lengths of two tangents to the right ball drawn from $M$ to the points of contact

$$MF_1 + MF_2 = MQ + MP = PQ.$$

No length $PQ$ is the same for all generators of the cylinder, this is the distance between the planes of the red circles. This means that whatever point $M$ on the ellipse we take, the equality $MF_1 + MF_2 = const$ will satisfy.

Thus, the sum of the distances from any point of the ellipse to its two foci is a constant value. This is the focal property of the ellipse. An ellipse is often defined as a set of points lying in the same plane, for which the sum of the distances to two points (foci) is constant.

**Drawing an ellipse using a thread**

Place a sheet of paper on the drawing board and pin two buttons (or pins, Fig. 11) to it. Throwing a ring of thread over them (the length of the ring should be greater than twice the distance between the buttons), pull the thread with the point of a pencil to form a triangle $F_1MF_2$. If you move a pencil along the paper so that the thread remains taut, the triangle will be deformed, but its perimeter will remain constant. The length of the side $F_1F_2$ will also be constant; this means $F_1M + F_2M = \mathrm{const}$ and the pencil will describe the ellipse.
