Let us denote the radius of the wheel $R-r$ by $\rho$. The curves described during the indicated rolling by the points of the wheel boundary are called *epicycloids*, their type is determined by the relation $\dfrac{\rho}{r}$; curves described by the points of the hoop - *pericycloids*. Let us prove that with the relationship between the radii ($R=r+\rho$) specified in the condition, the pericycloids coincide with the epicycloids.

Let's fix one point on the wheel and on the hoop. Let at the initial moment the points observed on the wheel and the hoop coincide with the same point $A$ of the boundary of the fixed circle (Fig. 2). For definiteness, let both the wheel and the hoop roll in a circle counterclockwise. If at some moment the wheel touches a stationary circle at point $B$, then the point observed on its boundary (point of the epicycloid) occupies such a position $C$ that the lengths $\smile AB$ and $\smile BC$ are equal ($\smile BC$ is chosen taking into account the direction of rolling (Fig. 3)): this is the condition for rolling without sliding.

Similarly, the position of the point $C'$, observed on the hoop (pericycloid point), at the moment when it touches the fixed circle at the point $B'$, is found from the condition that the lengths $\smile AB'$ and $\smile B'C'$ are equal (taking into account the direction of rolling) (see figure 4).

(From our proof it will also be clear how to choose $B'$ by $B$.)

This solution can be briefly written using vectors. If you use “generalized angles” - angles of rotation $\varphi$, which can take any values ​​(not only in the range from 0 to $2\pi$), then you will not have to consider separately different cases: you only need to use that for any three vectors $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{c}$

$$\widehat{\mathbf{ab}} + \widehat{\mathbf{bc}} = \widehat{\mathbf{ac}}$$

(here $\widehat{\mathbf{ab}}$ is the magnitude of the angle between vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$).

Let $\mathbf{e}\,(\varphi)$ be a vector of length 1 resulting from a clockwise rotation through angle $\varphi$ from the initial vector $\mathbf{e}_0 = \mathbf{e}\,(0)$ (say upward). Then $\mathbf{e}\,(\varphi + \pi) = -\mathbf{e}\,(\varphi)$.

For point $M_1$ of the wheel (Fig. 7, a)

$$\overrightarrow{OO_1} = (\rho + r)\,\mathbf{e}\,(\varphi),$$

$$\overrightarrow{O_1M_1} = \rho\mathbf{e}\,(\pi + \varphi + \varphi_1).$$

Since $\rho\varphi_1 = r\varphi$ (rolling condition without sliding), $\rho = R - r$, then

$$\overrightarrow{OM_1} = \overrightarrow{OO_1} + \overrightarrow{O_1M_1} = R\mathbf{e}\,(\varphi) - (R - r)\,\mathbf{e}\left(\frac{R}{R-r}\,\varphi\right). \quad (1)$$

For point $M_2$ of the hoop (Fig. 7, b)

$$\overrightarrow{OO_2} = (R - r)\,\mathbf{e}\,(\psi + \pi),$$

$$\overrightarrow{O_2M_2} = R\,\cdot\,\mathbf{e}\,(\psi - \psi_1),$$

and since $R\psi_1 = r\psi$, then

$$\overrightarrow{OM_2} = \overrightarrow{OO_2} + \overrightarrow{Q_2M_2} = R\mathbf{e}\left(\frac{R-r}{R}\,\psi\right) - (R - r)\,\mathbf{e}\,(\psi). \quad (2)$$

Comparing (1) and (2), we see that $\overrightarrow{OM_1} = \overrightarrow{OM_2}$ at $\dfrac{R}{R-r}\varphi = \psi$. This means that the set of points $M_1$ and $M_2$ coincide.

**M263.** Given two numbers $p$ and $q$, greater than 1. On the sides $BC$ and $DC$ of the rectangle $ABCD$ the points $P$ and $Q$ are taken so that $|BC| = p\cdot|BP|$ and $|DC| = q\cdot|DQ|$. At what ratio of the lengths of the sides $AB$ and $AD$ will the angle $PAQ$ have the greatest value? What is this largest value in the special case $p = 2$, $q = \dfrac{3}{2}$?

⟦column⟧

Following the letter from *Boris and Lev Rabinovich*, who proposed this problem, let us immediately consider a board of size $n\times n$ and prove that it is impossible to arrange more than $\dfrac{4n}{3}$ rooks on it as required in the condition.

Let $k$ rooks be located on the board $n\times n$ subject to the condition. On each square where the rook is located, we will write the number 0. In each of the $n$ columns we will perform the following operation: if there are two numbers in the column, then we will add 1 to both, if one is a number, then we will add 2 to it (we will not write anything in the empty column). Then we will do exactly the same operation with each line. It is clear that in place of each of the $k$ rooks the result will be written a number not less than 3 - namely, either 3 or 4 - therefore the sum $S$ of all written numbers is not less than $3k$; on the other hand, since we added at most 2 to each of the $n$ columns and then to each of the $n$ rows, the sum of $S$ is at most $4n$. So, $3k \leqslant S \leqslant 4n$, where $k \leqslant \dfrac{4n}{3}$ comes from.

In particular, for $n = 8$ we obtain $k < \dfrac{32}{3}$, that is, $k \leqslant 10$. An example of a 10 rook arrangement that satisfies the condition is shown in figure 8.

It is easy to check that for any natural number $n$ in the same way it is possible to place $\left[\dfrac{4n}{3}\right]$ rooks on the board $n\times n$ subject to the condition ($[x]$ means the integer part of the number $x$).

Let's move on to the queen placement problem. It is clear that, subject to the conditions of the problem, one can place no more queens than rooks. figure 9 shows that 10 queens can be placed on board $8\times 8$. So, in both problems a) and b) the answer is the same: 10.

As for the generalization of the Rabinovich problem on the arrangement of queens for the board $n\times n$, none of the readers completely solved it. We can verify that for $n = 3, 4, 5$ the largest number of queens that can be placed on the board $n\times n$ so that each gets hit by at most one blow is one less than $\left[\dfrac{4n}{3}\right]$. But for large $n$ the solution of the problem apparently requires either a very large search of options or the use of additional considerations.

Before proceeding with the solution, we note that in the problem statement the reference to figure 10 is *essential* - it is part of the condition; so, for example, for the city shown in figure 11 (with one blue and $n$ green squares), all the conditions of problem I are met, but the statement is incorrect: it is impossible to drive from part I to part II.

Let us now move on to solving our problem. Let's split the proof into two parts: we prove that

a) from any green square you can get to the blue one;

b) from the blue square you can get to any green square.

Let's agree to indicate the direction of movement on the streets with arrows.

a) Suppose that it is impossible to get from the green square $A_1$ to the blue square. Since it is possible to leave square $A_1$, then from it, driving along one street, we will reach the green square $A_2$. From square $A_2$ you also cannot get to the blue square. Having left it, we will reach square $A_3$, then $A_4$, etc. Since there are only $n$ green squares, having driven along $n$ streets, we will return to square $A_1$ and make sure that the arrows in the city are placed as in figure 12. But then you cannot come to the blue square, which is a contradiction conditions of the task.

b) Let's change the directions of all the arrows. The resulting motion scheme satisfies the conditions of the problem. Therefore, as shown in point a), with this arrangement of arrows you can get from any green square to the blue one. But this means that with the old arrangement of arrows, you can get from the blue square to any green one.

The problem of B. Rozenshtein about a city with one-way traffic can be generalized as follows. Let there be no “dead ends” in the city (in this case, you can arrange directions on the streets so that you can come to each square and leave from each). A necessary and sufficient condition is that with any such arrangement of arrows from each square one can get to any other; can be formulated as follows: *any two circular routes* (through a city on the streets of which there are no arrows yet) *must pass through the same square.* Prove this.

**M267.** *In the sequence of triplets of integers (2, 3, 5) (6, 15, 10) . . . each triple is obtained from the previous one in this way: the first number is multiplied by the second, the second by the third, and the third by the first, and the resulting products give a new triple.*

*Prove that none of the numbers obtained in this way will be a power of an integer: square, cube, etc.*

Note that the order of the numbers within the triple is not significant. The numbers included in the next three do not depend on it.

Let us prove that a triple with number $2l + 1$ has the form $\{2^k{}^+{}^13^k5^k,\ 2^k3^k{}^+{}^15^k,\ 2^k3^k5^k{}^+{}^1\}$, and with number $2l$ - $\{2^m{}^-{}^13^m5^m,\ 2^m3^m{}^-{}^15^m,\ 2^m3^m5^m{}^-{}^1\}$, where $k$ and $m$ depend on $l$. Let's use mathematical induction for this. Suppose that the triples numbered $2l$ and $2l + 1$ have this form. It is easy to see that from the triple $\{a,\ b,\ c\}$ after two steps we get the triple $\{a^2bc,\ ab^2c,\ abc^2\}$. Substituting here instead of $a$, $b$ and $c$ the elements of the $2l$th and $2l + 1$th triples, we get

$$\{2^{4m-2}3^{4m-1}5^{4m-1},\ 2^{4m-1}3^{4m-2}5^{4m-1},\ 2^{4m-1}3^{4m-1}5^{4m-2}\}$$

and $\{2^{4k}{}^+{}^23^{4k}{}^+{}^15^{4k}{}^+{}^1,\ 2^{4k}{}^+{}^13^{4k}{}^+{}^25^{4k}{}^+{}^1,\ 2^{4k}{}^+{}^13^{4k}{}^+{}^15^{4k}{}^+{}^2\}$

respectively, that is, triples of the same type. Since the first and second triples satisfy the induction hypothesis, all triples satisfy it.

So, it has been proven that all numbers included in triplets have the form $p_1^{n\pm1} \cdot p_2^n \cdot p_3^n$, where $p_1$, $p_2$ and $p_3$ are the numbers 2, 3 and 5, taken in any sequence. Obviously, none of them can be a power of a natural number.

**F274.** *The diode is connected to the circuit shown in figure 18, a. The idealized current-voltage characteristic of the diode is shown in figure 18, b. The capacitor is not pre-charged. Key K is closed. How much heat will be released at resistance R when charging the capacitor? Capacitance of the capacitor C, e. d.s. source E. The internal resistance of the source is negligible.*

⟦column⟧

When a bullet decelerates, its kinetic energy turns into heat. To estimate the maximum temperature to which the gas in the cylinder can be heated, we will assume that all the released heat goes to change the internal energy of the gas. That is, we will not take into account energy losses due to heating of the bullet and cylinder walls. Then we can write

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \Delta U .$$

Since argon is a monatomic gas, then $U = \frac{3}{2} nRT$ ($n$ is the number of moles of gas) and

$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} nRT_{\max} - \frac{3}{2} nRT_0 = \frac{3}{2} nR (T_{\max} - T_0).$$

The value $n$ can be found from the equation of state of an ideal gas:

$$n = \frac{p_0 V_0}{R T_0} .$$

That's why

$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}\, p_0 V_0\, \frac{T_{\max} - T_0}{T_0} .$$

Hence,

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{3}{2}\, p_0 V_0\, \frac{T_{\max} - T_0}{T_0} ,$$

where

$$T_{\max} = T_0 \left( 1 + \frac{mv^2}{3 p_0 V_0} \right) \approx 30\,000\ ^\circ\mathrm{K} .$$

After the key is closed, current will flow through the circuit. The voltage across the capacitor will increase and the current will correspondingly decrease. In this case, until the current decreases to the value $I_0$, the voltage on the diode will always be equal to $U_0$ \*). Let the current decrease to $I_0$ in time $t_1$. According to the law of conservation of energy, the work $A$ performed by the source in the entire circuit is equal to the sum of the work on individual sections of the circuit:

$$A = A_{\mathrm{д}} + A_{\mathrm{c}} + A_R.$$

The voltage across the capacitor at time $t = t_1$ is

$$U_{\mathrm{c}} = E - U_0 - I_0 R,$$

The work done by the source during this time is

$$A_1 = q_1 E = CE (E - U_0 - I_0 R).$$

The work on the sections containing the diode and capacitor is equal

$$A_{\mathrm{д}} = q_1 U_0 = C U_0 (E - U_0 - I_0 R),$$

$$A_{\mathrm{c}} = W_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{q_1^2}{2C} = \frac{C (E - U_0 - I_0 R)^2}{2} .$$

where $\Delta W_c$ is the change in the energy of the capacitor, and $Q = Q_2 + Q_r$ is the amount of heat released at resistances $R$ and $r$.

When the current in the circuit stops, the voltages across the diode and across the resistance $R$ will be zero, so the voltage across the capacitor will be $E$ and the charge will be $q' = CE$. Therefore, during the time $t_2$ a charge passed through the circuit

$q_2 = q' - q_1 = CE - C\,(E - U_0 - I_0 R) = C\,(U_0 + I_0 R)$.

Then

$$A_2 = q_2 E = CE\,(U_0 + I_0 R),$$

$$\Delta W_c = \frac{q'^2}{2C} - \frac{q_1^2}{2C} = \frac{CE^2}{2} - \frac{C}{2}(E - U_0 - I_0 R)^2$$

And

$$Q = A_2 - \Delta W_c = \frac{C}{2}(U_0 + I_0 R)^2.$$

Resistances $r$ and $R$ are connected in series, that is, at any time the same current flows through these resistances. Therefore, the amounts of heat $Q_2$ and $Q_r$ released at the resistances $R$ and $r$ at the same time are related as resistance values, that is

$$\frac{Q_2}{Q_r} = \frac{R}{r}.$$

Knowing the sum of $Q_2$ and $Q_r$ and their ratio, we can find $Q_2$:

$$Q_2 = \frac{R}{R+r}\,Q = \frac{R}{R+r}\;\frac{C\,(U_0 + I_0 R)^2}{2}.$$

Then finally

$$Q_R = Q_1 + Q_2 = \frac{C\left[(E-U_0)^2 - (I_0R)^2\right]}{2} +$$
$$+ \frac{R}{R+r}\;\frac{C\,(U_0 + I_0 R)^2}{2}.$$
