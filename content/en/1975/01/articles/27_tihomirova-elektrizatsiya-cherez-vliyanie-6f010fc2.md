## Electrification through influence
V. A. Tikhomirova

⟦figure⟧

Can two similarly charged bodies attract each other?

Is it possible to use one charged body to charge another body so that its charge is greater than the charge of the first body?

To answer these questions, let's look at one interesting physical phenomenon - the phenomenon of electrostatic induction, or, in other words, the electrification of bodies through influence.

Let's consider this experience. An uncharged metal ball $B$ (Fig. 1) is brought to the ball $A$, charged with a positive charge $q_A$. In this case, charges appear on the surface of the ball $B$: negative on the side closest to the ball $A$, and positive on the far side. This can be clearly seen if you touch the electroscope ball

corresponding areas of the surface of the ball $B$. It is not difficult to explain this distribution of charges. The fact is that free electrons, which were previously evenly distributed throughout the entire ball $B$, under the influence of the charges of the ball $A$ come into motion and are redistributed throughout the ball so that there are more of them on the left than on the right. Thus, a negative charge $-q_B$ is induced, that is, induced, on the left side of the ball, and a positive charge $+q_B$ is induced on the right side. This is the phenomenon of electrostatic induction. Let us note by the way that in our case the value of the induced charge $q_B$ is, of course, less than the value of the induced charge $q_A$, since the influence of the induced charge also extends to other bodies surrounding it, where

Electric force always acts between charged bodies. The same will happen with balls $A$ and $B$. More precisely, in this case, two forces arise - the attractive force $F_{\text{пр}}$ between the ball $A$ and the left part of the ball $B$ and the repulsive force $F_{\text{от}}$ between the ball $A$ and the right part of the ball $B$ (Fig. 2). Since the negatively charged part is closer to the ball $A$, the force $F_{\text{пр}}$ is greater than the force $F_{\text{от}}$ and as a result the balls will be attracted to each other by the net force $F = F_{\text{пр}} - F_{\text{от}}$.

What will change if the ball $B$ is first charged with some charge $q'_B$, also positive? Of course, all the previous arguments about the induction of charges remain valid, but now we must also take into account the interaction of charges $q_A$ and $q'_B$. Another force $F'_{\text{от}}$ appears - the force of repulsion of charges $q_A$ and $q'_B$. The final result will depend on the relationship between the magnitudes of the forces $F$ and $F'_{\text{от}}$, or rather, on the relationship between the magnitudes of the charges $q_B$ and $q'_B$, since the greater the magnitude of the charge, the greater the force of the electrical interaction.

If the magnitude of the induced charge $q_B$ turns out to be greater than the magnitude of the charge $q'_B$ present on the ball, then as a result, similarly charged balls will still be attracted.

⟦column⟧

When is this possible? We have already said that the magnitude of the induced charge on the conductor is less than the magnitude of the influencing charge. Therefore, only if the charge $q_A$ is much larger than the charge $q'_B$, it may turn out that the magnitude of the charge $q_B$ will be greater than the charge $q'_B$.

So, two similarly charged bodies can attract each other, but for this to happen, the charge of one of them must be much greater than the charge of the other.

We have shown that, under the influence of a charged body, electric charges can be induced on a conductor, but the conductor as a whole remains electrically neutral (Fig. 3). Is it possible to really charge it so that the total charge is not zero? It turns out it is possible, and even quite simple. To do this, it is enough to ground the conductor in the presence of a charged body for some time, and then remove both the grounding and the charged body (Fig. 4). In this case, the sign of the charge of the conductor will be opposite to the sign of the charge of the body. Indeed, when grounding, free electrons, under the influence of a positive charge on the body, come from the ground and remain on the conductor, charging it as a whole negatively.

The magnitude of the induced charge can be significantly increased, almost bringing it closer to the magnitude of the induced charge, if the charged body is placed inside a long metal

a metal cylinder (or ball) with a small hole. In this case, the influence of the charged body on other bodies is almost excluded, therefore, on the inner surface of the cylinder (or ball) a charge will be induced, almost equal in magnitude to the charge of the ball, but opposite in sign, and on the outer surface - the same in magnitude and sign. If a charged body touches the inner wall of the cylinder, charges of opposite sign are neutralized, and charges of the same sign will remain on the cylinder as were on the charged body (Fig. 5). Thus, it is possible to transfer almost completely the charge from one body to another.

Now let's try to answer the second question posed at the very beginning. Let us have: a charged ball $A$, an uncharged ball $B$, a hollow metal ball and an electroscope on which we want to accumulate a charge greater than the charge of the ball $A$.

First of all, let’s charge the ball $B$ by induction (naturally, with a charge of the opposite sign). Let's bring ball $B$ inside the grounded ball for a while, and then remove both the grounding and ball $B$. A charge will remain on the ball, equal in magnitude to the charge of the ball $B$, but opposite in sign, that is, coinciding with the sign of the charge of the ball $A$.
