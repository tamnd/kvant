⟦folio 31⟧

Conic sections

In the articles “Ellipse”, “Hyperbola” and “Parabola”*) these curves were defined in different ways: the first - as a result of stretching or rolling a circle, and the second and third - as graphs of certain functions. Each of them has its own focal property, which is often taken to be the definition of a curve. Let us recall these “focal definitions” (it is assumed that all points under consideration for each line lie in the same plane):

An ellipse is the set of all points, the sum of the distances of each of them to two points (foci) is the same.

A hyperbola is the set of all points, the difference in the distances of each of them to two points (foci) is the same.

A parabola is the set of all points equally distant from a point (focus) and from a line that does not pass through it (director).

The ancient Greek mathematician Menaechmus, who discovered these curves, defined them differently: as sections of a circular cone by a plane perpendicular to one of the generatrices. He called the resulting curves sections of an acute-angled, rectangular and myno-angled cone, depending on the angle of the cone. The first, as we see below, is an ellipse, the second is a paraboly, the third is one branch of a hyperbola (Fig. 1, a, b, and c). The names "Ellipse", "Hyperbola" and "Parabola" were introduced by Apollo*. Before us we should receive (7 books out of 8) Apollo’s essay “On Conchex Sections”. In this work, Apollonius considers both halves of the cone (Fig. 2) and intersects the cone with planes that are not necessarily perpedicular to one of the generatrix.

Theorem. If the section plane intersects all the generatrices of the cone (only one of its half), then the intersection line (red line on figure 2) is an ellipse;

if the plane is parallel to one generatrix (and can intersect one floor), then the intersection line (blue) is a parabola;

if the plane is parallel to two generators (we intersect both floors), then the intersection line (black) is an eperbola.

An elegant proof of this theorem was proposed in 1822 by the Selgian engineer Dandel, who used spheres that are now pleasantly called Dandelen spheres.
