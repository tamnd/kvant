⟦folio 34⟧

The plane П intersects the circular cone parallel to the generatrix $St$. Let us inscribe a sphere tangent to the plane P into the cone. The point of tangency $F$ is the focus. The cone and the sphere are in contact along a circle $k$, the plane I of which intersects P along a straight line $d$. This is the director.

To prove this, let us draw a plane II parallel to I through the point $M$ lying on the generatrix $SHM$ of the cone. It will intersect the cone along the second circle $k'$. Then we lower the perpendicular $MD$ from $M$ to the straight line $d$. It is easy to see that it is parallel to the generatrix $St$ and that the figure $MDEB$ is a parallelogram. We have: $MF = MH$ (as two tangents from the point $M$ to the sphere); $MH = BE$ (as two generatrices of a truncated cone limited by planes I and II); $BE = MD$ (like opposite sides of a parallelogram). From the equality $MF = MD$ for any point $M$ of the section it follows that this section is a parabola.

**Directional property of conic sections. Eccentricity**

The focal property of a parabola can be given the following formulation: the ratio $\dfrac{MF}{MD}$ of the distances of any point of the parabola to the focus $F$ and to the directrix $d$ (Fig. 5) is a constant value (equal to unity).

A similar property also holds for the ellipse and hyperbola.

*In the plane of an ellipse (hyperbola) for each focus $F$ there is such a straight line $d$ (directrix) that the ratio of the distances from any point $M$ of the ellipse (hyperbola) to the focus $F$ and to the directrix $d$ is a constant value.* It is called the eccentricity of the ellipse (hyperbolas) and is denoted by the Greek letter $\varepsilon$ (epsilon). For a parabola, therefore, $\varepsilon = 1$; from what follows it turns out that for an ellipse $\varepsilon$ is less than 1, and for a hyperbola $\varepsilon$ is more than 1. A parabola has one directrix, and an ellipse (hyper-

Let us prove this property, for example, for an ellipse. The directrixes $d_1$ and $d_2$ are the lines of intersection of plane P with planes I and II (see Fig. 3). For an ellipse, they are located outside the cone and, therefore, outside the curve. From the similarity of triangles $MD_1P_1$ ($MD_1 \perp d_1$) and $MD_2P_2$ ($MD_2 \perp d_2$) we have: $MP_1 : MD_1 = MP_2 : MD_2$. Let us denote these relations by $\varepsilon$. Since $MP_1 = MF_1$, $MP_2 = MF_2$, then $MF_1 = \varepsilon \cdot MD_1$, $MF_2 = \varepsilon \cdot MD_2$. Adding these equalities, we get

$$MF_1 + MF_2 = \varepsilon (MD_1 + MD_2) = \varepsilon \cdot D_1D_2.$$

But the left side, according to the focal property of the ellipse, is constant and equal to $2a$, the length $D_1D_2$ (the distance between the directrixes) is also constant. So, $\varepsilon = \mathrm{const}$.

The reasoning is similar for a hyperbola.
