Indeed, all experiments seemed to indicate that an electron is a particle charged with negative electricity and behaving as particles should. We already know what this means. First, electrons can be counted individually (for example, by measuring the total charge and dividing it by the charge of one electron); no one has ever observed half an electron. Secondly, each electron in the beam is characterized by its own trajectory, which can be changed by influencing the beam with an electric or magnetic field. The moment an electron hits the target can be detected by a flash in the luminescent coating of the target. This flash is almost point-like, which means that the electron affects only a very small area of ​​the target. Consequently, we can talk about the coordinate of the electron, about its trajectory, that is, about everything that characterizes it as a particle.

It was on the basis of the concept of the electron as a particle that the so-called planetary model of the atom, proposed by Rutherford, arose. In this model, the atom was considered as a miniature Solar system, in which “planets” - electrons - move in orbit around the “Sun” - the atomic nucleus. The dimensions of the atom, that is, the radii of electron orbits, were soon found out. They turned out to be on the order of $10^{-8}$ cm, or one angstrom. There were differences of opinion regarding the size of the electron itself, but everyone agreed that its radius should, of course, be less than $10^{-8}$ cm. After all, an electron is a part of an atom, and a part, in any case, cannot be larger than the whole. But in 1927, American physicists Davisson and Germer carried out their famous experiments with electron beams. And it immediately became clear that the view of electrons as small

such hard balls, to put it mildly, is naive. The nature of the electron turned out to be much more complex.

We will examine here only one of the experiments of Davisson and Germer. A parallel beam of electrons was directed to the crystal face and reflected from it. *The reflected beam remained parallel* (as in the case of reflection of visible light from a mirror). That's it. At first glance, there is nothing surprising in this fact. After all, we know that under certain conditions particles can experience specular reflection from a solid surface. But we must not forget that “from the point of view of the electron” the surface of a crystal is by no means a continuous surface. A crystal is made up of atoms separated by gaps of empty space. The dimensions of the gaps are approximately equal to the dimensions of the atoms themselves and amount to a value of the order of $10^{-8}$ cm. And electrons are small particles, their radius is much smaller than $10^{-8}$ cm. This means that when an electron beam collides with a crystal, a situation should occur where the particle sizes are smaller than the sizes of irregularities. Therefore, the reflection of electrons must be diffuse.

Thus, either electrons must be considered to be very large balls, much larger than the atoms themselves (then they would be reflected specularly from the crystal), or... or electrons do not obey the laws of classical mechanics and are not particles in the usual sense of the word. The first possibility leads to obvious nonsense: a large ball cannot orbit inside a small atom. This means that there remains a second possibility, no matter how incredible it may seem.

Only waves whose length is greater than the distance between the atoms in the crystal can be specularly reflected from the surface of a crystal. Therefore, we must assume that the electron beam behaves like a wave.
