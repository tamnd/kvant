$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}.$$

Note that the quantity $h$ in this formula is the famous Planck constant, equal to $6{,}62 \cdot 10^{-27}$ $эрг \cdot с$. It connects the frequency $\nu$ of oscillations of the electromagnetic field with the energy $E$ of its quantum (photon) according to Planck’s formula: $E = h\nu$, or the wavelength with the momentum of the photon: $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}$. De Broglie's idea arose from the assumption of symmetry in nature. The theory showed that waves have in some sense the properties of particles. Couldn't particles, in turn, have wave properties? De Broglie's hypothesis is that these relationships apply equally to both photons and particles of matter. Numerous experiments carried out subsequently confirm this.

The Planck and de Broglie formulas connect the mechanical characteristics of particles (momentum, energy) with their wave characteristics (wavelength and frequency). The extremely small (from the point of view of the scales we are used to) value of Planck’s constant shows that wave effects in the movement of particles become significant only in the microworld. Indeed, a ping-pong ball of mass 1 $г$ traveling at speed 1 $м/с$ has a de Broglie wavelength equal to $6{,}6 \cdot 10^{-29}$ $см$. This is a fantastically short length, trillions of times smaller than the size of an atomic nucleus.

⟦column⟧

It is absolutely clear that no wave effects (for example, diffraction) can be detected in the motion of such a body. In contrast, for an electron in an atom, the de Broglie wavelength is approximately $10^{-8}$ $см$ ($m \approx 9 \times 10^{-28}$ $г$, $v \approx 10^{8}$ $см/с$), that is, on the order of the size of the atom itself in which the electron is moving. Under these conditions, the wave behavior of the electron plays a decisive role. All the specifics of atomic phenomena and, in particular, their quantum nature are associated precisely with this wave-like behavior of electrons. We will now consider the simplest example showing how the discreteness of the properties of an atom follows from the wave nature of the electron.

Under normal conditions, electrons are held within the atom by forces of electrostatic attraction and cannot move too far away from the nucleus. Thus, the electrons belonging to an atom move in a limited region of space. These are usually called bound electrons. On the contrary, electrons in a beam created by an electron gun can, in principle, fall into any region of space, and therefore are called free. The example in question captures precisely this feature of the atomic electron: the limited region of space available for its movement.

For simplicity of reasoning, let us imagine that the electron can only move along a certain straight line and does not go beyond the boundaries of the segment $AB$ (Fig. 4). Let's say that at points $A$ and $B$ there are solid walls through which the electron cannot penetrate. Such a system is a one-dimensional model of an atom. Of course, atoms are much more complex, and our example does not pretend to be a quantitative correspondence to a real atom. But the main qualitative property of atoms is the existence of discrete energy levels.
