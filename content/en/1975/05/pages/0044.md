Let us now turn to the flow of water through a hole in the wall of the vessel.

Let us take a cylindrical vessel filled with water, in the side wall of which there is a small hole of area $s_1$ (Fig. 3). Through this hole, water flows out under the pressure of a liquid column of height $H$. The hole is considered small if its dimensions are at least ten times smaller than the height of the column $H$ creating the pressure. In this case, all points of the small hole are located at approximately the same depth from the surface of the liquid, and the flow rates at all points can be considered the same.

A hole in a thin wall is a hole whose edges are sharp enough so that the wall thickness does not affect the shape and conditions of the jet. This condition is met when the edge thickness is less than three hole diameters.

*) Flow rate is the amount of water flowing out per unit time.

What determines the speed at which water flows through a small hole in the thin wall of a vessel? To answer this question, we use the law of conservation of energy.

Let a small amount of water $\Delta m$ flow out of the vessel in a short period of time $\Delta t$. The potential energy of water in the vessel decreased by values ​​$\Delta mgH$ (the time is short enough to consider the level $H$ of water in the vessel not changing). This change in potential energy is equal to the kinetic energy of the mass of water $\Delta m$ flowing out of the hole at a speed of $v$, that is, $\frac{\Delta mv^2}{2} = \Delta mgH$. From here

$$v = \sqrt{2gH}.$$

How long will it take for water to flow out of the vessel? Obviously, this depends on the flow rate and the area of ​​the hole. Let's look at speed first.

As we have already shown, $v = \sqrt{2gH}$. But this speed can be considered constant only over a short period of time (as long as $H = const$ can be considered). As the liquid flows out, the level rises, and consequently, the flow rate decreases from $v_0 = \sqrt{2gH}$ (at the initial moment of time $t = 0$) to $v_T = 0$ (the flow out).
