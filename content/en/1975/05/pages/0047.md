EXIT
into space

I. F. Sharyzin

Try solving the following puzzle: make four regular triangles out of six matches so that each side has a whole match. Attempts to solve it do not seem to lead to success. Moreover, it can be proven that such a construction is not feasible on a plane (try this). How can this be? It turns out that you need to go out into space and put together the correct tetrahedр (Fig.1) from matches. Impossible on a plane but feasible in three-dimensional space!

Entering space was also useful in solving some planimetric problems. A classic example is Desagrag's theorem. If two triangles $A_1B_1C_1$ and $A_2B_2C_2$ are located on a plane so that lines $A_1A_2$, $B_1B_2$, $C_1C_2$ intersect at one point, then at the point of intersection of lines $A_1B_1$ and $A_2B_2$, $B_1C_1$ and $B_2C_2$, $C_1A_1$ and $C_2A_2$ are located on the same straight line (Fig. 1).

The statement of the theorem becomes obvious if you see a spatial figure on figure 2, namely a trihedral angle intersected by two planes: one plane intersects the edges of the trihedral angle at points $A_1$, $B_1$ and $C_1$, and the other at points $A_2$, $B_2$ and $C_2$. At the intersection points of the corresponding sides of triangles $A_1B_1C_1$ and $A_2B_2C_2$ belong to the lines of intersection of these planes and, therefore, lie on the same straight line.
