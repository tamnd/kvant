Now the knight eats both f-pawns and then takes the g3 rook with checkmate (at this moment the black queen is on h1). So, with a queen on the white square h1, the knight declares mate from the black square g3. Since in this ending the queen changes the color of the square on each move, and the knight always has this property (see on this subject «Kvant», 1971, No. 9), then at the moment the knight appears, the squares on which the queen and knight stand must have different colors. That's why on the first move White moved his pawn just one square!

## To the article “Mathematical games on a chessboard”
(see «Kvant», 1975, no. 4)

1. White loses (and wins in giveaways) regardless of the turn of the move.

a) 1. d8K!, and this knight quickly sacrifices itself;

b) any move of the black knight is followed by d8С!, and the bishop soon comes under attack from the knight.

2. 1. Kpe4! Qd8! (otherwise the white king will come under attack already on the second move) 2. Kpd4!, and on the next move the king commits suicide.

4. In the table on figure 6, the first and third digits in the margins are the numbers of the verticals and horizontals passing through them, the second digit is the number of the diagonal parallel to the line h1 - a8. If the queens do not threaten each other, then on the eight squares occupied by them, all the left numbers are different and, therefore, form a complete set of numbers 1,2,...,8. The same goes for the second and third digits. It follows from this that the sum of all 24 digits written on the squares with queens is equal to $(1+2+...+8)\cdot3=108$. From figure it is clear that the sum of the digits of each field is divisible by 8, that is, the found sum must also be divisible by 8. However, 108 is not divisible by 8 - a contradiction!
