In a short period of time $\Delta t$ a mass of water $\Delta m = \rho v_1 s \Delta t$ flows out of the vessel (ρ is the density of the liquid; $\Delta t$ it was assumed that during this time the speed $v_1$ is constant). During this time, the water level in the vessel will decrease by $\Delta h = \frac{\Delta m}{S}$ (S is the area of ​​the bottom of the vessel). Therefore, at the next moment of time ($t_2 = \Delta t$) the outflow velocity is less than $v_1$ and equal to $v_1 - \Delta v = \sqrt{2g(H - \Delta h)}$. Let's square the last equality:

$$v_1^2 - 2v_1 \Delta v + (\Delta v)^2 = 2gH - 2g\Delta h.$$

Neglecting the values ​​$(\Delta v)^2$ and taking into account that $v_1^2 = 2gH$, we obtain

$$v_1 \Delta v = g \rho v_1 \frac{s}{S} \Delta t, \quad \Delta v = g \rho \frac{s}{S} \Delta t.$$

Hence,

$$\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = g \rho \frac{s}{S} = \text{const}$$

(of course, for the given $s$, $S$ and $\rho$). This means that the exhaust velocity depends linearly on time. Therefore, we can assume that during the entire time $T$, while the liquid flows out, the outflow velocity is equal to the average value (for the time interval $T$), that is, equal to

$$v_{cp} = \frac{v_1 + 0}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{gH}{2}}.$$

Now let's see how the area of ​​the hole affects the time it takes for water to flow out. In a unit of time, a mass of liquid $Q = \rho s v_{cp}$ should flow out of the vessel (ρ is the density of the liquid). In this case, we believe that the speed at a given moment in time at all points of the hole is directed strictly perpendicular to the cross-sectional plane of the hole. However, if it flows from a small round hole in a thin wall, then the diameter of its cross section is less than the diameter of the hole. The jet seems to be disappearing. The compression of the jet occurs due to the fact that the liquid flows to the hole from all sides and its particles, moving towards the hole along the walls of the section, continue to align with the axis of the scab. Only at a certain distance from the edge of the hole, approximately equal to the radius of the hole, do the particle trajectories become almost parallel. The compression of the jet continues further, but now incomparably slower, imperceptibly for the eyes. The section at which the sharp visible compression of the scab ends is called compressed. The ratio of the area of ​​the compressed section $s_2$ to the cross-sectional area of ​​the hole $s_1$ is called the compression ratio $\varepsilon$:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{s_2}{s_1}.$$

Since the distance between sections $s_1$ and $s_2$ is small (as we have already said, it is approximately equal to the radius of section $s_1$), then the speed of water at all points of section $s_2$ is practically equal to $\sqrt{2gh}$ (h is the height of the water level in the vessel at a given moment in time) and is perpendicular to the section plane. Therefore, water consumption

$$Q = s_2 v_{cp} = \varepsilon s_1 v_{cp} = \varepsilon s_1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{2gH}.$$

Now it’s quite easy to calculate the time during which water flows out of the vessel. The volume of water flowing out is $V = SH$, therefore,

$$T = \frac{V}{Q} = \frac{SH}{\frac{1}{2} \varepsilon s_1 \sqrt{2gH}} = \frac{S \sqrt{2gH}}{\varepsilon s_1 V g}.$$
