After we have understood the peculiarities of the flow of a stream of water from a hole in the wall of a vessel, let us turn to a beautiful and at the same time simple experiment that simulates the main element of fiber optics - a light guide.

Typically, a light guide consists of tens or tens of thousands of very thin (several microns in diameter) glass fibers. Their length can reach several meters. The fibers are laid into a dense bundle, similar to a stranded wire, and enclosed in a protective sheath. Light rays enter the fiber and travel along each fiber independently from one end to the other.

The operation of the light guide is based on the phenomenon of total internal reflection, which means that light rays can exit from an optically denser medium to a less dense one only if they fall on the interface at angles that change the limiting angle of total internal reflection. For the glass-air interface this angle is $42^\circ$, for the water-air interface $49^\circ$. All rays that make an angle greater than the limiting one with the interface are reflected back into a denser medium. This phenomenon also occurs in light guide fibers, where the beam, having entered one end of the fiber, is reflected many times, and reaches its other end.

with a jar or cylindrical bucket. At a distance of 30-40 mm, two holes should be drilled, one pointing the other (Fig. 4). The diameter of one hole is 2 mm, the diameter of the other is 5-10 mm. The holes at the edges are notched; be sure to smooth them out with sandpaper or cut them with a drill of a larger diameter, otherwise the flowing stream will turn out to be inexpensive.

Cover the large hole with transparent material, such as polystylene film or cellophane. If it is good enough and hermetically sealed, you can even seal the hole with rubber glue. Everyday life is best carried out in a darkened room. The sealed hole must be illuminated with a directional light source. They can serve as a common flashlight. As long as the vessel is not filled with water, the light from the flashlight passes through both holes. If you fill the vessel with water, the trajectory of the light beam will bend in the shape of the flowing stream.

The fact is that, passing through the window, the light, through the second hole in the wall, enters the stream flowing out of the hole. Light rays strike the water-air interface at angles greater than the angle of total internal reflection. Therefore, light cannot exit through the side surface and follows along the jet, which is likened to a curved light guide.
