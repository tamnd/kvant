Experiments with water jet
M. P. Golovey

The science that studies the laws of equilibrium and fluid motion and how to apply these laws to solve various practical problems is called *hydraulics*. It is difficult to name a field of technology where the laws of hydraulics are not applied. They are used in the construction of hydroelectric power stations, places, water pipelines, canals; they are needed for the construction of irrigation machines and jedeval installations.

We also encounter various hydraulic devices at home: water supply, sewerage, heating. The flow of water from a tap, a hose, a connection in a vessel - in all these, at first glance, simple phenomena, it is not so easy to determine patterns.

You don't have to look far for examples. What shape do you think the cross-section of the stream of water coming from the square-shaped outlet has? And from the answer in the shape of a triangle? circle? Take your time with your answer. If the response is triangular, the cross-section of the jet takes the shape of a cross with thin ribs at the crown (Fig. 1, b), if it is round, it takes the shape of an ellipse (Fig. 1, ​​c).

Thus, the cross-section of the jet emerging from the square outlet first has the shape of an octagon, and then becomes cruciform. This phenomenon is called jet inversion.

Let us now turn to the flow of water through a hole in the wall of the vessel.

Let us take a cylindrical vessel filled with water, in the side wall of which there is a small hole of area $s_1$ (Fig. 3). Through this hole, water flows out under the pressure of a liquid column of height $H$. The hole is considered small if its dimensions are at least ten times smaller than the height of the column $H$ creating the pressure. In this case, all points of the small hole are located at approximately the same depth from the surface of the liquid, and the flow rates at all points can be considered the same.

A hole in a thin wall is a hole whose edges are sharp enough so that the wall thickness does not affect the shape and conditions of the jet. This condition is met when the edge thickness is less than three hole diameters.

What determines the speed at which water flows through a small hole in the thin wall of a vessel? To answer this question, we use the law of conservation of energy.

Let a small amount of water $\Delta m$ flow out of the vessel in a short period of time $\Delta t$. The potential energy of water in the vessel decreased by values ​​$\Delta mgH$ (the time is short enough to consider the level $H$ of water in the vessel not changing). This change in potential energy is equal to the kinetic energy of the mass of water $\Delta m$ flowing out of the hole at a speed of $v$, that is, $\frac{\Delta mv^2}{2} = \Delta mgH$. From here

$$v = \sqrt{2gH}.$$

How long will it take for water to flow out of the vessel? Obviously, this depends on the flow rate and the area of ​​the hole. Let's look at speed first.

As we have already shown, $v = \sqrt{2gH}$. But this speed can be considered constant only over a short period of time (as long as $H = const$ can be considered). As the liquid flows out, the level rises, and consequently, the flow rate decreases from $v_0 = \sqrt{2gH}$ (at the initial moment of time $t = 0$) to $v_T = 0$ (the flow out). What is the nature of the change in speed over time? Let at the initial moment of time $(t_1 = 0)$, when the door is opened

Neglecting the values ​​$(\Delta v)^2$ and taking into account that $v_1^2 = 2gH$, we obtain

Hence,

$$\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = g \rho \frac{s}{S} = \text{const}$$

$$v_{cp} = \frac{v_1 + 0}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{gH}{2}}.$$

Now let's see how the area of ​​the hole affects the time it takes for water to flow out. In a unit of time, a mass of liquid $Q = \rho s v_{cp}$ should flow out of the vessel (ρ is the density of the liquid). In this case, we believe that the speed at a given moment in time at all points of the hole is directed strictly perpendicular to the cross-sectional plane of the hole. However, if it flows from a small round hole in a thin wall, then the diameter of its cross section is less than the diameter of the hole. The jet seems to be disappearing. The compression of the jet occurs due to the fact that the liquid flows to the hole from all sides and its particles, moving towards the hole along the walls of the section, continue to align with the axis of the scab. Only at a certain distance from the edge of the hole, approximately equal to the radius of the hole, do the particle trajectories become almost parallel. The compression of the jet continues further, but now incomparably slower, imperceptibly for the eyes. The section at which the sharp visible compression of the scab ends is called compressed. The ratio of the area of ​​the compressed section $s_2$ to the cross-sectional area of ​​the hole $s_1$ is called the compression ratio $\varepsilon$:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{s_2}{s_1}.$$

Since the distance between sections $s_1$ and $s_2$ is small (as we have already said, it is approximately equal to the radius of section $s_1$), then the speed of water at all points of section $s_2$ is practically equal to $\sqrt{2gh}$ (h is the height of the water level in the vessel at a given moment in time) and is perpendicular to the section plane. Therefore, water consumption

$$Q = s_2 v_{cp} = \varepsilon s_1 v_{cp} = \varepsilon s_1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{2gH}.$$

Now it’s quite easy to calculate the time during which water flows out of the vessel. The volume of water flowing out is $V = SH$, therefore,

$$T = \frac{V}{Q} = \frac{SH}{\frac{1}{2} \varepsilon s_1 \sqrt{2gH}} = \frac{S \sqrt{2gH}}{\varepsilon s_1 V g}.$$

After we have understood the peculiarities of the flow of a stream of water from a hole in the wall of a vessel, let us turn to a beautiful and at the same time simple experiment that simulates the main element of fiber optics - a light guide.

Typically, a light guide consists of tens or tens of thousands of very thin (several microns in diameter) glass fibers. Their length can reach several meters. The fibers are laid into a dense bundle, similar to a stranded wire, and enclosed in a protective sheath. Light rays enter the fiber and travel along each fiber independently from one end to the other.

The operation of the light guide is based on the phenomenon of total internal reflection, which means that light rays can exit from an optically denser medium to a less dense one only if they fall on the interface at angles that change the limiting angle of total internal reflection. For the glass-air interface this angle is $42^\circ$, for the water-air interface $49^\circ$. All rays that make an angle greater than the limiting one with the interface are reflected back into a denser medium. This phenomenon also occurs in light guide fibers, where the beam, having entered one end of the fiber, is reflected many times, and reaches its other end.
