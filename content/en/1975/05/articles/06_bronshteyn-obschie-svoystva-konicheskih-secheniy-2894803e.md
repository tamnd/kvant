Conic sections

In the articles “Ellipse”, “Hyperbola” and “Parabola”*) these curves were defined in different ways: the first - as a result of stretching or rolling a circle, and the second and third - as graphs of certain functions. Each of them has its own focal property, which is often taken to be the definition of a curve. Let us recall these “focal definitions” (it is assumed that all points under consideration for each line lie in the same plane):

An ellipse is the set of all points, the sum of the distances of each of them to two points (foci) is the same.

A hyperbola is the set of all points, the difference in the distances of each of them to two points (foci) is the same.

A parabola is the set of all points equally distant from a point (focus) and from a line that does not pass through it (director).

The ancient Greek mathematician Menaechmus, who discovered these curves, defined them differently: as sections of a circular cone by a plane perpendicular to one of the generatrices. He called the resulting curves sections of an acute-angled, rectangular and myno-angled cone, depending on the angle of the cone. The first, as we see below, is an ellipse, the second is a paraboly, the third is one branch of a hyperbola (Fig. 1, a, b, and c). The names "Ellipse", "Hyperbola" and "Parabola" were introduced by Apollo*. Before us we should receive (7 books out of 8) Apollo’s essay “On Conchex Sections”. In this work, Apollonius considers both halves of the cone (Fig. 2) and intersects the cone with planes that are not necessarily perpedicular to one of the generatrix.

Theorem. If the section plane intersects all the generatrices of the cone (only one of its half), then the intersection line (red line on figure 2) is an ellipse;

if the plane is parallel to one generatrix (and can intersect one floor), then the intersection line (blue) is a parabola;

if the plane is parallel to two generators (we intersect both floors), then the intersection line (black) is an eperbola.

An elegant proof of this theorem was proposed in 1822 by the Selgian engineer Dandel, who used spheres that are now pleasantly called Dandelen spheres.

**Directional property of conic sections. Eccentricity**

The focal property of a parabola can be given the following formulation: the ratio $\dfrac{MF}{MD}$ of the distances of any point of the parabola to the focus $F$ and to the directrix $d$ (Fig. 5) is a constant value (equal to unity).

A similar property also holds for the ellipse and hyperbola.

*In the plane of an ellipse (hyperbola) for each focus $F$ there is such a straight line $d$ (directrix) that the ratio of the distances from any point $M$ of the ellipse (hyperbola) to the focus $F$ and to the directrix $d$ is a constant value.* It is called the eccentricity of the ellipse (hyperbolas) and is denoted by the Greek letter $\varepsilon$ (epsilon). For a parabola, therefore, $\varepsilon = 1$; from what follows it turns out that for an ellipse $\varepsilon$ is less than 1, and for a hyperbola $\varepsilon$ is more than 1. Let us prove this property, for example, for an ellipse. The directrixes $d_1$ and $d_2$ are the lines of intersection of plane P with planes I and II (see Fig. 3). Since $MP_1 = MF_1$, $MP_2 = MF_2$, then $MF_1 = \varepsilon \cdot MD_1$, $MF_2 = \varepsilon \cdot MD_2$. Adding these equalities, we get

$$MF_1 + MF_2 = \varepsilon (MD_1 + MD_2) = \varepsilon \cdot D_1D_2.$$

But the left side, according to the focal property of the ellipse, is constant and equal to $2a$, the length $D_1D_2$ (the distance between the directrixes) is also constant. So, $\varepsilon = \mathrm{const}$.

Origin of the named ellipse and hyperbola. Second order curves

Let us call the main chord of an ellipse, hyperbola, or parabola the chord that passes through the focus peredicularly to the focal axis (this is the name of the axis passing through the focus of the curve). An ellipse and a hyperbola, unlike parabolas, have two main chords. We will denote the length of half of the main chord by the letter p and call it the focal parameter of the end section *).

For any point M of the finite section, we construct a yellow rectangle ABCD (whose height is equal to the main chord) and a blue square NMEH (Fig. 7, a, 6, b). As we know**), for parabolas this rectangle and square have the same area.

Below we will see that for an ellipse, a square has less area than a rectangle, that is, this square does not have enough (elleipsis). For a hyperbola, a square has a larger area than a rectangle, that is, it has an excess (hyperbole). This fact, known to Apollo, is the basis for the name of both curves.

Some other properties

In conclusion, we present several more properties of finite sections without proof.

The property of conjugate diameters, proven for the ellipse **), with some variations, is transferred to the parabola and hyperbola. In a parabola, the set of midpoints of parallel chords lies on a ray parallel to the axis of symmetry of the parabola - each such ray is the diameter of the parabola. In one branch of the hyperbola, the midpoints of the parallel chords also lie on the ray. The straight line on which this ray lies passes through the center of the hyperbola and is called diametrical. Explore for yourself where the midpoints of parallel chords of another type lie - segments, one end of which lies on one and the other on the other branch of the hyperbola.
