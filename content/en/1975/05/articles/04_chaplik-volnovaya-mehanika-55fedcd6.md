Wave mechanics

About fifty years ago, quantum mechanics was born - the science of the laws of motion of microscopic particles of materials (atoms, atomic nuclei, electrons, etc.). These laws turned out to be so surprising, so different from what physicists had encountered so far, that at first even leading scientists refused to believe the conclusions of quantum mechanics. And even today, discussions about the physical foundations of this science continue, although many of its sections have already become engineering disciplines.

In the early years of quantum mechanics, it was called wave mechanics. Now this name has already begun to be forgotten, but meanwhile it contains an indication of the most important feature inherent in the phenomena of the microworld. After all, the quantum, that is, discrete nature of the processes in which microparticles participate, is associated precisely with their wave properties. For example, the fact that the energy of electrons in an atom can only take on a certain discrete set of values ​​is a consequence of the wave nature of the electron.

In this article we will try to understand what the words “wave nature of an electron” mean and why it leads to discreteness of its properties. We'll have to start from afar.

Particles and waves

How do we describe the movement of large bodies, for example, the movement of an artillery shell, a satellite of the Earth, a planet around the Sun? Such bodies are called microsoptic (as opposed to microparticles), and the features of good motion are known. Of course, the Earth's satellites and artillery shells harmed the wave can be considered offended things. But the laws that govern their movement are easy to understand from our everyday experience.

We are accustomed to the fact that each body occupies a certain place in space, and this place can be specified by specifying the coordinates of the body. If a body moves, then over time it changes its position in space. To describe the movement of a body, you can specify the positions that it occupies at successive moments of time, that is, (to be more precise) you can specify the dependence of the coordinates of the body on time. This is the language of mechanics, or rather, even its alphabet. But he guarded against absence with the confidence that it is possible at every moment of time to determine the position of a body in space by specifying its coordinates? The answer is completely clear: from the same place from which all our knowledge was guarded - from experience. It is everyday experience that serves as the source of concepts and representations that are used by general mechanics - the mechanics of microsoptic bodies (it is also called classical). It is important to remember this circumstance: in everyday experience we are dealing with microsoptic bodies, therefore the usual

ideas about movement in space relates to just such topics. According to these ideas, every moving body has a trajectory, and at each point of the trajectory one can indicate the speed of the body, its acceleration, etc. This is how the movement of bodies is described in classical mechanics.

We have to do things differently when describing wave processes, for example, sound or waves on the surface of water. The first thing to note is that the concept of a trajectory for waves is meaningless. After all, sound travels from its source in all directions. If you still ask the question: where is the sound wave at a given moment, then the answer should be as follows: this is a certain part of space, limited by the surface, to which the waves managed to reach at a given moment in time. Inside this surface at each point (of course, if the source of work is not interrupted) there are fluctuations in air density, which are sound. This means that it makes no sense to talk about the coordinate of sound, about the point in space where the sound is located. This becomes especially clear in the case of waves in a closed cavity, where standing waves can occur. An example would be a Cameron resonance box, wind musical instruments (such as an organ or socophone), a drum, etc. When a drum is struck, standing waves are established inside it, and at the same time the entire volume of air contained within the drum is sounded. Thus, we can say that a wave fills all the space available to it, and concepts such as coordinate or trajectory do not apply to waves. Here you should use other other methods. Waves are characterized by their amplitude, frequency, phase, speed of propagation, and wavelength.

Let us dwell on one more difference in the properties of waves and particles, which is very important for further study of the basic waves - extremely attractive waves. A wave, in the literal sense of the word, can turn a corner, a frequency cannot. For example, you can hear speech while standing around the corner of a house and not seeing people talking.

The phenomenon of dough diffraction is associated with the behavior of waves when they are reflected from a rough surface. If the reflecting surface were ideally smooth, then the waves and wave particles would behave exactly the same (Fig. 1). For both of them, the well-known law would be fulfilled: the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection (of course, it must be noted that the impact of particles on the surface is assumed to be absolutely elastic). It is easy to assemble that in this ideal case, a parallel beam of flying particles remains parallel even after reflection from the surface, since the speed of each particle rotates through the same angle. The same applies to waves.

Let's now see what happens in the real case, that is, when the surface is not an ideal plane, but has some

Their horizon dimensions are smaller than the height and width of the surface teeth, so such particles can be considered as material points. Each of these particles hits a certain area of ​​the surface, a certain slope of a tooth, and is reflected from this slope according to the same law: the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. But since each tooth is inclined at its own specific angle to the direction of the primary flow of particles, particles falling on different teeth fly off in different directions. As a result, the reflected particles no longer form a parallel beam, but are placed randomly at different angles.

If large particles fall on the surface, which, when in contact with the surface, cover many teeth at once, then the picture turns out different (Fig. 3). “From the point of view” of such a particle, a surface with small teeth differs little from an ideal plane. For example, a ping-pong table is called (the plywood is unevenly processed, the paint is not applied in an equal layer, etc.), however, the ball bounces almost the same way as a leveling plane. The fact is that a large ball, touching many small surface teeth at once upon impact, seems to upsaverage their actions, and this upsaverage action turns out to be almost the same as for an ideal plane. In such cases they talk about specular reflection.

Reflection of waves from a rough surface can also occur in two ways. Long waves (wavelength greater than the size of the irregularity) are diffused by the irregularities; As a result, the reflection is close to ideal. For example, the surface irregularities of an ordinary mirror are much smaller than the wavelength of visible light. Reflection of light occurs directionally (specular). However, hard RENT rays, the wavelength of which is thousands of times shorter than that of visible light, are reflected from the same mirror by scattering (diffusely) - the reflected rays go in different directions.

So, when particle flows or waves are reflected from surface irregularities, the decisive role for particles is played by the correspondence between the sizes of irregularities and the particle sizes, and in the case of waves, the correspondence between the sizes of irregularities and the wavelength.

We now come to the main part of our story.

How do electrons behave?
When J. J. Thomson discovered the electron at the very end of the century,

⟦folio 17⟧

he imagined it as a particle carrying a certain portion of electric charge. Indeed, all experiments seemed to indicate that an electron is a particle charged with negative electricity and behaving as particles should. We already know what this means. First, electrons can be counted individually (for example, by measuring the total charge and dividing it by the charge of one electron); no one has ever observed half an electron. Secondly, each electron in the beam is characterized by its own trajectory, which can be changed by influencing the beam with an electric or magnetic field. The moment an electron hits the target can be detected by a flash in the luminescent coating of the target. This flash is almost point-like, which means that the electron affects only a very small area of ​​the target. Consequently, we can talk about the coordinate of the electron, about its trajectory, that is, about everything that characterizes it as a particle.

It was on the basis of the concept of the electron as a particle that the so-called planetary model of the atom, proposed by Rutherford, arose. In this model, the atom was considered as a miniature Solar system, in which “planets” - electrons - move in orbit around the “Sun” - the atomic nucleus. The dimensions of the atom, that is, the radii of electron orbits, were soon found out. They turned out to be on the order of $10^{-8}$ cm, or one angstrom. There were differences of opinion regarding the size of the electron itself, but everyone agreed that its radius should, of course, be less than $10^{-8}$ cm. After all, an electron is a part of an atom, and a part, in any case, cannot be larger than the whole. But in 1927, American physicists Davisson and Germer carried out their famous experiments with electron beams. And it immediately became clear that the view of electrons as small

such hard balls, to put it mildly, is naive. The nature of the electron turned out to be much more complex.

We will examine here only one of the experiments of Davisson and Germer. A parallel beam of electrons was directed to the crystal face and reflected from it. *The reflected beam remained parallel* (as in the case of reflection of visible light from a mirror). That's it. At first glance, there is nothing surprising in this fact. After all, we know that under certain conditions particles can experience specular reflection from a solid surface. But we must not forget that “from the point of view of the electron” the surface of a crystal is by no means a continuous surface. A crystal is made up of atoms separated by gaps of empty space. The dimensions of the gaps are approximately equal to the dimensions of the atoms themselves and amount to a value of the order of $10^{-8}$ cm. And electrons are small particles, their radius is much smaller than $10^{-8}$ cm. This means that when an electron beam collides with a crystal, a situation should occur where the particle sizes are smaller than the sizes of irregularities. Therefore, the reflection of electrons must be diffuse.

Thus, either electrons must be considered to be very large balls, much larger than the atoms themselves (then they would be reflected specularly from the crystal), or... or electrons do not obey the laws of classical mechanics and are not particles in the usual sense of the word. The first possibility leads to obvious nonsense: a large ball cannot orbit inside a small atom. This means that there remains a second possibility, no matter how incredible it may seem.

Only waves whose length is greater than the distance between the atoms in the crystal can be specularly reflected from the surface of a crystal. Therefore, we must assume that the electron beam behaves like a wave.

us, and then the question arises about the characteristic parameters of these waves: their frequency, wavelength, etc. By the time of the experiments of Davisson and Germer, the “crazy” hypothesis of the French scientist de Broglie (stated in 1923) was already known that every particle behaves like a wave, the length of which $\lambda$ is related to the momentum $p=mv$ of the particle by the following formula (de Broglie’s formula):

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}.$$

Note that the quantity $h$ in this formula is the famous Planck constant, equal to $6{,}62 \cdot 10^{-27}$ $эрг \cdot с$. It connects the frequency $\nu$ of oscillations of the electromagnetic field with the energy $E$ of its quantum (photon) according to Planck’s formula: $E = h\nu$, or the wavelength with the momentum of the photon: $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}$. De Broglie's idea arose from the assumption of symmetry in nature. The theory showed that waves have in some sense the properties of particles. Couldn't particles, in turn, have wave properties? De Broglie's hypothesis is that these relationships apply equally to both photons and particles of matter. Numerous experiments carried out subsequently confirm this.

The Planck and de Broglie formulas connect the mechanical characteristics of particles (momentum, energy) with their wave characteristics (wavelength and frequency). The extremely small (from the point of view of the scales we are used to) value of Planck’s constant shows that wave effects in the movement of particles become significant only in the microworld. Indeed, a ping-pong ball of mass 1 $г$ traveling at speed 1 $м/с$ has a de Broglie wavelength equal to $6{,}6 \cdot 10^{-29}$ $см$. This is a fantastically short length, trillions of times smaller than the size of an atomic nucleus.

It is absolutely clear that no wave effects (for example, diffraction) can be detected in the motion of such a body. In contrast, for an electron in an atom, the de Broglie wavelength is approximately $10^{-8}$ $см$ ($m \approx 9 \times 10^{-28}$ $г$, $v \approx 10^{8}$ $см/с$), that is, on the order of the size of the atom itself in which the electron is moving. Under these conditions, the wave behavior of the electron plays a decisive role. All the specifics of atomic phenomena and, in particular, their quantum nature are associated precisely with this wave-like behavior of electrons. We will now consider the simplest example showing how the discreteness of the properties of an atom follows from the wave nature of the electron.

Under normal conditions, electrons are held within the atom by forces of electrostatic attraction and cannot move too far away from the nucleus. Thus, the electrons belonging to an atom move in a limited region of space. These are usually called bound electrons. On the contrary, electrons in a beam created by an electron gun can, in principle, fall into any region of space, and therefore are called free. The example in question captures precisely this feature of the atomic electron: the limited region of space available for its movement.

For simplicity of reasoning, let us imagine that the electron can only move along a certain straight line and does not go beyond the boundaries of the segment $AB$ (Fig. 4). Let's say that at points $A$ and $B$ there are solid walls through which the electron cannot penetrate. Such a system is a one-dimensional model of an atom. Of course, atoms are much more complex, and our example does not pretend to be a quantitative correspondence to a real atom. But the main qualitative property of atoms is the existence of discrete energy levels.

For example, it quite well describes the behavior of electrons in thin films of metals or semiconductors. In this case, the segment $AB$ represents the thickness of the film, and the two surfaces of the film play the role of walls that do not let the electron out.) To calculate the characteristics of such a one-dimensional atom, use the de Broglie formula. Since the electron is “locked” in a limited region of space (in this case, on a straight line segment), we must talk about standing waves. Similar mechanical one-dimensional story waves arise, for example, when a string fixed at two points vibrates. Naturally, these anchoring points should be compared to points $A$ and $B$, beyond which the electron cannot penetrate. The string strings also do not penetrate through the fixed ends. This means that the confusion of the points of the string during oscillations is always such that the points $A$ and $B$ are nodal (their mixtures are always equal to zero). From here it immediately follows that the segment $AB$ must contain an integer number of half-waves. Thus, a vibrating string with fixed ends can only be in certain states of motion, which can be numbered by the number of half-waves that fit between points $A$ and $B$. In other words, only those waves are possible in a string whose length $\lambda$ satisfies the following relation: $AB = n\lambda/2$, where $n$ is an integer taking the value 1, 2, 3, etc. On figure 4 sluans I, II, III, IV correspond to $n$, equal to 1, 2, 3 and 4. Let us denote the length of the segment $AB$ by the letter $L$. Then the possible wavelengths $\lambda_n$ are given by the formula

$$\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}; \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Now let's return to the electron running between two walls and apply de Broglie's formula to it. Since only a discrete set of waves $\lambda_n$ can exist, the electron speed $v_n = \frac{h}{m\lambda_n}$ can also take only a discrete set of values:

$$v_n = \frac{h}{m\lambda_n} = \frac{h}{m2L} n; \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

The kinetic energy of an electron is $E = mv^2/2$, and it also cannot be arbitrary. There are only discrete energy levels:

$$E_n = \frac{h^2n^2}{8mL^2}; \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

(the number $n$ is called a quantum number). We have obtained one of the first formulas of quantum mechanics - the energy levels of an electron “locked” between two impenetrable walls. From the point of view of classical mechanics, the very formulation of the question of permissible values ​​of energy for this case is meaningless. If a classical ball jumps between two walls, elastically reflecting from them, then its energy $E = \frac{mv^2}{2}$ depends on the initial speed $v_0$ and can take any value. Generally speaking, the expression for energy levels can also be formulated for a classical particle, for example, the same ping-pong ball. The energy ladder turns out to be so dense that the practical energy can change continuously, as it should be for a classical particle. This is the ultimate transition from quantum mechanics to classical mechanics.

In addition to the wave nature of the microparticle, the fact that the particle moves in a limited region of space is extremely important for the appearance of a discrete spectrum. In this case, as we have already said, waves arise, and oscillations are characterized by discrete wavelengths, and therefore frequencies. The frequency multiplied by $h$ is equal to the energy of the particle. The general tendency is that the smaller the size of the available area of ​​motion, the greater the distance between adjacent energy levels (see, for example, formula (*)).

If a microparticle moves under the influence of such forces that allow it to go to infinity, then standing waves do not arise, and the energy can have any value. In this case, the quantum system is said to have a continuous spectrum of energy (for example, the beam of an electron gun).

In a hydrogen atom, an electron is removed near the nucleus by forces of electrical attraction. If the state of an electron is such that it does not have enough energy to overcome attraction and go an infinitely large distance from the nucleus, then it has a discrete spectrum. However, if in some way you impart enough energy to the electron (this can be done, for example, by illuminating the atom with light), then the electron overcomes the attraction to the proton and can move away from it as much as desired. Starting from this energy, the electron spectrum becomes continuous, that is, the electron energy can take on any value.

In conclusion, it should be emphasized that events occurring in the microcosm at the level of atoms, molecules and atomic nuclei became understandable only after the idea of ​​the wave nature of microparticles appeared. Their dual nature (on the one hand, they are particles, on the other, waves) has no analogues in the macrosopper world inherent in us, so it would be useless to try to imagine, for example, what an electron moving in an atom is like. It is unlike anything we have known so far. An electron has both particle and wave properties, but is neither a particle in the general sense of the word nor an ordinary wave. We can only talk about an electron as a particle as an approximation, without forgetting about its wave properties. For example, for an ordinary, classical particle, you can precisely specify its coordinate, momentum, and energy at any time. What can be said about these parameters when describing the behavior of microparticles?

According to de Broglie's formula, a particle with a given momentum, that is, determined by the value of its speed, corresponds to a wave of a certain length. Let us determine that only harmonic waves are characterized by the value of the wavelength. But the harmonic wave is limitless in space. This means that we cannot talk about localization, about the spatial coordinates of a microparticle at a given moment in time.

If we believe that the particle is localized in space, then we have thereby limited the region in which the oscillatory process described by the wave properties of this particle is concentrated. But such vibrations cannot be described by a single harmonic wave of a certain length. Consequently, according to de Broglie's relation, it is impossible to say anything definitely about the momentum of a particle at that moment of time at which we consider its coordinates to be fixed.

Thus, it makes no sense to talk about simultaneous exact values ​​of the coordinates and momentum of the microparticle. These parameters can be determined at the same point in time only approximately, with a certain degree of accuracy. In 1927, the German physicist Werner Heisenberg showed that there is a relationship between the inaccuracies $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ in determining the coordinates and momentum of a microparticle at a given time:

$$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq h^*).$$

This relation is called the uncertainty relation.

Today we cannot answer the question why microparticles are structured this way and not otherwise, and it is unlikely that such a question is one of the correctly posed ones. But we now know for certain that the most familiar, seemingly unshakable microparticles defined about nature can be determined by the flow of new facts and observations. Therefore, we treat with great caution all kinds of categorical judgments about the properties and laws of the world around us, or each such “absolute” statement is actually based on limited human experience.

*) $\Delta p$ is the component of the total momentum of the particle on the corresponding coordinate axis. In this case - on the $X$ axis.
