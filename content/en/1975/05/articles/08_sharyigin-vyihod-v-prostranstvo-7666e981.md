Try solving the following puzzle: make four regular triangles out of six matches so that each side has a whole match. Attempts to solve it do not seem to lead to success. Moreover, it can be proven that such a construction is not feasible on a plane (try this). How can this be? It turns out that you need to go out into space and put together the correct tetrahedр (Fig.1) from matches. Impossible on a plane but feasible in three-dimensional space!

Entering space was also useful in solving some planimetric problems. A classic example is Desagrag's theorem. If two triangles $A_1B_1C_1$ and $A_2B_2C_2$ are located on a plane so that lines $A_1A_2$, $B_1B_2$, $C_1C_2$ intersect at one point, then at the point of intersection of lines $A_1B_1$ and $A_2B_2$, $B_1C_1$ and $B_2C_2$, $C_1A_1$ and $C_2A_2$ are located on the same straight line (Fig. 1).

With the help of exiting into space, Brianchon's theorem is also proven. The design containing the vertices of the described hexagon intersect at one point.

To prove the existence of such a hexagon $A_1B_1C_1D_1E_1F_1$, it is enough to take one vertex, for example, $A_1$, arbitrarily on the redistribution to the plane $p$, restored at the point $A$; then the remaining vertices are uniquely determined. In fact, let $a, b, c, d, e, f$ be the length of the tangents to the circle drawn correspondingly through the points $A, B, C, D, E, F$ and $h$ the distance from $A_1$ to the hexagon plane $ABCDEF$. Then $B_1$ is on the other side of the plane, compared to $A_1$ at a distance $hb/a$, $C_1$ along the angle of the side of the plane as $A_1$, at a distance $\frac{hb}{a} \cdot \frac{c}{b} = \frac{hc}{a}$ from the plane, etc. Finally, we find that $F_1$ lies on the other side of the plane than $A_1$, at a distance $hf/a$, and, therefore, $A_1$ and $F_1$ lie on a straight line passing through the point of tangency $AF$, with the circle. Any two opposite sides of the hexagon $A_1B_1C_1D_1E_1F_1$ are located in the same plane (prove it yourself). Consequently, any two diagonals connecting opposite vertices of the hexagon $A_1B_1C_1D_1E_1F_1$ intersect, and hence all three diagonals of the hexagon $A_1B_1C_1D_1E_1F_1$ (they do not lie in the same plane) intersect at one point. Since the hexagon $ABCDEF$ is the projection of the hexagon $A_1B_1C_1D_1E_1F_1$, the theorem is proven.

Note that a more general fact can be proved in a similar way. If lines $AB, BC, CD, DE, EF$ and $FA$ touch the same circle, then lines $AD, BE$ and $CF$ intersect at one point or are parallel. One of the possible cases of equal alignment of points $A, B, C, D, E, F$ is shown in figure 4.

Only the previously proven Brianchon theorem turns out to be valid for a hexagon described around an arbitrary finite section (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola), since an arbitrary finite section can be projected with respect to some point onto another plane so that its projection will be a circle. In this case, the lines tangent to the final section will turn into straight lines tangent to the circle.

For the plane $\beta$ we take an arbitrary plane intersecting the plane $\alpha$ along a straight line $l$ not passing through the center of the given circle. Let $AB$ be the diameter of a given circle perpendicular to $l$, and $N$ the intersection point of lines $AB$ and $l$. As point $O$—the center of the projection—we take the intersection point of lines $AA_1$ and $BB_1$. Obviously, the middle of the segment $AB$ is projected to a point different from the middle of the segment $A_1B_1$.

Let's take an arbitrary point $D$ on a given circle $DM \perp AB$. The projection of $DM$ will be $D_1M_1 \perp A_1B_1$. Let us prove that $D_1$ lies on the circle constructed with $A_1B_1$ as a diameter. Let's use the fact that $M_1D_1 \cdot MO = MD \cdot M_1O$ ($\Delta DMO \approx \Delta D_1M_1O$) and $MD^2 = BM \cdot MA$. We need to prove that $M_1D_1^2 = B_1M_1 \cdot M_1A_1$, or, using the two previous equalities, obtain that

$$\frac{BM \cdot MA}{MO^2} = \frac{B_1M_1 \cdot M_1A_1}{M_1O^2},$$

$$\frac{BM}{MO} \cdot \frac{MA}{MO} = \frac{B_1M_1}{M_1O} \cdot \frac{M_1A_1}{M_1O}.$$

Replacing the ratio of the segments in the last equality with the ratio of the snes of the opposite angles of the corresponding triangles, we obtain the evaluative equality

$$\frac{\sin \varphi}{\sin \gamma} \frac{\sin \psi}{\sin \mu} = \frac{\sin \varphi}{\sin \mu} \cdot \frac{\sin \psi}{\sin \gamma}.$$

One of the most beautiful and necessary illustrations of the theme “Exit into space” is, perhaps, the solution of the well-known Apollon problem using this method. Let us assign to each circle in a given plane two points of space located on opposite sides of the plane, distant from this plane at a distance equal to the radius of the circle, and also that the projection of each of them onto the plane is the center of the circle. Conversely: each point in space corresponds to a circle in our plane, the center of which coincides with the projection of the point onto the plane, and the radius is equal to the distance from the point to the plane. Before moving on to solving the Pollonian problem A, let us pay attention to the following circumstances: if $P$ is one of two points corresponding to the circle $k$, then any point located on a finite surface whose vertex is the point $P$, and the guide is the circle $k$ (both sides of this surface are considered), corresponds to the circle $k$ (Fig. 6).

This example, of course, is only half serious, but here is a completely serious example. Let three planes intersect along one straight line. Let's consider three triangular angles, the vertices of which are located on this line, and the edges lie in these planes (we assume that the corresponding edges, that is, edges located in the same plane, do not intersect at one point). Then the three intersection points of the corresponding boundaries of these three-edged angles lie on the same straight line (Fig. 8). Since to prove this theorem we want to “go out” into four-dimensional space, we will first talk about some properties of this space.

The simplest figures of four-dimensional space will be: a point, a line, a plane and a three-dimensional manifold, which we will call a hyperplane. The first three figures are our old signs from three-dimensional space. True, some statements associated with them need rephasing. For example, together with the following axiom of three-dimensional space: if two different planes belonging to the same hyperplane have a common point, then they intersect in a straight line. The introduction of a new geometric image - a hyperplane - forces the introduction of a group of axioms associated with it, similar to how, in the transition from geometric planes to geometric three-dimensional space - planes, a group of axioms (remember which ones?) is introduced, expressing the basic properties of planes in space. This group consists of the following three axioms:

1. Whatever the hyperplane, there are points that belong to it and points that do not belong to it.
2. If two different hyperplanes have a common point, then they intersect along a plane, that is, there exists a plane belonging to the hyperplane.
3. If a straight line not belonging to a plane has a common point with it, then

From these axims it immediately follows that four points that do not belong to the same plane define a hyperplane; in the same way, three lines that do not belong to the same plane, but have a common point, or two different planes that have a common line, define a hyperplane. We will not prove these claims; try to do it yourself.

For further reasoning, we need the following fact, which is valid in four-dimensional space: three different hyperplanes that have a common point have a common line. In fact, by Axim 2, any two of the three hyperplanes have a common plane. Let's take two planes along which one of the three hyperplanes intersects with the other two. These two planes, belonging to the same hyperplane - a three-dimensional manifold, have a common point, and therefore intersect in a straight line or coincide.

Let us now move on to the proof of our statement. If the three planes mentioned in the conditions were located in four-dimensional space, then the statement would be obvious. Indeed, every triangular angle defines a hyperplane. Two hyperplanes intersect in a plane. This plane does not belong to the third hyperplane (according to the conditions, these hyperplanes intersect one of these planes along three straight lines that do not pass through one point), and, therefore, intersects with it in a straight line. Any three corresponding faces of trihedral angles lie in one hyperplane, defined by two planes on which the corresponding faces are located and have a common point. When these points belong to three hyperplanes defined by trihedral angles, and, as has been proven, lie on the same straight line. Now, to complete the proof, it is enough to “see” in the given conditions of the problem the projection of the corresponding four-dimensional configuration of the plane and trihedral angles.

In conclusion, I would like to advise the reader that one should not overly trust one's “three-dimensional intuition” when it comes to four-dimensional space.

How many do you think a convex polyhedron can have for which each segment connecting any two of its vertices is an edge? On a plane only a triangle has this property, in space - a tetrahedron.
