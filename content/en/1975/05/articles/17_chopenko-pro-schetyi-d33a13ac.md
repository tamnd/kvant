The first to introduce the Indo-Arabic number system in Europe was Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci. He outlined it in his beautiful book “Liber Abaci” (1202). The spread of Arabic numerals in Europe led to the emergence of computational algorithms that completely abandoned the Roman system of notation for numbers in favor of a more advanced Indo-Arabic system. The name algorithm comes from the name of the 9th century Arab mathematician Al-Khwarizmi and is the predecessor of the modern word algorithm. But let's get back to the bills. The modern abacus is essentially a modified counting board on which counters slide along wires or rods. Currently, three types of abacus are used: Chinese suan pen, Japanese soroban and Russian abacus.

The best abacus is designed for addition and subtraction. Multiplication and division in abacus is carried out by repeating these operations many times. Addition and subtraction on accounts is done in the same way as on paper when performing operations in a column. If you thoroughly learn the finger movements needed for addition, then the movements needed for subtraction need not be learned. A great practice exercise in abacus addition involves this number trick. You write the “magic” number 12,345,679 and ask someone present to name any number. Let's say the number 7 is named. Then you propose to multiply the magic number by 63. It turns out - presumably to everyone's amusement - that the product consists of only sevens (to determine the multiplier, you simply need to multiply the named number by 9).

Here's how to use this "magic" number for an abacus exercise. Put 12,345,679 on the accounts and add the same number to it eight times. If you did not make any mistakes during these additions, then you will receive a number on the abacus consisting of only units. Add the magic number nine more times to get a number of twos, and nine more times to get a number of threes. Finally, after 80 additions you should end up with a number that is all nines. This exercise is replaced by the fact that with its help it is easy to check the accuracy of work at each of the nine stages.

In addition to 12,345,679, there are also an infinite number of “magic” numbers. For example, $37 \times 3 \times d$, where $d$ is a single-digit number, consists of only $d$. The lowest magic number for seven is 15,873; for 13 - 8 547, for 99 - 1 122 334 455 667 789. Find the most constant smallest “magic” number for 17.
