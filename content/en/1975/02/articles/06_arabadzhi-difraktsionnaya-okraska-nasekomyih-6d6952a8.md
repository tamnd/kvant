## Diffraction staining of insects

Diffraction (structural) coloration of birds, butterflies and beetles is very common in nature.
A wide variety of shades of diffraction colors is characteristic of peacocks, pheasants, black storks, hummingbirds, butterflies of the tropical zone and many species of beetles (among them stag beetles, weevils, leaf beetles, etc.).
The diffraction coloring of animals was studied not only by biologists, but also by physicists, for example Rayleigh, Wood, Michelson.

The outer surface of the plumage of many birds and the upper body cover of butterflies and beetles are characterized by regular repetition of structural elements with a period of one to several microns.
In some animals, similar structures can cover the body in several layers, forming a multi-story (volumetric) diffraction grating.

Along with diffraction, animals can also have purely interference coloring, formed on thin and transparent outer covers (wedge-shaped in cross-section along the main axis of symmetry) with a thickness of the optically active layer from tenths of a micron to several microns.

In Figures 1 and 2 you see photographs of the wing of a butterfly and the back of an Asian leaf beetle (areas with diffraction coloring are selected).
Images 1, 2, 3 were taken using a microscope with a hundredfold magnification.
Side lighting was used to identify structural elements.
The photographs clearly show the repetition of structural elements.

Obviously, the color of the surface is associated with the reflection of rays.
Let us consider quantitatively diffraction in reflected light.
Let the rays of light fall on the grating at an angle $\theta_0$, and reflect from it at an angle $\theta$ (Fig. 4).
The difference between the paths of rays *1* and *2* is equal to
$$
\Delta=\Delta_2-\Delta_1=d\sin\theta_0-d\sin\theta,\tag1
$$
or $$
\Delta=2d\cos\dfrac{\theta_0+\theta}2\sin\dfrac{\theta_0-\theta}2.\tag2
$$

Since for diffraction maxima $\Delta=k\lambda$ ($k=1$, 2, $\ldots$), then $$
k\lambda=2d\cos\dfrac{\theta_0+\theta}2\sin\dfrac{\theta_0-\theta}2.\tag3
$$

The highest energy is localized in the first diffraction maximum ($k=1$).
For this maximum, assuming that $\theta$ is close to $\theta_0$, from expression (3) we obtain
$$
\lambda=(\theta_0-\theta)d\cos\theta_0.\tag4
$$

Let $\theta_0=65^\circ$ (in the case under consideration, diffraction in reflected light is observed with oblique incidence of rays), and $\theta_0-\theta=3^\circ$ (approximately 0.05 rad).
Then, for example, for $d=2\cdot10^{-3}~\text{см}$ the wavelength of the diffracted light from equation (4) is equal to $\lambda\approx0{,}4\cdot10^{-4}~\text{см}$.

The resulting value $\lambda$ corresponds to the blue region of the spectrum.
Most often, diffraction coloring in nature is observed in green, blue or cyan light.
From formula (4) it is clear that the wavelength $\lambda$ depends on the angle of incidence of light $\theta_0$.
Indeed, if the surface is illuminated from different angles, the color may change.

In the case of a multilayer diffraction grating, the condition for the formation of diffraction maxima is described by the equation
$$
k\lambda=2l\cos\theta_0,\tag5
$$
where $l$ is the distance between adjacent lattice layers.
This equation is obtained by determining the path difference $\Delta=\Delta_1+\Delta_2$, between beams *1* and *2* (Fig. 5) and replacing $\Delta$ for the diffraction maxima through $k\lambda$.

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 5

Assuming $k=1$, from expression (5) we obtain
$$
\cos\theta_0=\dfrac{\lambda}{2l}.\tag6
$$
It follows that diffraction by a multilayer structure is possible at $2l\gt\lambda$.
