SPEED OF LIGHT
AND ITS DIMENSION

A. V. Eleukiy

Many laws of modern physics contain the speed of light c as a parameter. This quantity is included in the equation of the electromagnetic field; the total energy of the substance, the energy levels of electrons in the atom, etc. are expressed through it.

Along with other constants, such as the electron charge and the gravitational constant, the speed of light is one of the fundamental physical constants. It is clear that the accuracy of physical laws is determined by the accuracy with which the values ​​of the corresponding constants are known, the only way to determine which is experiment.

The principle of measuring the speed of light, the idea of ​​which was first proposed by Galileo, is based on measuring the time it takes for a light signal to travel a certain distance. The development of this method - from Römer's astronomical observations to Michelson's filigree interferometric measurements - has been described in detail in our journal *). According to measurements of this kind, the speed of light was taken to be 299,792.6 ± 0.25 km/s. Note that the most relative accuracy achieved when measuring the speed of light using this method was obtained in the 50s of our century and is about one decimal part of a percent, or 10⁻⁸.

Let us recall that any measurement can be characterized by relative and absolute errors. Absolute error is the possibility of deviation of a measurement result from the exact value of the measured value. Such deviation may be due to imperfections in the measuring instrument or measurement process and should always be assessed in advance. Relative measurement error is the ratio of the absolute error to the measured value itself. Although it is appreciated in advance that when making accurate measurements it is necessary to reduce both absolute and relative error, in most cases the most important characteristic of measurement accuracy is the relative error. In fact, a measurement calculated from Earth to Lunya with an absolute error of ±1 m is always an accurate measurement, and a measurement with the same absolute error of the size of a room is just a rough estimate. This would become clear immediately if we pointed out that in the first case the relative error was 10⁻⁸, and in the second case - 10⁻¹.

Further increasing the accuracy of measuring the speed of light using interference methods encounters fundamental difficulties. A distance that we can measure with high accuracy (this distance is comparable to the length standard -

In this case, the optical method is used to measure large distances, but to use it it is necessary to know the speed of light in advance. It turns out to be a vicious circle.

It was possible to resolve this contradiction in recent years, when it became practically possible to use another principle for measuring the speed of light, based on the fact that the wavelength λ and frequency ν of electromagnetic waves in a vacuum are related to each other by the fundamental relation

$$c=\lambda\nu. \quad (1)$$

By measuring the wavelength and frequency of the same electromagnetic radiation independently, we could simply multiply these quantities to obtain the speed of light. The accuracy of such an experiment will be very high if we can satisfy two requirements simultaneously.

First, we must be able to measure the wavelength and frequency of electromagnetic waves with a high degree of accuracy.

Secondly, the frequency of oscillations of the electromagnetic radiation with which we are going to experiment must be maintained as constant as possible.

From relation (1) it follows that for an electromagnetic wave the relative degrees of inconstancy of frequency and wavelength coincide.

However, although the frequency and wavelength of laser radiation are constant with high accuracy, measuring them together faces significant technical difficulties. Let's look at this in more detail.

## Standard for measuring length

Every measurement ultimately comes down to comparison with some standard. There are standards for measuring a wide variety of parameters of physical objects and processes: for example, standards of length, time, current, mass of matter, etc. People try to choose standards

in such a way that their values ​​do not depend on external factors, such as temperature and air pressure, altitude above sea level, etc.

In particular, the modern standard of length is associated with the wavelength of electromagnetic radiation arising when an electron in a krypton atom transitions from one excited state to another.

Electrons in any atom can be in different states, differing from each other in energy. If a complete set of such states is known for a particular atom, then the spectrum of the atom is said to be known. For a physicist, the atoms of different chemical elements differ from each other mainly in their spectra, so that the spectra serve as “portraits” of the atoms. From a state with higher energy $E_2$ electrons can spontaneously transition to a state with lower energy $E_1$. In this case, energy $E_2 - E_1$ is released in the form of a quantum of electromagnetic oscillations. The frequency $\nu$ of these vibrations is related to the energy released by the relation

$$\nu = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{h}, \tag{2}$$

where $h$ is Planck’s constant.

Since the energy of the states in which electrons can exist in atoms is extremely weakly affected by external conditions, the frequency of vibrations emitted by atoms turns out to be constant with a high degree of accuracy. And since the degrees of constancy of the frequency and wavelength of electromagnetic oscillations coincide, the wavelength of the emitted light also turns out to be strictly constant. Therefore, it is convenient to use such light as a very accurate “ruler” for measuring length.

By definition, the international standard of length - the meter - contains 1,650,763.73 wavelengths of radiation that occurs during the transition between two specific states of the krypton atom. ## Laser wavelength measurement

Measuring length using a krypton standard is based on the phenomenon of light interference.

At each point in space where a light wave propagates, an electromagnetic field arises, and the magnitudes of the vectors of electric field strength and magnetic field induction change according to a harmonic law. When two coherent light rays pass through a certain point in space in the same direction, the total effect will depend on the phases in which the electric (or magnetic induction) field vectors of both rays oscillate at that point. If the oscillation phases coincide, that is, the intensity vectors are directed equally, then the light intensity at this point is maximum. If, on the contrary, the phases of oscillations are opposite, that is, the intensity vectors are directed opposite to each other, the light intensity at the point is minimal. Thus, if two beams of light of the same frequency with a constant phase difference (that is, with a constant relationship between the vectors of electric field strength or magnetic field induction) are directed onto a screen, then we will see alternating light and dark stripes on the screen - the so-called interference pattern.

## Cesium frequency standard

The situation with measuring the frequency of laser radiation is much more complicated.

The radiation from a cesium maser, a quantum generator of electromagnetic radiation with a wavelength in the radio wave range, is used as a frequency standard. The operating principle of a cesium maser is as follows. The cesium atom has a magnetic moment, that is, it is the smallest magnet. Under normal conditions, there are two types of cesium atoms, differing from each other in the magnitude of the magnetic moment. In other words, the magnetic field created by an atom of one kind is slightly greater than the field created by an atom of another kind. If such atoms are placed

⟦column⟧

put into a magnetic field (for example, between the poles of a magnet), then the strength of interaction between the magnetic moments of atoms and the field will depend, firstly, on the magnitude of the external magnetic field, and secondly, on what magnitude of the magnetic moment a given atom has. The greater the force, the greater the potential energy of cesium atoms. Thus, in a magnetic field, two types of cesium atoms will have different energies. Let us denote these energies, depending on the magnitude of the external magnetic field, by $E_1$ and $E_2$ $(E_1<E_2)$.

Let us expose cesium pairs to electromagnetic radiation, the quantum energy of which $h\nu$ is equal to the energy difference $E_2-E_1$:

$$h\nu = E_2 - E_1.$$

Then atoms of the first type, whose energy is equal to $E_1$, having absorbed such a quantum, will increase their energy to the value $E_2$, that is, they will turn into atoms of a different type. Since the energy difference between cesium atoms at a certain magnetic field value is very weakly affected by external conditions, such radiation can be used as a frequency standard. But for this you need to be able to separate atoms of one type from atoms of another type. This sorting is possible using a magnetic field. If a beam of atoms of both types is passed through a nonuniform magnetic field, then the trajectories of atoms of different types will be different, since the energies of interaction of the magnetic moment of the atom with the field are different. A beam of cesium atoms sorted in this way is exposed to an electromagnetic field. If the frequency of field oscillations corresponds to the energy difference $E_2-E_1$, then the atoms transform into another state, so that their trajectory in a non-uniform magnetic field changes.

The value of the frequency of electromagnetic oscillations at which such a transition occurs can be recorded with a relative

It is clear that the comparison of columnar frequencies of different scales is a task as ineffectively difficult as, for example, accurately measuring the distance from the Earth to the Moon using a meter ruler. Accurate measurement is only possible by comparing quantities that are close in scale. Therefore, to measure the frequencies of various oscillations using a caeser eton, it is necessary first of all to bring the measured frequency closer to the frequency of the eton. Currently, this problem is solved by using non-converting technology, non-converting frequency conversion of electromagnetic radiation.

Nonlinear system

Frequency conversion by multiplying it several times will occur; for example, when a sinusoidal oscillation of a certain frequency v is exposed to a nonlinear element. In order to understand what a nonlinear element is, let us recall Ohm's law, which relates the voltage $U$ applied to a conductor with resistance $R$, and the current $I$ flowing through this conductor: $U=IR$. the water conductor is a linear element. Conversely, the resistance of nonlinear elements depends on either the applied voltage or the current flowing through them. For example, conventional resistors are linear elements, but a semiconductor diode is a nonlinear element.

The most important characteristic of any system, both linear and nonlinear, is the connection between the signal supplied to the input of such a system and the signal returned to its output. For linear systems, only a change in the signal amplitude is possible with a constant dependence of the signal magnitude on time. By determining the coefficient proportional between the amplitudes of the signals and the nature of the input signal, we will uniquely determine the output signal (in the case of linear resistances that obey Ohm’s law, this coefficient is the resistance value $R$). Since the properties of nonlinear systems themselves depend on the signal supplied to their input, the shape of the output signal will no longer exactly repeat the shape of the signal supplied to the input. In order to characterize a nonlinear system completely, it is not enough to specify a proportional coefficient connecting the magnitudes of the input and output signals. It is also necessary to know the dependence of this coefficient on the magnitude of the input signal.

For example, if a piece of elastic material is stretched, then the increase in its length $\Delta l$ will be related to the applied reinforcement $F$ by Hooke’s law: $F=k\Delta l$. For small values ​​of $F$ the dependence is linear, that is, the coefficient $k$ does not depend on $F$, and the sample under consideration is a linear system. As the applied force increases, linearity is disrupted. It turns out that at large values ​​of $F$ all materials become nonlinear, that is, it becomes significantly dependent on $F$.

Among nonlinear elements, nonlinear oscillatory systems occupy a large place. A typical example of an oscillatory system with nonlinear properties is the sea surface. While the amplitude of oscillations (in this case, this is the height of the waves)

is small, the shape of the will is a sinusoid. When the amplitude of the oscillations reaches a sufficiently large amplitude, the waves become nonlinear, that is, their shape is determined by their amplitude - “wings” appear. With a further increase in amplitude, the waves completely lose their resemblance to a sinusoid.

The number of examples of nonlinear oscillations of systems existing in nature and technology can be continued.

A common property of all nonlinear elements is their ability to distort the signal supplied to their input. In particular, if the input signal is sinusoidal with frequency $v$, at the output it is no longer such. By adding to the input of such a converter oscillations not only the frequency $v_1$, but also harmonics, that is, oscillations of frequencies $2v_1$, $3v_1$, etc. Therefore, the elements are also called nonlinear frequency converters.

As an example of a nonlinear converter, consider a semiconductor diode whose current-voltage characteristic can be approximated by a parabola:

$$i = a + bu + cu^2,$$

where $a, b$ and $c$ are some constants. Let the direction $u$ supplied to the diode change according to the law

$$u = U_0 \cos \omega t = U_0 \cos 2\pi vt.$$

Then

$$i = a + bU_0 \cos 2\pi vt + cU_0^2 \cos^2 2\pi vt =$$

$$= a + bU_0 \cos 2\pi vt + \frac{cU_0^2}{2}(1 + \cos 4\pi vt) =$$

$$= \left(a + \frac{cU_0^2}{2}\right) + bU_0 \cos 2\pi vt +$$

$$+ \frac{cU_0^2}{2} \cos 4\pi vt.$$

Thus, the current through the converter under consideration is not harmonic, but is the sum of three components: a constant component, oscillations with a fundamental frequency $v$ and oscillations with a double frequency $2v$.

If the dependence $i (u)$ is more complex and therefore it must be represented by a larger number of terms:

$$i = a + bu + cu^2 + du^3 + \dots + lu^k,$$

then the output signal will contain cities of higher harmonics (2v, 3v, ..., kv).

An even more complex picture is obtained at the output if a signal more complex than a sinusoidal one is received at the input of the linear system. Of particular interest is the case where the input signal is the sum of two sinusoidal signals with frequencies $v_1$ and $v_2$. It turns out that in this case not only harmonics of each of the frequencies appear at the output, but also so-called combination frequencies: $v_1 + v_2, v_1 - v_2, 2v_1 + v_2$, etc. (this can be shown by checking calculations similar to those above). In general, combination frequencies can be represented as follows: $nv_1 \pm mv_2$, where $n$ and $m$ are any integers.

Laser frequency measurement

Now we can return to the specific problem - how to measure the frequency of laser radiation. But first, let's get acquainted with the general principle of measuring frequencies close to the reference ones. The studied oscillations, together with the reference ones, are fed to a nonlinear converter. As we have already said, various combination frequencies appear at the output, among which there is a frequency $v_1 - v_2$, called the binine frequency. Provided that the frequencies $v_1$ and $v_2$ are close to each other, the binine frequency turns out to be very small and can be measured by conventional methods (for example, using an oscilloscope). In this case, although the relative error in measuring the oscillatory frequency using an oscilloscope is quite larger ($\approx 10^{-4}$), the absolute measurement error turns out to be of the order of 1 - 10 $2\mu$. And since the reference frequencies have values ​​of approximately $10^{10} \epsilon$, the relative error in measuring such high frequencies will be $10^{-9} - 10^{-10}$.

To measure frequencies that are very different from the standard, in addition to the target standard, which is the primary frequency standard, it is necessary to have a set of secondary standards.
