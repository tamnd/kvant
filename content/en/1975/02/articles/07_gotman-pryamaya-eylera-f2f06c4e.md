The properties of a triangle were well studied by the ancient Greeks.

In Euclid's significant Elements it is proved that the center of a circle circumscribed about a triangle is the point of intersection of the middle of the perediculars to its sides.

Archimedes, determining the position of the center of gravity of a homogeneous triangular plate, found that it lies on each of the three medians. The point of intersection of the medians of a triangle is called the center of softness or the center of the triangle.

It was later proven that the three altitudes of a triangle also intersect at one point, which is called its orthocenter.

The regularity in the location of these three remarkable points of the triangle - the center O of the circumcircle, the centroid G and the ortcenter H - was first discovered by the famous mathematician Leonhard Euler (1707-1783). Let us first examine one special case: right-angled triangle ABC (Fig. 1).

The middle O of the hypotenuse AB is the center of the circle circumscribed around it. The centride G divides the median CO in the ratio 2:1, counting from the vertex C. The legs AC and BC are the altitudes of the triangle, therefore the vertex C of the right angle coincides with the orthocenter H of the triangle. Thus, the points O, G, H lie on the same line, and $\overrightarrow{OH} = 3\overrightarrow{OG}$. Using the method of coordinates, Euler proved that the same connection exists between the three indicated points or about a triangle. We will prove this fact using vectors, and the reader is required to be familiar with only the simplest operations: adding vectors and multiplying a vector by a number.

Division of a segment in a given relation

Let A, B and O be given points in the plane, and it is known that the point G divides the segment AB in the ratio k: $\frac{\overrightarrow{AG}}{\overrightarrow{GB}} = k$ (Fig. 2). Let us express the vector $\overrightarrow{OG}$ in terms of the vectors $\overrightarrow{OA}$ and $\overrightarrow{OB}$. To do this, we substitute into equality $\overrightarrow{AG} = k \times \overrightarrow{GB}$ the expressions of all vectors through $\overrightarrow{OG}$, $\overrightarrow{OA}$ and $\overrightarrow{OB}$: $\overrightarrow{OG} - \overrightarrow{OA} = k (\overrightarrow{OB} - \overrightarrow{OG})$. Solving this equation for $\overrightarrow{OG}$, we get

$$\overrightarrow{OG} = \frac{\overrightarrow{OA} + k\overrightarrow{OB}}{1 + k}.$$ (1)

For example, if G is the midpoint of the segment $\overrightarrow{AB}$, then $k = 1$ and $\overrightarrow{OG} = \frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{OB})$.

$$3\overrightarrow{PG} = \overrightarrow{PA} + \overrightarrow{PB} + \overrightarrow{PC},$$

$$\overrightarrow{PG} = \frac{\overrightarrow{PC} + 2\overrightarrow{PD}}{3},$$

pose $\overrightarrow{PG'} = \overrightarrow{PG}$, and the point $G'$ coincides with the point $G$. Consequently, all three medians of the triangle intersect at one point $G$, determined by correspondence (2).

**Eulerian straight line**

From the proven Theorems 1 and 2, the property of remarkable points of a triangle that interests us follows.

Theorem 3. *The center $O$ of the circumscribed circle, the centroid $G$ and the orthocenter $H$ of any triangle lie on the same straight line, and the point $G$ lies between the points $O$ and $H$ and $|OG| : |GH| = 1 : 2$.*

Comparing this equality with equality (3), we obtain

In stereometry, the simplest polyhedron - a tetrahedron (triangular pyramid) plays the same role as a triangle in planimetry. The properties of a triangle and a tetrahedron are largely similar. Let's try to extend the property of remarkable points of a triangle to a tetrahedron.

**Sphere circumscribed about a tetrahedron**

It is known that around any tetrahedron one can describe a sphere, its center $O$ lies on the perpendiculars to the faces of the tetrahedron, constructed at the centers of the circles described around the faces.

**Medians of a tetrahedron**

The segment connecting the vertex of the tetrahedron with the centroid of the opposite face is called the *median* of the tetrahedron. The properties of the medians of a tetrahedron are similar to the properties of the medians of a triangle.

Theorem 4. *Four medians of the tetrahedron $ABCD$ intersect at one point $G$, which divides each of them in the ratio $3:1$, counting from the vertex of the tetrahedron, and*

$$4\overrightarrow{PG} = \overrightarrow{PA}+\overrightarrow{PB}+\overrightarrow{PC}+\overrightarrow{PD}, \quad (4)$$

*where $P$ is any point in space.*

Proof. Let us take the point $G$ on the median $DG_1$ of the tetrahedron $ABCD$, defined by the relation $|DG| : |GG_1| = 3:1$ (Fig. 5). According to formula (1),

$$\overrightarrow{PG} = \frac{\overrightarrow{PD}+3\overrightarrow{PG_1}}{4}.$$

Considering that the centroid $G_1$ of triangle $ABC$ satisfies the relation $3\overrightarrow{PG_1} = \overrightarrow{PA}+\overrightarrow{PB}+\overrightarrow{PC}$, we obtain

By calculating the vector $\vec{PG'}$ with its end at the point $G'$ dividing any of the other three medians of the tetrahedron in the ratio $3:1$ (counting from the vertex), we obtain the same expression. This means that all four medians of the tetrahedron intersect at one point $G$, satisfying relation (4). Point $G$ is called the *center of gravity* (or *centroid*) of the tetrahedron.

**Tetrahedron heights**

The altitudes of a triangle always intersect at one point. By analogy, we can assume that the heights of any tetrahedron also intersect at one point. However, this is not true.

For example, consider a tetrahedron $ABCD$ with a right dihedral angle at edge $AB$, in which $|AC| = |BC|$ but $|AD| \neq |BD|$ (Fig. 6). The altitudes $CE$ and $DF$ of the tetrahedron lie respectively in the faces $ABC$ and $ABD$, but the point $E$ is the middle of $AB$, and $F$ is not. If the lengths of the edges $DA$ and $DB$ were equal, then the bases $E$ and $F$ of the heights would coincide, but the other two heights of the tetrahedron cannot pass through the point $E$.

Thus, even two heights of a tetrahedron may not have a common point.

Nevertheless, there are also tetrahedra, all four heights of which intersect at one point. This would be, for example, the tetrahedron $ABCD$ with right flat angles at the vertex $D$. The edges $DA$, $DB$ and $DC$ are its heights, and the vertex $D$ is the *orthocenter* (the point of intersection of all four heights).

Theorem 6. The cenir O of the circumscribed sphere, the cenirod G and the orthocentric terahedra ABCD lie on the same straight line, and the points O and H are symmetrical with respect to the point G.

Proof. According to formulas (4) and (5)
