On the other hand, the time it takes light to travel long distances (say, over 10 km) can be recorded quite accurately, but significant error accumulates when measuring the distance itself using a length standard. In this case, the optical method is used to measure large distances, but to use it it is necessary to know the speed of light in advance. It turns out to be a vicious circle.

It was possible to resolve this contradiction in recent years, when it became practically possible to use another principle for measuring the speed of light, based on the fact that the wavelength λ and frequency ν of electromagnetic waves in a vacuum are related to each other by the fundamental relation

$$c=\lambda\nu. \quad (1)$$

By measuring the wavelength and frequency of the same electromagnetic radiation independently, we could simply multiply these quantities to obtain the speed of light. The accuracy of such an experiment will be very high if we can satisfy two requirements simultaneously.

First, we must be able to measure the wavelength and frequency of electromagnetic waves with a high degree of accuracy.

Secondly, the frequency of oscillations of the electromagnetic radiation with which we are going to experiment must be maintained as constant as possible.

From relation (1) it follows that for an electromagnetic wave the relative degrees of inconstancy of frequency and wavelength coincide.

In fact, let the frequency of the electromagnetic wave ν change by a small amount δν ≪ ν. This will entail a change in wavelength by the amount δλ, so that

So, if we have a source of electromagnetic oscillations of constant frequency, then the wavelength of such oscillations will be to the same extent constant. This is a special case of the general statement that if two physical quantities are inversely proportional to each other, then their relative inaccuracies are equal, if only the proportionality coefficient (in our case, c) is considered to be exactly known.

The creation of electromagnetic oscillations, the wavelength and frequency of which remain constant with a high degree of accuracy for a long time, has become possible in recent years thanks to the advent of lasers - sources of powerful monochromatic radiation. However, although the frequency and wavelength of laser radiation are constant with high accuracy, measuring them together faces significant technical difficulties.
