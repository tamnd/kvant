The frequency values ​​of the secondary standards change smoothly, which makes it possible to achieve greater frequency proximity and obtain as few beat frequencies as possible. The accuracy of secondary standards is no worse than the accuracy of the first eton, since they are constantly checked using cesium eton.

So, if the frequencies being compared are of different scales, the smaller frequency is raised by repeated multiplication to a value close to the larger frequency. Moreover, if the quantity under study occurs with a frequency less than the eton, its frequency is multiplied. If the quantity being studied is higher frequency, as in the case of laser radiation, the multiplication must be performed for that frequency.

Here, for example, is how the frequency of the hydrocyanic acid vapor laser HCN was measured (wavelength 3.4 × 10⁻² cm, frequency 890,758 Meters). Using a nonlinear transformation, this radiation is mixed with the twelfth harmonic of the signal emitted by a microwave oscillation generator (klystron). At the output of the nonlinear transformation, oscillations of a difference frequency equal to 30 kHz arose. The klystron frequency is in the same region of the spectrum as the cesium ethon frequency, so it can be changed directly. And knowing the klystron frequency and the frequency difference (beat frequency), it is easy to determine the frequency of laser radiation.

After the frequency of the hydrocyanic acid vapor laser radiation was measured, it became possible to measure the frequency of shorter wavelength radiation. Thus, the radiation frequency of the heavy water vapor laser D₂O was measured (wavelength 8.4·10⁻³ cm, frequency 3,557,143 Meters) which is close to the quadruple harmonic of the radiation of the previous laser (890,758 Mets×4=3,563,032 Mets). Then the frequencies of the carbon dioxide laser were measured.
