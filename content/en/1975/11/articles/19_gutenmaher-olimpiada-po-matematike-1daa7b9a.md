Olympida
in mathematics
V. L. Gutenacher

The IX All-Russian Olympiad in Mathematics was held from April 17 to April 23 in Saratov. According to the new “Regulations”, this year students in the Olympiad accepted only teams from republics and winners of last year’s All-Russian Olympiad (144 people in total). The Olympics, as always, was held in two rounds (April 18 and 20), separately for eighth, ninth and tenth grades. From the many problems proposed by the jury members and sent to «Kvant», varnatas were compiled for each class.

Olympic problems were sent from Saratov, Moscow, Leningrad, Kazan, Sverdlovsk, Kharkov, Yaroslavl and Chernovtsy. Let us note that some problems of the second day of the Olympiad have already been presented in mathematical sciences.

The conditions for mailing all the problems offered at the Olympid (except for three) were published in «Kvant» Nos. 7, 8, 9 in the “Problem «Kvant»” section, and therefore we do not place them here. (Complete varnat olympids in the magazine “Matematika at school”, 1975, no. 6.)

This year, like last year, a lot of the tasks were related to tables. Schoolchildren preparing for the next mathematics Olympiad can be safely advised to learn to solve such problems.

Oddly enough, the Olympiad participants poorly solved relatively simple geometry problems. They coped much more successfully with algebraic and logical problems, and various, sometimes unknown to the jury, solutions were found.

Let us now move on to a brief analysis of those three problems that were not included in “Problem «Kvant».

8th grade, task No. 3.
What is the smallest perimeter that a convex 32-gon can have, all of whose vertices lie in the nodes of the terminal paper from side cell 1?

This problem is equivalent to the following. Let $O$ be the checkered paper node on the side of cell 1. We need to define 32 non-zero vectors starting at point $O$ so that a) the ends of the vectors lie in the checkered paper nodes;
b) the vectors did not match in direction;
c) the sum of the lengths of these vectors was minimal.

figure 1 shows 32 vectors satisfying conditions a) - b): these are four vectors of length 1, four vectors of length $\sqrt{2}$, eight vectors of length $\sqrt{5}$, eight vectors of length $\sqrt{10}$ and eight vectors of length $\sqrt{13}$. Ptak, the smallest perimeter is $4(1 + \sqrt{2}) + 8(\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{10} + \sqrt{13})$.

9th grade, task No. 1.
In the convex six-jawed figure $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ $A_5A_6$ the midpoints of the diagonals $A_6A_2$, $A_1A_3$, $A_2A_4$, $A_3A_5$, $A_4A_6$, $A_5A_1$ are denoted by the corresponding ones by $B_1$, $B_2$, $B_3$, $B_4$, $B_5$, $B_6$. Let us prove that if the six-jawed $B_1B_2B_3B_4$ $B_5B_6$ is convex, then its area is four times smaller than the area $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ $A_5A_6$.

Consider the quadrilaterals $A_1A_2A_3A_6$ and $B_3B_4B_5B_6$ (see Fig. 2). The diagonal $A_1A_3$ of the first quadrilateral is parallel to the diagonal $B_4B_6$ of the second and $|A_1A_3| = 2|B_4B_6|$, since the diagonal $B_4B_6$ is the midline in the triangle $A_1A_3A_5$. Likewise, the $A_2A_3$ diagonal is parallel to the $B_3B_5$ and $|A_2A_3| = 2|B_3B_5|$ diagonals, since the $B_3B_5$ diagonal is the midline in the $A_2A_6A_4$ triangle.

Since the area of ​​a convex quadrilateral is equal to half the product of the length of the diagonals and the sine of the angle between them, we find that the area of ​​the quadrilateral $B_3B_4B_5B_6$ is four times less than the area of ​​the quadrilateral $A_1A_2A_3A_6$. Similarly, the area of ​​the quadrilateral $B_6B_1B_2B_3$ (it is not present in figure) is four times smaller than the area of ​​the quadrilateral $A_3A_4A_5A_6$. The indicated quadrilaterals form a correspondence between the first and second hexagons, and thus we require that the correspondence is proven.

10th grade, task No. 2
Let us prove that for positive $a, b$, с the inequality $a^3 + b^3 + c^3 + 3abc \geq ab (a + b) + bc (b + c) + ac (a + c)$ holds.
Since the expression $a^3 + b^3 + c^3 + 3abc$ is symmetric with respect to $a, b, c$, we can assume without loss of generality that $a \geq b \geq c > 0$. The written expression can be converted into the following:

$$(a - b)^2 (a + b - c) + c (c - a) (c - b).$$
From the assumption $a \geq b \geq c > 0$, it obviously follows that

$$(a - b)^2 (a + b - c) \geq 0$$
and $c (c - a) (c - b) \geq 0$.

Adding these two inequalities, we obtain the desired inequality.

At the Sixth International Mathematical Olympiad the following problem was proposed:

Let $a, b$ and $c$ be the lengths of a row of some triangle. Prove that $a^2 (b + c - a) + b^2 (c + a - b) + c^2 (a + b - c) \leq 3 abc$.

It is easy to see that this inequality coincides with the inequality of problem No. 2 just analyzed, however, additional conditions simplify the solution.

And finally, a few words about what it means to “solve a problem” at the Olympiad. Solving a problem is not everything: you need to be able to write its solution correctly. At the Olympiad, many complex, non-standard problems were proposed, recording the solution to which actually requires a certain skill. Here, first of all, there is a lack of general acceptance of designations and terminology. Schoolchildren come out of difficult situations in different ways: some more successfully, others less successfully. They come up with their own definitions, introduce their own designations, and sometimes the jury is written to decipher it when they come to put in a “consultation.” Most often, despite the complexity of the presentation, the solution to the problem turns out to be absolutely correct from a mathematical point of view - to some extent, it was even possible to logically justify the complexity of phrases and phrases.
