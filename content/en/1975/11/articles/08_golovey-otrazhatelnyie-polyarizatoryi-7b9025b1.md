Watching sunbeams reflected from glass or from the surface of water, you may have never thought about what reflected light is. At first glance, it seems that the reflected ray is no different from the incident ray in anything except its direction. In fact, this is not so. The reason for this is the transverse nature of light waves.

Back in 1808, the French scientist Etienne-Louis Malus discovered that light reflected from glass is partially polarized. This means that the intensity of the waves that make up reflected light is no longer the same in different directions, as with natural light. The direction image appears. In the reflected beam, the intensity of waves with this direction of oscillation is maximum, and the intensity of waves with the direction of oscillation perpendicular to the image is minimal. Partial polarization of light occurs during specular reflection from the boundaries of any two dielectrics.

How is light polarized when reflected, and what does the degree of polarization depend on? To understand this phenomenon, let's take a closer look at it.

You can make a polarizer yourself quite simply. The refractive index of a typical stack is approximately 1.5–1.6. The Brewster angle in this case is 56-58. This means that any piece of a sheet stack, tilted so that the light falls on it at an angle of 56 to the normal, will serve as a polarizer quite well, and with its help you can observe all the effects associated with the polarization of light. Even more convenient polarizers can be opaque dark dielectrics, for example, black glass or exposed and developed negatives. In this case, I observe no less light passing through the plate. However, the possible designs of a reflective polarizer are shown in figure 1. The polarizer consists of a cylindrical or rectangular body, which can be glued together from cardboard and coated with black paint on the inside. At an angle equal to 90° - φ₀ ≈ 30° to the axis of the body, a glass plate or blackened negative is fixed. A hole is made in the side of the body for observation. A beam of light directed along the axis of the housing falls on glass or some other dielectric from a smooth surface at the Brewster angle and is observed by the eye through a hole in the housing. With a pair of these polarizers, it is possible to make polarizers and several other designs.

Since the light reflected from the boundary of two dielectrics is polarized using directional vibrations that are perpendicular to the plane of incidence, the refracted beam must also be polarized. When falling at the Brewster angle, the polarization of the refracted rays is maximum, but far from complete. Light passing through the air-glass interface is noticeably polarized. If light passes inside a plane-parallel plate, then refraction occurs again on the second surface at the same Brewster angle, and the polarization of the light passing through the plate increases even more. If you stack several plates in succession (several folded plates are called a stack), then the polarization of the transmitted light will increase rapidly as the number of plates in the stack increases. After passing through 8-10 plates, both transmitted and reflected light will be practically completely polarized. The intensities of the reflected and transmitted rays in this case are equal to each other and amount to half the intensity of the incident light (if, of course, absorption in the glass can be neglected). The frame for such a polarizer can also be glued together from cardboard, only observation should now be carried out along the axis of the body, in the light. The appearance of color is due to the fact that polarized light incident on the anisotropic sample from the first polarizer disintegrates in the sample into two beams having different propagation velocities and mutually perpendicular directions of polarization. At the exit from the test sample, these two beams are added. Since the velocities of these rays in the sample were different, the rays acquire a phase difference, the magnitude of which depends on the anisotropy of the sample and on the wavelength of the incident light. Therefore, when added, the rays of light give an interface color.

When looking at different objects between polarizers, you will easily find products with optical anisotropic areas. Look how beautiful the bottom of a coffee melange cup looks between the polarizers (Fig. 2). Ordinary cellophane, polythene film, and mica acquire a color similar to the color of soap films or oil stains on water. From pieces of such materials you can make a mosaic ornament or a small picture, and the desired color can be selected by rotating individual pieces around an axis or by placing them on top of each other. In this way, the picture shown in figure 3 was made from cellophane. The cellophane was taken from common packaging bags in which sweets are sold in stores. The polarization of scattered light can also be observed.

Take any vessel with transparent walls of any shape: a cylindrical glass, a spherical flask or a cell with flat walls, a half-liter or liter glass jar. You can also take a bottle made of unpainted glass. Fill the vessel with goda, then add a few drops of ink or ink to it and stir. Illuminate the vessel with some source that creates a directed beam of light, for example, an electron flashlight or an overhead projector. If you look perpendicular to the direction of light propagation, you will see a trace of the beam in the bulb. This is light scattered by particles. Observing it through a polarizer, one can find that it is plane-polarized, since when the polarizer is rotated, the trace of the light beam in the bulb is extinguished twice per revolution of the polarizer. The same thing is revealed if you look at the vessel vertically from above or below.

The same effect can be observed when sunlight is scattered in the atmosphere.

The property of a sow to polarize when reflected from a dielectric is widely used in technology; you can observe some of the features of this phenomenon yourself. Using polarizers, you can eliminate or reduce light reflected from glass, enamel, plastic, asphalt, liquids and other non-metallic surfaces. For example, when taking photographs, you can weaken or completely remove the glare from the lenses of glasses that interfere with your eyes; reflection of the sky in the window glass of buildings or on the water; glare that makes it difficult to see glass paintings or things in display cases, and so on.

Very interesting effects can be obtained by observing and photographing the surfaces of water bodies. When light falls obliquely on the surface of the water, part of it is specularly reflected and blinds the eyes, exposes the photographic film, and interferes with the observation of underwater objects. However, if you use an appropriately oriented polarizer (the desired degree of glare suppression is achieved simply by rotating the field)

Tree leaves and grass partially polarize obliquely incident light. The intensity of the radiation from the sun and sky reflected by the leaves is greatest when the leaves are tilted in such a way that the specularly reflected light is directed towards the observer. Through neutral (gray) polarized sunglasses, the colors of leaves, grass and flowers are softer and more saturated than without polarizers.

Polaroid windows are used in airport control towers to improve visibility of aircraft landing and taking off. Polarizers are also used on assembly lines and during product quality inspection to improve the visibility of minor defects. The polarization properties of reflected light are even used in the fishing industry to protect the eyes from the glare of fish scales when sorting fish in fisheries.

In practice, we quite often encounter not natural, but partially polarized light. By making polarizers, you can easily see this for yourself.
