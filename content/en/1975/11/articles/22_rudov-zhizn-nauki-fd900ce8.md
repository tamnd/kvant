⟦folio 68⟧

Life of Science

This is the name of a book, unusual in many respects, published by the Nauka publishing house at the end of 1973 *). It contains introductory articles and prefaces to the most famous works of major scientists in the history of science, published over the last five hundred years. The book opens with a preface by Nicolaus Copernicus to his famous work “On the Rotation of the Celestial Spheres” and ends with a preface by Wolfgang Pauli to his book on the theory of relativity.

Galileo, Kepler, Hooke, Newton, Lomonsov, Freiel, Ampere, Faraday, Maxwell, Boltzmann, Hertz, Loretz, Sklodskaya-Curie, Rutherford, Planck, Bohr, Einstein, Mandelstam, Landdu, Fermi - these are the names of some of the authors of this book in the part where it deals with problems of physics. Mathematics is represented by articles by Euler, d'Alembert, Lagrange, Laplace, Gaus, Lobachevsky, Cauchy, Poincaré, Hilbert, Neumann and other outstanding scientists. And in the section on the science of the Earth and the Universe one encounters the names of Kant and Tsiolkovsky, Vernadsky and Hubble. The book also includes portraits of all the scientists whose forewords form the content. Where this was possible, scientists are processed in time closer to the time of writing the works used in this book.

The compiler of the book and the author of biographical sequences, Professor of the Moscow Physical Institute S.P. Kapitsa, continued the enormous work of selecting and processing the vast literary heritage. Academicians P. L. Kapitsa, V. A. Engelhardt, P. K. Anokhin and V. M. Kedrov, corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences L. A. Vaishtein and a number of other scientists participated in the preparation of this book. Some of the prefaces published in it were published for the first time in Russian.

Like most books, The Life of Science is not free from minor errors and inaccuracies. In particular, it addresses individual errors in dates. For example, in Ampère’s brief biography, the year of publication of his main work, “The Theory of Electronic Phenomena Derived Exclusively from Experience” (1823 together with 1826), is incorrectly indicated. However, these little things do not affect the character of the book - which is certainly original and extremely interesting.

Of course, a schoolchild, even in the tenth grade, cannot master everything that is written in this book. But much of what is presented in it is accessible to schoolchildren; they will find there a rich forest for reflection. As for students of pedagogical institutes and universities, as well as teachers, their acquaintance with this book will certainly be very fruitful.

In conclusion, let us cite as an example a brief biography of Isaac Newton, which will be preceded by the prefaces to his main works “Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy” and “Optics”.

We would like future readers of this book to be convinced for themselves of the undoubted merits of the biographical material presented in it.

“Isaac Newton was born in the village of Wolstrop, in Lincoln, 200 km north of London. Newton's mother came from a simple farming family; those who knew characterized her as a woman of “exceptional merit and kindness.” Newton's father was a "wild, strange and weak man"; he died before the birth of his son, who was born prematurely and weak. Nevertheless, Newton was distinguished by his good health: he lived to be 84 years old.

Newton was brought up in the house of his great-grandmother Hey-skow. He attended school in Gretham, near Woolstrop. When he was 18 years old, on the advice of his day, associated with Ayscough, he entered Trinity College, Cambridge University. In 1665 he became a bachelor; a year earlier, an attempt to obtain this first academic degree was unsuccessful due to an unsatisfactory exam in geometry!

The years 1665-1667 were exceptional for Newton, and essentially for science, as Newton spent in desperate villages, where he went to escape from the plague that was evident at that time. It was during these two years of solitude and concentration that his research in optics was completed: he decomposed white light into a spectrum, found rings called Newton's rings, and proposed a reflective telephone. At the same time, he obtained the most important results in the field of mechanics, discovered the expansion of the binomial and invented mathematical analysis. During these same years, he outlined a program of research in physics, the implementation of which he devoted his life to.

Returning to Cambridge, Newton became Master and Fellow of Trinity College in 1668. The following year he took the Lucasian chair, left by his teacher and friend Barruu. Newton presented his first work on optics to the Royal Society in 1672 and soon became a member of that society. Newton also studied chemistry, inventing glory for the mirror telescope, and alchemy, trying to obtain gold. True, he did not publish a single line in this area.

Attracted by Hooke's writing to the problem of explaining the motion of the Moon and planets using the force of gravity, which varies inversely with the square of the distance, Newton turned to celestial mechanics, and in 1679 he deduced Kepler's laws. The results of his research, which led to the creation of classical mechanics, were presented in “Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy,” published in 1686. Apparently, the successful creation of this great book, written in a year and a half, was reflected in Newton’s condition, and for some time he suffered from a nervous disorder. In the last years of his life, Newton took up theology. However, Newton's approach to the Holy Scriptures led his precise mind to contradict the dogmas of the church, which at that time was far from safe. Apparently, it was only thanks to the presence of influential friends that he was able to take a break from these activities. In 1696, Newton moved to London, where he was appointed first custodian and then director of the Mint.

In 1701, Newton was elected Member of Parliament from Cambridge University and finally, in 1703, he became Chairman of the Royal Society, which he remained until his death. Newton was buried in Westminster Abbey.

Newton was not married and had few friends. He never left England. His life was closed and monotonous. Towards the end of his life, he became intolerant of criticism, and spent a lot of energy and feelings arguing about priority with Hooke, Flamsteed, Leibniz; nevertheless, he was reluctant and slow to publish his results. Thus, his “Optics” was published only in 1704, after Hooke’s death.”
