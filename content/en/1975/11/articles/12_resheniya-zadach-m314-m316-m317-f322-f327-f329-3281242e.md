M314. Among all 9-digit numbers whose decimal notation contains the digit 0, find the one for which the difference between the number itself and the product of its digit is a) the smallest;
b) the largest;
What is the answer for n-digit numbers for any n?

We will solve the problem immediately for n-digit numbers.
Let's start with task a). Let the required number - let's denote it by A - be written in numbers $a_1, \dots, a_n$: $A = \overline{a_1, \dots, a_n}$. Then
$$A = a_1 \cdot 10^n - 1 + a_2 \cdot 10^n - 2 + \dots + a_{n-1} \cdot 10 + a_n.$$
Let us denote the difference between the number A and the product of its digits by B:
$$B = a_1 \cdot 10^n - 1 + \dots + a_{n-1} \cdot 10 + a_n - a_1 \cdot a_2 \dots a_n.$$
Let us show that if B is the smallest difference, then the number A cannot simultaneously have two digits other than one or nine.
In fact, let there be two such numbers: $a_k$ and $a_l$. Then, by increasing and then decreasing the digit $a_k$ by one, we can create new n-digit numbers (with no zeros among the digits):
$$A_1 = a_1 \cdot 10^n - 1 + \dots + (a_k + 1) \cdot 10^n - k + \dots + a_{n-1} \cdot 10 + a_n;$$
$$A_2 = a_1 \cdot 10^n - 1 + \dots + (a_k - 1) \cdot 10^n - k + \dots + a_{n-1} \cdot 10 + a_n.$$
Let us write down the differences $B_1$ and $B_2$ between the numbers $A_1, A_2$ and the product of their digits:
$$B_1 = A_1 - a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot (a_k + 1) \cdot \dots \cdot a_{n-1} \cdot a_n = (A - a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_n) + 10^n - k - a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{k-1} \cdot a_{k+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n = B + (10^n - k - a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{k-1} \cdot a_{k+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n);$$
$$B_2 = A_2 - a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot (a_k - 1) \cdot \dots \cdot a_{n-1} \cdot a_n = B + (-10^n - k + a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{k-1} \cdot a_{k+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n).$$
By assumption, B is the smallest difference; therefore, the inequalities $B \leqslant B_1$ and $B \leqslant B_2$ must be satisfied simultaneously:
$$\begin{cases} 10^n - k - a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{k-1} \cdot a_{k+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n \geqslant 0, \\ -10^n - k + a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{k-1} \cdot a_{k+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n \geqslant 0. \end{cases}$$
where
$$a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{k-1} \cdot a_{k+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n = 10^n - k.$$

In exactly the same way we get that
$$a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{l-1} \cdot a_{l+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n = 10^n - l.$$

Dividing (1) by (2), we get:
$$\frac{a_l}{a_h} = 10^{l-k} \quad (l \neq k),$$

but this cannot be, since $a_l$ and $a_k$ are natural numbers less than 10.

This means that the desired number cannot have two digits other than 1 and 9 at the same time.

Suppose that the number you are looking for has one digit different from 1 and 9—let it be the digit $a_{n-p}$. Then, as above, we have
$$10^p = a_1 \cdot \dots \cdot a_{n-p-1} \cdot a_{n-p+1} \cdot \dots \cdot a_n,$$

where the numbers $a_1, \dots, a_{n-p-1}, a_{n-p+1}, \dots, a_n$ are equal to 1 or 9. But with $p \geqslant 1$ such an equality cannot exist: its left side is divisible by 10, and its right side is not divisible.

If $p = 0$, then all digits $a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}$ must be ones, and the digit $a_n$ can be any. The number itself has the form $\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-1} a_n$. Note that the difference between a number of this type and the product of its digit is constructed and does not depend on the last digit $a_n$:

$$\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-1} a_n - 1 \dots 1 \cdot a_n = \underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-1} 0.$$

Below we will show that it is precisely this difference that is the smallest (that is, that the number $\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-1} a_n$ is the required one).

Ptak, if the exclusive number has a digit other than 1 and 9, then it must be of the form $\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-1} a_n$.

Let us now assume that all the digits of the exclusive number are ones or nines. Let this number consist of $k$ nines and $n-k$ ones. Then the product of its digit is equal to $9^k$ - the constructed number, and in order for the difference between the number and the product of its digit to be the smallest, it is necessary that the number itself (composed of $k$ nines and $n-k$ ones) be the smallest.

It is clear that such a number will be the number $\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-k} \underbrace{9 \dots 9}_{k},$ in which all ones are in the highest digits.

Let's make up the difference

$$B' = \underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-k} \underbrace{9 \dots 9}_{k} - 9^k.$$

Now take the number that has one more unit: $\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-k+1} \underbrace{9 \dots 9}_{k-1}$. For it the corresponding difference is equal to

$$B'' = \underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-k+1} \underbrace{9 \dots 9}_{k-1} - 9^k - \underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-k+1} \underbrace{9 \dots 9}_{k-1} + 9^k-1 = 8(10^k-1-9^k-1) \geqslant 0$$

(Equality is sufficient for $k=1$); that is, $B' \geqslant B''$.

This means that by replacing the first nine on the left with one each time, we thereby reduce the difference between the resulting number and the product of its digits. Therefore, the number consisting of only ones and nines, for which the indicated difference is minimal, is the number $\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-1}$.

By virtue of what has been said above, the difference does not depend on what digit is in last place for a number, all other digits of which are equal to one; that is, any number can be in the last place.

So, the answer in problem a) is: eliminating $n$-digit numbers has the form $\underbrace{1 \dots 1}_{n-1} a_n$, where $a_n$ is any digit.

Since the velocities of stars rotating around the center of mass of the system are constant in absolute value, the stars move in circles, and these circles are concentric and their common center is located at the center of mass of the system. Let us denote by $m_1$ and $m_2$ the masses of the stars, and by $r_1$ and $r_2$ the radii of the corresponding circles. F326. The connection, closed with a lid, is filled to the top with rigidity, in which there are two springs. How will the pressure in the liquid change if the springs merge? The initial pressure in the liquid is $p_0$, the tension surface coefficient is $\sigma$, the radius of each spring is $r_0$. Consider the process isometric.

Let us assume that the system under consideration is in a state of weightlessness. This is equivalent to the fact that there is no hydrostatic pressure, and therefore the pressure in the liquid is always the same. Let us write down the equilibrium condition for each spring before merging:

$$p_0 + \frac{2\sigma}{r_0} = p_r,$$

where $\frac{2\sigma}{r_0}$ is the additional pressure caused by the merging of the surface tension of the liquid, $p_r$ is the gas pressure in the spring.

After merging two springs into one, the gas pressure will not change, since the volume occupied by the gas remains constant, and the additional pressure will change, since the radius of the spring becomes different. Radnus $r$ of the new spring will be found from the condition for constructing the volume:

$$2 \frac{4}{3} \pi r_0^3 = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3.$$

From here

$$r = r_0 \sqrt[3]{2}.$$

The equilibrium condition for this spring can be represented as

$$p + \frac{2\sigma}{r} = p_r$$

(p is the new pressure in the liquid). Then from (1) - (3) the change in pressure in the liquid is equal to

$$\Delta p = p - p_0 = \frac{2\sigma}{r_0} \left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}\right).$$

Try to solve this problem yourself for the case of springs of different radii.

At the amperage of the magnetoelectric system, a variable current is passed through the turns of a light movable frame rigidly attached to the pointer. The frame is located between the positions of the permanent magnet. Two spiral springs connect the coils of the frame to the external electrical circuit.

The magnetic forces acting on the current-carrying frame are proportional to the magnitude of the current in the frame, which means that the rotational moment of these forces is also proportional to the magnitude of the variable current. When the frame is rotated, a counteracting moment arises to control the forces of the springs. This moment is proportional to the angle of rotation of the frame. When the counteracting torque of the control forces becomes equal to the rotational moment of the magnetic forces, equilibrium will be established. Thus, the angle of rotation of the frame in the magnetoelectric amperage is directly proportional to the variable current.

In electrodynamic amperage, the movable frame is placed inside a stationary coil, the turns of which are already included in the external electrical circuit (series or parallel to the turns of the movable frame). In this case, the magnetic field of the stationary coil is not constant, but changes in proportion to the magnitude of the current. Consequently, the magnetic forces acting on the movable frame and their rotational moment change in proportion to the square of the current. The counteracting moment of control of the spring forces, as in the first case, is proportional to the angle of rotation of the frame. Therefore, the angle of rotation of the frame, and therefore the arrow of the electrodynamic system device, is proportional to the square of the variable current.

Everything we say relates essentially to direct current. How will the devices behave when they are connected to an alternating current circuit? Obviously, the torques in both instruments will change over time. However, due to inertia, the clamping frame does not keep up with the change in the rotational moment, but reacts only to its average value if the period of current change is much less than the period of the frame’s own oscillations (as is assumed in the conditions of the problem).

Therefore, the angle of rotation of the frame of a magnetoelectric amperor will be proportional to the average value of the alternating current over the period, and the angle of rotation of the electrodynamic amperor will be proportional to the average value of the square of the current over the period.

Since the average value over the period of the magnetoelectric system will not respond to such a current. This device can only be used for direct current changes.

Electrodynamic system devices are suitable for both direct and alternating currents. Generally they are graded according to direct current. When an amplifier is connected to an alternating current circuit, the angle of rotation of the frame is proportional to the average value of the squared current $I^2(t)$ over the period, therefore, the reading of the electrodynamic amperor is equal to $\sqrt{I^2(t)}$, i.e., the effective (effective) value of the alternating current.

F329 *). Many of you have probably noticed that the moment you step on wet sand, it becomes lighter. This is due to the fact that the sand becomes dry. But as soon as you remove the nose, the trail left by the nose immediately fills with water. Explain this phenomenon.

To explain what happens to sand on the river bank, let's start with... balls. Identical balls can be placed on a plane so that each of them touches six other balls. In the holes between the balls of the first layer you can put the balls of the second layer. Each of them will touch three balls of the lower layer and six neighbors of its layer, etc. The arrangement of balls obtained in this way is called a close packing of balls. If you break the tight packing by moving the balls of one of the holes between the balls of the lower layer, the gaps between the balls will increase. The volume of the entire system will also increase. This means that if a system of densely packed balls is subject to forces that break the tight packing, the volume of the system increases due to an increase in the spaces between the balls.

Any granular medium behaves similarly. Take, for example, millet (or coffee), fill a glass with it, shaking the glass slightly so that the grains are arranged, forming the densest possible package. Then press down on the millet. Pressure will lead to an increase in the volume occupied by the grains, that is, to a violation of the tight packing. Some of the grains will spill out. If you now lightly move around the glass so that the grains are again “packed” as tightly as possible, the glass turns out to be not filled to the top.

Now let's return to the sand on the shore. It is also tightly packed. When pressure is applied to the sand, the dense packing is destroyed, and the volume of the sand increases due to the increase in the space between the sands. Water from the upper layers of sand goes deeper, filling these enlarged gaps. The sand seems to be drying up. When the foot is removed, the dense packing is restored, and the water displaced from the newly reduced spaces is filled with the trace left by the foot.
