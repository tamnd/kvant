Averages of various phenomena of different physical natures can often be found in positive phenomena that exhibit the same signs and patterns. In such cases, we talk about physical analogies, or analogous (that is, positive) systems. Physical analogies that exist between electrical, mechanical, acoustic and other systems have long been successfully used in research and calculations. Methods based on the use of analogy, in a number of cases, turn out to be very fruitful in solving problems. They allow one to reduce the solution of some problems to the solution of other (already known) problems (often from other branches of physics).

For example, one cannot misunderstand the analogy between the law of universal gravitation and Coulomb's law.

Expressions for the gravitational force between two material points with masses $M$ and $m$ and for the force of electrostatic interaction between two exact charges $Q$ and $q$ have the form

$$F_T = \gamma \frac{Mm}{r^2}; \quad F_{3.1} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Qq}{r^2}.$$

Here $\gamma$ is the graviton constant, $\varepsilon_0$ is the electric constant, $r$ is the distance between the interacting bodies.

Since forces are expressed by similar formulas (in general cases, inversely proportional to the square of the distance), the movement of point materials in a graviton field (in the gravitational field) and the movement of a charged body in the field of a gravitational charge are described by the same equations. True, there is a difference. Gravitational forces always tend to lick bodies (body attraction), and electrical forces, depending on the signs of interacting charges, can be both attraction and repulsion.

Let us give another example of a physical analogy between mechanical and electrical systems. The processes occurring in the electrical oscillatory circuit (Fig. 1) are similar to external oscillations of a weight.

This analogy is discussed in detail in the school textbook (see the textbook for the 10th grade of high school). Systems are similar because the phenomena occurring in them are described by the same mathematical relationships. Newton's second law for an external load and Ohm's law for an oscillatory circuit are written in the same way:

$$m \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = -kx, \quad L \frac{\Delta i}{\Delta t} = -\frac{1}{C} q,$$

where $v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ is the speed of the load, $i = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}$ is the current in the circuit.

From this it can be seen that the mass of the load $m$ can be compared with the inductance of the coil $L$, the stiffness of the spring $k$ - with the inverse value of the capacitance $\frac{1}{C}$. The analogy of these two systems is also preserved in the presence of energy dissipation: the friction coefficient $\beta$ at low speeds of body motion in a liquid or gas is similar to the electrical resistance $R$.

Let us now consider several specific problems, the solutions of which are simplified if we use the analogue method.

Task 1. The clearing is partially filled with liquid. The cutting section is the same everywhere; the total length of the filled part of the cabin is equal to 1. Determine the period of oscillation of the liquid level in this system.

Let's try to check analogues with the well-known problem of oscillations of a load suspended from the outside, and thus find the period of oscillations of this oscillatory system.

Let the liquid level in one of the elbows decrease by $x$, and in the other, correspondingly, increase by $x$ (see Fig. 2), then the difference in levels will be equal to $2x$, and the liquid in the left elbow will be affected by the uncompensated gravity force of a liquid column with a height of $2x$:

$$F = -2S\rho gx.$$

Here $S$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the cutting, $\rho$ is the density of the liquid. The minus sign in this formula means that the force $F$ tends to return the system to the equilibrium position (as in the case of an external load). The value $2S\rho g$, constant for this system, corresponds to the spring stiffness $k$. Thus, the movement of the fluid occurs under the action of quasi-elastic *) motion. Task analogy:

The tasks are similar, since in all three cases we are faced with the same patterns. Let's look at this in more detail.

In problem 2 you need to find the work done on the deformation of the spring. This work can be determined graphically. Work on a small section of the path $\Delta x$ (i.e. on such a section that the force $F = kx$ can be considered constant) is:

$$\Delta A = F\Delta x = kx\Delta x.$$

This means that the total work $A$ when the coordinates change from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is equal to the shaded area on figure 4, i.e.

$$A = \frac{k}{2} \left( x_2^2 - x_1^2 \right).$$

Let us recall that the quantities $\frac{kx^2}{2}$ are the potential energy $\Pi$ of an elastically deformed spring. Knowing the expression for this energy, we could immediately write the formula for work ($A = \Delta \Pi$). But here we want to make sure that a similar situation will arise in other physical analogous problems.

When solving problem 3, the waste process in small portions $\Delta q$ (such that the potential difference $u$ on the capacitor can be considered constant) is transferred from one plate to another. The work of transferring the cost $\Delta q$ will be written as:

$$\Delta A = u\Delta q = \frac{1}{C} q\Delta q.$$

Problem 4 requires determining the work done by a gas during expansion. If the volume change $\Delta V$ is taken to be small enough (such that the pressure in this elementary process can be considered constant with a good approximation), then the work is written in the form: $\Delta A = p\Delta V$. Since in our case $T = \alpha V^2$, we obtain the following equations for the gas state:

$$p = \frac{RT}{V} = R\alpha V.$$

Hence,

$$\Delta A = R\alpha V\Delta V.$$

Now the analogy with problems 2 and 3 becomes obvious, and we can write an expression for the total work $A$ during gas expansion:

$$A = \frac{R\alpha}{2} \left( V_2^2 - V_1^2 \right) = \frac{R}{2} \left( T_2 - T_1 \right).$$

Let us now consider another, somewhat more complex problem.

Problem 5. What amount of heat $Q$ do we get from one mole of gas when the temperature changes from $T_1$ to $T_2$ ($T_2 > T_1$), if in the process of heating the gas it changes inversely proportional to the temperature:

$$V = \frac{\beta}{T},$$

where $\beta$ is some constant? The heat capacity of the gas at constant volume $C_V$ is given.

The solution to this problem is quite cumbersome. We will draw attention here to the unexpected analogy of this thermal problem with the well-known problem of potential charge.

Based on the first law of thermodynamics, we can write:

$$Q = \Delta U + A.$$

Here $\Delta U$ is the change in the internal energy of the gas, $A$ is the work done by the gas. The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature ($U \sim T$). Therefore, the change in internal energy in any process, and therefore in our case, is the same as in a process with a constant volume, when the entire suspended body goes only to increase the internal energy:

$$\Delta U = C_V \left( T_2 - T_1 \right).$$

Thus, the problem was reduced to calculating the work done by the gas. To find this work, let us first consider an elementary process in which the volume of a gas increases by a sufficiently small amount $\Delta V$, so that the pressure remains practically unchanged. For elementary work we can write:

$$\Delta A = p\Delta V = \frac{RT}{V} \Delta V = R\beta \frac{\Delta V}{V^2}.$$

We need to find the work done by a gas when its volume changes from the value $V_1 = \frac{\beta}{T_1}$ to the value $V_2 = \frac{\beta}{T_2}$. Here is an example of an electrical problem, from which the solution to Problem 5 immediately follows.

Problem 6. Let us name the capacitance of a spherical capacitor formed by two concentric conducting spheres $R_1$ and $R_2$.

The capacitance of the capacitor $C$ is equal to the relative charge of the capacitor $q$ to the potential difference $\Delta \varphi$ between its plates. The potential difference (by definition) is equal to the revolution made by electrical forces when moving a unit positive charge from one plate to another.

Let the inner sphere of the capacitor have a charge $q$, and the outer sphere have a charge $-q$. Then the electric field in the space between the spheres coincides with the field of the exact charge $q$, located in their common center $*$), and the direction of the field

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}; \quad R_1 \leqslant r \leqslant R_2.$$

Let a single positive charge move over a small distance $\Delta r \ll R_1$ (so that the field can be considered uniform). The work done by the electric field on this section of the path is

$$\Delta A = E \Delta r = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\Delta r}{r^2}.$$

(*) See, for example, the article by L. P. Bakanina and S. M. Kozel “Principles of superposition in electrostatics”, «Kvant», 1973, No. 3.

The total work $A$ of electrical forces when the distance changes from $R_1$ to $R_2$ can be expressed in terms of the field potential of the exact charge:

$$A = -(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1} - \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{R_2 - R_1}{R_1 R_2}.$$

Hence the capacitance of the spherical capacitor is equal to

$$C = \frac{q}{\Delta \varphi} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_2 - R_1}.$$

Let us now return to problem 5 and complete its solution, using the results of problem 6. Let us compare the expression (*) for the capacitive work during gas expansion and the expression (**) for the capacitive work of moving a unit charge in an electrostatic field. It is easy to see that these expressions are similar (in problem 5 the variable is the volume of gas $V$, and in problem 6 it is the distance $r$). This allows us to conclude that complete works $A$ in obiha servants are expressed in the same way through the variables $V$ and $r$, respectively. Therefore, by analogy with expression (***), we can write down an expression for the work of a gas when its volume changes from $V_1$ to $V_2$:

$$A = R \beta \frac{V_2 - V_1}{V_1 V_2}.$$

Taking into account that $V = \frac{\beta}{T}$, we get:

$$A = R \frac{\frac{V_2}{\beta} - \frac{V_1}{\beta}}{\frac{V_1}{\beta} \frac{V_2}{\beta}} = R \frac{\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}}{\frac{1}{T_1} \frac{1}{T_2}} = -R(T_2 - T_1).$$

The minus sign in this formula is determined by the fact that gas rolling ($A < 0$) occurs in the process under consideration.
