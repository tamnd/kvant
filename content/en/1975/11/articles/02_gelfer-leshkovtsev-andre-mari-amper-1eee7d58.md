This year marks the 200th anniversary of the birth of the great French scientist André-Marie Ampère. In the history of science, he is known mainly as the founder of electrodynamics. Meanwhile, he was a universal scientist, with merits in the fields of mathematics, chemistry, biology, and even linguistics and physiology. He was a lecturer's mind, amazing with his encyclopedic knowledge all the people who were close to him. But the name of Ampere is most firmly entrenched in the history of physics, which is reflected in such terms as “ampere” - for units of current, “Ampere’s law”, “Ampere’s force”, etc.

Ampere himself was a great master at inventing new scientific terms. It was he who introduced such words as “electrostatics”, “electrodynamics”, “solenide” into the everyday life of scientists. It is also interesting to note that, while dealing with the important problem of learning - the issue of classification of sciences, Ampere expressed the idea that in the future, it is believed, a new science will emerge about the general laws of control processes. He proposed precisely this as “cybernetics”.

Ampere's prediction came true. Currently, such a science exists and bears the name he proposed.

Andre-Marie Ampère traces her ancestry back to linguistic craftsmen. His father Jean-Jacques Ampere, together with his brothers, traded linguistic silks. He received a good education, had a good command of ancient languages ​​and tried to keep up with everything that the great French educators of the 18th century taught. He had an excellent library composed of works by famous philosophers, writers and scientists.

At the age of 38, Jean-Jacques Ampère married Jeanne Sarcet, the daughter of one of the major linguistic merchants. André-Marie Ampère was born on January 22, 1775. He spent his childhood in a small house called Polemier, bought by Jean-Jacques in the vicinity of Lyon.

By the beginning of the 19th century, electrical and magnetic phenomena were still largely mysterious; their nature was not clear. Scientists were only familiar with the properties of static charges and permanent magnets. All electrical and magnetic phenomena discovered in the experiment were explained as a result of the action of special electrical and magnetic liquids (“fluids”). So, for example, the St. Petersburg academician Epinus in his work “Olt of the Theory of Electricity and Magnetism,” published in 1759, wrote:

“There is a liquid that produces all magnetic phenomena, which can be called magnetic. This liquid is extremely thin and can pass through any feathers in the body; its particles, like the particles of the electrical fluid, mutually repel each other. This liquid, in most other bodies found in the world, does not cause any reaction; she is neither attracted nor repelled by him. However, there is a certain number of bodies, parts of which accept magnetic/material and are accepted; the body endowed with this property is the first iron, and then all the bodies called glands...”

The only quantitative characteristic of electrical and magnetic phenomena was Coulomb's law, discovered at the end of the 18th century.

Progress began only after electric current was first produced. In 1800, Volta invented the first current source, which was called the Volta table. Everything about this invention has generated enormous interest in the scientific world. Napoleon invited the Ilyan physicist to Paris to repeat his experiments in front of a select audience. There, Volta quickly delivered a report in which he expressed the idea that static electricity and electric current are identical in nature—the “motivating electric fluid,” in the terminology of that time.

Numerous experiments have shown that electric current can cause various effects - thermal, chemical, light. It was also natural to assume that there must be a connection between electricity and magnetism, which has been explored by scientists for several centuries. At the beginning of the 19th century, many scientists attempted to make this connection clear. Danish physics professor Hans-Christan Ørsted also looked for it. Back in 1807, he announced his intention to study the effect of electric current on a magnetic needle. However, for many years, experiments confirming the existence of such an action failed. Only in 1820, having placed a magnetic needle parallel to the wire connecting the two ends of a voltaic column, he saw with his own eyes the phenomenon that he had been waiting for for so many years: under the influence of the current in the conductor, the needle deviated from its usual direction. Thus, a fundamental phenomenon was discovered, which laid the foundation for electrodynamics.

In July 1820, Ørsted published his discovery in a short article entitled "Olt's Theory of Electrical Conflict on the Magnetic Arrow." He called electric current “electrical confection.”

A month later, Oersted's experiments were built in Geneva by the Swiss physicist Auguste de la Rive. Many scientists and intermediate scientists were present at the demonstration - the famous French physicist Arago. Upon returning to Paris, Arago made a report on this at two meetings of the Academy of Sciences and reproduced Oersted's experiments. He began his report with the following words: “Gentlemen, Professor Oersted in Copenhagen managed to make a wonderful discovery... which has consequences that the inquisitive, but limited, are not yet able to predict.

First of all, he repeated Oersted’s experiment, trying to better understand the nature of the phenomenon discovered by the Danish physicist. He experimentally proved that statistical electricity does not act on a magnetic needle. Only moving electricity - electric current - is able to cause such an effect. “What is the reason for this phenomenon? - Ampere asked himself. “Why does a current-carrying conductor act on a magnetic needle?”

It would be natural to assume that an electric current passing through a conductor turns it into a magnet. This is what physicists believed, in particular, the famous French physicist Biot. Ampere had a different point of view. He expressed a general idea: the only principle of action of a conductor with current on a magnetic needle is moving electricity; magnetism is just one of its many manifestations. It is not the conductor through which the current flows that becomes the magnet, but, on the contrary, the magnet is a collection of currents. Ampere's hypothesis at that time seemed extremely bold and unjustified, so it was met with critical criticism by scientists.

Ampere developed a new view of the nature of magnetic phenomena as a result of a whole series of experiments. Already at the end of the first week of directed work, he made a discovery of no less importance than Oersted - he discovered the interaction of currents. If two electrified bodies mutually accept or repel, then wouldn’t conductors through which current flow behave similarly? Ampere placed parallel straight sections of two conductors connecting the ends of two Volga columns. One conductor was fixed, the other was made movable (Fig. 1). Having passed a current through the conductors, it is observed from the interaction: when the currents are in the same directions, they are attracted, when they are opposite, they are repulsive. It also turned out that the forces acting between current-carrying conductors are not central, that is, radial differences from electrostatic forces. Ampere proposed to reflect such a sharp difference in the manifestations of static electricity and electric current in terms of correspondence. He called the area of ​​phenomena associated with charges at rest 电磁electrostatics, and with moving charges - electrodynamics.

Another week has passed. Ampere carried out new experiments that developed and confirmed his ideas. The experiment confirmed these assumptions.

Ampere's new ideas were not understood by all scientists. Some of his named colleagues, including Bio, did not agree with him. Biot and his supporters sought to prove that there was nothing fundamentally new in Ampere’s experiments, that all of them, done every day, were just variations of Oersted’s experiment.

Despite the attacks of his scientific opponents, Ampera continued his experiment. He decided to find the law of interaction of currents in the form of a strict mathematical formula and found this law, which theoretically bears his name. In the most general form, the expression for the magnitude of the force acting on the side of a small segment of conductor $\Delta l_1$, through which current flows with force $I_1$ (the first current element), on an arbitrarily located segment $\Delta l_2$ of another conductor with current $I_2$ (second current element), has the following form:

$$F_{12} = k \frac{I_1 \Delta l_1 \sin \alpha_1 I_2 \Delta l_2 \sin \alpha_2}{r_{12}^2}.$$

The direction $n$ coincides with the forward movement of the gimlet as he rotates his arm from $\Delta l_1$ to $r_{12}$. $k$ - coefficient, depends on the choice of unit system. Thus, step by step, Ampere’s works expressed a new science - electrodynamics, based on experiments and mathematical theory. All the basic ideas of this science, as Maxwell put it, essentially “came out of the head of this Newton of electricity” within a week.

From 1820 to 1826, Ampere published a number of theoretical and experimental works on electrodynamics and gave a report on this topic at almost every meeting of the Academy's physics department. In 1826, his final classic work, “The Theory of Electrodynamic Phenomena, Deduced Exclusively from Experience,” was published. The work on this book took place under very difficult conditions. In one of the letters written at this time, Ampère reported: “I am forced to read two courses of lectures; however, I do not want to completely abandon my work on Voltanic conductors and magnets.
