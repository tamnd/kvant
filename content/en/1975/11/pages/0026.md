The appearance of color is due to the fact that polarized light incident on the anisotropic sample from the first polarizer disintegrates in the sample into two beams having different propagation velocities and mutually perpendicular directions of polarization. At the exit from the test sample, these two beams are added. Since the velocities of these rays in the sample were different, the rays acquire a phase difference, the magnitude of which depends on the anisotropy of the sample and on the wavelength of the incident light. Therefore, when added, the rays of light give an interface color.

When looking at different objects between polarizers, you will easily find products with optical anisotropic areas. Look how beautiful the bottom of a coffee melange cup looks between the polarizers (Fig. 2). Ordinary cellophane, polythene film, and mica acquire a color similar to the color of soap films or oil stains on water. From pieces of such materials you can make a mosaic ornament or a small picture, and the desired color can be selected by rotating individual pieces around an axis or by placing them on top of each other. In this way, the picture shown in figure 3 was made from cellophane. The cellophane was taken from common packaging bags in which sweets are sold in stores. It is somewhat more difficult to make pictures from polystelene film. In order for the color of the film to be sufficiently bright and rich, the film must first be stretched greatly.
