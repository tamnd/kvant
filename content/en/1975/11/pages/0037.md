a) Calculate the sum of squares $2n+1$ of consecutive natural numbers. Denoting the least of them by $x-n$, we get:

$$S_{2n+1} = (x-n)^2 + (x-n+1)^2 + \dots + x^2 + (x+1)^2 + \dots + (x+n)^2 = (2n+1)x^2 + 2(1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + \dots + n^2) = (2n+1)x^2 + \frac{1}{3}n(n+1)(2n+1).$$

Specifically, $S_3 = 3x^2 + 2$, $S_5 = 5x^2 + 10$, $S_7 = 7x^2 + 28$, $S_9 = 9x^2 + 60$, $S_{11} = 11x^2 + 110$. The sum of the squares of three consecutive integers cannot be an exact square, since the square of any integer is either divisible by 3 or leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by 3, and the number $S_3 = 3x^2 + 2$ leaves a remainder of 2 when divided by 3.

Let us prove that none of the numbers $S_5$, $S_7$ and $S_9$ is also a perfect square for any integer $x$. To do this, we will use this obvious property that follows from the only factorization into prime factors: if a square is divisible by a prime $p$, then it must be divisible by $p^2$. It remains to note that $x^2 + 2$ is not divisible by 5, $x^2 + 4$ is not divisible by 7, and $3x^2 + 20$ is not divisible by 3.
