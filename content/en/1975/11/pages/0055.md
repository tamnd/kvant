Problem 4 requires determining the work done by a gas during expansion. If the volume change $\Delta V$ is taken to be small enough (such that the pressure in this elementary process can be considered constant with a good approximation), then the work is written in the form: $\Delta A = p\Delta V$. Since in our case $T = \alpha V^2$, we obtain the following equations for the gas state:

$$p = \frac{RT}{V} = R\alpha V.$$

Hence,

$$\Delta A = R\alpha V\Delta V.$$

Now the analogy with problems 2 and 3 becomes obvious, and we can write an expression for the total work $A$ during gas expansion:

$$A = \frac{R\alpha}{2} \left( V_2^2 - V_1^2 \right) = \frac{R}{2} \left( T_2 - T_1 \right).$$

Let us now consider another, somewhat more complex problem.

Problem 5. What amount of heat $Q$ do we get from one mole of gas when the temperature changes from $T_1$ to $T_2$ ($T_2 > T_1$), if in the process of heating the gas it changes inversely proportional to the temperature:

$$V = \frac{\beta}{T},$$

where $\beta$ is some constant? The heat capacity of the gas at constant volume $C_V$ is given.

The solution to this problem is quite cumbersome. We will draw attention here to the unexpected analogy of this thermal problem with the well-known problem of potential charge.

Based on the first law of thermodynamics, we can write:

$$Q = \Delta U + A.$$

Here $\Delta U$ is the change in the internal energy of the gas, $A$ is the work done by the gas. The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature ($U \sim T$). Therefore, the change in internal energy in any process, and therefore in our case, is the same as in a process with a constant volume, when the entire suspended body goes only to increase the internal energy:

$$\Delta U = C_V \left( T_2 - T_1 \right).$$

Thus, the problem was reduced to calculating the work done by the gas. To find this work, let us first consider an elementary process in which the volume of a gas increases by a sufficiently small amount $\Delta V$, so that the pressure remains practically unchanged. For elementary work we can write:

$$\Delta A = p\Delta V = \frac{RT}{V} \Delta V = R\beta \frac{\Delta V}{V^2}.$$

We need to find the work done by a gas when its volume changes from the value $V_1 = \frac{\beta}{T_1}$ to the value $V_2 = \frac{\beta}{T_2}$. Mathematically, the problem was reduced to an integration operation that went beyond the scope of the high school curriculum.
