It is clear to me what an important role in technology should be played by questions related to the study of the reflection of light from the boundary of two dielectrics.

You can make a polarizer yourself quite simply. The refractive index of a typical stack is approximately 1.5–1.6. The Brewster angle in this case is 56-58. This means that any piece of a sheet stack, tilted so that the light falls on it at an angle of 56 to the normal, will serve as a polarizer quite well, and with its help you can observe all the effects associated with the polarization of light. Even more convenient polarizers can be opaque dark dielectrics, for example, black glass or exposed and developed negatives. In this case, I observe no less light passing through the plate. However, the possible designs of a reflective polarizer are shown in figure 1. The polarizer consists of a cylindrical or rectangular body, which can be glued together from cardboard and coated with black paint on the inside. At an angle equal to 90° - φ₀ ≈ 30° to the axis of the body, a glass plate or blackened negative is fixed. A hole is made in the side of the body for observation. A beam of light directed along the axis of the housing falls on glass or some other dielectric from a smooth surface at the Brewster angle and is observed by the eye through a hole in the housing. With a pair of these polarizers, it is possible to make polarizers and several other designs.

Since the light reflected from the boundary of two dielectrics is polarized using directional vibrations that are perpendicular to the plane of incidence, the refracted beam must also be polarized. When falling at the Brewster angle, the polarization of the refracted rays is maximum, but far from complete. Light passing through the air-glass interface is noticeably polarized. If light passes inside a plane-parallel plate, then refraction occurs again on the second surface at the same Brewster angle, and the polarization of the light passing through the plate increases even more. If you stack several plates in succession (several folded plates are called a stack), then the polarization of the transmitted light will increase rapidly as the number of plates in the stack increases. After passing through 8-10 plates, both transmitted and reflected light will be practically completely polarized. The intensities of the reflected and transmitted rays in this case are equal to each other and amount to half the intensity of the incident light (if, of course, absorption in the glass can be neglected). The frame for such a polarizer can also be glued together from cardboard, only observation should now be carried out along the axis of the body, in the light.
