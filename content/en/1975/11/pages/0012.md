Thus, step by step, Ampere’s works expressed a new science - electrodynamics, based on experiments and mathematical theory. All the basic ideas of this science, as Maxwell put it, essentially “came out of the head of this Newton of electricity” within a week.

From 1820 to 1826, Ampere published a number of theoretical and experimental works on electrodynamics and gave a report on this topic at almost every meeting of the Academy's physics department. In 1826, his final classic work, “The Theory of Electrodynamic Phenomena, Deduced Exclusively from Experience,” was published. The work on this book took place under very difficult conditions. In one of the letters written at this time, Ampère reported: “I am forced to read two courses of lectures; however, I do not want to completely abandon my work on Voltanic conductors and magnets. I have only a few minutes." Here we are talking about Ampere's lectures on higher mathematics, which involved numerous cases. However, from 1822 to 1824, one of them was a young mathematician from Russia, Michal Vasilievich Ostropradsky.

Ampère's fame grew rapidly; Scientists spoke especially flatteringly about his experimental work on electromagnetism. Famous physicists visited him, and he received a number of invitations from other countries to give presentations on his work. But his health was undermined, and his financial situation was unstable. He was burdened by work at the Polytechnic School and inspector duties. He still dreamed of teaching a course in physics, not mathematics, and reading it in a non-radical manner, including a new section in the course - electrodynamics, of which he himself was the creator. The most suitable place for this was one of the oldest educational institutions in France - the Collège de France. Founded back in 1530, this institution seemed to combine the functions of an educational and research institute. There were all conditions for fruitful creative activity. After many inconsistencies and intuitions, in 1824 Ampère was elected to the position of professor at the Collège de France. He was given the chair of general and experimental physics.

As can be seen from the letters that Ampere wrote during this period, he was extremely happy with his chosen one. True, this joy was tempered by the need for time to oppose the order that reigned at the institute. Ampere was especially outraged by the intuitions that arose when choosing important candidates for release. He was always intolerant of faili and injustice. Moreover, he could not remain equal when personal interests, the pursuit of high titles and ranks overshadowed the true scientific value of the candidates.

The ends are the most fruitful period of Ampere's activity. In 1831, Faraday discovered the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction. Many outstanding physicists of the mid-19th century - Weber, F. Neumann and K. Neumann, Wiedeman, Lenz and others - were involved in the development of electrodynamics. It was fully completed in Maxwell’s classic work, “A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism,” which was published in 1869.
