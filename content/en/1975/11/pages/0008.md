⟦folio 6⟧

By the beginning of the 19th century, electrical and magnetic phenomena were still largely mysterious; their nature was not clear. Scientists were only familiar with the properties of static charges and permanent magnets. All electrical and magnetic phenomena discovered in the experiment were explained as a result of the action of special electrical and magnetic liquids (“fluids”). So, for example, the St. Petersburg academician Epinus in his work “Olt of the Theory of Electricity and Magnetism,” published in 1759, wrote:

“There is a liquid that produces all magnetic phenomena, which can be called magnetic. This liquid is extremely thin and can pass through any feathers in the body; its particles, like the particles of the electrical fluid, mutually repel each other. This liquid, in most other bodies found in the world, does not cause any reaction; she is neither attracted nor repelled by him. However, there is a certain number of bodies, parts of which accept magnetic/material and are accepted; the body endowed with this property is the first iron, and then all the bodies called glands...”

The only quantitative characteristic of electrical and magnetic phenomena was Coulomb's law, discovered at the end of the 18th century.

Progress began only after electric current was first produced. In 1800, Volta invented the first current source, which was called the Volta table. Everything about this invention has generated enormous interest in the scientific world. Napoleon invited the Ilyan physicist to Paris to repeat his experiments in front of a select audience. There, Volta quickly delivered a report in which he expressed the idea that static electricity and electric current are identical in nature—the “motivating electric fluid,” in the terminology of that time.

Numerous experiments have shown that electric current can cause various effects - thermal, chemical, light. It was also natural to assume that there must be a connection between electricity and magnetism, which has been explored by scientists for several centuries. At the beginning of the 19th century, many scientists attempted to make this connection clear. Danish physics professor Hans-Christan Ørsted also looked for it. Back in 1807, he announced his intention to study the effect of electric current on a magnetic needle. However, for many years, experiments confirming the existence of such an action failed. Only in 1820, having placed a magnetic needle parallel to the wire connecting the two ends of a voltaic column, he saw with his own eyes the phenomenon that he had been waiting for for so many years: under the influence of the current in the conductor, the needle deviated from its usual direction. Thus, a fundamental phenomenon was discovered, which laid the foundation for electrodynamics.

In July 1820, Ørsted published his discovery in a short article entitled "Olt's Theory of Electrical Conflict on the Magnetic Arrow." He called electric current “electrical confection.”

A month later, Oersted's experiments were built in Geneva by the Swiss physicist Auguste de la Rive. Many scientists and intermediate scientists were present at the demonstration - the famous French physicist Arago. Upon returning to Paris, Arago made a report on this at two meetings of the Academy of Sciences and reproduced Oersted's experiments. He began his report with the following words: “Gentlemen, Professor Oersted in Copenhagen managed to make a wonderful discovery... which has consequences that the inquisitive, but limited, are not yet able to predict.
