◆ We will call tetrahedra whose faces are all congruent $K$-tetrahedra. Let us establish several properties of such tetrahedra.

$1^\circ$. *The sum of three plane angles at each vertex of a $K$-tetrahedron is $180^\circ$.*

Indeed, these three angles are congruent to the angles of the same triangle $ABC$, opposite its three different sides $AB$, $BC$, $CA$ (this reasoning is also valid for triangles $ABC$, in which some two sides are equal in length), and the sum of the angles of a triangle is $180^\circ$ (Fig. 3).

$2^\circ$. *The faces of the $K$-tetrahedron are acute triangles.*

In fact, in any trihedral angle, each plane angle is less than the sum of the other two. For the angles of a triangle, this condition is satisfied if and only if it is acute.

$3^\circ$. *The development of the $K$-tetrahedron has the form of an acute triangle, in which the middle lines are drawn.*

This follows immediately from $1^\circ$ and $2^\circ$ (Fig. 4). Of course, the converse is also true: *any acute-angled triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ with midlines $AB$, $BC$, $CA$ drawn in it can be considered as a development of a $K$-tetrahedron.* (Think why it is impossible to form a tetrahedron from an obtuse $\triangle A_1B_1C_1$.)

Note that when we add a tetrahedron from the development, fixing the face $ABC$ and rotating the other three faces relative to the edges $AB$, $BC$, $CA$, then the points $A_1$, $B_1$, $C_1$ will move along circles (lying in planes perpendicular to $ABC$), and their projections onto the plane $ABC$ will move along the altitudes of the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ up to the point of their intersection $H$. So, we also got the following property:

$4^\circ$. *The projection $H$ of the vertex $D$ of the $K$-tetrahedron $ABCD$ onto the plane $ABC$ lies at the point of intersection of the altitudes of the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$.*

Note that in any tetrahedron its “medians” - the segments connecting the vertices with the centers of gravity (the intersection points of the medians) of opposite faces - intersect at one point M - the “center of gravity” of the tetrahedron - and are divided by this point in the ratio $3:1$, counting from the vertex (Fig. 5; the segments connecting the midpoints of opposite edges also pass through the same point M).

$5^\circ$. *In a $K$-tetrahedron, the center of gravity coincides with the center of the circumscribed sphere.*

There is a displacement of space in which the tetrahedron $ABCD$ is juxtaposed with itself such that the face $DCB$ becomes $ABC$ (i.e. $A—D$, $B—C$, $C—B$, $D—A$). During this movement, only two points of the tetrahedron surface remain motionless: these are the midpoints of the edges $AD$ and $BC$. It is clear that with such a movement, the center of the circumscribed ball $O$ and the intersection point of the medians $M$ are motionless (since the tetrahedron itself is combined with itself). If points $M$ and $O$ are different, then all points of the line $MO$ must also be fixed. This means that this line passes through the midpoints of the edges $AD$ and $BC$. In exactly the same way (considering other self-alignments of the tetrahedron) we will prove that the line $MO$ also passes through the midpoints of other edges. But this is impossible. This means that points $M$ and $O$ coincide.

Let us denote the center of gravity of the triangle $ABC$ by $P$, the center of its circumcircle (projection of the point $O=M$ onto the plane $\triangle ABC$) by $Q$, and the projection of the vertex $D$ onto the plane $\triangle ABC$ by $H$. figure 6 shows all these points lying in a plane perpendicular to the face $ABC$ and passing through the center of the ball $O$ and the vertex $D$; $E$ and $F$ are the points of intersection of this plane with the circumscribed circle of the triangle $ABC$, the radius of which will be denoted by $\rho$. Let us prove that for $n > 3$ the n-gon cannot be divided into triangles in the required way.

Let's assume the opposite - that we still split the n-gon ($n > 3$) into triangles.

Let us map the set of sides of the partition triangles into the set of their vertices. Let us distinguish between two cases.

1. The side of the tiling triangle does not lie on the side of the n-gon.

Then:
a) if the side inside this side of the triangle contains vertices of other triangles of the partition, then map it to one of such vertices;
b) if there are no other vertices inside this side, then, by the definition of a partition, one of the ends of this side must lie sideways inside some other side (another triangle of the partition), then we’ll map our side to exactly that end (if the ends of the side lie sideways inside some other side, then we’ll map it to any of its ends).
2. The side of the tiling triangle lies on the side of the n-gon.

Let us reflect such a side to the end that meets first when going around the side of the n-gon clockwise.

Let us now consider the set of all vertices of the partition triangles. All vertices are divided into three types.

Type I The vertices of an n-gon and those vertices of the partition triangles that lie on the sides of the n-gon.

Let there be vertices of type I t. Then the sides that are mapped to these vertices are also t, since it is very indo that only sides lying on the sides of the n-gon are mapped to such vertices, and this mapping is one-to-one.

II type. Vertices that do not lie on the sides of the n-gon and do not lie sideways inside the sides of any triangles of the tiling (for example, as on figure 11).

Let there be m vertices of type II. According to the construction, no side will be displayed at such vertices.

III type. Vertices that do not lie on the sides of the n-gon, but lie sideways inside some sides of the partition triangles (for example, as on figure 12).

Let vertices of type III be $k$. No more than three sides can be reflected into a type III vertex (the side within which this vertex lies, and two sides for which this vertex serves as a cone - for one left, for the other - right). Therefore, no more than $3k$ sides of triangles can be reflected in $k$ vertices of type III.

Let there be $x$ triangles in the partition. Let us express the number $3x$ of sides of triangles in terms of $t$, $k$ and $m$. To do this, write down the sum of the angles of these triangles.

On the one hand, the sum of angles $x$ of triangles is equal to $π(t-2)+2πm+πk$, where $π(t-2) = π(t-n)+π(n-2)$ is the sum of angles at vertices of type I, $2πm$ is the sum of angles at vertices of type II, $πk$ is the sum of angles at vertices of type III.

Hence,

$$πx = π(t-2) + 2πm + πk,$$

and the total side number is

$$3x = 6m + 3t + 3k - 6.$$

Since each of the sides of the triangles is divided into some vertex of type I or III, it follows from the above that the total number of sides can be no more than $t+3k$. Means,

$$6m+3t+3k-6 \leq t+3k,$$

whence $t+3m \leq 3$.

But $t>0$ and $m>0$; in addition, $t \geq n>3$, and we get a contradiction.

In other words, we got that there can be no more than three vertices on the sides of a $n$-gon, and therefore the sides are divided; This means that the $n$-gon has at most three sides (since on each side of the $n$-gon there must be at least one side that is broken).

In addition, from the last inequality it follows that when dividing a triangle, type II vertices cannot be obtained ($t=3$, therefore, $m=0$).

F328. Rigid rods of lengths $l_1$ and $l_2$ are connected by a hinge at point $A$. Their free ends move away from each other evenly at the speed of the corresponding $v_1$ and $v_2$, directed along one straight line (Fig. 13). The most important point is $A$ at the moment when the rods connect to each other at $90^\circ$. The movement of the rods will occur in one plane.

Let us imagine the vector $a$ of the total acceleration of point $A$ at the moment when the rods make an angle $90^\circ$ with each other, as the sum of two accelerations $a_1$ and $a_2$, directed along the corresponding rods (see Fig. 13). To calculate $a_1$ and $a_2$ we take advantage of the fact that the acceleration vector remains constant when moving from one inertial reporting frame to another.

Let us move to the reporting system moving at speed $v_2$ to the left. In this reporting system, the end of the left rod (point $B$) ends; the end of the right rod (point $C$) moves to the right with speed $v_1+v_2$, and point $A$ describes a circle of radius $l_2$ (Fig. 14). Therefore, the acceleration $a$ can be decomposed into centripetal (normal) acceleration and tangential (tangential) acceleration. These are the accelerations $a_2$ and $a_1$ of the corresponding one.

Modulus of centripetal acceleration $a_2 = v^2/l_2$, where $v$ is the linear speed of motion of point $A$ around the circle. At the moment when the rods make an angle $90^\circ$ with each other, the velocity vector $v$ is directed along the right rod. The other rod $AC$ has at this moment a speed $v_1+v_2$ directed to the right. Since the rod is rigid, the projections of the velocities of its ends onto the straight line along which it is located must be the same (otherwise the length of the rod would change). Therefore (see Fig. 14),

F330. In the cylinder under the weightless piston planes $S$ there is air at atmospheric pressure $p_0$ and temperature $T_0$. The internal volume of the cylinder is divided into two equal parts by a fixed horizontal partition with a small deviation. We put a mass load $M$ on the piston, under the action of which the piston reaches the partition. The least $T_1$ air in the cylinder, if the cylinder walls and the piston do not conduct menlo.

◆

Since the vessel is heat-plated, the change in the internal energy of the gas is equal to the work of external forces acting on the piston—gravity force $Mg$ and atmospheric pressure force $p_0S$:

$$\Delta U = (Mg + p_0S) h$$

(h is the piston stroke).

Since the internal energy of a gas depends only on temperature, then in any process the change in internal energy is the same as in a process occurring at constant volume, that is

$$\Delta U = C_V z \left( T_1 - T_0 \right).$$

Here $C_V$ is the heat content of one mole of gas at constant volume, $z$ is the number of moles of gas. Therefore in our case

$$\left( Mg + p_0S \right) h = C_V z \left( T_1 - T_0 \right).$$

From here

$$T_1 = T_0 + \frac{\left( Mg + p_0S \right) h}{C_V z}.$$

The number of moles of air in the cylinder can be found from the equation of state of the gas before the stage:

$$p_0V = zRT_0,$$

where

$$z = \frac{p_0V}{RT_0}.$$

Air consists mainly of diatomic gases - nitrogen and oxygen. Therefore we can count $C_V = \frac{5}{2} R$. Substituting the values ​​$z$, $C_V$ and $h = V/2S$ into (*), we finally find:

$$T_1 = T_0 \left( 1 + \frac{Mg + p_0S}{5p_0S} \right).$$

Величина $e. d. c. induction excited in a coil of radius $r_i$ is equal to

$$\mathcal{E}_i = -\frac{\Delta \Phi_i}{\Delta t},$$

where $\Delta \Phi_i$ is the change in magnetic flux through the plane limited by the coil for a short period of time $\Delta t$:

$$\Delta \Phi_i = S \Delta B = \pi r_i^2 B_0 \left\{ \cos \left[ \omega (t + \Delta t) \right] - \cos \omega t \right\} = -2 \pi r_i^2 B_0 \left[ \sin \omega \left( t + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \right) \sin \omega \frac{\Delta t}{2} \right] \approx -\pi r_i^2 B_0 \omega \Delta t \sin \omega t$$

since $\Delta t$ is small, then $\sin \omega \left( t + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \right) \approx \sin \omega t$, $\sin \omega \frac{\Delta t}{2} \approx \omega \frac{\Delta t}{2}$. The e.m.f. induced throughout the entire spiral differs in the amount of e.m.f. d.s. induced in turns, that is

$$\mathcal{E} = \sum \mathcal{E}_i = \sum -\frac{\Delta \Phi_i}{\Delta t} = B_0 \omega \sin \omega t \sum \pi r_i^2.$$

The quantity $\pi r_i^2$ can be considered the volume of a cylinder, the height of which is equal to one, and the area of ​​the base is $\pi r_i^2$. Then $\sum \pi r_i^2$ is the sum of the volumes of such cylinders. Since the difference between the radii of the bases of “neighboring” cylinders is small (the number of turns $n$ is large), the volume of all such cylinders is close to the volume of a cone with a height $n$ and a base area $\pi R^2$ (Fig. 16):

$$\sum \pi r_i^2 = V = \frac{1}{3} \pi R^2 n.$$

Thus, e. d.s. induced in the spiral is equal to

$$\mathcal{E} = \frac{1}{3} \pi R^2 n B_0 \omega \sin \omega t.$$

F334.*) The bead slides without friction along a vertical wire of length $l$, which is designated as a harmonic of length $l/2$. How many times is the time the bead slides longer than the time it takes to fall from a height of $l$? The amplitude of the wiring changes is much less than its length. The dimensions of the bead are negligible compared to the length of the harmonic “elbow”.

When a bead moves from one straight section of wiring to another, the absolute peak of its speed does not change. Only the direction of the bead's speed changes: it becomes parallel to the new section of the wiring. This means that the time it takes for the bead to move along the wire is the same as when the bead moves along an inclined plane—the wire unfolds. This time is equal

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{g \sin \alpha}} = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{gh/l}} = 2 \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$$

($l$ is the length of the inclined plane, $\alpha$ is the angle of inclination of the plane to the horizon), i.e. it is $\sqrt{2}$ times longer than the time

$$t' = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{g}}$$The bead falls from the height $l$.
