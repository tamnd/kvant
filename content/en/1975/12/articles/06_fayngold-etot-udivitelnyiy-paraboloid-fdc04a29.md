The article by V. G. Boltyansky (see p. 19) talks about the optical properties of conic sections, in particular parabolas. The “physical” definition of the optical property of a parabola can be formulated as follows: a ray of light incident on a parabola parallel to its symmetry axis, after reflection, passes through the focus of the parabola. From the property of reciprocity of light rays it follows that a ray coming from the focus of a parabola, after reflection from it, becomes parapel to its symmetry axis.

In this article we will talk about some of the “physical” features of the reflection of light from a parabolide - a surface formed by the rotation of a parabola around the axis of symmetry.

A parabolic mirror is a parabolide whose inner surface specularly reflects the light falling on it. If light falls on such a mirror parallel to the symmetry axis of the parabolide, then after reflection all the rays pass through its focus, as if collected in it. And vice versa, rays from an exact source located at the focus, reflected from the surface of the parabolide, emerge as a parapel ray.

It is important to note that each of the effects mentioned is the result of only one reflection of rays from the surface of the parabolide. But if you make a parabolic mirror deep enough, then most of the rays into it will experience two reflections (Fig. 1). After the first reflection, each ray, passing through the focus, is again reflected from the “opposite” side of the mirror. Let us first explain this fact using visual considerations. Let $OZ$ be the axis of symmetry, $r'$, $r''$ the distances from the axis to the incident and corresponding outgoing ray (Fig. 3). The farther from the axis $OZ$ the beam falls, the closer to it it comes out, that is, the more $r'$, the less $r''$. Therefore, the energy of the “peripheral” regions of the incoming beam is “transferred” to the axis of the outgoing one. If the incident beam is wide enough (large radius of the mirror), then this will lead to a significant concentration of energy along the axis. As a result, the energy comes out practically in the form of a narrow “light cord”, and the wider the incoming beam, the narrower the outgoing cord.

So, we come to the conclusion: a paraboloid can concentrate energy not only at the focus, but also along the axis of symmetry.

It is these rays, converging at the focus and diverging from it, that seem to form a fictitious point source of light here. However, this source differs from the actual accurate one. There is something mystical red about all these parabolon properties!

However, the conclusions we have made are valid only within the framework of idealized ideas that do not exactly correspond to reality. We talked about the parabolon as a mathematically ideal surface. A real mirror only approximately corresponds to this “ideal”. Its surface is not strictly symmetrical, has roughness, etc.

Next, we operated with the concepts of a ray and a parabolic beam of rays. In fact, every beam of rays propagates within a certain telescope angle that is not equal to zero. We can only talk about a practically parabolic beam, that is, a beam that, although it diverges, is so insignificant that this divergence within a defined region of space can be avoided. For example, a beam of rays falling on a parabolide from a star can be considered practically parabolic. But even in this case, the emerging beam will undergo the so-called diffraction divergence, due to the wave nature of light *). Moreover, the narrower this beam (that is, the more

$$\alpha \approx \frac{\lambda}{r_{\min}^2},$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of parabolic light. Thus, the thinner and more concentrated the “cord of light”, the more it diverges. Let us estimate the distance $z$ at which the increase in the radius of the cord due to divergence is equal to the radius $r''_{\min}$ “at the beginning of the bundle” (up to this distance the emerging cord can be considered practically parabolic). Obviously,

$$z \approx \frac{r''_{\min}}{tg\alpha} \approx \frac{r''_{\min}}{\alpha} \approx \frac{(r''_{\min})^2}{\lambda}.$$

According to this formula, the thinner and more intense the beam, the smaller, other things being equal, its “parabolic length”. Consequently, the creation of a very deep parabolon is not only practically impossible, but also meaningless from a theoretical point of view. The wave nature of light does not allow us to obtain a consistently strong and parabolic beam of rays, similar to that described in A. Tolstoy’s novel “Engineer Garin’s Hyperbolon”.

Thus, the red and unexpected effects associated with the twofold reflection of light from the parabolon of rotation arose within the limits within which it is permissible to ignore the wave nature of light, namely, when even the smallest of all dimensions of the system - the radius $r''_{\min}$ - is many times larger than the wavelength. This condition imposes a limitation on the degree of “amplification” of light.
