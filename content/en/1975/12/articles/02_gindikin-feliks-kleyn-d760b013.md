## Felix Klein

Felix Klein1849—1925

Felix Klein died on June 22, 1925. The fame of a great mathematician was brought to him by the work he completed over the course of one decade. Klein stopped active studies in mathematics at the age of 33, but until the end of his days he remained at the center of scientific and organizational life, completely devoting himself to teaching and literary activities.

### 1. "Knight's Spurs"

F. Klein was born in 1849 in Düsseldorf. Here he graduated from high school; in 1865 he entered the University of Bonn. The very next year, Professor Julius Plücker (1801–1868) hired a seventeen-year-old student as a physics assistant. Plücker began his scientific career as a geometer, but gradually switched to experimental physics. However, in the last years of his life, after a twenty-year break, Plücker returned to geometry. “This turn played a decisive role in my own development,” Klein wrote. A posthumous edition of Plücker's last memoir (1869) was prepared by Klein. This may have been the reason why his dissertation (1868), with which, according to Klein himself, he “earned knightly spurs,” and his first publication (1869) were geometric.

Having lost his teacher, Klein becomes a “knight errant”. He visits the main mathematical centers of Germany (Göttingen, Berlin), establishes personal contacts with Clebsch, Weber, Weierstrass. A promising young scientist who wants and knows how to learn is immediately noticed. Klein's contacts with his peers are no less important. Especially happy was Klein's friendship with the great Norwegian Sophus Lie (1842-1899); they met in 1870 in Berlin. S. Lee was seven years older than Klein, but in 1870 he took only his first steps in geometry. |

Soon Klein and Lee leave for Paris. Here they become acquainted with the techniques of French geometers, who were able to obtain important geometric results with amazing ease, “through the air” (S. Lee). Of particular importance for the further scientific fate of Klein and Lee were their meetings with Camille Jordan (1838-1922). Just in 1870, Jordan published an extensive work on the theory of *finite groups*, which attracted widespread attention to the work of Galois (1811-1832). Perhaps Klein’s first work, devoted to the geometric study of the so-called *Kummer surface*, the algebraic study of which Jordan had previously undertaken, served as a “pass” to Jordan for his friends.

The Franco-Prussian War forced Klein to leave France. At the very beginning of the war, Klein fell ill with typhus; Having recovered from his illness, he settled in Göttingen. For Klein, the time of great achievements is coming. N. Bourbaki writes that Klein completed the “golden age” of geometry. But before we talk about the brilliant end of this century, let us remember its beginning.

### 2. "Golden Age" of Geometry

Back in the 17th century, Desargues (1593-1662) and Pascal (1623-1662) managed to obtain remarkable geometric results using central design. These results were forgotten for almost a century and a half. Gaspard Monge (1746-1818) again drew attention to the great possibilities of the design method; he talked about this in a course on descriptive geometry, which he taught at the Polytechnic School. N. Bourbaki dates the “golden age” of geometry from Monge’s “Descriptive Geometry” (1795).

Among Monge's listeners was Victor Poncelet (1788-1867). “The trait that elevates him above all his predecessors is a new type of geometric intuition - “projective thinking” (Klein). Poncelet created projective geometry during the two years he spent in captivity in Saratov after the War of 1812. Poncelet shared his results with his fellow prisoners, who also listened to Monge at the Ecole Polytechnique. These results were published in 1822 in the “Treatise on the Projective Properties of Figures.”

Like his predecessors, Poncelet supplements each line with a point *at infinity, considering that all lines parallel to each other have a common point at infinity (“intersect” at it). All points at infinity form a straight line at infinity. On a completed plane, parallelism becomes a special case of intersection and does not require special consideration (for example, the statement that a single straight line parallel to it passes through a point outside a line turns into a statement that a single straight line passes through two different points, one of which is ordinary and the other at infinity). With central projection, the end point may not have an image (“go to infinity”), but on a plane filled with infinitely distant points this mapping is already one-to-one.

Central design translates one plane into another; having performed several projections in a row, we can return to the original plane, obtaining a transformation of this plane. Such transformations (they began to be called *projective*) include displacements, homotheties, and dilations. *Projective transformations are one-to-one (on the completed plane) and transform lines into straight lines* (later it turned out that any transformation with these properties is projective). Projective transformations that take a straight line at infinity into itself are called *affine*; affine transformations are one-to-one on the ordinary plane. Poncelet studied geometric objects that are preserved under projective transformations. It turns out that under projective transformations a conic section also transforms into a conic section (but, for example, a hyperbola can transform into a parabola, and any conic section can be transformed into a circle by a projective transformation). The following observation turned out to be extremely fruitful. Let $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ be points lying on the same line, $\{A,B,C,D\}=\dfrac{|AC|\cdot|BD|}{|AD|\cdot|BC|}$ be a *double* or *anharmonic ratio of four points*. Let, under some projective transformation, the points $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ go to the points $A'$, $B'$, $C'$, $D'$ (they will necessarily lie on the same line). Then $\{A,B,C,D\}=\{A',B',C',D'\}$, i.e., under projective transformations, the double relation of the four points is preserved. If one of the points, for example, $D$ is an infinitely distant point, then $\{A,B,C,D\}$ is set equal to $\dfrac{|AC|}{|BC|}$‚ and we get that under affine transformations the ratios of the lengths of segments lying on the same line are preserved (why?).

Next, Poncelet tries to eliminate exceptional cases of mutual arrangement of conic sections. Why, for example, can two ellipses intersect at four points, but a pair of circles at most two? This question has a surprising answer. In addition to a pair of real points of intersection, the circles have a universal (the same for all circles on the plane!) pair of common points, not noticed due to the fact that they are... imaginary and infinitely distant at the same time. These points are called *cyclic*.

Now - a few words about four German mathematicians: Ferdinand Möbius (1790-1868), Jacob Steiner (1796-1863), Christian von Staudt (1798-1867) and the already mentioned Plücker. Their names are associated with a fierce struggle between the analytical and synthetic directions in geometry.

Here the words “analysis” and “synthesis” are used in a non-standard sense: analytical geometry uses the coordinate method, as a result of which the use of algebra and analysis in geometry is possible; synthetic geometry operates with direct spatial structures.

The most fierce duel was between the analyst Plücker and the synthetic Steiner; Möbius (analyst) and Staudt (syntheticist) stayed away from the struggle. It was very easy for Klein to become involved in the struggle on the side of the analysts, but he managed to stay above the fray, perhaps guided by the rule of his friend the physiologist Ludwig: “You need to move 600 kilometers from the place of dispute and from there reconsider the relationship.”

The activities of analysts first of all required improving the coordinate method. In synthetic terms, it was important to give coordinate-free definitions of objects of projective geometry, for example, second-order curves. This was done by Steiner, a very colorful figure in the history of mathematics. A Swiss peasant who “walked behind the plow until he was 19 years old,” he began studying mathematics in adulthood. Steiner was strongly opposed to imaginary quantities in geometry, calling them “ghosts” or “the kingdom of shadows.” However, von Staudt showed that with imaginary objects arising in projective geometry, it is possible to associate purely material constructions equivalent to them. Another important achievement of Staudt was that he was able to determine the double ratio of four points directly, without using distances (which are not preserved under projective transformations).

And finally, another name is the English mathematician Arthur Keley (1821-1895), who worked in mathematics for a long time without interrupting his legal practice. We will focus on one of Keli’s works - the famous “Sixth Memoir of Forms” (1859). Keli noticed that Euclidean displacements stand out from all projective transformations in that they *preserve cyclic points*. As a result, using cyclic points, all objects of Euclidean geometry (distances, angles, etc.) can be defined through projective concepts (preserved under projective transformations). Keley calls projective geometry *descriptive*, and Euclidean geometry *metric* and writes: “Metrical geometry is thus a part of descriptive geometry, and descriptive geometry is all geometry.” It should be borne in mind that previously the situation seemed exactly the opposite, namely, that projective geometry is a comparatively poor part of Euclidean geometry. Kely further notes that, based on projective geometry, it is possible to introduce distances other than Euclidean (*metrics* or *Keley's measure*): each such distance on the plane is associated with some second-order curve (real or imaginary), so that this distance does not change under all projective transformations that preserve the curve in question.

### 3. Keley-Klein model

In 1869, Klein became acquainted with Keley's theory, and at the end of the same year, rather superficially, with Lobachevsky's geometry. He immediately had the idea that one of Keley’s metrics led to Lobachevsky’s geometry. It was a guess, almost devoid of argumentation. Kely's theory and Lobachevsky's theory were radically different in appearance (Kely's calculations with a double ratio and Lobachevsky's axiomatic presentation), and Kely's geometries were not yet sufficiently developed to be able to test the axioms of Lobachevsky's geometry. In February 1870, Klein, while giving a report on Kehley's theory at Weierstrass's seminar, decided to make his hypothesis public. At this seminar, it was not customary to discuss science fiction projects: the “presumptuous” young man was explained that “these are two systems far removed from each other”; Klein was so little prepared to defend his hypothesis that he “allowed himself to be convinced.” He later complained about Weierstrass that he “had no inclination to recognize from a distance the outlines of heights not yet reached.” But Klein did not stop believing in his hypothesis. In the summer of 1871, with the help of his friend Stolz, he had already thoroughly studied non-Euclidean geometry and was convinced of the validity of his guess. Even with proof, Klein had a hard time convincing others that he was right. Perhaps the most annoying thing for Klein was that Keli remained among those who disagreed with his statement until the end of his life. “An aged spirit is not able to draw conclusions from the positions it has created,” wrote Klein.

A few words about the Kehli-Klein model itself. “Points” in this model are the internal points of a circle (the circle can be replaced by an area bounded by any second-order curve), and “straight lines” are the chords of this circle (without ends). The intersection points of the “straight lines” are determined naturally; it is clear that an infinite number of “straight lines” pass through a “point” outside the “line” without intersecting the original one, that is, there is a negation of the axiom of parallels from Euclidean geometry. We must also make sure that all other Euclidean axioms for the described model are satisfied: this will mean that the Klein model is a model of Lobachevsky geometry. The axioms concerning the relative positions of points and lines are relatively easily verified. But when it comes to testing the axioms of equality, then first of all we need to agree on which segments are considered congruent; it is impossible to inherit the corresponding concepts of Euclidean geometry. Klein, following Kely, assumes the length of the segment $AB$ equal to: $|AB|=\ln\{A,B,\alpha,\beta\}$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the points of intersection of the “straight line” $AB$ with the boundary of the circle under consideration (this circle is called *absolute*). Projective transformations that preserve the absolute preserve the so-defined “distance,” i.e., they are displacements in the Keley-Klein model of Lobachevsky geometry.

The Italian mathematician Eugenio Beltrami (1835-1900) outlined a different path to substantiating Lobachevsky's geometry back in 1868. He discovered a surface - *pseudosphere* - the shortest lines on which (*geodesics*) behave like straight lines in Lobachevsky geometry. Then Beltrami somehow mapped the pseudosphere into a circle and obtained the same formulas that Klein later used in his theory.

Klein investigated other non-Euclidean geometries to which Keley metrics lead, discovering, in particular, the model of Riemann geometry (in Riemann geometry the sum of the angles of a triangle is greater than $\pi$, in Lobachevsky geometry it is always less than $\pi$).

Let us now discuss what the Keley-Klein model provides for Lobachevsky geometry. First of all, this is a different way of presentation from the axiomatic one, more visual. Klein prefaces his publication (1871) with the words that his goal is “to give a new visual presentation of the mathematical results of works related to the theory of parallels, and to make them accessible to clear understanding” (Beltrami formulates his goal in approximately the same way). However, building a model solves far more than just a methodological problem. Nowadays, the Keley-Klein model is considered primarily as a means of proving the consistency of Lobachevsky geometry. In the Keley-Klein model, objects of Lobachevsky geometry are formed in the language of Euclidean geometry, so that after translation into this language, theorems of Lobachevsky geometry turn into theorems of Euclidean geometry, and thus Lobachevsky geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is consistent.

Klein saw the main significance of the model he built in something else. He puts projective geometry at the forefront, of which the geometries of Euclid and Lobachevsky are equal and independent parts. In this regard, the independence of the constructed model of Lobachevsky’s geometry from Euclidean geometry was emphasized, for which, in turn, the ability to build projective geometry without using Euclidean geometry was important (according to Staudt, see paragraph 2). It was this moment that made Keli suspicious of the existence of a vicious circle. Klein wrote: “Instead of constructing images of non-Euclidean geometry within our metric geometry, we substantiate a projective geometry free from any metric representations, which contains, as special cases that can be clearly classified, all known geometric systems.”

### 4. Erlangen program

For centuries, the word "geometry" was used only in the singular. But then Lobachevsky’s geometry appeared, then Riemann’s geometry, and finally, mathematicians realized that there are many different geometries. A natural question arose: what is geometry? In 1872, Klein expressed his point of view in a lecture he gave in connection with his assumption of a professorship in Erlangen. This is how the Erlangen Program appeared, apparently Klein's most famous work. Essentially, there are no new results in it; all attention is concentrated on the search for a principle that allows us to systematize the very amorphous formation into which geometry had turned by that time.

According to Klein, *the main attribute of any geometry is a certain set $G$ of one-to-one transformations of some set* $M$. There must be enough transformations so that each point of the set $M$ can be transferred to another by some transformation from $G$ (in this case they say that $G$ acts on $M$ *transitively*). This point of view was inspired, of course, by projective geometry, in which from the very beginning certain transformations (central projections) were primary, while in Euclidean geometry (in the traditional presentation) other objects are primary: straight lines, segments, congruent figures, etc.

The next point is that the transformation set $G$ must be a *group*. This means that any two transformations from $G$ performed in a row can be replaced by one transformation, also from $G$; in addition, along with each transformation $g\in G$ to $G$ the *inverse to it* is included: $g^{-1}$ (if $g$ translates $x$ into $y$, then $g^{-1}$ translates $y$ into $x$).

So, each group of transformations $G$ is associated with some geometry. What is the content of such geometry? First of all, finding *invariants of the group* $G$ - properties that are preserved under the action of transformations from $G$ (more precisely, if some object of our geometry has an invariant property, then no matter what transformation from $G$ we act on it, we will get an object that also has this property). For the group of displacements of Euclidean geometry, all known geometric properties are invariants, since we do not distinguish the positions of figures on the plane. However, even in a traditional geometry course there are non-trivial statements about invariants of transformations that are not displacements. With homotheties, the congruence of angles, the property of a curve to be a circle, the ratio of the lengths of segments, and the ratio of areas are preserved. Having a certain supply of invariant properties, you can construct new ones. With respect to homotheties, the property of a straight line being the bisector of an angle and the property of a curve being a semicircle will be invariant. With respect to axial extensions, the property of a curve being a circle will no longer be an invariant, but the property of a curve being an ellipse (as well as a hyperbola or parabola) will be an invariant; the ratio of the lengths of segments lying on the same straight line (but not on different ones), and the ratio of areas are preserved. The consequence is the invariance of the property of a point to divide a segment in a given relation, the property of a line to be the median of a triangle. It can be shown that any affine transformation can be represented as a composition of displacements and axial stretches, and therefore all of the indicated properties are invariant under affine transformations (examples of projective invariants are given on p. 3).

The selection of invariants is only the first layer of geometry. Its main content consists of *theorems on relationships between invariant properties* (they are called *syzygies*). For example, the medians of a triangle are its affine invariants; the existence of a common point dividing the medians in the ratio $2:1$ is a non-trivial relationship between these three lines. In the theorems on the intersection of bisectors and heights, the dependence between homothety invariants is derived.

Chasles drew attention to the possibility of using geometric transformations to obtain new theorems in 1837: “Now everyone is able to take some known truth and apply to it various general principles of transformations; thus he will receive other truths... Genius is no longer necessary in order to contribute to the construction of the majestic temple of science.” However, if we take Schall’s recipe literally: take any theorem and apply an arbitrary transformation to it, then you will get a true statement, but with such a clumsy formulation that it will have little chance of remaining in the “temple of science.” Think, for example, what will the theorem about the intersection of bisectors turn into if we do an axial stretch? How to explain which line the bisector will go to? Klein explains that it is important, on the contrary, to understand which of the transformations do not change the statement, select transformations that simplify the picture as much as possible, and prove the statement in the resulting (simpler) form. Here's a traditional example. By an affine transformation, any triangle can be turned into an equilateral triangle, and since the theorem on the point of intersection of medians deals with the relationship between affine invariants, it is enough to check this theorem for an equilateral triangle (which is already very simple).

These considerations allow us to refine the Shalya recipe. Let some relationship be noted between affine invariants in an equilateral triangle of unit area: for example, let $\lambda$ be the area of ​​a hexagon formed by “*tridians*” - straight lines connecting the vertices of the triangle with points dividing the opposite side into three equal parts (the number $\lambda$ is uniquely defined). Then in any triangle the ratio of the area of ​​the hexagon formed by the tridians to the area of ​​the entire triangle is $\lambda$. Now you can easily come up with other theorems of this kind.

One of the most important points in Klein's reasoning is the clarification of the relationship between the geometries associated with the groups $G_1$ and $G_2$, if $G_1\subset G_2$. ($G_1$ is said to be a *subgroup* of $G_2$.) The larger group $G_2$ has fewer invariants than $G_1$, and all theorems associated with the group $G_2$ also hold for the geometry associated with the smaller group $G_1$. Therefore, in each specific geometry it is important to find statements that remain valid for geometries with wider groups of transformations. Sometimes the possibility of “transferring” a statement to geometry with a wider group of transformations becomes clear only after reworking the formulation of the statement.

Keley's ideology in the language of the Erlangen program is that one can move in the opposite direction, considering a group of transformations that preserve some fixed object. In this case, often invariants for a subgroup can be constructed using invariants for a group (distances in Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries - using a double relation).

Invariants for a larger group and the relationships between them are usually easier to describe. In particular, for the projective group the problem of finding invariants can be made completely algebraic and solved.

The Erlangen Program ended the “golden age” of classical geometry. The number of new geometries is increasing; Gradually, geometric language permeates a significant part of mathematics. “Classical geometry has outgrown itself, and from a living independent science has turned into a universal language of modern mathematics, possessing exceptional flexibility and convenience” (N. Bourbaki).

### 5. Extern at the Riemann School

After the Erlangen Program, Klein turns to the theory of algebraic functions - an area in which Gauss, Legendre, Abel, Jacobi, Weierstrass, and Riemann worked. The ideas closest to Klein turned out to be Riemann (1826-1866), with whom he was not personally acquainted. According to Klein, he was “an external student at the Riemann school, ... and external students, as you know, if they take on any business, they work with special zeal, because only deep interest motivates them to work.” Klein later wrote that he saw his task in the combination of Riemann and Galois, that is, in the penetration of group theory into the geometric theory of functions of a complex variable. In Klein's own opinion, this was the main area of ​​his scientific activity.

Unfortunately, we will not be able to talk about this activity of Klein, since here it is already necessary to require from the reader special knowledge that goes far beyond the scope of the school curriculum. But we will still mention one circumstance.

Klein dealt with the so-called *uniformization problem*. By considering important cases, he hoped to eventually understand the general problem. But in 1881, Klein discovered a series of articles by an unknown French mathematician Henri Poincaré (1854-1912), who essentially solved the problem of uniformization. Klein greeted this dramatic event with dignity. He began correspondence with Poincaré; they exchanged 26 letters. Klein, already a famous mathematician (although only 5 years older than Poincaré), acts as a very tactful teacher. He introduces Poincaré to Riemann's theory, which he had no idea about, but instantly grasped. Klein decides to compete with Poincaré: he improves the proof of the main result and outlines its generalization. This story ended sadly for Klein: “The price that I had to pay for my work was, in any case, very high, since my health turned out to be completely shaken... Only by the autumn of 1884 the situation improved somewhat, but I never reached the previous degree of creative activity... My own creative activity in the field of theoretical mathematics ended in 1882.”

### 6. Last 40 years

Since 1886, Klein has been working in Göttingen. Thanks to Klein, this city turned into a true capital of mathematics. On his initiative, talented young mathematicians (among them Hilbert) were invited to Gottingen. Klein never stopped being interested in new ideas. His lecture courses, partially recorded and published, are devoted to a variety of areas of mathematics, mechanics, and physics. Klein's organizational and social activities are multifaceted. For 50 years he led the publication of one of the main mathematical journals, Mathematische Annalen. Klein's swan song was his “Lectures on the Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century,” read in 1914–1919 and published posthumously by his students Courant and Neubauer. Here is an excerpt from their preface: “These lectures are the ripened fruit of a rich life spent in the center of scientific events, the expression of penetrating wisdom and deep historical understanding, high human culture and a masterful gift of presentation.”

Klein spent a significant part of his time and effort on developing problems in school mathematics teaching and training teachers, something that probably no other mathematician of this caliber had done before him. “There is hardly a subject,” wrote Klein, “in the teaching of which there would be such a routine as in the teaching of mathematics. The course of elementary mathematics fell into a certain framework and seemed to freeze once and for all within established limits. From time to time, for one reason or another, some tasks are replaced by others, some paragraphs are excluded and others are introduced; but essentially this has almost no effect on all the material in school mathematics. New algebra textbooks bear the imprint of Euler's algebra, just as new geometry textbooks bear the imprint of Legendre's geometry. One might think that mathematics is a dead science, that nothing changes in it, that in this field of knowledge there are no new ideas, at least those that could become the property of non-specialists, a subject of general education.”

Klein strives to take into account the state of modern science and the connection between mathematics and physics in his teaching. He recommends systematically using transformations in geometry and abandoning the traditional division of school mathematics into subjects. The school course should be permeated with the concept of function; Ways of instilling “functional thinking” in students are carefully thought through. The presentation of geometry, according to Klein, should begin in a non-axiomatic version, and the axiomatic method should appear only when students are able to understand it.

With great tact, Klein maintained contacts with people involved in school mathematics, clearly limiting the range of his competence, never interfering in issues that required experience of direct work in school. Klein gave lectures for teachers, which were partially published. The most famous is his “Elementary Mathematics from a Higher Point of View.” These are not lectures on mathematics methods or an extended course in school mathematics. “I want this book to be useful in that it will encourage another teacher in our secondary school to independently think about a new, more appropriate presentation of the educational material that he teaches. It is exclusively from this point of view that one must look at my book, and not consider it a ready-made curriculum; I leave the development of the latter entirely to those who work at the school. If anyone suggests that I understood my activities differently, then this is a misunderstanding” (Klein).
