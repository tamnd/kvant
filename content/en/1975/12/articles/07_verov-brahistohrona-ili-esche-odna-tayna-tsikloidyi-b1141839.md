This article ends the series dedicated to one of the most popular curves in mathematics of the 17th century - cyclopides: “Tangents to rolls”, “Secrets of the cyclopides”.
All articles talk about how the 17th century mathematicians Galileo, Huygens, Newton, Leibniz, the brothers Johann and Jacob Bernoulli solved mathematical problems using physical considerations.

Galileo's error

At the very beginning of the 17th century, young Galileo tried to experimentally test his guess that free falling is an equally rooted movement. When he transferred his observations from the Leaning Tower of Pisa to the laboratory, he became very bothered by the fact that bodies were falling “too quickly.” To replace this movement, Galileo decided to replace the free falling of bodies with their movement along an inclined plane, assuming that it would also be equally rooted. Conducting these experiments, Galileo drew attention to the fact that at the final point the magnitude of the speed of a body standing on an inclined plane does not depend on the angle of inclination of the plane, but is determined only by the height H and coincides with the final speed of a body freely falling from the same height (as you well know above, in both cases $|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{2gH}$). Switzerland. Late 17th century

“Walking through the streets of Basel and discussing all sorts of mathematical questions, Johann and Jacob Bernoulli came up with the following question: what shape would a freely high chain, fixed at its two ends, take? They quickly and easily agree that the chain will take on that form of equilibrium in which its center of gravity will lie as low as possible... The physical part of the problem is thus exhausted. Determining the curve with the lowest center of softness for a given face between two points A and B is already a purely mathematical problem.” (Max).

Having studied a catenary line (this is the name of a line whose shape is taken by a flexible heavy non-stretching thread suspended at two points), the Bernoulli brothers became interested in other problems in which curves were sought that corresponded to the smallest value of a particular quantity. In 1696, Johann Bernoulli published a note “A new problem, mathematicians are invited to solve the problem.” However, this “new” problem was already considered by Galileo. We were talking about finding the brachistochrone - a line connecting a fixed pair of points along which the material point will descend under the influence of the force of the heavier forces of the highest. The problem of the brachistochrone, inaccessible at the beginning of the century even to the great Galileo, turned out to be very timely at the end of the century. It was very quickly solved by Johann Bernoulli himself and his brother Jacob, and their teacher Leibniz, as well as Newoton and L'Hopital. We'll tell you about Johann Bernoulli's solution: it uses geometric optics in a completely unexpected way!

“Without any other method, with the help of his geometric imagination alone, Johann Bernoulli solves the problem skillfully with one glance, while using the fact that the case has been extracted—the picture is a simple and amazingly beautiful one. Pricip Farm

Back in 140 BC. Claudius Ptolemy compiled a detailed table of the dependence of the angle of refraction of a light beam when passing from air into water from the angle of incidence; but only in 1621 Snell guessed the anomalous pattern connecting these angles:

$$\frac{\sin \alpha_{\text{наденя}}}{\sin \alpha_{\text{преломления}}} = k,$$

where $k$ is the refractive index, a constant for a fixed pair of media.

In 1650, Fermat gave a remarkable interpretation of this law. He started from the fact known to Heron of Alexandria that the equality of the angles of incidence and reflection can be deduced from the assumption that when reflected, light chooses the shortest path (Fig. 2).

Fermat suggested that the path of light between two points is the path that takes the least amount of time for light to travel compared to any other path between these points - now this statement is called “Fermat's reason”.
From Fermat's principle, in particular, it follows that since the speed of light in a homogeneous medium is constant, the shortest time travels along the path of the shortest length. It follows that the path of light in a homogeneous medium that does not have a pregative is rectilinear, as well as the law of reflection.
If the medium has a variable density, and the speed of light in its different parts is different, then the path of light propagation, which takes the least time to travel, should no longer be rectilinear. Let's see what happens in the case of refraction.
(All our further considerations refer to the flat case).

The empty straight line $l$ separates two media (on the plane), in the first of which the speed of light is $c_1$, and in the second - $c_2$; $A_1$ and $A_2$ are points lying on opposite sides of $l$. Let us find on $l$ a point $B$ such that $\frac{\sin \alpha_1}{\sin \alpha_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2}$, where $\alpha_1$ is the angle of incidence, $\alpha_2$ is the angle of refraction (see Fig. 3). The existence and uniqueness of such a point $B$ is easy to prove. Empty $C$ — any other point of the line $l$. Let us drop from it the variables $CE$ and $CF$ to $A_1B$ and $A_2B$, respectively.

Then $ECB = \alpha_1$, $FCB = \alpha_2$, and passing segment $BE$ at speed $c_1$ will take as much time as passing segment $BF$ at speed $c_2$. This means that light needs the same amount of time to travel the path $A_1BA_2$ as it does to travel two segments: $A_1E$ at a speed of $c_1$ and $FA_2$ at a speed of $c_2$. Since the lengths of the segments $A_1C$ and $A_2C$ are greater than the lengths of the segments $A_1E$ and $FA_2$, respectively, the light needs more time to travel the path $A_1CA_2$ than to travel the path $A_1BA_2$, and, therefore, the point $C$ is not suitable.

Thus, from Fermat’s principle, Snell’s law of refraction follows, and the refractive index of a light beam from one medium to another turns out to be equal to the ratio of the speeds of light in this medium $*$).

**Cycloid again!**

The cycloid (see Fig. 5) was the most popular curve among mathematicians of the 17th-18th centuries. One of the most remarkable properties of the cycloid was discovered by Huygens and was that the cycloid is a *tautochrone*. This means that if you make a trench in the shape of an inverted cycloid and throw a ball along such a trough, then from any height to the lowest point (coinciding with the top of the inverted cycloid!) it will arrive in the same time\*).

Mathematicians of the 17th century were “accustomed” to the fact that the cycloid is a “magic wand” in many matters. And now she was again destined to confirm her “reputation” - *brachistochrone also turned out to be a cycloid!*

In fact, if we denote by $\alpha\,(y)$ the angle that the tangent drawn to the cycloid *of radius r* (obtained when a circle of radius $r$ rolls without sliding along the straight line $\{y = 0\}$) at a point with ordinate $y$ makes with the vertical, then

$$\sin\alpha\,(y) = \sqrt{\frac{y}{2r}} \qquad (3)$$

(see «Kvant» No. 8, p. 21, relation (1)).

It can be shown that the cycloid is the only curve that satisfies relation (3). Thus, *a brachistochrone connecting two given points A and B*

**Multiple tasks**

Let's return to optics. Now we know that if in a flat inhomogeneous medium the speed of light changes according to the law $c\,(x, y) = k\sqrt{H-y}$ (i.e., similar to the change in the speed of a material point moving under the influence of gravity), then in such a medium light between two points will propagate along the arcs of inverted cycloids with points on the straight line $\{y = H\}$.

Try now to solve several problems on finding the path of light propagation between two points in an optically inhomogeneous medium, if the law of change in the speed of light is specified in this medium.

Task 1. The speed of light varies according to the law $c(x, y) = k \cdot (y - a)$. Prove that light between two points will propagate along two semicircles with diameters on the line $\{y = a\}$ (and the “starting” point is on this line).

Task 2. The speed of light varies according to the law $c(x, y) = \frac{k}{\sqrt{a - y}}$. Prove that in this case the light between two points will propagate along two parabolas.

So far in all problems the speed of light depends only on $y$. If an optical medium such as this dependence is more complex, for example, the speed of light is constant not on horizontal lines, but on some curves - the line of constancy of the speed of light - then the light between two points will propagate along such a curve $L$, for which

$$\frac{\sin \alpha \left(c(x_0, y_0)\right)}{c(x_0, y_0)} = \text{const},$$

where $\alpha \left(c(x_0, y_0)\right)$ is the angle between the tangent to the curve $L$ and the normal to the line of constant speed of light $c(x, y) = c(x_0, y_0)$.

Task 3. The speed of light varies according to the law $c(x, y) = k \sqrt{1 - r^2}$, where $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is the distance from the origin. Prove that in such a medium, light between two points will propagate along two circles perpendicular to a circle of radius 1 with a center at the origin.

Task 4. The speed of light varies according to the law $c(x, y) = kr$. Prove that in this case the light between two points will spread along two zonopicloud (see Newmon’s exercises at the end of the article “Secrets of Cyclops”, «Kvant», 1975, No. 8, p. 27).

If in problems 1-4 $c(x, y)$ is interpreted as the speed value of inhomogeneous mechanical motion, then the trajectories of light propagation obtained by solving these problems will be brachistochrones for corresponding mechanical systems.

Analogy between mechanics and optics

The main task of mechanics is to determine the position of a moving body at any given time.

I.K. Kikoin, A.K. Kikoin, A textbook on physics for the 8th grade of secondary school.

So, in mechanics, the trajectory of a material point is usually sought if the forces acting on the point are known and the initial position and velocity vector (initial conditions) are given. However, it is possible to intersect not with individual trajectories, but with a description of all the properties of trajectories for a given law of change in the acting forces (an additional specification of initial conditions will then select a specific trajectory from this set of trajectories). Thus, Galileo's classic result on the motion of a thrown body (horizontally or at an angle to the horizon) is that in the case of force, many trajectories consist of each other parabolas.

The use of optics in purely mechanical tasks suggests an attempt to highlight the possibility of trajectories for a specific mechanical system, under some condition of a minimum farm. Leibnitz thought about this, but the first formulation belongs to Maupertion. However, his constructions concerned the entire universe as a whole and did not contain precise statements. The first precise formulation is due to Euler (who studied mathematics with Johann Bernoulli). It relates to the following special situation.

Let a material point move along a plane under the influence of such a force that the potential energy depends only on the position of the point: $U = U(x, y)$. Due to the law of conservation of energy, the magnitude of the speed of the point $|v|$ then also depends

only from $(x, y)$:

$$|\vec{v}\,(x,y)| = \sqrt{\frac{2}{m}\,(E-U(x,y))}\,.$$

Let us consider a flat inhomogeneous optical medium in which the speed of light varies according to the law $c\,(x,y) = \dfrac{k}{|\vec{v}\,(x,y)|}$. Euler's principle is that *the trajectories of light propagating in such a medium will coincide with the possible trajectories of the original mechanical system* (a material point of mass $m$ with potential energy $U\,(x,y)$). Of course, Euler's principle can be formulated in such a way that it does not deal with the propagation of light.

In particular, from Problem 2 and Euler’s principle follows Galileo’s above statement about the trajectories of a material point moving under the influence of gravity.

Let us now explain Euler's principle. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the case when $U\,(x,y)$, and therefore $|\vec{v}|$, depends only on $y$. Since the potential energy is constant on horizontals, the force will be directed vertically, the horizontal component of the acceleration vector is zero, and the horizontal component of the velocity vector is constant, that is

$$|\vec{v}\,(y)|\cdot\sin \alpha\,(y) = \mathrm{const}, \qquad (4)$$

where $\alpha\,(y)$ is the angle between the velocity vector and the vertical at the trajectory point with ordinate $y$. Relation (4) together with (1) gives Euler’s principle for this particular case. (In the general case, it should be taken into account that the forces act perpendicular to the lines of constancy of potential energy and that, therefore, the components of the velocity vector tangent to these lines of constancy do not change.)

In modern mechanics, principles generalizing Euler's principle (Hamilton's principle, etc.) play an extremely important role.
