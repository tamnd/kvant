For a group of variable key geometry, all known geometric properties are invariants, since we do not distinguish the positions of figures on the plane. However, even in a traditional geometry course there are non-trivial statements about transformation invariants that are not variables. When ready, the congestion of angles, the property of a curve to be a circle, the ratio of the lengths of segments, and the ratio of areas are preserved. Having a certain supply of invariant properties, you can construct new ones. Relatively ready-made invariant will be the property of a straight line to beat a Bisserti angle, the property of a curve to be obtained. With respect to axial dilations, the property of a curve being a circle will no longer be an invariant, but the property of a curve being an ellipse (as well as a hyperbola or parabola) will be an invariant; the ratio of the lengths of segments lying on the same line (but not on different ones), and the ratio of areas are preserved. The consequence is the invariance of the property of a point to divide a segment in a given relation, the property of a straight line to be the median of a triangle. It can be shown that any affine transformation can be represented as a composition of a variable and axial dilations, and therefore all the indicated properties are invariant under affine transformations (examples of design invariants are given on p. 3).

The selection of invariants is only the first layer of geometry. Its main content consists of the autour theorem on the correspondence between invariant properties (they are called sizing properties). For example, the medians of a triangle are its affine invariants; the existence of a common point dividing the medians in a ratio of 2:1 is a non-trivial correspondence between these triangles. In the theorems on the intersection of bisserties and the determination of the dependence between invariants, it is ready.

Chasles drew attention to the possibility of using geometric transformations to obtain new theorems in 1837: “Everyone is able to take some known truth and apply to it various general principles of transformation; so he will receive other truths... Geneus is no longer necessary in order to contribute to the construction of the magnitude of the temple of science.” However, if Shaw decides literally: to take any theorem and apply an arbitrary transformation to it, then the result will be a true statement, but with such a crude formulation that it will have little chance of being included in the “temple of science.” Think, for example, what does the theorem about the intersection of bissertirics turn into if you do your own stretching? How can we explain which line the bissertirica goes to? Klein explains that it is important, on the contrary, to understand which of the transformed statements do not change, select transformations that simplify the picture as much as possible, and prove the statement in the resulting (simpler) form. Here's a traditional example. By means of an affine transformation, any triangle can be turned into an equilateral one, and since in the theorem about the intersection point of the medians of the redient area: for example, let $\lambda$ be the area of ​​a hexagon formed by “mridians” - straight lines connecting the vertices of the triangle with the point dividing the opposite side into three equal parts (the number $\lambda$ is uniquely defined). Then in any triangle the ratio of the area of ​​the hexagon formed by the tridians to the area of ​​the entire triangle is $\lambda$. Teller you can easily give other theorems of this kind.

One of the most important points in Klein's reasoning is the clarification of the reciprocity between the geometries associated with the groups $G_1$ and $G_2$ if $G_1 \subset G_2$. ($G_1$ is said to be a subgroup of $G_2$.) The larger group $G_2$ has fewer invariants than $G_1$, and all theorems associated with the group $G_2$ also hold for the geometry associated with the smaller group $G_1$.
