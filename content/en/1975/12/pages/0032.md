Switzerland. Late 17th century

“Walking through the streets of Basel and discussing all sorts of mathematical questions, Johann and Jacob Bernoulli came up with the following question: what shape would a freely high chain, fixed at its two ends, take? They quickly and easily agree that the chain will take on that form of equilibrium in which its center of gravity will lie as low as possible... The physical part of the problem is thus exhausted. Determining the curve with the lowest center of softness for a given face between two points A and B is already a purely mathematical problem.” (Max).

Having studied a catenary line (this is the name of a line whose shape is taken by a flexible heavy non-stretching thread suspended at two points), the Bernoulli brothers became interested in other problems in which curves were sought that corresponded to the smallest value of a particular quantity. In 1696, Johann Bernoulli published a note “A new problem, mathematicians are invited to solve the problem.” However, this “new” problem was already considered by Galileo. We were talking about finding the brachistochrone - a line connecting a fixed pair of points along which the material point will descend under the influence of the force of the heavier forces of the highest. The problem of the brachistochrone, inaccessible at the beginning of the century even to the great Galileo, turned out to be very timely at the end of the century. It was very quickly solved by Johann Bernoulli himself and his brother Jacob, and their teacher Leibniz, as well as Newoton and L'Hopital. We'll tell you about Johann Bernoulli's solution: it uses geometric optics in a completely unexpected way!

“Without any other method, with the help of his geometric imagination alone, Johann Bernoulli solves the problem skillfully with one glance, while using the fact that the case has been extracted—the picture is a simple and amazingly beautiful one. We must recognize in Johann Bernoulli a truly artistic nature, working in the field of natural plating. His brother, Jacob Bernoulli, was a scientific character of a completely different kind. He had more criticism removed, but much less creative imagination. And he solved the same problem, but in a much more cumbersome way. But he did not miss the opportunity to develop a method for solving a problem of this kind with greater thoroughness of sheathing. Thus, we find in everyday life separated those two sides of scientific talent, which in the greatest studies of nature, such as Newton, are combined with extraordinary force.” (Max).

Pricip Farm

Back in 140 BC. Claudius Ptolemy compiled a detailed table of the dependence of the angle of refraction of a light beam when passing from air into water from the angle of incidence; but only in 1621 Snell guessed the anomalous pattern connecting these angles:

$$\frac{\sin \alpha_{\text{наденя}}}{\sin \alpha_{\text{преломления}}} = k,$$

where $k$ is the refractive index, a constant for a fixed pair of media.

In 1650, Fermat gave a remarkable interpretation of this law. He started from the fact known to Heron of Alexandria that the equality of the angles of incidence and reflection can be deduced from the assumption that when reflected, light chooses the shortest path (Fig. 2).
