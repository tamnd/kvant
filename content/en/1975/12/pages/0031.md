Brachistochrone, or another mystery of the Cyclops

This article ends the series dedicated to one of the most popular curves in mathematics of the 17th century - cyclopides: “Tangents to rolls”, “Secrets of the cyclopides”.
All articles talk about how the 17th century mathematicians Galileo, Huygens, Newton, Leibniz, the brothers Johann and Jacob Bernoulli solved mathematical problems using physical considerations.

Galileo's error

At the very beginning of the 17th century, young Galileo tried to experimentally test his guess that free falling is an equally rooted movement. When he transferred his observations from the Leaning Tower of Pisa to the laboratory, he became very bothered by the fact that bodies were falling “too quickly.” To replace this movement, Galileo decided to replace the free falling of bodies with their movement along an inclined plane, assuming that it would also be equally rooted. Conducting these experiments, Galileo drew attention to the fact that at the final point the magnitude of the speed of a body standing on an inclined plane does not depend on the angle of inclination of the plane, but is determined only by the height H and coincides with the final speed of a body freely falling from the same height (as you well know above, in both cases $|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{2gH}$). Having studied the movements on inclined planes*), Galileo moved on to consider the movement of a mathematical point under the influence of force

*) For some observations of Galileo related to the motion of a body along inclined segments, see the article by S. G. Verov “Secrets of the Cyclopidae” (Galileo’s Problems), «Kvant», 1975, No. 8.
