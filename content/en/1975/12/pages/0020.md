position $A_0$, and at time $t$ - in position $A_t$ (Fig. 8). Arc $A_0A_t$ has length $t$, meaning the central angle $A_0OA_t$ contains $t$ radians. According to the definitions of sine and cosine, the ordinata of the point $A_t$ is equal to $\sin t$, the abscissa of the point $A_t$ is equal to $\cos t$. This means that the projection $B_t$ of the point $A_t$ onto the abscissa axis of motion is according to the law $x = \cos t$, and the projection $C_t$ of the point $A_t$ onto the ordinat axis is according to the law $y = \sin t$. Let's find the speeds of these movements.

Note that the linear velocity of the point $A_t$, like the angular velocity, is equal to 1. Let us decompose the linear velocity into two components - horizontal and vertical. The velocity vector $\vec{v}$ of point $A_t$ (having length 1) is directed tangent to the circle drawn at point $A_t$, and therefore forms an angle $t + \frac{\pi}{2}$ with the axis $Ox$, and an angle $t$ with the axis $Oy$. Therefore, the projection of the vector $\vec{v}$ onto the axis $Ox$ (that is, the speed of movement of the point $B_t$) is equal to $v_x = \cos (t + \frac{\pi}{2}) = -\sin t$, and its projection onto the axis $Oy$ (that is, the speed of movement of the point $C_t$) is equal to $v_y = \cos t$.

Since the speed is the product of the path in time, then, taking into account that the law of motion of the point is $B_t : x = \cos t$, and the speed is $v_x = -\sin t$, we obtain that $(\cos t)' = -\sin t$.

For the point $C_t : y = \sin t$, $v_y = \cos t$, from where $(\sin t)' = \cos t$.
