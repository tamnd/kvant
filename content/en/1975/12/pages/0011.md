6. Last 40 years

Since 1886, Klein has been working in Göttingen. Thanks to Klein, this city turned into a true capital of mathematics. On his initiative, talented young mathematicians are invited to Gottingen (the middle ones are Hilbert). Klein never ceased to be interested in new ideas. His lecture courses, partially recorded and published, are devoted to various areas of mathematics, mechanics, and physics. Klein's organizational and social activities are multifaceted. For 50 years he led the publication of one of the main mathematical journals, Mathematische Annalen. Klein's swan song was his “Lectures on the Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century,” read in 1914–1919 and published posthumously by his students Courant and Neubauer. Here is an excerpt from their preface: “These lectures are the mature fruit of a rich life spent in the center of scientific events, an expression of penetrating wisdom and deep historical understanding, high human culture and a masterful gift of presentation.”

Klein spent a significant part of his time and energy on developing problems in school mathematics teaching and training teachers, which, it is believed, no other mathematician of this magnitude had done before him. “There is hardly a subject,” wrote Klein, “in the teaching of which there would be such a routine as in the teaching of mathematics. The course of elementary mathematics resulted in a certain framework and precisely froze once and for all within the established limits. From time to time, for one reason or another, one task is replaced by others, one paragraph is excluded and others are introduced; but essentially this has almost no effect on all the material in school mathematics. New algebra textbooks bear the imprint of Euler's algebra, just as new geometry textbooks bear the imprint of Legendre's geometry. One might think that mathematics is a dead science, that nothing changes in it, that in this area of ​​knowledge there are no new ideas, at least those that could be made available to non-specialists, a subject of general education.”

Klein strives to take into account in his teaching the state of modern science, the connection between mathematics and physics. He recommends systematically using transformations in geometry and abandoning the traditional development of school mathematics subjects. The school course should be riddled with understandable functions; Ways of instilling “functional thinking” in students are carefully thought out. The presentation of geometry, according to Klein, should begin in a non-axiomatic version, and the axiomatic method should appear only when students are able to understand it.

With great support, Klein maintained contacts with people involved in school mathematics, clearly limiting the range of his competence, and never interfere in issues that required experience of unrepresented work at school. Klein gave lectures for teachers, which were partially published. The most famous is his “Elementary Mathematics from a Higher Point of View.” These are not lectures on mathematics methods or extensions of a school mathematics course. “I want this book to be useful in that it will encourage our high school foreign language teacher to independently develop the material, allowing him to use the materials he teaches. It is solely from this point of view that one should look at my book, and not consider it a ready-made curriculum; The processing of the latter is entirely presented to those who work at the school. If anyone suggests that I understand my activities differently, then this is a misunderstanding” (Klein).
