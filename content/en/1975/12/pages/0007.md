3. Keley-Klein model

In 1869, Klein became acquainted with Keley's theory, and at the end of the same year - rather superficially - with Lobachevsky's geometry. He immediately had the idea that one of Keli’s metrics leads to Lobachevsky’s geometry. It was an agreement, almost devoid of argumentation. Kely's theory and Lobachevsky's theory were radically different in appearance (Kely's calculations with a double ratio and Lobachevsky's axiomatic presentation), and Kely's geometry was not yet sufficiently developed to be able to test the axioms of Lobachevsky's geometry. In febrero 1870, Klein, giving a report on Kehley's theory at Weierstrass's seminar, decided to give evidence to his hypothesis. At this seminar, it was not customary to discuss science fiction projects: the “presumptuous” young man was explained that “these are two systems far removed from each other”; Klein was so prepared to defend the hypothesis that he “allowed himself to be convinced.” He later complained about Weierstrass that he “did not have the inclination to recognize from the squad the queues of heights that had not yet been reached.” But Klein did not stop believing in his hypothesis. In the summer of 1871, with the help of his friend Stolz, he had already thoroughly studied non-Euclidean geometry and was convinced of the validity of his idea. Further, having the proof, Klein could not convince others that he was right. Perhaps what was most fitting for Klein was that Keli remained among those who disagreed with his statement until the end of his life. “The combination of two is not able to draw conclusions from the provisions created by itself,” wrote Klein.

A few words about the Kehli-Klein model itself. “Points” in this model are the interior points of a circle (the circle can be replaced by a cloud bounded by any second-order curve), and “straight lines” are the chords of this circle (without ends). The intersection point of the “straight lines” is determined naturally; it is clear that an infinite number of “straight lines” pass through a “point” outside the “line” without intersecting the original one, that is, there is a negation of the parallel axiom of Euclidean geometry. We still need to make sure that all the other Euclidean axioms for the described model are satisfied: this will mean that the Klein model is a model of Lobachevsky geometry. The axioms concerning the relative position of a point and straight lines are relatively easily verified. But when it comes to testing the axioms of equality, then first of all you need to trust which segments are considered congruent; it is impossible to inherit the corresponding concepts of Euclidean geometry. Klein, following Kely, assumes the length of the segment $AB$ equal to: $|AB| = |\ln \{A, B, \alpha, \beta\}|$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the points of intersection of the “straight line” $AB$ with the boundary of the circle under consideration (this circle is called the absciotom). Project transformations that preserve the absciote preserve the so-defined “distance,” i.e., they are displacements in the Keley-Klein model of Lobachevsky geometry.
