Let vertices of type III be $k$. No more than three sides can be reflected into a type III vertex (the side within which this vertex lies, and two sides for which this vertex serves as a cone - for one left, for the other - right). Therefore, no more than $3k$ sides of triangles can be reflected in $k$ vertices of type III.

Let there be $x$ triangles in the partition. Let us express the number $3x$ of sides of triangles in terms of $t$, $k$ and $m$. To do this, write down the sum of the angles of these triangles.

On the one hand, the sum of angles $x$ of triangles is equal to $π(t-2)+2πm+πk$, where $π(t-2) = π(t-n)+π(n-2)$ is the sum of angles at vertices of type I, $2πm$ is the sum of angles at vertices of type II, $πk$ is the sum of angles at vertices of type III.

Hence,

$$πx = π(t-2) + 2πm + πk,$$

and the total side number is

$$3x = 6m + 3t + 3k - 6.$$

Since each of the sides of the triangles is divided into some vertex of type I or III, it follows from the above that the total number of sides can be no more than $t+3k$. Means,

$$6m+3t+3k-6 \leq t+3k,$$

whence $t+3m \leq 3$.

But $t>0$ and $m>0$; in addition, $t \geq n>3$, and we get a contradiction.

In other words, we got that there can be no more than three vertices on the sides of a $n$-gon, and therefore the sides are divided; This means that the $n$-gon has at most three sides (since on each side of the $n$-gon there must be at least one side that is broken).

In addition, from the last inequality it follows that when dividing a triangle, type II vertices cannot be obtained ($t=3$, therefore, $m=0$).

F328. Rigid rods of lengths $l_1$ and $l_2$ are connected by a hinge at point $A$. Their free ends move away from each other evenly at the speed of the corresponding $v_1$ and $v_2$, directed along one straight line (Fig. 13). The most important point is $A$ at the moment when the rods connect to each other at $90^\circ$. The movement of the rods will occur in one plane.

Let us imagine the vector $a$ of the total acceleration of point $A$ at the moment when the rods make an angle $90^\circ$ with each other, as the sum of two accelerations $a_1$ and $a_2$, directed along the corresponding rods (see Fig. 13). To calculate $a_1$ and $a_2$ we take advantage of the fact that the acceleration vector remains constant when moving from one inertial reporting frame to another.

Let us move to the reporting system moving at speed $v_2$ to the left. In this reporting system, the end of the left rod (point $B$) ends; the end of the right rod (point $C$) moves to the right with speed $v_1+v_2$, and point $A$ describes a circle of radius $l_2$ (Fig. 14). Therefore, the acceleration $a$ can be decomposed into centripetal (normal) acceleration and tangential (tangential) acceleration. These are the accelerations $a_2$ and $a_1$ of the corresponding one.
