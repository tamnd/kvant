The region of the parabola lies on one side of $l$, which means that $l$ is a *tangent* to the parabola. This provides proof of the optical property of the parabola: $\angle 1 \cong \angle 2$, since $l$ is the bisector of the angle $AMK$ *).

One more way to prove an optical property can be indicated; Let's consider it using the example of a hyperbola. Let us take two very close points on the hyperbola $M$ and $M_1$ (Fig. 6), and let $P$ be the intersection point of the lines $AM$ and $BM_1$, and $Q$ the intersection point of the lines $AM_1$ and $BM$. Let us now look “through a magnifying glass” at the quadrilateral $M_1PMQ$ (Fig. 7). We can assume (approximately) that $[PM_1] \parallel [MQ]$ and $[PM] \parallel [M_1Q]$, since the points $A$ and $B$ are very far away - compared to the dimensions of the quadrilateral $M_1PMQ$. So, we will assume (approximately) that $M_1PMQ$ is a parallelogram. Let us now draw circles with centers $A$ and $B$ passing through the point $M_1$. Near the parallelogram under consideration (see Fig. 7), the arcs of these circles will appear as straight lines $M_1K$ and $M_1L$, perpendicular to the sides of the parallelogram. We have:

$$|AM| - |BM| = (|AK| + |KM|) - (|BL| + |LM|) = (|AM_1| + |KM|) - (|BM_1| + |LM|) = (|AM_1| - |BM_1|) + (|KM| - |LM|).$$

But since both points $M$, $M_1$ lie on the hyperbola, then $|AM| - |BM| = |AM_1| - |BM_1|$, and therefore $|KM| = |LM|$. The resulting equality means that the right triangles $KMM_1$ and $LMM_1$ are congruent (on the hypotenuse and leg), and therefore $\angle 1 \cong \angle 2$.

---

*) For other proofs of the optical properties of the ellipse, hyperbola and parabola, see the article by I. N. Bronstein “General properties of conic sections”, «Kvant», 1975, No. 5.

This provides a proof of the optical property (in order to make this “approximate” reasoning accurate, we still need to go to the limit at $M_1 \to M$).

Now let's look at some optical and mechanical interpretations of this proven property. Let us assume that the ellipse is a “mirror” curve from which a ray of light is reflected according to the law “the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.” If a point source of light is placed at one focus of such a mirror ellipse, then after reflection from the walls of the ellipse, all rays will pass through the second focus - this is a direct consequence of the optical property. The described phenomenon can be observed in reality, in three-dimensional space. To do this, you need to take a surface obtained by rotating an ellipse around a straight line passing through its focal point.
