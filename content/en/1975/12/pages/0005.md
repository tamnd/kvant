**2. "Golden Age" of Geometry**

Back in the 17th century, Desargues (1593-1662) and Pascal (1623-1662) managed to obtain remarkable geometric results using central design. These results were forgotten for almost a century and a half. Gaspard Monge (1746-1818) again drew attention to the great possibilities of the design method; he talked about this in a course on descriptive geometry, which he taught at the Polytechnic School. N. Bourbaki dates the “golden age” of geometry from Monge’s “Descriptive Geometry” (1795).

Poncelet created this geometry during the two years he spent in captivity in Saratov after the War of 1812. Poncelet shared his results with his fellow prisoners, who also listened to Monge at the Ecole Polytechnique. These results were published in 1822 in the “Treatise on the Projective Properties of Figures.”

Like his predecessors, Poncelet supplements each line with an *infinitely distant* point, considering that all straight lines parallel to each other have a common point at infinity (“intersect” at it). All points at infinity form a straight line at infinity. On a completed plane, parallelism becomes a special case of intersection and does not require special consideration (for example, the statement that a single straight line parallel to it passes through a point outside a line turns into a statement that a single straight line passes through two different points, one of which is ordinary and the other at infinity). With central projection, the end point may not have an image (“go to infinity”), but on a plane filled with infinitely distant points this mapping is already one-to-one.

Central design translates one plane into another; having performed several projections in a row, we can return to the original plane, obtaining a transformation of this plane. Such transformations (they began to be called *projective*) include displacements, homotheties, and dilations. *Projective transformations are one-to-one (on the completed plane) and transform lines into straight lines* (later it turned out that any transformation with these properties is projective). Projective transformations that take the line at infinity into itself are called *affine*; affine transformations are one-to-one on the ordinary plane. Poncelet studied geometric objects that are preserved under projective transformations. It turns out that under projective transformations a conic section also transforms into a conic section (but, for example, a hyperbola can transform into a parabola, and any conic section can be transformed into a circle by a projective transformation).
