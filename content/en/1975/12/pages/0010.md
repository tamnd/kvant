For Posny, in each specific geometry it is important to find statements that remain valid for geometry with wider groups of transformations. Sometimes the possibility of “transferring” a statement to geometry with a wider group of transformations becomes clear only after reworking the formulation of the statement.

Kelly's ideology in the language of the Ergangen program is that one can move in the opposite direction, considering a group of transformations that preserve some fixed object. In this case, often invariants for a subgroup can be constructed using invariants for a group (distances in ενclidian and non-Euclidean geometries - using a double relation).

Invariants for the larger group and, accordingly, between them usually describe the process. In particular, for a project team, the problem of finding invariants can be made completely algebraic and solved.

The Ergangen Program ended the “golden age” of classical geometry. The number of new geometries is increasing; Gradually, geometric language produced a significant part of mathematics. “Classical geometry outgrew itself, and from a living independent science turned into a universal language of modern mathematics, possessing exceptional flexibility and convenience” (N. Bourbaki).

5. Extern at the Riemann School

After the Ergangen Program, Kelly turns to the theory of algebraic functions - an area in which Gauss, Legendre, Abel, Jacobi, Weierstrass, and Riemann worked. The ideas of Riemann (1826 - 1866), with whom he was not personally acquainted, turned out to be much closer to Klein. According to Kelly, he was “an external student at the Riemann School, ... and external students, as you know, if they take on any business, they work with special time, or they are motivated to work only by deep interest.” Kelin later wrote that he saw his task in combining Riemann with Galya, that is, in the penetration of group theory into the geometric theory of functions of a complex variable. In Kelin's own opinion, this was the main area of ​​his scientific activity.

Unfortunately, we will not be able to tell stories about this activity of Kelin, since here it is already necessary to require from the reader special knowledge that goes far beyond the scope of the school curriculum. But we will still mention one circumstance.

Klein deals with the so-called problem of uniformity. Considering important cases with diversity time and with the general task. But in 1881 Klein discovered the serial work of the unknown French mathematician Henri Poincaré (1854-1912), who essentially solved the unification gap *). Klein greeted this dramatic event with dignity. He began correspondence with Poincaré; they exchanged 26 letters. Klein, already a famous mathematician (though only 5 years older than Poincaré), acts as a very tactical teacher. He introduces Poincaré to Riemann's theory, which he had no idea about, but instantly grasped. Klein decides to do modern things with Poincaré: he improves the proof of the main result and outlines its generalization. This story ended sadly for Kelin: “The price that I had to pay for my work was, in any case, very high, since my health turned out to be completely shaken... Only by the autumn of 1884 did I no longer reach a higher degree of creative activity... My own creative activity in the field of theoretical mathematics came to an end in 1882.”

*) The general problem of uniformity still appeared among the problems of Hilbert (1900) and was completely solved in 1907 independently by Poincaré and Kebe.
