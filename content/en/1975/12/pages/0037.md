Let us consider a flat inhomogeneous optical medium in which the speed of light varies according to the law $c\,(x,y) = \dfrac{k}{|\vec{v}\,(x,y)|}$. Euler's principle is that *the trajectories of light propagating in such a medium will coincide with the possible trajectories of the original mechanical system* (a material point of mass $m$ with potential energy $U\,(x,y)$). Of course, Euler's principle can be formulated in such a way that it does not deal with the propagation of light.

In particular, from Problem 2 and Euler’s principle follows Galileo’s above statement about the trajectories of a material point moving under the influence of gravity.

Let us now explain Euler's principle. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the case when $U\,(x,y)$, and therefore $|\vec{v}|$, depends only on $y$. Since the potential energy is constant on horizontals, the force will be directed vertically, the horizontal component of the acceleration vector is zero, and the horizontal component of the velocity vector is constant, that is

$$|\vec{v}\,(y)|\cdot\sin \alpha\,(y) = \mathrm{const}, \qquad (4)$$

where $\alpha\,(y)$ is the angle between the velocity vector and the vertical at the trajectory point with ordinate $y$. Relation (4) together with (1) gives Euler’s principle for this particular case. (In the general case, it should be taken into account that the forces act perpendicular to the lines of constancy of potential energy and that, therefore, the components of the velocity vector tangent to these lines of constancy do not change.)

In modern mechanics, principles generalizing Euler's principle (Hamilton's principle, etc.) play an extremely important role.
