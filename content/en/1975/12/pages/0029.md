Since $\Delta r' \ll r'$ and $\Delta r'' \ll r''$, the last term in this expression can be neglected and written

$$r''\Delta r' - r'\Delta r'' = 0.$$

It follows that

$$\frac{\Delta r''}{\Delta r'} = \frac{r''}{r'} = \frac{4f^2}{r'^2},$$

$$\frac{\Delta r''}{\Delta r'} = \frac{4f^2}{r'^2}, \quad \Delta r'' = \Delta r'\ \frac{4f^2}{r'^2}. \qquad (6)$$

Thus, the energy that in the incident beam passed through the annular area of ​​width $\Delta r'$, after double reflection of the rays from the mirror, passes through the annular area $\Delta r'' < \Delta r'$. This means that the amount of energy passing per unit time through a unit area (this characteristic is called intensity) in the “reflected” ring is greater than in the “incident” ring. We considered the case when $r' > 2f$. If $r' < 2f$, then the “reflected” ring is wider than the “incident” one, which means that the intensity of the reflected light is less than the intensity of the incident one.

However, this does not mean that on the axis itself the intensity becomes infinitely large. After all, those rays that were on the periphery of the incident beam come out closest to the axis. It is obvious that the maximum distance $r'_{\max}$ from the incident beam to the axis of symmetry is equal to the radius of the mirror.
