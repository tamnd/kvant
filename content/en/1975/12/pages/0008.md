Klein investigated other non-Euclidean geometries to which Keley metrics lead, discovering, in particular, the model of Riemann geometry (in Riemann geometry the sum of the angles of a triangle is greater than $\pi$, in Lobachevsky geometry it is always less than $\pi$).

Let us now discuss what the Keley-Klein model provides for Lobachevsky geometry. First of all, this is a different way of presentation from the axiomatic one, more visual. Klein prefaces his publication (1871) with the words that his goal is “to give a new visual presentation of the mathematical results of works related to the theory of parallels, and to make them accessible to clear understanding” (Beltrami formulates his goal in approximately the same way). However, building a model solves far more than just a methodological problem. Nowadays, the Keley-Klein model is considered primarily as a means of proving the consistency of Lobachevsky geometry. In the Keley-Klein model, objects of Lobachevsky geometry are formed in the language of Euclidean geometry, so that after translation into this language, theorems of Lobachevsky geometry turn into theorems of Euclidean geometry, and thus Lobachevsky geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is consistent.

Klein saw the main significance of the model he built in something else. He put projective geometry at the forefront, of which the geometries of Euclid and Lobachevsky are equal and independent parts. In this regard, the independence of the constructed model of Lobachevsky’s geometry from Euclidean geometry was emphasized, for which, in turn, the ability to build projective geometry without using Euclidean geometry was important (according to Staudt, see paragraph 2). It was this moment that made Keli suspicious of the existence of a vicious circle. Klein wrote: “Instead of constructing images of non-Euclidean geometry within our metric geometry, we substantiate a projective geometry free from any metric representations, which contains, as special cases that can be clearly classified, all known geometric systems.”

## 4. Erlangen program

For centuries, the word "geometry" was used only in the singular. But then Lobachevsky’s geometry appeared, then Riemann’s geometry, and finally, mathematicians realized that

there are many different geometries. A natural question arose: what is geometry? In 1872, Klein expressed his point of view in a lecture he gave in connection with his assumption of a professorship in Erlangen. This is how the Erlangen Program appeared, apparently Klein's most famous work. Essentially, there are no new results in it; all attention is concentrated on the search for a principle that allows us to systematize the very amorphous formation into which geometry had turned by that time.

According to Klein, *the main attribute of any geometry is a certain set $G$ of one-to-one transformations of some set $M$.* There must be enough transformations so that each point of the set $M$ can be transferred to another by some transformation from $G$ (in this case they say that $G$ acts on $M$ *transitive*). This point of view was inspired, of course, by projective geometry, in which from the very beginning certain transformations (central projections) were primary, while in Euclidean geometry (in the traditional presentation) other objects are primary: straight lines, segments, congruent figures, etc.

The next point is that the transformation set $G$ must be a *group*. This means that any two transformations from $G$ performed in a row can be replaced by one transformation, also from $G$; in addition, along with each transformation $g \in G$ to $G$ the *inverse to it* is included: $g^{-1}$ (if $g$ translates $x$ into $y$, then $g^{-1}$ translates $y$ into $x$).

So, with each *transformation group* $G$ there is some geometry associated. What is the content of such geometry?
